Patient and clinician perspectives in the use of machine learning and artificial intelligence in the context of acute neurology
Authors: Egide Abahuje, Ethan J Houskamp, Juliana Silva Pinheiro do Nascimento, Elaf M Agha, William K Thompson, Kelly N Michelson, Andrew Naidech
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinician perspectives on machine learning and artificial intelligence (ML/AI) vary with discipline. However, fewer studies describe patient perspectives and address medical situations that require rapid decisions with durable consequences for patient outcomes. This study characterized perspectives (qualitatively) and sentiment (quantitatively) regarding the use of ML/AI for patient management in neurological emergencies.MethodsWe conducted semi-structured interviews with survivors (or their proxy) of intracranial hemorrhage, and clinicians who care for such patients. Interviews were analyzed qualitatively using thematic analysis, and quantitatively using sentiment analysis to assess attitudes using a transformer-based language model with scores from -1 (most negative) to 1 (most positive).ResultsWe enrolled 21 participants (14 patients, 1 proxy, and 6 clinicians) and reached thematic saturation. Help with clinical decision-making was cited as an advantage of ML/AI. Participants noted the importance of considering ML/AI as an adjunct to clinical care, not as a replacement for clinicians. Over-reliance on recommendations, potentially leading to diminution of clinician skill, incorrect ML/AI recommendations, potential liability, and bias were cited as challenges. Clinician and patient education were noted as a potential burden. Median sentiment scores ranged from 0.0 (neutral) to 0.3 (positive). Sentiment varied with question type (P < .001). Questions about clinicians using ML/AI for patient care had the highest sentiment score.Discussion and ConclusionPatients and clinicians expressed mixed views about ML/AI. Potential benefits related to improved decision-making and concerns focused on bias, liability, and the need for further education. Future work should address how best to incorporate ML/AI into education and obviate potential burdens as ML/AI is integrated into clinical care.

Background and significance

The use of machine learning and artificial intelligence (ML/AI) is rapidly expanding in clinical medicine. ML/AI algorithms typically use large amounts of data, are intended for use on previously unevaluated data (eg, data of an individual patient not used to create the algorithms), and are not easily explainable by humans (eg, humans cannot generally explain the workings of a many-layered neural network). ML/AI has the potential to improve patient care, particularly through the automation of routine analysis and image recognition (eg, histopathology).1 However, ML/AI is hampered by several inadequately addressed technical, legal, and human roadblocks.

Several aspects of ML/AI have been inadequately addressed, such as potential bias in algorithms,2 lack of interpretability, and uncertain liability when clinical care is impacted by recommendations from ML/AI. In contrast to medical devices or drugs, the regulation, liability, and acceptability of ML/AI algorithms remain a moving target.3 Different medical specialties have developed different recommendations regarding the use of ML/AI in patient care. For example, the Australian College of Dermatology recommended obtaining patient consent before using ML/AI algorithms for the diagnosis of skin cancer,4 although the perspective and knowledge of patients are understudied. A radiology group advised using anonymized patient data to train ML/AI, potentially requiring a different consent when using health data.5 Other similar examples abound, including but not limited to endoscopy,6 breast imaging,7 and cytopathology.8 Practices for ML/AI continue to evolve, with heightened challenges for patients and when medical decisions must be made rapidly.5

Patients generally have had positive attitudes towards the use of AI in healthcare to enhance prediction, improve diagnostic accuracy, and plan treatment.6,7 However, patients experienced concerns about clinical decision-making, data privacy, and diagnostic inaccuracies.8 Patients recommended that clinicians oversee the use of AI in healthcare with the goal of maintaining physician-patient relationships.9 Patient perceptions of the use of AI were driven by their past experiences with illness and technology literacy.10

The setting of patient care influences how AI may be used. Some settings allow for time needed to make treatment plans (eg, planning for radiation treatment in cancer).9 Rapid decision-making is crucial in the time-limited setting of acute neurology because decisions that must be made in minutes have permanent consequences on patient outcomes (eg, the decision to administer fibrinolytic treatments that may lead to intracranial hemorrhage).10–12 There are few data that describe the perspective of patients on ML/AI algorithms in the acute setting, despite potentially higher risks. Additionally, it is not known if the perceptions of clinicians and patients on use of ML/AI are aligned. We qualitatively ascertained the perspectives of patients, and clinicians on the use of ML/AI in acute neurology. An exploratory objective was to quantitatively assess participant sentiment (positive or negative feelings) regarding the use of ML/AI in patient care.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study involved qualitative and exploratory quantitative analysis of semi-structured interviews conducted at an urban academic institution. We enrolled patients with a history of spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage, their patient-identified proxies (ie, those people who make decisions on behalf of patients, such as spouses), and clinicians who care for patients with acute neurological conditions. We chose intracranial hemorrhage because this is a model acute neurological condition (health prior to onset, presentation, acute treatment, rehabilitation, outcome assessment) that has multiple potential points of intersection with ML/AI algorithms, such as neuroimaging, prediction of outcome, and management of seizures.11,13,14

Participants were identified from a prospective registry of patients in a healthcare system that enrolls patients predominantly near metropolitan Chicago. Patients or their proxy in the registry were eligible if the patient experienced a confirmed intracranial hemorrhage, could communicate fluently in English, and agreed to the interview. Recruitment was done by emailing and/or calling potential patients and proxies from the registry using a standard script to assess their willingness to give consent and be interviewed.

Clinicians (adult neurology, neurosurgery, and emergency medicine specialists) were identified from the NIH Stroke Trials network (requested by A.M.N. with blinded recruitment). Participation in the study was voluntary, and all participants provided verbal consent before participating. All participants received the same monetary payment ($50) at the conclusion of the interview. The Northwestern University Institutional Review Board reviewed and approved the study (Committee Reference Number: IORG0000247). The study sponsor had no role in the conduct of the study, data collection, analysis, or decision to submit for publication.

Data collection

We used a semi-structured interview guide to collect data. An experienced clinician (A.M.N.) and qualitative data researcher (K.N.M.) supervised the process of interview guide development, and qualitative data collection and analysis. To create the interview guide, we used the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability conceptual model, developed to obtain input on the acceptability of clinical interventions.15 We also based our interview guide on existing literature related to the bioethical challenges of AI implementation in medicine.15,16 The interview guide sought to understand participants’ perspectives about using ML/AI to support clinical decision-making for patients following hemorrhagic stroke. Participants were given information about how ML/AI could be used, but the questions were agnostic to what the ML/AI would do. The domains of the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability addressed in the interview guide included (1) affective attitude, (2) ethicality, (3) opportunity cost, (4) perceived effectiveness, and (5) self-efficacy.15 We added topics related to the roles of government regulation of ML/AI and clinician accountability/legal liability in situations when the ML/AI provides an incorrect recommendation or if a clinician does not follow an ML/AI recommendation. We designed one interview guide for patients and proxies and a version with parallel questions modified for clinicians. The interview guides were pilot-tested and refined with eligible participants before data collection. We initially tested the interview guide with 3 non-participating clinicians. During the pilot test of the interview guide, we reworded and revised the order of the questions. During pilot testing of the interview guide with 2 patients, we found that no further modifications were appropriate for the patient/proxy interview guide, and kept the transcripts that were obtained during interview guide pilot testing with patients because questions did not change. Both interview guides are included as Supplementary Materials 1. Two study team members (E.A. and J.S.P.N.) conducted the interviews via Zoom. E.A. was a postdoctoral fellow at the institution where the study was conducted. E.A. has experience in qualitative and mixed-methods research. J.S.P.N. is a research coordinator at the institution where the study was conducted. Neither E.A. nor J.S.P.N. had prior relationship with the participants before the onset of this study. No one else was present besides the participants and the researchers during the interviews. No interviewee stopped the interview once it started. The interviews lasted approximately 30 minutes on average, with the duration depending on the time needed to thoroughly address all the questions in the interview guide. Interviews were audio recorded, professionally transcribed, and then de-identified before coding. We stopped conducting interviews after 21 participants and confirmed thematic saturation (see analysis below).17

Qualitative data analysis

We uploaded de-identified transcripts into Dedoose software (v9, SocioCultural Research Consultants, Los Angeles, CA). We analyzed interview transcripts using framework analysis. First, we deductively organized the data into domains defined by the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability.15 Then, we inductively created additional domains to describe contents that were not covered by the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability.15 Finally, we created subcodes within each domain. A post-doctoral fellow with experience in qualitative data analysis (E.A.), developed the initial codebook by reviewing 2 transcripts. Additional research team members (E.A., J.S.P.N., E.M.A., and K.N.M.) reviewed and agreed on the codebook. This initial testing of the codebook on 3 transcripts by 2 study team members (E.A. and E.M.A.) required no additional changes. Thereafter, 2 members of the study team independently coded the first half of the transcripts (E.A. and E.M.A.) and then met to determine and discuss the coding process; no changes in the codebook were suggested at this point, preliminarily confirming data saturation. The second half of the interviews were then coded by the same 2 study team members (E.A., E.M.A.). Discrepancies between coders were reviewed and recorded based on the consensus of the 2 coders. Through review of the codes by all the co-authors, latent themes, and subthemes were identified. We did not quantitatively assess inter-rater reliability because our goal was to communicate the diversity views rather than reveal universal objective facts.18

To further validate the transferability of our findings, after conducting qualitative data analysis, we sent the interview guide, and a table summarizing the themes and representative quotes to 7 clinicians who were eligible for enrolment in the study, did not previously participate, and were at a different institution from participating clinicians. We asked them to rate the results on a scale from 1 (least reasonable) to 5 (most reasonable) and to describe the rationale behind their rating. This was done in lieu of member checking. Of 7 to whom an email was sent, 1 email was inoperative, 1 clinician felt uncomfortable rating ML/AI, and the remaining 5 clinicians responded to our invitation to provide feedback on our results. The median score of the 5 responders was 4.5. Participants recommended moving a few quotes under different subthemes where they fit best. As a result, we identified different quotes that better reflected those domains.

We followed the steps of the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) reporting guideline for qualitative studies.19

Quantitative data analysis

We used sentiment analysis to quantitatively assess participants’ sentiment regarding ML/AI (ie, feeling positive or negative about the use of ML/AI in patient care). Sentiment analysis evaluates the emotional tone of text (in this case, the transcripts) based on its lexical features and by categorizing the words’ valence (ie, the words’ semantic orientation). To conduct this exploratory analysis, unmodified responses were classified by question type (see Supplementary Materials 2) defined by expert medical input (E.J.H., A.M.N.) with oversight by an experienced computational linguist (W.K.T.). Responses to each question type were evaluated on a positive to negative scale using a publicly available20 transformer-based model, the same underlying technology as ChatGPT.21 Text was converted to numeric values represented as a sentiment, with -1 representing a completely negative sentiment and 1 representing a completely positive sentiment. Examples of sentences with positive and negative sentiment can be found in Supplementary Materials 3. These non-normally distributed data are presented as median [Q1–Q3]. We tested the hypothesis that sentiment varied across question type with Kruskal-Wallis H, and corrected for multiple comparisons using Dunn’s test. We used open-source software (Python v3.11) for the analysis of transcriptions.

Affective attitude refers to participants’ perception of ML/AI as positive or negative. Clinicians and patients shared positive and negative attitudes about using ML/AI in managing hemorrhagic stroke.

Positive attitudes described that ML/AI as an additional resource for clinicians leading to improved quality of patient care. Clinicians felt that ML/AI could help them make more accurate decisions by incorporating large amounts of information leading to better predictions of patient outcomes. Additionally, clinicians perceived that AI would help speed up the process of triage and prioritization of care. Some patients expressed a hope that clinicians would interact and explain care to them instead of having ML/AI reduce interactions with clinicians. While supportive of ML/AI, some patients noted that humans are better at interpersonal interactions, thus claiming that the need for human involvement should continue even as ML/AI is introduced.

Negative attitudes were expressed by clinicians and patients about using ML/AI. Clinicians noted that some of the clinicians may use ML/AI without correctly understanding its applications and limitations. For example, a clinician might rely on ML/AI for stroke from one etiology when it was approved for stroke from a different etiology. Others described concern that reliance on ML/AI by clinicians could be problematic when the ML/AI is imperfect or could lead to a reduction in clinicians’ decision-making skills. Potential inaccuracies were a concern. Some clinicians expressed concern about the potential for glitches in the system leading to inaccurate predictions. Some patients expressed concerns about the possibility that ML/AI might use false data and produce inaccurate predictions.

Ethicality refers to the fit of ML/AI with the values of those involved. Sub-themes of ethicality related to data privacy, transparency, and bias. Clinicians felt routinely obtained medical data could be used to develop and refine ML/AI algorithms if existing standards surrounding privacy (eg, HIPAA) were followed. Some patients noted that data is “everywhere,” eroding previous expectations of privacy of medical data. Some patients felt privacy concerns were outweighed by the benefits of using their data to help others. Views related to transparency about the use of ML/AI were mixed. Some clinicians felt no need to disclose the use of ML/AI to patients. Some patients described that they would want clinicians to disclose whether ML/AI was used in their care, while others did not. A few clinicians addressed concerns about bias. These concerns focused on the possibility that ML/AI could potentiate existing healthcare inequalities by using data that reflects existing societal biases. However, some clinicians noted that ML/AI could be a solution to problems related to bias because ML/AI might not be influenced by subjectivity from clinicians.

Opportunity cost considers how ML/AI could draw attention and resources from elsewhere. Subthemes noted potential impacts on critical thinking and adverse impacts on clinical education. Some patients described how reliance on ML/AI to interpret images could negatively impact clinician’s ability to think critically. Both clinicians and patients shared concerns about how technology may affect what trainees are required to learn because ML/AI would take cognitive processing from clinicians, particularly those with less experience. For example, if ML/AI “read” a computed tomography scan and identified the image(s) which best showed an area of ischemia, the trainees would learn to interpret summary images rather than the entire set of images. One patient noted that ML/AI could lead students to focus more on the information delivered by ML/AI rather than learning what informs the results provided by the ML/AI, leaving them without the skills to make the decisions themselves. Clinicians noted that to work with ML/AI, clinicians will need to acquire new skills not currently taught in medical school.

Perceived effectiveness refers to expectations of ML/AI’s likelihood to achieve its purpose. Both clinicians and patients noted that ML/AI could be effective because it incorporates large datasets, so it considers more information than a human can. There was a general recognition that ML/AI is in its infancy, will continue to change, and will likely expand in unforeseen ways. This led some patients to express caution about the current effectiveness of ML/AI. Some patients were concerned that unusual cases (eg, cases the differ from the norm or involve unique circumstances/complications or reflect a rare condition) might lead to inaccurate recommendations since there are limited data for training ML/AI about unusual cases. Some clinicians cautioned how, when consequential decisions must be made in a timely manner, inaccurate ML/AI recommendations could be dangerous.

Self-efficacy indicates confidence that participants could effectively use ML/AI. Since we asked participants about a hypothetical situation, we included comments from patients and clinicians about what was needed for them to feel comfortable using the technology. Patients and clinicians described clinicians’ roles and responsibilities and provided recommendations for implementation. Some patients described that clinicians should revise the recommendations provided by ML/AI and use their critical thinking to make decisions regarding patient care. Furthermore, patients shared that the technology developers must be transparent on how the technology was developed. Clinicians mentioned that they must understand the strengths and limitations of the ML/AI and where it should be applied. Additionally, clinicians said that some of their colleagues may require additional education to better understand the strengths and limitations of ML/AI.

Accountability and liability: Both patients and clinicians expressed concerns about liability related to ML/AI if they made a different decision from an ML/AI recommendation and the patient had a poor outcome, or, conversely, if they followed an ML/AI recommendation that was incorrect. Considering these situations, many patients described that some accountability and potentially even legal liability would rest with clinicians based on the clinical decisions made, regardless of whether their clinical decisions were in line with ML/AI recommendations. Clinicians described they should use ML/AI as an adjunct to, rather than replacement for, their own medical decision-making. Some clinicians felt that it will be challenging to determine who (manufacturers, hospitals, or clinicians) should be held responsible for mistakes made when ML/AI is used.

Government regulation of ML/AI: Given concerns about effectiveness, clinicians and patients expressed the importance of regulation and the need for government involvement in regulation. They described that the ML/AI technology should be approved by regulatory authorities before being used in supporting clinical decision-making.

Discussion and conclusion

We found variable attitudes from patients (including one patient proxy) and clinicians regarding ML/AI in the context of neurology. Positive aspects of ML/AI included help with clinical decision-making, increased time for patient-clinician interactions, and considering ML/AI as an adjunct to clinical care. Potential challenges reported by participants related to the use of ML/AI included over-reliance on automated recommendations, potential reductions in clinician skill, identifying incorrect ML/AI recommendations, liability, and bias. Clinicians described the need for education starting in medical school and lasting throughout a medical career. Quantitative sentiment analysis of the text found neutral to moderately positive median sentiment scores, mirroring qualitative analysis.

Our results fit into a context of rapidly evolving literature and practice. While there are few data on how patients feel about the use of ML/AI in their care, these data suggest patients are broadly supportive, with some major caveats in terms of clinical skill, liability, and bias. The consequences of diagnostic errors are higher when decisions must be made in minutes and have enduring consequences for patient outcomes than fields where there is time for further expert human review. Compared to the existing literature, these data provide needed information about the perspective of patients and on acute diagnosis and management that is supported by ML/AI.

The use of ML/AI would require additional clinician vigilance to ensure that ML/AI recommendations are accurate.22 This additional vigilance is likely to require additional education because ML/AI is novel, rapidly evolving, and most clinicians have little to no ML/AI training. Educating clinicians about ML/AI requires educational programming from undergraduate medical education to continuing medical education for more experienced clinicians.23 ML/AI uses advanced biostatistics, computer science, and quantitative text analysis that is far afield from the basic statistical tests (eg, t-tests) typically taught in many current undergraduate medical course offerings. It is not clear how ML/AI should fit into an already crowded medical school curriculum, or what depth of education is appropriate for clinicians. Furthermore, some consideration about how much residents and fellows should be exposed to education about ML/AI in their post-graduate training is needed. One argument for focusing ML/AI education in residency and fellowship training is that the content could be tailored to that needed for the learner’s medical specialty.23–25 Challenges to incorporating ML/AI education in post-graduate medical education include expanding subspecialties and work-hour restrictions. For those in practice, continuing medical education is the typical mechanism for refreshing skills and encountering new knowledge. The infrastructure to teach practicing clinicians about ML/AI will require additional capacity, expert faculty, and prioritization in continuing education. Given the challenges of educating clinicians on ML/AI, an alternative option would be for the ML/AI developers to incorporate visual cues to alert clinicians in situations where the accuracy of AI output is uncertain.22

Clinician decision-making is often the focus of liability or inadvertent errors. It is not clear when ML/AI recommendations should become “standard of care,” or how much weight should be given to automated recommendations. For decisions that are already considered high risk (eg, administration of fibrinolytic and acute stroke treatment),26,27 the effects of ML/AI could both ease decision-making burden (eg, prompt correct decision from complex information) and complicate decision-making (eg, lead to erroneous or biased recommendations). Having clinicians document in the medical record how ML/AI contributed to clinical decision-making could be helpful, though requiring such documentation would add to already burdened clinical workloads. It is crucial to make ML/AI workflows intuitive, informative, and unobtrusive.28 It is also important for clinicians to have a voice in the changing legal landscape addressing liability related to ML/AI.

Bias is a potential concern in the use of ML/AI models. Participants were concerned about bias in ML/AI algorithms. Bias in ML/AI in healthcare has been well demonstrated, and disparities in medical treatment persist related to race, socioeconomic status, gender, and geolocation.2 However, fewer than half of hospitals have examined ML/AI algorithms in use for bias.29 Some ML/AI models explicitly note racial differences in neurological care (eg, Black race in the prediction for gastrostomy after intracerebral hemorrhage, where AI would predict the need for more gastrostomy in Black patients).30–32 Other algorithms have found not been shown to lead to racial disparities once mediators are accounted for (eg, a reduced likelihood of Black patients to receive anti-seizure medication disappears when risk factors for seizures are included in the algorithm).33 Additional support, education, and tools are needed to routinely assessment for bias in ML/AI algorithms.

Others have noted a concern that clinician jobs will be lost to automation by ML/AI.34,35 We did not find that concern expressed in our cohort. On the contrary, participants hoped that ML/AI would free up clinicians’ time to allow them to interact more with patients. It is possible that ML/AI would improve humanism in medicine by allowing both clinicians and machines to play to their respective strengths.36 We analyzed patients and clinicians qualitative data together. Our findings highlighted that clinicians and patients’ perceptions on ML/AI were similar. However, future studies should use quantitative methods to compare clinicians and patients’ perceptions on the use of ML/AI in healthcare.

Quantitative sentiment analysis was neutral to supportive of ML/AI, and corroborated the qualitative analysis. The most negative sentiments were associated with potential disagreements between ML/AI algorithms and clinicians, and liability. In future work, automated analysis of interviews using large language models may be an additional resource.37

There are limitations to these results. We selected patients and caregivers who previously agreed to participate in a registry. Their familiarity with medical research may have made participants more amenable to ML/AI. Patient participants were drawn from a predominately urban geographical area. ML/AI is likely to have different impacts for people living in rural settings because in-person expertise is less available and referral centers are further away. Smaller healthcare centers are unlikely to have the data and expertise needed to develop ML/AI algorithms specific for their patient population.

Clinicians held positions around the country, and were generally familiar with ML/AI already. Our sample size is small. However, others have showed data saturations with similar or smaller samples, and we achieved thematic saturation.38 We shared the qualitative results with clinicians who did not participate, and they corroborated our findings. Quantitative results buttressed the qualitative results, adding to the consistence of the underlying themes.

Although we used the standard Theoretical Framework of Acceptability, there was substantial overlap between themes. Positive, negative and affective attitudes were consistent. Accountability, liability, and government regulation are necessarily related. In particular, FDA regulation of ML/AI is a rapidly moving target as the field advances. Future research might be more highly focused.

We included one proxy because patients with neurological conditions often have someone to advocate on their behalf; however, nearly all the responses were from patients. Thus, our results may not be immediately transferable to proxies. This applies most in situations where patients are unable to articulate their own wishes (eg, severe trauma).

In sum, patients and clinicians expressed mixed but generally positive views about ML/AI. Potential benefits were related to improved decision-making and concerns focused on bias, liability, and the need for further education. Future questions include how best to incorporate ML/AI into education and obviate potential burdens as ML/AI is integrated into clinical care.
