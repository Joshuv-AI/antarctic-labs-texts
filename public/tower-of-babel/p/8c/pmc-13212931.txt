A new soft-switched trans-inverse quasi-Z source DC-DC converter with low switch voltage stress
Authors: Sara Hasanpour, Tohid Nouri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper proposes a new ultra-high-voltage gain, soft-switching quasi-Z-source DC-DC converter for distributed generation system converters. This converter retains the benefits of the quasi-Z-source network and the switched-capacitor techniques, resulting in high voltage gain, high efficiency, low input current ripple, low switch voltage stress, and a common ground between the input and output sides. The presented circuit uniquely combines a Three-Winding Coupled-inductor (TWCI) with a voltage multiplier to achieve exceptional voltage conversion in a trans-inverse manner. This approach enables a higher voltage gain with a small total turns ratio in the TWCI. Notably, the single power switch attains zero-current switching (ZCS) at the turn-on instant across its entire operating range. Moreover, the efficiency of the introduced topology is further enhanced by recycling the leakage inductance energy of the TWCI through a clamp circuit across the power switch. In addition, all the converter diodes are operated without a reverse recovery issue. A comprehensive theoretical analysis, including operational principles and steady-state performance, is complemented by comparative evaluations with similar topologies. The feasibility of the proposed converter was confirmed through steady-state analyses and the implementation of a 200 W, 25 V-to-400 V sample prototype.

Introduction

The non-isolated, high-voltage gain, soft-switching DC-DC converter is ideally suited for distributed generation systems that utilize low-voltage sources (20–50 V) and transition to standardized DC bus levels (380–400 V)1. Beyond their primary applications in photovoltaics and fuel cells, these converters are critical to a diverse range of fields. They enable 48 V-to-380 V power distribution in data centers, powering solar microinverters and UPS systems, and are essential components in a wide range of applications, including HID lighting, medical equipment, portable electronics, and robotic actuators1,2. Optimal converters for these demanding applications must deliver ultra-high voltage gain, high efficiency, simple structure, and a compact footprint, while simultaneously minimizing input current ripple and component voltage stress.

Despite their structural simplicity, traditional step-up DC-DC structures such as the boost and SEPIC converters are inadequate for high-gain applications in renewable energy systems (RES). Their operational requirement for extreme duty cycles to achieve high voltage conversion induces voltage stress across components and degrades efficiency through substantial power loss. To overcome these limitations, the field has seen the development of several effective voltage boosting methods, including switched-capacitor (SC) and switched-inductor (SI) networks, voltage multiplier (VM) cells, active-network, stacked-boost, as well as interleaved and cascaded methods2–5. While these techniques achieve higher voltage gains, many require numerous voltage multiplier (VM) cells, which substantially increase the converter’s complexity, size and cost. The trade-off between component count and performance is inherently nonlinear; adding components typically improves one performance metric while degrading another. For instance, increasing the number of power switches to achieve soft-switching and higher efficiency introduces trade-offs in the form of increased cost, gate drive complexity, dead-time management challenges, and PCB area requirements. Similarly, while the addition of capacitors can enhance voltage gain and reduce output voltage ripple, it simultaneously compromises reliability, particularly when electrolytic capacitors are used, and exacerbates ESR-induced losses and inrush current. Furthermore, although magnetic components such as inductors improve power density and reduce current ripple, their excessive use adds weight and volume, increases core losses, and raises manufacturing costs. Hybrid approaches have recently emerged as promising alternatives, integrating magnetic components such as the Coupled-Inductor (CI) technique with conventional boosting methods6,7. These magnetic-based topologies offer distinct benefits, such as voltage gain adjustability through turns ratio selection, a reduced component count compared to VM-based methods, and enhanced design flexibility. Despite these benefits, CI implementations face some challenges. The leakage inductance in these topologies causes damaging voltage spikes across the power switches, requiring protective clamp circuits7.

By integrating magnetic devices (two-winding/three-winding) with innovative voltage-boosting methods, recent high step-up DC-DC structures demonstrate significantly improved performance characteristics8. However, recent developments in quadratic converters have significantly enhanced voltage gain by integrating dual boost stages9. Coupled-inductor (CI) based quadratic converters are particularly promising, achieving ultra-high voltage gain with low duty cycles and moderate turns ratios6,8. In10,11, two different types of quadratic converters are suggested. Although these converters are capable of providing high voltage gains, their disadvantages include significant losses in the input diodes due to their high average currents and the use of two active switches.

The coupled-inductor (CI) technique, while effective for enhancing voltage gain, introduces significant challenges in high-gain converters. Specifically, achieving a higher voltage gain requires an increased turns ratio, which exacerbates leakage inductance and winding equivalent series resistance (ESR). This, in turn, results in greater power loss and elevated electromagnetic interference (EMI). Addressing these challenges has led to the development of advanced magnetic-based step-up DC-DC converters that utilize trans-inverse and semi-trans-inverse configurations12. These advanced topologies achieve ultra-high voltage gain at remarkably low turns ratios (n < 1), a significant departure from conventional designs. This approach offers key advantages, including reduced winding requirements, improved magnetic efficiency, and minimized parasitic effects. While semi-trans-inverse topologies see a sharp increase in voltage gain as the turns ratio approaches unity (n→1)12, integrated magnetic trans-inverse configurations demonstrate a more dramatic, exponential increase as the turns ratio approaches zero (n→0)13–16. By enabling ultra-high voltage gain with far fewer winding turns than semi-trans-inverse designs, the trans-inverse topology is particularly suited for high-performance applications.

While the Z-source converter is a prominent step-up topology, its pulsating input current and lack of a common ground with the load limit its applicability1. The quasi-Z-source (qZs) converter was developed to overcome these drawbacks, achieving a continuous input current via an LC filter and providing a common ground between the source and load17–19. So far, various qZs structures have been introduced. The integration of magnetic devices, like coupled inductors with advanced voltage boosting techniques in qZs converters, has yielded significant performance gains, including higher efficiency and power density. In20,21, two new three-winding coupled inductor qZs high step-up DC–DC converters are suggested. However, due to the series connection of the coupled inductor and the input source, these converters suffer from high input current ripple. In22,23, new high step-up, common-grounded qZS DC-DC converters utilizing a coupled inductor are presented. However, the topologies’ main drawbacks are their hard-switching operation and the requirement for two power switches. Also, two new fully soft-switched high step-up qZs converters with low switch voltage stress and low input current are proposed in24,25. However, in these structures, an additional switch and a diode are used to achieve soft switching. In26,27, new double-switch qZs circuits with soft-switching ZVS and low input current ripple is introduced. In28, a new CI-based structure of impedance converters with high voltage gain using a large number of elements is presented. Furthermore, a new single-switch qZs converter based on CI and SC with low input current is suggested in29. However, high switch voltage stress is the main disadvantage of this topology.

Semi-trans-inverse qZS structures have recently emerged as a promising approach for enhancing the performance of impedance networks. In30,31, two double-switch structures of fully soft-switched high voltage gain Y-source qZs converters with continuous input current and low switch voltage tension are proposed. However, these converters have a quasi-inverted transformer characteristic. In addition, in32, a novel single-switch hybrid Y-Source qZs circuit with low component count and a semi-trans-inverse manner is suggested.

This paper introduces a novel qZs DC-DC converter that synthesizes a single-switch, trans-inverse architecture with a TWCI to achieve ultra-high voltage gain and low switch tension. The proposed topology provides the following key advantages:

Topology derivation

The architecture of the proposed step-up qZs DC-DC converter is illustrated in Fig. 1. The core design integrates a single power switch (S) and an input inductor (Lin) with a Three-Winding Coupled Inductor (TWCI). The TWCI is characterized by the turn ratios n21= N2/N1and n31= N3/N1. Furthermore, the circuit incorporates the Voltage Multiplier (VM) cell to achieve a high voltage gain. To maintain the clamp loop of the power switch S in the quasi-Z source network, one end of the capacitor C2 is connected to D1. This converter ensures that the power switch turns on with soft-switching ZCS conditions and that all diodes operate without reverse recovery issues. The resonant cell, formed by the inherent leakage inductance of the TWCI, generates sinusoidal current waveforms that help to reduce switching losses. This manifests as alleviated turn-off losses for the main power switch and mitigated reverse recovery issues in diode D3. The following assumptions are adopted to facilitate the circuit analysis:

Mode 1 [t0 - t1] (Fig. 3a): In this state, the power switch (S) begins to conduct, Do remains in the ON state, and the other diodes are reverse blocked. Also, the capacitor C4 and magnetizing inductor of the CI are energized by the C3. This main mode also features a resonant tank, formed by the leakage inductance of the CI and C1, which reduces switching loss by decreasing the switch current value at turning off time. Comparison study

A comprehensive performance comparison between the proposed converter and recent topologies is summarized in Table 2. This table illustrates that the primary contribution of this manuscript is the introduction of a trans-inverse structure for quasi-Z-source (qZs) converters. The analysis assumes turns ratios of n21 = 0.4 and n31 = 0.8 (n = n21+ n31 = 1.2) for all designs. To ensure an equitable comparison, the converter in10 was evaluated with n21 = 0.8 and n31 = 0.4, maintaining an identical total turns ratio.

Voltage Gain: Fig. 9 compares the voltage gain of the converters versus duty cycle, revealing that the proposed converter and the converter in22 offer the highest gain. However, the converter in22 uses two more components than the proposed converter. Also, Fig. 10 demonstrates a key advantage of the proposed topology: its superior voltage gain per component count (M/N). With 12 components, the proposed topology achieves the highest M/N ratio over a significantly wider duty-cycle range than all other converters in the comparison. As shown in the comparison table, converters in9,11,22,27,30,31 have additional active switches, which increase the cost of the driver circuit. Also, converters in12,12,16,22,28 have a higher component count than the proposed converter. Increasing the number of elements increases the cost and volume, as well as reducing the power density of the converter, which results in a more limited application. Also, according to Fig. 10, the converters in27,32, which have fewer elements than the proposed converter, have a much lower voltage gain factor.

Switch and diode maximum voltage tensions: As shown in Figs. 11 and 12, the proposed converter exhibits the lowest normalized switch voltage stress and acceptable diode stress across all duty cycles. This advantage permits the use of low-voltage-rated switches and diodes, thereby minimizing switching power losses. Moreover, the key practical specifications of the converters in the comparison table, namely input/output voltages, voltage gain, output power, switching frequency, and measured efficiency are provided in Table 3. It is worth noting that the experimental results reported for different converters were obtained under varying operating conditions, including switching frequency, input/output voltage levels, gain, and the type of components used. Each of these parameters individually influences the overall efficiency. In particular, increasing the voltage gain or reducing the input voltage level leads to higher current stresses on the circuit elements. Despite operating under such demanding conditions, the proposed converter achieves an acceptable efficiency at high voltage gains, as demonstrated in the table. The estimated costs of the converters, based on a common operating point (200 W, 25 V/400 V, n21 = 0.4, n31 = 0.8, n = n21+n31 = 1.2, ΔILin=25%, ΔILM=50%, and 50 kHz), are summarized in Table 3. These cost estimates were obtained from the Mouser, DigiKey, and nz.rs-online websites. As shown in the table, the proposed converter and the converters presented in12,14–16,20 exhibit the lowest total cost. Furthermore, Table 3 also provides a comparison of power density, calculated based on the volume of the magnetic components and capacitors under the same operating conditions. The results indicate that the proposed topology achieves a favorable and acceptable power density.

Experimental verification

In order to validate the converter’s feasibility, a 200 W laboratory prototype was built and tested with a voltage gain of M = 16 (converting Vin=25 V to Vo=400 V). The component values are listed in Table 4. Due to the low voltage stress level of the power switch, a MOSFET with very low ON-state resistance (RDS) has been used. The key voltage and current waveforms were captured using a GDP-25 differential voltage probe (with selectable division ratios of 20X, 50X, and 200X) and a PA-667 high-frequency current probe (with sensitivities of 500 and 50 mV/A.

The experimental waveforms of the single power switch under nominal load, shown in Fig. 17a, confirm successful ZCS operation with a drain-source voltage stress of approximately Vds=50 V. This value is only 12% of the output DC voltage (400 V), a key factor enabling the use of low RDS(on) MOSFETs for higher efficiency. In addition, the waveforms validate the critical resonant mode operation. The ringing observed in the VDS waveform during turn-off is primarily attributable to the resonant tank formed by the MOSFET’s output capacitance (Coss) and the parasitic inductance present in the power loop, specifically within the drain to source leads and PCB traces. The experimental results in Figs. 17b and 18a–c, show that the proposed circuit achieves low reverse recovery (LRR) characteristics across all diodes. The experimental voltage stresses across diodes D1−3 and Do were measured at approximately VD1 =50 V, VD2 =180 V, and VD3 = VDo = 300 V, a value substantially below the output DC voltage and in strong agreement with theoretical expectations.

Furthermore, Fig. 18d details the current waveforms of the TWCI’s primary and secondary sides (iN1 and iN2). The performance of the prototype converter in continuous conduction mode (CCM) is demonstrated by the experimental waveforms in Fig. 19a, which include the input inductor current (iLin) and the output DC voltage. A key outcome of the full soft-switching operation is a clean output voltage, characterized by a significant reduction in switching noise. The dynamic performance of the suggested topology is validated by its response to a load step change from RL=800 Ω to RL=1600 Ω, as shown in Fig. 19b. It is worth noting that the load resistance is sequentially switched with the help of an additional switch at a very low frequency. The waveform confirms the system’s ability to maintain a stable Vo=400 V output DC voltage.

Conclusion

A novel quasi-Z-source step-up DC-DC converter is proposed, achieving ultra-high voltage gain with only a single active switch. Key features of the design include low input current ripple, low switch stress, and an inherent common ground. Furthermore, the topology minimizes the reverse recovery issue and reduces component count, leading to a cost-effective and efficient solution. A Three-Winding Coupled Inductor (TWCI) combined with voltage multiplier cells achieves a high voltage gain, providing three degrees of freedom for an efficient design. The converter’s trans-inverse characteristic enables high voltage gains with a low turns ratio and a low duty cycle, offering a significant advantage over existing counterparts. A regenerative passive clamp circuit recycles the leakage inductor energy, thereby reducing the maximum voltage stress on the single power switch. Furthermore, the switch achieves soft-switching operation. Additionally, the leakage inductor of the TWCI is utilized to form a resonant cell, which enhances the overall converter performance. The feasibility and effectiveness of the proposed topology are validated through a 200 W laboratory prototype converting 25 V to 400 V, achieving a high voltage gain of M = 16. At the nominal operating point, with a small duty cycle of D = 0.3 and a total turns ratio of n = n21 + n31 = 1.2 for the TWCI, the power switch is turned on under zero-current switching (ZCS) with quasi-resonant behavior. All diodes in the circuit exhibit very low reverse recovery losses, while the voltage stress across the power switch is approximately 12% of the output DC voltage. The converter achieves an efficiency of 94.9% at full load. These results confirm that the proposed converter is a well-suited and promising candidate for renewable energy applications.
