The longevity effects of reduced IGF-1 signaling depend on the stability of the mitochondrial genome
Authors: Sarah J. Shemtov, Eric McGann, Lucy Carrillo, Sangmin Lee, Herbert Anson, Eric Hwang, Claire S. Chung, Jaye L. Weinert, Maria-Eleni Anagnostou, Guan-Ju D. Lai, Bert M. Verheijen, Junxiang Wan, Ivetta Vorobyova, Monica Sanchez-Contreras, Cheryl A. Conover, Max A. Thorwald, Pinchas Cohen, Scott R. Kennedy, Jean-François Gout, Suraiya Haroon, Marc Vermulst
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Suppression of insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) signaling extends mammalian life span and protects against a range of age-related diseases. Unexpectedly, we found that reduced IGF-1 signaling fails to extend the life span of mitochondrial mutator mice. Most of the longevity pathways that are normally initiated by IGF-1 suppression were either blocked or blunted in the mutator mice. These observations suggest that the prolongevity effects of IGF-1 suppression critically depend on the integrity of the mitochondrial genome, revealing an unexpected hierarchy in the pathways that control mammalian aging. Together, these findings deepen our understanding of the interactions between the hallmarks of aging and underscore the need for interventions that preserve the integrity of the mitochondrial genome.

INTRODUCTION

Aging is a complex biological process that is characterized by multiple molecular and cellular hallmarks, including genomic instability, mitochondrial dysfunction, and deregulated nutrient sensing (1). While substantial progress has been made in our understanding of the individual hallmarks of human aging, their interactions remain poorly defined. In addition to obscuring our insight into the basic biology of aging, this gap in our knowledge complicates the development of effective antiaging interventions, as targeting one hallmark of aging may inadvertently exacerbate others.

One strategy to elucidate the relationships between the hallmarks of aging is to investigate how the disruption of one hallmark affects the trajectory of another. In doing so, it may be possible to assess whether these processes act independently, synergistically, or in opposition of each other as they shape human life span. In addition, this strategy may reveal if a hierarchy exists between aging pathways, which could lead to a more integrated and causally ordered model of the aging process. In this study, we apply this strategy to investigate the relationship between two critical drivers of the aging process, mitochondrial mutagenesis (2) and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) signaling (3).

A large body of evidence supports the idea that instability of the mitochondrial genome (i.e., changes in nucleotide sequence, copy number, and organization due to replication errors and DNA damage) leads to a progressive decline in mitochondrial function, which accelerates the natural aging process and contributes to a wide variety of age-related diseases, including sarcopenia, neurodegeneration, and heart failure (2). A similar body of work describes the role of IGF-1 signaling in the aging process. IGF-1 regulates the growth and metabolism of human tissues, and reduced IGF-1 signaling can not only extend mammalian life span but also confer resistance against various age-related diseases, including neurodegeneration, metabolic decline, and cardiovascular disease (3). However, how mitochondrial mutagenesis and IGF-1 signaling interact with each other to shape mammalian life span remains unclear.

It is likely that mitochondrial mutagenesis and IGF-1 signaling intersect at multiple decisive junctions during the aging process. At a molecular level, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) mutations can be a powerful source of oxidative stress (4), a form of cellular damage that is directly influenced by IGF-1 activity (5). At a systemic level, mitochondria serve as central hubs of metabolic activity and respond dynamically to changes in nutrient availability and growth signals (6), processes that are tightly regulated by IGF-1 signaling (7). Last, at a macroscopic level, mitochondrial mutations directly contribute to a variety of age-related diseases (2, 8, 9), many of which can be prevented or slowed down by reduced IGF-1 signaling (10–14).

Together, these observations suggest that mitochondrial mutagenesis and IGF-1 signaling do not operate in isolation but instead converge on overlapping biological pathways that shape the rate and quality of aging. If so, it may be possible to counteract the harmful effects of mtDNA mutations by suppressing IGF-1 signaling, which could improve antioxidant defenses (5), enhance mitochondrial turnover (15), and delay the onset of age-related diseases (16). In support of this idea, we previously found that reduced insulin/IGF-1 signaling can alleviate muscle dysfunction in a Caenorhabditis elegans model of mtDNA disease (17). Alternatively, mitochondrial mutagenesis and IGF-1 signaling may act through parallel but mechanistically distinct pathways, in which case interventions targeting IGF-1 signaling would fail to overcome the progeroid effects of mtDNA instability.

To distinguish between these possibilities, we reduced IGF-1 signaling in mice that carry an error-prone version of DNA polymerase γ [PolgD257A; (18)], the enzyme that replicates the mitochondrial genome. These mice accumulate mtDNA mutations at an accelerated pace (19, 20), which reduces their life span by 40% and leads to a wide variety of age-associated phenotypes, including sarcopenia, cardiomyopathy, anemia, and inflammation (18, 21). We crossed these mice into a background that is deficient for Pappalysin 1 (Pappa), a metalloproteinase that increases IGF-1 bioavailability by locally cleaving IGF-binding protein 4 [IGFBP4; (22)]. In the absence of PAPPA, IGFBP4 persists and sequesters IGF-1 away from receptors, thereby reducing IGF-1 signaling. When Pappa is deleted in WT mice, they exhibit a 35% increase in life span and a reduction in age-related pathologies (23–25).

Unexpectedly, we found that Pappa deletion failed to extend the life span of PolgD257A mice, suggesting that the integrity of the mitochondrial genome is required for life-span extension by reduced IGF-1 signaling. Moreover, many of the prolongevity programs normally initiated by Pappa deletion, including improved proteostasis, enhanced DNA repair, and better telomere maintenance failed to initiate in PolgD257A; Pappa−/− mice. These observations suggest that mtDNA mutations represent a major barrier to life span extension and that mitochondrial function is required for the successful activation of multiple longevity programs.

RESULTS

Life span extension by IGF-1 suppression depends on the integrity of the mitochondrial genome

To examine how IGF-1 signaling and mitochondrial mutagenesis interact to shape mammalian life span, we crossed PolgD257A mice into a Pappa−/− background and compared the life span of wild-type (WT) mice to PolgD257A mice, Pappa−/− mice, PolgD257A; Pappa−/− mice, Pappa+/− mice, and PolgD257A; Pappa+/− mice. As expected, we found that both young and old Pappa−/− mice display a marked reduction in both body size and weight as a result of reduced IGF-1 signaling, one of the primary determinants of body size in mammals (26) (Fig. 1, A to C). PolgD257A; Pappa−/− mice displayed an identical reduction in body size, suggesting that IGF-1 signaling is equally reduced in PolgD257A mice upon Pappa deletion (Fig. 1, A to C). When we examined the life span of these mice, we also observed the expected decrease in life span of PolgD257A mice, with all PolgD257A mice expiring before they reached 17 months of age (Fig. 1D), a time point when all WT mice were still alive. In contrast, the first Pappa−/− mouse did not expire until it reached 28 months of age, consistent with their extended life span (12). However, the loss of the Pappa gene failed to extend the life span of PolgD257A mice (Fig. 1D). Instead, PolgD257A; Pappa−/− mice seemed to exhibit enhanced frailty, characterized by extensive weight loss as they aged (fig. S1, A and B). The depletion of the Pappa gene, by deleting just one copy of Pappa, failed to improve the life span of PolgD257A mice as well (Fig. 1D), although this depletion was better tolerated, as indicated by their normalized body size and weight (Fig. 1, A and B). Because Pappa deletion reduces body size in PolgD257A mice but does not extend their life span, age-related mtDNA instability appears to uncouple the effects of IGF-1 signaling on development from its impact on longevity.

Deletion of Pappa rescues splenomegaly in PolgD257A mice

Next, we tested whether loss of Pappa could attenuate the age-related pathology of the mutator mice. Both male and female PolgD257A mice exhibit pronounced splenomegaly at 12 months of age (Fig. 2, A to C), and unexpectedly, this phenotype was rescued in both sexes by homozygous deletion of the Pappa gene, even after we corrected for the reduced size of Pappa−/− animals. A heterozygous deletion of Pappa also rescued spleen size in male PolgD257A mice, although not in female mice, suggesting that male PolgD257A mice are more sensitive to IGF-1 depletion than females. Thus, the depletion of Pappa can partially recapitulate the impact of a full deletion of the Pappa gene. These findings indicate that while Pappa deletion is insufficient to extend life span in PolgD257A mice, it is capable of rescuing distinct pathologies that are driven by mtDNA instability.

Depletion of Pappa partially rescues anemia in PolgD257A mice

The reduced spleen size of PolgD257A; Pappa−/− mice prompted us to investigate whether other phenotypes associated with the PolgD257A allele were attenuated as well. A second characteristic phenotype of PolgD257A mice is anemia (27), which is thought to arise from multiple factors, including splenomegaly, which is an accelerant for the destruction of red blood cells (RBCs), impaired hematopoietic stem cell function, and increased damage to circulating erythrocytes (28). We observed that old, but not young, PolgD257A mice displayed a marked reduction in RBCs and hemoglobin content (Fig. 2, D to G, and fig. S2). Excitingly, heterozygous deletion of the Pappa gene partially restored both the RBC levels and hemoglobin content in PolgD257A Pappa+/− mice (Fig. 2, D to G). In these experiments, as well as others (see below), we noticed that PolgD257A; Pappa+/− mice frequently outperformed PolgD257A; Pappa−/− mice, suggesting that a dose-dependent reduction in IGF-1 signaling is required for PolgD257A mice to achieve the greatest benefits.

Depletion of Pappa partially rescues the sex-specific, inflammatory profile of PolgD257A mice

Chronic inflammation is a well-established consequence of mitochondrial dysfunction that is frequently driven by increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production (29), the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (30), and activation of innate immune pathways, such as the Stimulator of Interferon Genes (STING) pathway (31, 32). In addition, inflammation is a major cause of anemia in patients with chronic diseases. Accordingly, we examined whether PolgD257A mice display elevated levels of inflammation as well and found that male PolgD257A mice exhibit significantly elevated serum levels of interleukin-2 (IL-2), IL-6, tumor necrosis factor–α (TNF-α), and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) cytokines, indicative of systemic inflammation and heightened splenic immune activity (Fig. 3). Heterozygous deletion of Pappa significantly reduced levels of all of these cytokines in males, attenuating their inflammatory phenotype. Notably, PolgD257A; Pappa+/− mice showed greater improvement than PolgD257A; Pappa−/− mice, reinforcing the importance of IGF-1 dosage in ameliorating mtDNA disease. In addition, we noted that female PolgD257A mice displayed a muted inflammatory profile compared to male PolgD257A mice, indicating a sex-specific inflammatory response to mitochondrial mutagenesis. This sexual dimorphism may be the result of the anti-inflammatory (33) and antioxidant activity (34) of estrogen or other sex-linked factors (35). Regardless, the limited inflammatory profile of female PolgD257A mice remained unchanged upon Pappa deletion (Fig. 3, B, D, F, and H), highlighting the heightened sensitivity of male PolgD257A mice to IGF-1 modulation.

Depletion of Pappa improves muscle function in male PolgD257A mice

The primary consequence of mitochondrial mutagenesis is impaired energy production, which is particularly detrimental to energy-demanding tissues such as skeletal muscle (36). As a result, aged (but not young; fig. S3) PolgD257A mice exhibit significant reductions in both grip strength and treadmill endurance compared to WT type controls (Fig. 4, A to D). These assays primarily assess type I (grip strength) and type II (endurance) muscle fiber performance, suggesting functional impairment across both fiber types. Consistent with our previous findings, we observed improved grip strength and endurance in PolgD257A; Pappa+/− males, but not females, and saw no improvement in PolgD257A; Pappa−/− mice (Fig. 4, A to D). Instead, PolgD257A; Pappa−/− mice tended to display reduced, rather than improved grip strength compared to PolgD257A mice, providing further evidence of their overall frailty.

Deletion and depletion of Pappa improve the sex-specific cardiac dysfunction of PolgD257A mice

Like skeletal muscle, cardiomyocytes rely heavily on continuous energy production, making them particularly vulnerable to mitochondrial dysfunction (37, 38). In PolgD257A mice, extensive mitochondrial mutagenesis leads to substantial cardiac hypertrophy and enlargement of the left ventricle, a phenotype that mirrors age-related cardiac pathology in humans. To determine whether Pappa deletion could mitigate these effects, we performed echocardiography on 12-month-old mice and found that male PolgD257A mice exhibited a significant increase in heart size (Fig. 4, E to I) and the internal diameter of the left ventricle in both diastole (LVIDd) and systole (LVIDs), indicating structural remodeling and reduced cardiac efficiency. Similar to the inflammatory profile of the mice, we did not observe overt cardiomyopathy in female PolgD257A mice, suggesting a sex-specific impact of mtDNA instability on cardiac health, consistent with previous reports (39). Because inflammation can drive cardiomyopathy (40), it is possible that the reduced inflammation in female PolgD257A mice is mechanistically linked to their cardiomyopathy. We found that both PolgD257A; Pappa+/− and PolgD257A; Pappa−/− mice showed significant improvement in ventricular dimensions compared to PolgD257A males (Fig. 4, F and H). These findings further confirm that the depletion of Pappa partially phenocopies a complete deletion. Of the 2950 genes that are significantly altered in the hearts of male Pappa−/− mice versus WT mice, 2309 move in the same direction in Pappa+/− mice, albeit in a dose-dependent manner (Spearman correlation of 0.46, P = 1.5 × 10−154; fig. S4). Together, these observations indicate that reduced IGF-1 signaling can partially restore cardiac function, even in the context of a high mtDNA mutation burden

The mutation frequency of mtDNA is not affected by deletion of the Pappa gene

To investigate the mechanism by which cardiac function is improved in male PolgD257A mice following Pappa deletion, we tested whether reduced IGF-1 signaling lowers the mitochondrial mutation burden using duplex sequencing (41). However, we found that single-base substitutions (Fig. 5A), deletions (Fig. 5B) and insertions (Fig. 5B) as well as the mutation spectrum (Fig. 5C), copy number (Fig. 5D and fig. S5), and mutation distribution across the mitochondrial genome (Fig. 5, E and F) were unchanged among PolgD257A, PolgD257A; Pappa+/−, and PolgD257A; Pappa−/− mice. Thus, the improvements in cardiac function are not due to reduced mtDNA mutagenesis or altered mtDNA copy number but must be mediated by processes that occur after mutation generation.

Deletion of Pappa slows clonal expansion of mtDNA mutations

While the mutation frequency remained unchanged upon Pappa deletion, the pathogenic consequences of these mutations do not manifest themselves until they clonally expand within individual cells. In both aging humans (36) and PolgD257A mice (19), these clonally expanded mutations disrupt cytochrome c oxidase (COX) activity, impair adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP) production, and promote cardiomyopathy. To determine whether Pappa deletion influences this expansion process, we stained heart sections from 3-month-old and 12-month-old mice for COX/succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) activity (Fig. 5G and fig. S6A), a technique that can identify cells in which mtDNA mutations have clonally expanded. While COX-negative cells were absent in young animals of all genotypes (fig. S6A) and in 12-month-old WT, Pappa+/−, and Pappa−/− hearts, PolgD257A mice exhibited a substantial accumulation of COX-negative cells, consistent with widespread clonal expansion of mtDNA mutations (Fig. 5G). This phenotype was partially rescued in both PolgD257A; Pappa+/− (P = 0.0046) and PolgD257A; Pappa−/− (P = 0.0002) mice (Fig. 5H). These findings suggest that although IGF-1 signaling does not affect the generation of mutations, it does affect the rate with which harmful mtDNA mutations clonally expand within cells, thereby preserving mitochondrial function and contributing to improved cardiac health in PolgD257A mice. Notably, this effect was not observed in rapidly dividing intestinal tissue, suggesting that this benefit may only extend to nondividing cells (fig. S6B). The parameters that determine the rate with which mutations reach homoplasmy in dividing and nondividing cells differ substantially, with cell division itself being the primary driver in dividing tissues (42, 43).

Transcriptomic profiling reveals molecular signatures of cardiomyopathy in male PolgD257A mice

To investigate why PolgD257A; Pappa−/− hearts display a reduced number of cells carrying homoplasmic mutations, and what the consequences might be, we analyzed the heart tissue from all genotypes by RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). First, we established a baseline, transcriptomic profile of PolgD257A hearts by comparing differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between PolgD257A and WT mice (fig. S7 and table S1). This analysis confirmed that the hearts of PolgD257A mice are under substantial metabolic and inflammatory stress. For example, when we analyzed the expression of canonical markers of heart failure and hypertrophy, we observed a 16-fold up-regulation of Myh7 (P < 0.0001) and a threefold up-regulation of Nppb (P < 0.0001), two of the most common markers for cardiac stress (44). To connect these markers to mitochondrial dysfunction, we then checked the expression of Gdf15 and Fgf21, two mitokines that are specifically released in response to mitochondrial stress and are well-established markers of heart failure (45). Consistent with a failing myocardium, we observed a threefold increase in Gdf15 expression (P = 0.0005) and a sevenfold increase in Fgf21 expression (P = 0.03). These transcriptional changes were accompanied by a significant down-regulation of genes that encode key components of the contractile apparatus, including Myl1 and Myl7. Key genes that are directly implicated in heart failure and disease in PolgD257A mice are highlighted in fig. S7 (A to D). Next, we sought to complement these findings with an unbiased analysis of pathways that are deregulated in PolgD257A hearts (table S1). These analyses paint a picture of cardiac failure driven by metabolic dysregulation, ROS, inflammation, hypertrophy, and apoptosis. For example, the expression profile of PolgD257A hearts is enriched in genes associated with metabolic processes related to glucose [Gene Ontology (GO):0005536], glycolysis (GO:0061621), and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (GO:0019677), as well as the cellular response to superoxide (GO:0071451) and glutathione availability (GO:0004602). At the inflammatory level, they display enhanced expression of chemokines (GO:0031640), Fc receptor signaling (GO:0038093), eosinophile chemotaxis (GO:0048245), and AIM2-mediated inflammation (GO:0140971), an inflammatory mechanism that is directly activated by cytosolic double-stranded DNA (including mtDNA that leaks out of dysfunctional mitochondria) and is known to drive innate immune activation (46). PolgD257A hearts also display enhanced expression of ribosomal genes (GO:0003735), consistent with an elevated translational program to support hypertrophic growth. Last, they show the up-regulation of apoptosis-related genes (GO:0048102), a finding supported by the increased presence of cell death in PolgD257A hearts (Fig. 5, I and J). Notably, this cell loss does not seem to be compensated for by enhanced cell division, as Ki67 levels remain unaltered (fig. S8). This GO analysis also points toward downstream responses to cardiac injury. For example, heart failure is often accompanied by deregulation of potassium channels that are critical to conduction as well as neuronal sprouting and growth to reinnervate denervated muscle cells. Genes that encode these channels (e.g., Kcnj2, Kcnj3, Kcnj4, Kcnj5, Kcnj14, and Kcnj15) were significantly deregulated (GO:0005242), while genes that positively regulate axon growth (e.g., Ngf and Lrp1) were up-regulated and canonical inhibitory genes (Ptprs, Epha7, and Rgma) were down-regulated in mutator hearts (GO:0048670; table S1).

Pappa deletion partially normalizes the global transcriptomic profile of male PolgD257A hearts

We then examined whether these gene expression changes were restored by Pappa deletion or depletion. First, we focused on global transcriptomic changes. PolgD257A hearts display a global trend of reduced gene expression [7678 down-regulated genes and 7058 up-regulated genes (P = 0.0015, Chi-squared test)], and found that this trend was reversed by deletion or depletion of Pappa. In PolgD257A; Pappa−/− hearts, 6739 genes were down-regulated and 7657 were up-regulated, and in PolgD257A; Pappa+/− hearts, 7101 genes were down-regulated and 7300 were up-regulated. When we focused on the genes that are most deregulated in PolgD257A animals (twofold or more), both partial and complete loss of Pappa strongly shifted the entire transcriptome of PolgD257A hearts to more normalized expression (Fig. 6, A and B). Of the 125 genes that were significantly down-regulated, more than twofold in PolgD257A hearts, the depletion and deletion of Pappa in PolgD257A animals shifted the expression level of these genes closer to WT levels by 315 and 320%, respectively (Fig. 6A). A similar trend was observed among up-regulated genes in PolgD257A mice, where the 145 genes that were up-regulated more than twofold were decreased ~337% with both the depletion and deletion of Pappa (Fig. 6B). In contrast, genes that were not dysregulated in PolgD257A mice (Padj > 0.05, with a fold change of <1.005) showed no significant change upon Pappa deletion (Fig. 6C).

Pappa deletion partially normalizes specific disease-related gene expression in male PolgD257A hearts

To determine whether loss of Pappa also normalizes genes specific to the cardiac dysfunction of the PolgD257A mice, we monitored the expression of genes that either mark or drive heart disease in PolgD257A mice (Padj < 0.05 or Padj < 0.1 and log2 fold change >1.5). This analysis showed that key genes, including Nppb, Myh7, Gdf15, Myl1, and Myl7 were all normalized upon Pappa deletion or depletion (figs. S7, A to D and S9, A to C). In addition, a variety of genes implicated in heart disease (e.g., Mstn, Ppp1r1b lncRNA, Sbk2 and Sbk3, Grp158, Fgf12, Aldh3b3, and Sln), inflammation (e.g., Cxcl13, Ccl2, Ccl6, and Ccl8), and those that encode potassium channels (Kcn genes) were normalized as well (fig. S9, A and B). Not all genes were corrected, as some genes were unaltered upon either Pappa deletion or depletion (e.g., Nudt14, Foxo6os, and mt-Tp; fig. S9, A and B). Next, we filtered the transcriptome for genes that change ≥8-fold in expression upon the deletion of either one or two copies of the Pappa gene to identify the strongest drivers of heart improvement. This analysis not only reidentified the contractile genes (Myl7, Myl4, and Sln) mentioned above but also identified genes that hint at widespread metabolic reprogramming (Fig. 6D). Although heart tissue primarily relies on fatty acid oxidation (47), it can switch to glucose utilization under stress (48). One gene that enables this metabolic flexibility is Pck1, the rate-limiting enzyme for gluconeogenesis, which we found significantly down-regulated in PolgD257A hearts, indicating metabolic rigidity. The deletion of Pappa strongly reactivates this gene, indicating enhanced gluconeogenesis and glucose mobilization, which is expected to improve the metabolic flexibility (Fig. 6D). Systemically, PolgD257A mice exhibit reduced expression of the adipokine adiponectin (Adipoq), consistent with global metabolic dysfunction. Pappa deletion reversed this trend, restoring both gene expression and protein levels (Fig. 6D and fig. S10). The restoration of Adipoq is particularly noteworthy, given its role in insulin sensitivity, mitochondrial biogenesis, and antioxidant defenses (49), factors that influence organismal resilience at multiple levels of scale. Further transcriptomic changes confirmed this shift toward improved lipid handling. For example, Pappa deletion also up-regulated Adipogenin [Adig, which promotes adipocyte differentiation (50)], Plin1 [which stabilizes lipid droplets and prevents lipotoxicity (51)], and Adrb3 [a regulator of lipid mobilization and adipocyte metabolism (52); Fig. 6D]. Notably, Adrb3 was corescued with Ucp1, suggesting improved thermogenesis and mitochondrial uncoupling and the activation of stress-protective metabolic programs (Fig. 6D). Many of these genes (including Ucp1, Plin1, Car3, Cfd, and CIDEC) were also up-regulated when Pappa was deleted in WT mice, demonstrating that Pappa deletion remodels the transcriptome of PolgD257A mice in a similar way (Fig. 6D and fig. S9, B and C). The overall improvement in heart function was also confirmed by an unbiased pathway enrichment analysis, which identified biological processes such as “cardiac pacemaker cell development” (GO:0060926), “positive regulation of peroxisome proliferator activated receptor signaling pathway” (GO:0035360), and “negative regulation of glycolytic process” (GO:0045820) among the functional categories with the largest fold enrichment upon Pappa deletion (table S2). Together, these changes suggest that Pappa deletion enables PolgD257A hearts to shift from metabolic exhaustion toward a more flexible, energy, and stress-resilient metabolic state.

The PolgD257A allele blocks antiaging programs triggered by Pappa deletion

Next, we analyzed the expression of genes that drive the extended life span of Pappa−/− mice and investigated whether these genes were robustly altered in PolgD257A; Pappa−/− mice as well (Fig. 6E). One mechanism to extend life span is reduced mechanistic target of rapamycin signaling (53). Consistent with their extended life span, we found that Pappa−/− mice show reduced mTor expression (P = 0.046), while short-lived PolgD257A mice exhibit increased expression (P = 0.01); however, mTor reduction was partially blocked when Pappa was deleted in PolgD257A mice, limiting its impact on longevity (Fig. 6E). Another hallmark of longevity is improved telomere maintenance (1). Consistent with their extended life span, Pappa−/− mice show up-regulation of Terf1 (P = 0.0004) and down-regulation of tankyrase (P = 0.036), two changes that shield telomeres from damage and recombination and prevent them from triggering cell cycle arrest (54). Neither gene changed expression when Pappa was deleted in a PolgD257A background (Fig. 6E). In addition, Pappa−/− mice exhibit overexpression of multiple DNA repair genes that are involved in double strand break repair and telomere maintenance, a key feature of longevity programs (55). However, these genes were not overexpressed in PolgD257A; Pappa−/− mice, demonstrating that the PolgD257A allele blunts or blocks this longevity intervention (Fig. 6E). Another feature of long-lived animals is their ability to maintain their epigenetic landscape, which is normally eroded by reduced expression of epigenetic regulators such as Hdac1. Accordingly, Pappa−/− mice overexpress multiple chromatin regulators, including Hdac1 (P = 0.005), Ezh2 (P = 0.0002), Chd1 (P = 0.03), Chd2 (P = 0.02), Chd3 (P = 0.0007), and Chd6 (P = 0.049), none of which were overexpressed in PolgD257A; Pappa−/− mice (Fig. 6E). As cells age, the structure of their nuclear envelope tends to deteriorate, a change that is frequently attributed to reduced expression of Lamin B (Lmnb1) (56, 57). Consistent with their increased longevity, Pappa−/− mice overexpress Lmnb1 (P = 0.002) together with Nemp1 (P = 0.006) and Nemp2 (P = 0.042), two structural proteins of the nuclear envelope; however, we found no overexpression of these genes in PolgD257A; Pappa−/− mice (Fig. 6E). As part of a shift from anabolic to catabolic metabolism, the deletion of Pappa decreases the expression of protein-coding genes from the mitochondrial genome, which may reduce mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS production (fig. S11A). This reduction seemed to be mediated by reduced expression of the mitochondrial RNA polymerase (Polrmt, P = 0.03) and potentially Lrpprc (P = 0.06), a protein that selectively protects mitochondrial mRNAs, but not transfer RNAs (tRNAs) (58, 59), as tRNAs were not systemically reduced in Pappa−/− mice (fig. S11A). This expression pattern was mirrored by nuclear DNA (nDNA)–encoded oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) genes (fig. S11B), where 70 of the 93 nuclear DNA-encoded OXPHOS genes were down-regulated upon Pappa deletion (P = 1.1 × 10−6, Chi-square test), consistent with long-standing observations that show that the expression of nDNA- and mtDNA-encoded OXPHOS genes are tightly coregulated (60). However, these changes were not observed in PolgD257A; Pappa−/− mice (fig. S11, A to C). Another hallmark of long-lived cells is improved proteostasis (1). Again, we found that although Pappa deletion significantly up-regulated numerous primary heat shock proteins (HSPs), these genes were not up-regulated in PolgD257A; Pappa−/− mice (fig. S12, A to C). We found that of the 32 primary HSPs we detected, 27 were down-regulated in PolgD257A mice, and none of these genes were rescued in PolgD257A; Pappa−/− mice (fig. S12A). We confirmed this finding for HSP70 at the protein level by immunofluorescence (fig. S12C). Notably, this pattern was not observed in the DnaJ family Hsp40 of cochaperones (fig. S12B), which, unlike the primary chaperones, are ATP independent (61). Thus, the loss of mtDNA integrity and reduced ATP production specifically inhibit the expression of ATP-dependent chaperones, contributing to proteostasis failure. Together, these results suggest that one reason why Pappa deletion does not extend life span in PolgD257A mice is that critical components of antiaging pathways, which are up-regulated in Pappa−/− mice, are not up-regulated or blunted in PolgD257A; Pappa−/− mice.

The PolgD257A allele modulates the IGF-1 pathway

To further investigate why Pappa deletion does not extend life span in the PolgD257 mice, we also examined components of the IGF-1 pathway itself. First, we monitored the expression of the Foxo genes, key effectors of IGF-1 signaling pathway in the context of aging. Foxo3, Foxo4, and Foxo6 were all suppressed in PolgD257A mice (P = 0.019, P = 0.023, and P = 0.024, respectively), while Foxo1 strongly trended toward suppression (P = 0.068). In each case, the deletion of Pappa rescued this expression pattern (Foxo3, P = 0.96; Foxo4, P = 0.08; Foxo6, P = 0.74; Foxo1, P = 0.96). However, Pappa deletion did not rescue PolgD257A-mediated silencing of Foxo6os (PolgD257A, P = 2.38 × 10−6; PolgD257A; Pappa−/−, P = 3.13 × 10−6), a long noncoding RNA that was recently shown to be a molecular scaffold that is key to heart function (fig. S13A) (62). Next, we examined the gene expression of various upstream components of the IGF-1 pathway. These analyses showed that the hearts of PolgD257A mice display increased levels of Igf1r (P = 0.038), Igf2r (P = 0.01), Igfbp3 (P = 5.02 × 10−6), Akt1 (P = 0.008), and Akt3 (P = 0.004; fig. S13A), all of which are critical to IGF-1 signaling. None of these genes were normalized by loss of Pappa, suggesting that they could contribute to the lack of life span extension by Pappa. In addition, PolgD257A hearts showed reduced expression of Igfbp5 (P = 0.016) and Igfbp6 (P = 0.016), which was corrected by Pappa deletion (fig. S13A). Because IGFBPs locally restrain IGF ligand availability, their normalized expression is expected to dampen growth-promoting signaling and thereby reduce hypertrophic drive observed in PolgD257A hearts (63, 64). Last, we measured total IGF-1 levels in the serum of the mice and found that, at 12 months of age, IGF-1 levels are significantly lower in PolgD257A mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 6F). IGF-1 levels were not corrected by the deletion of Pappa gene, suggesting that another mechanism by which the PolgD257A allele can curb life span extension by loss of Pappa.

Pappa deletion triggers context-dependent metabolic rewiring

Aging cells frequently exhibit not one but multiple hallmarks of the aging process. These combinations could lead to complex, context-dependent interactions that result in emergent gene expression states that neither hallmark produces alone. To identify these interactions between mitochondrial mutagenesis and IGF-1 signaling, we filtered the transcriptome for genes that remain unchanged upon the deletion of Pappa in WT mice (Pappa+/− mice) but were strongly up-regulated (>4-fold) in PolgD257A; Pappa+/− mice. Of the 13 genes that were identified by this method, 7 were directly involved in lipid metabolism, storage, or mobilization (Fig. 6G). These included Adipoq, Cidec and Plin1 (51, 65), and Apoc1, a lipid- and immune-regulatory apolipoprotein (66). Last, we identified Fasn, an enzyme that is central to fatty acid synthesis (67); Elovl6, which catalyzes the elongation of long-chain fatty acids (68); and Lrp2, a multifunctional endocytic receptor that internalizes lipoproteins, insulin, leptin, and amyloid-β (69). These results reveal a notable lipid-centric response to a partial deletion of Pappa that only emerges in the presence of mitochondrial dysfunction, underscoring how aging hallmarks can interact with each other to produce context-dependent transcriptional programs. Notably, some of these interactions also occur when both copies of Pappa are deleted, demonstrating that mitochondrial mutations can sensitize cells to modulation by Pappa deletion and offering a plausible explanation for why partial Pappa deletion can improve the phenotype of PolgD257A mice as well. A similar effect is seen when columns 4 and 5 of Fig. 6D are compared.

DISCUSSION

Aging is a multifactorial process, characterized by the simultaneous emergence of multiple, interconnected molecular and cellular hallmarks. In naturally aging organisms, these hallmarks do not arise independently but instead accumulate and interact within the same tissues and cells over time. As a result, there is a growing recognition that the hallmarks of aging are inextricably intertwined, and to fully understand the aging process, it will be essential to tease apart these complex interactions.

In this study, we investigated how mtDNA instability interacts with IGF-1 signaling to shape mammalian life span and health span. The suppression of IGF-1 signaling through Pappa deletion extends life span in WT mice by triggering well-known prolongevity programs, including enhanced stress resistance, DNA repair, proteostasis, and metabolic reprogramming. However, the same intervention fails to extend life span in PolgD257A mice, which accumulate mtDNA mutations at an accelerated pace. This observation echoes prior studies that showed that calorie restriction (70), exercise (71–74), or overexpression of a mitochondrially targeted catalase (75) did not extend the life span of PolgD257A mice either. Together, these studies support the idea that a hierarchy exists among the hallmarks of aging, where mtDNA integrity overrides the impact of longevity interventions, such as IGF-1 signaling, nutrient sensing, exercise, and oxidative stress to curb mammalian life span.

The benefits we observed were especially pronounced in male mice, which seemed to suffer more from mtDNA instability than female mice, possibly due to the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects of estrogen (76). Although Pappa deletion did not extend the life span of PolgD257A mice, it did attenuate their splenomegaly and cardiomyopathy. These findings echo those observed with a mitochondrially targeted catalase, which failed to extend life span as well but did improve the cardiomyopathy in PolgD257A mice (37, 75). Heterozygosity for Pappa conferred even greater tissue-specific improvements onto the PolgD257A mice, including reduced splenomegaly, anemia, skeletal muscle decline, and cardiomyopathy. In addition, heterozygosity rescued the inflammatory profile of the PolgD257A mice. In the future, it will be interesting to determine whether this rescue was mediated by a correction of the immune cell composition of the animals or a reduction in the inflammatory stimuli themselves. Regardless, this dose-dependent effect suggests that complete loss of Pappa may excessively suppress IGF-1 signaling in the context of high mitochondrial stress, where some degree of anabolic signaling remains necessary to maintain cellular homeostasis. Our transcriptomic analyses demonstrate that the health improvements in PolgD257A; Pappa+/− mice were mediated, at least in part, by context-dependent interactions between mtDNA instability and IGF-1 signaling. Specifically, we observed gene expression states in PolgD257A; Pappa+/− mice that were not observed in either genotype alone. Overall, these context-dependent effects underscore how important the interactions between the hallmarks of aging are: The outcome is not merely the sum of two interventions, but a new state shaped by their interplay.

This principle was illustrated by the failure of Pappa deletion to activate canonical longevity pathways in PolgD257A mice. In WT mice, Pappa deletion up-regulates a suite of antiaging pathways that improve DNA repair, telomere maintenance, proteostasis, and chromatin integrity. However, in the presence of mtDNA mutations, most of these pathways are either blocked or blunted. These observations suggest that mtDNA instability may activate an as-yet-unidentified molecular switch that prevents reduced IGF-1 signaling from engaging canonical antiaging programs. Identifying the nature of this block will be an important goal for future studies. Most likely, it involves not one but a variety of mechanisms, including energy deficiency. For example, we found that reduced IGF-1 signaling results in the induction of molecular chaperones that require energy to function. Notably, these ATP-consuming chaperones were not induced in mice with mtDNA instability. In addition, we found that mtDNA instability can affect IGF-1 signaling itself. At old age, PolgD257A mice exhibit reduced levels of circulating IGF-1, thereby undercutting the impact of Pappa deletion on IGF-1 signaling. Previously, a similar observation was made in mice with nDNA instability (77–80), suggesting that reduced IGF-1 signaling is a common consequence of DNA instability. Because mtDNA instability is capable of inducing nDNA instability (81, 82), the reduction we observed could arise from this secondary nuclear disruption, which would unify both phenotypes under a single mechanistic framework. It would be interesting to examine this possibility in future experiments. Regardless, both of these mechanisms (energy deficiency and reduced IGF-1 levels) are likely to contribute to the lack of life-span extension upon Pappa deletion.

This insight has major implications for the development of antiaging therapies. First, they suggest that mtDNA integrity is not simply one of the many hallmarks of aging, but rather the foundation upon which others are built. And when that platform is broken, downstream hallmarks such as proteostasis or DNA repair cannot be engaged by typical means. Second, it suggests that interventions that target nutrient-sensing pathways may fail, or even backfire, when applied to organisms or tissues with high levels of mitochondrial damage. Hence, the next generation of geroprotective treatments must be tested in diverse models of aging, including those that combine multiple hallmarks, to better understand the scope and boundaries of their efficacy. Last, the efficacy of those treatments could be amplified by measures that improve the stability of the mitochondrial genome. While a reduction in IGF-1 signaling did not alter the frequency of mutations in WT or PolgD257A mice, it did slow the pace with which they reached homoplasmy. Thus, although it may not be possible today to reduce mitochondrial mutagenesis in human cells, our data show that it may already be possible to curtail the impact of mtDNA mutations on mammalian health span by slowing their clonal expansion in nondividing cells, the cells that are most sensitive to metabolic dysfunction.

While the precise mechanism by which Pappa influences clonal expansion of mtDNA mutations remains uncertain, several plausible explanations can be proposed. In the absence of cell division (the major driver for homoplasmy in dividing cells), the progression of mtDNA mutations toward homoplasmy is primarily driven by random genetic drift, the rate of mtDNA replication, and mitochondrial quality control. Thus, it is likely that loss of Pappa influences one of these three processes. Loss of Pappa may either reduce the rate of random genetic drift (potentially by changing mitochondrial fusion and fission or the spatial segregation of semi-isolated pockets of mtDNA), reduce the rate of mtDNA replication (less replication lowers the chance that a mutant mtDNA molecule expands enough to reach homoplasmy), or improve mitochondrial quality control by degrading mitochondria with mutant mtDNA molecules. It will be important to distinguish between these possibilities in future work to clear the way for novel interventions aimed at curbing the impact of mtDNA mutations on human health.

Regardless of the mechanism, these findings provide a compelling example of how the interplay between distinct hallmarks of the aging process can fundamentally alter the outcome of otherwise beneficial interventions. They reveal that the efficacy of antiaging strategies such as IGF-1 suppression is not absolute but context dependent. They are contingent on the integrity of underlying systems, including proteostasis and DNA repair. Without an intact mitochondrial genome, these pathways cannot be engaged, indicating that mtDNA integrity is required for these critical antiaging pathways. More broadly, our results underscore the need for a more integrated model of aging, one that considers not only individual pathways but also their interactions, hierarchies, and points of failure. By mapping these interactions, we can better anticipate the limitations of existing interventions and design next-generation therapies that are robust to the complex biology of aged tissues. In this light, strategies that target the expansion of mtDNA mutations, rather than their origin, may offer a powerful new axis for preserving tissue function and extending health span, even when the underlying genomic damage cannot be undone.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Transgenic mice

PolgD257A mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (strain #:017341), while Pappa−/− mice were a gift from C. Conover (12). Male PolgD257A/+; Pappa+/− and female PolgD257A/+; Pappa+/− were bred to generate mice the genotypes described in this study. To limit mtDNA mutations from being inherited through the germ line, separate PolgD257A/+; Pappa+/− × WT C57Bl6/j crosses were set up to generate “first-generation” PolgD257A/+; Pappa+/− females to be used for breeding. At 21 days, mice were weaned and ear-punched, and two polymerase chain reaction (PCR) reactions were run to genotype each mouse. The first reaction genotyped the PolgD257A allele using primers forward: 5′-GCCTCGCTTTCTCCGTGACT-3′ and reverse: 5′-GGATGTGGCCCAGGCTGTAACTCA-3′. To genotype the Pappa allele, primers Forwardcommon: 5′-TAAGCAGGGGTGGGTCCTTT-3′, Forwardneo: 5′-TCGCCTTCTATCGCCTTCTTG-3′, and Reverse: 5′-CACTCCTCAGCTTCGGCTTTCA-3′ were used. PCR reactions were run on agarose gels by gel electrophoresis and imaged before analysis. Mice were assayed at 3 or 12 months of age for experimental studies, while a subset of mice were aged for life-span experiments and weekly body weight measurements. All animal research was approved by University of Southern California’s (USC’s) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee under protocol #20929. Animals were scored by our laboratory and USC’s Department of Animal Resources veterinarians to establish humane end points. Mice were euthanized by CO2 exposure.

Grip strength

Grip strength was assessed using a horizontally mounted bar attached to a sensor (TSE-Systems, 303500-M/E1) designed to measure forelimb strength. Each mouse was allowed to securely grasp the bar before being swiftly and steadily pulled backward. Measurements were recorded only if the mouse let go with both forelimbs at the same time. Ten measurements were taken per animal and averaged to obtain a final value.

Endurance

Mice were acclimated to the treadmill instrument (TSE 303401-M-04/C) 24 hours before testing. During acclimation, animals were placed on the stationary treadmill for 5 min, followed by an increase in speed to 2 m/min for five additional minutes. Mice were then tested once each day on the 2 days following acclimation. The treadmill protocol consisted of 1 m/min for 1 min, followed by a 1 m/min increase every minute until exhaustion. Time of exhaustion was recorded in seconds. Mice that refused to run would feel a mild shock at the back of the treadmill. The values recorded for each mice over both test days was averaged and reported.

Echocardiography

One week before dissection, mice were transported to the molecular imaging center at USC for echocardiography using the VisualSonics Vevo 3100, MX 550 transducer 22 to 55 MHz. Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane, and a depilatory cream (Nair) was used to remove fur before echocardiography. Mice were placed in the supine position onto the warmed platform to maintain optimal physiological conditions, and their limbs were taped onto the metal electrocardiogram leads. Heart rates were monitored and generally maintained at 400 to 500 beats per minute. Warmed echocardiography gel was placed on the shaved chest, and the heart was imaged with a 30-MHz transducer. By placing the transducer along the long axis of LV and directing it to the right side of the neck of the mouse, two-dimensional LV long axis can be obtained. The transducer was then rotated clockwise by 90°, and the LV short-axis view was obtained. Transmitral inflow Doppler spectra were recorded in an apical four-chamber view by placing the sample volume at the tip of the mitral valves. After the scans were concluded, the residual gel was removed, and the mouse was returned to the cage for recovery. Images were subsequently analyzed using the Vevo LAB software.

Tissue collection and analysis

Upon euthanasia, mice were measured and photographed, and the heart was punctured for blood draws for blood composition experiments and serum isolation. Tissues were dissected, placed on a grid sheet for measurements and photography, and cut into four pieces. One piece was stored in formalin, one piece was frozen in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, and two pieces were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for DNA and RNA extractions. Formalin-fixed tissue was left at room temperature overnight before being washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and stored at 4°C in 70% EtOH. Serum was collected by letting blood clot for 30 min, followed by a 10-min spin at 1000g at 4°C. Serum was then collected by aspirating the supernatant and stored at −80°C. IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-6, TNF-α, and IGF-1 levels in serum were measured with commercial immunoassays using a custom V-PLEX Mouse Biomarker Kit (Meso Scale Discovery). Blood composition was measured using 40 μl of blood collected in EDTA tubes using the Hemavet 950FS instrument.

Duplex sequencing

DNA was extracted from the hearts of experimental mice (n = 5 to 6 per group) using the QIAGEN DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (catalog no. 69506), quantified by Qubit 1x dsDNA HS Assay Kit, and stored at −20°C. A total of 500 ng of DNA per sample was fragmented using the Biorupter Pico (30-s on, 90-s off, six cycles), and the size was measured using the Agilent 4200 TapeStation instrument (fragment size of ~300 base pairs). Published protocols (41, 83) were followed with minor adjustments. Briefly, the DNA was end-repaired, and adapters (a gift from the S.R.K.’s laboratory) were ligated to DNA fragments using the Ultra II DNA Library Preparation Kit from New England Biolabs (NEB; catalog no. E7103). Samples were cleaned up, and libraries were quantified by quantitative PCR using SYBR Green iTaq Supermix (Bio-Rad, catalog no. 1725124) compared to previously generated libraries with target family sizes and on-target efficiencies. This quantification enables us to input the correct number of molecules into the preenrichment PCR depending on mtDNA content for each sample to sequence at the target parameters (depth, target raw reads, family size, and on-target reads). Libraries were then enriched for mtDNA sequences using the xGen Hybridization Capture Assay [Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT)] with a custom Discovery Pool of biotinylated probes (data S1), along with their protocol. Final libraries were quantified, pooled, and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 S2 kit (150PE). The standard duplex sequencing pipeline was used to analyze the sequencing data (https://github.com/Kennedy-Lab-UW/Duplex-Seq-Pipeline). Mutation data are attached as supplementary files (data S2).

mtDNA copy number measurements

DNA extracted for duplex-sequencing experiments was used for copy number determination. By quantitative PCR, relative mtDNA copy number was measured using 50 ng of input DNA and primers targeting mitochondrial ND1 (forward: 5′-GCCTGACCCATAGCCATAAT-3′ and reverse: 5′-TATTCTACGTTAAACCCTGA-3′) and nuclear RPP30 (forward: 5′-GCAACCGGAACATAGAGACA-3′ and reverse: 5′-CTGGCCTTGGAATGGGTAAT-3′). iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix was used in these reactions, and the protocol was followed accordingly (Bio-Rad, catalog no. 1725124). By droplet digital PCR (ddPCR), Bio-Rad ddPCR copy number assay primers/probe for Rpp30 (HEX, assay ID: dMmuCNS822293939) and for ND1 (FAM, assay ID: dMmuCNS343824284) were used with 2× ddPCR Supermix for probes (no deoxyuridine triphosphate) (Bio-Rad, catalog no. 186-3023).

RNA sequencing

Heart tissue from 12-month-old male mice (n = 6 per group) was dounced in TRIzol/chloroform and homogenized with with zirconia beads for 20 min at 4°C. Samples were then at 10,000g for 5 min, and the supernatant was purified according to the RiboPure Yeast RNA Purification Kit (Invitrogen, catalog no. AM1926). RNA was deoxyribonuclease-treated, and mRNA was enriched using the GenElute Direct mRNA miniprep kit (Sigma-Aldrich, catalog no. DMN70-1KT) and RNA-seq libraries were generated using the SMARTer Stranded RNA-Seq Kit (Takara Bio, catalog no. 634838) and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 SP PE150 kit. Sequencing reads were trimmed using fastp (84) (version 0.23.2) with the following parameters: “--trim_poly_g --poly_g_min_len 6 --length_required 120”. Trimmed reads were then used to obtain transcripts abundance with Kallisto quant (85) (version 0.46) against the Ensembl r96 annotation for Mus musculus. Abundance estimates from Kallisto were then analyzed in R with the package DESeq2 (86) following the standard pipeline, removing transcripts with sum of counts lower than 30. To identify genes that change in expression in the PolgD257A mice conditional on the Pappa genotype, we combined the two factors of interest (Polg genotype and Pappa genotype) and extracted the corresponding interaction from the DESeq2 results with: “results (dds, name = ‘pappa_genotypepappaMut.polg_genotypepolgMut’)”. The unbiased pathway enrichment was performed in R with the function “enrichGO” from the clusterProfiler package (version 3.0.4) using a P value cutoff of 0.05 for the gene selection, a minGSSize (minimal size of genes annotated by Ontology term for testing) of 10, and a maxGSSize of 2000. Correction for multiple testing by the enrichGO function was done with the Benjamini-Hochberg method. The list of significant functional categories was then pruned to remove redundant results with the “simplify” function from clusterProfiler using a similarity cutoff value of 0.5 and a selection on the maximum fold enrichment. Sequencing data were deposited to Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under accession PRJNA1271490.

COX-SDH staining

Frozen OCT blocks containing heart or intestine tissue were sectioned onto slides using a Leica CM 1860 Cryostat in 10-μm sections and stored at −80°C until they were ready to be stained. COX-SDH staining protocol was adapted from (36). Briefly, heart sections placed on a slide were circled using a PAP pen, fully covered with COX-staining solution, and incubated at 37°C for 12 min (30 min for intestine at room temperature). Slides were then rinsed in 0.1 M tris-HCl and incubated at 37°C with SDH-staining solution for 10 min (45 min for intestine at 37°C). Afterward, slides were dehydrated in 70% ethanol, 95% ethanol, 100% ethanol (2×), and xylene before being coverslipped with Cytoseal 60. Slides were imaged on an Echo Revolve microscope at ×4, ×10, and ×20 magnifications. Images were analyzed and scored for their % COX-negative cells using QuPath2 software.

Immunofluorescence staining

Frozen OCT blocks containing heart tissue were sectioned onto slides using a Leica CM 1860 Cryostat in 10-μm sections and stored at −80°C until they were ready to be stained. Slides were air-dried for 10 min. For caspase-3 (Proteintech, #66470) and adiponectin (Proteintech, #66239), slides were fixed and permeabilized with 100% acetone for 10 min at −20°C. For Ki67 (Abcam, #ab15580) and Hsp70 (Proteintech, #66183), slides were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, washed 3× with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.02% digitonin for 10 min at room temperature. All slides were then washed 3× with PBS. Sections were blocked using a blocking buffer (0.1% Tween 20, 2.5% bovine serum albumin, 5% normal goat serum in PBS) for 1 hour at room temperature. Sections were then incubated with primary antibody at 1:150 dilution in a humidified chamber overnight at 4°C. Slides were washed in PBS 3× for 5 min, and then sections were incubated with secondary antibodies [Goat anti-Rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, A-11034) and Goat anti-Mouse Alexa Fluor 658 (Invitrogen, A-11004)] at 1:800 dilution in the dark for 1 hour at room temperature. Slides were washed in PBS 3× for 5 min, mounted using VECTASHIELD Antifade Mounting Medium with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Vector Labs, H-1200-10), and coverslipped. Slides were imaged on the Leica Stellaris Confocal microscope at ×63 magnification. All images were analyzed using ImageJ. Caspase, Ki67, and Hsp70 images were quantified by positive cell count over total number of nuclei, while adiponectin images were quantified by positive fluorescent area.

Statistics

Group differences means (± SEM) were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Fisher’s Least Significant Difference (LSD) test, Kruskal-Wallis with uncorrected Dunn’s test for nonparametric distributions, or Welch’s ANOVA when variances were unequal across groups. Significance was defined as P < 0.05. Analyses used GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).
