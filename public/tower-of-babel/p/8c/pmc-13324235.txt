Bioengineered miR-148a-3p suppresses glycolysis and amino acid homeostasis in hepatocellular carcinoma cells by regulating multiple solute carrier transporters
Authors: Su Guan, Xin Li, Yimei Wang, Mei-Juan Tu, Ai-Ming Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells are metabolically reprogrammed for excessive uptake and metabolism of many nutrients. The tumor suppressive microRNA-148a-3p (miR-148a-3p) is downregulated in HCC, whereas its function in regulating HCC cell metabolism remains obscure. Herein we aimed to delineate the role of miR-148a-3p in HCC cell metabolism by using novel bioengineered miR-148a-3p (BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p) agent produced in vivo.MethodsBioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p was designed by using human leucyl transfer RNA fused hsa-pre-miR-34a carrier, overexpressed in Escherichia coli (E. coli), and purified to high homogeneity. After transfection into HCC cells, the released miR-148a-3p levels were assessed by reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). Cell proliferation was determined by CellTiter-Glo assays. Targets were validated by dual-luciferase reporter assays, immunoblotting, and immunofluorescence confocal imaging. Glycolysis capacity was evaluated by Seahorse XF assays, and glucose, lactate, and amino acid levels were quantified by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) methods.ResultsBioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p was efficiently processed into target miR-148a-3p in HCC cells to effectively inhibit cell proliferation in a dose- and time-dependent manner. Mechanistically, miR-148a-3p suppressed the protein levels of glucose transporter GLUT1/SLC2A1 and L-type amino acid transporter LAT1/SLC7A5 via acting on their 3′-untranslated regions, as well as amino acid transporter ASCT2/SLC1A5. These, in turn, led to a reduction of glucose uptake, lactate production, and glycolytic flux in HCC cells, and alteration of intracellular amino acid metabolome including glutamine, leucine, phenylalanine, tyrosine, and methionine.ConclusionsReintroduction of miR-148a-3p into HCC cells modulates glucose and amino acid metabolism via regulating multiple SLC transporters, thereby suppressing HCC cell viability. These findings highlight the role of miR-148a-3p in HCC cell metabolism and potential of bioengineered miRNA molecules for functional studies and therapeutic development.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), MicroRNA-148a-3p (miR-148a-3p), Cell metabolism, Glucose, Amino acids

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains high incidence and leads to high mortality rate, ranking the third leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide.1,2 Despite the advanced therapeutic methods including surgical resection and pharmacotherapies, such as multi-kinase inhibitors and immune checkpoint inhibitors, the survival or prognosis of advanced HCC patients remains poor.3, 4, 5 The limited efficacy of current treatments is largely attributed to tumoral heterogeneity as well as intrinsic or acquired resistance to therapeutics, both involving a profound metabolic reprogramming of the HCC cells which promotes tumor progression or resists existing therapies.6,7 Among these alterations, enhanced intracellular uptake and metabolism of crucial nutrients such as glucose and amino acids are prominent traits, which supply necessary substances and energy for dysregulated carcinoma cell survival and proliferation, making them attractive targets to improve HCC therapy.8, 9, 10

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small, noncoding RNAs derived from the genome to control posttranscriptional gene regulation via complementary binding with target mRNAs, leading to mRNA degradation or translation suppression.11 In HCC, aberrant expression of functional miRNAs is a common phenomenon, especially the reduction or even loss of onco-suppressive miRNAs.12,13 Therefore, reintroduction of such miRNAs has been shown to impede oncogenic signaling, including pathways controlling glucose and amino acid metabolism. However, delineation of miRNA functions and development of miRNA-based therapeutics has been hampered by inherited challenges in RNA stability, delivery efficiency, and potential off-target effects, limited to the use of miRNA mimics chemically synthesized in vitro.14,15 As such, our laboratory has successfully developed a platform to achieve high-yield and large-scale production of bioengineered or recombinant RNA (BioRNA) molecules, which uses human transfer RNA (tRNA) fused pre-miRNA carriers to assemble target miRNAs (BioRNA/miRNA).16, 17, 18 Unlike chemically synthesized miRNA mimics, BioRNA/miRNA agents are produced and tolerated in live cells in vivo. Upon transfection, BioRNA/miRNA molecules can be efficiently processed into target miRNAs in a variety of mammalian cells to exert their functions and possibly, therapeutic explorations.18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23

MiR-148a-3p, an onco-suppressive miRNA, has been consistently revealed to be downregulated in HCC and correlated with a poor prognosis.24, 25, 26 Functional studies have shown that miR-148a impairs cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis by targeting some specific oncogenes and signaling pathways. Recent work further demonstrated that miR-148a-3p can suppress glycolysis in HCC cells by targeting transmembrane protein 54 (TMEM54), highlighting its role in metabolic regulation.27 Yet, the precise downstream effectors that connect miR-148a-3p to metabolic reprogramming in HCC remain largely obscure, and whether it plays any role in amino acid metabolism has not been elucidated.28

In the present study, we first employed our BioRNA platform17,18,29 to produce a novel biologic miR-148a-3p molecule (Fig. 1A) and further utilized it to investigate the role of miR-148a-3p in HCC cell metabolism. Specifically, we established the actions of miR-148a-3p in the regulation of glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1/SLC2A1), L-type amino acid transporter 1 (LAT1/SLC7A5), and alanine-serine-cysteine transporter 2 (ASCT2/SLC1A5) in HCC cells towards modulating glycolysis and homeostasis of amino acids as well as cell viability. Our findings highlight a previously unrecognized mechanism by which onco-suppressive miR-148a-3p controls HCC cell metabolism and build up a foundation for therapeutic explorations of unparalleled BioRNA molecules produced in vivo.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and materials

DMEM medium (Cat# 11965092), EMEM medium (Cat# 10009-CV), 0.05% trypsin-EDTA (Cat# 25300-054), phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Cat# 10010023), fetal bovine serum (FBS; Cat# A56707-01), Opti-MEM (Cat# 31985070), radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Cat# 89901), bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Cat# 23227), and Lipofectamine 3000 (Cat# L3000001) were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Grand Island, NY, USA). Direct-zol RNA MiniPrep kit (Cat# R2072) was purchased from Zymo Research (Irvine, CA, USA), and primers were ordered from Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, IA, USA). Ethanol (Cat# E7023) and complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Cat# P8340) were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). TGX Stain-Free FastCast Acrylamide Kit (10%; Cat# 1610183), Clarity Western ECL substrates (Cat# 1705061), polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Cat# 1620184), and iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Cat# 1725124) were purchased from Bio-Rad (Hercules, CA, USA). The CellTiter-Glo 2.0 Cell Viability Assay kit (Cat# G9241) was obtained from Promega (Madison, WI, USA). Unless otherwise specified, additional chemicals, organic solvents, and materials were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific or Sigma-Aldrich. All reagents and consumables were free of DNase and RNase contamination.

Cell culture

Human Hep3B (HB-8064) and HEK293 (CRL-1573) cell lines were bought from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA), and Huh7 (JCRB0403) was purchased from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources (JCRB) Cell Bank (Osaka, Japan). Huh7 and HEK293 cells were propagated in DMEM, whereas Hep3B cells were maintained in EMEM. All media were supplemented with 10% FBS, and cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified environment containing 5% CO2. Cell numbers were determined using the Countess II FL automated cell counter (Life Technologies).

Expression and purification of recombinant RNA agent

The human leucyl transfer RNA fused hsa-pre-miR-34a carrier-based miR-148a-3p (BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p) (Fig. 1A) and the corresponding control RNA (BioRNALeu/control RNA) were generated through in vivo microbial fermentation production, as previously described.17,29,30 Briefly, following the heterologous overexpression, BioRNAs were purified through anion exchange chromatography using an ENrich Q 10 × 100 mm column (Bio-Rad; Cat# 7800003) on an NGC Quest 10 Plus fast protein liquid chromatography (FPLC) system (Bio-Rad). After validated by urea polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, FPLC fractions containing target BioRNAs were pooled, precipitated with ethanol, and subsequently desalted and concentrated by using Amicon Ultra centrifugal filters (2 mL, 30 kDa cutoff, EMD Millipore, Cat# UFC203024). Final BioRNA purity was determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) method on an LC-20 HPLC system (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan), and endotoxin content was measured with the Pyrogent-5000 kinetic LAL assay (Lonza; Cat# N383).

Confocal microscopy imaging studies

Huh7 cells and Hep3B cells were seeded at a density of 50,000 cells per well into 8-well chamber slides and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were then transfected with 20 nM (nmol/L) of BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p, control RNA, or Lipofectamine 3000 alone (vehicle), with three replicates per group (n = 3). Seventy-two hours after transfection, cells were incubated with medium containing membrane indicator wheat germ agglutinin (WGA; 5 μg/mL, Cat# 25559, AAT Bioquest, Pleasanton, USA) for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by staining procedures as previously described.20 Cells were subsequently fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and blocked using 3% bovine serum albumin in PBS. Primary antibody against GLUT1 (1:4000 dilution, Abcam, Cat# ab115730) for both cells, and antibody against LAT1 (1:100 dilution, Cat# 5347S, Cell Signaling Technology) for Huh7 cells were applied in blocking buffer and incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by secondary antibody incubation with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000 dilution, Cat# 4412S, Cell Signaling Technology). For nuclear visualization, cells were stained with DAPI with VECTASHIELD Antifade mounting medium (18 μL per well, Vector Laboratories, Inc., Newark, CA, USA, Cat# H-1200). Imaging was performed using a Leica Stellaris 5 confocal microscope integrated with a Leica Dmi8 inverted microscope and operated with the Leica Application Suite software (v4.4.0.24861), employing 10 × and 63 × objectives. The experiment was independently repeated twice, yielding consistent results.

Protein isolation and immunoblot analysis

Protein extraction and Western blotting were conducted as previously described17,19,20 with minor modifications. Briefly, Huh7 and Hep3B cells were plated in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and transfected with 20 nM of RNAs or vehicle alone, in triplicates (n = 3 per group). After 72 h, cells were collected, lysed, and clarified by centrifugation, and protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA protein assay kit. Proteins (20 μg per lane) were separated on 10% TGX Stain-Free SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against GLUT1 (1:4000 dilution, Abcam, Cat# ab32551), ASCT2 (1:1000 dilution, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 8057S), LAT1 (1:1000 dilution, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 5347S), or β-actin (1:3000 dilution, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# A5441). Following washes, membranes were exposed to HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, either anti-rabbit IgG (1:10,000 dilution, Jackson ImmunoResearch, Cat# 111-035-003) or anti-mouse IgG (1:3000 dilution, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 7076S). Signal detection was performed using Clarity Western ECL substrates, and chemiluminescent images were captured with a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System. Band intensities were quantified with Image Lab software (Bio-Rad) and normalized to corresponding β-actin levels. Each experiment was independently repeated at least twice with consistent outcomes.

Luciferase reporter assay

The human GLUT1/SLC2A1 3′-untranslated region (3′UTR) and LAT1 3′UTR (full length for both) containing the miR-148a-3p responsive elements (MRE) identified by TargetScan (http://www.targetscan.org/) were cloned into the dual-luciferase pEZX-MT06 vector (Cat# CmiT000001-MT06, GeneCopoeia, Rockville, MD, USA) downstream of the firefly luciferase gene as described recently.20 Sequence-verified plasmids were supplied by GeneCopoeia.

HEK293 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 104 cells per well, incubated overnight, and co-transfected with 50 ng of either GLUT1 or LAT1 3′UTR luciferase reporter plasmid together with 20 nM of BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p, control RNA, or vehicle only. Each treatment condition included five biological replicates (n = 5 per group). After 48 h, firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured by using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Cat# E1910) on a SpectraMax M3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices). Firefly signals were normalized to respective Renilla activities, and vehicle control group was set as 100%. All assays were independently repeated at least twice, yielding consistent results.

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis

HEK293, Huh7, and Hep3B cells were plated respectively in 6-well dishes at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and transfected with 20 nM of BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p, control RNA, or vehicle alone, with four biological replicates per group (n = 4). After 48 h of incubation, total RNA was extracted, and RT-qPCR was carried out as previously described19, 20, 21 with gene-specific primers on a CFX96 Touch real-time PCR machine (Bio-Rad). In particular, 5′-GTCGTATCCAGTGCAGGGTCCGAGGTATTCGCACTGGATACGACACAAAG-3′ was used as stem-loop RT primer, and 5′-GAGGCTCAGTGCACTACAGAA-3′ as forward and 5′-GTGCAGGGTCCGAGGT-3′ as reverse qPCR primers for the analysis of miR-148a-3p, while forward primer 5′-CTCGCTTCGGCAGCACA-3′ and reverse primer 5′-AACGCTTCACGAATTTGCGT-3′ were used for the analysis of U6 small RNA following RT with random primers. Relative expression of miR-148a-3p was calculated by using the 2−ΔΔCT formula where U6 served as internal reference. The experiment was repeated at least twice, which produced similar results.

Real-time live-cell analyses of glycolysis

Huh7 and Hep3B cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 5 × 105 cells per well and transfected with 20 nM of BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p, control RNA, or vehicle only, in quadruplicates (n = 4 per group). After 48 h, cells were replated into XFe24 microplates (Agilent, Part No. 100777-004) at a density of 8.0 × 104 cells/well for both cell lines. Following 24 h of culture, glycolytic activity was assessed by using the Seahorse XF Glycolytic Rate Assay Kit (Agilent, Cat# 103344-100) on a Seahorse XFe24 Flux Analyzer, according to manufacturer's instructions. Extracellular acidification was quantified as proton efflux rate (PER), and glycolysis-derived proton efflux (GlycoPER) was calculated to specifically reflect glycolytic activity, as recommended for Seahorse XF Glycolytic Rate Assays. Data were normalized to respective protein concentrations that were determined by BCA assay. Each assay was independently performed at least twice, and reproducible results were obtained.

Cell growth and viability assays

Cell viability was assessed using the CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega, Cat# G9241) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Huh7 and Hep3B cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5000 cells per well and incubated overnight before treatment. Cells were then exposed to increasing concentrations (1–30 nM) of BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p or control RNA for 72 h. Luminescence was measured using a SpectraMax iD5 microplate reader (Molecular Devices), and values were normalized to vehicle control groups. Dose-response inhibition curves were generated, and half-maximal effective concentrations (EC50) as well as Hill slope values were determined by nonlinear regression analysis (normalized response with variable slope; Y = 100/(1 + 10ˆ((LogEC50-X)∗HillSlope)) using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Each condition was assayed in four or six biological replicates (n = 4–6 per group), and experiments were independently repeated at least twice with reproducible results.

Quantification of glucose, lactic acid, and amino acids by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) methods

Glucose, lactate, and amino acids were quantified by using selective LC-MS/MS methods essentially as those reported previously.19,31, 32, 33 Huh7 and Hep3B cells were seeded in six-well plates and treated with 20 nM of BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p, control RNA, or vehicle for 72 h. Culture media were collected, and cells were harvested, washed, and lysed by freeze-thaw cycles, followed by normalization to protein concentrations that were determined by BCA assay. For glucose and lactate analyses, protein was precipitated with acetonitrile containing 0.4% formic acid and 5-fluorouracil (as an internal standard, 0.5 mM, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# F6627). For amino acid analysis, a [13C, 15N]-stable isotope-labeled cell-free amino acid mixture (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 767964) was used as the internal standard and prepared as a working solution by diluting 20,000-fold in acetonitrile/water/formic acid (v:v:v = 80:20:1). Supernatants were subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis on an AB Sciex 4000 QTRAP system (AB Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA) coupled to a Shimadzu Prominence HPLC system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) using an Intrada Amino Acid column (3 × 50 mm, 3 μm; Imtakt, Portland, OR, USA; Product No. WAA32). Multiple reaction monitoring in negative electrospray ionization mode was applied for compound detection, and data were processed with Analyst v1.6.2 (AB Sciex).

Statistical analysis

All results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons were performed using one- or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests or Student's t-test (GraphPad Prism, version 10, San Diego, CA, USA). The probability value of P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

Design, overexpression, and purification of recombinant miR-148a-3p agent

As depicted in Fig. 1A, the human leucyl tRNA fused hsa-pre-miR-34a carrier was used to assemble the recombinant miR-148a-3p molecule, namely BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p, where the miR-34a duplexes were replaced by miR-148a-3p and its passenger strand.17,29 Its 3D structure was generated by using AlphaFold 3 and visualized in PyMOL 3.1.3 as we reported most recently.17,34 Consistent with our reports,17,18 BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p was highly overexpressed in Escherichia coli (E. coli), accounting for >40% of total bacterial RNA (Fig. 1B). The BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p was subsequently purified by anion exchange FPLC method, with a representative UV trace and corresponding fractions shown in Fig. 1C. HPLC analysis confirmed high homogeneity (>98%) of the final product (Fig. 1D). In addition, endotoxin testing showed low contamination levels (<1.0 EU/μg RNA), indicating high-quality BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p molecules for the following functional studies.

BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p inhibited cell proliferation of human HCC cells

To determine whether BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p was efficiently processed into mature miR-148a-3p in HCC cells, we performed selective RT-qPCR assay with gene-specific primers. As shown in Fig. 2A, treatment with BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p markedly increased miR-148a-3p levels in both Huh7 and Hep3B cells compared with control RNA groups. Specifically, miR-148a-3p expression was elevated by ∼18-fold in Huh7 cells and ∼35-fold in Hep3B cells, confirming robust intracellular release and accumulation of payload miR-148a-3p from the BioRNA in human HCC cells. In contrast, control RNA did not alter miR-148a-3p levels in HCC cells as compared to vehicle treatment. Therefore, all subsequent analyses were performed by comparing BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p with control RNA to evaluate the impact of miR-148a-3p.

BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p is processed to target miR-148a-3p in human HCC cells to inhibit cell proliferation in a dose-dependent and time-dependent manner. (A) The levels of miR-148a-3p were remarkably higher in cells treated with BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p than those treated with control RNA or vehicle, as determined by selective stem-loop RT-qPCR assay. n = 4 per group. (B) Dose and (C) time dependent inhibition of cell viability by BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p. Estimated EC50 values were 9.46 ± 1.05 nM and 7.09 ± 1.04 nM in Huh7 and Hep3B cells, respectively. n = 4–6 per group. Values are mean ± standard deviation. ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, compared with corresponding control RNA treatment (one- or two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests or Student's t-test). To evaluate the antiproliferative activity of BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p, Huh7 and Hep3B cells were treated with increasing concentrations of BioRNA molecules. As shown in the dose-response curves (Fig. 2B), BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p markedly inhibited cell viability in a dose-dependent manner, whereas the control RNA exhibited only marginal effects. Consistently, the calculated EC50 values were significantly lower for BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p compared with the control RNA (9.46 ± 1.05 nM vs. 78.8 ± 1.3 nM in Huh7 cells, and 7.09 ± 1.04 nM vs. 113 ± 1 nM in Hep3B cells), highlighting the potency of miR-148a-3p in inhibiting HCC cell viability.

Time-course analyses further confirmed a strong inhibition of HCC cell viability by biologic miR-148a-3p (Fig. 2C). In both Huh7 and Hep3B cells, BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p treatment (20 nM) significantly reduced relative viability beginning at 24 h, with progressive decline through 72 h. By contrast, the control RNA modestly inhibited HCC cell viability over the same period when compared with vehicle control, whereas to much less extents when compared with BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p. Together, these data demonstrate that BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p is efficiently processed to payload miR-148a-3p in HCC cells to exert potent antiproliferative activities.

MiR-148a-3p controls posttranscriptional gene regulation of GLUT1 in HCC cells

To explore the role of miR-148a-3p in HCC cell metabolism, we first examined GLUT1/SLC2A1 as a direct target of miR-148a-3p as predicted by TargetScan (Fig. 3A) that was also evaluated in gastric and pancreatic cancer.35,36 Upon transfection, payload miR-148a-3p was released from BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p and accumulated to high levels in HEK293 cells (Fig. 3B), as determined by selective stem-loop RT-qPCR method. In parallel, a reporter assay was performed in the HEK293 cells, and the results showed that GLUT1 3′UTR-luciferase reporter activities were reduced by BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p for approximately 30% when compared with control RNA that did not alter basal activity level (Fig. 3B).

To determine the influence of miR-148a-3p on GLUT1 protein levels in HCC cells, we carried out Western blot analyses. The results (Fig. 3C) showed that BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p significantly reduced GLUT1 protein levels in both Huh7 and Hep3B cells after 72 h treatment, while control RNA had no or minimal effects. Densitometric quantification revealed that GLUT1 protein levels were decreased by miR-148a-3p for ∼75% in Huh7 cells and ∼30% in Hep3B cells, as compared with respective controls.

We further conducted immunofluorescence confocal imaging to visualize the modulation of GLUT1 protein expression and possibly subcellular location by miR-148a-3p. In both Huh7 and Hep3B cells, GLUT1 protein staining was mainly found on the plasma membrane, colocalized with the membrane marker WGA (Fig. 4A and B). Consistent with the immunoblotting results (Fig. 3C), BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p treatment markedly reduced GLUT1 fluorescence intensity in both Huh7 and Hep3B cells when compared with control RNA treatment (Fig. 4), indicating effective suppression of GLUT1 protein levels. Collectively, these results demonstrate that miR-148a-3p directly targets the GLUT1 3′UTR and suppresses GLUT1 protein levels in HCC cells.

Biologic miR-148a-3p modulates overall glycolytic capacity and glucose metabolism in HCC cells

Given the important role for GLUT1 in glucose uptake and metabolism, we thus performed the Seahorse Glycolytic Rate Assay to define to what degrees BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p would alter the real-time glycolytic flux in HCC cells. Proton efflux rate (PER) and glycolytic proton efflux rate (GlycoPER) were determined to evaluate glycolytic activity. The results showed that BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p markedly suppressed both overall glycolysis and GlycoPER in Huh7 and Hep3B cells, when compared with control RNA treatment (Fig. 5A). PER and GlycoPER measurements consistently showed that, compared with control RNA, biologic miR-148a-3p reduced basal glycolysis in Huh7 and Hep3B cells by approximately 55.6% (95% CI: 30.7%–80.5%) and 25.2% (95% CI: -3.1%–53.4%), respectively, and compensatory glycolysis by 58.5% (95% CI: 40.9%–76.1%) and 22.2% (95% CI: -3.4%–47.8%), respectively (Fig. 5A). These results indicate that recombinant miR-148a-3p modulates both basal and compensatory glycolysis in HCC cells in vitro, with a stronger impact in Huh7 cells.

To further delineate the impact of miR-148a-3p on glucose metabolism, we employed an accurate and selective LC-MS/MS method to directly quantitate intra- and extra-cellular levels of glucose and its end metabolite lactate (Fig. 5B). In Huh7 cells, compared with control RNA treatment, bioengineered miR-148a-3p markedly reduced intracellular glucose (21.7 vs. 29.9 nmol/mg protein, P < 0.001) and lactate (below the LLOQ vs. 0.31 nmol/mg protein), while concomitantly increasing extracellular glucose (14.4 vs. 13.1 mM, P < 0.05) and decreasing extracellular lactate (16.6 vs. 19.6 mM, P < 0.05). In Hep3B cells, BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p significantly decreased intracellular glucose (54.1 vs. 89.9 nmol/mg protein, P < 0.001), and intracellular lactate was essentially undetectable (below LLOQ) as compared to 5.72 nmol/mg protein in control RNA treatment group, indicating a profound suppression of lactate production from glucose. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that bioengineered miR-148a-3p attenuates glycolysis in HCC cells by limiting glucose uptake and reducing lactate production, consistent with the downregulation of GLUT1 protein levels.

Regulation of amino acid transporters LAT1 and ASCT2 by miR-148a-3p in HCC cells

Because aminolysis is intertwined with glycolysis, we next investigated whether miR-148a-3p regulates any important amino acid transporters such as LAT1/SLC7A5 and ASCT2/SLC1A5 (Fig. 6A). As the 3′UTR of ASCT2 has no MRE for miR-148a-3p, the LAT1 3′UTR does consist of one MRE for miR-148a-3p (Fig. 6B). Likewise, dual-luciferase reporter assays in HEK293 cells revealed that miR-148a-3p significantly reduced LAT1 3′UTR luciferase reporter activities as compared with control RNA (Fig. 6C). Immunoblot analyses demonstrated that BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p significantly reduced both LAT1 and ASCT2 protein expression in Huh7 cells, with densitometric quantification showing ∼52% and ∼46% decreases, respectively, when compared with respective control RNA treatments (Fig. 6D). In Hep3B cells, ASCT2 protein was also reduced ∼20% by miR-148a-3p, whereas LAT1 expression was not significantly altered (Fig. 6D). Consistent with these results, immunofluorescence confocal imaging study on Huh7 cells further showed that BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p treatment diminished LAT1 protein levels localized on plasma membranes (Fig. 6E). Together, these data identify the role for miR-148a-3p in the regulation of amino acid transporters LAT1 and ASCT2 in HCC cells.

Modulation of amino acid homeostasis by miR-148a-3p in HCC cells

To define the influence of miR-148a-3p on amino acid homeostasis, we examined the natural amino acid profiles within and outside of HCC cells following BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p treatment, by using a selective LC-MS/MS method. Because LAT1 and ASCT2 downregulation was more evident in Huh7 cells (Fig. 6D and E), Huh7 cells were chosen in this study. As shown in Fig. 7A, BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p treatment led to the reduction in intracellular levels of multiple amino acids, when compared with control RNA. Among them, many such as glutamine, leucine, phenylalanine, tyrosine, and methionine are established substrates of LAT1 or ASCT2 that regulate amino acid influx.37,38 Rather, extracellular amino acid pools remained largely unchanged between RNA treatments (Fig. 7B). These findings indicate that miR-148a-3p interferes with LAT1 and ASCT2 transporters to modulate intracellular amino acid metabolome in Huh7 cells.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that recombinant BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p heterologously overexpressed in E. coli and purified to high degree of homogeneity, is efficiently processed into payload miR-148a-3p in human HCC cells and exerts potent anti-proliferative activity by targeting cell metabolism and energetics. Specifically, miR-148a-3p represses glucose transporter GLUT1 and amino acid transporter LAT1 protein levels through direct targeting of their 3’UTRs, and downregulates transporter ASCT2, thereby disrupting glycolytic capacity and amino acid homeostasis. These findings provide new insights into understanding the molecular and cellular mechanisms by which miR-148a-3p acts as an onco-suppressor in HCC and support the utilities of BioRNA as an effective modality to restore target miRNA for functional studies and possibly, miRNA replacement therapeutic development. Indeed, our recent studies have demonstrated a high specificity of BioRNA/miRNAs in the control of target gene expression, as supported by omics-based studies followed by miRNA enrichment analyses.39,40 On the other hand, BioRNA/miRNAs exhibit greater or equal activities when compared to the same doses of chemically synthesized counterparts.17

Consistent with previous findings on the onco-suppressive function of miR-148a-3p, which is frequently downregulated in HCC and associated with poor prognosis,24, 25, 26,41 the present study demonstrates that restoring miR-148a-3p levels leads to a strong inhibition of HCC cell viability in vitro. Multiple studies have established the onco-suppressive role of miR-148a-3p in HCC through diverse biochemical mechanisms, including the regulation of epigenetic control via the small nucleolar RNA host gene 3 (SNHG3)/miR-148a-3p/DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) axis, modulation of tumor progression through integrin subunit alpha 5 (ITGA5)/PI3K/Akt signaling in the tumor microenvironment, involvement in immune-related pathways by targeting of cathepsin A (CTSA), and suppression of glycolysis via targeting of the transmembrane protein TMEM54.27,42, 43, 44 In addition, research on pancreatic cancer and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma has shown that miR-148a regulates GLUT1 expression.35,45 Collectively, these findings indicate that miR-148a-3p may act on various targets and thus influence multiple pathways in HCC. Different from previous findings that usually involve the use of miRNA mimics chemically synthesized in vitro, the present study identifies multiple SLC transporters (GLUT1, LAT1, and ASCT2) as targets for miR-148a-3p to coordinately modulate the transport and homeostasis of key nutrients (glucose and amino acids) in HCC cells, by utilizing a unique miR-148-3p agent produced by recombinant miRNA technology.17,18 These findings shall improve the mechanistic understanding of onco-suppressive actions of miR-148a-3p in HCC cells.

While the present study establishes GLUT1 as a direct target of miR-148a-3p in HCC cells, the observed changes in glucose uptake, overall lactate production, and apparent glycolytic activity are cumulative effects. It should be noted that hepatic glucose transport and metabolism is intrinsically complex. Normal hepatocytes predominantly express the high-capacity glucose transporter GLUT2 (SLC2A2), which enables bidirectional glucose transport in response to systemic glucose levels.46,47 In HCC, however, multiple studies have revealed a metabolic shift characterized by the upregulated GLUT1 along with downregulated GLUT2, particularly under hypoxic or oncogenic conditions, suggesting an important role for GLUT1 in sustaining basal glucose uptake and aerobic glycolysis.48,49 Nevertheless, the cumulative effects such as lower intracellular lactate levels and diminished glycolytic activity in HCC cells upon the reintroduction of miR-148a-3p may not be solely attributable to the suppression of GLUT1 to which this study is limited. One cannot exclude possible involvement of some other factors such as relevant transporters, metabolic enzymes, and their regulators,50 or other related targets and pathways aforementioned. Therefore, despite that quantifications of glucose and lactate levels, together with live-cell glycolytic rate analyses, support an overall attenuation of glycolysis by miR-148-3p, more extensive studies are warranted for the identification of possible and precise contribution of individual glucose transporters, such as GLUT1 versus GLUT2, as well as other downstream metabolic effectors.

Besides glucose metabolism, our study also uncovers a novel dimension of miR-148a-3p function in amino acid transport and homeostasis. SLC transporters such as LAT1 (SLC7A5) and ASCT2 (SLC1A5) are central mediators of the uptake and thus metabolism and homeostasis of many essential amino acids plus glutamine, driving mTORC1 activation and anabolic growth.51, 52, 53 Using unparalleled recombinant RNA agent, this study demonstrates that miR-148a-3p suppresses LAT1 protein levels in HCC cells via acting on its 3′UTR, while concomitantly reducing ASCT2 protein levels. It is notable that, although ASCT2 does not seem to be a direct target of miR-148a-3p as evaluated by computational analyses, miR-148a-3p could indirectly reduce ASCT2 protein levels through targeting its regulators. Indeed, we have found that sorting nexin 27 (SNX27), a trafficking adaptor shown to promote ASCT2 plasma membrane localization,54 is a direct target of miR-148a-3p predicted in silico (http://www.targetscan.org/) yet awaiting experimental validation. Nonetheless, the repression of amino acid transporters by miR-148a-3p limits the uptake of their substrates,37,38 as manifested by notable changes of intracellular amino acid metabolome, much lower levels of multiple amino acids, such as glutamine, leucine, phenylalanine, tyrosine, and methionine, in HCC cells. Given the critical role of leucine and glutamine in mTORC1 activation,55,56 the observed reduction in intracellular amino acids suggests that miR-148a-3p–mediated suppression of LAT1 and ASCT2 might have broader consequences on nutrient-sensing signaling pathways, including mTORC1, which warrants further investigations.

Our findings in this study underscore the multifaceted role for miR-148a-3p in modulating the transport and homeostasis of key nutrients glucose and amino acids in HCC cells. Glycolysis and amino acid metabolism are highly interconnected by supplying tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates to drive the synthesis of macromolecules and maintain energy homeostasis for cell survival and proliferation.57,58 By targeting both glucose and amino acid transport, BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p disrupts the two major nutrient metabolism pathways in HCC cells. The dual functions provide a mechanistic explanation for the observed potent anti-proliferative effect of miR-148a-3p. Moreover, these results demonstrate the success of using novel BioRNA molecules to introduce payload miRNA or other forms of small RNAs into mammalian cells. Compared with RNA mimics chemically or enzymatically synthesized in vitro, BioRNAs are produced and tolerated in live cells in vivo, making them a great addition to existing tools for functional studies and therapeutic development.18, 19, 20, 21,23,31

Some limitations should be recognized. First, the present study is limited to the use of gain-of-function approach with a novel recombinant miRNA molecule, given the facts that miR-148a-3p is downregulated in HCC cells and miR-148a-3p acts as an onco-suppressor. Rescue experiments or loss-of-function approaches along with relevant model systems would provide additional information that might strengthen the understanding of role of miR-148a-3p in HCC metabolism. Further, while this study clearly establishes the impact of miR-148a-3p on HCC cell metabolism and viability in vitro, the therapeutic potential of BioRNALeu/miR-148a-3p should be critically evaluated with relevant diseased animal models in vivo. More research is warranted to determine its stability, pharmacokinetics, antitumor efficacy, and safety in preclinical HCC models before extensive clinical investigations. Moreover, correlation analyses with clinical specimens will not only be invaluable to strengthen the understanding of the role of miR-148a-3p in the regulation of GLUT1 and LAT1 but also support the development of miR-148a-3p replacement therapeutics. Nevertheless, HCC is characterized by profound heterogeneity, and compensatory metabolic pathways may impede the efficacy and response rate of monotherapy acting on single target or pathway.59,60 Combination therapy using biologic miR-148a-3p along with existing therapeutics such as multikinase inhibitors or immune checkpoint inhibitors, would offer an enhanced therapeutic outcome.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study produced and used a novel recombinant miR-148a-3p agent to demonstrate potential roles for onco-suppressive miR-148a-3p in coordinative modulation of glycolysis and amino acid metabolism in HCC cells by regulating multiple SLC family transporters. These findings should not only advance our understanding of miRNA functions in nutrient uptake, metabolism, and homeostasis but also provide a rationale for the development of BioRNA-based miRNA replacement therapy through interference with cancer cell metabolism.
