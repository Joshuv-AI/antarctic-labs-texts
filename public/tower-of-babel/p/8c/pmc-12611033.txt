WARS2-Associated Neuropsychiatric Phenotype in Childhood: A Case-Based Review
Authors: Shabnam Kalita, Vishnupriya Veeraraghavan, Kaushik Ragunathan, Jaya Shankar Kaushik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mitochondrial WARS2-related disorders are known to exhibit a broad phenotypic spectrum, ranging from infantile-onset epilepsy syndromes to hyperkinetic movement disorders. We report a case of an 11-year-old male who presented with tremors, dystonic falls, social anxiety, and impulse control disorder (ICD), with onset at 6 years of age. Genetic analysis revealed a likely compound heterozygous mutation in the WARS2 gene, involving exon 4 (p.Thr154ProfsTer66) and exon 1 (p.Trp13Gly). He demonstrated a remarkable clinical response to levodopa. This case is notable for its rarity and to our knowledge, represents the first reported instance of a WARS2-related disorder from India.

Introduction

Mitochondrial aminoacyl-transfer ribonucleic acid (tRNA) synthetases comprise a diverse set of genes including the nuclear-encoded WARS2, which encodes tryptophanyl-tRNA synthetase, a key enzyme in the mitochondrial translation machinery.[1] Clinical features can be extremely diverse, and diagnosis is often delayed due to nonspecific symptoms and normal neuroimaging. This case highlights the need for suspecting mitochondrial disorders when neuropsychiatric features co-exist with movement abnormalities, even in the setting of normal neuroimaging, as it carries specific targeted therapeutic implications such as dopaminergic modulation.

Case Report

An 11-year-old boy from rural Assam, born to nonconsanguineous parents after an uneventful perinatal period and with normal early developmental milestones, presented with a history of postural tremors affecting both upper limbs from 6 years of age, which gradually worsened over the next 3 years. Simultaneously, he also developed brief paroxysmal movement episodes of sudden worsening of tremors in both upper limbs, truncal instability, and dystonic posturing of the trunk and limbs, often causing him to fall backward, usually triggered by emotional stress, sudden noise, movement, or occurring spontaneously. He had worsening dystonic posturing during daily activities, such as while holding a pen or writing. There was no seizure or cognitive decline. There were no dysmorphic features, neurocutaneous markers, or hypokinetic features. He had hypotonia with preserved reflexes, truncal and gait ataxia, and normal muscle strength.

His mother also reported psychiatric symptoms, including excessive fear, social anxiety, difficulty initiating conversations in social settings, and performance-related fears. The exacerbation of his tremors was evident in situations such as writing on the blackboard at school or crossing the road. Additionally, he exhibited emotional lability and frequent anger outbursts, eventually leading him to avoid school. The Screen for Child Anxiety and Related Disorders score was 43, and he was diagnosed with social anxiety disorder based on International Classification of Diseases-11 (ICD-11). Before reaching our center, he had been treated by multiple psychiatrists elsewhere under a diagnosis of dissociative/functional disorder.

In the setting of subacute onset of neuropsychiatric features at 5–6 years of age, Wilson disease was suspected, which was ruled out after a normal brain Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) and biochemical profile. Other possibilities, such as untreated autoimmune disorders, juvenile Huntington, creatine deficiency disorders, and neurotransmitter metabolism defects, were considered. He showed no response to symptomatic treatment with sodium valproate and trihexyphenidyl (doses up to 8 mg/day were tried). Whole exome sequencing (WES) revealed a likely compound heterozygous mutation in exon 4 (p.Thr154ProfsTer66, likely pathogenic variant) and exon 1 (p.Trp13Gly, reported as a variant of unknown significance) of the WARS2 gene. Functional studies could not be done to confirm the pathogenicity of the variants. There was no response to the mitochondrial cocktail. In line with literature evidence of secondary dopamine deficiency in mitochondrial tryptophanyl-tRNA synthetase defects and a few case reports supporting the use of dopa in WARS2-related mitochondrial dysfunction, the child was started on a levodopa trial (initiated at 1 mg/kg/day, titrated up to 5 mg/kg/day until response), along with sertraline, propranolol, and behavioral interventions. Over subsequent follow-ups, there was a notable decrease in the frequency of his episodes. His anxiety significantly reduced, and he resumed school attendance. At one point, an inadvertent 24-h cessation of levodopa by the parents led to a significant worsening of the symptoms, further supporting a dramatic response to dopaminergic modulation. After 6 months of therapy, they noted the emergence of sudden, unprovoked anger outbursts and impulsive thoughts about hitting others. These episodes were not repetitive but impulsive in nature. The patient experienced feelings of guilt and shame after acting out verbally or physically but struggled to control his behavior. Based on the ICD-11 classification, a diagnosis of impulse control disorder secondary to WARS2 mutation (ICD code 6E66) was established, and his sertraline dose was increased, to which he responded.

Discussion

To date, 19 mitochondrial aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases have been identified, with ongoing research expected to expand this number. To date, at least 34 cases of WARS2-related mitochondrial disorders have been reported.[2] A detailed summary of reported cases with WARS2-related disorder is tabulated in Table 1. Biallelic WARS2 pathogenic variants have been distinctly associated with at least two major phenotypes: the epilepsy phenotype (also called Neurodevelopmental Disorder, Mitochondrial, with Abnormal Movements and Lactic Acidosis, with or without Seizures), with onset in early infancy, presenting with refractory seizures and carrying a high risk of mortality. Movement disorder phenotypes are characterized by levodopa-responsive parkinsonism/dystonia, progressive myoclonus-ataxia, or hyperkinetic movement disorders. Symptoms typically appear in early childhood and progress into early adulthood.[3] It is usually associated with the variant c.37T > G (p.Trp13Gly), which partially mislocalizes WARS2 in cells and is typically hypomorphic—meaning an allele with reduced but not complete loss of function—and hence manifests only in the compound heterozygous state. The clinical severity and spectrum depend on the second variant.[4] The presence of tremors, dystonia, and psychiatric symptoms or cognitive decline, particularly in the setting of normal neuroimaging and an inconclusive metabolic work-up, warrants early work-up for an underlying genetic etiology. In such cases, WES is typically the first-line diagnostic approach. While awaiting results, detailed clinical phenotyping and systematic exclusion of acquired causes help refine the differential diagnosis. If WES is inconclusive, the next steps include mitochondrial DNA analysis, followed by whole genome sequencing in selected cases. For variants of uncertain significance, further interpretation requires functional correlation, parental segregation studies, and comparison with existing genomic databases. In the present case, a pathogenic mutation in the nuclear genome (WARS2) was identified, and therefore, further genetic investigations were not necessary. A breakthrough in the treatment of WARS2-related disorders came with the discovery of levodopa’s role and the impact of dopaminergic modulation in mitochondrial conditions. Most patients with the hypomorphic allele respond well to levodopa, with many exhibiting a honeymoon phase, similar to that observed in elderly patients with parkinsonism.

Conclusion

While WARS2-related disorders are chronic and currently not fully curable, levodopa responsiveness provides a vital therapeutic option, potentially extending survival into adulthood, even though its effectiveness may fluctuate over time.
