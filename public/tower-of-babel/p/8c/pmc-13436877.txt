Asystole during laryngoscopy with a videolaryngoscope in a patient with an anticipated difficult airway: A case report
Authors: Ryosuke Osawa, Naoi Tsurumachi, Yohei Tamura, Takero Arai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAsystole during tracheal intubation using a direct laryngoscope has been reported, but there has been no report of asystole during tracheal intubation using a videolaryngoscope. We report a case of asystole during videolaryngoscopy in a 76-year-old woman with marked tracheal deviation caused by a large thyroid adenoma. General anesthesia was induced with remimazolam 6 mg/kg/h, fentanyl 0.1 mg, and remifentanil 0.25 μg/kg/min, under apneic oxygenation using high-flow nasal oxygenation. After administration of rocuronium 40 mg, a McGrath™ MAC videolaryngoscope provided an excellent glottic view. Immediately before tracheal tube insertion, sudden bradycardia progressed to asystole. Laryngoscopy was discontinued, and intravenous atropine 0.5 mg was administered, resulting in the return of spontaneous cardiac activity within 10 s without chest compressions. The patient’s vital signs remained stable after the event, and tracheal intubation was achieved without further complications. Anesthesiologists should remain vigilant for this rare but potentially life-threatening complication.

Introduction

During induction of general anesthesia, insertion of a direct laryngoscope and tracheal intubation could be associated with severe bradycardia or asystole, potentially requiring resuscitation with medication and chest compressions.[12] Severe bradycardia or asystole is likely attributed to a vagal reflex (or the laryngocardiac reflex) evoked by stimulation during direct laryngoscopy.[12] Recently, videolaryngoscopes have increasingly been used for tracheal intubation, because, compared with direct laryngoscopy, videolaryngoscopy is associated with several advantages, such as a high first-attempt success rate, less cervical extension, and reduced oropharyngeal stimulation.[3456] Therefore, vagal reflexes would be less likely to occur during the use of a videolaryngoscope. While cases of severe bradycardia or asystole during laryngoscopy with a direct laryngoscope have been reported,[12] no such cases have been reported with a videolaryngoscope.

We report a case of asystole during laryngoscopy with a videolaryngoscope in a patient with an anticipated difficult airway.

Case Description

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for reporting this case and images. A 76-year-old woman, 142 cm, 56 kg, was scheduled for open hernia repair to treat an abdominal wall incisional hernia. Her medical history included a laparoscopic cholecystectomy for gallstones and lumbar spine surgery. She had no history of heart disease. Although she had hypertension, with a systolic blood pressure of approximately 150 mmHg, she was not receiving any antihypertensive medication. A left thyroid adenoma (4.0 × 3.5 × 4.5 cm) had been noted for the past 10 years, but her thyroid hormone levels were within the normal range. She continuously refused to undergo surgery for the thyroid adenoma. She also had been diagnosed with vertigo 10 years ago, but had since remained asymptomatic.

Preoperative examination identified a significant tracheal deviation caused by the thyroid mass, without tracheal stenosis [Figure 1]. Given the anticipated risk of a difficult airway, we planned to oxygenate the patient using high-flow nasal oxygenation (HFNO) and to induce general anesthesia with remimazolam.

Preoperative imaging of the patient demonstrating tracheal deviation caused by a thyroid tumor. (a) Chest radiograph. (b) Coronal computed tomography (CT) image. (c) Axial CT image

On arrival in the operating room, routine monitors, such as a noninvasive blood pressure cuff, an electrocardiogram (ECG), a pulse oximeter, a bispectral index monitor, and a neuromuscular blockade monitor, were applied. Before induction of anesthesia, the blood pressure was high (164/97 mmHg), heart rate 68 beats/min in sinus rhythm (without apparent arrhythmias), and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) of 94–98% on room air. An intravenous catheter was inserted, while a nasal cannula (F&P Optiflow Trace Nasal Interface Fisher & Paykel Healthcare, Auckland, New Zealand) was applied to the patient’s nose, and oxygen was provided at 50 L/min using a high-flow generator (Optiflow THRIVE nasal high flow therapy system, Fisher & Paykel Healthcare, Auckland, New Zealand) for 3 min. SpO2 increased to 100% immediately.

General anesthesia was induced with remimazolam 6 mg/kg/h, fentanyl 0.1 mg, and remifentanil 0.25 μg/kg/min. After confirming the loss of eyelid reflex, oxygen flow was increased to 70 L/min, and no manual ventilation by a face mask was used (apneic oxygenation). Remimazolam was reduced to 1 mg/kg/h, and neuromuscular blockade was achieved with rocuronium 40 mg. Three min after administration of rocuronium, the train-of-four ratio was 0, the heart rate 62 beats/min (in sinus rhythm), blood pressure 83/55 mmHg, and SpO2 99–100%.

The patient’s mouth could be opened easily and adequately wide enough to insert a videolaryngoscope (McGrath™ MAC, Covidien, Tokyo, Japan: blade 3). Laryngoscopy easily provided a clear glottic view (Cormack-Lehane grade 1), without application of the backward, upward, and rightward pressure maneuver. When a tracheal tube was about to be inserted to the patient’s oral cavity, the heart rate decreased suddenly and culminated in asystole [Figure 2].

ECG showing an episode of asystole lasting 8 s. The subtle electrocardiographic deflection indicated by the downward arrow may represent a blocked premature atrial contraction or electrical noise. ECG = Electrocardiogram

Laryngoscopy was immediately halted, and intravenous atropine 0.5 mg was injected. An anesthesiologist promptly moved to the patient’s side to initiate chest compressions; however, as the heartbeat resumed within 10 s, chest compressions were not performed. The heart rate returned to 61 beats/min in sinus rhythm, blood pressure 103/65 mmHg, and SpO2 of 99%, with no further episodes of deterioration. ECG findings showed no peaked or flattened T waves suggestive of potassium abnormalities.

We immediately consulted a consultant cardiologist to investigate the possible cause of asystole. Considering the timing of the event and the ECG changes observed, the consultant concluded that the asystole was most likely to be induced by a vagal reflex triggered by videolaryngoscopy.

Given the stability of the vital signs, and after discussion with the consultant, laryngoscopy was cautiously repeated with the same videolaryngoscope, and tracheal intubation was successfully performed without further complications. The surgical procedure proceeded uneventfully, and the patient’s trachea was extubated without complications. She was transferred to the ward in stable condition. Serum electrolytes measured immediately after surgery showed no abnormalities other than mild hypocalcemia, with the following values: sodium 143 mmol/L, potassium 4.1 mmol/L, chloride 108 mmol/L, calcium 8.6 mg/dL, and magnesium 2.1 mg/dL.

She was discharged from our hospital on the 5th postoperative day.

Discussion

During induction of general anesthesia, bradycardia and asystole caused by vagal reflexes evoked by laryngoscopy and tracheal intubation are rare but clinically significant complications. Severe vagal reflexes are thought to be mediated by the activation of afferent parasympathetic nerve fibers in response to stimulation of the lower pharynx, the larynx, and the trachea.[127]

Severe bradycardia and asystole could be caused by general anesthetics (such as remifentanil and rocuronium), hypoxia, electrolyte disturbances (such as hyperkalemia or hypokalemia), increased intrathoracic pressure related to positive end-expiratory pressure during HFNO, or a vagal reflex.[8910] In this case, the most likely cause is a vagal reflex.

Although the risk factors for severe vagal reflexes associated with laryngoscopy remain unclear, stretching of the carotid sinus has been suggested as a potential trigger.[7] In this patient, compression of the carotid sinus by the thyroid mass might have increased the susceptibility to a strong vagal reflex.

Compared with direct laryngoscopy, videolaryngoscopy would be associated with less stimulus to the oropharyngeal structure, so that the risk of severe vagal reflex is theoretically lower. Nevertheless, severe vagal reflexes may occur even with videolaryngoscopy.

Conclusion

We report that asystole by a vagal reflex could occur during laryngoscopy, even with a videolaryngoscope, and that appropriate management may be required in such cases.

Key messages

Asystole due to a vagal reflex can occur even during videolaryngoscopy. Prompt discontinuation of stimulation and immediate treatment may lead to rapid recovery.
