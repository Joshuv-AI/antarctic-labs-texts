Sulfated hyaluronic acid for wound repair: Mechanisms, materials, and translational challenges
Authors: Min Lin, Danni Zhou, Kaixuan Chen, Hankang Jiang, Wenjie Chen, Jing Wu, Guizhi Zhu, Kailei Xu, Peng Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sulfated hyaluronic acid (sHA) is emerging as a promising biomaterial for advanced wound dressings because it combines the intrinsic biocompatibility and extracellular matrix (ECM)-like hydration of hyaluronic acid (HA) with added chemical functionality that enhances stability and bioactivity. Chronic wounds, particularly diabetic ulcer, —remain a major clinical challenge due to persistent inflammation, protease/reactive oxygen species (ROS)-rich microenvironments, impaired angiogenesis, and poor growth-factor retention; native HA is limited by rapid enzymatic degradation, low protein affinity, and potentially pro-inflammatory low-molecular fragments, motivating strategies to introduce sulfate groups onto HA to tune its interactions with cells and proteins. In this review, we performed a comprehensive literature review of the biological functions and applications of sHA in wound healing. Key results demonstrate that sHA significantly enhances anti-inflammatory responses, promotes sustained release of cationic therapeutics, improves angiogenesis, and reduces fibrosis. Furthermore, sHA-functionalized hydrogels accelerate diabetic wound healing by modulating macrophage polarization, enhancing re-epithelialization, and supporting vascularization. In further, we also offered a practical roadmap for researchers and clinicians to rationally design next-generation sHA-based dressings with improved reproducibility, controlled release, and safety.

Introduction

The skin, the body's largest organ, comprises the epidermis, dermis, and hypodermis and serves as the primary barrier against pathogenic invasion [1]. It also mediates sensory perception, prevents transepidermal water loss, and contributes to thermoregulation [[2], [3], [4]]. Consequently, cutaneous injury can result in significant morbidity. Wounds are classified as acute or chronic: acute wounds typically heal within 2–3 weeks, whereas chronic wounds are commonly defined as those that fail to progress through the normal stages of healing within three months or that show no measurable improvement after four weeks of standard care [5,6].

Acute wounds typically involve the epidermis and dermis, with or without deeper tissue injury, and arise from abrasions, contusions, lacerations, minor burns, or small surgical incisions [7]. They have a sudden onset and normally repair rapidly through the sequential phases of hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling. However, perturbations during these stages can lead to chronic non-healing wounds or to excessive scarring, such as hypertrophic scars and keloids [8]. Chronic wounds are characterized by persistent infection, prolonged inflammation, impaired angiogenesis, accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and elevated matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) activity [[9], [10], [11]]. They include diabetic ulcers, severe burns, venous or arterial ulcers, and pressure sores [12,13]. Among these, diabetic wounds are especially prevalent, prone to recurrence, and often refractory to complete healing; consequently, their management represents a major global healthcare burden [14].

Wound dressings are essential in the clinical management of diverse wound types. Conventional dressings, such as gauze bandages, cotton, and hemostatic sponges, provide hemostasis and absorb exudates but often adhere strongly to tissue during use [15,16]. This adhesion increases patient discomfort, exacerbates bleeding, and risks iatrogenic injury upon removal [17]. Standard treatments for diabetic wounds typically involve glycemic control, surgical intervention, and negative pressure wound therapy. However, these approaches are constrained by side effects, recurrence, and the absence of real-time biomarker feedback [18]. To overcome these limitations, modern functional wound dressings, including nanofibers, hydrocolloids, and hydrogels, have been developed in recent years [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. Among these, hydrogels have emerged as particularly promising due to their excellent biocompatibility, favorable hydrophilicity, and three-dimensional (3D) porous architecture that mimics the extracellular matrix (ECM) [23,24]. In addition, hydrogels provide versatile platforms for the delivery of drugs, cytokines, and growth factors [25].

Hyaluronic acid (HA), a widely used hydrogel biomaterial, is a naturally occurring linear glycosaminoglycan (GAG) composed of repeating disaccharide units of D-glucuronic acid and N-acetyl-D-glucosamine [26]. Abundant in the ECM, HA exhibits favorable biomaterial properties, including biocompatibility, biodegradability, and gelation capacity [27]. However, its biomedical applications are limited by rapid degradation, low protein affinity and the potential to induce inflammation [28]. In particular, degradation products such as low-molecular-weight fragments can trigger inflammatory responses at implantation sites [29]. Moreover, compared with heparin and other anionic GAGs, HA's relatively weak negative charge reduces its capacity to electrostatically bind cationic drugs [24]. To overcome these shortcomings, researchers have introduced sulfate groups into HA via chemical modification to enhance its biological activity [30]. Sulfated HA (sHA) offers several advantages: (i) sulfate groups increase stability by impeding hyaluronidase-mediated degradation [31]; (ii) the higher negative charge density improves binding affinity and enables sustained release of cationic therapeutics [32,33]; (iii) sHA suppresses pro-inflammatory activation in fully differentiated human macrophages (iMФ) [34]; and (iv) sHA promotes angiogenesis through multifaceted regulation of cellular functions [24]. Recently, sHA-based hydrogels have attracted considerable interest as advanced wound dressings, yet a comprehensive review of their therapeutic potential remains lacking.

Wound healing

Wound healing process

Previous research has identified four sequential phases of wound healing (Fig. 1A): hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling [35]. In physiological skin repair, hemostasis begins immediately after injury through three coordinated mechanisms: vasoconstriction to reduce bleeding, platelet plug formation via receptor-mediated adhesion and aggregation, and stabilization of a provisional fibrin matrix [36]. Following clot formation, polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) are the first immune cells to infiltrate the wound bed and dominate the early inflammatory response [40]. PMNs eliminate microbes through phagocytosis, generation of ROS, and protease-mediated matrix degradation [41]. Their timely clearance, achieved through programmed apoptosis or macrophage-mediated efferocytosis, is essential to prevent prolonged inflammation [37]. Subsequently, monocyte-derived macrophages migrate into the wound and transition from pro-inflammatory (M1-like) phenotypes to diverse functional states. These macrophages coordinate phagocytosis, secrete growth factors, and stimulate angiogenesis to support granulation tissue formation and re-epithelialization [41]. In diabetic or ischemic wounds, however, persistent neutrophil infiltration perpetuates inflammation and disrupts progression toward resolution, thereby contributing to impaired healing in chronic wound conditions [37].

During the transition from inflammation to proliferation, macrophage-derived mediators activate fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and keratinocytes, stimulating their migration and proliferation [41]. These cellular activities promote granulation tissue formation through ECM deposition and myofibroblast (MFb) differentiation, facilitating wound contraction [42]. Concurrently, angiogenesis is initiated within hours of injury, as endothelial tip cells migrate and branch to establish a highly vascularized provisional matrix [37].

Re-epithelialization occurs through two complementary pathways: activation of basal-layer epidermal stem cells and the plasticity of terminally differentiated keratinocytes. Together, these mechanisms reconstruct the stratified epidermis and adnexal structures [42], thereby shifting the wound microenvironment from inflammatory signaling toward matrix remodeling. Wound maturation can begin as early as 72 h post-injury, characterized by enzymatic collagen degradation and matrix reorganization [41]. Under normal conditions, repair restores barrier integrity and approximates native tensile strength. By contrast, chronic wounds, prevalent in diabetic, vascular-impaired, and elderly populations, arise from compounded defects in the healing cascade. Poorly managed lesions are prone to recurrent breakdown, substantially increasing the risks of limb ischemia, septic complications, and mortality [42].

Wound types

Conventional wounds typically follow a spatiotemporally organized sequence of repair (Fig. 1B). In contrast, chronic wounds are defined as those that fail to heal within three months or show no measurable improvement after four weeks of standard care [5]. Diabetic wounds, most often manifesting as diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs), represent a major subgroup of chronic wounds and are among the most clinically challenging complications of diabetes mellitus. Normal wound repair progresses through the overlapping phases of haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling. In diabetic wounds, however, this progression is disrupted by multiple pathological factors. Persistent hyperglycemia, sustained inflammation, neuropathy, and microvascular dysfunction collectively impair oxygen delivery and adaptive tissue responses [43]. Immune dysregulation further compromises inflammatory resolution, markedly increasing susceptibility to infection. Once established, infection perpetuates a self-reinforcing cycle of microbial persistence, chronic inflammation, and impaired tissue regeneration [44]. In addition, excessive oxidative stress combined with reduced antioxidant capacity creates a redox imbalance, a critical driver of delayed healing [45].

Angiogenic homeostasis is also disrupted, with diabetes shifting the balance toward anti-angiogenic signaling, thereby suppressing neovascularization and impairing both wound closure and vascular restoration [46]. Hyperglycemia exacerbates these effects by inhibiting hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1) and damaging endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs), leading to deepened tissue hypoxia [47]. Neuropathy-associated depletion of neuropeptides further weakens inflammatory responses, vasodilation, angiogenesis, ECM production, and re-epithelialization, while oxidative neuronal injury directly links neuropathy to impaired healing [48]. In combination with hyperglycemia, neuropeptide loss compromises microcirculation and promotes ischemia in the wound bed [43].

Consequently, developing effective therapeutic strategies for diabetic wounds remains a pressing clinical priority. Current standard-of-care approaches, including glycemic control, surgical debridement, wound coverage, pressure offloading, vascular assessment, and infection management, provide benefits but remain limited in efficacy [49].

Effect of sHA in wound healing

HA, a major component of the ECM, neural tissues, and synovial fluid, plays critical roles in maintaining tissue hydration, preserving extracellular space, providing lubrication, and regulating osmotic balance [27,50]. Its excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability have led to widespread application in wound dressings and tissue engineering scaffolds. When formulated as a hydrogel dressing, HA's strong water-retention capacity supports a moist microenvironment that promotes cell migration and proliferation, thereby accelerating wound repair. However, its biomedical utility is limited by rapid enzymatic degradation, low protein affinity, and potential pro-inflammatory effects [28,29].

To address these challenges, extensive modification strategies have been pursued, with sHA emerging as a promising derivative. Compared with native HA, sHA exhibits enhanced anti-inflammatory activity and high protein-binding affinity [51]. sHA exerts potent anti-inflammatory effects by reducing ROS and downregulating tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), while lowering the macrophage M1/M2 ratio. sHA hydrogels used to treat rheumatoid arthritis, which effectively alleviated joint swelling, underscoring its potential to suppress excessive inflammation and enhance antioxidant defenses [52]. The negatively charged sulfate groups of sHA also mediate strong protein interactions. For example, Linda et al. demonstrated that sHA electrostatically binds to Lys40 of transforming growth factor-β receptor I (TβR-I), preventing transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β1) from binding and thereby offering a basis for designing biomaterials that modulate TGF-β1-driven skin fibrosis [32]. Similarly, incorporating sHA into growth factor-based hydrogels showed enhanced protein sequestration, prolonged the bioavailability of protein therapeutics, and highlighted its potential for regenerative medicine [31]. In another study, Dong-Kwon [30] et al. selectively sulfated the C-6 hydroxyl of N-acetyl-glucosamine in HA, enabling sHA to bind the pro-angiogenic vascular endothelial growth factor 165a (VEGF165a) isoform while sparing the anti-angiogenic vascular endothelial growth factor 165b (VEGF165b), thus demonstrating its potential for modulating pathological angiogenesis.

Beyond immunoregulation, sHA also plays a critical role in modulating angiogenesis. Rother et al. demonstrated that adding sHA to porcine aortic endothelial cell cultures attenuated vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGF-A) and tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases-3 (TIMP-3) signaling by disrupting their binding to vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR-2) in a sulfation-dependent manner, thereby revealing a novel mechanism of angiogenic regulation (Fig. 2C) [33]. Similar effects were confirmed in human bone marrow stromal cells (Fig. 2D) [55]. In follow-up work, Rother et al. designed composite hydrogels containing acrylated HA, chondroitin sulfate, or sHA, and showed that sHA significantly enhanced endothelial cell proliferation and metabolic activity, supporting its role in regulating protein–matrix interactions and directing cellular responses (Fig. 3A) [54]. To further assess endothelial behavior, a 3D angiogenesis model using Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cell (HUVEC) spheroids was employed, where sHA treatment significantly increased sprout length and number. Furthermore, sHA has also been applied to modulate fibroblast activity and fibrotic signaling. van der Smissen et al. developed artificial ECM containing sHA and collagen, with adsorbed TGF-β1 immobilized on its surface. Dermal fibroblasts (dFb) cultured on this artificial ECM showed significantly higher proliferation of dFb in a sulfation-dependent manner, while ECM components collagen and HA were reduced (Fig. 3C) [56]. In further study, it was found that the bioactivity of TGF-β1 was significantly reduced by sHA, where sHA prevents TGF-β1 signaling by occupation of the TβR-I binding site and thereby inhibited TGF-β1-induced expression of α-smooth muscle actin (αSMA), collagen, and ED-A splice variant of fibronectin (ED-A FN), leading to reduced TGF-β1-driven MFb differentiation. Hence, sHA seems to be an interesting option to modify TGF-β1-driven effects or even to interfere with fibrotic processes (Fig. 3D) [57].

Collectively, these findings highlight the broad regulatory potential of sHA across multiple cell types, from suppressing inflammatory macrophage responses to modulating angiogenic signaling and attenuating fibrotic remodeling. Such multifunctionality underscores its promise in the design of advanced biomaterials for regenerative medicine.

Sophia Hauck et al. encapsulated sHA in acrylated-HA/collagen (HA-AC/coll)-scaffolds as immunoregulatory component during wound healing (Fig. 4) [51]. Peritoneal cells and bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM) cultured on the hydrogel demonstrated significant downregulation of inflammatory genes compared with controls that contains low or high molecular weight HA. For in vivo translation, sHA encapsulated HA-AC/collagen hydrogels allowed delivery of sHA into wounds over a period of at least one week. Comparative advantages of sHA-Based hydrogels

Multiple therapeutic strategies are currently employed for the management of chronic wounds, including conventional treatments, cell-based therapies, and polysaccharide-based materials such as sHA. Conventional therapies encompass debridement, traditional dressings, and negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT), all of which exhibit specific drawbacks. Debridement effectively removes devitalized tissue, controls infection, and mitigates inflammation; however, its non-selective nature can inadvertently damage newly formed granulation tissue [62]. Traditional dressings are cost-effective and widely accessible but require frequent replacement and may adhere to the wound surface [63]. NPWT enhances granulation tissue formation and reduces bacterial burden, yet its high cost and potential to injure local vasculature and nerves limit its use [64]. Cell-based therapies have demonstrated efficacy in treating conditions such as degenerative musculoskeletal disorders, corneal and retinal diseases, and rheumatoid arthritis. In chronic wound management, specific cellular components, including macrophages, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and fibroblasts, play critical roles in promoting repair. Macrophages exhibit a dual role in wound healing: M2 macrophages promote inflammation resolution, whereas diabetic wounds are often dominated by pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages that perpetuate chronic inflammation [67]. Tregs regulate both inflammation and fibrosis, yet activation of latent TGF-β may induce keratinocyte-derived C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CXCL5) expression, leading to neutrophil and T helper 17 (Th17) cell recruitment and ultimately hindering tissue regeneration [35,65]. Fibroblasts are essential for ECM deposition and remodeling, though their excessive activation can result in fibrosis [66]. Despite the therapeutic promise of cell-based strategies, substantial post-delivery cell loss remains a key limitation. Incorporating these cells into hydrogel-based systems has emerged as an effective approach to enhance cell retention and accelerate chronic wound healing.

Numerous hydrogels have been developed for chronic wound treatment. Given that sHA is a polysaccharide, which is a fundamental ECM component, this comparison focuses on sHA and other polysaccharide-based biomaterials. Among them, chitosan, derived from the partial deacetylation of chitin, has been extensively investigated for its strong antibacterial activity, adhesion, oxygen permeability, biocompatibility, and biodegradability [68]. It shows significant potential for antibacterial wound dressings, though its anti-inflammatory capacity is limited. Alginate, a linear anionic polysaccharide composed of β-1,4-linked D-mannuronic acid (M) and L-guluronic acid (G) residues and derived from brown algae or bacteria, is another widely used wound care material. Its hydrophilicity, high biocompatibility, and liquid absorption capacity help maintain a moist microenvironment, provide soothing effects, and facilitate cell migration [69]. Consequently, alginate has found broad application in wound healing, tissue engineering, and drug delivery, although it possesses limited bioactivity and antifibrotic potential. More recently, carrageenan (CRG), a high-molecular-weight sulfated polysaccharide extracted from red seaweeds, has gained attention for its antiviral, antibacterial, immunomodulatory, antihyperlipidemic, antioxidant, and antitumor properties. Composed of galactose and anhydro-galactose (AG) units linked by glycosidic bonds, CRG holds promise for advanced drug delivery, wound healing, and tissue regeneration applications. Nevertheless, CRG-based hydrogels suffer from low mechanical strength, limited stability, and rapid degradation under physiological conditions, restricting their effectiveness as wound dressings [70]. Compared with those materials, sHA-based hydrogels provide a more favorable platform for chronic wound healing, as their sulfation pattern enhances structural stability, supports sustained growth factor retention, and modulates pro-fibrotic signaling. Through combined anti-inflammatory, pro-angiogenic, and antifibrotic actions, sHA-based systems address multiple pathological pathways simultaneously. Therefore, integrating sHA with complementary biomaterials may further augment therapeutic efficacy, representing a promising direction for advanced wound management.

Future perspective

Despite the promising therapeutic effects of sHA in wound healing, several critical challenges remain that hinder its clinical translation and standardized application. These issues include heterogeneity in sulfation chemistry, uncontrolled release and degradation, and translational barriers to clinical practice. Addressing these problems will be essential for advancing sHA-based wound dressings from experimental models to reliable clinical solutions.

A primary challenge lies in the pronounced heterogeneity of sHA preparations. The biological activity of sHA is highly sensitive to its fine chemical structure — principally the sulfation pattern, the degree of substitution, and the polymer's molecular weight and polydispersity. Small changes in any of these parameters can markedly alter electrostatic surface, chain conformation, and therefore binding to proteins such as growth factors, cytokines, proteases, and cell-surface receptors. In practice, widely used chemical sulfation protocols (varying reagents, solvents, temperature, reaction time, and protection/deprotection strategies) produce mixtures in which sulfate groups are distributed non-uniformly along the backbone and across molecular weight fractions. This leads to products with inconsistent physicochemical properties, such as differences in charge density, solubility, and viscosity, that in turn produce variable biological readouts across labs and experiments. To overcome these barriers, the field needs tighter control over synthesis and much more rigorous characterization. Technically, this can be addressed by advancing site-selective chemistries and chemoenzymatic routes that use sulfotransferases or engineered enzymes to install sulfate groups at defined positions and densities. Parallel process controls, like fractionating polymers by size, removing low-molecular-weight byproducts, and carefully controlling reaction stoichiometry will reduce batch variability. Furthermore, developing libraries of sHA variants with well-defined and reported sulfation degree, sulfation patterns, and narrow molecular weight ranges will allow high-throughput and hypothesis-driven studies that correlate specific structural features with biological endpoints.

sHA, although chemically more stable than native HA due to the presence of sulfate groups, remains vulnerable to degradation in the hostile biochemical milieu of chronic wounds. Elevated levels of hyaluronidases, proteases, and ROS in chronic and infected wounds accelerate polymer breakdown, undermine the structural integrity of sHA-based dressings, and shorten the duration of therapeutic action. In practice this can produce rapid loss of mechanical support, premature release of payloads, and diminished capacity to modulate cell behavior or local growth factor gradients, all of which reduce clinical benefit. Current approaches to extend sHA persistence, such as covalent crosslinking to other polymers, incorporation into interpenetrating networks, or conjugation with protective moieties, can slow degradation but often introduce trade-offs. For example, the increased stiffness that impairs conformability, reduced bioavailability of the sHA binding motifs, or manufacturing complexity. Moreover, many crosslinked systems still display undesirable initial burst release of encapsulated factors or fail to release cargo in a therapeutically useful, wound-specific manner. Achieving both long-term stability and controlled, context-appropriate release therefore remains a central engineering challenge. Future sHA dressings should move from passive reservoirs toward “smart,” stimuli-responsive systems that sense and react to key wound microenvironment cues. Several design strategies can be combined to realize that goal. For localized, pathology-triggered delivery, sHA can be incorporated into matrices bearing cleavable linkers that respond selectively to wound-associated inputs. For instance, ROS-labile linkers that break down in high-oxidative environments to liberate sHA and co-loaded therapeutics. pH-sensitive bonds, like imine/Schiff-base or acetal chemistries, that hydrolyze in acidic infected wounds to trigger release. Enzyme-cleavable peptide sequences or hyaluronidase-sensitive motifs that allow material remodeling only when specific proteases are active. Architecturally, embedding sHA in nanoparticles or microparticles and then distributing those particles within a bulk hydrogel can substantially reduce burst release and permit tunable, multi-phase delivery. In summary, the next generation of sHA dressings will combine precise chemistry, hierarchical architecture, and environment-responsive mechanisms to deliver sHA and therapeutic cargos only when and where they are needed.

Although sulfation retards hyaluronidase-mediated degradation of HA [32], the long-term fate of sHA fragments in the body remains a concern. It is well-established that low-molecular-weight fragments of native HA can exhibit pro-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic properties [29]. The biological activity of the degradation products of sHA is not fully understood. There is a potential risk that accumulated sulfated oligosaccharides could elicit unforeseen immune responses or interfere with normal cellular functions over extended periods. Therefore, detailed studies on the pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, and clearance of sHA and its metabolites are essential before clinical translation, especially for chronic wounds requiring long-term dressing placement.

Conclusion

sHA represents a highly promising biomaterial for advanced wound care, particularly in the management of chronic wounds such as diabetic ulcers. By overcoming the inherent limitations of native HA, such as rapid degradation, low protein affinity, and potential inflammatory effects, sHA offers enhanced stability, high binding capacity for therapeutic agents, and potent immunomodulatory properties. However, challenges remain in standardizing sulfation patterns, controlling degradation kinetics, and ensuring consistent biological responses across different wound types. Future efforts should focus on the rational design of smart, stimulus-responsive sHA-based dressings that can dynamically interact with the wound microenvironment, offering personalized and adaptive therapy.
