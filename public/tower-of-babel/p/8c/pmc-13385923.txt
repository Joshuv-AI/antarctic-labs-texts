Effect of lactic acid bacteria inoculation and urea on fermentation quality and aerobic stability of sugarcane top silage
Authors: Smerjai Bureenok, Nittaya Pitiwittayakul, Benya Saenmahayak, Lin Sun, Achara Lukkananukool, Jan Thomas Schonewille
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study was to investigate the effect of Lactiplantibacillus paraplantarum ST1 and Limosilactobacillus fermentum G3 and their combination with urea on the aerobic stability of sugarcane tops silages. The fermentation profile was not affected by the interaction between urea and lactic acid bacteria (LAB), except for the lactic acid content (P = 0.012). Lactic acid content was higher in LAB silage (P = 0.008). The use of urea resulted in higher levels of pH value, butyric acid, and ammonia-nitrogen compared to LAB. The addition of urea increased the crude protein, and fiber content in silage and decreased the in vitro dry matter digestibility of silage by 7.1% (P < 0.001). In the urea-treated silages, the pH values remained stable, and the yeast counts remained low during five days of air exposure. In contrast, yeast counts increased in the silages without urea treatment, irrespective of the use of LAB. Clostridium sensu stricto 12 was found exclusively in urea-treated silages. The use of LAB additives without urea appears to be advantageous for the fermentation process of sugarcane tops silage. Despite improving the aerobic stability, the addition of urea is not warranted because it results in a poor-quality silage.Keywords: Acetic acid, Aerobic stability, Agricultural waste, Fungi, Hetero-fermentative, Homo-fermentativeSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Microbiology

Introduction

Various forage crops, including sugarcane (Saccharum officinarum), are widely available for ensiling in tropical and sub-tropical regions. In practice, however, the tops of sugarcane are left unused after harvesting and sometimes burned in the field, thereby contributing to environmental pollution. Sugarcane tops, however, can be considered as a valuable feed resource for ruminants1 and their use contributes to the sustainability of ruminant production. In Thailand, sugarcane is cultivated across most regions of the country and in 2024, 1.5 million hectares were harvested with an average yield of 60 t/ha2. Such a yield is associated with ~ 7 to 12 tons of sugarcane tops3.

In principle, sugarcane tops can easily be ensiled due to their high content of water-soluble carbohydrates (WSC) and low buffering capacity4. However, Zhang et al.5 reported that a high abundance of yeast (Pichia) can occur during either the last phase of the ensiling process or due to the exposure to air, leading to ethanol production, dry matter (DM) loss6 and subsequent spoilage upon aerobic exposure7. Moreover, Ren et al.1 showed that the high moisture content of unwilted, fresh sugarcane tops predispose for an increased abundance of Clostridiaceae and butyric acid (BA) production during the ensiling process. Interestingly, it has been shown by Wang et al.8 that the addition of homofermentative lactic acid bacteria (LAB) strains, such as Lactiplantibacillus plantarum (LP), can inhibit the growth of undesirable microorganisms, including Clostridiaceae. Thus, the addition of L. plantarum to fresh sugarcane tops can be considered of interest in preventing the growth of Clostridiaceae during ensiling.

In view of its low crude protein (CP) content, it can be considered opportune to supplement sugarcane tops with urea prior to ensiling9. Caution, however, is warranted because upon enzymatic hydrolysis of supplemental urea, ammonia is produced and in view of its alkaline property, ammonia can counteract the action of lactic acid (LA) in obtaining a low silage pH. Thus, in case of urea supplementation of sugarcane tops, fortification with LAB may, at least partially, neutralize the action of ammonia on silage pH. It is of interest to note that the addition of urea to high WSC silage material, such as sugarcane tops, has been shown to improve the aerobic stability in silage because urea inhibits the growth of yeast and mold10,11. Furthermore, heterofermentative LAB strains produce, next to LA, also acetic acid (AA) and the latter is known for its antifungal properties12,13 and thus may also improve the aerobic stability of silage. Clearly, the net result of the combined supplementation of urea and heterofermentative LAB, such as Limosilactobacillus fermentum (LF)14, on silage quality cannot be easily predicted. Thus, we considered it appropriate to also investigate the effect of urea supplementation and its interaction with LAB on the process of ensiling in sugarcane tops. It was hypothesized that the addition of urea would improve the aerobic stability of silage when L. paraplantarum, but not L. fermentum, was used to ensile unwilted sugarcane tops.

Results

Fermentation characteristics and nutritive value

With the exception of the LA and ethanol concentrations of the silages, all other variables related to the fermentation profile of the silages (Table 1) were not affected by the interaction between urea and LAB. The pH value was higher for the urea-treated silage than that of the silage without urea treatment (P < 0.001). The addition of LAB did not affect the pH, but when urea was added to the sugarcane tops, the pH value was found to be 40.8% higher after 60 d of ensiling. Applying either LAB species as an inoculant to silages without urea treatment resulted in higher LA contents; however, both LAB applications had no effect on the LA concentration in the silages treated with urea. Upon ANOVA, ethanol concentrations were significantly affected by the treatments (P = 0.044), but significant differences between individual silages could not be identified by Tukey’s t test. The AA concentrations of urea-treated silages were ~ 3-fold higher compared to those without urea treatment (P = 0.005). Both propionic acid (PA) and BA concentrations were found to be lower than the detection limit in the silages without urea and BA could also not be detected in the sugarcane tops treated with LAB only. The BA content was found to be 13.6 times greater in the silages treated with urea compared to the untreated silage (P < 0.001). The ethanol concentrations were significantly influenced by the interaction between urea and LAB treatments (P = 0.044). The ammonia nitrogen (NH3-N) concentrations were not affected by LAB (P = 0.310), but urea treatment increased the NH3-N concentrations by 16.7 times (P < 0.001). The LAB counts were significantly influenced by the interaction between urea and LAB treatments (P = 0.038). Inoculating sugarcane tops with either LAB did not affect LAB counts; however, LAB counts were lower in silages without urea treatment compared to those treated with urea. No interaction between urea and either LAB was observed for yeast counts (P = 0.270); however, yeast counts were found to be higher in silage without urea treatment (P = 0.002). Yeast counts were lower in silage inoculated with L. fermentum G3 (LF-silage) compared to the silage treated with L. paraplantarum ST1 (LP-silage) (P = 0.048). With respect to the chemical composition of the silages, only the DM content of the silage was affected by the interaction between urea and LAB species (P = 0.020). The highest DM content was found in the silages without urea treatment but treated with LP, while the lowest DM value was found in the silages treated with urea. Urea treatment increased the CP content of silages (P < 0.001), while LF decreased the CP content of silage not treated with urea (P < 0.034). Treating sugarcane tops with urea was found to increase the NDF and ADF contents and to decrease the in vitro dry matter digestibility (IVDMD) of the silages. The contents of both NDF and ADF, and the IVDMD were not affected by LAB.

Aerobic stability

Upon exposure to air (Fig. 1), the pH value of the silages was affected by a three-way interaction between time, urea treatment and LAB treatment (P = 0.008). The pH remained stable during the 5 days of air exposure in the silages treated with urea. However, the pH values steadily increased up to at least 5.66 in the silages without urea treatment when treated with either LP or LF. The increase in pH was less overt when the silage was neither treated with urea nor LAB species. The yeast counts of the silages were not affected by time × urea × LAB (P = 0.109), but they were influenced by the interaction between time and urea treatment (P < 0.001). Over time, yeast count remained low in the urea-treated silages but increased in the silages without urea treatment during the 5 days of air exposure. Yeast counts were also affected by urea × LAB (P = 0.001), inoculation with LP or LF caused higher yeast counts only in the silages without urea treatment.

Microbial community

Alpha diversity analysis showed no statistically significant difference among the silages, while the Shannon evenness tended to be lower in LP- and LF- silages (P = 0.063) (Fig. 2a–d). According to principal coordinates analysis (PCoA), and multidimensional scaling (NMDS), obvious differences in the succession of bacterial communities among treatment groups were observed. (Fig. 3). The PCoA plots indicated a clear separation in microbe profile between silages treated with LAB and without urea treatment. The unique OTU number of all silages was much higher than that of the fresh sugarcane tops or material crop before ensiling, especially the OTU in the control silage, which showed the highest number (Fig. 4). Before ensiling, the main epiphytic bacteria at the genus level with higher relative abundance in sugarcane tops were Weissella spp. (37%) and Lactococcus (24%), (Fig. 5). Discussion

Aerobic stability and fermentation

With respect to the aerobic stability of the silages, the current data indicate that the aerobic stability of silage was improved when supplemental urea was used, irrespective of the use of either LP or LF. Our hypothesis was therefore rejected. The lack of effect of addition of either LP or LF on the aerobic stability of silages treated with urea is most likely related to the high ammonia concentrations in those silages. Indeed, Santos et al.15 reported that high ammonia concentrations inhibit the growth of microorganisms, because such high concentrations are toxic for LAB10,16. This notion is in line with both the low LA concentrations and the low bacterial community of LAB observed in the silages treated with urea. It thus appears that the applied dose of urea in the current study overruled the potential of LF to improve the aerobic stability of silage. This reasoning is corroborated by results reported by Moselhy et al.17, who demonstrated that the addition of urea (5 g/kg FM) plus molasses in Hedychium gardnerianum silage resulted in stable silage after air exposure, regardless of the use of LAB.

In addition to the effect of urea, it cannot be excluded that the associated high concentrations of AA and BA were also instrumental in inhibiting the growth of yeast18. Regarding the high AA concentrations we observed in the three silages supplemented with urea, it is of interest to note that an increased abundance of Clostridium sensu stricto 12 was observed those silages. Thus, Clostridium sensu stricto 12 was, at least partly, also responsible for the higher AA concentrations in the urea treated silages.19.

The lack of effect of LF on the aerobic stability of the silage without urea treatment is not easy to explain, because the AA concentration in this silage was found to be 5.6 g/kg DM. Indeed, Gomes et al.4 has shown that such an AA concentration has the potency to inhibit yeast growth in sugarcane top silage. However, we found a relatively great abundance of Acetobacter in the LF silage without urea. This observation can be considered of interest because Wang et al.20 reported that Acetobacter can stimulate the growth of yeast. We therefore speculate that Acetobacter counteracted the action of AA on the growth of yeast. Interestingly, the use of urea caused in increase in LAB counts, irrespective the LAB species used. This observation is probably related to the fact that during the early stage of the fermentation process, the pH was relatively high in the urea treated silages. Indeed, optimum pH values for maximum growth rate of LAB are reported to range from 5.5 to 6.5 21. It is well established that urea yields ammonia upon hydrolysis of urea22, thus formed ammonia acts as a buffer23, and can therefore stimulate the growth of LAB. Thus, supplemental urea can be considered advantageous during the early stage of the fermentation process. However, in view of the current results on the use of urea to ultimately achieve a good quality silage should not be advocated.

It is of interest to note that even in the silage without any treatment, a pH below 4 and a BA content lower than 5.0 g/kg DM was observed, thereby indicating that the process of fermentation went well in this silage24. This finding contrasts with the study of Ren et al.1 who reported that untreated sugarcane tops silage had high pH and BA content and the authors1 held both the high moisture of the crop and the high ambient temperature (35 °C) responsible for their observation on pH and BA. However, both the moisture content of the silages and the storage temperature in the current study were comparable with those reported by Ren et al.1. The difference in pH and BA concentrations of untreated sugarcane top silage observed in the current study and those reported by Ren et al.1, is not easy to explain but we speculate that the difference in microbial profile plays a role. Indeed, with the exception of the abundance in LAB species, the resemblance on the microbial profiles is poor between the current study and the one reported by Ren et al.1.

Microbial community

In the current study, Weissella and Lactococcus were the dominant bacterial groups in fresh sugarcane tops. These groups constituted more than 50% of the relative abundance contributing to LA production during the early phase of the ensiling process. This result contrasts with those of Wang et al.20, who found that fresh sugarcane tops had less than 1% relative abundance of LAB, including Leuconostoc, Lactobacillus, Weissella, Lactococcus, and Pediococcus. In this study, pH values below 4.0 were found in both the control and LAB-inoculated silages, which may have inhibited the growth of Weissella spp. due to their lower tolerance to low pH compared to Lactobacillus25. Therefore, Lentilactobacillus became the most abundant bacteria in these silages.

In all silages without urea treatment, LA concentrations were high, pH was low and BA values were low or below detection limit. The Shannon index, which measures species diversity, was lower in LP- and LF- inoculated silages compared with all urea-treated silages. Moreover, the bacterial community in the LF- and LP- inoculated silages, without urea, were clearly different from the other groups. The latter observation can be explained by the action of the two LAB species when supplemented to high WSC materials, where the two LAB promoted the production of LA, which subsequently inhibited the growth of other microorganisms and therefore influences bacterial community succession. The current results are in line with that reported by Du et al.26, who found that the Shannon index was lower in high-LA sugarcane top silage treated with L. plantarum. The LF- and LP- inoculated silages had a high relative abundance of Proteobacteria compared to other silages. The high relative abundance of Proteobacteria in this study may be explained by a high concentration of Acetobacter that belongs to this phylum. Acetobacter can grow in low pH conditions and induce aerobic deterioration in silages after air exposure27.

Nutritive value and IVDMD

In this study, the determination of DM content relied on oven-dried samples, leading to the loss of volatile nitrogen compounds, primarily ammonia and volatile fatty acids28. This loss potentially caused DM reduction in urea treated silage. Concerning CP, the highest mean contents were found in silages with the addition of urea. This increase resulted from the non-protein nitrogen content of urea, which facilitated an increase in silage’s protein content. The reduction in CP content was observed in sugarcane tops silage treated with LF. This may be explained by Pseudomonas in LF silage (Fig. 5b) as this bacterium is proteolytic and capable of hydrolyzing proteins, which results in protein loss in the silage29. The addition of LAB to sugarcane tops silage did not affect the NDF, or ADF contents. However, 28–31% of the initial ADF content of the silages was lost during the ensiling process. This is consistent with our previous research, which showed that inoculation with L. paraplantarum ST1 reduced the ADF content of the ensiled material crop14. This may have been caused by the acid solubilization of the fiber component during the ensiling process27. According to several studies, urea has the potential to reduce the fibrous component of silage by controlling the growth of mold and yeast, with released ammonia causing alkaline hydrolysis and partial solubilization of hemicellulose29. However, in contrast to other scientific studies, we found that the addition of urea did not reduce the fibrous fraction of the silages. The urea-treated silage in this study exhibited clostridial fermentation, resulting in more significant losses in easily degradable nutrients compared to structural carbohydrates, indirectly increasing the proportion of structural carbohydrates. This is consistent with the results of Moselhy et al.17, who found that the use of urea increased cell wall contents of silage. In the present study, the addition of urea decreased IVDMD, which may have been caused by the increase in plant cell wall fraction (Table 1). This is consistent with the results of Moselhy et al.17, who found that the use of urea resulted in an inferior process of fermentation, which consequently decreased the DM recovery. Subsequently, DM loss caused the disruption of cell wall component, which increased IVDMD.

Conclusion

The addition of LAB without the use of urea improved silage fermentation but failed to improve the aerobic stability of sugarcane tops silage. Urea addition appears to be an effective strategy to aerobic stability, but it results in a poor-quality silage. This study found no positive synergism between LAB and urea with sugarcane tops silage.

Materials and methods

Silage preparation

The experiments were conducted at the Forage and Silage Microbiology Laboratory of the Department of Animal Science at Faculty of Agricultural Innovation and Technology, Rajamangala University of Technology Isan, Thailand. All experimental procedures involving plants were conducted in accordance with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and regulations. This study also adheres to the ethical standards set forth by Thai regulatory authorities.

Fresh sugarcane tops were harvested from experimental fields located at the Faculty of Agricultural Innovation and Technology, Rajamangala University of Technology Isan, Thailand. Previously, we demonstrated that supplementation with either the heterofermentative bacterium L. fermentum and L. paraplantarum improved the silage quality of Stylo legume (Stylosanthes guianensis)14. These strains were originally isolated from naturally fermented plant juices obtained from local crops, representing part of the indigenous microbial flora, and are currently maintained under standardized laboratory conditions in our laboratory. Therefore, L. fermentum G3 (LF) and L. paraplantarum ST1 (LP) were used in the present study. The lactic acid bacteria (LAB) used in this study are classified as Generally Recognized As Safe (GRAS) and are non-pathogenic. All microbiological procedures were carried out under the supervision of qualified personnel, in compliance with established biosafety protocols. The characteristics of both inoculants are shown in Table 3. The LAB strains were inoculated in MRS broth and incubated at 30 °C for 48 h. Then, six experimental silages were prepared directly after cutting, with treatments arranged in a 2 × 3 factorial fashion. This experimental design included forage with or without additional urea and with or without additional LAB from either LP or LF. The applied dose was equal for both LAB, i.e. 106 CFU/g fresh matter (FM). An equivalent amount of sterilized distilled water was sprayed on the untreated and urea-treated sugarcane tops then thoroughly mixed. The urea addition was equivalent to 5 g/kg FM; this dose has been shown to inhibit the growth of yeast after aerobic exposure13. The experimental forages (∼200 g) were subsequently packed into plastic bags and vacuum-sealed using a vacuum packing machine. In total, 72 silos (3 replicates per treatment) were prepared and stored at room temperature (~ 30 °C) for 60 days for subsequent chemical analysis and assessment of aerobic stability.

Fermentation characteristics, chemical composition and microbial counts

After 60 days of ensiling, 18 silos (3 replicates/treatment) were opened. Subsample (20 g) was added with 70 ml of distilled water, mixed thoroughly and stored at 4 °C for 24 h. Then, the samples were filtered, and the pH was measured using a pH meter (SI analytics GmbH, Mainz, Germany). The filtrate obtained was used to analyze NH3-N concentration30, as well as the concentrations of organic acids, and ethanol. High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (Shimadzu Ltd., Kyoto, Japan) with a 30GX340EKN Vertex plus column (300 mm ⋅ 8 mm i.d; column temperature, 60 °C; flow rate, 0.80 ml/min, mobile phase 0.005 M H2SO4) (Knauer, Berlin, Germany) was used to determine the concentrations of organic acids, and ethanol.

The pre-ensiled materials and silage samples (150 g) were dried in a hot air oven at 60 °C for 48 h. Subsequently, the dried samples were ground to pass through a 1.0-mm sieve for determination of chemical composition and in vitro dry matter digestibility (IVDMD). Crude protein (CP, Nitrogen-Kjeldahl x 6.25) was determined using the standard AOAC methods31. Neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and acid detergent fiber (ADF) were determined according to the method of Van Soest et al.32, without the use of amylase, and were expressed exclusive of residual ash. The WSC content was determined by the method of Dubios et al.33.

Ten grams of the pre-ensiled materials and silage samples were assessed for bacteria enumeration. LAB were determined using MRS Agar (MRS; Acumedia Manufactures Inc., Baltimore, MD, USA) and incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 72 h. Yeasts and molds were counted on potato dextrose agar (PDA; Acumedia Manufactures Inc., Baltimore, MD, USA) and acidified with sterilized tartaric acid solution to pH 3.5. These agar plates were incubated at 30 °C for 72 h.

In vitro dry matter digestibility

The goats used in this experiment were obtained from the Department of Animal Science at Faculty of Agricultural Innovation and Technology, Rajamangala University of Technology Isan, Thailand. Rumen fluid was collected from three adult female goats (20–25 kg body weight, BW) before morning feeding, following a protocol approved by the Animal Ethical Committee of the Rajamangala University of Technology Isan (No. 39/2565). All animal experiments were complied with the ARRIVE guidelines and relevant laws and were carried out in accordance with the Ethics of Animal Experimentation of the National Research Council, Thailand.

The goats were fed with concentrate (1% of BW) and ad libitum of Leucaena and rice straw as a roughage source. Rumen fluid was collected from each donor animal prior to the morning feeding using a stomach tube connected to a vacuum pump and subsequently transferred to an Erlenmeyer flask. After collecting, the rumen fluid was filtered through four layers of cheesecloth and subsequently mixed with a buffer solution (1:2 v/v) and kept at 39 °C in a water bath under continuous CO2 flushing. Approximately 0.5 g DM of ground silage were precisely weighed into serum bottles, which were then closed with rubber stoppers and aluminium seal caps. Forty ml of rumen medium was injected into each sample bottle. Eighteen bottles (3 bottles per treatment) were prepared and incubated in a water bath at 39 °C for 72 h. Each bottle was opened and filtered through a glass filter crucible. Then, the crucible was dried at 105 °C in a forced air oven for 24 h and weighed to determine the IVDMD34.

Microbial community

The content of DNA was amplified by the bacterial 16 S rRNA primers targeting the V3–V4 regions of 341 F (CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG) and 805R (GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC). The resulting amplicons were labeled with different sequencing adaptors using the QIAseq 16 S/ITS Region Panel Sample Index PCR Reaction (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). DNA library quality and quantity were evaluated using the DeNovix QFX Fluorometer and QIAxcel Advanced (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), respectively. Sequencing was performed on an illumina Miseq600 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Bioinformatic analysis was performed using DADA2 (v1.20) for quality filtering (with standard parameters), amplicon sequence variant (ASV)35 inference (using default settings), and taxonomic classification based on the SILVA database (v138.1)36. Subsequent microbiome analyses, including taxonomic profiling, alpha diversity, and beta diversity assessments, were conducted using the phyloseq package (v1.36.0) in R. Alpha diversity indices (Observed, Chao1, Shannon, and PD whole tree) were computed using the DADA2 software. For beta diversity, non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity and principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) were plotted using Phyloseq data. Pairwise comparison of alpha diversity indices (Observed, Chao1, Shannon, and PD whole tree) was calculated using the Kruskal-Wallis test (P < 0.05). Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was performed to evaluate significant differences in beta diversity among groups at P < 0.05.

Assessment of aerobic stability

The aerobic stability of experimental silage was determined as described by Liu et al.37. Briefly, after 60 days of ensiling, 54 silos (6 treatments×3 exposure days×3 replicates) were opened, and the silage mass was transferred into sterilized 600-ml glass bottles without compaction and allowed to be exposed to air for 5 days at room temperature (~ 30 °C). To prevent drying and dust contamination, each bottle was covered with two layers of cheesecloth. During aerobic exposure on days 1, 3, and 5, three replicated silages of each treatment were sampled. The samples were used to determine the pH value, yeast and mold counts.

Statistical analysis

All data were subjected to ANOVA using SAS 9.2 (SAS Inst. Inc., Cary, NC, USA). The model used was Yij = µ + Ui + LABj + (U × LAB)ij + eij, where Yij represents the response variable, µ is the overall mean, Ui = urea (i = with or without), LABj = lactic acid bacteria (j = none, LP or LF), (U × LAB)ij = the interaction term between U and LAB and eij = residual error. Tukey’s test was used to identify significant differences among the variables. Statistical significance was declared at P < 0.05.
