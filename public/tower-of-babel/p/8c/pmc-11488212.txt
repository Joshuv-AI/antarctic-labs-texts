Applicability of the adjusted morbidity groups algorithm for healthcare programming: results of a pilot study in Italy
Authors: Roberta Papa, Francesco Balducci, Giulia Franceschini, Marco Pompili, Marco De Marco, Josep Roca, Rubèn González-Colom, David Monterde
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPopulation-based Health Risk Assessment (HRA) tools are strategic for the implementation of integrated care. Various HRA algorithms have been developed in the last decades worldwide. Their full adoption being limited by technical, functional, and economical factors. This study aims to apply the Adjusted Morbidity Groups (AMG) algorithm in the context of an Italian Region, and evaluate its performance to support decision-making processes in healthcare programming.MethodsThe pilot study used five Healthcare Administrative Databases (HADs) covering the period 2015–2021. An iterative semi-automated procedure was developed to extract, filter, check and merge the data. A technical manual was developed to describe the process, designed to be standardized, reproducible and transferable. AMG algorithm was applied and descriptive analysis performed. A dashboard structure was developed to exploit the results of the tool.ResultsAMG produced information on the health status of Marche citizens, highlighting the presence of chronic conditions from age 45 years. Persons with high and very high level of complexity showed elevated mortality rates and an increased use of healthcare resources. A visualization dashboard was intended to provide to relevant stakeholders accessible, updated and ready-to-use aggregated information on the health status of citizens and additional insight on the use of the healthcare services and resources by specific groups of citizens.ConclusionThe flexibility of the AMG, together with its ability to support policymakers and clinical sector, could favour its implementation in different scenarios across Europe. A clear strategy for the adoption of HRA tools and related key elements and lessons learnt for a successful transferability at the EU level were defined. HRA strategies should be considered a pillar of healthcare policies and programming to achieve person-centred care and promote the sustainability of the EU healthcare systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20398-9.Keywords: Health Risk Assessment, Adjusted morbidity groups, Integrated Care, Healthcare Administrative databases, Multimorbidity, Chronic diseases

Introduction

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) represent a major burden for European societies, causing about 90% of deaths [1] and more than 80% of Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) [2] with a cost of 115 billion euros annually [3]. In Italy, NCDs afflict about 16% of the entire population, especially older people, often assisted through outpatient and long-term care services, that account for 32% and 11% of the national health expenditure, respectively [4]. Managing NCDs effectively is a priority for European, national, and regional governments, which can be achieved by reorienting healthcare services toward primary care and integrated care models [5, 6]. Population-based Health Risk Assessment (HRA) strategies could facilitate changes in the healthcare systems [7–9] by: (1) identifying [10] and predicting [11] the social-health needs of the population; (2) determining the expected utilization of the health care resources [12]; (3) supporting the healthcare professionals in the clinical decision-making processes [13] ; (4) fostering the adoption of integrated care [7]; and (5) improving the equity of the healthcare system [14]. Various HRA strategies and tools have been developed worldwide in the last decades [13, 15], taking advantage of the available health administrative databases (HADs).

Among the Italian regions, Marche, is affected by a high presence of both older people and NCDs [16]. These conditions highlighted the need to regionally implement HRA strategies to manage chronic patients, providing the health and social services they need and, thus, improving their quality of life. Based on this context, the Joint Action on the “implementation of person-centred integrated care supported by digital technologies” (JADECARE) ( [17]) aimed to support the transformation of health systems by transferring good practices through validated methodologies [18], incorporating them into national policies, to ensure their long-term sustainability. In the framework of JADECARE project, Marche Region (Italy) acted as a Next Adopter aiming to implement the HRA tool Adjusted Morbidity Group (AMG) algorithm [13, 19] part of the good practice Open innovation hub on ICT-supported integrated care services for chronic patients of Catalonia (Spain). The main objective of the study is to implement the AMG tool and assess its performance to support decision-making processes in healthcare programming. Also, this study aims to test and evaluate the applicability of AMG in the Italian framework.

Background

Health Risk Assessment tools and adoption challenges

At international level, the most frequently implemented population-based HRA tools are the Charlson Comorbidity index [20], the Chronic Disease Score (CDS) [21, 22], the number of chronic diseases (CDs) [23, 24]; the Clinical Risk Groups (CRG) [25] and the Adjusted Clinical Groups (ACG) System [26]. Although these tools rely on routinely available data, their full operation could be limited by functional factors - they are calibrated on the American population features and healthcare system’s structure (15), data sources (e.g., only drug prescriptions), and outcomes (e.g., only1-year mortality) [27]. Moreover, the adoption of these tools is often hampered by inadequate funding mechanisms, technological issues (e.g., interoperability), and regulatory aspects related to data protection and privacy [28, 29].

In light of these challenges, the Spanish Health Ministry together with the Catalan public health commissioner (CatSalut) developed the AMG algorithm [13, 19]. AMG tool is a data-driven, more interpretable, and affordable alternative that outperform the prevailing HRA solutions in different facets [30, 31], especially for chronic and geriatric population [12]. Acting as a morbidity grouper, AMG quantifies patients’ disease burden for each disease state by considering the number and complexity of concurrent disorders. Subsequently, these weights are aggregated [32]. Differently from other tools, it employs disease-specific weightings obtained from statistical analyses that factor in mortality rates and healthcare resource utilization, facilitating quick adaptation to different territories [13]. AMG is currently used in Spain for policy and clinical purposes, such as data analysis on health and resources consumption/allocation, benchmarking between health facilities, identification and integrated care management of complex/at risk patients [19]. These characteristics, together with the possibility to adapt the algorithm to the regional context of Marche, were the main driver for the current application.

National healthcare system organization in Italy and the Marche context

The Italian National Healthcare System (Servizio Sanitario Nazionale, SSN) provides universal coverage at the point of service. The national care benefits (i.e., Essential Levels of Care, Livelli Essenziali di Assistenza, LEAs) are established by the central government, allocating the necessary resources to the Regions, which are responsible for planning, delivering and monitoring the healthcare services at the local level [33]. General practitioners (GPs) and paediatricians are independent contractors and act as gatekeepers to higher levels of care. The recent health reforms introduced e-health tools, such as e-prescription, Electronic Health Record (EHR), and telemedicine [33]. The management of health data is done through the New Health Information System (Nuovo sistema informativo sanitario, NSIS), introduced in 2003 for measuring the quality, efficiency and appropriateness of care, and guaranteeing the uniform provision of the LEAs [33]. NSIS represents the most important national health database, collecting all available regional Healthcare Administrative Databases (HADs) on diagnoses, procedures, resource usage, and the costs of the healthcare services [34]. HADs are nowadays used also for epidemiological and research purposes [35]. Additional data are provided by two nationwide surveillance systems, PASSI (Progressi delle Aziende Sanitarie per la Salute in Italia - Progresses in Local Health Units for Health in Italy) and PASSI d’Argento [36], collecting information about health status, risk factors for NCDs, and adherence to preventive measures of adults (18–69 years old) and older people (64 years and over), respectively.

This study was carried out in the Italian central region of Marche, about 1,5 million inhabitants, characterized by older population (25% aged 65 years and over), a life expectancy at birth (79.7 years M; 84.4 F) higher than the Italian average, and high presence of NCDs (39% of citizens have at least one NCD; 21% two or more) [16]. The Regional Healthcare system includes 5 local health authorities, one university hospital and a National Institute focused on care and research on aging [37]. Strategies and care pathways are in place for the management of NCDs (e.g., the Regional Diabetes Network), also supported by e-health tools (e.g., e-prescription, EHR, telemedicine). In this context, the need to manage the high burden of NCDs, as well as the obligation to fulfil the requirements of the Italian health regulations on healthcare planning and monitoring, were the triggers to test the application of the AMG tool [38].

State of the art in Italy on HRA

In Italy, population HRA is a national challenge, encouraged by the coexistence of several ongoing programs and projects (e.g., National Recovery and Resilience Plan, National Chronicity Plan, Ministerial Decree No. 77 on new standards of community healthcare services, PONGOV Cronicità) [39–43]. A recent National project provided an overview of experiences and HRA tools developed in Italy [43]. At national level, a collaborating network elaborated and validated a Multisource comorbidity score based on different HADs, to predict mortality, hospital admissions and healthcare costs [44]. At regional level, some Regions and autonomous provinces (Veneto, Bolzano, Umbria, Calabria and Lombardy) [42, 45] implemented available commercial tools (i.e., ACG, CRG). Other regions (Lombardy, Emilia Romagna, Apulia, and Piedmont) developed their own tools, such the Chronic Related Group (CReG) classification [46], the Risk-Emilia Romagna (Risk-ER) tool [47], the Drug Derived Complexity Index [48] and a Predictive model for frailty [49], respectively.

The above experiences present heterogeneous levels of application in the daily practice, different levels of maturity, and have been elaborated mainly to address local needs, i.e., outcomes related to specific groups of population, using a wide range of data sources coming from different healthcare settings [43]. For example, the CReG algorithm, a classification system based on health costs, was developed using as source population the patient group attended by one Primary Care provider [13], as the predictive model for frailty, which addresses only older people [49]. Others are based on some specific HADs, i.e., drug prescriptions, in the case of The Drug Derived Complexity Index [48]. These aspects, together with legal barriers linked to privacy issues [50, 51], hampered the transferability and scalability of these tools to other contexts.

Although Marche has not yet implemented an official HRA tool, it participated in HRA-related studies [52–54]and presents a quite mature context [55] to test the application of a stratification algorithm such as the AMG.

Methods

The study adhered to the STROBE [56] guidelines for comprehensive reporting of observational research.

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational study was carried out in the Marche region (Italy). Detailed descriptions of the approach used is provided elsewhere [38].

The study was carried out using a Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) methodology [57] and included 5 phases: (1) feasibility study; (2) data preparation and in-depth analysis with key experts; (3) launching of the AMG tool; (4) data analysis and evaluation; (5) development of a dashboard for data visualization. All along the process, Marche and Catalan team exchanged information, instructions, results and learnings. The feasibility study, carried out in January 2022, showed that AMG is applicable in the regional context and that the health data are of adequate quality as needed by the AMG tool [38]. This paper focuses on the subsequent phases of the implementation, carried out from April 2022 to June 2023. The methodological workflow is showed in Fig. 1.

The methodological workflow of the study

Data and data sources

Data used for this study are derived from the regional HADs, managed by the Regional Health Agency of the Marche Region [17]. HADs contain information on healthcare services provided to citizens by public and private accredited providers. The following five HADs were included: (a) Exemptions from healthcare co-payment for citizens affected by specific diseases and socio-economic conditions; (b) Hospital discharge records occurred in public and private hospitals; (c) Emergency and Urgency care; (d) Home care, comprising health and socio-health interventions provided by a multidisciplinary team of professionals (e.g. GP, physician, nurse, physiotherapist, dietitian, social worker, etc.) at the patient’s home, under an individual planned programme; (e) Regional Health Service Registry, including demographic data and vital status of citizens living in the Marche region. Data of HADs are yearly available, after an average period of about 5–6 months for validation and consolidation. Data for this study are restricted to inhabitants of Marche region and the period from 2015, ensuring a high level of completeness and quality, to 2021, representing the most recent data available at the start of the study. Overall, these procedures collected more than 5.6 million diagnoses.

Variables

The AMG tool uses health information (i.e., diagnosis) of the population to generate the health risk strata pyramid of the general population considering multimorbidity and complexity of the diseases [58]. Input data are diagnostic codes catalogued through the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codebook (versions: ICD-9-CM), date of diagnosis, sex, birthdate and subject identity code. The birth date and sex are used for validation purposes only.

The output includes a string for each citizen with a set of information. Each citizen is assigned to a one out of seven morbidity groups (healthy; acute; pregnancy and childbirth; chronic diseases in 1 system; chronic diseases in 2–3 systems; chronic diseases in > 3 systems; active neoplasms) and one out of five complexity levels (from 1 to 5, except for the healthy group), for a total of 31 groups. The Morbidity Burden score is a continuous measure that quantifies a patient’s disease burden by considering both the number and complexity of coexisting conditions. It is an additive metric, summing disease-specific weights associated with all diagnoses a patient has. For chronic conditions, the score captures the cumulative burden over the patient’s lifetime. In the case of neoplasia, only diagnoses made within the 2 years preceding the assessment are included, while for acute conditions, the timeframe is restricted to 1 year prior to evaluation. Each condition is weighted based on its contribution to mortality and healthcare resource utilization, derived from population-wide statistical analyses. The score is used to stratify patients into distinct risk categories across percentiles—50th, 80th, 95th, and 99th—which correspond to healthy, low-risk, moderate-risk, high-risk, and very high-risk groups. These cut-off points were deliberately chosen to reflect the realities of healthcare resource allocation in Europe, where the top 5% of the population accounts for nearly 50% of total healthcare costs. Additional measures are the number of chronic diseases and anatomical systems involved, the presence of 15 major chronic conditions (hypertension, diabetes, chronic ischaemic heart disease, stroke, arthrosis, heart failure, chronic renal failure, COPD, arthritis, dementia, depression, chronic pain, osteoporosis, cirrhosis, HIV), and a descriptive clinical label.

Data processing and analysis

Data preparation and AMG application

The data preparation phase aimed to bring the source data into the format required by the algorithm. The procedure, while respecting a homogeneous structure, was carried out separately for each HAD, in order to adapt to their different specificities. Firstly, for each database, we developed a standardised procedure to extract relevant data. Secondly, two rounds of quality checks were conducted to verify the completeness of the data, identify and address any missing and inconsistent data. A short report was produced for each HAD and follow-up meetings were organised with regional experts to validate the procedure. This resulted in additional analysis, revision of the procedure, and identification of potential activities to improve data quality (e.g., training of healthcare professionals, revision of the data collection procedures, software updates, communication and stakeholder engagement). The standardised procedures have been revised and run again to create one input data file (i.e., diagnosis) for each HAD. Thirdly, an additional standard procedure was developed to merge the input files into one bigger database, following the AMG technical requirements (e.g., format of data, codes of variables, data separator, etc.). Diagnoses are linked to each citizen, using a univocal pseudo-anonymized subject ID produced for each HAD. Specifically, the data were filtered to consider only inhabitants of Marche region being alive in a given year (i.e., 2021) (i.e., included in the Health Registry), which represents the reference period of calculation. Diagnoses are then attached to each citizen, so that those not having received any diagnosis in the reference year were still included in the analysis and subsequently coded by AMG as ‘healthy’. Finally, additional checks and data filtering were applied before launching the algorithm by activating the executable AMG file.

The software produces two files, one with the output data and one including the validation operations carried out during computations. A descriptive analysis was conducted to verify the correct operation of the AMG tool. In the last phase, a complete revision of the procedure was performed to produce a simplified and semi-automated process able to reproduce all the steps (extraction, elaboration, cleaning, formatting, launching) when needed. A devoted technical manual was produced to describe it in details (Supplementary material 1).

Analysis

Descriptive statistics are computed from the AMG output file, for the reference year 2021. The results are reported by region and province. Data are presented as frequencies, means (standard deviation, SD) or median (interquartile range, IQ). Percentiles are used to calculate risk pyramid strata, based on 50, 80, 95, 99 positions.

Health-related indicators (Table 1) are computed linking output data with the original databases through the individual codes, extracting and/or computing the necessary fields. Indicators are presented for the total population and by each pyramid strata, in order to relate the AMG classification with the health scenario in terms of morbidity burden and use of healthcare services. The procedures and analysis are carried out through the statistical software R (v. 4.2.3).

Dashboard

We elaborated the structure of a dashboard to be used as a support tool for stakeholders and citizens to visualize AMG results. Analysis of existing tools, including those developed by the Catalan hub was carried out. Main characteristics identified as important were the possibility to perform cross comparisons among areas and time and the integration of information on health status of the citizens with indicators on the use of healthcare services. A rapid review of (grey) literature and the analysis of existing indicators at local and national levels was carried out to define a list of healthcare indicators for the dashboard. The criteria of selection were indicators which are actually used, standardized and consolidated, developed for national and/or regional monitoring and planning, and allowing comparability across settings and geographical areas [13, 36, 43, 59]. The list of indicators was subsequently refined through consultations with a sample of five healthcare managers of the Regional Health Agency of the Marche Region, head of the Departments of key areas of interest for the study, such as Health Technology Assessment and biomedical technologies, administrative health databases and monitoring of the regional healthcare system, community and hospital-community integration, hospital and emergency care, pharmaceutical services. The selection criteria, in this case, were the priority for the first implementation of the dashboard (Table 1), their utility in clinical and programming terms, the availability of data and the possibility to do the data linkage with the AMG output data.

Dashboard

The proposed structure of the dashboard includes a list of variables to be calculated by a set of relevant indicators (Table 4), in order to provide information about the overall health status of the population (e.g., complexity levels, morbidity groups, chronic conditions). The sixteen health-related indicators (see Table S6 of Supplementary material 2) provide additional insight on the use of the healthcare services and resources by specific groups of citizens, e.g., those with high complexity or those affected by a specific chronic condition. Discussion

This study described the first attempt to transfer the AMG tool, developed in Spain, into another EU region, namely Marche region (Italy). It provides details about the methodology used and the technical procedures needed to use heterogeneous multi-source health databases as input for the AMG algorithm, as is the case for the Italian HADs. Moreover, it contributes to the international discussion on promoting the adoption of HRA strategies as essential public health tools that can lead to significant improvements in health outcomes and operational efficiency, by providing a uniform approach to patient-centred care and morbidity classification. Overall, the results confirmed the applicability of the AMG algorithm in Marche region and its potential for supporting decision-makers in the healthcare services programming, and allowed to identify key factors that could influence the successful implementation and transferability of HRA strategies at the EU level. In addition, this study provided a concrete example of successful application of the JADECARE methodology [18, 38], which uses a theory-driven and pragmatic approach to facilitate the transfer of complex interventions across different contexts.

Technical challenges

The AMG algorithm was developed to address the need for an open, flexible, high- performance and transferable HRA tool. Indeed, AMG tool requires a small set of standardised patient-level information (i.e., diagnosis) which can be derived from multiple databases (e.g., hospitalizations, community services, primary care, etc.) and expressed through different international classifications. Building on previous recommendations on good practices of HRA strategies and key operational steps for its implementation [13], this study confirmed the flexibility and transferability of the AMG tool in a real-world scenario. Indeed, our study showed the ability to adopt, with the necessary adjustments, a regional strategy on HRA, using the standardized framework and the clear technical procedures offered by the AMG algorithm [38]. Although Italian HADs fitted the above requirements, their heterogeneous structure (i.e., multiple tables, variety of units, fields and coding) [60], different from the Spanish one, needed the development of an additional semi-automated process to merge the selected HADs through data linkage. The technical manual (Supplementary material 1) represents a key achievement of this study and a fundamental step to support the integration of the tool in the daily practice as well as its sustainability. The procedure has several advantages in terms of time saved, limited skills needed, and flexibility [61]. Moreover, as Italian HADs have the same structure across the regions, the procedure could be considered a good practice easily transferable to other contexts, for example to support the ongoing National projects in this field.

Clinical relevance of the risk strata

The results provided by the AMG tool are consistent and in line with other available data [16] in terms of the prevalence of chronic conditions (35% in our study vs. 40% in national self-reported data). AMG classified the Marche population into groups of increasing levels of complexity, reflected by increasing mortality, hospitalization, and use of healthcare services [19, 62, 63]. These results confirm that the Italian HADs appropriately identify chronic conditions and multimorbidity (Canova et al., 2020; Corrao et al., 2017), even though further analysis is needed to evaluate the tool’s performance towards a wider range of outcomes. Moreover, they show that AMG algorithm works properly in our context, highlighting the relevance of relying only on statistical criteria, to facilitate quick adaptation to different territories [13]. The quality of the health data sources [64], is a key aspect for developing and monitoring HRA strategies and public health interventions [65]. Although completeness and accuracy of Italian HADs have improved over time, differences among Italian Regions have been documented [35].

In this regard, Marche developed devoted activities to improve the quality of regional HADs in the last years, which clearly emerged during the data processing phase. Experts involved in the study suggested further improvement actions, such as changes in the collection procedures, training of the healthcare professionals, awareness campaigns, and monitoring activities [65].

Towards person-centred care

Information on the population’s health status and the use of healthcare services is usually partly available through fragmented sources [15]. For example, in Italy, data on chronic conditions are mainly provided by nationally standardized surveys (e.g., Passi and Passi d’Argento, Multipurpose Household Surveys) [36] on non-longitudinal representative samples of the regional population, whereas health-related indicators are calculated by the National Health Performance System from HADs. These data and indicators are usually provided in aggregate form (e.g., by region) and cannot be linked to perform further analysis. HRA tools such as AMG exploits the richness of HADs integrating different sources of information and summarizing complex and vast amount of data in easy-to-use and comparable data. This study call attention to the adoption of a population-health approach as done by the AMG tool [13] - such as through the coverage of the entire inhabitants of the region -, to get a comprehensive overview of the healthcare services provided to all citizens and, at the same time, facilitate the identification of needs and gaps. The proposed dashboard represents a simple tool for relevant stakeholders and citizens to consult the data. The indicators allow analysis on age- and sex-related pathologies and time-space comparisons. For example, it is particularly relevant to investigate health conditions and needs of citizens living in specific area (i.e., internal/rural zones), which are characterized by depopulation, high presence of older people, limits in the availability and accessibility of services [66, 67]. Most importantly, HRA tools promote a change of paradigm, from a system performance-oriented to a person-centred approach, which is fundamental for fostering the deployment of proactive care and integrated care [7–9].

This study defined key steps for the adoption of HRA tools. The in-depth analysis of the data, together with the involvement of experts and stakeholders, represent a solid basis for the sustainability of the actions. Integration of the tool into the regional infrastructure could be done through the semi-automated procedure, which allows updating the results when new HADs data are available. A detailed sustainability plan has been also developed, aligning it to the priorities set out in the Marche Regional Health and Social Plan 2023–2025.

However, full implementation of the tool, as currently is in Spain, is hindered by national legislation on the secondary use of health data [50, 51]. A clear legal framework is needed to exploit the potential of HRA tools as supporting instruments enabling both policymakers and healthcare professionals to strengthen the capacity of the healthcare systems.

Strengths and limitations

This study comes with some limitations. Results are preliminary and further analyses are needed to validate the AMG algorithm, such as the calculation of proper weights referred to the Italian population. The data sources (hospital discharges, emergencies, exemptions, home care) could underestimate certain chronic conditions managed through other care pathways. Specifically, we acknowledge the lack of primary care data, which is instead included in Spain, and out-of-pocket healthcare services. The proposed dashboard represents a methodological study, also based on the tools used in Catalonia for the programming and monitoring of the healthcare services. Further activities are needed to evaluate the feasibility of its integration in the Regional Healthcare System. Despite these limitations, this study developed, for the first time, a transferable procedure to dynamically manage multiple HADs for implementing HRA tools, namely AMG algorithm. It also provides relevant implications for researchers and decision-makers in the public health field.

Lessons learnt

This study confirms the relevance of transferring good practices across and within EU countries to promote the adoption of evidence-based innovations in the healthcare systems [18, 38, 68]. Some lessons learnt could be derived to promote the adoption of HRA strategies in the EU countries. Core elements of a successful implementation are the maturity of the adopting context (e.g., availability of devoted infrastructure), the collaboration with the tool’s owner, economic resources (e.g., devoted funds to perform the start-up activities), and the alignment with local, regional, national, EU priorities, policies, and initiatives. In light of these considerations, it is also extremely relevant that those oriented to apply HRA tools are able to: (i) guarantee the quality and the completeness of health data; (ii) collect information from multiple, and integrated databases; (iii) invest on technological/IT infrastructures, to effectively manage the data complexity, (iv) develop an interactive network of stakeholders, health policy managers and healthcare professionals, to pave the way for the adoption of the innovation.

Conclusions

The structured implementation process and the great flexibility of the AMG tool favoured its successful implementation in a different EU region context, such as Marche (Italy), characterized by both different healthcare system organization and a complex and heterogeneous HADs structure, thus showing great potential for further transferability in different scenarios across the EU. A clear strategy for the adoption of HRA tools is essential to enhance person-centred care and improving the citizens’ quality of life, while fostering the sustainability of the EU healthcare systems.

The increasing developments in the use of HADs for epidemiology and research purposes and the various ongoing initiatives at the EU level, such as the European Health data Space, represent milestones and precursors to an effective and fruitful implementation of HRA strategies and tools.
