Sociodemographic and disease-related factors associated with treatment adherence among patients with type 2 diabetes: a cross-sectional study in southern Iran
Authors: Zahra Hosseini, Nahid Shahabi, Amin Ghanbarnejad, Teamur Aghamolaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTreatment adherence in type 2 diabetes(T2D) is essential for glycemic control and preventing complications. The aim of the present study was to determine the status of adherence behaviors to diabetes treatment and identify sociodemographic factors associated with these behaviors in patients with type 2 diabetes in southern Iran.MethodsThis cross‑sectional study was conducted from November 2022 to January 2023 among patients with type 2 diabetes attending a diabetes clinic in Bandar Abbas. A total of 396 patients were included using systematic sampling. Data were collected using a validated researcher-made questionnaire that assessed sociodemographic characteristics and five treatment-adherence behaviors, including regular medication use, blood glucose monitoring, physician visits, dietary adherence, and physical activity. Associations between demographic and disease-related factors and the outcomes were assessed using multivariable logistic regression, and crude and adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were reported. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 24.ResultsParticipants (n = 396) had a mean age of 54.4 ± 11.5 years (range: 27–78). Among the treatment adherence behaviors, regular medication use showed the highest level of adherence (86.9%), while regular physical activity had the lowest level (49.7%). In the multivariable analysis, frequent medication use was associated with older age, higher education, higher economic status, and diabetes duration of 6–10 years. Frequent blood glucose monitoring was associated with female sex, older age, and diploma education. Frequent physician visits were associated with older age and longer diabetes duration. Dietary adherence was associated with university education, and frequent physical activity was associated with living alone.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that adherence to treatment among patients with T2Dvaries across different domains and is shaped by individual, social, and clinical factors. It seems that, Lifestyle behaviors such as diet and physical activity are largely influenced by social and environmental contexts, whereas healthcare‑seeking behaviors are more closely linked to disease severity and patients’ interaction with the health system.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes mellitus; Treatment adherence; diabetes self-management, Medication adherence, Physical activity, Cross-sectional study

Background

Diabetes is a chronic metabolic disorder with a global prevalence [1]. According to the International Diabetes Federation Atlas, currently 589 million adults (20–79 years) are living with diabetes worldwide. based on future trends in population growth, aging, and urbanization, the global number of people with diabetes will increase 45% to 853 million by 2050, with particularly large increases in the Africa (142%) and the Middle-East and North Africa (95%) regions [1]. Type 2 diabetes (T2D), which accounts for about 90 to 95% of people with diabetes, is influenced by various factors such as obesity, physical inactivity, stress, and aging [2, 3]. The prevalence of T2Din Iran was estimated at 10.8% [4].

Standard care for patients with T2Dincludes a combination of dietary therapy, regular physical exercise, appropriate use of glucose‑lowering medications, continuous blood‑glucose monitoring, and periodic medical check‑ups [5, 6]. These components reflect the multidimensional nature of diabetes self-management, in which adherence to one domain does not necessarily imply adherence to others. The management of chronic diseases such as diabetes is usually a long-term process, and its success depends not only on pharmacological therapy but also on adherence to multiple self-care behaviors recommended as part of standard care [7].

Treatment adherence refers to the extent to which individuals follow the behaviors and instructions recommended by their physicians for managing and treating their condition [8]. However, recent evidence shows that adherence to treatment among patients with diabetes is not satisfactory in many communities, and multiple factors may influence it [9, 10]. Poor adherence in patients with diabetes is associated with adverse outcomes, including poor glycemic control, increased incidence of microvascular and macrovascular complications, higher hospitalization rates, and considerable financial burdens on the healthcare system [11, 12].

Demographic characteristics such as age, gender, educational level, and economic status are among the factors that may influence health-related behaviors, particularly treatment adherence [3, 13–15]. These factors can shape patients’ treatment behaviors by affecting their level of awareness, access to health resources, social support, and their ability to manage the disease [16, 17]. Treatment-related behaviors may also vary according to the cultural, social, and healthcare system contexts of different regions. In southern areas of Iran, including the city of Bandar Abbas, specific socioeconomic characteristics, cultural differences, lifestyle patterns, and the hot and humid climate may influence the management of chronic diseases such as diabetes [18–20]. Findings of previous studies regarding the role of demographic factors in treatment adherence have not been entirely consistent, and variations have been reported across different social, cultural, and healthcare settings [21, 22]. Despite the significant burden of diabetes in Iran, there is limited evidence regarding the sociodemographic factors associated with multiple diabetes self-management behaviors among patients with T2D in southern Iran [23]. Therefore, the present study was conducted to assess five diabetes treatment-adherence behaviors and to examine their associations with sociodemographic factors among patients with T2D in southern Iran.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This analytical cross-sectional study was conducted between November 2022 and January 2023 at the diabetes clinic in Bandar Abbas, the capital of Hormozgan Province in southern Iran. The study population consisted of all individuals with an active medical record at the diabetes clinic who had been diagnosed with T2D.

The inclusion criteria were as follows:

Willingness to participate in the study and completion of the informed consent form.

Willingness to participate in the study and completion of the informed consent form.

Having an active medical file at the diabetes clinic with a documented diagnosis of T2D.

Having an active medical file at the diabetes clinic with a documented diagnosis of T2D.

A minimum of one year of diabetes treatment history.

A minimum of one year of diabetes treatment history.

Current residence in the city of Bandar Abbas.

Current residence in the city of Bandar Abbas.

The exclusion criterion was incomplete completion of the questionnaire by the participants.

Sample size

According to the study by Tanharo, the standard deviation of the treatment-adherence score was reported as 1.33 [24]. Assuming a 95% confidence interval, a statistical power of 80%, and an effect size of d = 0.5, the required sample size was estimated to be 344 participants.

To account for possible sample attrition (about 20%), the initial sample size was increased to 412 individuals. Finally, questionnaires were distributed to 412 individuals. After eliminating 16 incomplete questionnaires, 396 questionnaires (with a final response rate of 96.12%) were included in the final analysis.

Sampling was performed using the Systematic Random Sampling method. For this purpose, by referring to the patient files of the diabetes clinic and based on the case number, eligible individuals were systematically selected and entered into the study.

Data collection instrument

The questionnaire items were developed based on findings from the authors’ qualitative study. The initial items were then reviewed by experts for clarity and relevance before finalization [25].

The first part of the questionnaire focused on demographic information, including age, gender, marital status, educational level, employment status, socioeconomic status (SES), duration of diabetes, type of medication used, and smoking status.

The second part included five items assessing treatment-adherence behaviors, rated on a five-point Likert scale (strongly agree = 5, agree = 4, neutral = 3, disagree = 2, strongly disagree = 1).

The items covered the following behaviors: checking blood glucose, visiting a physician, diet, physical activity, and medication use.

To establish content validity, the questionnaire was reviewed by ten experts in health education and health promotion, internal medicine, and endocrinology, and their comments were used to revise the instrument. The CVR values were calculated using Lawshe’s method, and items with values below 0.62 were removed. The CVI was calculated based on Waltz and Bausell’s method, and items with values below 0.79 were revised or removed. To assess reliability, a test-retest method was applied. The questionnaire was administered twice to 22 individuals with characteristics similar to those of the study participants, with a two-week interval between the two administrations. Each item from the first test was compared with its corresponding item from the second test; items with a correlation coefficient above 0.7 were retained. The internal consistency of the scale was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.739.

Data collection

Data were collected from December 2022 to January 2023 during both morning and afternoon clinic hours using a researcher made questionnaire. The data collection process was carried out by the second author. As a native of the region and familiar with the local culture and dialect, the researcher was able to establish effective communication with participants and facilitate the data collection process. The questionnaires were completed in person at the clinic by patients with type 2 diabetes. Before completing the questionnaire, the purpose of the study and the method of responding to the questions were explained to the participants to ensure they had a clear understanding of the items. After receiving the necessary explanations and providing informed consent, participants proceeded to complete the questionnaire. In cases where participants were unable to complete the questionnaire independently due to illiteracy, low reading and writing skills, or visual impairment, the researcher read the questions aloud in a clear voice. The questions were presented in Persian or, when necessary, in the local dialect commonly spoken in the region to ensure full comprehension. The participants’ responses were then recorded by the researcher exactly as stated, without any interpretation or modification. On average, completing each questionnaire took approximately five minutes. Throughout the data collection process, the researcher remained present at the clinic to provide clarification whenever needed. This continuous presence helped improve the quality of the collected data and reduce potential response errors.

Ethical considerations

To ensure compliance with ethical principles and legal requirements, the data collection process began only after obtaining official approvals, written authorization, and an introduction letter from the Hormozgan University of Medical Sciences, followed by full coordination with the authorities of the diabetes clinic. At the first step, the researcher formally introduced herself to eligible participants and provided a complete explanation of the study’s objectives, importance, and procedures. All participants were assured that participation in the study was entirely voluntary and that refusal to participate or withdrawal at any stage would not affect their medical care or the services they received at the clinic. Written informed consent was then obtained from all individuals who agreed to take part in the study. To ensure confidentiality and protect participant privacy, it was emphasized that all collected information would remain strictly confidential and accessible only to the research team. Moreover, to guarantee anonymity, the questionnaires did not include names or personal identifiers; instead, identification codes were used.

This study, which forms part of a PhD dissertation, was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Hormozgan University of Medical Sciences with the ethics code IR.HUMS.REC.1400.377. All stages of the research were carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the national guidelines for ethics in biomedical research.

Data analysis

For describing quantitative variable, we used mean, SD, minimum and maximum. Also, frequency and percent were reported for categorical variables. In this research, 5 questions about Physical Activity, Diet, Medication use, Checking Glucose and Visiting Physician on 5-point Likert scale ranging from (0: not at all to 4: always) were asked from participants. These variables treated as outcome. For better interpretability, each variable converted into dichotomous outcome as “1: Frequent” and “0: Infrequent”. A cut‑off point of 3 was applied; scores greater than 3 were categorized as Frequent, whereas scores less than or equal to 3 were classified as Infrequent. The chi-square test was used. For investigating the association of demographic variables and disease related factors with outcome variables, odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval are reported. Variables with p < 0.2 in univariate analysis were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals were reported. All the analysis were done in SPSS 24.

Results

In this cross-sectional study, 396 patients with T2D were included. The mean age of participants was 54.4 ± 11.5 years, ranging from 27 to 78 years. Of the participants, 254 (64.1%) were female and 142 (35.9%) were male. Most participants were ever married (93.9%), had under-diploma education (54.8%), were non-smokers (68.2%), and lived with others (93.7%). Regarding employment status, 163 (41.2%) were housewives, 91 (23.0%) were retired, 64 (16.2%) were employed, 44 (11.1%) were freelancers, 29 (7.3%) were unemployed, and 5 (1.3%) were students. In addition, 225 participants (56.8%) had middle economic status. The duration of diabetes was 10 years or more in 165 patients (41.7%), and 207 (52.3%) used oral medication.

Discussion

The present study aimed to assess the level of adherence to different treatment‑related behaviors among patients with T2D and to identify the sociodemographic and clinical factors associated with these behaviors. In this study, five dimensions of diabetes treatment adherence including medication use, blood glucose monitoring, physician visits, adherence to dietary recommendations, and physical activity were examined among individuals with diabetes in Bandar Abbas. The findings indicated that the highest level of adherence was related to medication use, whereas the lowest level was observed for physical activity. In addition, several individual and clinical characteristics, including age, gender, economic status, employment status, duration of diabetes, and type of treatment, were associated with different levels of treatment adherence behaviors.

Medication adherence showed the highest level among the treatment-related behaviors in patients with type 2 diabetes. This may be because patients tend to adhere more to behaviors that are precise, easily trackable, and directly prescribed by physicians. Older patients were more likely to take their medications regularly than younger patients, a finding consistent with studies conducted in India [26] and Japan [27]. This association may reflect greater perceived risk, more contact with healthcare services, or more stable daily routines among older adults. Patients with higher education demonstrated greater adherence to regular medication use, which may reflect better health literacy and a stronger understanding of medication importance. Patients with high economic status reported more regular medication use, aligning with prior studies by Ekenberg [28] and Tanharo [24]. These findings underscore the role of financial capacity in securing uninterrupted access to medications, covering transportation and clinic visit costs, and maintaining continuous care. In the present study, patients with 6–10 years of diabetes had lower odds of medication adherence than those with a disease duration of ≤ 5 years. This finding contrasts with that of Tanharo et al. [24] in Iran, who reported better adherence among patients with longer disease duration. A possible explanation is that patients may experience treatment fatigue or reduced motivation over time. Differences in study populations and adherence assessment methods may further explain the inconsistent findings. Improvements in medication adherence may reduce complications and hospitalizations. Overall, these results indicate that medication adherence is influenced by age, education, socioeconomic status, and disease duration. Accordingly, intervention programs to improve adherence among patients with T2D in southern Iran should prioritize younger individuals and individuals with lower economic status.

The findings of the present study indicated that gender, educational level, and age were associated with self-care behaviors related to blood glucose control. In the present study, women had lower odds than men of adhering to regular blood glucose monitoring, which may reflect differences in self-care priorities, daily responsibilities, and contact with healthcare services. Although previous studies have not directly examined blood glucose monitoring, this pattern is consistent with earlier reports by Gisinger et al. [29] and Kaul et al. [30] on gender differences in diabetes care. Education also played a significant role; patients with a diploma demonstrated the highest rates of regular glucose monitoring. This suggests that health literacy facilitates understanding the importance of preventive behaviors and provides the necessary skills for accurate glucometer use [31]. Older patients were more likely to monitor their blood glucose regularly, which may reflect better self-management habits, consistent with a study conducted in India that reported higher self-care adherence among older adults with diabetes [32]. However, other study has reported inconsistent findings [33], suggesting that the association between age and self-monitoring of blood glucose may vary across healthcare settings and populations. A study among patients with type 2 diabetes in Iran also explored e-health literacy in the use of self-care mobile health applications [34]. Blood glucose monitoring also appears to depend on health literacy, so younger adults and those with lower education may benefit most from simpler education and better access to testing supplies. In addition, mobile health apps may help improve adherence, but only when patients have enough e-health literacy to use them comfortably and effectively.

Regarding regular physician visits, the results showed that older age and longer duration of diabetes were associated with this behavior. Middle‑aged and older adults sought medical care more frequently than younger groups, consistent with findings by Ashman [35], who reported that older individuals tend to visit physicians more often. This pattern is typically attributed to the increased need for health monitoring, management of potential complications, and closer treatment adjustment as age advances. Older patients also tend to have a heightened perception of risk, which can motivate more consistent follow up. Patients with a disease duration of more than 10 years were also more likely to attend medical appointments regularly. Reverse causality is also possible, as patients with more severe disease may require more frequent visits. This may reflect the progressive nature of diabetes, greater treatment complexity, and the need for closer clinical oversight over time [24]. Overall, physician visitation appears to be more strongly influenced by disease history and treatment intensity than by social factors. Irregular follow-up may delay treatment adjustment and worsen outcomes. Interventions should therefore focus on younger patients, newly diagnosed individuals, and smokers through regular phone reminders, proactive home‑based screening, and smoking‑cessation programs integrated into diabetes care.

Our results showed that educational level was the only significant factor influencing adherence to dietary recommendations. Patients with a university education were less likely to adhere to dietary guidelines. This finding may be related to demanding work schedules, greater reliance on convenience foods, or an overreliance on pharmacological treatment. Higher education does not necessarily translate into healthier behaviors; background factors such as lifestyle and workplace environment appear to play a more decisive role. Therefore, nutritional interventions for diabetic patients should go beyond general education and be tailored to lifestyle patterns and the social characteristics of different groups.

Consistent with the results, physical activity had the lowest level of adherence among all self-care behaviors. However, previous studies in Iran and Denmark have reported a broader set of factors associated with physical activity adherence, including education, marital status, income, residence, and gender [36, 37]. In our study, only living arrangement remained significant after adjustment. This difference may reflect variations in population characteristics, socioeconomic context, measurement approaches, and model specification. Patients living with others were less physically active than those living alone, which may be related to family responsibilities or a more sedentary lifestyle. In the family-oriented culture of southern Iran, household duties and limited privacy for exercise may be barriers. Individuals living alone often have greater flexibility for walking or engaging in home-based exercise [38]. Regular physical activity among patients with T2Dis known to improve glycemic control, reduce blood pressure, enhance mood, and support weight management [39]. Therefore, simple, practical empowerment programs and physical-activity counseling should be developed and implemented for these patients.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. Due to its cross-sectional design, causal relationships between sociodemographic factors and treatment adherence behaviors cannot be established. Additionally, the data were collected through self-reported single-item measures, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Participants may have tended to overreport their adherence to appear more consistent with medical advice. To minimize this, the researcher clearly assured them that their responses would remain confidential and anonymous and would not affect their medical care. Moreover, the study was conducted in a single diabetes clinic in Bandar Abbas, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other regions of Iran or different healthcare settings. It is important to note that this clinic serves as the major diabetes care center in the city of Bandar Abbas. Clinical variables such as HbA1c, BMI, diabetes complications, and comorbidities were not collected in this study, which may have limited the ability to examine their potential influence on adherence behaviors. In addition, some residual confounding may persist because not all potentially important confounding variables were measured.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicated that adherence to treatment-related behaviors among patients with T2D varies across different domains and is associated with a combination of individual, social, and clinical factors. Medication adherence was the highest level of compliance, whereas physical activity had the lowest. Sociodemographic characteristics and disease related factors including age, gender, educational level, economic status, occupation, duration of diabetes, and type of treatment, were associated with adherence behaviors. Lifestyle-related behaviors, such as diet and physical activity, were more strongly shaped by social and contextual factors, whereas behaviors related to healthcare utilization were more closely linked to disease severity and the level of interaction with the health system. Based on these findings, it seems that the development of targeted educational and behavioral interventions may help improve adherence to diabetes management, particularly among younger patients, men, and individuals with low socioeconomic status. Longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to clarify causal pathways and evaluate the effectiveness of adherence promoting strategies.
