Drivers of unnecessary diagnostic imaging for uncomplicated low back pain among family physicians: a theory-informed qualitative study
Authors: Yamile Jasaui, Sameh Mortazhejri, Shannon M Ruzycki, Shawn Dowling, D’Arcy Duquette, Kim Giroux, Kelly Mrklas, Gloria Wilkinson, Jeremy M Grimshaw, Andrea M Patey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMost major society guidelines recommend against diagnostic imaging for uncomplicated low back pain (LBP), referring to LBP that is not accompanied by symptoms that suggest a serious diagnosis, as it does not benefit patients. Despite this, diagnostic imaging for uncomplicated LBP remains common and contributes to overdiagnosis, overtreatment, and increased healthcare costs.AimThe purpose of this qualitative study was to use a framework to rigorously understand the factors influencing family physicians' decisions to order diagnostic imaging for uncomplicated LBP in our context.Design and SettingThis qualitative study used the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) to design and analyze semi-structured interviews with family physicians in Alberta, Canada, to examine drivers of diagnostic imaging for uncomplicated LBP.MethodsFamily physicians in Alberta were recruited to participate in semi-structured, one-on-one interviews through a provincial physician newsletter. After deductive coding, the study team generated belief statements for each section of coded text and inductive themes that described each group of belief statements were written. We identified the themes expected to be important barriers or facilitators to ordering imaging for uncomplicated LBP using predefined criteria.ResultsData saturation occurred after 13 interviews. There were five key themes related to unnecessary imaging for uncomplicated LBP: Beliefs about consequences, Skills, Environmental context and resources, Social influences, and Reinforcement. Participants used diagnostic imaging to reassure themselves that they had not missed an important diagnosis. Though participants considered the need to maintain the therapeutic relationship with their patients, most felt that this could be achieved without unnecessary diagnostic imaging.ConclusionsInterventions to reduce unnecessary diagnostic imaging in uncomplicated LBP should address physicians' fears of missing serious diagnosis rather than only providing recommendations.

Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) is a common symptom managed by primary care physicians, experienced by about 8% of people worldwide [1]. Uncomplicated LBP, also called mechanical LBP or nonspecific back pain in other settings, refers to LBP that is not accompanied by worrisome signs or symptoms that suggest a serious underlying diagnosis (often referred to as “red flag features”) [2], accounts for 85%–95% of all LBP [3]. Patients with uncomplicated LBP will often recover within 4–6 weeks without any investigation or intervention [3]. Based on the reassuring natural history of uncomplicated LBP, most major society guidelines recommend against imaging for uncomplicated LBP [4–8], as it does not benefit patients and can contribute to overdiagnosis, overtreatment, and increased healthcare costs [3, 9]. Despite this, unnecessary diagnostic imaging for uncomplicated LBP remains common [10], including in Canada, where imaging was ordered for about 25%–30% of patients with uncomplicated LBP in 2019–2020 [11].

Ordering unnecessary diagnostic imaging for uncomplicated LBP is a complex problem [12] that has been challenging to address [13, 14]. The drivers of unnecessary imaging for uncomplicated LBP include patient-level, physician-level, and systems-level factors [15, 16]. For example, physicians may feel that patients expect imaging for their LBP, worry about damaging the physician–patient relationship by refusing, and lack time to discuss the natural history of uncomplicated LBP with patients [12]. Many studies exploring drivers of unnecessary diagnostic imaging for uncomplicated LBP did not use a theory or framework to explore physician perspectives [17–19], which is necessary to ensure rigor and that important drivers are not missed when designing interventions to address low-value care [20]. In a single Canadian province, a framework-guided qualitative study with family physicians identified that lack of time to educate patients, a healthcare system that incentivizes imaging, fear of litigation and missing serious conditions, patient demands, and limited access to alternative treatments and educational materials contributed to their imaging of uncomplicated LBP [16]. Several of these drivers differ from those reported in studies performed in other settings, suggesting that the Canadian context may differ or that the use of a theoretical framework may have provided additional insights.

These drivers of unnecessary diagnostic imaging for uncomplicated LBP may differ across Canadian provinces, which would signal a need for local interventions to reduce unnecessary diagnostic imaging for uncomplicated LBP. Alternatively, if the drivers of unnecessary imaging are similar between provinces, a pan-Canadian approach to reducing imaging for uncomplicated LBP may be appropriate and less resource-intensive than multiple local initiatives. The purpose of this study was to use a theory-informed approach to understand the local factors influencing family physicians' decisions to order diagnostic imaging for uncomplicated LBP in Alberta, Canada.

Methods

This qualitative study used theory-guided, semi-structured interviews with family physicians in Alberta, Canada, to examine drivers of diagnostic imaging for uncomplicated LBP. A similar study exploring patient perspectives is reported elsewhere [21]. This study was approved by the University of Calgary Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board and is reported according to the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines [22].

Setting

Alberta, a Canadian province with a population of over 4.5 million people, operates under a single healthcare system offering public health insurance. Medically necessary diagnostic imaging tests are covered by public insurance and can only be ordered by physicians. Imaging requests are triaged locally, and wait times for nonurgent imaging can vary depending on the indication and clinical scenario. For example, the wait times for nonurgent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT) scans can range from weeks to a year [23]. Albertans can pay privately for expedited diagnostic imaging, though these tests would not be captured as unnecessary diagnostic imaging in Choosing Wisely Canada data, which uses publicly funded billing data.

Most Albertans live in an urban center with only 20% of the population living in rural settings [24]. About 75% of Albertans identify as white; South Asian, Filipino, and Black are the next most common racial groups in Alberta [24]. Compared to people living in other Canadian provinces, Albertans typically have a higher socioeconomic status [24]. Family physicians deliver most primary healthcare services, and people without a family physician typically access primary care through walk-in clinics or emergency departments. Approximately 85%–95% of Albertans have a family physician whom they regularly visit for their healthcare needs [25].

Theoretical framework

A theory-based approach to understand contextual drivers and barriers of behavior is crucial for developing effective strategies to promote guideline adherence and reduce unnecessary imaging [26–28]. The Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) [29] is often used to understand patient and physician-related barriers to adopting behaviors. The TDF was co-developed by psychologists and implementation scientists and thus offers a comprehensive method to identify and address the determinants of behaviors, in particular related to quality improvement and implementation interventions. The 14 domains of the TDF capture individual and systems-level factors that may influence physicians' behavior, including Knowledge, Skills, Social/professional role and identity, Beliefs about capabilities, Optimism, Beliefs about consequences, Reinforcement, Intentions, Goals, Memory, attention, and decision processes, Environmental context and resources, Social influences, Emotion and Behavioral regulation.

Participants

Eligible participants were family physicians practicing in Alberta who had seen one or more patients with uncomplicated LBP in the past 3 months. Family physicians were recruited through researcher's professional networks, advertisements in the Alberta College of Family Physicians newsletter and research portal, and chain sampling. We used purposive sampling to guarantee the inclusion of participants from rural and nonacademic (community) practice settings, as well as individuals with diverse lengths of clinical work experience to ensure representation of all Albertan contexts. Participants provided informed consent and received a $100 gift card as reimbursement for their time, in keeping with local reimbursement guidelines.

Interviews

The semi-structured interview guide was developed based on the study question using the TDF (version 2) [29], clinical expertise from frontline healthcare providers who see people with LBP (S.D.), and the study team's previous work [30] (Supplementary Appendix S1). Interviews were 1:1 and conducted via telephone by Y.J. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and de-identified. Sample size was determined using the 10 + 3 data saturation approach, meaning that 10 initial interviews were conducted, followed by an additional three interviews until no new concepts/ideas emerged from the interviews [31].

Data analysis

NVivo 12 (Burlington, MA) was used for coding and analysis. Two team members (Y.J. and S.M.) coded the interviews independently and in parallel by using deductive and inductive approaches. The initial coding guide was developed using the TDF domains [29] and previous studies of this study team [30]. Interrater reliability of initial coding was calculated using Cohen's Kappa coefficient; [32] codes with a Cohen's Kappa coefficient less than 0.80 (considered strong agreement [33]) were discussed between both team members to reach consensus. Disagreements were resolved by a third researcher with expertise in TDF (A.M.P.). Once all interviews were coded, one researcher (Y.J.) reviewed each code and generated belief statements, with each statement summarizing the core message expressed by each section of coded text. Similar belief statements were grouped together, and themes that described each group of similar belief statements were written. These themes were then reviewed by the second coder (S.M.), health psychologist (A.M.P.) and patient partners (G.W., D.D., K.G.) for agreement and validity.

Lastly, the team used the following predetermined criteria [30, 34] to identify whether each theme was expected to be notable or important for ordering unnecessary imaging for uncomplicated LBP (“key themes”): (i) Beliefs that contradicted guideline recommendations; (ii) Conflicting beliefs by the same participant on a single theme; or (iii) A belief that could significantly influence the target behavior. Quotations are corrected for grammar and identifying information is redacted, when necessary, and domains of the TDF are italicized.

Reflexivity

Y.J. and S.M. are research associates with training and experience in qualitative methods, including interviewing and analysis using the TDF. A.M.P. is a health psychologist with extensive experience in implementation science and theory, behavior change frameworks, and qualitative methods. The study team also included physicians (S.D., an emergency physician and S.M.R., an internist), patient partners with experience of advocacy in the Alberta healthcare system and/or LBP (D.A., K.G., G.W.) and implementation scientists with experience in qualitative work and low value care (K.M., J.G.).

Results

Overview

Thematic saturation was reached after 13 interviews. Most family physician participants were women (n = 9, 69.2%) and worked in urban settings (n = 8, 61.5%) (Table 1). All participants worked in community rather than academic settings, and the median time in clinical practice was 11 years (interquartile range [IQR] 10.5–18.0). Participants reported that between 5 to 40% of their patients had uncomplicated LBP. In total, 1039 utterances from the 13 interviews were coded into the 14 TDF domains. Interrater reliability for each interview ranged from “moderate agreement” (k = 0.54) to “almost perfect” (k = 0.88; mean 0.72 ± 0.09).

The analysis identified five domains from the TDF that influenced the ordering of unnecessary imaging for uncomplicated LBP among physicians in our setting (Fig. 1). These were as follows: Beliefs about capabilities, Beliefs about consequences, Environmental context and resources, Social influences, and Behavioral regulation. These domains and the key themes written for each are explored further below. Additional themes that did not meet our predefined criteria for importance were: Knowledge, Skills, Social/professional role and identity, Optimism, Reinforcement, Intentions, Goals, Memory, attention, and decision processes, and Emotion.

Missing serious diagnosis despite absence of red flag features

Many participants mentioned potential adverse patient outcomes and/or litigation for the exceptional patient who could have benefited from imaging despite having no red flag features (Beliefs about consequences). For example, one participant (P02) shared, “we practice population-based medicine, so the guidelines are driven by what's best for most people, so there's always going to be that one person or small number of people that you didn’t order a test for … but they would have benefited from.” Most participants shared similar sentiments: “You might miss a zebra… you might miss a very rare finding” (P12). In particular, one participant felt that “you can’t generalize everybody into uncomplicated low back pain… there are so, so, so many different kinds of low back pain and they don’t all fit into one nice model… I think the drawbacks of not imaging outweigh the benefits of not imaging sometimes” (P05). Some also expressed uncertainty when assessing older patients or those with atypical presentations, as one participant noted, “the challenges that I'm seeing is [in] patients in their older age… they have additional symptoms… that sometimes requires further work up” (P11). This uncertainty occasionally led to precautionary imaging, even when no clear red flags were present. Despite these concerns, only one participant had encountered a patient who had an adverse outcome from not having imaging earlier; most participants made similar statements to a participant who shared that “clinically, what I see is the majority of people do get better and don’t need any imaging and so [the guideline recommendations] seem to bear out in my clinical practice as well” (P09, Reinforcement). Similarly, a few participants could vaguely refer to “some legal cases where an older individual comes in and no x-ray was ordered; it turns out to be cancer” (P06), though no participant had any firsthand experience of this (Reinforcement).

Missing serious diagnosis due to missing red flag features

In addition to missing serious diagnosis in patients without red flag features, participants worried that they would miss a red flag feature on history or physical exam and not order imaging for a patient who needed it (Beliefs about consequences). Many participants worried that they “might miss something that didn’t get picked up on the history and physical that the imaging might pick up” (P10; Beliefs about capabilities, Beliefs about consequences). These concerns contrasted earlier statements by all participants who felt that the assessment of LBP were very basic Skills that are “a part of [everyone's] medical school training or family medicine residency training” (P02). These skills included “good clinical skills… tak[ing] a good history and a good physical exam” (P01) and a “need to know the anatomy” (P05).

Patient harms from imaging

Participants listed multiple patient harms from unnecessary imaging (Beliefs about consequences). Many cited unnecessary radiation exposure, and others noted that imaging might alter how patients perceive their symptoms by making them “less likely to start conservative therapy right away” (P03) and by framing their symptoms as “a problem for the back as opposed to a chronic pain” (P02). Participants also highlighted the risks of incidental findings, which could lead to patient anxiety and unnecessary follow-up testing. As one participant shared “…so now you tell the patient they have arthritis in their back, and now they’re really worried… “Oh maybe I shouldn’t walk so much,’… or “Oh I shouldn’t do any [weight] lifting, I shouldn’t go to the gym anymore”” (P06). Despite these concerns, some participants felt that imaging could be helpful; for example, one shared that “when I get the reports back on x-rays, MRIs, and CT scans, I like that information… some of it maybe is overcalling things or nonspecific, but at least we have more information on those patients who I’m struggling with” (P05) and another felt, “we might be missing a piece of information that could potentially provide more information” (P10).

System and resource availability

Participants indicated that accessibility, ease of ordering, and convenience may increase the likelihood of image (Environmental context and resources). One participant explained, “I have x-ray available to me right next door, but my patients would have to travel 100 km to get a CT or MRI” (P02). Limited access to multidisciplinary teams also shaped management choices, as one participant stated, “Better access to multidisciplinary health teams, social worker, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, exercise therapists are the team which is actually going to help the best” (P01; Behavioral regulation, Environmental context and resources). Time constraints had little influence on imaging decisions, though a few participants felt that “you have to take more time to convince patients that they don’t need an x-ray” (P09). Others felt that avoiding imagine saved time by “not hav[ing] to read another report” (P02), or not needing to explain incidental findings to patients (Environmental context and resources; Beliefs about consequences).

Loss of therapeutic relationship

Although patient requests for imaging did not appear to be a major driver, participants still worried that refusing imaging could “upset the patient” (P03) or “damage the [physician–patient] relationship” (P08; Beliefs about consequences, Social influences) but emphasized the role for “communication skills so [the patient] can understand why you feel it's not required to order imaging, so they can understand and feel comfortable with that” (P07; Skills). A few participants stated that they would order an unnecessary test if the patient was insistent: “If the patient is going to pay for an MRI, then I will order it. If a patient is in excruciating pain and demands an x-ray, I might order it,” (P05; Social influences). However, most emphasized communication and education as key strategies to reduce unnecessary imaging (Behavioral regulation). For example, one participant highlighted the importance of active listening, stating “it's important to make sure they feel heard… I'm not saying no forever” (P13). Another underscored patient education, explaining “I think as long as I go through very clearly what the specific red flags or indications are [for imaging] they are usually comfortable coming back if those things develop and get imaging” (P08; Behavioral regulation). Finally, participants also promoted follow-up to reassure patients and avoid unnecessary imaging “I'll always ask for a follow-up of treatment, so if they’re not getting better, then they know they can back to me and we can discuss it again” (P02; Behavioral regulation).

Discussion

Summary

This theory-driven qualitative study of drivers of unnecessary diagnostic imaging for patients with uncomplicated LBP among family physicians in Alberta suggests that the decision to order imaging is primarily driven by physician concerns of missing a critical diagnosis despite a lack of red flag features. These concerns were mentioned despite most participants also being aware of peer-reviewed evidence and guideline recommendations that reinforce that there is no benefit from diagnostic imaging for patients with no red flag features. Similarly, participants worried that they would misdiagnose a patient with uncomplicated LBP, even though all participants felt that the assessment of uncomplicated LBP was a basic set of skills taught in medical school and family medicine residency. Physicians were confident in their relationship-building and communication skills and did not feel that time pressures limited their ability to reassure patients who requested imaging, though some participants stated they would order unnecessary diagnostic imaging for patients who insisted that they had imaging. While participants felt that avoiding unnecessary costs and radiation were benefits of not ordering imaging, these were not strong incentives to avoid imaging. Education played a key role in managing patient expectations, with strategies like providing handouts and follow-ups supporting conservative management, though access to multidisciplinary teams could help reduce reliance on imaging by offering alternative management strategies.

Strengths and limitations

This study shares the known limitations of most qualitative studies. First, the study population includes only family physicians working in Alberta, which limits the transferability of these findings to other settings or specialties. Participants likely had an increased interest in the management of LBP compared to physicians who did not respond to requests to participate. Including perspectives from other healthcare professionals, such as physiotherapists, emergency medicine physicians, or surgeons, might provide additional insights into the factors influencing imaging decisions. Though the TDF captures physician perspectives of patient-level factors that drive unnecessary diagnostic imaging, patient perspectives of diagnostic imaging for uncomplicated LBP have been described elsewhere [21].

Comparison with existing literature

A systematic review of qualitative studies found that the fear of missing a serious cause of uncomplicated LBP despite the absence of red flag features is a commonly identified driver of unnecessary imaging among physicians [15]. A similar qualitative study of family physicians in Newfoundland and Labrador, performed by our team, identified the use of diagnostic imaging as a “fail-safe” and was also an important driver of unnecessary imaging [16]. Our results add granularity to these concerns by differentiating between a fear of missing an important diagnosis in patients with uncomplicated LBP and a fear of incorrectly classifying a patient as having uncomplicated LBP due to red flag features that were missed on history or exam. Physicians’ fear of missing serious diagnoses appears to contradict our finding that most participants were confident in their knowledge and skills for identifying uncomplicated LBP. However, this finding emphasizes a potential gap between knowledge and emotions that should be addressed in physician-facing interventions to reduce uncomplicated LBP. Further, participants in our study emphasized how unnecessary imaging could harm patients by delaying appropriate care, leading to incidental findings, and unintentionally encouraging patients to avoid exercise. Interventions to reduce unnecessary imaging should address both concerns, emphasize the harms of unnecessary imaging, and should focus on reassuring physicians rather than just providing information.

Unlike other settings [16], most physicians in our study expressed a high level of confidence in their ability to manage uncomplicated LBP without imaging, in part due to their familiarity with guidelines. This confidence may contribute to the decreasing rate of unnecessary diagnostic imaging seen in Alberta compared to other Canadian provinces; [11] a qualitative study that compared American physicians with lower rates of unnecessary imaging to those with higher rates of unnecessary imaging found that physicians who ordered less unnecessary imaging had greater knowledge of and confidence in guideline recommendations [35]. Patient requests for imaging was an important driver for family physicians in Newfoundland and Labrador [16] but was not as emphasized by participants in our study, who generally felt that their communication skills allowed them to address these requests without ordering unnecessary tests and believed avoiding imaging ultimately saved time. Similarly, one study reported that patient requests for imaging were comparable among physicians with both high and low imaging rates, but those who ordered fewer tests felt more confident navigating these requests [35]. This may suggest that interventions to address physician communication skills around unnecessary imaging for uncomplicated LBP should not be the sole focus of interventions to address patient-level drivers of unnecessary imaging.

Beyond physician decision-making, system-level influences also shaped imaging practices. Some participants noted that better access to multidisciplinary teams, comprised of physiotherapists, social workers, and other allied health professionals, would be the most effective way to manage LBP conservatively. This finding aligns with prior research showing that multidisciplinary care can lead to better patient outcomes, significantly decrease the use of imaging, and reduce future healthcare utilization and costs [36–39]. However, limited access to these resources often meant physicians relied on imaging as a fallback when nonimaging options were not readily available.

Implications for future research and practice

Based on our findings, effective strategies to reduce unnecessary imaging for uncomplicated LBP in Alberta should target the specific factors driving current imaging practices among family physicians. These include fears of missing an important red flag sign or symptom and the possibility of a rare, serious diagnosis in a patient with uncomplicated LBP, despite physician’s confidence in their skills and the guidelines. Interventions could highlight the more common patient harms from unnecessary imaging, such as avoiding exercise and increased patient anxiety over clinically unimportant findings. Tools and resources that support physician–patient communication may also be beneficial. Emphasizing why imaging is often unnecessary can prevent later discussions of incidental findings and reduce time spent reviewing imaging results. Interventions should not simply disseminate guideline recommendations, as knowledge of these recommendations is high, and lack of knowledge is not an important driver of unnecessary imaging among family physicians in Alberta. In addition to physician-focused efforts, addressing system-level barriers such as improved access to physiotherapy and multidisciplinary teams is equally important. Lastly, the differences between drivers of unnecessary imaging for uncomplicated LBP among physicians in Alberta and other settings suggest that an evaluation of local drivers should be undertaken before designing and implementing an intervention to reduce diagnostic imaging in other settings.
