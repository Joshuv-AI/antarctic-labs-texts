Leptospirosis Seroprevalence in Humans and Livestock of East Africa: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis (2000–2024)
Authors: Alebachew Tilahun Wassie, Andnet Yirga Assefa, Yihenew Getahun Ambaw, Solomon Keflie Assefa, Simachew Getaneh Endalamew
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLeptospirosis is a rapidly spreading and neglected zoonotic disease, whose transmission is interlinked between animals, humans and the environment. Even though leptospirosis is an endemic disease in East Africa, there is no updated information regarding the overall seroprevalence in humans, cattle, goats and sheep. This systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to estimate the pooled seroprevalence of leptospirosis and associated factors among cattle, humans, goats and sheep in East Africa (from 2000 to 2024). Upon literature search from databases and registries, 36 articles reporting the seroprevalence of leptospirosis in humans, cattle, goats and sheep were included for the final meta‐analysis. The pooled seroprevalence was higher in cattle at 23% (95% CI: 17.2–27.01), followed by 19.9% in goats (95% CI: 2.7–68.9) and 12.5% in humans (95% CI: 7.05–211). Regarding countries, there was the highest seroprevalence of leptospirosis in Ethiopia (25.7% [95% CI: 14.3–41.8]) and the lowest in Uganda (13.01% [95% CI: 2.11–50.97]). Seroprevalence of leptospirosis in different diagnostic methods was 16.5 (95% CI: 8.52–29.46) and 16.5 (95% CI: 12.3–23) in ELISA and MAT, respectively. The meta‐regression analysis identified the sample size and study country as significant moderators (p < 0.1). The model incorporating sample size explained 58.89% of the heterogeneity. The observed high seroprevalence highlights the urgent need for improved diagnostic confirmation, as well as strengthened control and prevention strategies in the region.Keywords: cattle, East Africa, ELISA, goats, humans, leptospirosis, MAT, meta‐analysis, seroprevalence, sheep, zoonotic disease

Introduction

Leptospirosis is a neglected disease, a rapidly spreading animal illness that can have serious negative effects on human health as well as the cattle industry's bottom line (Ellis 2015; World Health Organization 2011). Due to intensifying storms and greater urban floods brought on by climate change, leptospirosis is becoming more common worldwide. In many tropical areas, leptospirosis is a common and potentially deadly zoonosis that spreads widely and sparks significant epidemics following periods of intense rain and flooding. The source of infection is direct or indirect contact with pathogen‐carrying reservoir host animals that shed leptospira in urine (Haake and Levett 2015).

The disease is caused by infection with the pathogenic strains of a bacterium called Leptospira, with more than 300 pathogenic serovars known worldwide (Bharti et al. 2003; Mohammed et al. 2011). Leptospirosis has a global distribution in nature, though it's most prevalent in tropical and humid climates due to favourable environmental conditions for the pathogen to thrive. Previous reports from mathematical modelling estimated the global annual incidence of leptospirosis to be 14.8 cases per 100,000 population, with approximately over one million cases and 60,000 deaths annually (Costa et al. 2015). The prevalence of the human disease is hyperendemic mostly in the Caribbean and Latin America, India, Southeast Asia, Oceania and sub‐Saharan Africa (World Health Organization 2011; Pappas et al. 2008).

Leptospirosis is a significant zoonotic disease resulting from a complex interaction between humans, animal reservoirs and the environment. In rural settings, transmission is primarily associated with increased rainfall, livestock farming and close contact with animals. Conversely, in urban areas, the disease is often rodent‐borne and linked to poor sanitation, inadequate waste management and overcrowding conditions prevalent in urban slums of developing countries (Maciel et al. 2008). Whilst the global epidemiology of leptospirosis has been extensively reviewed (World Health Organization 2011; Pappas et al. 2008), data specific to East Africa remain limited. Africa currently has the second‐highest urban growth rate globally, with an estimated 0.23 million people added to cities each week over the past decade and subsequently high rates of slum development (Habitat 2013). The interplay of climate change, increased flooding, population growth and unplanned urbanization is likely to exacerbate the burden of leptospirosis in sub‐Saharan Africa (Lau et al. 2010).

Leptospirosis is predominantly reported in tropical regions, where warm and humid conditions favour bacterial survival and transmission, particularly in communities living in close contact with animals (Hartskeerl et al. 2011; Adler and de la Peña Moctezuma 2010). Evidence indicates that the disease poses a substantial burden in rural and semi‐urban settings, including countries in East Africa. A previous study estimated the highest annual incidence in Africa (95.5 per 100,000 population), followed by the Western Pacific (66.4 per 100,000), the Americas (12.5 per 100,000), South‐East Asia (4.8 per 100,000) and Europe (0.5 per 100,000) (World Health Organization 2011). Improving understanding of risk factors and transmission dynamics is critical for designing integrated interventions that address the disease burden, support vaccination strategies and provide evidence‐based guidance for policymakers. Such efforts, framed within a One Health approach, are essential to reducing the annual incidence of leptospirosis.

To address the existing knowledge gaps on human and animal Leptospira infections in East Africa, a systematic review and meta‐analysis (SRMA) were conducted on peer‐reviewed articles published between 2000 and 2024, following the PRISMA guidelines and checklist. This study aimed to determine the pooled seroprevalence of leptospirosis in humans, cattle, sheep and goats and to provide an evidence‐based estimate of the disease burden in East Africa.

Materials and Methods

Design and Inclusion Criteria

The study was structured using the condition, context and population (CoCoPop) framework (Munn et al. 2015). The authors used Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) for the present reviews (Table S1) (Moher et al. 2010). The protocol was registered in PROSPERO 2024 with an ID CRD42024577444. The study population consisted of humans, cattle, goats and sheep. Inclusion criteria were: (a) cross‐sectional studies reporting leptospirosis seroprevalence in humans, cattle, goats or sheep; (b) open access peer‐reviewed articles or academic theses (MSc/PhD) from institutional repositories; (c) use of standardized diagnostic methods (MAT or ELISA); (d) publication period (2000–2024). The inclusion criteria were defined based on Joanna Briggs Institute recommendations for systematic reviews of prevalence (Munn et al. 2017). The types of studies were cross‐sectional and published in the English language.

Exclusion Criteria

Research articles were excluded for one of the following reasons: (a) knowledge, attitudes and practices of Leptospirosis (qualitative studies), (b) articles with insufficient information or records with missing outcomes of interest, (c) personal opinions, correspondence, letters to the editor, proceedings and reviews. In addition, to enhance the study's precision, articles with sample size below 100 were excluded (Chandler et al. 2019).

Article Search Strategy and Data Extraction Procedures

The literature search was conducted to identify all published studies reporting the prevalence of leptospirosis in humans, cattle, goats and sheep in different databases and repositories. The logic grid was drawn and the articles were searched in electronic databases and citation indexes, including PubMed, Embase, Google Scholar, African Journals Online, Scopus and Web of Sciences, for studies on leptospirosis conducted from 2000 to 2024 in East Africa. In addition to electronic searches, a manual review was performed using Google Scholar and by screening the reference lists of relevant reviews and included articles. Search queries were developed using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms combined with Boolean operators to ensure comprehensive retrieval of relevant literature. The same set of keywords and MeSH terms (Table S2) was applied across all databases.

All studies about leptospirosis in East Africa in humans, cattle, goats and sheep were exported to EndNote reference manager and the full text was searched automatically, downloaded and attached to the electronic EndNote X7 library. Duplicates from different databases were removed in EndNote X7. Data extraction was performed using the standardized data extraction format, with Group 1 (ATW, SGE) and Group 2 (SKA, YHA, AYA) independently conducting the extraction. Any discrepancies in the extracted data between the two groups were resolved through discussion. The compiled data included the following study characteristics: author name, sample size, species of the animals, number of positive animals, seroprevalence, with 95% CI, the country where the study was conducted, the diagnostic method employed and year of publication.

Evaluation of the Quality of Studies and Risk of Bias Assessment

The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute's critical appraisal tool, specifically designed to evaluate cross‐sectional studies. Two independent groups, group one (ATW, SGE) and group two (SKA, YHA, AYA), employed ‘Yes’, ‘No’, ‘NA’ or ‘Unclear’ answers to evaluate the quality of the included articles. Any discrepancies between the reviewers were addressed through discussions and input was sought from a team of experienced researchers.

In the scoring protocol, a value of 1 was assigned for each ‘yes’ response, whilst ‘no’ or ‘unclear’ answers (including instances where information was not reported or deemed irrelevant) received 0 points. Cumulative scores for each article were calculated on a scale of 0–9. Based on these totals, studies were classified into three quality orders: high (7–9 points), moderate (4–6 points) or low (0–3 points) (Table S3). For this SRMA, only articles ranked as high or moderate quality were retained for final analysis (Munn et al. 2015).

Statistical Analysis

All quantitative analyses were performed by the ‘Meta’ package (version 8.0‐1) from R Studio software (version 2023.03.1‐446). Descriptive statistics were conducted to summarize the included reports with their full description. The overall meta‐analysis results were presented using forest plots with 95% CIs. A random‐effects model was employed to calculate the pooled estimate for the overall meta‐analysis and to conduct subgroup analyses. The logit‐transformed proportion (PLOGIT) demonstrated the most appropriate fit for the assumption of normality, as evidenced by the Shapiro–Wilk test (W = 0.96291, p = 0.1518). Therefore, it was chosen to normalize the data and stabilize the variance estimation for the final meta‐analytic model.

Given the anticipated substantial heterogeneity, a random‐effects meta‐analysis was conducted, with heterogeneity estimated utilizing the DerSimonian and Laird method based on the inverse variance approach (DerSimonian and Laird 1986). Between‐studies heterogeneity variance estimate was assessed using I 2 and Cochran's Q method. I 2 values of 25%, 50% and 75% were considered low, moderate and high heterogeneity, respectively (Ades et al. 2005). The heterogeneity was further investigated via subgroup analysis and meta‐regression analysis. Subgroup analyses were conducted, stratifying the studies by country, animal species from which samples were collected and diagnostic methods employed to reduce variance in the estimated points across the primary studies. Meta‐regression was conducted using the location or country where the primary research was conducted, sample size and diagnostic method using the random effects model. Publication bias was assessed using the Begg's rank correlation test and Egger's regression test, supplemented by visual examination of the funnel plot for asymmetry. A statistically insignificant Egger's test (p > 0.05) indicates the absence of a small study effect. Sensitivity analysis with leave‐one‐out was also performed to assess the influence of individual studies on the pooled estimates.

Results

Study Selection

Database and citation index article searches yielded a total of 75 relevant papers. A total of 20 duplicate articles and 15 additional articles were excluded after careful reviews of their titles published by different first authors and the availability of full documents. About 36 studies met specific inclusion and exclusion criteria for the final meta‐analysis (Figure 1).

The Pooled and Prediction Interval Estimate of Leptospirosis in East Africa

Based on the random‐effects model, the pooled prevalence was 18.16% (95% CI: 13.25%–24.39%) with significant heterogeneity (I 2 = 97.6% [97.2%; 98.0%], p < 0.001). The prediction interval for this SRMA ranges between 0.04 and 0.51 (Figure 2). Therefore, if a new study is added, the effect size is likely to fall within these ranges.

Forest plot for pooled seroprevalence of leptospirosis in East Africa.

Handling Heterogeneity

The heterogeneity analysis demonstrated significant variability in effect sizes across the included studies (Q = 1473.67, df = 35, p < 0.001). The between‐study variance was estimated at τ 2 = 0.5618 (95% CI: 0.5020; 1.4323), with an I 2 statistic of 97.6% (97.2%; 98.0%), indicating a high degree of heterogeneity (Figure 2). To account for the significant heterogeneity observed in the random‐effects model pooled estimate, sensitivity analysis, subgroup analysis and meta‐regression were performed. These approaches serve to identify potential sources of heterogeneity and assess the robustness of the results.

Sensitivity

Baujat diagnostic plot (Baujat et al. 2002) was used to detect studies that overly contribute to the heterogeneity in a meta‐analysis. The plot shows the contribution of each study to the overall heterogeneity (as measured by Cochran's Q) on the horizontal axis and its influence on the pooled effect size on the vertical axis. Accordingly, studies by Onafruo et al. (2024) were found to be extremely influential studies graphically, which contributed overly to the overall heterogeneity. In addition, studies conducted by S. K. Motto et al. (2023), was found to be influential or outliers (Figure 4).

Baujat plot for influential study effect.

In addition, influence diagnostics were also carried out graphically using several influence diagnostics for each of the included studies. Higher values of difference in fits (DFFITS), standardized residuals and Cook's distance were found in a study by Onafruo et al. (2024), indicating that this may be an influential case because its impact on the average effect is larger. The covariance ratio value was also below one for this study, and studies by Kigozi et al. (2023) and Maze et al. (2016), indicating that removing these studies could result in a more precise estimate of the pooled effect size. In addition, leave‐one‐out τ 2 and 𝑄 values that estimate heterogeneity as measured by τ 2 and Cochran's 𝑄 are also lower if these studies were removed, resulting in lower heterogeneity. In the last row, we saw the study weight and hat value for these studies were comparable with others, indicating that the study may not negatively affect the robustness of our pooled result, though it is influential (Figure 5).

Influence of graphical diagnostic.

Finally, sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the influence of a single study on the overall effect size estimate. In this analysis, a specific study was excluded, and a meta‐analysis was performed on the remaining (n−1) studies. If the confidence interval of the study did not encompass the overall effect size estimate, it was deemed that the study had a substantial impact on it (Hedges and Olkin 2014). In this study, the overall effect size estimate was 0.1816, and it fell within the confidence intervals of all the studies. As a result, excluding one study did not have a substantial impact on the overall effect size estimate (Figure 6).

Forest plot for leave‐one‐out meta‐analysis.

Meta‐Regression

Univariable and multivariable meta‐regression models were employed to investigate sources of data heterogeneity. Univariable meta‐regression was conducted using sample size and publication year as continuous variables; study country, detection methods and species as categorical variables using the mixed effect model. These variables were assessed to see a linear relationship with the dependent variable, the effect sizes. In univariable meta‐regression, sample size, study location and species of the animal demonstrated statistically significant associations (p < 0.25) with variability across studies. The meta‐regression model by sample size explains 10.53% amount of the heterogeneity. Whilst the meta‐regression model by study country explained 26.43% amount of the heterogeneity (Table 3). The multivariable regression model explained 45.22% amount of the variability and was statistically significant among the included studies based on the three moderators.

Publication Bias

The assessment of publication bias was conducted with both graphical (funnel plot) and statistical tests with the application of both Begg's and Egger's tests. The results of these tests revealed no evidence of publication bias, as the p values were 0.3260 and 0.1371, respectively for Egger's and Begg's tests. Additionally, the funnel plots demonstrated a symmetrical distribution (Figure 7).

Discussion

This SRMA provide comprehensive evidence on the seroprevalence of Leptospira infection in humans and livestock (cattle, goats and sheep) across East Africa. The findings emphasize the significant zoonotic burden of leptospirosis in the region, where the disease remains a neglected public health issue despite its well‐documented global importance. The pooled seroprevalence of leptospirosis in East Africa was 18% (95% CI: 13%–24%), indicating a substantial level of exposure in both humans and animals.

When compared with other global hotspots, the seroprevalence estimate of approximately 18% in East Africa is only modest in contrast. In the Americas, particularly Latin America and parts of North and Central America, pooled seroprevalence reaches approximately 28% (95% CI: 23%–32%), with especially high estimates in the United States (41%), Colombia (29%) and Brazil (21%) (Browne et al. 2023). In South Asia, leptospirosis is recognized as a significant public health issue driven by monsoon‐associated flooding and rural occupational exposures. In India, studies have shown the disease accounts for approximately 12.7% of acute febrile illness presentations in hospital settings, and in the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, community seroprevalence has been documented at up to 55% (Victoriano et al. 2009). These geographic differences underscore how environmental and socioeconomic contexts, such as flooding, poor sanitation and poverty, critically shape leptospirosis transmission dynamics across regions.

The pooled human seroprevalence in this review was 12.5% (95% CI: 7.1%–21.1%), which is lower than estimates from Iran (27.8%) (Khalili et al. 2020). Such discrepancies are likely due to differences in climate, flooding frequency, rodent density and livestock management practices (Maskey et al. 2006; Pappachan et al. 2004; Kawaguchi et al. 2008; Victoriano et al. 2009; Pellizzer et al. 2006; Barcellos and Sabroza 2001). Subgroup analysis showed Ethiopia with the highest seroprevalence (25.7%), which may relate to high livestock density, occupational exposure among rural farmers and limited access to diagnostic services. Subgroup meta‐analysis indicated Ethiopia has the highest seroprevalence (25.70%) of leptospirosis among the rest of the East African countries. This could be attributed to economic factors, as the impact of leptospirosis is often more pronounced in economically vulnerable populations, particularly in developing countries (Allan et al. 2015).

The high heterogeneity (I 2 = 98%) observed in this meta‐analysis suggests substantial variability across the included studies, which cannot be attributed to random chance alone. This variation may be attributed to ecological differences, sample size differences, variations in diagnostic capacity and methodological inconsistencies among studies. Notably, the meta‐regression model explained 45.22% of the heterogeneity, with the sample size being a significant factor in this variability. Larger sample sizes tend to provide a more precise estimate of leptospirosis seroprevalence, whereas smaller sample sizes may limit the ability to accurately capture the true prevalence, potentially leading to a bias in the pooled estimate. This finding underscores the importance of ensuring adequately powered sample sizes in future studies to enhance the robustness of the prevalence estimates.

Furthermore, variations in the study country emerged as significant predictors of seroprevalence, contributing substantially to observed heterogeneity. Differences in geographical regions likely account for a considerable portion of the variability, as local climate, environmental conditions and socioeconomic factors can influence the prevalence of leptospirosis. Additionally, regional differences in healthcare infrastructure and diagnostic capabilities may lead to disparities in the accuracy and availability of leptospirosis detection, further contributing to the variability in the findings. The meta‐regression model by study country explained 26.43% of the heterogeneity, highlighting the importance of considering geographical context when interpreting seroprevalence estimates.

Significant heterogeneity is observed in this meta‐analysis, as it is common in most prevalence studies. This variability may arise from differences in study populations, geographic settings, diagnostic methods and study designs. Whilst pooled prevalence estimates offer a useful overview, the substantial heterogeneity observed necessitates cautious interpretation. The variation likely reflects both genuine differences in leptospirosis occurrence across regions and inconsistencies in methodology among the included studies. Therefore, these summary estimates should be viewed as general indicators rather than exact prevalence figures applicable to all contexts. This emphasizes the importance of region‐specific surveillance and targeted interventions tailored to local epidemiological and ecological conditions.

The observation that cattle exhibited the highest seroprevalence, followed by sheep, goats and humans, points to the role of livestock as reservoirs for infection and a source of significant economic loss. The dual impact on animal production and human health highlights the necessity of adopting an integrated One Health approach. Such an approach would strengthen collaboration across human, animal and environmental health sectors to ensure coordinated surveillance, timely diagnosis and effective control strategies. Integrated measures should include risk‐based vaccination of livestock, farm‐level biosecurity improvements, rodent control and community education. Public health programs should prioritize leptospirosis alongside other endemic zoonoses, ensuring that diagnostic capacity and reporting systems capture both human and animal cases. Additionally, policymakers must recognize the significance of neglected zoonotic diseases in East Africa and prioritize comprehensive prevention and control programs to protect public health, safeguard livestock productivity and enhance regional trade security. The results of this review point to major gaps in national and regional surveillance systems.

Most leptospirosis research in East Africa has been concentrated in Tanzania, Uganda, Ethiopia, Kenya and Rwanda, whilst countries such as South Sudan, Somalia, Sudan and Djibouti remain underrepresented. This imbalance likely reflects differences in research capacity and resource allocation for zoonotic disease control. Without systematic surveillance, outbreaks may go undetected, leading to underestimated disease burden and delayed interventions.

Policymakers should integrate leptospirosis into national zoonotic disease control strategies and ensure that routine serological surveillance and community‐based reporting systems are in place. Strengthening cross‐border animal movement regulations is crucial to reduce transboundary transmission. Additionally, a regional coordination framework is needed to harmonize diagnostic protocols and facilitate the exchange of epidemiological data, enabling an evidence‐based response to leptospirosis and other neglected zoonoses.

Leptospirosis poses a major diagnostic challenge, as its clinical presentation overlaps with febrile illnesses such as malaria and typhoid (Pinto et al. 2022). Misdiagnosis leads to delayed treatment and inappropriate antibiotic use, increasing morbidity and mortality. The economic impact of the disease through livestock abortion, infertility and reduced productivity further exacerbates poverty in rural communities (Carvalho et al. 2024). A multi‐sectoral strategy is therefore required, combining veterinary interventions, human health services and environmental management to disrupt transmission cycles.

Although there is growing research in both humans and livestock on leptospirosis, indicating growing recognition of its zoonotic nature, significant gaps remain particularly in under‐researched countries. Future research should focus on risk factor analysis, spatial distribution mapping and impact assessment of integrated control strategies to guide effective interventions.

Study Limitations

This study was limited to studies published in English between 2000 and 2024 and focused only on selected species. Differences in diagnostic tests and data scarcity in some countries may influence pooled estimates.

Conclusion and Recommendations

This review demonstrates a considerable burden of leptospirosis in East Africa among both humans and livestock, with important implications for public health, livelihoods and regional trade. Effective control requires coordinated One Health strategies, integrating surveillance, diagnostics and preventive interventions across sectors. Policymakers should prioritize leptospirosis in national health agendas, strengthen surveillance systems and support research to close existing data gaps.
