Rationale for multi-epitope TGFβ vaccination in pancreatic cancer: evidence from immunologic and clinical correlates
Authors: Josephine Hallundbæk Ruders, Shamaila Munir Ahmad, Rasmus Erik Johansson Mortensen, Özcan Met, Mikkel Byrdal, Inna Markovna Chen, Susann Theile, Morten Orebo Holmström, Mads Hald Andersen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transforming growth factor beta (TGFβ) plays a crucial role in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) progression by promoting immune suppression, fibrosis, and metastasis. Given its central role in shaping the tumor microenvironment, TGFβ has attracted significant clinical interest as a target for immunotherapy. One promising and innovative approach involves TGFβ-based immune-modulatory vaccines (IMVs). In this study, we explored the immunogenic potential of multiple TGFβ-derived epitopes and assessed the clinical relevance of TGFβ-specific T cells in PDAC patients. We identified T cell responses towards multiple epitopes, TGFβ‑15, TGFβ‑33, and TGFβ‑38, in both healthy donors and PDAC patients, with TGFβ‑33 eliciting particularly robust responses in the patient cohort. These primarily CD4⁺ T cells displayed proinflammatory, as well as notable cytotoxic, properties and recognized target cells in a TGFβ-dependent manner. Moreover, we found that higher baseline frequencies of TGFβ‑33-specific T cells correlated with improved clinical outcomes following combined immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy and radiotherapy. Interestingly, patients with baseline responses to multiple TGFβ-derived epitopes had longer overall and progression-free survival compared to those with responses to none or a single epitope. Using a single mRNA construct encoding multiple TGFβ epitopes, we demonstrated effective activation of T cells specific for distinct TGFβ epitopes. These findings support the potential of a multi-epitope TGFβ-derived immune-modulatory vaccine to enhance anti-tumor immunity, representing a promising strategy to improve therapeutic responses in PDAC.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed the treatment landscape for various cancers by reactivating exhausted T cells. However, their effectiveness remains limited to patients with immune-inflamed tumors,1 as the immunosuppressive nature of many solid tumor microenvironments (TMEs) hampers effective T-cell infiltration and function.2 Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma exemplifies a cancer highly resistant to immunotherapy.3 Although it ranks only tenth in incidence, PDAC is projected to become the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths in the EU.4 Its poor prognosis, marked by a global 5-year survival rate of less than 10%, is primarily caused by late-stage diagnosis, high metastatic potential, and profound immune evasion. A significant obstacle to immune-mediated elimination in PDAC is its immunosuppressive and fibrotic TME.3 Such TMEs are characterized by a dominance of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), regulatory T cells (Tregs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs),2 many of which contribute to immunosuppression by secreting inhibitory cytokines. Among these, transforming growth factor beta is a major contributor to the suppressive microenvironment in PDAC. This pleiotropic cytokine is produced by various cell types within the TME, including CAFs and cancer cells, and promotes both fibrosis, tumor growth, and metastasis.5 In addition to directly promoting tumor progression, high TGFβ activity also blocks T-cell infiltration and function, contributing to the “cold” immune phenotype characteristic of PDAC.5

To counteract this, IMVs targeting immunosuppressive elements of the TME have emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy. These vaccines aim to activate anti-regulatory T cells (anti-Tregs), a subset of pro-inflammatory CD4+ and CD8+ T cells that recognize and eliminate immunosuppressive cells, thereby reducing the inhibitory signals within the TME.6 Thus, these vaccines have the potential to synergize with ICIs, which rely on the presence of functional effector T cells to mediate tumor regression. The recognition of immunosuppressive cells by anti-Tregs is dependent on HLA-restricted epitopes derived from molecules such as programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1), indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), arginase-1/2 (Arg1/2), and, recently, TGFβ.6–11 Building on this concept, preclinical studies using the Pan02 murine PDAC model demonstrated that a multi-peptide TGFβ-based IMV could reprogram the TME.12 This study further revealed that the vaccine activated antigen-specific T cells and altered the TME into a pro-inflammatory, anti-tumor state, indicating its potential to counteract immunosuppression and fibrosis.12 Together, these findings suggest that targeting TGFβ-expressing cells within the TME may enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy in tumors historically resistant to ICIs, such as PDAC, and underscore the potential of IMVs to convert “cold” tumors into immune-responsive phenotypes.

In humans, several immunogenic epitopes derived from TGFβ have been identified, with TGFβ-15 being extensively studied. This epitope elicits strong cytotoxic T cell responses in vitro.11 Mortensen et al. further emphasized its clinical relevance by demonstrating that pre-existing TGFβ-15-specific immunity was associated with improved survival in patients with PDAC who received ICIs and radiotherapy.13 Together, these findings supported the initiation of a Phase I clinical trial (CheckVAC, NCT05721846), which is currently evaluating a TGFβ-15 peptide vaccine in combination with nivolumab, ipilimumab, and radiotherapy.14 However, single-epitope vaccines may not fully exploit the therapeutic potential of TGFβ-targeted immunity, as preclinical evidence suggests that multi-peptide vaccines can more effectively reprogram the TME.12 Given this, we aimed to assess the immunogenicity of various TGFβ-derived epitopes in PDAC patients and to explore the rationale for a multi-epitope TGFβ IMV to enhance anti-tumor immune responses in this group and improve the immunotherapy response.

In addition to TGFβ-15, earlier studies suggested the potential of other TGFβ-derived epitopes, which have also been shown to elicit antigen-specific immune responses in healthy donors in vitro.11 Thus, in this study, we evaluated the immunogenicity of these TGFβ-derived epitopes in patients with PDAC. By assessing the ability of multiple epitopes to elicit antigen-specific T-cell responses, our work provides a rationale for a multi-epitope TGFβ IMV designed to more comprehensively target the immunosuppressive TME. This approach represents a novel strategy in the field, aiming to convert the inherently “cold” PDAC microenvironment into an immune-responsive state that may synergize with existing therapies, including ICIs and radiotherapy. Our findings have potential translational relevance, offering a foundation for the development of more effective immunotherapeutic interventions in PDAC and other tumors characterized by profound immune evasion.

Results

Spontaneous immunity towards highly immunogenic TGFβ-derived T-cell epitopes in healthy donors and pancreatic cancer patients

A library of TGFβ-derived peptides was previously created by dividing the complete TGFβ protein sequence into 38 overlapping 20-mer peptides, each with a 10-amino-acid overlap.11 Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from multiple healthy donors (HDs) were examined for spontaneous immune responses against these epitopes, revealing various immunogenic epitopes throughout the protein sequence, particularly highlighting TGFβ-02, TGFβ-15, TGFβ-33, and TGFβ-38.11 TGFβ-02 is in the signal sequence, and TGFβ-15 is in the latency-associated protein (LAP) region, whereas peptides TGFβ-33 and -38 are found in the active TGFβ region (Fig. 1a). Based on preliminary results (not shown), we chose to confirm and further characterize the immunogenicity of TGFβ-15, -33, and -38 by screening 31 HDs using in vitro IFNγ ELISPOT assays. The immunogenicity of TGFβ-15 was verified, and we also observed robust and frequent immune responses to TGFβ-33 and -38 (Fig. 1b, c). Responses to TGFβ-33 and TGFβ-38 were similar, as were responses to TGFβ-15 and TGFβ-38 (Fig. 1c). Our group has previously shown that patients with prostate cancer and metastatic melanoma harbor T cells specific for TGFβ-derived epitopes.11 Moreover, TGFβ-15 immunity has previously been observed in PDAC patients.13 We sought to verify TGFβ-15 immunity and to determine whether TGFβ-33 and TGFβ-38 could also induce immune responses in patients with pancreatic cancer. To evaluate this, 14 patients with PDAC from the TRIPLE-R trial (NCT04258150)15 were randomly selected and analyzed in IFNγ ELISPOT. We found that TGFβ-15, TGFβ-33, and TGFβ-38 could induce IFNγ production in these patients (Fig. 1b, d). The medians of the response amplitudes for TGFβ-15 and TGFβ-38 were similar and relatively low; however, strong responses to both peptides were observed in a subset of patients (Fig. 1d). Although not statistically significant, the data indicated a trend toward more frequent and potent immune responses against TGFβ-33 (Fig. 1d).

The phenotype of the TGFβ-33- and TGFβ-38-activated cells was evaluated using intracellular cytokine staining (ICS) (supplementary Fig. 1). We found that both peptides activated CD4+ T cells in HDs and PDAC patients (Fig. 1e, supplementary Fig. 2). While CD8+ T cell responses were less frequent, we did identify small CD8+ responses against TGFβ-33 (Fig. 1f). Thus, the immunogenicity of TGFβ-15 was verified, and TGFβ-33 and TGFβ-38 were identified as promising immunogenic epitopes. Moreover, both peptides activated CD4+ T cells, with TGFβ-33 also driving CD8+ T cell responses.

Healthy donors and pancreatic cancer patients harbor circulating TGFβ-33-specific memory CD4⁺ T cells

After confirming that TGFβ-33- and TGFβ-38-specific T cells are present in both HDs and PDAC patients and can be activated in vitro, we sought to investigate whether TGFβ-specific T cells are also activated in vivo and contribute to the peripheral memory T-cell pool. We chose to focus on TGFβ-33 because this epitope appeared to be the most immunogenic in the patient cohort (Fig. 1d), and, thus, we examined whether we could identify TGFβ-33-specific memory T cells in HDs and PDAC patients. Using MACS, we isolated CD4+CD45RO+ memory T cells from PBMCs of two HDs and two PDAC patients, in whom in vitro responses to TGFβ-33 had previously been observed (Fig. 1c, d, and data not shown). A prior study found that in vitro stimulation of isolated memory T cells alone produced only weak responses, likely due to an insufficient number of antigen-presenting cells.16 Therefore, we first isolated CD14+ monocytes and pulsed them with the peptide for two hours before using them to stimulate the memory T cells, which were isolated from the CD14-negative population. Successful isolation of the memory T cells was verified by FACS (Fig. 2a). The monocytes and memory T cells were incubated for 12 days before performing ELISPOT and ICS analysis. We detected strong responses against TGFβ-33 in the memory T cells isolated from both HDs and PDAC patients in both IFNγ ELISPOT (Fig. 2b) and ICS (Fig. 2c). Baseline immunity to multiple TGFβ-derived epitopes is associated with clinical benefit in patients with pancreatic cancer

Having established the natural presence of TGFβ-33- and TGFβ-38-specific T cells, we next sought to assess their clinical relevance. Specifically, we aimed to investigate whether baseline immune responses to the peptides correlated with clinical outcomes in patients with PDAC, as observed for TGFβ-15.13 This previous study found that TGFβ-15 immune responses at baseline predicted survival in PDAC patients treated with radiotherapy combined with checkpoint inhibitors in a randomized, phase II study (CheckPAC, NCT0286638317,13). We aimed to determine if this also applied to TGFβ-33, as our results indicated that this epitope is more immunogenic in the patient cohort than TGFβ-38 (Fig. 1d). Using IFNγ ELISPOT, we identified TGFβ-33-specific responses in PBMCs isolated at baseline and after four treatment series (Fig. 3a). To analyze whether the responses correlated with clinical benefit, the 27 patients studied were divided into two categories. Achieving a partial response (PR) or stable disease (SD) was classified as having clinical benefit from the treatment (15 patients). In comparison, progressive disease (PD) was defined as having no clinical benefit (12 patients). We observed that patients with clinical benefit had significantly higher baseline TGFβ-33 responses than those with PD (Fig. 3b left), whereas no significant difference was observed after treatment (Fig. 3b right). This suggested that TGFβ-33 immune responses at baseline, before therapy initiation, might influence the clinical response to ICIs and radiotherapy. To investigate this further, we examined whether baseline TGFβ-33-specific immunity was associated with improved survival in the cohort. The patients were stratified according to whether we detected baseline responses to the epitope above or below the median normalized TGFβ-33-specific immune response. Interestingly, we observed significantly longer overall survival (OS) (Fig. 3c) and progression-free survival (PFS) (Fig. 3d) in patients with a TGFβ-33-specific response above the median at baseline compared to those with a response below the median. Preclinical mouse studies have demonstrated the antitumor effects of an IMV based on multiple peptides derived from TGFβ.12 Therefore, to further validate the prognostic value of baseline TGFβ immunity in PDAC patients from the CheckPAC trial, we added these observations to the previously published data on TGFβ-15.13 Thus, we assessed the clinical impacts of a baseline response above the median to neither TGFβ-15 nor TGFβ-33, to only one of the epitopes, or both within the same patients. When evaluating both OS and PFS, we observed a clear trend that patients with baseline responses to both TGFβ-15 and TGFβ-33 above the median had better outcomes than the other groups (Fig. 3e, f). Specifically, there was no significant difference in OS or PFS between patients who exhibited no response to either peptide compared to those with a response above the median to only one. TGFβ-33- and TGFβ-38-specific T cells recognize autologous myeloid-derived dendritic cells as well as the malignant myeloid cell line THP-1 in a peptide- and protein-dependent manner

Given the observed association between TGFβ-specific T cells and clinical benefit, we next assessed whether these cells could recognize relevant target cells. First, we generated T cell cultures specific to the respective peptides using the rapid expansion protocol (REP). We established six bulk TGFβ-33-specific T cell cultures and two bulk TGFβ-38-specific T cell cultures (Fig. 4a, b, and supplementary Fig. 3). Additionally, we generated a CD4+ TGFβ-38-specific oligoclonal culture using limited dilution (supplementary Fig. 3a right, 3b). These cultures primarily consisted of CD4+ T cells (supplementary Fig. 3b). Although CD8+ populations were detectable, we did not observe noticeable TGFβ peptide-specific reactivity in these populations (data not shown). We established a single CD8+ T cell culture specific to TGFβ-33 (Fig. 4b, supplementary Fig. 3b). To examine the target-recognition ability of the specific T cells, we generated autologous dendritic cells (DCs) from monocytes isolated from PBMCs. The phenotype of the mature DCs was verified by microscope and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis (supplementary Fig. 4). The DCs were pulsed with the TGFβ-33 peptide and cocultured with autologous TGFβ-33-specific T cell cultures in ICS assays. We detected slight activation of the TGFβ-33-specific T cells by the unpulsed DCs (Fig. 4c), which, in part, could be explained by the upregulation of co-stimulatory molecules (supplementary Fig. 4b) and the fact that the mature DCs expressed TGFβ on the mRNA level (supplementary Fig. 5a). However, when pulsed with the peptide, we observed increased activation of both CD8+ (Fig. 4c left) and CD4+ (Fig. 4c right) T cells through the induction of various activation markers. The most pronounced activation was observed in CD4+ T cells. Next, we aimed to determine whether TGFβ-33- and TGFβ-38-specific T cells could recognize target cells in a TGFβ protein-dependent manner. This is crucial for establishing whether the immune system can recognize relevant immunosuppressive cells that express TGFβ. We selected THP-1 cells because this malignant cell line naturally expresses TGFβ, which can be further enhanced by treatment with recombinant TGFβ1 for 48 h.11 We validated this at the mRNA level using RT-qPCR, where we observed baseline TGFβ expression and a 3.2-fold increase after 48 h of treatment with five ng/mL TGFβ1 (supplementary Fig. 5b). To further verify TGFβ-dependent recognition, we also included a condition with specific siRNA-mediated knockdown of TGFβ. We selected two TGFβ-33-specific and two TGFβ-38-specific T cell cultures. All cultures displayed T cell activation upon co-culture with untreated mock-transfected THP-1 cells, consistent with their baseline expression of TGFβ (Fig. 4d, supplementary Fig. 6). This activation was significantly reduced when the untreated THP-1 cells were transfected with TGFβ siRNA (Fig. 4d). When mock-transfected THP-1 cells were instead pretreated with TGFβ1, we observed significantly enhanced T cell activation compared with untreated cells in all but one culture (supplementary Fig. 6). If the target cells were co-treated with TGFβ1 and TGFβ siRNA, this activation was significantly reduced (Fig. 4d), illustrating that the recognition is partially TGFβ-dependent. Although the effect was mainly mediated through TNFα and CD107a expression (Fig. 4d, supplementary Fig. 6), the TGFβ-dependent activation was also detectable in IFNγ ELISPOT (Fig. 4e). We performed FACS analysis on the THP-1 cells to ensure that the effect on activation of the CD4+ T cells was not due to more or less HLA-II on the surface of the THP-1 cells following treatment with TGFβ1 or TGFβ siRNA. Interestingly, we detected a decrease in the percentage of HLA-II+ THP-1 cells when cells were pre-treated with TGFβ compared to untreated THP-1 cells (Fig. 4f). Furthermore, we observed an increase in both the percentage of HLA-II+ THP-1 cells and HLA-II MFI upon siRNA-mediated knockdown of TGFβ (Fig. 4f). We observed reduced activation of TGFβ-specific CD4⁺ T cells when THP-1 cells were treated with TGFβ siRNA, despite the significant increase in HLA-II expression. Together, these findings demonstrate that the responses depicted in Fig. 4d, e, and supplementary Fig. 6 are driven, in part, by TGFβ-dependent T cell activation rather than by modulation of the peptide presentation machinery. Thus, the TGFβ-33- and TGFβ-38-specific T cells can recognize TGFβ-expressing target cells in a TGFβ-dependent manner.

Autologous dendritic cells transfected with TGFβ multi-epitope mRNA can activate T cells with distinct specificities

Multiple TGFβ-derived epitopes can be delivered via an IMV using various platforms, including peptide-based formulations or mRNA encoding the epitopes. In previous experiments, we demonstrated peptide- and protein-induced activation of the TGFβ-specific T cells. To explore the potential of mRNA-based antigen delivery of the TGFβ-derived epitopes, we designed and tested a single mRNA construct containing multiple TGFβ-derived epitopes. First, we designed a plasmid (TGFβ-ALL) that encoded a TGFβ multi-epitope region (Fig. 5a left). This region included the sequences of the four TGFβ-derived epitopes of interest, TGFβ-02, TGFβ-15, TGFβ-33, and TGFβ-38 (Fig. 5a middle). The plasmid was linearized and used as a template for in vitro transcription of the TGFβ multi-epitope mRNA construct. We then investigated whether DCs transfected with this mRNA could process the multi-epitope sequence, present the encoded epitopes, and activate T cells with distinct TGFβ epitope specificities (Fig. 5a right). The transfection method was verified using mRNA encoding eGFP (supplementary Fig. 7). To assess the multi-epitope presentation, we generated additional CD4⁺ T-cell cultures specific to TGFβ-02 and TGFβ-15 (Fig. 5b, supplementary Fig. 3) and complemented these with existing TGFβ-33- and TGFβ-38-specific cultures. Compared to mock-transfected DCs, coculture of the specific T cells with autologous DCs transfected with the TGFβ-ALL mRNA resulted in significantly increased activation across all four TGFβ specificities, as measured by TNFα and/or CD107a expression (Fig. 5c). Discussion

TGFβ promotes tumor progression by suppressing antitumor immunity, inducing epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) to facilitate metastasis, and driving pathological fibrosis via activation of CAFs.18–20 Most cell types in the TME respond to TGFβ, and these cell-type-dependent effects collectively sustain an immunosuppressive, tumor-promoting milieu via a TGFβ-driven feedback loop.18 In PDAC, TGFβ signaling drives key disease hallmarks, and its therapeutic targeting has been explored, e.g., with monoclonal antibodies (NIS793)21 and small-molecule receptor inhibitors (galunisertib).22 While these approaches reduced tumor growth and improved chemotherapy response, limitations, including limited efficacy, toxicity, and resistance, highlight the need for more effective strategies.18,23 Because immunosuppressive cells rarely depend exclusively on TGFβ signaling, targeting the cells rather than the cytokine has emerged as a promising alternative.

IMVs based on TGFβ-derived epitopes aim to activate T cells that recognize and eliminate TGFβ-expressing cells, thereby targeting both cancer cells and the tumor-promoting immunosuppressive cells within the TME.12 Preclinical studies demonstrated that multi-peptide TGFβ vaccination elicited antitumor activity in a mouse model of PDAC,12 and a Phase I trial combining a TGFβ single-peptide vaccine with radiotherapy and ICIs is currently underway (NCT05721846). However, given that the preclinical success relied on a multi-epitope approach, we sought to investigate the rationale for vaccines incorporating multiple TGFβ-derived epitopes in pancreatic cancer patients.

TGFβ is frequently upregulated in pancreatic cancer and associated with poor prognosis.24 Along with prior clinical evidence supporting therapeutic targeting of the TGFβ signaling axis,21,22 this provided a strong rationale for evaluating the immunogenicity of multiple TGFβ-derived epitopes in PDAC patients. Consistent with previous findings, we confirmed strong immune responses to all tested TGFβ-derived peptides in HDs and demonstrated their immunogenicity in PDAC patients. Most TGFβ-specific responses were mediated by CD4⁺ T cells in vitro, likely reflecting the use of long peptides and short stimulation durations (5–6 h), which may have limited the antigen processing and cross-presentation necessary for efficient CD8+ T cell activation. However, long peptide vaccines have been shown to induce robust CD8⁺ T cell responses in vivo.25 Thus, long peptides remain a promising strategy for engaging both CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, which are both critical for durable anti-tumor immunity.26 Interestingly, TGFβ-33 triggered stronger and more frequent responses in the patients. This could reflect enhanced in vivo exposure and expansion of TGFβ-33-specific memory T cells due to TGFβ overexpression in the TME. Memory CD4⁺ T cell experiments confirmed this trend, with patient-derived T cells exhibiting markedly higher activation. The detection of TGFβ-specific memory T cells in healthy donors suggests pre-existing immune priming. However, TME-mediated suppression likely restrains their antitumor activity, indicating that vaccination may require combination with ICIs to restore T cell function. We therefore evaluated the clinical relevance of TGFβ-specific T cells in PDAC patients receiving combined ICI and radiotherapy. Since TGFβ-33 induced the strongest responses, we focused on TGFβ-33-specific T cells. Notably, patients with higher pre-treatment TGFβ-33-specific T cell reactivity exhibited improved clinical outcomes following combined ICI and radiotherapy. Post-treatment declines could potentially reflect T cell trafficking into the tumor, which might explain the improved OS and PFS. Furthermore, baseline responses to several epitopes (TGFβ-15 and TGFβ-33) correlated with longer OS and PFS, supporting preclinical observations that multi-epitope vaccines induce stronger antitumor immunity than single-epitope approaches.12 It is essential to consider that this association might reflect broader immune competence rather than epitope-specific reactivity. However, Mortensen et al. found no difference in responses to common viral epitopes between patients with strong versus weak TGFβ-15 responses, or between clinical responders and non-responders to ICI and radiotherapy, arguing against general immune fitness as an explanation.13 Alternatively, the more pronounced presence of TGFβ-15- and TGFβ-33-specific T cells may indicate greater antigen availability due to higher tumor burden or a more inflamed TME, both of which could enhance ICI responsiveness. Nevertheless, preclinical evidence and their enrichment in patients with better clinical outcomes suggest that T cells specific to multiple TGFβ-derived epitopes actively contribute to tumor control, reinforcing the rationale for a multi-epitope TGFβ-based IMV therapeutic strategy.

To achieve antitumor effects, it is essential that the TGFβ-specific T cells can recognize target cells that express endogenous TGFβ. Recognition of target cells was confirmed using THP-1 cells, in which TGFβ upregulation enhanced activation of the TGFβ-specific T cells, whereas siRNA-mediated TGFβ knockdown reduced it, indicating that activation depended on endogenous TGFβ expression. Interestingly, we observed that TGFβ treatment decreased HLA-II expression on the target cells, whereas TGFβ knockdown increased it. This likely reflects an autocrine TGFβ signaling pathway, highlighting the intricate suppressive properties of the cytokine.27 Importantly, despite the improved antigen presentation capacity upon TGFβ knockdown, T cell activation remained lower, supporting the conclusion that decreased T cell activation was due to reduced TGFβ antigen availability rather than a general impairment of antigen presentation. Thus, the TGFβ-specific T cells recognized malignant cells in a TGFβ-dependent manner.

From a translational perspective, multi-epitope peptide or mRNA-based vaccines could efficiently deliver multiple TGFβ epitopes, engaging both CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells.28 We verified that autologous DCs pulsed with TGFβ-33 peptide effectively activated CD4⁺ T cells and, to a lesser extent, CD8⁺ T cells. While CD4⁺ T cells are traditionally recognized to support CD8⁺ responses, they can directly kill tumor cells in an HLA-II-dependent manner ex vivo.29 Interestingly, the activated CD4+ T cells upregulated granzyme B and granulysin, a phenotype associated with direct tumor cell killing.30 Since the TGFβ-33-specific CD4⁺ T cells were effectively activated by peptide-pulsed DCs, a TGFβ-derived IMV based on peptides could serve as a promising strategy to activate these cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells in vivo, thereby enhancing antitumor immunity. Aside from peptides, a TGFβ IMV could also be mRNA-based, which offers some advantages, including enhanced stability and solubility,31,32 self-adjuvanticity,33 and potential for broader epitope coverage and thus stronger antitumor immunity.34,35 We successfully demonstrated that autologous DCs transfected with multi-epitope mRNA effectively activated corresponding T cells. These data demonstrate that a single mRNA construct encoding multiple TGFβ-derived epitopes enables processing and presentation of all included epitopes, supporting an mRNA approach for multi-epitope immunization.

Aside from selecting the optimal IMV delivery platform, therapeutic development must consider the dual role of TGFβ, a cytokine that promotes tumor progression in advanced stages but acts as a tumor suppressor early in disease.36 Thus, careful timing is essential to maximize benefits while minimizing potential adverse effects. The adjuvant activation of TGFβ-specific T cells could both counteract TGFβ-driven immunosuppression in the TME and inhibit pro-metastatic processes like EMT and pre-metastatic niche formation. Therefore, multi-epitope TGFβ vaccines could be integrated with ICIs, neoantigen vaccines, or adoptive T-cell therapies to optimize clinical benefit. Early clinical trials targeting other immunosuppressive pathways with multi-peptide IMVs (e.g., IDO1/PD-L1 in melanoma, NCT05155254) suggest safety and efficacy (not published), further validating this strategy.

Aside from PDAC, clinical evidence from a range of diseases underscores the therapeutic promise of targeting TGFβ. For instance, in metastatic breast cancer, TGFβ blockade with fresolimumab during radiotherapy was well tolerated and associated with prolonged survival.37 Preclinical murine data have also provided a rationale for anti-TGFβ therapies in patients with myelofibrosis,38 and vaccination against TGFβ1 has been shown to reduce fibrosis in a mouse model of chronic colitis,39 suggesting potential applications beyond pancreatic cancer and oncology.

In summary, TGFβ represents a compelling immunotherapeutic target due to its central role in immune evasion, fibrosis, and metastasis in PDAC. Our findings demonstrate that multiple TGFβ-derived epitopes elicit robust, functional T cell responses in both healthy donors and PDAC patients, with multi-epitope reactivity correlating with improved clinical outcomes following ICI and radiotherapy. Mechanistic studies confirm that the TGFβ-specific T cells can recognize TGFβ-expressing target cells, exhibit cytotoxicity, and likely contribute to antitumor immunity in vivo. The presence of naturally occurring memory T cells specific for TGFβ further supports the feasibility and safety of activating these T cells. Overall, our data provide a compelling rationale for advancing multi-epitope TGFβ-targeted IMVs, whether peptide- or mRNA-based, to enhance responses to ICIs, radiotherapy, and other immunotherapies in pancreatic cancer, while also offering a platform that could be extended to other immunologically “cold” tumors and fibrotic diseases.

Materials and methods

Patients and donors

Buffy coats from healthy, anonymized donors were obtained from the Blood Bank at Rigshospitalet, Copenhagen, Denmark. PBMCs were obtained from cancer patients at the Department of Oncology, Copenhagen University Hospital, Herlev, Denmark. PBMCs were isolated using Lymphoprep (Axis-Shield, Oslo, Norway) and cryopreserved in fetal calf serum (FCS, Life Technologies, California, USA) with 10% DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, Massachusetts, USA).

Peptides

All peptides were provided by Schafer (Copenhagen, Denmark) and obtained at high purity (>90%). Upon arrival, lyophilized peptides were dissolved in DMSO to 10 mM and stored at −20 °C.

In vitro cultures and enzyme-linked immunospot assay (ELISPOT) for the detection of TGFβ-specific immune responses

In vitro immune responses to TGFβ-15, TGFβ-33, and TGFβ-38 were assessed using ELISPOT assays. PBMCs were thawed and stimulated with 2 µL of 10 mM peptide for 2 h at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. X-VIVO (Lonza, Belgium) with 5% human serum (Merck Life Science, Darmstadt, Germany) was added, and the cultures were kept under the conditions described above. On day 2, IL-2 (120 U/mL; Novartis, Switzerland) was added to support T cell expansion. After 12–14 days, cell viability and concentration were measured using a Countess II Automated Cell Counter (ThermoFisher, Massachusetts, USA), and ELISPOT assays were performed to detect TGFβ-specific cells. PVDF ELISPOT plates (Merck Life Science) were coated with anti-IFNγ capture antibody in PBS (7.5 µg/mL; Mabtech, Stockholm, Sweden) and incubated for 24 h at room temperature. Plates were washed with PBS and preconditioned with X-VIVO for 2 h at 37 °C. Cells were plated at 300,000 cells per well for nonspecific PBMC cultures and at fewer cells for specific T-cell cultures, as specified in the experiments. Cells were re-stimulated with 5 µM peptides or co-cultured with target cells and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Detection was done using a biotinylated anti-IFNγ antibody (0.75 µg/mL), streptavidin-alkaline phosphatase (1:1000), and a substrate, with PBS washes between steps, following Mabtech’s instructions. Plates were dried overnight and analyzed using an ImmunoSpot S6 Ultimate Analyzer (CTL Analyzers, Shaker Heights, OH, USA).

Intracellular cytokine staining (ICS) and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) to investigate the phenotype of the TGFβ-specific cells

To assess T cell responses via ICS, PBMCs were stimulated in vitro as above and restimulated with peptide or target cells in 96-well round-bottom plates (Corning, New York, USA). CD107a-BV421 (0.3 µL/well; BD Biosciences, New Jersey, USA) was added immediately after stimulation. After 1 h at 37 °C, GolgiPlug (BD Biosciences) was added (1:1000), followed by 4 hours of incubation. Cells were washed with FACS buffer (PBS + 2% FCS; 1500 RPM, 5 min, 4 °C), then resuspended in 50 µL extracellular antibody mix containing CD3-AF700, CD8-BV605, CD4-BV711 (all Nordic Biosite, Copenhagen, Denmark), and Near-IR viability dye (ThermoFisher) for 30 min at 4 °C. Cells were fixed/permeabilized overnight at 4 °C in fixation/permeabilization buffer (Invitrogen, Massachusetts, USA). Next day, cells were washed with diluted 10× permeabilization buffer (Invitrogen) and stained intracellularly for 30 min at 4 °C in 50 µL antibody mix containing Perforin-FITC (Nordic Biosite), Granzyme B-APC (Fisher Scientific, New Hampshire, USA), IFNγ-BV510, Granulysin-PE (both Nordic Biosite), TNFα-PE-CF594 (BD Biosciences), and IL-2-PE-Cy7 (AH Diagnostics, Aarhus, Denmark). All flow analysis was performed on the same NovoCyte Quanteon flow cytometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA) using the appropriate application settings and compensation controls. Data were processed using NovoExpress software V1.5.0.

Enrichment and expansion of TGFβ-specific T cells

TGFβ-specific T cells were enriched from primary PBMC cultures using magnetically activated cell sorting (MACS). PBMCs were stimulated in vitro with the relevant peptide as described above. After 3–6 h of restimulation with the antigen, cells were enriched using the MACS IFNγ Secretion Assay Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). Enriched antigen-specific T cells were expanded in 20 mL X-VIVO + HS via a rapid expansion protocol (REP) by co-culturing 20 × 10⁶ irradiated allogeneic feeder PBMCs from three HDs with enriched T cells in the presence of 6000 U/mL IL-2, 0.6 µg αCD3 antibody (AH Diagnostics), and 0.5 mL HEPES (Life Technologies). Additionally, a TGFβ-38-specific T cell oligoclone was generated by limiting dilution: 0.5–1 enriched T cell was plated per well in 96-well plates with 1 × 10⁵ irradiated feeder cells, <0.3 µg phytohemagglutinin, and 120 U/mL IL-2. Specificity of the T cell cultures was confirmed by ICS.

Generation of dendritic cells (DCs) by the fast-generated DC (FastDC) protocol and peptide-pulsing

To generate autologous mature DCs for antigen presentation, CD14+ monocytes were isolated from PBMCs by positive selection using the MACS CD14 microbead kit (Miltenyi Biotec). Monocytes were plated at 5 × 10⁵ cells/mL in R10 (RPMI-1640 + 10% FCS) with 1000 U/mL GM-CSF and 250 U/mL IL-4 (both Fisher Scientific) for 24 h. DCs were matured for 36 h with 1000 U/mL IL-1β, 1000 U/mL TNFα, 1000 U/mL IL-6 (all Fisher Scientific), and 1 μg/mL PGE2 (Sigma-Aldrich).

For peptide pulsing, mature DCs were resuspended in 500 µL RPMI-1640 and pulsed with 2 µL of 10 mM peptide in a 24-well plate (Corning) for 2 h. 1.5 mL R10 was added, and the cells were rested overnight.

FACS analysis of maturation- and DC-specific marker expression on FastDCs

FACS analysis was conducted at all stages of the FastDC protocol. To minimize nonspecific antibody binding to Fc receptors, cells were incubated with Fc block (Nordic Biosite) for 10 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, cells were stained for 30 min with an extracellular antibody mix comprising CD3-BV510, HLA-DR-PerCP-Cy5.5, CD14-PE-CF594, CD40-APC-R700 (all BD Biosciences), CD86-BV786, and CD11c-PE (both Nordic Biosite), as well as Near-IR viability dye.

From plasmid to mRNA: E. coli transformation, plasmid DNA extraction and linearization, and in vitro ARCA-capped mRNA synthesis

All plasmids were based on the pSP73-A64 vector containing a T7 promoter and flanking 5′ and 3′ UTRs around the epitope insert. Lyophilized plasmids (GeneArt, ThermoFisher Scientific) were reconstituted in nuclease-free water and stored at −20 °C. For amplification, 1 μg of plasmid DNA was used to transform One Shot TOP10 E. coli (Invitrogen) via standard heat-shock. The cells were recovered in SOC medium at 37 °C in a gyratory shaker-incubator for 1 h and plated on LB agar with ampicillin (LB broth, ThermoFisher Scientific; agar powder, Amresco, Ohio, USA; ampicillin 1:2000 from a 100 μg/mL stock, Sigma-Aldrich). Colonies were selected and expanded in LB with ampicillin for 16–20 h, and plasmid DNA was extracted using the PureLink™ HiPure Plasmid Maxiprep Kit (Invitrogen), quantified by Nanodrop (ThermoFisher Scientific), and stored at −20 °C. For in vitro mRNA transcription, the pDNA was linearized with SpeI (New England Biolabs) and purified using the Wizard DNA Clean-Up Kit (Promega). mRNA was synthesized using the mMessage mMachine T7 Ultra Kit (Invitrogen), purified with MEGAclear™ (Invitrogen), and stored at −80 °C.

Transfection of DCs with mRNA and siRNA-mediated TGFβ silencing in THP-1 cells

Electroporation was used to transfect DCs with TGFβ-encoding mRNA and to knock down TGFβ in THP-1 cells using siRNA. Approximately 2–4 × 10⁶ cells were washed in cold Opti-MEM (Invitrogen). DCs were electroporated with 5 μg mRNA using a BTX 830 square-wave electroporator (Harvard Apparatus, Massachusetts, USA)40 and rested in R10 medium for 2 h before co-culture.

For siRNA-mediated TGFβ silencing, a set of three stealth siRNAs (HSS110683, HSS110684, HSS110685; Life Technologies) was used, with the sense sequences 5′-GGUGGAAACCCACAACGAAAUCUAU-3′, 5′-UCCUGGCCCUGUACAACCAGCAUAA-3′, and 5′-CCACCUGCAAGACUAUCGACAUGGA-3′.41 THP-1 cells were resuspended in Opti-MEM with siRNAs (0.25 nmol) and electroporated using established parameters.11 Transfections with siRNA were performed twice, on days -5 and -2, before co-culture and HLA analysis. Mock-transfected and eGFP controls were processed in parallel.

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and analysis of TGFβ expression using real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

RT-qPCR was used to quantify TGFβ mRNA in mature DCs and THP-1 cells. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and reverse-transcribed with the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, California, USA). Reactions were run on a thermal cycler under standard conditions. qPCR was performed using LightCycler TaqMan Master Mix (Roche Diagnostics, Copenhagen, Denmark) and TaqMan Gene Expression Assays (ThermoFisher Scientific), with TGFβ1 (Hs07289533_m1) normalized to RPLP0 (Hs99999902_m1). Reactions included no-reverse transcriptase (no-RT) and no-template controls and were run on an AriaMx Real-Time PCR system (Agilent) under standard cycling conditions.

TGFβ treatment of THP-1 cells and FACS analysis of HLA-II expression

To enhance endogenous TGFβ expression, THP-1 cells were treated with TGFβ1 (5 ng/mL; ThermoFisher Scientific) 48 h prior to assaying. HLA-II surface expression was assessed by dividing cells for fluorescence minus one (FMO) control or HLA-II staining. Fc receptors were blocked for 10 min, followed by 30 min staining with HLA-DR,DP,DQ-FITC (BD Biosciences) and NIR viability dye.

Isolation and identification of circulating memory CD4+ T cells specific to TGFβ-33

CD4⁺CD45RO⁺ memory T cells were enriched using the human Memory CD4⁺ T Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec).42 CD14⁺ monocytes were isolated from PBMCs using MACS CD14 MicroBeads16 and pulsed with peptide (10 nM) for 2 h. CD4⁺CD45RO⁺ memory T cells were isolated from the CD14− fraction and co-cultured with peptide-pulsed monocytes. IL-2 (120 U/mL) was added the following day. After 12 days, cells were restimulated with TGFβ-33 and analyzed by ELISPOT and ICS. Isolation efficiency was verified by staining with NIR viability dye and surface markers using appropriate FMO controls: CD8-BV605, CD4-BV711 (both from Nordic Biosite), CD45RO-PE, CCR7-PE, CD14-PE-CF594, and CD3-AF700 (all from BD Biosciences).

Statistical analysis

ELISPOT data were analyzed using the distribution-free resampling (DFR) and the more conservative DFR(2×) methods as described by Moodie et al.43 Normalized spot counts were calculated by subtracting negative control means from the means of the peptide-stimulated wells.

Peptide screening data were analyzed using a one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Unpaired data were assessed with a two-tailed non-parametric Mann-Whitney test. Survival distributions were compared using the Kaplan-Meier analysis with the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test; median survival and hazard ratios (95% CI) were calculated. Graphs and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism V.10.4.1.
