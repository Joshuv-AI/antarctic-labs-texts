A 78-year-old female, with Sjögren syndrome, treated hypertension, controlled atrial fibrillation, previous central retinal artery occlusion, and a previous transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) for aortic stenosis 6 months earlier, presented with gradual onset breathlessness.

She had no occupational dust exposure and was an ex-smoker (10 pack-years smoking history). 2 years ago, forced expiratory vital capacity was 68% predicted. A transthoracic echocardiogram recorded the week before showed a normal left ventricle size with hyperdynamic systolic function, a basally dilated right ventricle with good function, grossly dilated left and right atria, a well-seated and functioning TAVI, severe mitral stenosis due to annular calcification with moderate mitral regurgitation, mild pulmonary regurgitation, mild tricuspid regurgitation and a high echocardiogram probability for pulmonary hypertension (pulmonary artery systolic pressure of 49 mmHg with the right atrial pressure added and a peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity of 3.5m·s−1). Her medication list was furosemide 80 mg once a day, atorvastatin 40 mg once a day, sulfasalazine 1 g twice a day, Revlar Ellipta 1 puff once a day, allopurinol 400 mg once a day, apixaban 5 mg twice a day and digoxin 125 µg once a day.

She had reduced air entry at the right base with dullness to percussion, a diastolic murmur and bilateral pitting oedema. A chest radiograph showed a right-sided pleural effusion (figure 1a). Under asepsis, ultrasound guidance and local anaesthetic, 1.2 L of dark milky pink pleural fluid was removed (figure 1b). Fluid analysis showed pH of 7.56, glucose 8.4 mmol·L−1, lactate dehydrogenase 103 U·L−1, total protein 28 g·L−1 (serum levels were 67 g·L−1), pleural fluid triglycerides 11 mmol·L−1 and pleural fluid cholesterol of 1.1 mmol·L−1. Chylomicrons were seen. Blood samples taken prior to the procedure noted haemoglobin 124 g·L−1, platelet count 236×109 per L, red blood cell count 4.08×1012 per L and haematocrit 0.375 L·L−1. Pleural fluid cytology showed a moderately cellular specimen containing varying size groups of mesothelial cells in a background of macrophages, lymphocytes and neutrophils. No atypical cells were identified. A computed tomogram (CT) showed no pleural thickening, masses or nodal disease. Abdominal ascites was absent in further imaging including on abdominal ultrasound and CT (figure 2 shows a representative slice).

There was a rapid clinical improvement, and furosemide was increased to 80 mg twice a day.

Answer 1

All options (a–d) are potential causes of the transudative chylothorax. Explanation regarding option c as part of the answer: the thoracic duct crosses over from right to left at T4–T6. Therefore, an injury from a cardiac procedure could be either side, depending on the nature of the procedure.

There was no nodal disease or masses on the CT to suggest a malignant process. There was no significant pleural thickening for a targeted biopsy, and a blind pleural biopsy would not have been sensitive enough for an actionable result. Flow cytometry of the pleural effusion showed no evidence of a lymphoid population/myeloid precursors, which effectively showed that they are not the cause for the effusion. Given the relatively recent TAVI, a thoracic duct injury was possible. A lymphangiogram was carried out. 8 mL of lipiodol was injected bilaterally through the inguinal nodes. There was a normal passage through the pelvic and abdominal lymphatics and normal appearances of the thoracic duct with no leak. There was sluggish drainage from the termination of the thoracic duct and lympho-venous junction. At the same time, 1.5 L of chyle was aspirated, and the pleural space was dry afterwards. 7 days later, the fluid had re-accumulated, and she was breathless again.

The diagnosis was of recurrent transudative chylothorax due to heart failure with preserved ejection fraction and mixed mitral valve disease, with the mitral stenosis being the main culprit. She was reviewed by the interventional cardiologists, and concerns were raised about her increasing frailty (her legs were heavily oedematous, and she could mobilise indoors, using a wheelchair when outside) and suitability for a general anaesthetic. A cardiac CT showed marked mitral annulus calcification with thickening of the valve leaflets suggestive of mitral stenosis, although the approximate planimeter orifice area was just within normal range. There was marked bi-atrial dilatation, particularly the left atrium, with bowing of the inter-atrial septum in keeping with raised left atrial pressures. There was mild left ventricular dilatation with preserved function. After several multidisciplinary team meetings, she was deemed unfit for open cardiothoracic repair of the mitral valve, and a percutaneous repair of the mitral valve was also not possible due to the quantity of calcium and its distribution.

As the above assessments were happening, she was undergoing weekly therapeutic aspirations due to rapid pleural effusion re-accumulation. We discussed her case widely, and most notably in the UK's national pleural multidisciplinary meeting.

Answer 2

a.

Due to her frailty, the risk associated with a general anaesthetic, and no leak on the lymphangiogram, the benefit of duct ligation was doubtful. We had been hopeful that the lipiodol injection could have helped the leak, with the next step being embolisation. However, the risk of developing chylous ascites was one the patient did not agree to. She had been on a medium chain triglyceride diet from day one with little effect. She was also keen to avoid hospital admissions, and we felt that she was too frail for further measures such as total parenteral nutrition and an octreotide infusion. In addition, the rate of fluid re-accumulation (∼400–450 mL per day) was too much for talc slurry. Ongoing recurrent aspirations were not an option anymore, as she found it increasingly difficult to travel. After her first few aspirations, we had discussed an IPC, but our concern was the risk of pleural space infection, which might have prevented any cardiological intervention. However, now, we proceeded with an IPC insertion, which was done under local anaesthetic and was uneventful. She was still peripherally oedematous, and her furosemide was periodically increased and then reduced whilst balancing her blood pressure and kidney function. She was draining ∼450 mL a day via the IPC and did so over the next 4 weeks with good symptomatic relief. She then abruptly started leaking from her IPC site, and the IPC itself stopped draining. A thoracic ultrasound showed a very loculated space. Her C-reactive protein (CRP) was increased at 190 mg·L−1 (normal value: <5 mg·L−1). The IPC site was red and painful. The IPC itself flushed easily and 10 mL of fluid was aspirated. A concurrent pleural tap was not done as she had still been taking her apixaban. Broad spectrum intravenous antibiotics (piperacillin/tazobactam) were started.

The next day, she was urgently seen as the IPC site was leaking, and the cuff had migrated out of the subcutaneous tract. Air was being sucked in through the fenestrations which were outside the pleural space. The IPC was removed, and an urgent chest radiograph showed a small pneumothorax, which did not require intervention. A stoma bag was placed onto the wound to collect any pleural fluid leaks, and a thoracic ultrasound showed a small loculated effusion. The CRP improved, and overall, the patient felt better, so no further intervention was required. The pleural fluid grew Acinetobacter radioresistens, Aerococcus urinae and Corynebacterium pseudodiphtheriticum, which were felt to represent colonisation of the IPC, given the normal white cell count and apyrexia. But, to cover for a potential pleural infection, a 10-day course of oral amoxicillin 500 mg three times a day, and co-amoxiclav 625 mg three times a day was started.

It is worth noting that concurrently, optimisation of her fluid status was attempted with increasing doses of diuretics and fluid restriction. Development of a degree of kidney dysfunction and postural hypotension prevented increasing doses. Within a week of the IPC removal, a large right-sided pleural effusion had re-accumulated with symptoms. During that time, due to a fall, she underwent brain imaging, which showed a large extra-axial dural based lesion in the left frontal lobe which extended along the anterior falx. It showed homogenous intense enhancement on the contrast study suggesting a meningioma. There was midline shift of about 8 mm to the right side and some oedema. Dexamethasone was started, and overall, a palliative approach was adopted. Due to increasing breathlessness, a new right-sided IPC was inserted. At this time, the colour of the pleural fluid had changed, prompting repeat pleural fluid analysis. Analysis showed fluid lactate dehydrogenase of 173 U·L−1, total protein <30 g·L−1 (serum levels were 71 g·L−1), fluid cholesterol 0.6 mmol·L−1, and fluid triglycerides of 0.4 mmol·L−1. The fluid was not a chylothorax anymore, and was simply a pure transudate, presumably due to her heart failure. Volumes gradually decreased over 4 weeks, and the IPC was removed. The patient was discharged to the care of the community palliative team (figure 3).

Discussion

A brief discussion about chylothoraces is warranted. Chylothoraces are rare, accounting for ∼3% of all pleural effusions [1]. They are the accumulation of lymph (a milky substance formed in the small intestine) mixed with emulsified dietary fats (chylomicrons) and fat-soluble vitamins in the pleural space. They have a classical milky white appearance, but this is only present in 50% of cases depending on the nutritional state of the patient, so pleural fluid analysis is imperative. Raised triglyceride levels >1.24 mmol·L−1 (110 mg·dL−1) and low cholesterol levels <5.18 mmol·L−1 (200 mg·dL−1) confirm the diagnosis, as does the presence of chylomicrons. The majority are exudative [2] by Light's criteria [3]. It was clear here that we were dealing with a chylothorax; what was unusual was that it was a transudate.

A chylothorax forms due to either the rupture or disruption of the thoracic duct, which is a 35–46 cm long structure that arises at the cisterna chyli in the abdomen. However, ∼10% of chylothoraces are idiopathic [1]. The thoracic duct has a long circuitous route (see figure 1 of Bhatnagar et al. [4]) and pathology at any level can result in chylothoraces. The commonest aetiological factors are malignancies such as lymphoproliferative disorders and rupture of the thoracic duct. Concurrent diagnoses such as malignant pleural effusion can contribute to the different colours of chylothoraces [5]. The classical scenarios include trauma to the thoracic cavity or surgeries such as oesophagectomies. Thoracic duct injuries because of catheter-delivered devices have been very rarely described and are usually due to left subclavian vein thrombosis [6], and were not shown on the patient's CT scan. A lymphangiogram would have identified a relatively high-volume leak [7, 8], and there was an approximately 50% chance of improving her symptoms due to the sclerosant effect of lipiodol, this was unsuccessful.

Transudative chylothoraces are rare. In 74 patients, Maldonado et al. [9] reported that 14% of included chylothoraces were transudative. They are associated with cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, cardiac failure and constrictive pericarditis [10–12]. Similar rates were observed by Porcel et al. [2]. Specifically for heart failure, it is thought that the increased central venous pressure raises flow through the thoracic duct [10, 13] and combines with the increased stiffness of the veno-lymphatic junction in the neck. There is a subsequent reduction in lymphatic flow, which then increases hydrostatic pressure, and reduces lymph reabsorption (which can lead to effusions forming). We surmise that her chylothorax was caused by her underlying heart failure, driven by her dysfunctional valves. Chylothorax in the context of mitral stenosis in the context of previous rheumatic heart disease has been described [14], and also in the context of mitral regurgitation [15].

Treatment of the underlying aetiology is recommended (in this case, diuresis was tried, and the valve could not be repaired or replaced). Repeated thoracocentesis can be used where fluid is causing significant compromise or accumulates rapidly. Prevention of effusion recurrence is challenging. Dietary modifications, such as a medium-chain fatty acid (MCT) diet can be used, replacing long-chain fats to reduce chyle flow, by focusing on MCT oils, lean proteins and low-fat dairy to bypass the lymphatic system, being directly absorbed by the portal circulation [4]. Additionally, avoiding high-fat foods like fatty meats, butter, nuts and oils to decrease the fat absorption by the gut allows the lymphatic system to heal [16]. This can be effective with or without enteral nutrition [16] and can be followed with total parenteral nutrition and somatostatin analogue (e.g. octreotide) infusion [17, 18]. To prove the efficacy of a MCT diet, a serial measurement could potentially have shown this. Unfortunately, this was not undertaken, therefore whether the MCT diet was beneficial in changing the nature of the fluid cannot be said for certain.

A patient-centred approach of repeated therapeutic aspirations was initially adopted. Due to the volume of chyle being produced, a chest drain and attempt at pleurodesis could not be done. We perform all advanced pleural procedures (including IPC placement) in theatre under strict aseptic conditions. The rate of IPC-related pleural space/skin infections is generally low (<5%), but we surmised that her risk would be higher as this was a transudative effusion, and that any infection could further reduce the chance of any cardiac interventions. The REDUCE study by Walker et al. [19] randomised 33 patients to an IPC and 35 to therapeutic thoracocenteses, with most patients having heart failure. There was no difference at 6 weeks in the mean breathlessness scores between the two groups, but there were more adverse events such as infection in the IPC group [19]. When her care shifted to a purely supportive approach, an IPC was inserted to minimise travel (as it took over an hour to travel to and from the hospital) and reduce procedure burden. This was in accordance with the European Respiratory Society consensus statement on the multidisciplinary management of patients with chylothorax [18]. Single centre studies have described the use of IPCs for effective symptom control in patients with chylothorax in advanced malignancies [20] and benign (non-cancerous) causes with favourable outcomes [21].

Infection rates from IPCs are low (<5% is an often quoted number), and many factors are contributory – how aseptically the IPC is inserted, the nature of the effusion and post-insertion drainage. There is some observational evidence pointing towards giving antibiotics at the time of IPC insertion, but there are no randomised controlled trial data, although one looking at topical antibiotics is under way [22]. IPC infection and colonisation are not well understood. IPCs get colonised very quickly, and samples from the tube usually grow multiple organisms, and the significance of those are unclear. Colonisation does not usually suggest infection as Sethi et al. [23] have demonstrated. Simultaneous pleural taps are usual as we have shown before [24], which were not performed here for the reasons described earlier.

Chylothoraces are considered bacteriostatic, with secondary infections rare [25]. Tuberculosis, filariasis and histoplasmosis can be implicated, although tuberculous effusions are more commonly associated with pseudo-chylothorax [26]. Infected chylothoraces have been described post-surgery and with prolonged drainage [27]. Post-infection pleurodesis is well described [28] and led to the second IPC being shortly removed afterwards. The resolution of the chyle leak in the interim, and the effusion turning into a pure transudate, associated very commonly with heart failure, is peculiar. The transudative nature of the chylothorax was due to heart failure producing an effusion which mixed with chyle. We surmise that increased diuresis in the late stages of the disease process enabled the resolution of the chyle leak as the pressures in the lympho-venous neck junction finally reduced – although it is worth noting that the patient had been on similar doses of diuretics before and had suffered from significant postural hypotension and kidney dysfunction. Once a predominantly palliative focus was adopted, kidney function was not monitored and with the patient's increasing immobility, postural symptoms did not affect her [12].

Conclusion

We describe a case of recurrent chylothorax due to severe mixed mitral valve disease and heart failure. Conservative and interventional measures failed, and according to the patient's wishes, an IPC was inserted, which caused an infection. This was successfully treated, and the IPC eventually removed due to pleurodesis of the pleural space. This case highlights the various causes of chylothorax such as cardiac conditions, and therefore having pleural fluid analysis is instrumental in the diagnostic process.
