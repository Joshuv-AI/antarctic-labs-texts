Trends in the use of sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists among ischemic stroke patients
Authors: Qinglan Ding, Jason J. Sico, Anthony J. Perkins, Laura J. Myers, Joanne K. Daggy, Ali Sexson, Stanley E. Taylor, Laura Burrone, Kimberly Waddell, Dawn M. Bravata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStroke is a leading cause of death and disability. Trials suggest that sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT-2i) and glucagon-like peptide-receptor-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) reduce cardiovascular risk in selected populations.ObjectivesTo describe facility-level use of SGLT-2i/GLP-1 RAs among patients hospitalized with ischemic stroke in the US Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) hospitals and to examine facility determinants of prescribing.MethodsWe performed a retrospective trend analysis across 88 VA facilities (fiscal years 2020–2023). Prescribing was ascertained from VA pharmacy data; the outcome was the proportion of ischemic-stroke admissions receiving an SGLT-2i or GLP-1 RA. Facility-level binomial logistic models evaluated associations with VA hospital complexity (1, high; 2, low complexity), rural-serving, and volume.ResultsAmong 17,093 admissions, facility-level prescribing ranged from 13.2% to 38.3%. Overall use increased from 21.8% (2020) to 30.1% (2023; P < 0.001), driven largely by patients with diabetes (38.9% to 51.5%); rates also increased in overweight patients (27.1% to 34.3%) but remained low in those without diabetes or overweight (3.4% to 7.0%, P = 0.010). Across years, 45.7% of patients with diabetes received therapy versus 7.8% without diabetes. In adjusted analyses, prescribing rates were lower at complexity 1b (OR 0.8, 95% CI 0.66–0.96) and 1c (OR 0.69, 95%CI 0.58–0.83) versus 2; rural-serving and volume were not associated with prescribing.ConclusionsUse of SGLT-2i/GLP-1 RAs among patients with ischemic stroke increased modestly but remained low in those without diabetes. Substantial between-facility variation—particularly low prescribing rates at complexity class 1b/1c sites—highlights the need for future studies to evaluate barriers in prescribing.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT04322162. Registration Date of 04/02/2020.

Background

Ischemic stroke is the second leading cause of premature death worldwide and a major cause of long-term disability [1]. Patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D) have a 2.5 to 3.5 times higher risk of ischemic stroke than non-diabetic individuals [2, 3]. Recently, novel antihyperglycemic agents, including sodium glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT-2i) and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs), have shown efficacy in cardiovascular risk management, decreasing major adverse cardiovascular events [4–8]. GLP-1 RAs, in particular, have demonstrated ischemic stroke risk reduction in both diabetic and non-diabetic populations [6]. SGLT-2i exhibit a neutral effect on ischemic stroke risk but protect against hemorrhagic stroke in high-risk diabetic patients [4, 5, 9].

Further supporting clinical relevance beyond glycemic control, the SELECT trial showed that semaglutide (a GLP-1 RA) decreased major adverse cardiovascular events, including nonfatal stroke, in adults with existing cardiovascular disease who were overweight or obese but without diabetes [8]. Guidelines from the American Heart Association (AHA) and American College of Cardiology (ACC) recommend these agents for cardiovascular risk reduction in high-risk patients, regardless of diabetes status [10, 11], and the AHA/American Stroke Association (ASA) guidelines highlight the importance of obesity management in patients with cerebrovascular events [12]. Despite these recommendations, prescribing practices for these agents vary significantly across specialties, with limited clarity on their use by neurologists and primary care practitioners for stroke patients, particularly those without T2D [13–15].

The Veterans Affairs (VA) health system is the largest integrated healthcare system in the United States with a uniform formulary [16], and low out-of-pocket costs, which help make cost-related underuse less prevalent than in non-VA settings [17]. The Veteran population also carries a high burden of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and atherosclerotic disease [18], making it possible to evaluate the adoption rates of SGLT-2i and GLP-1 RAs at healthcare facilities that differ in complexity and rural location across the country.

Given the established cardiovascular benefits of SGLT-2i and GLP-1 RAs, it is essential to examine how these treatments are used at different facilities to characterize variation in prescribing patterns, thus creating opportunities to explore barriers to adoption. This study aimed to assess the variation in the use of these agents for ischemic stroke patients across US Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) facilities nationwide. It also sought to compare their utilization between patients with and without T2D, and those categorized as overweight or obese. The research specifically examined facility-level usage patterns and cannot determine patient-level appropriateness or clinical benefit.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective trend analysis among VA facilities with at least 10 ischemic stroke admissions per year (fiscal years [FY] 2020 to 2023). The cohort included Veterans admitted for ischemic stroke who had at least one VA primary care visit or hospital admission in the two years before the index stroke admission (to ensure adequate patient characteristic data). If patients had multiple stroke admissions during the study period, we used the most recent for analysis. Ischemic stroke and diabetes status (type 1 and 2) were determined using International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) diagnosis codes. Exclusion criteria were in-hospital death, hospice or palliative care prior to discharge, acute care transfer, and significant renal impairment (dialysis, end-stage renal disease, creatinine ≥ 2.5 mg/dl, or estimated glomerular filtration rate < 30 ml/min). Patient-level characteristics included age, sex, race, overweight status based on body mass index ≥ 25 kg/m2, and comorbidities. Mean hemoglobin A1c levels were also assessed using VA Corporate Warehouse Data.

The primary outcomes were facility-level utilization rates of SGLT-2i and GLP-1 RAs among ischemic stroke patients. Medication use was identified using VA pharmacy data for patients who received at least a 30-day supply within 30 days post-discharge. Given that facilities were the unit of analysis, prescribing rates should not be interpreted as the percentage of eligible patients who received treatment.

We calculated facility-level utilization rates for each year (FY2020-2023) across key subgroups: patients with T2D, overweight patients, and those without T2D or overweight. Descriptive statistics compared characteristics between patients receiving SGLT-2i or GLP-RAs and those not receiving medications. Mixed effects logistic regression, with a random effect for VA medical centers and a fixed effect for the variable of interest, assessed temporal changes in prescription rates; p-values were obtained from the logistic regression models.

To better understand facility characteristics associated with the adoption of SGLT-2/GLP-1 RAs among ischemic stroke admissions, we modeled facility adoption (number prescribed/ischemic-stroke admissions) with binomial logistic regression including VA complexity class, rural-serving designation, and facility volume (log of the number of admissions). We report odds ratios (ORs) with 95% Cis and covariate-adjusted predicted percentage by complexity class. The VA Facility Complexity Model categorizes each medical center into one of the five levels (1a, 1b, 1c, 2, 3) by integrating patient volume and risk, clinical program complexity, and the size of research and education activities [19]. Level 1a represents the highest complexity facilities with large volumes, highest-risk patients, and extensive clinical and academic programs, whereas Level 3 reflects the lowest complexity facilities with limited specialty services and minimal or no research and training infrastructure [19]. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, and analyses were conducted using SAS Enterprise Guide v8.3 [20]. The VA Central Institutional Review Board approved the study.

Missing data and sensitivity analyses

Body mass index (BMI) was missing for 153 patients (0.9%); therefore, BMI-dependent subgroup analyses were performed exclusively on complete cases. One facility lacked complexity classification and was designated as unknown. No other variables had missing values. Because admissions and internal-medicine bed count were highly correlated (r = 0.77), the primary facility model included log admissions only; we assessed collinearity and performed sensitivity analyses substituting bed count for admissions and excluding the site with unknown complexity.

Results

Our analytic cohort included 17,093 ischemic stroke patients from 88 VA facilities. There was substantial facility-level variation in the prescription rates of SGLT-2i and GLP-1 RAs. Overall, the facility-mean rate of medication prescription among ischemic stroke patients ranged from 13.2% to 38.26% (Fig. 1).

The overall utilization rates of SGLT-2i and GLP-1 RAs among ischemic stroke patients increased from 21.8% (2020) to 30.1% (2023, P < 0.001, Table 1). Among patients with diabetes, the prescription rates increased from 38.9% (2020) to 51.5% (2023, P < 0.001). Similarly, prescription rates increased among overweight patients from 27.1% (2020) to 34.3% (2023, P < 0.001). Prescription rates among patients without diabetes or overweight remained relatively low but increased over time, rising from 3.4% (2020) to 7.0% (2023, P = 0.010).

When comparing patient characteristics by medication use status (Table 2), patients receiving SGLT-2i or GLP-1 RAs were younger (mean age: 68.6 versus 71.2 years, P < 0.001) than those not receiving these medications. Additionally, a higher proportion of patients prescribed SGLT-2i or GLP-1 RAs had comorbid conditions such as ischemic heart disease (13.0% vs. 6.2%, P < 0.001), peripheral vascular disease (24.3% vs. 18.2, P < 0.001), heart failure (31.9% vs. 15.4%, P < 0.001), and hyperlipidemia (86.1% vs. 70.2%, P < 0.001). Overweight was also more prevalent among patients receiving these medications (80.8% vs. 64.6%, P < 0.001). Additionally, we noted that patients using these medications had higher HbA1c levels (mean: 8.1% vs. 6.3%, P < 0.001). Patients with T2D accounted for 73.3% of those receiving these medications, compared with only 32.2% of those not using them (P < 0.001).

The overall utilization rates of SGLT-2i and GLP-1 RAs were significantly higher among patients with diabetes than among those without (Table 2). Specifically, 45.7% of patients with diabetes received one of these medications, compared to 7.8% of non-diabetic patients. Similarly, 30.9% of overweight patients received one of the medications, compared with 16.1% of non-overweight patients. When considering the overlap of diabetes and overweight, among overweight patients, 49.3% of those with diabetes received the medications, whereas only 9.2% of overweight non-diabetic patients received them. In patients who were not overweight, the utilization rate was 34.0% for those with diabetes and 5.5% for those without diabetes. The interaction between diabetes and overweight status (Table 3) was not statistically significant (P = 0.390).

Among 88 VA facilities, the median number of admissions was 182.5 (IQR 108-262.5), the median internal-medicine beds were 66.5 (IQR 48–84), and the median adoption rate was 26.2% (IQR 23.3%-30.0%). The distribution of complexity was as follows: 39 facilities (44.3%) were classified as 1a, 20 (22.7%) as 1b, 19 (21.6%) as 1c, 9 (10.2%) as group 2, and 1 (1.1%) as unknown. A total of 15 facilities (17.0%) served rural populations (Table 4). Facility complexity was strongly associated with adoption of SGLT-2i/GLP-1 RAs (Type3 p < 0.001). In adjusted models (Table 5 & Fig. 2), complexity class 1b (OR 0.80, 95% CI 0.66–0.96) and 1c facilities (OR 0.69, 95% CI 0.58–0.83) had lower odds of prescribing than complex class 2 facilities. Rural serving status (OR 0.99, 95% CI 0.89–1.10) and volume (OR 0.92, 95% CI 0.83–1.02) were not associated with medication prescribing. Adjusted predicted adoption by complexity (controlling for rurality and volume) was 29.4% (95% CI 26.3%–32.8%) at class 2, 28.0% (26.4%–29.6%) at 1a, 25.0% (23.3%–26.7%) at 1b, and 22.4% (20.6%–24.4%) at 1c (Table 6) facilities.

Discussion

Our research focused on facility-level prescribing rates of SGLT-2i and GLP-1 RAs for patients admitted to hospitals with ischemic stroke, and did not evaluate individual patient eligibility, contraindications, or clinical outcomes. The findings illustrate differences in the use of these newer medications among VA facilities from FY2020 to 2023. We observed a modest overall increase in the use of these medications, particularly among patients with diabetes. Considerable variability in utilization rates was evident across VA facilities.

Despite increasing evidence of cardiovascular benefits from GLP-1 RAs in reducing stroke risk in individuals with T2D and overweight [8, 21], our findings suggest that adoption of these medications remained low among ischemic stroke patients. Because of the ecological nature of this analysis, we cannot determine whether patients who did not receive treatment were suitable candidates, had contraindications, or refused the treatment. Low prescribing rates observed in our study should not be interpreted as inappropriate care or missed opportunities among eligible patients. The finding is consistent with prior reports of infrequent prescription even in T2D patients, for whom guidelines endorse these agents for cardiovascular risk reduction [12, 13, 15, 22, 23]. The recently published SELECT trial highlighted the potential of GLP-1RAs for stroke prevention beyond glycemic control, showing a 20% reduction in major cardiovascular events, including non-fatal stroke, in non-diabetic patients with obesity [8]. Similar benefits have been observed with SGLT-2i, which may indirectly contribute to stroke prevention through effects on blood pressure, weight, and renal function [4, 9, 12]. These findings emphasize the need to clarify both the eligibility requirements and the shared decision-making process for these therapies after stroke, particularly for patients without diabetes.

This analysis describes that adoption was low rather than exploring why it was low. Administrative data cannot capture clinical decision-making, patient preferences, contraindications, or workflow constraints. Within the VA system, low prescribing rates at complexity 1b/1c facilities, independent of rurality and volume, suggest that structural capacity rather than geography, may contribute to variation in the update of novel antiglycemic therapies. The VA facility complexity is a composite measure that reflects staffing, scope of services, and clinical/programmatic complexity. Variations in adoption across complexity levels may reflect difference in the on-site availability of multidisciplinary cardiometabolic teams, academic resources, discharge medication pathways, and capacity for early post-stroke follow-up; however, because this measure aggregates multiple structural features, our data cannot isolate which components account for the observed differences. Future research is needed to identify low-prescribing outliers and then perform targeted barrier assessment (e.g., eligibility/contraindications by chart review and brief prescriber surveys or interviews about knowledge, comfort with non-diabetic indications, and process/formulary hurdles) to inform future site-specific quality improvement efforts.

Prior research indicated differences in prescription patterns for SLGT-2i and GLP-1 RAs across medical specialties, particularly among internists/primary care providers, cardiologists, and endocrinologists [13, 15, 22]. It may be that neurologists caring for stroke patients may be more conservative in adopting these therapies, particularly those without diabetes. This cautious approach may reflect intentional decision-making regarding peri-stroke clinical considerations (e.g., hemodynamic stability), alongside a lack of stroke-specific guidelines for these agents [24].

By examining the use of SGLT-2i and GLP-1 RAs in ischemic stroke patients with and without T2D, as well as with and without overweight/obesity, our findings extend prior research, which has focused mainly on diabetic populations. While these agents are endorsed by guidelines for general cardiovascular disease risk reduction [10], specific recommendations for their initiation in the post-stroke setting are less established, which may contribute to them being prescribed infrequently among ischemic stroke patients. Addressing this gap may involve conducting rigorous studies to evaluate stroke-related benefits of these agents, particularly for patients with overlapping metabolic and cardiovascular risks.

Limitations

Several limitations merit consideration. First, this investigation is an ecological, facility-based study focusing on prescribing practices rather than clinical outcomes. Consequently, this design precludes inferences about patient-level appropriateness, contraindications, or clinical benefits. Second, the available administrative data did not include prescriber specialty or clinical reasoning, such as contraindications, patient refusal, intolerance, peri-stroke hemodynamic concerns, or formulary/process constraints. Therefore, we cannot determine whether facility variation reflects differences in specialty involvement. Attributing low utilization solely to underadoption risks oversimplification, as administrative data cannot distinguish missed opportunities from prescriber intent or patient preferences. Third, as with any observational study, residual confounding by unmeasured facility factors remains. Future work could link pharmacy records with laboratory findings and outcome measures to evaluate whether adoption of these new agents translates into improved risk profiles and lower recurrent ischemic stroke events. Finally, because initiation may be deferred for clinically appropriate reasons, our 30-day window may underestimate longer term adoption.

Conclusions

To conclude, this study revealed a modest increase in the use of SGLT-2i and GLP-1 RAs among ischemic stroke patients within the VA system, mainly driven by prescription among patients with diabetes. The facility-level variation in prescribing these treatments highlights areas for future investigation into barriers to adoption. The findings pinpoint where implementation support is most needed and targets for future research; understanding why use is low at specific sites is essential before deploying targeted interventions. These facility-level differences describe variation in prescribing and should not be interpreted as evidence of poor-quality care, overuse, or underuse among ischemic stroke patients.
