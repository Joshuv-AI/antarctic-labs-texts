The association of premature atherosclerosis with ischemic stroke in young people with HIV in Lusaka, Zambia
Authors: Stanley Zimba, Owen Ngalamika, Emmanuel Mukambo, Theresa Shankanga, Bwalya Mulenga, Mike Chisha, Violet Kayamba, Lloyd Mulenga, Omar Siddiqi, Owen A. Ross, Masharip Atadzhanov, Deanna Saylor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Premature atherosclerosis has been observed among people with HIV (PWH) with high risk of cerebrovascular disease. The aim of this study was to evaluate premature atherosclerosis in young PWH with and without ischemic stroke.Methods:We conducted a prospective case-control study at the University Teaching Hospital in Lusaka, Zambia between March 2022 and October 2024, comparing young PWH with non-cardioembolic ischemic stroke (cases) with PWH without a history of stroke (controls) matched (1:2) for age, sex and race. Premature atherosclerosis was assessed using a Mindray DC-40, linear probe 8–13 MHz high resolution B-mode ultrasound to measure carotid intima-media thickness (cIMT; abnormal≥0.70 mm) and pulse wave velocity (PWV; abnormal≥10.00m/s).Results:We analyzed results for 50 cases and 100 controls. Compared to controls, cases were more likely to have traditional stroke risk factors such as hypertension (42 % vs. 1 %, p=0.001); shorter duration from HIV diagnosis (<3 months: 16 % vs. 1 %, p=0.001);and markers of atherosclerotic disease, including higher PWV [10.89 (9.99–12.15) m/s vs. 8.97 (8.16 – 9.54) m/s, p<0.001] and increased cIMT [0.79 (0.70–0.99) mm vs. 0.63 (0.58 – 0.67) mm, p<0.001]. Poor WHO HIV clinical stage (stage 3: aOR 58, 95 % CI 1–3213, p=0.04), higher PWV (aOR 8.7, 95 % CI 2.0–38.1, p=0.004) and urban residence (aOR 25.5, 95 % CI 1.6–413.4, p=0.02) were independently associated with ischemic stroke in multivariable analyses.Conclusion:In this cohort of young-onset HIV-associated non-cardioembolic ischemic stroke, premature atherosclerosis, as indicated by higher PWV, was independently associated with stroke.

Introduction

Stroke is the third leading cause of death worldwide, with 80 % of stroke-related deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)1,2. Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) shoulders a disproportionate HIV and stroke burden with the majority of the 35.3 million people with HIV (PWH) worldwide residing there, and it has the highest age-standardized stroke incidence, prevalence, and mortality rates worldwide3,4. HIV infection is an independent risk factor for stroke, doubling stroke risk5,6. The commonest form of stroke is ischemic, which accounts for about 80 to 85 % of strokes in Whites and about 60 to 70 % in Blacks and Asians7,8. About one-fourth of ischemic strokes occur in the working age population, with globally increasing incidence in this age group from the 1980s due to the HIV epidemic and its disproportionate burden in young adults below 50 years9. Almost half of the entire stroke burden worldwide now occurs in young patients aged <50 years - a cut-off age most often used to define “young-onset stroke”10–12. This cut-off age is biologically arbitrary, but it is known that clinical characteristics, risk factors and etiologies of stroke differ between younger and older people13.

Although traditional risk factors are accepted to play a role in HIV-associated stroke14,15, the role of HIV-specific mechanisms have not been clearly elucidated. HIV may cause young-onset ischemic stroke through accelerated atherosclerosis leading to HIV-associated vasculopathy, indirect effects of opportunistic infections16,17, or as a consequence of antiretroviral therapy (ART) drugs, particularly protease inhibitors (PIs), which are associated with metabolic syndromes that may increase stroke risk with prolonged use18. A study from Malawi, another country in SSA, found PWH are at highest risk of stroke within six months of ART initiation, possibly due to an immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS)-like process in response to HIV itself, which could result from drugs such as integrase inhibitors that rapidly suppress HIV19.

Carotid intima media thickness (cIMT) assessed with B-mode ultrasound is a well-recognised and validated surrogate marker for atherosclerosis in the cardiovascular system20. The presence of an increased cIMT has been shown to be predictive of future cardiovascular events, including stroke20,21. Pulse wave velocity (PWV) non-invasively measures large artery stiffness and is also a well-recognised marker of arteriosclerosis22. Therefore, cIMT and PWV measurements may assist in objectively identifying young PWH at risk of stroke. We evaluated premature atherosclerosis as a risk factor for stroke by comparing young PWH with and without ischemic stroke. We used the Trial of Org 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST) classification to select only individuals with non-cardioembolic ischemic strokes for a better evaluation of premature atherosclerosis23.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted at the University Teaching Hospital (UTH) in Lusaka, Zambia. UTH is an 1800 bed teaching hospital and the largest tertiary care centre in Zambia, a SSA country of approximately 22 million citizens24. The hospital serves a large population of patients referred from all parts of the country for both outpatient and inpatient care.

Study design and period

We conducted a prospective matched case-control study of young PWH with and without ischemic stroke from March 2022 to October 2024. Cases were PWH aged between 18 and 49 years with imaging-confirmed acute ischemic stroke within two weeks of symptom onset that was determined to be of non-cardioembolic origin using the TOAST criteria. Cases were recruited from the UTH inpatient neurology service and neurology clinics. The cases were matched with two asymptomatic controls by age- (± 3 years), sex- and race. Controls were consecutive patients accessing routine outpatient HIV care services at UTH who were confirmed to be stroke-free after a neurological assessment and had no history of other neurological disorders. They also answered the questionnaire for Verifying Stroke-Free Status (QVSFS) for negative reporting of stroke history to avoid bias of misclassification25,26. When answers for all items contained in the QVSFS were negative, they were labelled as “Control”. The QVSFS has a sensitivity of 95 % and a specificity of 96 %26. Individuals with sickle cell disease, cardiac diseases, meningitis or any other central nervous system (CNS) space-occupying lesions were excluded from the study. Similarly, HIV-uninfected adults with stroke were excluded on the basis of previous findings that they tend to be older than PWH and stroke counterparts and that premature atherosclerosis is an epi-phenomenon of HIV15,27,28.

Recruitment process and measurements

All participants underwent clinical history and neurological examination by a neurologist (SZ). Stroke severity was graded according to the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) and the modified Rankin Scale (mRS)29,30. Hypertension and diabetes mellitus were defined per World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines31,32. Hyperlipidaemia was defined by self-report, statin use or standard laboratory cutoffs, including low density lipoprotein (LDL) greater than 3.36 mmol/l, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) <1.29 mmol/l, total cholesterol >5.17 mmol/l or triglycerides greater than 3.88 mmol/l33. Cigarette smoking was classified as active smoker (current or former smoker for <1 year), passive smoker (household member or coworker who regularly smoked in his/her presence for more than one year during the last 10 years) or non-smoker, and alcohol use (ex-drinker for <1 year or current alcohol use).

Carotid Doppler studies were performed using a Mindray DC-40, linear probe 8–13 MHz high resolution B-mode ultrasound and using previously described methods34. A carotid IMT ≥0.7 mm was considered to be pathological, and suggestive of premature atherosclerosis20. Patients were placed in the supine position with their head in the midline position and tilted slightly upwards, and the heart in systole. For each participant, a total of eight measures were obtained: left and right carotid primitive and bulb region in both proximal and distal walls. The cIMT value was defined as the mean of all values excluding those corresponding to atherosclerotic plaque thickness. According to Manheim cIMT consensus, atherosclerotic plaque presence was defined as a focal structure encroaching into the arterial lumen by at least 0.5 mm or 50 % of the surrounding cIMT value34. Stenosis referred to vessel narrowing of 70 % or more35.

For pulse wave velocity (PWV), the examination began with the patient in a supine position and the carotid artery at the supraclavicular level (1–2 cm of the bifurcation) located using the B-mode ultrasound machine36. The wave doppler flow was identified simultaneously with electrocardiography. The process was repeated on the common femoral artery in the groin. Three recordings of the carotid artery and three recordings of the femoral artery in the groin were performed. Each recording involved at least two cardiac cycles. The transit time was determined by measuring the time from the R wave of QRS to the foot of the waveform using digital callipers. Six heart rate measurements were taken and the average was calculated. To determine the velocity, the distance from the sternal notch to the femoral artery at the groin was measured. A PWV above the threshold of ≥10.00m/s was considered as suggestive of increased large artery stiffness22. The cIMT and PWV examinations were performed by a single operator (MC), a radiologist who had undergone dedicated training in the relevant techniques. The operator was blinded to the category of the patient with regards to case or control.

Stroke classification

All brain imaging were interpreted by a radiologist (MC) and stroke classification was done independently by two study investigators (SZ and MA). Classification was adjudicated by a third neurologist (DS) when there was disagreement among the first two reviewers. Ischemic stroke cases were further classified using the TOAST classification23. Individuals with strokes from cardio-embolism were excluded from this study. It is important to mention that patient evaluation at UTH is hypothesis driven although all strokes receive a standard blood panel in addition to cardiac work up and neuroimaging. Extra investigations such as computer tomography/magnetic resonance angiographies and trans-oesophageal echocardiogram with gradient bubble are only done depending on the etiological hypothesis generated by the neurology/stroke unit team after clinical evaluation and/or standard results.

Sample size

This was an exploratory study with no previous studies to help with sample size calculations. Based on our clinical registry27, we estimated we could recruit 50 PWH with non-cardioembolic ischemic stroke during a one-year recruitment period which was lengthened by the COVID-19 pandemic. These cases were age-, sex- and race-matched (1:2) with 100 PWH without history of stroke.

Statistical analysis

All data were entered into a secure REDCap database hosted by the Zambian Ministry of Health Infectious Diseases Directorate and analysed using SPSS version 27 (IBM, New York, USA)37,38. Demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized with descriptive statistics. Chi square test was used to determine the association between categorical predictor variables and ischemic stroke. Since all the continuous variables were not normally distributed, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U-test was used to determine association between ischaemic stroke and continuous variables. P values of less than or equal to 0.001 were taken as statistically significant in order to correct for increased type 1 error due to multiple comparisons.

A multivariable conditional logistic regression model was then used to adjust for confounders and to identify factors independently associated with young-onset HIV-associated non-cardioembolic ischemic stroke. Variables statistically significant in bivariable analyses and those associated with stroke using biological plausibility were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. Different models were run, and we selected the best predictive model based on one with the largest area under the receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve, lowest Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and the highest likelihood ratio. We checked fitness of the model using Hosmer-Lame-Show goodness of fit test and confirmed that there was no multicollinearity using variance inflation factor (VIF) values. P values of <0.05 were taken as statistically significant.

In order to determine whether or not the known reference values for PWV and cIMT can also apply to our population of PWH, we plotted the cases and controls against PWV and cIMT values, and selected values that appeared more discriminatory between cases and controls. We then tested the reference values and our new values for sensitivity, specificity, and other parameters.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Zambia Biomedical Research Ethics Committee (No. 1945–2021), National Health Research Authority (REF: NHREB00008/30/09/2021) and permission was obtained from UTH management to conduct the study. Written informed consent to participate in the study as well as for publication was obtained from each of the study participants. In the event that the participant had altered mental status, informed consent was obtained from the participant’s designated surrogate. This only applied to cases as all controls were able to give informed consent.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

In total, 50 cases and 100 controls were enrolled (Fig. 1). All recruited participants tested negative for COVID-19 infection through antigen/PCR testing. For all cases, this was their first-ever stroke, and they presented with a median NIHSS score of 7 (3–17), and mRS score of 4 (2–5). The majority (n = 32; 64 %) of cases had either large artery atherosclerosis or small artery occlusion. Compared to controls, cases were more likely to have hypertension, a shorter duration from HIV diagnosis and urban residence. Cases had higher diastolic BP, were more likely to have a higher WHO HIV clinical stage, had poorer immunological and virological status compared with controls although not statistically significant. Cases were also more likely to have abnormal PWV and cIMT when compared with controls (Table 1).

Independent predictors of ischemic stroke

Our final and selected multivariable logistic regression model had an area under ROC curve of 92 % (Supplemental Figure 1). Poor WHO HIV clinical stage (stage 3 compared to stage 1, but not stage 2 versus stage 1), higher PWV, and urban residence were independent predictors of young-onset HIV-associated ischemic stroke in multivariable analyses (Table 2). We further plotted the cases and controls on PWV versus cIMT values in order to identify the best discriminatory values (Supplemental Figure 2). From here we determined that PWV≥9.54m/s and cIMT≥0.67 mm would be similar or even better cut off values for ischemic stroke risk in our population of young PWH. However, the original cut offs of PWV≥10.00m/s and cIMT≥0.70 mm had better sensitivity and specificity (Table 3).

Discussion

Premature atherosclerosis defined by higher PWV is an independent risk factor for young-onset HIV-associated non-cardioembolic ischemic stroke. This finding is novel as many prior studies of HIV-associated stroke have highlighted traditional risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes and smoking as the main risk factors for stroke39–41. It is important to note that most previous studies have investigated stroke risk in the general HIV population as opposed to our study which focused only on young-onset stroke, a group representing the most affected among PWH. Nevertheless, previous studies have also demonstrated that HIV-associated stroke tends to occur at a significantly younger age compared to stroke in HIV-negative individuals27.

Although both PWV and cIMT were associated with non-cardioembolic ischemic stroke, only the former showed independent association on multivariable conditional regression analysis. This finding might suggest that PWV is a better predictive tool for ischemic stroke risk in PWH, but further studies are warranted to confirm this. Nonetheless, PWV has been demonstrated to be an important predictor of premature atherosclerosis in other studies21,22,42,43. Our findings also suggest that the known cut off values for PWV and cIMT are applicable to the HIV population. This is an important finding especially in our setting where normative values are lacking even for the general population.

The subtypes of ischemic stroke based on the TOAST classification demonstrated that large artery atherosclerosis was the most common followed by small artery occlusion in our cohort. These findings are similar to our previous studies on HIV-associated ischemic stroke where we found large artery atherosclerosis as the most common, although our previous studies were in all adult PWH and did not exclude older individuals or individuals with concomitant conditions such as meningitis, cardiac disease and cardioembolic stroke27,44. However, other studies have found that most HIV-associated ischemic stroke tends to be from small vessel atherosclerosis, although findings in other SSA cohorts are variable40,41,45–47.

What is clear from our results is that young-onset HIV-associated non-cardioembolic ischemic stroke is mediated by both traditional and HIV-associated risk factors. Hypertension was a significant traditional risk factor associated with stroke in univariable analyses in our cohort while we did not find any association with diabetes, hyperlipidaemia, alcohol use or smoking. It is interesting that none of the traditional risk factors for atherosclerosis featured in the final regression model. This partly raises the concern for a non-atherosclerotic vasculopathy in these young PWH which has previously been described in PWH and stroke42. Shorter duration from HIV diagnosis and poorer WHO clinical stage were associated with ischemic stroke risk. These findings speak to a number of important aspects one considers when it comes to young-onset HIV-associated ischemic stroke. Firstly, HIV on its own is a risk factor for cerebrovascular diseases and stroke5,6,48. Our young PWH are at risk for stroke by merely having the HIV infection. They could also have some of the traditional risk factors because of HIV. Secondly, the interaction between HIV and traditional risk factors could have an additive effect and exponentially increase the risk of ischemic stroke.

Another crucial aspect when one looks at HIV-associated risk factors for stroke is ART. There have been many reports of a high rate of vascular events in the first year of ART49. A study from Malawi suggested that an IRIS phenomenon can occur as ischemic stroke within the first six months of initiation of ART19. Our findings revealed that more PWH with ischemic stroke were within the first 1 year of initiation of ART compared to controls, but the difference was not statistically significant. Similarly, a small case control study from Portugal did not find any difference in the median time from initiation of ART to stroke46. The role of ART in stroke risk is another important topic this study was not designed to answer as there are potentially different risks with different ART regimens, and there is long-term metabolic consequences associated with some ART regimens18,50–52.

Our data also showed that poor WHO staging was independently associated with risk of ischemic stroke. This is not an unusual finding and has been demonstrated in literature16,17. We excluded individuals with clinical signs of meningitis, but these findings could support the presence of co-existing HIV-associated infections such as Varicella Zoster, Ebstein Barr, Herpes Simplex and other viruses which might increase the risk of stroke in these patients53–56. Similarly, this could explain the significant univariable finding of shorter duration of HIV infection (<3 months) which was associated with ischemic stroke although the bulk of the HIV diagnoses (7/8, 88 %) were made at the time of presentation with stroke. We did not objectively confirm absence of these viral infections or examine for cerebrospinal fluid immune inflammatory signatures which might be important to elucidate these findings. Nonetheless, it is important to recognise that the actual mechanism of stroke in this population of young onset HIV-associated ischemic stroke is likely multifactorial with one of the firm foundations on which stroke occurs being premature atherosclerosis.

Another interesting result from our study was urban residence being an important predictor of ischemic stroke in our cohort. While UTH is located in an urban setting and is likely to be accessed easily by urban dwellers, it is also important to explain that it is a national referral hospital and sees its fair share of rural dwellers. It is important to add that urban dwellers are more likely to present with other risk factors such as sedentary lifestyle, westernized diet, stress of urban lifestyle with depression and anxiety tied to it. All of these could compound the traditional and HIV-associated risk factors and further increase stroke risk, but we did not assess these factors in our study.

Our study has several limitations including some findings were based on information from the routine care available at the hospital during the study. The BMI for a few patients could not be calculated because of lack of a proper weighing scale for bedridden patients. Resource limitations also meant that not every participant was thoroughly evaluated for stroke risk factors such as hyperlipidaemia and diabetes (Supplemental Table 1). Failure to complete brain imaging rendered some patients unable to be included in the study. This introduces the possibility of selection bias, which could have systematically excluded the most severely ill subjects and/or subjects with late-stage HIV. We did not thoroughly evaluate ART history including specific drug combinations the participants were on, although first line therapy was dolutegravir based. However, a strength of this study is that all participants received a thorough clinical evaluation by trained neurologists making the clinical characterizations of this cohort more reliable than in many other SSA populations in which participants are assessed by non-neurologist healthcare workers.

Another limitation is the lack of normative data on cIMT and PWV in our setting, but we were able to generate decision cut-off values which compared poorly with known cut-off values, thereby validating the latter’s use in young PWH for stroke prediction. The study would have been strengthened if we had assessed for intracranial atherosclerosis, but extracranial atherosclerosis is a reasonable proxy for the former34,57. Finally, this was a single-centre study with a relatively small sample size at a national referral hospital, which might make the generalizability of these results difficult even within Zambia, especially to first-level health facilities. Because this was an exploratory study, some of the associations found here may be due to chance alone. Nevertheless, our expectation is that these results are likely generalizable to similar settings in other populations of PWH in SSA, and they provide preliminary data that can be used to guide the development of interventions to reduce stroke risk in young PWH.

In conclusion, young-onset HIV-associated non-cardioembolic ischemic stroke is associated with both traditional and HIV-specific factors such as hypertension, shorter duration from HIV diagnosis, which could be connected to premature atherosclerosis. However, only poor WHO HIV clinical stage, urban residence and higher PWV were independently associated with non-cardioembolic ischemic stroke. Future research is needed to gain a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms leading to premature atherosclerosis in young PWH in order to allow for the development of targeted and effective interventions for both primary and secondary stroke prevention as well as acute stroke treatments for PWH.
