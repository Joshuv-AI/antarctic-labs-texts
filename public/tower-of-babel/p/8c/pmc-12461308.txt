Hydrochemical Evolution of Karst Groundwater and Mining-Induced Activation Effects in a Coal Mining Area: A Case Study from the Tengxian Coalfield, China
Authors: Bo Tian, Chang Lu, Deqiang Zhao, Jianyang Li, Haifeng Zhu, Fangying Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The prevention, control, and utilization of karst groundwater are prevalent issues in coal mining operations across North China. Karst water serves as both a potential hazard to safe coal production and an essential resource for domestic and agricultural use. However, decades of intensive, large-scale mining have significantly disrupted subsurface karst systems and intensified hydrogeochemical processes, particularly water–rock interactions. This study focuses on the Tengxian Coalfield in North China, where Ordovician and 14th limestone aquifers were sampled, and a multiwell drainage experiment was conducted in the Ordovician aquifer. Through a combination of multimethod approaches, the hydrochemical characteristics of karst groundwater under natural conditions and mining disturbances were analyzed. The evolutionary patterns and controlling factors of major ion concentrations in karst water were revealed. Results indicate that SO4 2– and Na+ are the dominant anion and cation, respectively, with Cl–Ca identified as the primary water type, alongside a subset displaying SO4–Na·Ca characteristics. Major ion sources include rock weathering and evaporative concentration, while mining-induced chemical evolution is primarily driven by cation exchange and anthropogenic activities. Under natural conditions, ion concentrations in the Ordovician limestone water remained relatively stable, indicating limited groundwater circulation and weak runoff. During water inrush events, enhanced water–rock interactions caused a transition in hydrochemical composition from low Na+(K+) and high Ca2+(Mg2+) to high Na+(K+) and low Ca2+(Mg2+). This research provides insights into the hydrochemical behavior and flow field evolution of karst groundwater in mining areas and offers a scientific basis for water hazard control and resource utilization under similar geological conditions.

Introduction

Large-scale coal mining is commonly accompanied by the dewatering of mine water, which disturbs the equilibrium and stability of karst groundwater systems. − In coal mining areas, karst groundwater poses dual significance: it is a major threat to mining safety and a critical water source for domestic and agricultural use. − Under natural conditions, karst groundwater typically exhibits good quality and is utilized extensively in many regions of China. However, mining-induced effects disturb the karst aquifer systems and alter groundwater hydrochemistry, thus threatening ecological balance. , Therefore, investigating the hydrochemical evolution of karst groundwater under mining disturbances is crucial for mine water treatment and resource recovery. ,

In recent years, the increasing focus on the ecological environment of mining areas and the active promotion of mine water reuse have shifted hydrogeochemical research toward the origin and mechanisms of groundwater in coal mining regions. , Current methods for studying groundwater hydrogeochemistry include field surveys, hydrochemical analysis, laboratory experiments, and numerical simulations. − Among these, hydrochemical analysis methods are the most widely applied. Tools such as Piper diagrams and Gibbs models enable the classification of water types and the identification of sources and hydraulic connections among aquifers. , Multivariate statistical methods help extract key information from large data sets, facilitating the efficient interpretation of ion composition patterns. Ion ratio analysis offers insights into the geochemical processes governing groundwater formation. Isotope tracing is also widely employed to identify recharge sources and detect groundwater contamination.

As a major coal-producing basin in China, the North China-type coalfield faces challenges such as karst water hazards, water quality degradation, and ecological imbalance. − The Tengxian Coalfield, located in this region, is significantly threatened by high-pressure Ordovician karst water beneath the coal seams. With increasing mining depth, disturbances to the karst aquifer system have become more pronounced, breaking the original groundwater equilibrium. , Previous studies have primarily focused on water quality evolution and mine water hazard control. − However, investigations into the hydrochemical evolution of karst water under mining-induced effects remain limited.

To address this gap, this study focuses on the karst groundwater of the Tengxian Coalfield. Groundwater samples were collected from the Ordovician and 14th limestone aquifers in both the southern and northern mining zones. Analytical methods, including the Piper diagram, correlation analysis, Gibbs model, ion ratios, saturation indices, and X-ray diffraction, were employed to explore the hydrochemical characteristics and controlling mechanisms. Furthermore, a multiwell drainage experiment in the Ordovician aquifer was conducted to reveal the evolution of major ion concentrations during mining-induced disturbances. The results provide a scientific foundation for water hazard prevention and resource utilization in coal mines under similar geological settings.

Study Area

This study investigates the hydrochemical evolution of karst groundwater under the influence of coal mining in the Tengxian Coalfield, located in Tengzhou City, Shandong Province, China. The coalfield represents a typical North China-type basin (Figure ), with geographical coordinates ranging from 116°45′E to 117°24′E and from 34°50′N to 116°17′N, covering an area of approximately 1495 km2. The stratigraphic sequence spans from the Ordovician to the Quaternary, with coal-bearing formations primarily found in the Carboniferous–Permian Taiyuan and Shanxi Formations. Hydrogeologically, the area is part of the loose alluvial plain of western Shandong, structurally controlled and characterized as a semiconfined to confined groundwater unit. The aquifers are deeply buried, with limestone formations exhibiting high water yields and variable permeability.

Location map and sampling points of the study area: ((a) map of China; (b) map of Shandong Province; (c) Tengxian Coalfield (North Part); and (d) Tengxian Coalfield (South Part)).

Ten representative coal mines were selected for this study. Among them, Chaili, Jiangzhuang, Cuizhuang, Daizhuang, Gaozhuang, Fucun, Qiwu, and Sanhekou Mines are located in the southern mining area, while Binhu and Xinan Mines are situated in the northern zone. These mines primarily exploit the 16th Coal from the Taiyuan Formation. Due to deep mining operations and complex geological conditions, frequent water inrush incidents, mainly from underlying limestone aquifers, have been reported. The Ordovician and Taiyuan 14th limestones serve as the principal aquifers threatening coal seam stability, characterized by substantial storage capacity, uneven water yields, and significant burial depth. Investigating the hydrochemical evolution of karst groundwater under mining-induced disturbances in this region offers valuable insights into water hazard mitigation and groundwater protection strategies for karst mining areas worldwide.

As shown in Figure , four major limestone aquifers are present: the 11th limestone, 12th limestone, 14th limestone, and Ordovician limestone aquifers, with thicknesses in the range of 2.14–8.63 m, 0.15–4.14 m, 10.38–16.55 m, and over 100 m, respectively. The average vertical distance from the 16th Coal to the 14th limestone and Ordovician limestone aquifers is approximately 30 and 54 m. Lithologies include limestone, mudstone, sandy mudstone, and argillaceous mudstone. Mineralogical analysis revealed quartz and feldspar as the main components in sandy mudstones, with significant clay mineral content, while limestones are composed predominantly of calcite, dolomite, and gypsum. The vertical spacing between the 14th limestone and Ordovician limestone aquifers ranges from 11 to 16 m (average of 13 m), and hydraulic connectivity may occur in areas with developed faults or fractures. Prolonged mine drainage has led to a regional decline in Ordovician limestone water levels, while water inrush incidents cause a rapid influx of Ordovician limestone water into the working face, both processes resulting in the hydrochemical evolution of karst groundwater.

Methods

Sample Collection and Analysis

A total of 30 karst groundwater samples were collected from the study area. In the southern mining zone, 20 samples were obtained, including 8 from the 14th limestone aquifer (coded SX1–SX8) and 12 from the Ordovician limestone aquifer (SO1–SO12). In the northern mining zone, 15 samples were collected, including 5 from the 14th limestone aquifer (NX1–NX5) and 10 from the Ordovician limestone aquifer (NO1–NO10). The sampling locations are listed in Figure .

All samples were analyzed at the Testing Center of the Shandong Coalfield Geology Bureau. The tests included measurements of major cations (Ca2+, Mg2+, K+, and Na+), major anions (HCO3 –, Cl–, and SO4 2–), pH, total dissolved solids (TDS), and SiO2. The results are presented in Table S1. The laboratory operates under strict quality control procedures aligned with national standards. To verify data reliability, the charge balance error (%CBE) was calculated for all samples. All samples had CBE values within ± 10%, confirming the validity of the data set. 1 %CBE=∑cation−∑anion∑cation+∑anion×100

Multiwell Drainage Test in the Ordovician Aquifer

To evaluate the hydrochemical evolution of karst groundwater under mining conditions, a multiwell drainage test was conducted in the Ordovician aquifer. The experiment included three drainage wells (2016–2, 2017–3, and 2017–4) and four observation wells (2018–1, 2018–2, 2019–1, and 2020–2). The test setup is shown in Figure .

Layout of the multiwell drainage test.

The experiment consisted of three stages during which Ordovician limestone water levels were continuously monitored. Water samples were collected at the beginning and end of each stage, yielding seven sets of groundwater samples (Table S2). The flow rate variations during the drainage process are illustrated in Figure . In Stage I (starting October 28), all drainage wells were opened, maintaining a total discharge of ∼60 m3/h, while the inrush site stabilized at ∼45 m3/h. In Stage II (starting November 3), well 2017–4 was closed, reducing total discharge to ∼ 40 m3/h, with the inrush site increasing to ∼50 m3/h. In Stage III (starting November 6), all drainage wells were shut, and only the inrush site remained active, discharging at ∼52 m3/h. The test concluded on November 8, when water levels reached equilibrium.

Results and Discussion

Groundwater Hydrochemistry

Ion Content Statistics of Groundwater

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the hydrochemical compositions of Ordovician limestone and 14th limestone aquifers in the study area. Table S1 provides summary statistics for the main chemical components in the southern and northern zones of the coalfield. The pH of limestone water samples ranged from 7.0 to 8.8, with a median value of 7.6, indicating that the groundwater was weakly alkaline. Concentration ranges of major chemical constituents (in mg/L) were as follows: K+ + Na+: 114.17–912.62; Ca2+: 62.12–689.10; Mg2+: 20.98–279.52; Cl–: 69.82–508.04; SO4 2–: 229.67–2675.90; HCO3 –: 3.97–258.11; TDS: 668–5254.

Boxplots of ion concentrations (Figure ) show that the average cation concentrations follow the order K+ + Na+ > Ca2+ > Mg2+, while anions follow the order SO4 2– > Cl– > HCO3 –. This pattern is consistent across the southern and northern coalfields. However, the 14th limestone water in the southern area shows greater variability, indicating more pronounced ion evolution, likely due to mining disturbances.

Hydrochemical Classification of Groundwater

The Piper diagram is widely used to visualize large datasets of groundwater chemistry and to analyze hydrogeochemical processes based on sample distribution. − Using the hydrochemical analysis module in Origin, major ion compositions of karst groundwater were plotted (Figure ). Most samples from both the 14th and 15th limestone and Ordovician limestone aquifers are located in Zone 6, indicating a predominant Cl–Ca water type. Some samples show features of the SO4–Na·Ca type, suggesting localized influence from cation exchange and rock weathering.

Piper diagram of limestone water samples from the Tengxian Coalfield.

In the cation triangle, groundwater samples from both aquifers were primarily classified as no dominant type (Zone B), with a few falling into the sodium type (Zone D). In the anion triangle, all samples were classified as the sulfate type (Zone F), with a concentrated distribution. A slight increase in Cl– was observed in some 14th limestone samples. These patterns suggest that cation exchange is a major factor controlling the hydrochemical composition of groundwater in the study area.

Correlation analysis was applied to assess the relationships among major ion concentrations. Figure a,b shows the correlation matrices for the 14th limestone and Ordovician limestone aquifers in the southern mining area, while Figure c,d represents those for the northern area. In general, weak correlations were observed among ions in the southern Ordovician limestone and both aquifers of the northern zone. However, in the 14th limestone aquifer of the southern zone, TDS was strongly correlated with Na+ + K+ (r = 0.91) and Cl– (r = 0.98), indicating a significant association with evaporative enrichment and anthropogenic influences.

Sources and Evolution of Major Ions in Karst Groundwater

Tracing Ion Sources through Rock Weathering and Dissolution Mechanisms

The Gibbs diagram is widely used to identify the controlling mechanisms of groundwater chemistry through the relationship between Na+, Ca2+, Cl–, HCO3 –, and TDS, distinguishing three end-member processes: evaporation–crystallization, rock weathering, and precipitation dominance. This method has been extensively applied in groundwater studies. , In this study, TDS values ranged from 668 to 5254 mg/L, with Na+/(Na+ + Ca2+) between 0.374 and 0.831 and Cl–/(Cl– + HCO3 –) between 0.296 and 0.991 (Figure ). These results suggest that the hydrochemical characteristics of karst groundwater in the study area are primarily controlled by rock weathering and the evaporation concentration.

Gibbs diagrams of water samples in the study area : ((a)­TDS vs Na/(Na + Ca); (b) TDS vs Cl/(Cl + HCO3)).

Spatially, TDS values were similar between the northern and southern mining areas, but higher Na+/(Na+ + Ca2+) ratios were observed in the 14th limestone aquifer in the south, suggesting the possible influence of mining-induced anthropogenic disturbances in addition to natural processes.

To further differentiate weathering processes, Ca2+/Na+ versus Mg2+/Na+ molar ratios were used to discriminate among silicate, carbonate, and evaporite weathering contributions. As shown in Figure , most groundwater samples cluster along the mixing line between silicate and carbonate end members, consistent with the regional geological background. Some samples fall within the intermediate zone, indicating a combined influence of carbonate, silicate, and evaporite dissolution. Southern samples from the 14th limestone show a tendency toward the silicate end member, implying a more complex water–rock interaction influenced by mining activities.

End- member diagrams of water samples in the study area : ((a) HCO3 –/Na+ vs Ca2+/Na+) (b) Mg2+/Na+ vs Ca2+/Na+).

The ratio relationships among major cations and anions also reflect water–rock interaction and ion origins. In Figure a, most samples lie above the 1:1 line in the Ca2+ + Mg2+ vs HCO3 – plot, especially in the southern zone, suggesting the dominance of carbonate dissolution. Na+, K+, and Cl– may originate from evaporite dissolution, anthropogenic input, and ion exchange. As shown in Figure b, most samples plot below the 1:1 line for Na+ + K+ vs Cl–, indicating the dominance of evaporite dissolution and significant cation exchange effects.

Cation Exchange

Cation exchange is typically evaluated using the milliequivalent relationship between (Ca2+ + Mg2+ - HCO3 – - SO4 2–) and (Na+ + K+ - Cl–). As shown in Figure a, with the exception of a few samples from the southern mining area, most karst groundwater samples are distributed near the 1:1 line, indicating that cation exchange processes are widespread in the aquifer system. This phenomenon can be described as 2 Ca2+(Mg2+)+2Na(K)X(rock)↔2Na+(K+)+Ca(Mg)X2(rock)

The magnitude of the chloroalkaline index (CAI) can be used to determine the intensity of cation exchange, and it is calculated using the following formula: 3 CAI‐1=Cl−−(Na++K+)Cl− 4 CAI‐2=Cl−−(Na++K+)HCO3−+SO42−

As shown in Figure b, all CAI-1 and CAI-2 values for the karst groundwater are negative, indicating that Ca2+ and Mg2+ are being exchanged for Na+ and K+, a typical signature of reverse cation exchange. This process leads to a decrease in the Ca2+ and Mg2+ concentrations and a corresponding increase in Na+ and K+ concentrations in the aqueous phase. The average intensity of cation exchange follows the order: southern 14th limestone water > northern 14th limestone water > northern Ordovician limestone water > southern Ordovician limestone water, with the 14th limestone generally exhibiting a stronger exchange effect than the Ordovician limestone aquifer. These results indicate that cation exchange is the dominant process controlling hydrochemical evolution of karst groundwater in the study area.

Variation of Mineral Saturation Index in Karst Groundwater

The saturation index (SI) is commonly applied to evaluate the saturation state of minerals in groundwater systems and is calculated using eq . An SI value less than zero indicates that the mineral is undersaturated and prone to dissolution; a value of zero suggests equilibrium between the mineral and the aqueous phase; and a value greater than zero implies that the mineral is supersaturated and likely to precipitate. 5 SI=log⁡IAPKsp

In the equation, I AP represents the ion activity product of the mineral constituents in the aqueous solution, and Ksp is the solubility product constant for the mineral dissolution reaction. In practical computations, a range of ± 0.5 is typically used to modify the equilibrium conditions. If the SI is less than −0.5, minerals are regarded as dissolved. When the SI is greater than 0.5, minerals are considered to be saturated. When SI lies within the range of −0.5 to 0.5, minerals are in a state of equilibrium dissolution.

Using PHREEQC software, saturation indices for calcite, dolomite, gypsum, and halite in karst groundwater were calculated for the study area (Figure ). The results show that, except for a few samples from the southern mining zone, the SI values of calcite, dolomite, and aragonite were close to zero, indicating that the groundwater is approximately saturated with respect to these carbonate minerals. The SI values of gypsum were generally less than zero, indicating an undersaturated state, though they approached saturation in some southern samples. The near-saturation or saturation states of calcite, dolomite, aragonite, and gypsum suggest that mineral dissolution and precipitation are not the dominant processes controlling the hydrochemical evolution of the karst groundwater.

In addition, XRD analysis was performed on limestone samples from the Ordovician limestone and 14th limestone aquifers (Figure ). The primary mineral components were identified as calcite, dolomite, pyrite, quartz, and clay minerals. The Ordovician limestone samples contained lower proportions of calcite and dolomite compared to the 14th limestone.

XRD spectra of rocks: (a) Ordovician Limestone; (b) 14th limestone.

These SI results are consistent with the ion correlation and ion ratio analyses. The dominant sources of Na+ and SO4 2– in the study area are halite and gypsum dissolution. Although some dissolution of Ca2+- and Mg2+-bearing carbonates occurs, evaporation concentration and reverse cation exchange contribute to increasing Na+ and K+ concentrations and decreasing Ca2+ and Mg2+ levels in groundwater, leading to a general increase in TDS in the aquifer system.

Hydrochemical Evolution under Mining-Induced Stress

Flow Field Evolution of the Ordovician Limestone Aquifer during the Drainage Test

The drainage test was conducted in three stages, with continuous monitoring of the Ordovician limestone water level throughout each phase. In Stage I, the system experienced the highest total flow rate, the largest drawdown, and the widest affected area. As the test progressed into Stages II and III, the drainage rate gradually decreased, and the influence on the flow field became more limited.

Water level variations at observation wells are shown in Figure . During Stage I, which lasted for 6 days, groundwater levels quickly declined and then gradually stabilized. The largest and most immediate drawdown (∼5.0 m) was recorded at well 2020–2, located closest to the drainage wells, indicating strong hydraulic connectivity in that zone. In contrast, well 2018–1, located farther away and to the east of fault F38–11, exhibited a slower and smaller decline with a clear delayed response, suggesting weaker connectivity across the fault. Well 2019–1, located near the inrush point, showed a distinct drop (∼4.0 m), whereas well 2018–2, positioned farther from the inrush zone and west of fault F121–1, displayed a slow and lagged decline, again reflecting the delayed hydraulic response.

Water level variation curves of observation wells : (a) 2018–1 borehole; (b) 2018–2 borehole; (c) 2019–1 borehole; and (d) 2020–2 borehole.

In Stage II, well 2017–4 was shut off, and the test continued for 3 days. The total flow rate decreased, and a notable rebound (∼3.0 m) in the water level was observed at well 2020–2, near the closed well. However, water level recovery at well 2018–1 remained limited, suggesting better recharge conditions on the western side of fault F38–11 and relatively low transmissivity across the fault. Meanwhile, well 2019–1 continued to decline, albeit at a slower rate, and well 2018–2, which had previously been stable, began to fluctuate. The zone of maximum drawdown shifted from the drainage wells to the inrush point, indicating a change in the local flow field.

In Stage III, all drainage wells were closed, and only the inrush point remained active. The water levels at wells 2020–2 and 2018–1 continued to recover, with well 2020–2 showing a more pronounced rise. Conversely, wells 2019–1 and 2018–2, located near the inrush point, experienced continued drawdown. Notably, well 2018–2 exhibited a faster rate of decline than well 2019–1. This suggests that prolonged water inrush may have enhanced connectivity between fault F121–1 and the surrounding aquifers, resulting in additional recharge to the Ordovician limestone aquifer.

Hydrochemical Evolution of Ordovician Limestone Groundwater

Ordovician limestone water samples collected during each stage of the drainage test were analyzed to evaluate the variation in the major ion concentrations. The ionic composition percentages are shown in Figure . The results indicate that Ca2+ was the dominant cation, accounting for 36.58–49.86% of total cations, with an average of 45.74%. This was followed by Na+ + K+ (37.35–52.83%, average 41.65%) and Mg2+ (10.60–14.00%, average 12.61%). Among anions, SO4 2– was the dominant species, comprising 77.99–79.81% of total anions (average 79.25%), followed by Cl– (15.38–15.86%, average 15.65%) and HCO3 – (4.44–6.15%, average 5.10%).

Proportions of major ion concentrations during the drainage test:(a) Cations;(b) Anions.

During the drainage process, ion concentrations remained relatively stable, with low coefficients of variation, indicating consistent hydrochemical characteristics across different sampling locations. The dominant cations were Na+, K+, and Ca2+, while the dominant anions were SO4 2– and Cl–. The water type was classified as SO4 2–·Cl–-Ca2+·Mg2+. The consistent ion variation patterns observed at each drainage well suggest that the Ordovician limestone aquifer in the study area exhibits good hydraulic connectivity.

Furthermore, a statistical analysis of TDS and pH variations across the three test stages is presented in Figure . The pH ranged from 7.56 to 8.05, with an average of 7.74, indicating weak alkalinity. TDS values varied from 4369.12 to 4464.66 mg/L, averaging 4411.12 mg/L. These results indicate that the Ordovician limestone aquifer exhibits limited hydraulic flushing, poor runoff conditions, and relatively stagnant groundwater circulation.

Based on the observed hydrochemical variations of Ordovician limestone groundwater in the Tengxian Coalfield and the regional hydroenvironmental circulation patterns, a conceptual model was developed to illustrate the evolution of flow pathways and hydrochemical responses during water inrush events (Figure ). The Ordovician limestone aquifer is identified as the primary source of deep mine water hazards, functioning as a pressurized reservoir with potential for progressive expansion and upward migration under stress. Under undisturbed conditions, the aquifer behaves as a closed and hydraulically stable system.

Evolution model of karst groundwater hydrochemical characteristics.

However, mining-induced disturbances act as a trigger, breaking the original hydrochemical and hydraulic equilibrium. This destabilization initiates fracturing and expansion within the aquifer, promoting vertical flow toward the overlying 14th limestone aquifer. Under the combined influence of mining stress and water pressure, the Ordovician limestone water gradually migrates upward through newly formed or reactivated pathways. As these processes continue, the water eventually breaches the coal seam floor and results in a sudden water inrus.

Hydrochemical analyses of original Ordovician limestone water show that it is characterized by low Na+ + K+ and high Ca2+ + Mg2+ concentrations. During water inrush events, the groundwater migrates through both primary and mining-induced fracture zones, substantially increasing the water-rock interaction surface area. This enhanced interaction promotes cation exchange processes, leading to elevated Na+ + K+ levels and a corresponding decline in Ca2+ + Mg2+ concentrations. Consequently, the hydrochemical signature transitions to a high Na+ + K+ and low Ca2+ + Mg2+ type, characteristic of typical mine inflow water. Overall, under mining-induced stress, the hydrochemistry of karst groundwater evolves both laterally and vertically, ultimately forming a distinct ion composition pattern driven by mining-related disturbances.
