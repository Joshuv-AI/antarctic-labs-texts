High sucralose exposure in early pregnancy increases the susceptibility of heart defects in mice offspring
Authors: Zehua Zhang, Ziwen Yu, Haiqun Shi, Tong Ding, Yan Shi, Jianyuan Zhao, Ke Cai, Feng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMaternal sucralose exposure during pregnancy has been demonstrated to interfere embryonic development, yet limited studies have investigated its potential hazards on fetal cardiogenesis. In the present study, we employed a mice model to investigate the impact of sucralose exposure in early pregnancy on the risk of heart defects in offspring. Pregnant C57BL/6J mice received either control or sucralose water. The incidence of heart defects in the sucralose group was 13.86%, significantly higher than that in the control group. Transcriptional downregulation of cardiogenic genes involving Gata4, Nkx2.5, Tbx5, and Tbx20 were confirmed, potentially due to increased CREB phosphorylation. N-acetylcysteine (NAC) reduced CREB phosphorylation, partially reversed sucralose-induced suppression of cardiogenic genes, and reduced the incidence of heart defects from 16.67% to 4.81%. Our study demonstrates that sucralose exposure during early pregnancy increases the risk of heart defects via transcriptional suppression of cardiogenic genes, while NAC potentially functions as a protective factor.Subject areas: endocrinology, developmental biology, embryology

Introduction

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is believed to be the most common birth defect in live newborns globally,1 resulting from both genetic and environmental factors.2 In the past decade, over-nutrition factors during pregnancy, such as diabetes, obesity, and high-fat diets, have been shown to increase the risk of CHD both in human and model animals.3,4,5 Currently, an increasing number of studies have focused on the impact of artificial sweeteners on reproductive health and embryonic development. As the most widely used artificial sweetener,6 sucralose is often regarded as an ideal substitute for sugar-containing diets in patients with diabetes and obesity since it neither raises blood sugar nor increases energy intake.7 Although sucralose is generally considered to be safe, some studies have reported its hazards on adverse pregnancy outcomes and offspring health.6 Therefore, the World Health Organization recommends women to avoid consuming artificial sweeteners such as sucralose during pregnancy.8

The intake of artificial sweeteners among pregnant women is demonstrated to be comparable to or even higher than that among the general population.9 Besides, the proportion of pregnant women consuming artificial sweeteners is increasing annually to as high as 30%, with the ratio up to 22.4% weekly and 13.1% daily, respectively.10,11 Additionally, the intake of artificial sweeteners during pregnancy tends to be higher than that before pregnancy.12 Generally, metabolic diseases comprising insulin resistance and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease were believed to be attributable to sucralose intake.13,14 Increased risks of cardiometabolic syndrome, atrial fibrillation, and cardiovascular disease have also been linked to high sucralose intake,15,16,17 thus leading to vascular endothelial dysfunction.18 Therefore, the outcomes of high sucralose exposure during pregnancy are in need to be assessed systematically both for mother and fetus.

Nowadays, sucralose has been reported in effluents and surface waters with concentrations of up to 155 ng/mL and 25 ng/mL, respectively.19 Following the consumption of sucralose-containing food or drinks, circulating sucralose can be detected in humans, with consumption of 250 mg sucralose resulting in plasma sucralose levels of around 200–400 ng/mL within 90–120 min,20 and it could persist in peripheral blood for over 18 h.21 It is reported that sucralose can pass through the placental barrier into fetal circulation, and its concentration in fetus is positively correlated with that in maternal plasma, resulting in its accumulation in amniotic fluid.22 However, high consumption of artificially sweetened beverages containing sucralose during pregnancy has been found to increase the risk of preterm birth,23 the rising body mass index and overweight in infants.24 Similarly, due to high sucralose exposure in pregnancy, mice offspring often presented with increased body weight, obesity, and insulin resistance. This metabolic disturbance was linked to an unrevealed mechanism involving alteration of pro-adipogenic and lipid metabolism-related genes.25 Inhibited intestinal development and impaired barrier function, manifesting with altered gut microbiome composition and diversity as well as mild inflammation, were also observed in 3-week-old mice offspring of mothers exposed to high sucralose during pregnancy.26 Meanwhile, zebrafish embryos exposed to high sucralose exhibited multiple malformations such as pericardial edema, scoliosis, and yolk deformation, which were associated with elevated oxidative stress and the upregulation of apoptosis-related genes.27 These findings suggest that high sucralose exposure during pregnancy can interfere with embryonic development, but its impact on embryonic cardiogenesis remains incompletely understood.

In the present study, we used the acceptable daily intake (ADI) recommended by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) (i.e., 15 mg/kg body weight per day) as the exposure concentration, and performed animal and cellular experiments to confirm the impacts of high sucralose exposure during early pregnancy on the incidence of heart defects in mice embryos. We further investigated the potential molecular mechanisms and intervention to minimize the hazards of high sucralose exposure during early pregnancy on embryonic cardiac development.

Results

Sucralose exposure during early pregnancy was associated with an increased risk of heart defects in mice offspring

Cardiac phenotypes of the embryos at E14.5 were examined using histological analyses. It was found that the proportion of pregnant mice bearing offspring with heart defects was much higher in the sucralose group (71.4%, 10 out of 14) than that in the control group (14.3%, 2 out of 14) (Figures 1A and 1B). In the sucralose group, 7 out of 101 live fetuses had atrial septal defects and 7 had ventricular septal defects, with an overall heart defects incidence of 13.86%, whereas in the control group, 3 out of 107 live fetuses exhibited atrial septal defects and none had ventricular septal defects, with an overall heart defects proportion of 2.8% (p = 0.0045) (Figure 1C). There were no significant differences in the number of live births, incidence of absorbed fetuses, body weight, and body size of the offspring between the two groups (Figure 1D). Therefore, sucralose exposure during early pregnancy might increase the risk of heart defects in mouse embryos.

It has been reported that sucralose exposure leads to abnormal metabolism of glucose and lipid.13,14 To rule out the inhibitory effect of early-pregnancy sucralose exposure on cardiogenesis in offspring via disturbing glucose and lipid metabolism or other high-risk factors, physiological evaluations of both the pregnant mice and embryos were confirmed. During the feeding period, the sucralose exposure did not affect the food intake, body weight, and fasting blood glucose of pregnant mice (Figures 1E, S1A, and S1C). However, the water intake was increased in the sucralose group, as compared with the control group (Figure S1B). Previous studies showed that sucralose could readily pass through the placental barrier and enter into the fetal circulation, and sucralose was also detected in the amniotic fluid, thus we measured sucralose levels in the maternal serum and amniotic fluid. Consistently, significant increases in sucralose levels were recorded in both maternal serum and amniotic fluid via placental circulation (Figures 1F and 1I). We also measured the levels of sucralose in the cardiac and liver tissues of pregnant mice. The results indicated an accumulation of sucralose in both tissues (Figures 1G and 1H). Correspondingly, sucralose accumulation was also detected in embryonic cardiac tissue (Figure 1J), thus potentially interfering embryonic heart development.

Sucralose exposure during early pregnancy inhibits the expression of transcription factors associated with embryonic cardiogenesis

To elucidate the mechanism through which sucralose exposure during early pregnancy elevates the risk of heart defects in offspring, we profiled the expression of key genes involved in cardiogenesis including Gata4, Gata6, Tbx1, Tbx3, Tbx5, Tbx20, Nkx2.5, and Notch1 in embryonic myocardial tissue in mice.28 As a result, the expressions of Gata4, Nkx2.5, Tbx5, and Tbx20 were specifically suppressed at the transcriptional level due to sucralose exposure (Figure 2A). To further validate this finding in vitro, neonatal primary mouse cardiomyocytes (NMCMs) were treated with 1 mM sucralose for 24 h—a concentration based on previous studies.29 Consistent with the in vivo data, sucralose treatment significantly downregulated the transcription of Gata4, Nkx2.5, Tbx5, and Tbx20 in NMCMs (Figure 2B). Furthermore, to determine whether the transcriptional suppression of key cardiac genes after sucralose exposure during early pregnancy exhibits a spatiotemporal specificity, we compared the mRNA levels of Gata4, Nkx2.5, Tbx5, and Tbx20 in the hearts (Figure 2C) and livers (Figure 2D) of pregnant mice between the two groups. No significant differences were found, indicating that the expression of cardiogenesis-related genes during early pregnancy is more sensitive to sucralose exposure.

Sucralose exposure reduces the expressions of key genes in cardiogenesis through the cAMP-CREB pathway

In order to explore the mechanism by which sucralose affects the expression of key cardiac genes, we further reviewed the literature and found sucralose can generate a sweet sensation via stimulating sweetness perception. The sweet-taste receptor is a heterodimeric class C G-protein-coupled receptor composed of TAS1R2 (taste receptor type 1, member 2) and TAS1R3 (taste receptor type 1, member 3) subunits.30 We further examined the expression levels of Tas1r2 and Tas1r3 in maternal and embryonic cardiac tissues. More Tas1r3 was detected than Tas1r2 in both cardiac tissues (Figure 3A). To confirm the expression disparity among different cells in cardiac tissues, we used western blotting analysis and found that TAS1R3 was specifically expressed in NMCMs instead of neonatal mouse cardiac fibroblasts (NMCFs) (Figure 3B). Furthermore, sucralose treatment showed no significant effects on TAS1R3 expression in NMCMs (Figure 3C).

As a biased agonist toward TAS1R3, sucralose interacted with TAS1R3, resulted in a distinct activation of the adenylate cyclase pathways and increased intracellular concentrations of cAMP.31,32 Since intracellular cAMP elevation in the cardiomyocytes during cardiogenesis is indispensable for the inhibition of T-box genes, cAMP alteration was believed to play a role in CHD.33 Activation of TAS1R3 was previously proved to induce CREB (cAMP response element-binding protein 1) phosphorylation.34 We therefore hypothesize that sucralose reduces the expression of key cardiogenic genes through TAS1R3-mediated activation of the cAMP-CREB pathway. We found that sucralose significantly increased the phosphorylation level of CREB in NMCMs compared to the controls (Figure 3D). It was the same case in AC16 cells (Figure 3E). Consistently, cellular immunofluorescence in HL1 also demonstrated an increase in the phosphorylation level of CREB after sucralose treatment (Figure 3F). To verify the role of sucralose-induced upregulation of CREB phosphorylation in different cardiac cells, we treated mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) and NMCFs with sucralose. It was demonstrated that sucralose did not increase the phosphorylation level of CREB in cardiac fibroblasts (Figures S2A and S2B). This suggests that sucralose-induced upregulation of CREB phosphorylation and subsequent suppression of target genes occurs specifically in cardiomyocytes.

Sucralose activates the cAMP-CREB pathway through TAS1R3

To confirm the role of TAS1R3 in sucralose-induced embryonic cardiac malformations, we utilized HEK 293T cells as a tool cell line to reveal the interaction between sucralose and the cAMP-CREB pathway by western blotting analysis. Although TAS1R3 level was not affected by sucralose, the phosphorylation level of CREB was significantly increased (Figure 4A). We then constructed a plasmid containing sgRNA targeted to knock out TAS1R3. As shown in Figure 4B, TAS1R3 was successfully knocked down by three different sg TAS1R3 RNAs, among which sg TAS1R3-3 was the most effective plasmid. What is more, the upregulation of the CREB phosphorylation level with sucralose treatment was diminished in HEK 293T cells transfected sg TAS1R3-3 (Figure 4C). These results suggest that sucralose might bind to TAS1R3 to activate the cAMP-CREB pathway.

It has been reported that NAC supplementation can reduce the phosphorylation level of CREB,35 thus we used 5 mM concentration of NAC to treat NMCMs and found that NAC can alleviate the over-phosphorylation level of CREB induced by sucralose exposure (Figure 4D). Meanwhile, compared to the control, while NAC alone treatment up-regulated Nkx2.5, Tbx5, and Tbx20 (not Gata4), sucralose + NAC group concurrently reversed the sucralose-induced suppression of all four cardiogenic genes (Figure 4E), further confirming that sucralose reduces the expression of key cardiogenic genes through TAS1R3 to activate the cAMP-CREB pathway in cardiomyocytes.

N-acetylcysteine supplementation can reduce the risk of heart defects in mice embryos caused by sucralose exposure during early pregnancy

Consistent with our previous results in NMCMs, which showed that NAC alleviated sucralose-induced CREB hyperphosphorylation and up-regulated the expression levels of cardiogenic genes (Figures 4D and 4E), we obtained further evidence in AC16 and HL1 cell lines. Western blotting demonstrated that NAC reduced CREB phosphorylation level induced by sucralose (Figures 5A and 5B). Consistently, cellular immunofluorescence suggested that NAC treatment inhibited the phosphorylation level of CREB in HL1 cells (Figure 5C).

We further verified the salvage effect of NAC on sucralose-induced suppression of embryonic cardiogenesis in mice. In the sucralose-water-fed mice, a daily dose of 1 g/kg NAC as reported previously,36 administered from E0.5 to E14.5, significantly decreased the susceptibility of heart defects in mice live fetuses, with the incidences of 16.67% compared to 4.81% (Figure 5D). HE staining was shown in Figure S3A, and the proportion of pregnant mice bearing offspring with heart defects was lower in the sucralose + NAC supplementation group (28.6%, 4 out of 14) than that in the sucralose group (71.4%, 10 out of 14) (Figure S3B). Additionally, compared to the control, while NAC alone supplementation up-regulated Tbx5 and suppressed Nkx2.5, sucralose + NAC supplementation group concurrently reversed the sucralose-induced suppression of all four cardiogenesis-related genes (Figure 5E). No significant differences in food intake and body weight gain among the four groups were observed (Figures S3C and S3E). However, the water intake was increased in the sucralose group and sucralose + NAC supplementation group, as compared with other groups (Figure S3D). There were no differences in the number of live births, weight and size of the embryos (Figures S3F, S3G, and S3H). These results suggest that NAC supplementation can counteract the suppression of cardiogenesis-related transcription factors caused by sucralose exposure in pregnant mice.

Discussion

The present study revealed that sucralose exposure during early pregnancy inhibited embryonic cardiogenesis, with a higher incidence of cardiac septal defects in mice offspring. Mechanistically, sucralose promoted CREB phosphorylation to downregulate the transcriptional levels of key genes related to cardiac septal defects including Gata4, Nkx2.5, Tbx5, and Tbx20.

There has been a growing concern regarding the potential adverse effects of gestational exposure to artificial sweeteners on embryonic development. In general populations, preterm delivery,23,37 obesity,24 asthma, and allergic rhinitis38 in children have been linked with maternal sucralose exposure during pregnancy. In mice offspring, inhibited intestinal development and impaired barrier function have also been observed.26 Furthermore, studies have revealed a positive correlation between fetal and maternal plasma concentrations of sucralose, with an observed accumulation in amniotic fluid.22 These findings are consistent with our measurements in murine maternal serum and amniotic fluid, collectively indicating a synchronous fetal exposure to sucralose together with the mother. Therefore, investigating the adverse effects of early-pregnancy sucralose exposure on embryonic development and its underlying mechanisms is imperative for developing preventive and therapeutic strategies against the potential birth defects.

The previously reported function of sucralose is mainly about its generation of a sweet sensation via stimulating the sweet taste perception machinery. The sweet taste receptor is a class C G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) heterodimer consisted of TAS1R2 and TAS1R3 subunits.30 However, in some tissues (e.g., the stomach, heart, liver, kidney, and lung), TAS1R3 is expressed only as a monomeric subunit, most likely in a homodimeric form.39 Prior studies indicated that sucralose activation of TAS1R3 contributed to hepatic steatosis by inducing endoplasmic reticulum stress and lipogenesis.14 Sucralose was also reported to induce insulin resistance via a TAS1R3-extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK)-1/2-dependent pathway.13 Additionally, activation of TAS1R3 was confirmed to cause an increased intracellular concentration of cAMP,31,32 which was believed to progressively decrease during cardiac development.40 In contrast, intracellular cAMP elevation in the cardiomyocytes of the developing heart was indicated to be responsible for the establishment of CHD.33,41 Meanwhile, cAMP has been shown to significantly transform fetal cardiomyocytes into cardiac pacemaker-like cells during cardiogenesis, increase gap-junction markers,42,43 and inhibit the transcription of T-box genes.33 Consistent with prior findings, our results demonstrated that sucralose downregulated the mRNA expression of cardiac transcription factors including Tbx5 and Tbx20. The cAMP-PKA-CREB signaling pathway constitutes a classic signaling axis that plays a critical role in regulating genes’ expression in cardiomyocytes,44,45 while activation of TAS1R3 was proved to increase CREB phosphorylation levels.46 Therefore, we speculated that sucralose might promote CREB phosphorylation by TAS1R3 and downregulate the transcriptional levels of key genes involved in cardiogenesis. Our results confirmed TAS1R3’s expression in cardiomyocytes and demonstrated that its activation by sucralose could promote CREB phosphorylation and downregulate mRNA expression of cardiac transcription factors comprising Gata4, Nkx2.5, Tbx5, and Tbx20.

NAC is a safe and well-tolerated compound displaying antioxidant properties, and can pass through the placenta.47 It has been widely used in clinical settings to treat or prevent disorders including polycystic ovary syndrome and recurrent pregnancy loss.48 Notably, CHD induced by high-palmitic acid (PA) diet and pre-gestational diabetes in pregnancy was reported to be alleviated by NAC supplement.49,50 In addition, NAC supplementation was reported to reduce the phosphorylation level of CREB.35 In the present study, NAC supplementation alleviated sucralose-induced CREB hyperphosphorylation and increased expression of cardiac transcription factors comprising Gata4, Nkx2.5, Tbx5, and Tbx20, further suggesting that sucralose-induced CREB hyperphosphorylation is the potential mechanism for the downregulation of these cardiac genes. NAC alone also regulated expression of cardiac transcription factors comprising Nkx2.5, Tbx5, and Tbx20 (not Gata4) compared to the control. This may be attributed to NAC’s function of decreasing the K-Hcy modified level of GATA4 and enhancing its transcriptional activity,49 which in turn regulates the expression of Nkx2.5 and Tbx5.28 The precise mechanism by which phosphorylated CREB suppresses these genes remains to be elucidated in future studies. Since NAC supplementation prevented heart defects, to a certain extent, in fetus caused by sucralose exposure in pregnant mice, further clinical evidence and more in-depth studies are needed to confirm the effectiveness of NAC in reducing risk of heart defects.

In conclusion, our study provides a preliminary insight into how sucralose exposure during early pregnancy suppresses embryonic cardiogenesis and identifies NAC as a potentially effective intervention. As sucralose is widely used in our daily life, an increasing body of studies both in laboratory and clinical settings should be conducted to further investigate the potential hazards of sucralose on embryonic cardiogenesis.

Limitations of the study

There are several limitations of the present study. On one hand, the limited availability of embryonic mouse serum and tissues restricted the detection of sucralose concentrations, leaving the tissue-specific accumulation of sucralose and its targeted inhibitory effect on cardiac development unclarified thoroughly. Therefore, further investigation is necessary to elucidate the tissue-specific accumulation and the effects of sucralose in embryos. On the other side, our study specifically focused on the impact of sucralose exposure during early pregnancy on embryonic cardiogenesis, follow-up studies at later stages (e.g., E18.5) are essential to assess whether heart defects persist in future. Moreover, an accumulating evidence indicates that sucralose exposure during both pregnancy and lactation may exert long-term effects on offspring.6 Consequently, our subsequent experimental and clinical research should systematically explore the implications of sucralose exposure during pregnancy for postnatal cardiac function and the overall cardiovascular health across the lifespan.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal model

Eight-week-old C57BL/6J mice were obtained from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and housed in a pathogen-free animal room at 20°C–26°C with a 12/12-h light/dark cycle. All mice adapted for one week before study. Male and female mice were paired in a ratio of 1:2 overnight. Mating was verified by observing vaginal plug in the following morning, which was considered E0.5. Pregnant females were randomly assigned to the control and sucralose water groups (n = 14 per group).

The maximal ADI of sucralose for humans has been established as 15 mg per kg (body weight) by the EFSA. Allometric scaling on the basis of body surface area equivalents can be used to convert human doses of drugs to mouse by adjusting for the increased metabolic rate in mice.51 Sucralose was dissolved in drinking water at the final concentration of 0.72 mg/ml29. Based on the recorded average daily water intake of 5.8 mL, sucralose intake of the pregnant mice (average body weight 23 g) was estimated to be 181.6 mg/kg/day by BSA equivalents (mouse Km factor 3; human Km factor 37),52 roughly equivalent to humans’ 14.7 mg/kg/day and nearly the ADI recommended by EFSA. Mice in the rescue group received 4 mg/mL N-acetylcysteine (NAC, 1 g/kg body weight per day) in drinking water.36 The rescue experiment comprised the following four groups (n = 14 per group): control water group, sucralose water group, N-acetylcysteine water group and sucralose + N-acetylcysteine water group. All the pregnant mice were housed from E0.5 to E14.5 with free access to diet and water. The physiologic indexes of the mice were monitored during pregnancy. Embryos were dissected from the decidual capsules in uteri at E14.5. Subsequently, the incidence of heart defects in mice offspring, defined as the primary outcome, was quantified through histological analysis. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Fudan University and conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Method details

Embryo histological analysis

Following fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde, whole mouse embryos were embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 8 μm. The sections were then deparaffinized, rehydrated in graded alcohol series, and stained with hematoxylin–eosin (HE). The four-chamber cardiac interface of the stained tissues was imaged by Nikon microscope (Ni-U, Nikon, Minato-ku, Japan) to assess cardiac malformations and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Evaluation of physiological indexes in the pregnant mice and embryos

Pregnant mice were housed individually in cages. Food intake, water intake and body weight of the pregnant mice were monitored daily at 9 a.m. from E0.5 to E14.5. The tail tips (approximately 1–2 mm) of pregnant mice were amputated with sterile scissors. Following gentle pressure, the first drop of blood was discarded, and the second drop was collected and applied to a glucose test strip. Glucose meter (Roche, Accu-Chek, Roche, Basel, Switzerland) was used to record fasting blood glucose of pregnant mice at E14.5. Besides, mice were photographed and weighed after cesarean delivery on day E14.5.

Sucralose quantification by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)

Mouse serum sample was obtained by the retroorbital plexus. One eyeball was removed with curved forceps completely after the mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane gas. When blood flow was exhausted, the mice were executed by dislocation method. Blood samples were left at room temperature for half an hour. Supernatants were collected after 15 min of centrifugation (3,000 × rpm, 4 °C) and stored at −80 °C after being immersed in liquid nitrogen. Amniotic fluid was collected in 1.5 mL centrifuged from all sacs in each dam’s uterus at E14.5. The supernatant was obtained after centrifugation and stored at −80 °C after being immersed in liquid nitrogen. Liver and cardiac tissues of the pregnant mice and cardiac tissues of the embryos were removed at E14.5 and stored at −80 °C until determination of sucralose content.

Serum, liver and cardiac tissues of pregnant mice, amniotic fluid and embryonic heart samples were allowed to thaw on ice for 30–60 min. Ice-cold methanol was subsequently added into 100 μL of serum and amniotic fluid in a ratio 4:1 (v/v). Liver and cardiac tissues of pregnant mice and embryonic cardiac samples were dried and ground into powder, and then added 80% ice-cold methanol(v/v) and sonicated for 20 min. After a short vortex step, the mixture was incubated on ice for 5 min. Centrifugation (13,000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C) was used to pellet the protein. The supernatant was transferred to an Eppendorf tube and dried in a rotary vacuum concentrator. Finally, 75 μL of a mixture of H2O: methanol (1:1) were added into the dried extract. The samples were subsequently detected by LC-MS/MS. The method for measuring sucralose is described elsewhere.53 LC-MS/MS analysis was conducted using API 6500 Qtrap mass spectrometer (Sciex, USA) together with a Waters H-Class UPLC system (Waters, USA).

Neonatal primary mouse cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblast isolation

Neonatal primary mouse cardiomyocytes (NMCMs) were isolated from heart tissue of newborn mice according to published methods.54 Briefly, the hearts were removed and washed with pre-cooled PBS before minced into small chunks of ∼1 mm3. Heart tissues were digested in trypsin (Cat#G4012, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 5 min at 37 °C, then in collagenase type І (Cat#9001-12-1, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 1-2h, and maintained at 37 °C with constant shaking at 62×rpm. At the end of the digestion, tissues were centrifugated at 800×rpm for 6 min and the deposition was resuspended in low-glucose DMEM (Cat#L170KJ, BasalMedia, Shanghai, China) with 20% fetal bovine serum (Cat#A5669701, Gibco, Grand Island, NY). All of the resuspended cells were seeded on a cell culture dish maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2 to allow neonatal mouse cardiac fibroblasts (NMCFs) to attach the plate. After nearly 1h, the supernatant containing the cardiomyocytes were collected and transferred to other dishes.

Cell culture and treatment

NMCMs were cultured in low-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Cat#L170KJ, BasalMedia, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (Cat#A5669701, Gibco, Grand Island, NY), 100 units/mL penicillin (Cat#15070063, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA), and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). Other cells were cultured in low-glucose DMEM (Cat#L170KJ, BasalMedia, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Cat#A5669701, Gibco, Grand Island, NY), 100 units/mL penicillin (Cat#15070063, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA), and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). In general, cells were pretreated for 24h with sucralose and N-acetylcysteine at the concentrations of 1 mM and 5 mM, respectively.29 The cells were incubated in 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Cell line generation

Single-cell clones of TAS1R3-deleted HEK 293T cells were generated using CRISPR–Cas9 technology.55 Briefly, sgRNAs targeting TAS1R3 were cloned into the BbsI site of pSpCas9(BB)–2A–Puro (no. PX459). The constructed plasmid containing sgRNA was transfected into HEK 293T cells cultured in a 60 mm dish (approximately 60% confluency) using Lipo3000(Cat#L3000015, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). Thirty-six hours after transfection, the cells were passaged into a 90 mm dish containing puromycin at a final concentration of 1 μg/mL for continued culture. A control group of untransfected HEK 293T cells was set up simultaneously. Once all cells in the control group had died, the sgRNA-transfected HEK 293T cells were diluted into 96-well plates (one cell per well). Knockout clones were confirmed by immunoblotting. The sgRNA sequences used are shown in Table S1.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from mouse tissues or cultured cells by Trizol reagents (Cat#19211, Yeasen Biotechnology (Shanghai, China) Co., Ltd) and converted into cDNA using the HiScript III 1st strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Cat#R312-02, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Gata4/6, Tbx1/3/5/20, Nkx2.5, Notch1, Tas1r2 and Tas1r3 mRNA levels were measured by qRT-PCR using ChamQ SYBR Color qPCR Master Mix (Cat#Q311-02, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) on the CFX96 Touch real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA) with Actin as an internal reference gene. Each reaction was performed three times. All primers used are listed in Table S2.

Western blotting

Mouse tissues and cells were washed twice with PBS and homogenized in 0.5% NP-40 buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP-40) with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Cat#4693116001, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany). After centrifugation at 12,000 × rpm and 4 °C for 15 min, the supernatant was collected for western blotting according to the standard procedures. Detection was performed by measuring fluorescence intensity on Amersham Typhoon (Cytiva, Shanghai, China) using Cytiva ECL Plex fluorescent (Cat#PA45009, Cat#PA45011, Cytiva, Shanghai, China). ImageJ was used to qualify the intensity of the blots. All western blotting experiments were repeated at least three times. All antibodies used are listed in key resources table.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were inoculated into pre-positioned dishes with treated coverslips. After cells were attached to the wall and treated accordingly, the coverslips were removed and washed twice with PBS. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min, incubated with 5%BSA for 1 h at room temperature to reduce non-specific binding, and incubated with anti-p-CREB antibody (1:500 in 5% BSA) at 4 °C overnight. Alexa Flour 488 donkey anti-rabbit secondary antibody was used to incubate the cells after washing with PBS for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Following incubation with DAPI for 5 min, the nuclei of the cells were displayed under a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Quantification and statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated by GPower version 3.1.9.7 calculation software as reported.56 There has been no report regarding the effect of maternal high sucralose exposure on cardiac embryogenesis. According to our data, the incidence of heart defects in mice offspring after maternal high sucralose exposure by histological analysis was 15% (6/40), compared to 2.5% (1/40) in the control group. We estimated that the study would have 90% power to show a significant difference, with a two-sided type I error rate of 0.05, and the GPower software showed that approximately 106 offspring in each group were required. Due to variations in the number of offspring per pregnant mice (averaging 7–8 fetuses per litter), we finally assigned 14 pregnant mice to each group to obtain a total offspring sample size close to the calculated requirement of 106.

The incidence of heart defects was analyzed using exact tests (Fisher’s exact test for two-group comparisons and the Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test for multiple-group comparisons).57 One-way ANOVA was performed for multi-group analyses, and unpaired t test with Welch’s correction was performed for two-group analyses. The data were expressed using the mean ± standard error (SEM). All statistical analyses were performed by using GraphPad Prism 10.2.0. Statistical significance was defined as the p-value was less than 0.05. ns (not significant), ∗, ∗∗, ∗∗∗, and ∗∗∗∗ denoted p ≥ 0.05, p < 0.05, p < 0.01, p < 0.001, and p < 0.0001, respectively.
