Qdenga-induced dengue caused by minor DENV-2 subvariant(s) in the vaccine, with two amino acid substitutions in the E protein
Authors: John H-O Pettersson, Erica Tibbelin, Gabriel Heyman, Johan Aarum, Oskar Karlsson Lindsjö, Kim Blom, Klara Sondén, Jonas Klingström
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract Highlight: We describe a Qdenga-induced DENV-2-infection in a Swedish traveller. Comparative sequencing suggests that the vaccine contained a small fraction of identical virus as detected in the patient, suggesting a selection of a DENV-2-substrain with unusual amino acid substitutions. Further research on selection of, and possible effects of, Qdenga-substrain-infections is warranted.Keywords: Dengue virus, dengue, Qdenga Tak-003, vaccine-induced infection, denv substrains, travel vaccine

The increasing incidence of dengue is an emerging threat to public health. Several novel vaccines have been developed. The live-attenuated tetravalent dengue vaccine TAK-003 (Qdenga) has shown good efficacy in endemic areas.1 Travellers to endemic countries is a risk group for dengue. In several non-endemic countries, including Sweden, travellers are therefore advised to be vaccinated with Qdenga. However, the safety profile for seronegative travellers is still unclear.2

Qdenga includes all four dengue virus (DENV) serotypes on a replicating DENV-2 backbone.1 After dose 1, vaccine viremia, almost exclusively caused by the DENV-2 component, can be detected during Days 9–15 and sometimes up to Day 30, in seronegative immunocompetent non-pregnant individuals.3,4 Furthermore, mutations of DENV-2 have been observed in vaccinees, including reversal of one of the three mutations associated with the attenuated phenotype.4

Here we report a case of vaccine-induced dengue in a previously healthy 48-year-old male patient. He sought medical attention three days after returning from a trip abroad, and DENV-infection was confirmed by real-time reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR). He was not hospitalized and fully recovered within two days. Before travelling he had received one dose of Qdenga. The blood-sample used for diagnostic RT-PCR was collected 16 days after the vaccination. Sequencing and phylogenetic analysis confirmed DENV-2 infection consistent with infection caused by the Qdenga DENV-2 subcomponent (Figure 1, see Appendix for methods). Further, this revealed amino acid substitutions, including in the E (R120I and N203D) and NS5 (I412V) proteins (Table 1).

We next sequenced the vaccine (EPI_ISL_241008B), and compared the obtained sequences with the DENV-2-sequence in the patient. In the vaccine, we detected fractions (≤0.8%–46.4%) of the Qdenga DENV-2 viruses containing the same mutations and amino acid substitutions as observed in the patient (Table S1). This suggests the infection in the patient was likely caused by an in the vaccine already pre-existing minor DENV-2 subvariant(s).

Vaccine-induced dengue can be mistaken for naturally acquired DENV-infection. Investigation with sequencing of RT-PCR-positive samples in recently vaccinated individuals is of importance for individual feedback to patients, for epidemiological purposes, and for a better understanding of vaccine-induced dengue.

The patient had previously been vaccinated against yellow fever, tick-borne encephalitis and Japanese encephalitis (Appendix). Interestingly, tick-borne encephalitis virus-specific antibodies can predispose for antibody-dependent enhancement of DENV-infection after vaccination against yellow fever.5 Further studies addressing the selection of Qdenga vaccine sub-variants and the possible effects of this are needed for a better understanding of the safety profile.
