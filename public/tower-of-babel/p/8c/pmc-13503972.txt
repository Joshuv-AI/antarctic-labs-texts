Discordant molecular biomarkers in unfavorable intermediate‐risk localized prostate cancer

CASE PRESENTATION

A Black gentleman aged 67 years was referred for evaluation of an elevated prostate‐specific antigen (PSA) level of 4.9 ng/mL detected during routine screening, representing an increase from a prior value of 3.6 ng/mL 1 year earlier and a gradual rise from values in the low 2 ng/mL range beginning in his mid‐50s. He reported no lower urinary tract symptoms and had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0. His past medical history was notable only for well controlled hypertension and hyperlipidemia. He had no family history of prostate cancer. He remained physically active and had an estimated life expectancy exceeding 10 years based on actuarial life tables, 1 with both parents having lived into their late 80s. His American Urological Association International Prostate Symptom Score was 6, and his score on the Sexual Health Inventory for Men was 19. Digital rectal examination revealed subtle induration involving the right peripheral zone of the prostate without fixation.

Multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the prostate demonstrated a 1.9‐cm lesion characterized as a Prostate Imaging‐Reporting and Data System score of 5 (very high likelihood of significant cancer). It was located within the right posterior peripheral zone at the mid‐gland, without radiographic evidence of extracapsular extension or seminal vesicle invasion. No suspicious pelvic lymph nodes were identified.

Transrectal ultrasound–guided, systematic and MRI‐targeted biopsies were subsequently performed. Pathologic sampling included a systematic 12‐core biopsy sampling of the entire prostate, from which seven of 12 cores were positive. The biopsy was enhanced by MRI‐informed targeting of the aforementioned lesion with a Prostate Imaging‐Reporting and Data System score of 5 and yielded two of three positive cores. Of all biopsies, the highest Gleason score was 4 + 3 = 7, representing an intermediate grade and correlating to International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) grade group 3, 2 with pattern 4 in approximately 60% and no cribriform features. Perineural invasion was present, and overall tumor involvement ranged from 20% to 70% within positive cores. Based on these findings, the patient was staged with clinical T2b, N0, M0 (cT2bN0M0) disease.

According to National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) criteria, the patient was classified as having unfavorable intermediate‐risk (UIR) prostate cancer based on the presence of ISUP grade group 3 2 adenocarcinoma and ≥50% of cores that were positive for cancer. Staging with prostate‐specific membrane antigen–positron emission tomography demonstrated no evidence of nodal or distant metastatic disease.

The patient was evaluated in a multidisciplinary clinic and expressed interest in definitive radiation therapy (RT) over surgery. Given his UIR disease, standard management options included RT alone or in combination with short‐term androgen‐deprivation therapy (ADT). However, the patient was particularly interested in understanding whether the addition of ADT would meaningfully improve his outcomes, given concerns about the potential metabolic, sexual, and quality‐of‐life (QoL) effects associated with hormone therapy. He also expressed substantial concern regarding cancer recurrence and cancer‐related death based on his prior experience caring for a family member with cancer and the emotional and logistical burden this placed on his extended family.

To further refine treatment selection, molecular biomarker testing was obtained. The Decipher prostate genomic classifier (Decipher GC) returned a score of 0.94 (Figure 1), 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 corresponding to a high genomic risk, which has been associated with an increased risk of distant metastasis (DM) and prostate cancer–specific mortality (PCSM) in multiple clinical cohorts. In contrast, an analysis using the Artera multimodal artificial intelligence (MMAI) platform (ArteraAI), which integrates digital histopathology features with clinical variables, predicted a low risk of metastasis and yielded a negative result for the short‐term ADT (ST‐ADT) predictive biomarker component (Figure 2). 1 Thus the two biomarkers provided discordant clinical guidance. The Decipher result suggested biologically aggressive disease and supported treatment intensification, whereas the MMAI predictive model suggested that RT alone would be similar to RT plus ST‐ADT, suggesting that the incremental benefit of ST‐ADT could be minimal.

In this article, we provide a broad overview of this increasingly common clinical presentation of intermediate‐risk, localized prostate cancer (IR‐PCa) in which biomarker guidance is discordant. Our objective is to provide a high‐level discussion of the interpretation of commercially available, guideline‐endorsed biomarker tests targeted at clinicians to help those in practice understand how to integrate these next‐generation tests into their clinical practice. We outline both the currently available evidence as well as knowledge gaps in using these biomarkers in modern clinical practice, attempting to summarize our institutional practice for IR‐PCa. We purposefully outline a case of extreme disagreement between two common biomarkers for localized prostate cancer to highlight an area of substantial practice uncertainty in which more evidence is needed, and we discuss several potential frameworks for reasoning through such a presentation.

BENEFIT OF ANDROGEN‐DEPRIVATION THERAPY IN INTERMEDIATE‐RISK LOCALIZED PROSTATE CANCER

ADT is an adjunct to definitive RT for men with localized prostate cancer. Multiple phase 3 randomized trials have demonstrated that limited duration ADT improves the efficacy of RT on multiple end points. However, the magnitude of absolute benefit varies substantially according to baseline disease risk, creating ongoing debate regarding which patients derive meaningful clinical benefit from treatment intensification.

The role of ST‐ADT (i.e., ≤6 months) in IR‐PCa was established by several randomized trials. 3 The phase 3 Radiation Therapy Oncology Group study RTOG 9408 4 randomized more than 2000 men with predominantly IR‐PCa to RT with or without 4 months of ADT. At 10‐year follow‐up, there was a statistically significant benefit in PCSM that translated into an improvement in overall survival (OS). 9 It is important to note that this benefit was observed in the overall study population and was driven largely by the IR‐PCa population given the lack of benefit in the low‐risk population. At long‐term follow‐up, the benefit in terms of DM (hazard ratio [HR] in the IR‐subset, 0.67; p = .01) and PCSM (HR in the IR‐subset, 0.53; p < .01) persisted, although OS appeared similar because of nonprostate cancer deaths at long‐term follow‐up. 8

More recently, the phase 3 NRG Oncology/RTOG 0815 trial re‐examined this question in the modern era using modern dose‐escalated RT (i.e., ≥75 grays). 10 In contrast to RTOG 9408, in RTOG 0815, trial patients were randomized to RT with or without 6 months of ADT, and the patient population was exclusively limited to those who had IR‐PCa. In RTOG 0815, ADT was associated with improvements in the time to biochemical failure (cause‐specific HR, 0.52; p < .001), DM (cause‐specific HR, 0.25; p < .001), and PCSM (cause‐specific HR, 0.10; p = .007), translating into estimated absolute improvements in these clinical end points of 8%, 4%, and 2% at 10 years, respectively. Despite the benefit in PCSM and no corresponding worsening in non‐PCSM (HR, 0.92; p = .56), that trial failed to demonstrate a benefit in terms of OS (HR, 0.85; p = .22). This may reflect the limited precision of the estimate of treatment effect on OS for a population in which the majority of deaths occur from competing nonprostate cancer causes 11 and/or improvements in therapies for relapsed disease.

In addition to RTOG 9408 and RTOG 0815, five additional randomized trials have evaluated the benefit of external‐beam RT with or without ST‐ADT. 4 , 5 , 12 , 13 , 14 They demonstrated that ADT in this population with IR‐PCa results in improvements in clinically meaningful end points. 3 Although this is true of the average‐risk patient with IR‐PCa, IR‐PCa encompasses a wide spectrum of prognoses as well as sensitivities to ADT, with only some subgroups deriving meaningful benefit, such as those with clinically UIR disease. Given the well recognized adverse effects of ADT, including metabolic dysfunction, cardiovascular risk, sexual dysfunction, and diminished QoL, there has been growing interest in informing patient selection for ADT with next‐generation biomarkers. Below, we discuss how biomarker assessment may inform patient counseling for patients with IR‐PCa who are under consideration for intensification with ST‐ADT.

DISCUSSION ON RISK STRATIFICATION FOR LOCALIZED PROSTATE CANCER

Standard clinicopathologic factors

Clinical decision‐making in localized prostate cancer has traditionally relied on risk stratification systems based on readily available clinicopathologic factors. One of the earliest and most influential frameworks was the D’Amico risk classification, which categorized patients into low‐risk, intermediate‐risk, and high‐risk groups according to clinical tumor stage, biopsy Gleason score, and PSA level. 15 This model demonstrated that combinations of these variables could meaningfully predict biochemical recurrence after definitive treatment, and became widely adopted as a foundation for treatment decision‐making.

Building on this paradigm, contemporary clinical guidelines, including those of the NCCN, have expanded these principles into more refined risk group classifications. 6 NCCN risk categories incorporate clinical stage, ISUP grade group 2 (i.e., Gleason score), and PSA while also accounting for additional features, such as the proportion of positive biopsy cores. This framework is used to guide treatment recommendations, including the use of ADT in conjunction with RT, which is currently recommended for those with UIR disease, but not those with favorable intermediate‐risk disease.

Despite their widespread adoption, clinicopathologic risk groups remain imperfect surrogates for underlying tumor biology. This limitation is particularly evident among patients who have IR‐PCa, who may range from individuals with relatively indolent tumors that behave similarly to low‐risk disease to those with more aggressive cancers whose clinical course resembles high‐risk disease.

Recognizing this variability, Zumsteg and colleagues 7 proposed and validated a modification to further subdivide intermediate‐risk disease into favorable intermediate‐risk and UIR groups (Table 1). Subsequent analysis of the randomized RTOG 9408 trial demonstrated that patients who had UIR disease experienced significantly higher risks of DM and PCSM compared with those who had favorable intermediate‐risk disease and that the benefit of ST‐ADT appeared to be largely confined to the UIR subgroup.

Rise of next‐generation biomarkers

These limitations in clinical risk stratification have motivated additional research into the development of both prognostic and predictive biomarkers, which provide additional insight into tumor biology and improve individualized treatment selection. Several commercially available, tissue‐based assays have been developed to refine prognostic assessment in localized prostate cancer, largely by quantifying RNA expression programs associated with proliferation, androgen signaling, and metastatic potential. 16 , 17 Although these assays can provide additional information beyond traditional clinical variables, their clinical use has largely focused on refining decisions regarding active surveillance or treatment escalation in selected settings.

Ensuring equitable access to molecular diagnostics is an increasingly important consideration in the era of precision oncology. 18 Historically, Black men have been underrepresented in genomic biomarker validation cohorts, raising concerns that disparities in access to testing could exacerbate existing outcome differences. Contemporary initiatives aimed at improving equitable implementation of precision oncology tools have shown promise.

In one example, the use of a precision medicine navigator program, a dedicated clinical support role that facilitates ordering, insurance authorization, and tissue acquisition, has been shown to significantly increase the use of genomic testing among patients with prostate cancer, including Black patients. Precision medicine navigators help clinicians and patients navigate the logistical and educational barriers associated with molecular diagnostics. 19 In addition to representation in validation cohorts, equitable access to these technologies will depend on addressing practical barriers, such as reimbursement, tissue availability, and access to digital pathology infrastructure.

Among the available genomic classifiers, the Decipher GC includes extensive clinical validation across multiple treatment contexts. 20 Decipher is a 22‐gene, transcriptomic assay derived from tumor tissue that estimates the risk of metastatic progression after definitive therapy. 21 Numerous retrospective and post‐hoc validations of prospective studies across surgical and radiation‐treated cohorts have demonstrated that Decipher provides independent prognostic information beyond established clinical risk factors, including the ability to stratify patients according to their risk of DM and PCSM. 20 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28

Advances in digital pathology and machine learning have enabled the development of computational approaches capable of extracting clinically relevant information directly from standard histopathology slides. One such approach is the ArteraAI MMAI test, 29 which applies deep‐learning algorithms to digitized hematoxylin and eosin–stained pathology images and integrates these features with clinical variables. This test has been validated in several post‐hoc assessments of prospective studies across surgical and radiation‐treated cohorts. 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 Unlike many assays that primarily provide prognostic information (Figure 2A), one component of the ArteraAI platform of tests has demonstrated the ability to predict patients more likely to benefit from the addition of ADT to RT (Figure 2B). 35 , 36

As AI‐based tools become integrated into oncology practice, concerns have also been raised regarding potential algorithmic bias and the need for validation across diverse patient populations. Analyses evaluating the ArteraAI MMAI test have demonstrated comparable performance across men of African ancestry and other populations, 37 supporting the broader applicability of this approach while emphasizing the importance of continued validation of AI‐driven biomarkers in diverse clinical cohorts.

It is important to note that both the Decipher GC and the ArteraAI MMAI platform have received national‐level guideline recognition with Simon level 1B evidence, 6 reflecting validation of these tools using samples from randomized clinical trials. Increasingly, in contemporary clinical practice, both tests are being ordered in parallel, although the frequency of such co‐testing has not been well characterized at a national level. Like other diagnostic and prognostic tests in oncology, discordant results may occur when both assays are applied to the same patient. Such discordance thus presents an emerging challenge for clinicians attempting to integrate multiple precision oncology tools into routine decision‐making. Our case illustrates how conflicting biomarker results may complicate treatment selection in localized prostate cancer, particularly when determining whether ST‐ADT should be incorporated into RT.

UNDERSTANDING BIOMARKER TEST DISCORDANCE

The integration of molecular and computational biomarkers into prostate cancer management has introduced new opportunities for treatment personalization but has also created new clinical challenges. One such challenge arises when different biomarker platforms provide discordant guidance regarding treatment selection, as highlighted in our case. Therefore, understanding the biologic and methodological differences between these tools is essential for interpreting their results in clinical practice.

The Decipher GC has primarily been demonstrated to be an independent prognostic biomarker beyond traditional clinicopathologic factors. Patients with high genomic risk scores have consistently been shown to experience higher rates of DM and PCSM, supporting its use as a tool for identifying biologically aggressive disease. In contrast, the ArteraAI MMAI platform of tests represents not only a prognostic biomarker assay but also a predictive biomarker component. Specifically, the predictive biomarker is meant to identify patients who are more likely to derive a meaningful benefit with the addition of ST‐ADT to RT, and because it pertains to the decision of whether or not to include ST‐ADT in the treatment package, the ArteraAI predictive biomarker provides a more direct answer to this than the ArteraAI prognostic biomarker.

Our comparative experience evaluating these two validated tests suggests that there is a clinically meaningful proportion of patients who have discordant findings, such as the example in the case presented. Here, our definition of discordance relies on the contrasting management implications resulting from two (or more) biomarkers in a given patient. Specifically in this case, the high Decipher GC prognostic risk score suggests that ST‐ADT may produce a clinically meaningful benefit. This suggestion is made based on the presumption that the average relative benefit of ST‐ADT is constant across the spectrum of Decipher risk. In contrast, the negative ArteraAI ST‐ADT predictive biomarker suggests that the patient belongs to a subgroup insensitive to ST‐ADT and thus likely will not derive a clinically meaningful benefit from ADT. Before adjudicating discordance, it is helpful to review the raw score (i.e., Decipher score, 0.94) rather than the categorical score (i.e., high risk) because discordance based on scores near an individual’s decision threshold may be ignored in some cases based on clinical discretion.

When a clinician determines that two tests are discordant, there is often uncertainty about how to act, raising an important practice question. Readily available options include: (1) prioritizing the use of one test over another, (2) attempting to combine biomarker estimates in some way, or (3) ignoring biomarker results when discordant and electing to default to standard clinical risk stratification in such cases.

Our case presented here illustrates this challenge. A high Decipher GC score suggested biologically aggressive disease with increased metastatic potential, which could support treatment intensification. However, the ArteraAI MMAI predictive test indicated that the addition of ADT to RT would likely provide minimal incremental benefit. Such discordance highlights both the promise and the practical challenges of precision oncology in prostate cancer and underscores the importance of multidisciplinary interpretation when integrating multiple biomarker platforms into clinical decision‐making.

MULTIDISCIPLINARY TUMOR BOARD PERSPECTIVE

The central clinical question in this case is whether the addition of ST‐ADT meaningfully improves outcomes beyond definitive RT alone. For patients who have UIR prostate cancer, contemporary guidelines generally recommend external‐beam RT combined with 4–6 months of ADT based on randomized trials demonstrating reductions in biochemical failure, DM, and PCSM. 6 However, ADT is also associated with well recognized adverse effects, including fatigue, metabolic dysfunction, weight gain, insulin resistance, sexual dysfunction, and potential cardiovascular risk. 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 Even relatively short courses of therapy can meaningfully affect QoL.

Nevertheless, these recommendations are based on population‐level trial results, and the absolute benefit of ADT varies substantially across patients. As illustrated in analyses of the RTOG 9408 trial (Figure 3), even among patients with UIR disease, many individuals experience durable disease control with RT alone, whereas others die from metastatic prostate cancer despite combined‐modality therapy. This variability highlights the biologic heterogeneity within clinically defined risk groups and underscores the appeal of incorporating molecular biomarkers to better personalize treatment intensity.

In our patient, the Decipher GC indicated high genomic risk, suggesting a tumor with increased metastatic potential and supporting treatment intensification. In contrast, the ArteraAI MMAI test predicted no significant benefit from the addition of ST‐ADT, raising the possibility that RT alone might achieve similar outcomes while avoiding the adverse effects associated with androgen suppression.

When discordant biomarker results arise, many clinicians may favor adherence to established guideline‐based therapy rather than de‐escalating treatment based on a single predictive biomarker, particularly as the real‐world evidence base for integrating these two tests continues to evolve. Consideration must also be given to the patient’s overall health status, life expectancy, and preferences regarding treatment‐related toxicity. It is important not to interpret this approach as favoring one biomarker over another, because both the Decipher GC and the ArteraAI MMAI platform have been validated. Rather, in the absence of an evidence‐based framework for reconciling discordant biomarker results, we elected to follow NCCN guideline–based management. This recommendation would have been unchanged had the direction of discordance been reversed.

After multidisciplinary discussion, the tumor board acknowledged that the discordant biomarker results created uncertainty. In the setting of discordance, the group favored a more conservative approach, consistent with NCCN‐recommended treatment, using RT and ST‐ADT. This recommendation was made to avoid the potential for undertreatment in a patient who had UIR disease, a high Decipher GC score, and substantial anxiety regarding recurrence and PCSM.

The patient was subsequently counseled regarding the potential benefits and side effects of ST‐ADT, including metabolic and QoL considerations. After discussion of the available evidence and the uncertainty surrounding the interpretation of discordant biomarker results, and consistent with a shared patient–physician decision approach, the patient elected to proceed with RT combined with ST‐ADT.

CONCLUSION

This case illustrates the increasing complexity of treatment decision‐making in the era of precision oncology. Although traditional clinicopathologic risk groups remain central to prostate cancer management, they incompletely capture the biologic heterogeneity that exists within localized disease. Molecular and computational biomarkers such as the Decipher GC and the ArteraAI MMAI test offer additional insights into tumor biology and treatment response, but they possibly interrogate different biologic dimensions of cancer and may occasionally provide discordant clinical guidance.

For clinicians, such discordance highlights the importance of understanding the conceptual distinction between prognostic biomarkers, which estimate the likelihood of disease progression, and predictive biomarkers, which estimate the probability of benefit from a specific therapy. Therefore, integrating these tools into clinical practice requires careful interpretation within the context of patient preferences, treatment toxicity, and multidisciplinary discussion.

As molecular diagnostics and AI‐based tools continue to evolve, situations in which multiple biomarkers offer differing recommendations will likely become increasingly common. Until prospective studies clarify how discordant prognostic and predictive biomarkers should be reconciled, clinicians should be cautious about both de‐escalating and escalating beyond established therapy guidelines solely on the basis of a single biomarker in the setting of discordance, with particular care taken to avoid undertreatment in patients with potentially aggressive disease. There remains substantial uncertainty based on the current real‐world evidence that precludes definitive algorithmic recommendations for how to combine biomarkers in settings of discordance. Multidisciplinary discussion and adherence to evidence‐based treatment principles remain essential when applying precision oncology approaches to individual patients.
