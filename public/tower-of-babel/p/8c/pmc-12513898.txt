Current Practices for Mental Fatigue Quantification and Induction in Movement Science: Introducing the SPeCIFY Guidelines
Authors: Emilie Schampheleer, Jelle Habay, Matthias Proost, Y Laurisa Arenales Arauz, Suzanna Russell, Manon Roose, Chao Bian, Romain Meeusen, Kevin De Pauw, Bart Roelands
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMental fatigue (MF) is a complex phenomenon with significant implications for human performance, for which there are numerous studies investigating the effects of MF. Nevertheless, there is considerable variability in the approaches used to induce and quantify MF, making it hard to compare findings across studies and draw well-supported conclusions. This review addresses the methodological variability in the induction and quantification methods of MF in movement science in the following ways: on the one hand, by providing an overview of task design strategies to induce MF, emphasizing the importance of tailoring task duration, difficulty, and nature to individual participants and specific research contexts; on the other hand, by providing an overview of current methods used to quantify MF, including behavioural, subjective, and physiological measures, and highlighting the strengths and limitations of each. Finally, this review proposes a best-practice framework for MF research, incorporating multi-method approaches for the induction and quantification of MF and introducing the Settings, Protocol establishments, Confounders, Individuals, Framework, and Yield (SPeCIFY) reporting guidelines that aim at improving consistency and transparency in future MF studies.

Key Points

Research studying mental fatigue in movement science suffers from a lack of standardized approaches to both mental fatigue induction and quantification, leading to inconsistent findings.Adopting standardized frameworks and reporting guidelines for mental fatigue induction and quantification will enhance the replicability of mental fatigue research and facilitate the comparison of results across different studies.Future research may consider adopting these frameworks and guidelines where appropriate, while continuing to refine our understanding of the quantification and induction of mental fatigue, including the development of a more concise definition of the phenomenon.

Introduction

Mental fatigue (MF) is a psychobiological state caused by prolonged periods of demanding cognitive activity [1]. MF can lead to a subjective feeling of tiredness, a measurable decline in performance, as well as alterations in brain activity [1–5]. These eliciting cognitive activities are typically tasks that require sustained attention, intense focus, and/or response inhibition [6]. Similar phenomena exist across the literature, with notable mentions being ego depletion [7], self-control strength depletion [8], and cognitive fatigue [9]. While MF and cognitive fatigue are sometimes used interchangeably, particularly in discussions involving sleep restriction [10] and time-on-task [11, 12], this review adopts the term MF to specifically refer to the state induced by cognitive effort. Importantly, while extended time-on-task can contribute to MF, it does not necessarily equate to MF, as performance declines can also result from other factors such as boredom or underarousal (i.e., significant decrease in arousal, described as the degree of concentration during a specific process [13, 14]). Overall, the present review will encompass all forms of cognitive exposure that could lead to MF perception and subsequent physical performance decrement in the context of movement science (i.e., the research domain focusing on different aspects of human movement).

The impact of MF on performance has been extensively studied, with research showing negative effects on both physical and cognitive performance [1, 15, 16]. The general conclusion of the research community is that the effects of MF on physical performance are tangible and clinically significant, based on empirical research and logical theoretical arguments. However, recent studies have suggested that there might be methodological issues present in the existing literature examining the effects of MF [17–19]. While these papers are not without criticism of their own (e.g., the need for a corrected meta-analysis [20]), they do point to different issues that seem to be present across the movement science literature [21]. One of these possible methodological limits is the variability that is present in the approaches used to induce and quantify MF [5, 15, 22]. Such variability is plausibly due to the lack of consensus on which approach is most valid and appropriate to induce and quantify MF, considering the unique contexts of individual experimental designs and settings. There is a need to shift toward producing high-quality research and establishing a framework that enhances consistency and transparency in the methodological reporting of MF studies, thereby promoting reproducibility in the field. It is important to note that these challenges are not unique to the field of movement science. The lack of standardized approaches is a recognized issue across multiple disciplines, including psychological research [23, 24] and other forms of fatigue, such as general fatigability [25–27]. In addition to the need for methodological consistency, improving transparency through greater adoption of practices, such as preregistration and open data, is also essential. These practices remain underutilized in movement science, which further complicates efforts to enhance reproducibility and comparability across studies [21].

By synthesizing the various methods used to induce and measure MF in movement science, we aim to provide clarity in this complex area of research. This effort is crucial in advancing the field of movement science, ensuring greater reproducibility, and guiding future research toward more reliable findings. This review therefore aims to provide a synthesis, framework, and guidelines of the methods employed to induce and measure MF. First, by offering an overview of induction and quantification approaches, our objective is to inform researchers and practitioners on what is currently known. Second, by providing a referenced guideline for a possible “best-practice” approach, our objective is to improve the quality, consistency, and transparency of future MF research. To ensure a comprehensive and robust evidence base, we examined studies that were collected during the data collection process of separate systematic reviews [5]. To make sure no relevant studies were missed, the reference list of other existing systematic reviews on MF in the domain of movement science was also screened for suitable articles [1, 15, 16, 28]. In a first round, 135 studies were identified that investigate the overall influence of MF on human performance. The criteria for selecting suitable articles were as follows: (1) the research needed to focus on an aspect of human movement; and (2) the studies should involve tasks specifically designed to induce MF in healthy participants. This process allowed us to build a foundation of studies (n = 109) that are directly relevant to the topic of MF in movement science and aligned with the aims of this review.

The majority of currently used intervention tasks are laboratory based and performed on a computer. The Stroop color–word task is the most commonly used cognitive intervention to induce MF in studies that investigate the subsequent effects on physical performance [6, 8, 29–71]. This is owing to the ease of use, the precedent established by numerous articles, and the integration of various cognitive functions (e.g., attention, working memory, and response inhibition) in a single task [72, 73]. The constraints of the task also provide a wide scope of adapting and specifying the task to suit a particular study purpose. Task duration varies across studies, with reported examples of 30 [41], 60 [39] and 90 min [70]. Most studies have participants perform the Stroop task on a computer with various equipment adaptations and variations, but some studies opt to use paper versions for ease of use [35, 55, 56, 60–62]. Similar to the Stroop task, the AX Continuous Performance Test (AX-CPT) is widely used in the field of MF and performance research [3, 9, 19, 62, 74–78]. This cognitive task also requires attention, working memory, and response inhibition [79, 80]. More recently, the TloadDback task has gained importance, as it shows an increased use of individualized cognitive tasks to induce MF [81–86]. The task combines a classical N-back task with an interfering second task, with the aim of recruiting as many working memory resources as possible [81]. Examples of other tasks that are used to induce MF include the N-back [36, 87], the rapid visual information processing (RVIP) [88–90], and the tasks based on the Go/NoGo paradigm [91, 92].

In the psychology research field, other methods are used to induce ego depletion, including difficult labyrinth and arithmetic tasks [93–95], text transcription with or without omission [96–100], emotional or thought suppression [101–104], crossing out letters [105–107], attention control tasks [108], and psychological constructs such as ostracism [109], impressions [110], social coordination [111], and guided imagery [112]. The difference in tasks is related to the differences in concepts between MF and ego depletion. Ego depletion research postulates that acts of self-control are performed using a limited reservoir in the mind, which, when depleted, results in a limited self-control capacity in subsequent acts [7, 113]. A key distinction between ego depletion and other prolonged MF tasks is the duration. Ego depletion tasks that are used in studies to investigate effects on physical performance are of short duration and typically last less than 10 min, with examples of tasks that are 8 [93, 114], 6 [96–98, 115], and even 3 min [31, 116] long.

Typically, only one cognitive task is used with the goal of eliciting MF. However, some studies use a combination of multiple tasks, often with the goal of optimizing cognitive load in a more task-representative manner [68, 117, 118]. A new cognitive test battery to induce MF was recently proposed, consisting of a combination of the AX-CPT, N-back, mental rotation, and visual search tasks [117]. Other articles have used similar cognitive test batteries to induce MF [53, 68, 118–120]. Beyond this, researchers have implemented approaches to induce MF that aim to mimic real life scenarios (e.g., smartphone use [121–128], playing video games [123, 128–130], whole-body coordination tasks [131], and motor imagery [132]).

For control tasks, the most commonly used task is watching an “emotionally neutral” documentary [3, 9, 19, 29, 34, 40, 45, 53, 55–57, 59, 64, 66, 69, 74–78, 124, 133–139] or reading “emotionally neutral” magazines [41, 58, 60, 62, 67]. Other studies use a less cognitively demanding version of the intervention task as the control task, with the congruent version of the Stroop as the most common example [6, 8, 30–33, 38, 43, 44, 48, 50, 51, 54, 59]. Some studies require participants to remain inactive, such as staring at a computer screen [49, 52, 140] or resting in a quiet room [89].

The Three Task Properties Framework

MF is induced by a variety of tasks. As a result, the lack of a standardized method for inducing MF undermines both the design of experimental studies as well as the comparison of findings across existing studies. At present, no framework exists that aids researchers in designing tasks to induce MF. Therefore, on the basis of practical experience within the field and the available literature, a framework was proposed to utilize or design cognitive tasks to induce MF (Fig. 1).

The three task properties framework to aid researchers in designing tasks to induce mental fatigue (MF). The three components (i.e., duration, difficulty, and nature) are represented in a cycle as they all influence and inform one another. Within each component, important considerations are identified for change to optimize the cognitive load and the inducement of MF. The center of the cycle represents the context in which the task is created (e.g., specific research question, environment, or available materials), which should always be used as a basis to inform the specification of each component. Task Duration

The duration of MF-inducing tasks in research studies has long been a discussion point. Van Cutsem et al. [1] was the first to only include tasks that lasted at least 30 min, on the basis of the subjective increase in the level of MF and the decrease in vigilance that is observed after 20–30 min of performing cognitive tasks [1]. However, subsequent meta-analyses [16, 28] showed that the duration of cognitive manipulations has no significant impact on the relationship between cognitive effort and physical performance decrements. Brown et al. [16] also cite previous studies demonstrating that more demanding cognitive tasks can result in significantly higher levels of cognitive exertion in a shorter duration (< 30 min) than less demanding tasks. In fact, there are numerous studies that report physical performance decrements related to prior cognitive effort of very short durations (e.g., 6 min) [8, 50, 51, 96, 97, 115]. Moreover, practical applied research tends to use protocols that reflect that practitioners identify lack of time-availability as a barrier to best managing MF [141]. Hence, when deliberately inducing MF, beyond what occurs naturally during training or competition, the effectiveness of 15- or 20-min training protocols is explored, which aim to offer solutions that can easily be implemented in real sporting environment settings [131].

The evidence highlights that duration should be considered and determined alongside the two other components of the framework, namely difficulty and nature. O’Keeffe et al. [82] demonstrated that a task of the shortest duration (i.e., 16 min) can elicit the highest levels of MF in subjects, as long as both the difficulty and the commitment to task success remain optimal [82]. Other research also suggests a subjective fatigue threshold, with examples of this threshold at 6 [34], 16 [142], and 45 min [71, 139]. However, it is important to note that a subjective plateau does not necessarily translate to behavioral effects [83]. In addition, Dallaway et al. [143] demonstrated that the minimal duration needed to find a behavioral effect depends on the type of physical task. So, even if duration can be optimized by adjusting the other components of the proposed framework, duration itself remains an important part of this framework. This also implies that the duration threshold of a behavioral effect of MF appears to differ depending on the type of subsequent performance task [16, 28]. Moreover, this might be the reason that there seems to be a discrepancy in effects between ego depletion and MF literature, and within the MF literature itself. The ego depletion literature often uses short intervention tasks and finds negative effects of these tasks on physical endurance tasks such as a handgrip squeeze [93, 114, 116]. MF research often uses more dynamic endurance tasks such as time to exhaustion or time trials [1]. Giboin et al. [28] proposed that whole-body tasks might be more controlled by automatic processes, compared with isolation tasks that are controlled by direct corticospinal projection. So, whole-body tasks might require less attentional control then single tasks, and as such, require less self-regulation, which could theoretically lead to a decreased effect [28].

Accordingly, the evidence surrounding the importance of duration in intervention tasks represents both sections of this narrative review, namely the coherence between all components of the task induction framework, as well as the difficulty in quantifying MF effects. As most studies in MF use more dynamic physical outcome tasks, task duration will need to be longer than 15 min to ensure that behavioral effects are induced [143, 144, 145]. However, overly prolonged task duration (> 60 min) can also trigger the induction of different cognitive states (e.g., boredom) together with MF, which can also lead to a decrease in vigilance [146]. In conclusion, duration should be carefully considered when designing a cognitively demanding task, and this should be adapted and determined with contextual factors and other task properties (i.e., nature and difficulty) in mind.

Task Difficulty

Establishing task difficulty to optimally induce fatigue is challenging. Even though some physical tasks, with, for example, a fixed cycling power output, permit externally controlled intensity, both in physical and cognitive tasks, the level of difficulty is not solely determined by task design but also by the participant’s engagement and effort. However, task difficulty itself can also influence participants’ engagement. If the difficulty of a task is too low, participants might consider it too simplistic and/or boring, which could reduce their engagement and hinder their commitment to perform at the highest level [147]. In contrast, increasing the optimal task difficulty level to an overly challenging one may result in low commitment and motivation. Participants should therefore be presented with an optimal challenge point, where a task is challenging enough to maintain interest and motivation without becoming so demanding that it leads to disengagement [17, 81, 148]. These types of challenge points are highly variable between participants, highlighting the importance of individualizing the difficulty of mentally fatiguing tasks.

Individualizing difficulty can be achieved using various modalities. Some studies perform multiple preliminary Stroop tasks during the familiarization trial, called the Stroop max tasks [71, 139, 149]. If participants can achieve an accuracy of ≥ 85%, they are allowed to proceed to the next level, which features a 100-ms decrease in stimulus presentation time [71, 139, 149]. The level that participants successfully reach dictates the level that they will perform at for an extended period during the experimental trial [71, 139]. The Stroop task features a number of different parameters (i.e., congruency, inter-stimulus time, stimulus presentation time, etc.) that could all be individualized to optimize the difficulty. Another example of an individualized task used in literature is the TDLoadBack task [82, 83]. The responses of the two cognitive tasks that are performed within this paradigm are alternated in a specific sequence, with a set number of attempts and a required accuracy of at least 85% [82, 83]. Moreover, O’Keeffe et al. [82] suggest that breaks should be added during the individualization session to maximize engagement of participants and avoid inducing MF in the familiarization session, to ensure maximal performance is obtained. The TDLoadBack task has been shown to elicit a significantly larger increase in MF parameters compared with more traditional tasks [82].

In conclusion, a key aspect of the general MF induction task is individualizing the difficulty for each participant. Researchers can use multiple modalities of the cognitive task to individualize it on the basis of task performance. However, cognitive tasks such as the Stroop and N-back are complex, and participants are often unfamiliar with them. Therefore, it is important to provide participants with the opportunity to familiarize themselves with the task before the actual experimental trial. In addition, some specific research questions can only be answered using nonindividualized tasks, meaning that researchers should always keep the context in mind when deciding on the difficulty of the task.

Task Nature

Choice of Possible Cognitive Function(s) to Include

Most studies opt to use single tasks that tax at least one cognitive function to induce MF. These cognitive functions are most often executive functions (i.e., higher-order cognitive processes underlying the selection, scheduling, and coordination and monitoring of complex, goal-directed processes [150, 151]), as these originate from the prefrontal cortex, basal ganglia, and cerebellum [150, 152], areas that are important for exercise-related decision-making [153, 154] and motivation [155]. There is no difference in MF effects when using different types of cognitive functions in the same population [79, 143]. However, response inhibition (i.e., the ability to override impulses [156]) has been suggested as an important feature of any MF induction task [54, 79]. Kurzban et al. [157] propose in their opportunity cost model that response inhibition is a vital part of a task designed to induce MF, as its addition entails that this vital cognitive skill cannot simultaneously be used for other tasks, which is in turn perceived as effortful. MF effects seem to persist for a longer period of time following tasks that require response inhibition [79], and the performance-deteriorating effects of response inhibition tasks also seem to be larger compared with other executive functions [87]. In practice, response inhibition can, for example, be emphasized in a Stroop task by only using incongruent stimuli, combined with the addition of a task switching procedure (reacting to the meaning of the word instead of the color when the color is red) [66, 71]. Apart from response inhibition, research has suggested that the most effective induction tasks require high amounts of attention [28, 158]. If the induction tasks do not require attention to maintain performance, subjects will switch to more automatic cognitive processes [159, 160]. Meanwhile, the subjective feeling of effort is linked to the more deliberate cognitive processes that require attention [161–161]. Moreover, research shows that performance in simple tasks that can be performed in an automatic way can be upheld for a long period of time, while performance in complex tasks, which require attentional control, deteriorates rapidly [162].

However, the use of combinations of different tasks to induce MF is becoming increasingly common [117, 118]. There is no clear consensus on whether single or combined induction tasks are recommended for MF research. Smith et al. [79] argue that tasks with low information load may elicit greater MF compared with combined tasks with higher cognitive demand, which potentially enhance motivation toward task performance [79]. The authors suggest that tasks that are both boring and require response inhibition cause MF [79]. Moreover, the inclusion of more cognitive tasks within a protocol does not necessarily mean more decrements in performance [36]. However, using a battery of cognitive tasks rather than a single task allows multiple cognitive domains to be addressed, and switching between these domains may add additional cognitive demands on individuals [117, 163, 164]. Hassan et al. [117] also propose that combined tasks better represent real-world situations that involve multitasking and the simultaneous use of different cognitive functions. Furthermore, participants may adapt to a single task during its completion, potentially reducing the effectiveness of MF induction [165]. Accordingly, alternating between different cognitive tasks could mitigate this habituation. However, we acknowledge that task switching might reduce MF. Therefore, if a combination task is used, the other domains of the framework need to be adapted to confirm that the appropriate level of MF is induced. Last, while respectfully disagreeing with the argument made by Smith et al. [79], most researchers agree that boredom and MF are different phenomena [14, 81, 166]. Boredom from single, monotonous tasks may reflect a form of passive fatigue, with performance declines linked to sleepiness and underarousal [14, 167]. Meanwhile, MF represents a more active form of fatigue, where performance decrements are related to attention withdrawal due to continuous mental pressure [14, 167]. While both forms of fatigue may influence performance, they are caused by very different mechanisms and should as such be examined separately [14]. Combining different tasks might help avoid passive fatigue, as it could possibly result in less task disengagement [36].

The choice of which cognitive function to target is challenging, as not one cognitive function or combination of functions is definitive, and there are numerous arguments to make for any executive functions to be included, and little research exists on the potential differing effects between various cognitive induction tasks. Any change in the nature of the task will therefore have unexpected consequences. However, on the basis of previous experimental findings and theoretical models of MF, there are some suggestions we can make. First, response inhibition seems to be an essential part to include in any induction task. Second, effort should be made to ensure that the induction tasks require individuals to pay attention, to avoid the activation of more automatic cognitive processes. Last, compared with single tasks, combined tasks appear to offer more benefits as they could engage multiple cognitive domains, increase cognitive demands, prevent passive fatigue, reduce habituation, and better simulate real-world situations. However, when making the final decision, the specific research objectives, available financial resources, relevant evidence, and theoretical considerations should also be considered. As combined tasks are not frequently applied, researchers should use either already tested and valid cognitive test batteries, or perform rigorous evaluation and pilot testing with the use of adequate manipulation checks to see which specific tasks and task properties to include, and in what order they wish to include the tasks [117].

Applied versus Fundamental Cognitive Tasks

Another notable difference in the nature of MF tasks can be observed between applied and fundamental tasks. Most tasks used in MF research are single laboratory-based tasks [15]. These tasks have been extensively researched and provide an easy, cost-effective, and comparable method to induce MF. However, concerns have been raised regarding the task representativeness of fundamental tasks, as they may not adequately reflect cognitive demands of real-world situations [117, 121, 131, 168, 169]. This is not to say that there is any basis for a difference in MF induction and effects between a sports-specific task and a laboratory-based cognitive task. However, the levels and time-course of MF experienced in real-world situations may differ to those induced in a laboratory setting [131, 170, 171]. Therefore, researchers have attempted to use real-world scenarios to induce MF, with examples including physical tasks [131, 170, 171], smartphone use [121], or simulated work environments [172]. Research shows that there are also differences in induced feelings of MF when comparing cognitive to physical and combined (cognitive and physical) tasks, with cognitive and combined tasks inducing significantly higher increases in the subjective feeling of MF compared with physical tasks alone [170]. Some studies have even developed combined tasks that seem to induce similar levels and effects of MF compared with traditional fundamental tasks [131, 171].

To create a suitable task, it is important to understand that several types of cognitive pressure can elicit MF in sports settings. Athletes and their sport science and medical staff in high-performance sports have reported that media engagement, studies, and work commitments are all examples of activities experienced in the daily training environment that elicit MF [141, 173]. Such findings inform us that there are numerous approaches that could be implemented for MF tasks to better represent real-world scenarios experienced in daily training and competition.

Overall, fundamental research contributes to an understanding of the underlying mechanisms and processes associated with MF, while the applied research approach enables practical application and translation. The decision to implement more fundamental or applied cognitive tasks to induce MF is determined by the specific research question and overall aim. While there are numerous arguments to better represent real-life cognitive pressure in MF research, there is little research available investigating the effectiveness and applicability of these tasks. As a result, it is difficult to offer concrete guidance on tasks that reflect the complexities of real-life cognitive pressure. Moreover, the practical benefits of applied tasks are limited in scenarios where researchers wish to investigate the fundamental basics (e.g., brain research) of MF. Fundamental tasks have also been used in numerous articles; they are often more affordable, and do not always require extensive preparation time or pilot testing. However, physical tasks that incorporate a cognitive component could be an important evolution in the way researchers induce MF, as athletes are often confronted with increased cognitive pressure during sport practice. It is important to note that almost no task is purely physical or cognitive. Physical tasks inherently involve some degree of cognitive engagement, while cognitive tasks often require a small physical component (e.g., actively pressing buttons). For the sake of clarity in this review, we use the term “cognitive” task when referring to tasks where the primary load is cognitive and “physical” task when the primary load is physical (i.e., engaging major muscle groups). In addition, when designing studies that use combination tasks to induce MF, researchers should carefully consider and control for potential neuromuscular effects (i.e., muscle fatigue). Moreover, researchers should clearly explain the context in which the particular experiments should be evaluated and interpreted, and how they might represent relevant sport-specific cognitive demands [174].

Need for an Appropriate Control Task

Most studies use an “emotionally neutral” control task as an antithesis to the MF induction task. This neutrality is important to reliably investigate the effects of MF on performance [175]. If a task provides too little cognitive demand or arousal, it might result in boredom, negatively affecting performance in the control group and diminishing a possible effect of MF [177–177]. If a task provides too much cognitive demand or arousal, it might result in an elevation of MF, which negatively affects performance, or in an increased level of arousal, which may positively affect performance [14].

One of the main difficulties in designing an appropriate control task is ensuring that it resembles the intervention task as closely as possible while maintaining a significantly lower cognitive load. That way, researchers assure that primarily cognitive load influences subsequent performance, while also incorporating a better translation to the real world. Using a similar task duration in each of the conditions also facilitates blinding of the participants to their group allocation. At first glance, the control tasks that might provide the best basis to compare between conditions are less cognitively demanding versions of the intervention task, such as a congruent Stroop task [54]. However, research shows that a congruent Stroop task can induce higher levels of perceived exertion in the following physical task compared with the incongruent Stroop task, used as the intervention [59]. This is linked to the possible induction of boredom in the control condition, which is substantiated by a follow-up study that used a relaxing nature scene as the control task and found no higher levels of perceived exertion [59].

Overall, researchers should keep in mind that the design of the control condition is of equal importance to the design of the intervention task, and the same important parameters (i.e., duration, difficulty, and nature) should be taken into account. Research into the optimal design of control tasks is scarce, but there are some guidelines that researchers can consider. Most importantly, control tasks should induce a neutral state in participants and provide an optimal cognitive load to avoid under- or overarousal. This requires a thorough period of pilot testing to compare and evaluate the usefulness of the control task. Moreover, there is value in ensuring the control task is matched to the experimental task in terms of task duration and specificity. However, using a cognitive task that is less cognitively challenging has a high probability of inducing underarousal. In addition, the task should evoke minimal change in emotions, which could be achieved by allowing participants to have as much autonomy as possible. For example, if researchers choose a documentary as their control task, participants should be able to select a preferred documentary, provided that the duration and emotional reactions are similar between choices [85]. These guidelines should provide a meaningful comparison for the utilized intervention task.

Task Design Conclusion

The section above details a number of methods that are currently used to induce MF, as well as a framework to aid researchers in the selection/design of a mentally fatiguing/control task for their study. Unlike physical fatigue, which may exhibit more straightforward relationships between the different task properties in relationship to performance declines, the interaction between these factors in MF is shaped by a more complicated relationship. This complexity is present in the overall framework: a significant increase in the difficulty of the task might lead to a decrease in motivation because the task is too hard, which might also lead to task disengagement. Moreover, a significant change in task nature, such as the change from isolated tasks to combined tasks, will also lead to unexpected consequences. The task properties framework is a complex interaction system, where all properties are inherently linked and influence each other in multidirectional and convoluted ways. Accordingly, when one component is altered, researchers should consider the potential impact on other components and whether they need to be adjusted to produce the intended inducement of MF. Moreover, the context is mentioned in the middle of the framework as it informs all aspects of the induction and control task. This context encompasses considerations such as the research question and environment, the available equipment, etc. The context should always be kept in mind during the construction of not only the different tasks but also the overall design of the study. Notably, if the context requires similar levels of fatigue across the study population, it is important to individualize the intervention task by tailoring task aspects such as time, difficulty, and nature to the individual level of the participant. This may mean a trade-off between differing task parameters; some aspects need to be limited (such as duration) in favor of augmenting other aspects (such as difficulty or complexity). A last consideration is the overall variability in utilized tasks, as this complicates the comparison of findings between studies. Researchers could opt to utilize a task with proven effectiveness, but the proposed framework also enables them to construct a new task that is rigorously tested and based on sound theoretical reasoning.

Different Ways of Quantifying Mental Fatigue

Behavioral

Enoka and Duchateau [178] postulate that fatigue is a self-reported symptom derived from two interdependent attributes: perceived and performance fatigability. These attributes cannot be separated in any way, as they both contribute to the overall presentation of fatigue. Therefore, even if some subjective and physiological changes (perceived fatigability) might be more sensitive to MF, behavioral changes (i.e., decrements of cognitive and physical performance; performance fatigability) are an important check for different types of fatigue [178]. Smith et al. [79] and Hockey et al. [179] further suggest that performance decrement during a cognitive task is the gold standard measure of MF.

A distinction can be made between behavioral checks that are performed during the mentally exhausting task, such as comparing performance of the first block of a Stroop task with the last block [36, 49], and behavioral checks that are completed separately from the mentally fatiguing or control task, such as the flanker task [66] or an abbreviated version of the Stroop task [47, 124]. The first option is useful because it allows researchers to track changes in cognitive performance over time within the same task. However, it is important to point out that this type of check can be attributed to time-on-task, which differs from the sequential task effect (i.e., inducing a clear negative effect on a subsequent and separate task). The time-on-task effect is easier to achieve than a sequential task effect, and the latter would provide more useful information when investigating an effect of MF on physical performance. Moreover, there is discussion on which results (i.e., consistent performance or performance decrement) constitute a clear effect of MF during inducing tasks. Consistent performance throughout the intervention task could indicate engagement of participants and a higher chance that MF was induced during the task [63]. Others indicate that behavioral effects of MF are apparent because of performance decrements during the intervention task [79, 158]. Overall, it is also crucial to keep in mind that time-on-task effects can be influenced by feedback and time awareness during the cognitive task and should therefore be taken into account. This discussion hinders using the results of the intervention task as an indication of the state of MF in participants. Therefore, it seems better to include a separate cognitive task to indicate the behavioral effects of MF. However, this approach might also induce possible issues. For example, using a different task may trigger possible positive effects of task switching, which would allow participants to maintain performance while mentally fatigued [22].

Therefore, to ensure the detection of a behavioral effect, the Three Task Properties Framework and the abovementioned considerations should be taken into account. Researchers must consider tasks that are easy to comprehend and perform, while also being as sensitive as possible to MF changes. If the task is too difficult (e.g. advanced N-back tasks), more than one familiarization trial should be included to ensure that learning effects are excluded in subsequent experimental trials. To increase task sensitivity, it is crucial to select tasks that challenge similar cognitive domains as the tasks that were used for inducement of MF (e.g. response inhibition [54, 79], attention [180], and working memory [36, 180]), as the type and complexity of the task may influence the relationship between performance decrements and MF [79]. Moreover, the similarity between tasks may also negate the possible positive effects of task switching on performance, increasing the overall detection of MF [181]. It is also recommended that researchers consult the existing literature to identify tasks that are consistently influenced by MF and refine existing or new tasks using rigorous pilot testing in new populations and environments. An example of a task that might benefit from adaptation is the flanker task. This task does not consistently detect MF [65, 66, 71], except when the task duration is long enough [182], which is not always feasible to accomplish. A solution could be to add a factor of response inhibition by adding a new stimulus where participants need to refrain from reacting, or constructing a mix of congruent and incongruent trials. These adjustments could possibly make the task more taxing without necessarily making it more complex to understand.

However, there are still numerous issues that are inherent in behavioral manipulation checks. On the one hand, there is debate surrounding the evaluation of performance on the induction task, as performance increase, maintenance, and reduction can indicate signs of fatigue. On the other hand, there is ambiguity in what actually causes a decrement in performance in the induction or subsequent task. For example, a decrease in performance after a Stroop task may not necessarily be attributed to MF but could also be related to underarousal [14]. While both “types” of performance reduction have been linked to MF, it is undeniable that they originate from very different cognitive processes. Moreover, there is also a large amount of interindividual variability in MF effects [5], and until the origin of this variability is clarified, it is challenging to directly determine whether differences in performance are solely related to the effects of MF. It is therefore important to include other manipulation checks and secondary outcome measures when interpreting trial results.

Subjective

In the current literature, subjective assessment is the most frequently used method and is proposed as the most sensitive method to detect MF [79, 183]. Typically, the visual analogue scale (VAS) or Likert scale are used, accompanied by questions such as “How mentally fatigued do you feel?” [66, 97]. VAS uses a continuous scale from 0 to 10/100, sometimes administered verbally [71], while Likert scales are categorical, with examples of 4- and 7-point scales [97, 115]. Both measurement methods show comparable validity and reliability [184]. Likert scales seem to be preferred for participant comfort [184], while VAS might be more sensitive to specific changes and variability because of the range of the scale [185]. Moreover, we should also mention the use of more general questionnaires such as the Brunel Mood Scale (BRUMS) [186] and the National Aeronautics and Space Administration Task Load Index (NASA-TLX) [187] to quantify the subjective level of MF.

Note that each questionnaire has benefits and drawbacks. A drawback that is present overall is response bias, especially in scales such as the VAS, where the specificity of the question (“How mentally fatigued do you feel?”) might lead to participants thinking about MF. It is also worth noting that most studies do not report whether participants were provided with a definition of MF or if they did, how this definition was formulated. This complicates interpretation and reproducibility, as the subjective experience and conceptualization of MF may differ across contexts and populations. Researchers can reduce response bias of the VAS by asking a more general question (i.e., “How tired do you feel?”). However, the specificity of the wording used in the question that details the subjective feeling of MF is imperative. Participants might subjectively relate MF to other experiences, such as underarousal or sleepiness [14, 117]. Moreover, asking general questions also presents specific issues, as general fatigue and MF are not always perceived as the same, and the participant may not know how to reliably distinguish between different forms of fatigue, even if the question is preceded by a purely cognitive task. Another way the response bias of the VAS might be negated is by using the fatigue scale of the BRUMS. However, the aim of questionnaires such as the BRUMS questionnaire is to detect a general feeling of fatigue, not specifically a feeling of MF. Moreover, the scale does this in an indirect way (by using descriptions of emotions that are translated into specific constructs), which also decreases the sensitivity and specificity of the subjective quantification. Last, BRUMS is more time-consuming and less practical for MF quantification. However, it can still provide valuable insights into the participant’s state and can therefore always be included in a trial (see also Sect. 3.2). Meanwhile, measures of workload, such as the NASA-TLX, are not sufficient to be used as a subjective scale to quantify MF. Since fatigue is highly individual [5], a higher workload does not always equate to higher MF. Therefore, it may not be appropriate to use questionnaires such as the NASA-TLX [187] as a subjective manipulation check. However, these types of workload measures can still be used to provide very valuable information (see also Sect. 3.2).

Overall, it seems that measurement scales such as the VAS and Likert are preferred over longer questionnaires owing to their efficiency and simplicity [79]. However, to negate the expected response bias as much as possible, an adequate explanation of the concept of MF to participants, without necessarily revealing the true study purpose, is imperative. That said, explaining MF presents a challenge given the absence of a universally agreed-upon definition. This lack of definition therefore complicates the validation of these scales. However, there are studies that researchers can use as a reference to support the use of specific subjective scales such as the VAS (e.g., Smith et al. [79] and Lee et al. [188]). Specific distinctions between MF and other cognitive phenomena might also help, such as explaining the difference between sleepiness and MF by mentioning that MF could be negated by different interventions, while sleepiness is only solved by taking a nap.

(Neuro)physiological

While subjective measures are the most common approach for measuring MF, there is an increasing interest in physiological markers of MF, since they provide more objective information [189]. These methods are related to the brain, as it has been evidenced that traditional peripheral physiological measures (e.g., lactate, glucose, etc.) do not change as a result of the inducement of MF [1].

Among these, electroencephalography (EEG) has been proposed as the most reliable way to quantify MF [4, 79]. Within EEG, multiple outcomes can be assessed, such as spectral band activity [4], event-related potentials (ERP) [92, 132, 139], and brain networks [190]. Research shows that increased EEG spectral activity (i.e., summary of communication between different cortical cell groups) in alpha and theta bands is associated with MF and time-on-task [4]. These increases are connected to a synchronization of brain waves (i.e., coactivation of neural cells) at different brain regions related to MF [4]. Subsequent research has also found desynchronized brain activity in subsequent cognitive and motor tasks, rather than synchronized patterns in the same frequency bands [71, 191]. This desynchronized activity was also found during resting-state measurements of EEG [90]. In addition to spectral band analysis, EEG can be used for ERP analysis, which refers to the electrical activity in response to specific events or stimuli [192]. Different potentials are related to varying brain processes [71, 193], and MF could impact these processes and potentials [71]. Research has already shown a decrease in amplitude in different ERPs of interest during the mentally fatiguing task, such as P300, which is linked to attentional resources [194, 195]. It is important to point out that the utilization of EEG in exercise settings is currently challenging owing to movement artifacts [194, 195] and long preparation/analyses time [196]. However, advancements in mobile EEG hardware (e.g., dry electrodes) and software (e.g., more advanced pipelines) may offer new opportunities in the field [197, 198].

Another physiological method that has received increasing attention as a potential marker for MF is heart rate variability (HRV) [199]. Mentally demanding activities and time-on-task can influence the autonomic nervous system, which is reflected in HRV patterns (i.e., variability between subsequent heartbeats) [199]. A recent review showed that low frequency components as well as time domain indices seem to be the most reliable markers of MF, with both showing an increase because of time-on-task [199]. Time domain indices are deemed more appropriate for tracking changes in autonomic activity during MF induction, while frequency domain analysis may be more related to how individuals experience and perceive MF symptoms [200]. Findings on resting-state HRV remain inconsistent [40, 55].

Measures of brain oxygenation using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) have also been proposed to quantify MF, as research has postulated that MF leads to decreased cerebral oxygenation [201, 202]. fNIRS is a noninvasive neuroimaging technique that measures changes in the concentration of oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin in the blood [10]. It is a sensitive index of workload [203] and provides a better temporal resolution, resistance to movement artifacts, and practical usability compared with traditional measures of cerebral blood flow [153]. However, studies have not yet provided a clear consensus on how fNIRS oxygenation changes due to MF [36, 84, 204]. Accordingly, the mixed findings indicate that the role of fNIRS in MF quantification requires further investigation.

Finally, there are other physiological measurements that are not yet extensively used in the context of MF and movement science literature but hold potential for future exploration. Pupil metrics are a complementary measure that can offer valuable insights [205]. Research has reported that fatigue induced increased saccade (i.e., rapid shifts in line of sight from one preferred location to the other [206]) duration and decreased magnitude/velocity. In addition, when the brain’s dopamine network perceives that task rewards do not meet expectations owing to MF, dopamine levels decrease leading to decreased blink frequency [205]. Moreover, pupil dilation and constriction can be indicative of MF [205]. Salivary measures have also been investigated as potential markers, with elevated cortisol, testosterone, and alpha-amylase levels related to increased MF [52, 207]. However, subsequent research has shown that these elevations do not correlate with MF in a practical setting, as they can be affected by various factors [208, 209]. As such, these measurements should be carefully controlled for and interpreted in the context of the specific study.

Thus, EEG and HRV have gained recognition as objective indicators of MF and can be used in the appropriate context to indicate a state of MF [4, 199]. However, it is important to mention that the majority of studies investigate these markers during the induction task, which might be more indicative of the level of effort/engagement during a prolonged task. Not many studies investigate MF during a resting state, which might show different results compared with measurements during the induction task. Moreover, multiple methodological limitations are presented here, including overlap with parameters such as sleepiness and limited usefulness in the field. A possible solution to this last limitation is to further investigate wearable commodity devices (e.g., smart watches, chest belts), which could bridge the gap between lab studies and practical application by providing on-field monitoring of MF. Multiple other detection methods have been introduced but are not yet as reliable at detecting MF compared with the other mentioned manipulation checks. Therefore, these measures should always be used in combination with valid methods to quantify MF. Moreover, the rigor and timing of collection of the different (neuro)physiological indicators are of utmost importance, and researchers should be cognizant that their collection and analysis approaches are of required scientific quality to demonstrate any potentially meaningful responses.

Confounders

In addition to subjective, behavioral, and physiological assessments of MF, other measurements are also recommended to obtain a more comprehensive understanding of the participant’s state. This is because both chronic conditions (e.g., burnout [210] and general fatigue [24]) and acute cognitive states (e.g., sleepiness [211] and boredom [146]) could interfere with MF quantification. For example, sleepiness results in decreased physical behavioral parameters [212]. Meanwhile, boredom could result in similar decreases in frequency bands when using EEG [71]. Therefore, studies that investigate MF need valid measures to control for different confounders.

Researchers should consider adding different screening measures to ensure that they are aware if their participants are currently suffering from any mental health challenges, to either exclude these participants, or consider their diagnosis and potential medications in later analyses. Examples of such measures are the Multidimensional Fatigue Index [213], the Burnout Assessment Tool [214], and the Beck Depression Inventory [215]. Another example of possible covariates is sleep or sleepiness, which can be assessed in objective and subjective ways, with varying levels of accuracy. A long-term (1-month) subjective measure of sleep is the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index [216], while the Karolinska [217] or Epworth Sleepiness Scale [218] can be used as more acute measures. Examples of objective measures of sleep include different wearables, with variability in the accuracy of their sleep-staging identification [219, 220].

Motivation, another confounder, is challenging to evaluate, as there are differences in instruments based on motivation type. Examples include a motivation VAS [221] (task-specific motivation), the Matthews Success Motivation and Intrinsic Motivation scales [222] (intrinsic motivation before the task), and the Situational Motivation Scale (situational motivation after task completion) [223]. Researchers should be careful to use these scales appropriately, and consider using more than one scale, to make sure that motivation is not an influencing factor in their measurements. Another important confounder to mention here is workload [187, 224, 225]. While measures such as the NASA-TLX cannot be used to directly measure fatigue, they provide important information on the overall difference in nature between tasks and could be used to investigate possible instances of task disengagement. Other measures that are often used in MF research are those that assess mood (e.g., BRUMS [186]), boredom (e.g., boredom VAS [139]), and anxiety (e.g., State Anxiety Index [96]), among many others.

This section provides only a brief overview of possible confounders that could influence MF research findings, and caution with interpretation of their findings is important. For example, a recent systematic review identified multiple different countermeasures that can be used to negate MF effects, such as caffeine [22]. These measures should in theory be controlled for, which is not always practically feasible. Nevertheless, researchers can use the proposed guidelines to determine the influence of confounders.

Using diverse quantification methods allows researchers to fully understand how MF was induced and to draw more accurate conclusions about participants’ mental states. A Way Forward

The findings of this review underscore a considerable need for stronger consensus and reduced methodological variability in MF research. While a crucial step forward would be to establish greater clarity and consistency in how MF is defined and measured, we acknowledge that achieving a single, unified definition may be challenging, given the broad range of constructs often associated with MF. A more immediate and practical step would be to ensure clear operational definitions of the specific type of fatigue being measured, alongside thorough reporting of the tasks used to induce it and the manipulation checks applied. We recommend that future research focuses on developing a consensus paper to consolidate existing knowledge, clarify overlapping terms, and establish standardized terminology. By improving clarity in reporting, research on the effects of MF on sport performance can progress with greater transparency and comparability.

To address the need for methodological consistency in MF research, this review advances the field by presenting perspectives on a robust best-practice approach. This framework approach should not be interpreted as a rigid set of rules but rather as a set of adaptable guidelines and tools that can be tailored to the specific research approaches and needs. Central to this approach are the MF-induction and MF-quantification frameworks. The MF-induction framework (Fig. 1) emphasizes the interactions between task properties, ensuring that tasks are tailored to the specific context and level of the individual. The MF-quantification framework (Fig. 2) addresses the limitations of single-method approaches and recommends using multiple measurement methods for a more comprehensive understanding of MF. Together, these frameworks aim to enhance the quality, consistency, and transparency of future MF research.

To further support this aim, we have developed the SPeCIFY reporting guidelines (Fig. 3), a dedicated resource designed to improve reporting standards for MF studies. SPeCIFY—an acronym for Settings, Protocol establishments, Confounders, Individuals, Framework, and Yield—is tailored specifically for research utilizing MF protocols. It is important to note that SPeCIFY could best be used in conjunction with general reporting guidelines (e.g., CONSORT [226] and STROBE [227]) to complement existing frameworks and contribute to a comprehensive and standardized approach. These guidelines align with existing knowledge and prompt researchers to systematically account for critical factors that potentially influence study outcomes. The SPeCIFY guidelines, presented in a checklist format, incorporate the MF induction and quantification frameworks and address essential aspects such as environmental settings, protocol specifics, and participant characteristics. While not all elements of the checklist will be relevant to each protocol, adherence to these guidelines is strongly recommended as standard practice for future MF research. It is proposed that consistent implementation of the SPeCIFY guidelines by researchers will significantly enhance the integrity, consistency, transparency, and impact of studies. Accordingly, this will advance the field of research investigating MF by addressing the commonly acknowledged and critiqued existing replication and reproducibility limitations in future work.

Conclusions

Research studying MF in movement science suffers from a lack of standardized approaches for MF induction and quantification, leading to inconsistent findings. By presenting frameworks for MF induction and quantification, this review provides a structured approach for improving the methodological quality of MF research and generalizability of study results. Moreover, SPeCIFY reporting guidelines offer a tool to enhance study transparency and replicability, addressing the key challenge of methodological variability. Future research should not only adopt these frameworks and guidelines but also focus on further improving our understanding of the induction and quantification of MF, with specific mention of establishing a clear and unified definition of MF.
