Molecularly imprinted polymer ZnO nanostructures derived from Pseuderanthemum palatiferum leaf extract for paper-based colorimetric sensing of chlortetracycline
Authors: Nguyen Ngoc Nghia, Tran Anh Ton, Cao Phat Hung, Hoang Xuan Khang, Phan Quang Huy Hoang, Tran Dang Khoa, Nguyen Huu Hieu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chlortetracycline (CTC) is a widely used tetracycline antibiotic in veterinary medicine, livestock production, and aquaculture, yet its excessive use and environmental persistence have raised increasing concerns regarding food safety, ecological contamination, and antibiotic resistance. In this study, we developed a portable and selective colorimetric sensing platform based on molecularly imprinted ZnO nanozymes integrated into a paper-based analytical device (PAD) for rapid on-site detection of CTC. ZnO nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) were synthesized via a green route using Pseuderanthemum palatiferum (P. palatiferum) leaf extract and subsequently surface-imprinted using 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane (APTES) and tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) in the presence of CTC as the template molecule. Structural characterization by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and X-ray diffraction (XRD) confirmed the successful formation of the imprinted polymer layer while preserving the hexagonal wurtzite crystalline structure of ZnO. The resulting molecularly imprinted polymer-modified ZnO (MIP@ZnO) retained intrinsic peroxidase-like activity and efficiently catalyzed the H2O2-mediated oxidation of 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB), generating a blue oxidized product with a characteristic absorbance at 652 nm. Upon selective rebinding of CTC to the imprinted cavities, the catalytic activity was suppressed through a binding-induced inhibition mechanism, resulting in a concentration-dependent decrease in absorbance. Under optimized conditions, the sensor exhibited a linear response over the range of 0–15 µM with a limit of detection (LOD) of 0.35 µM. Integration into a capillary-driven PAD enabled rapid visual analysis within minutes with minimal reagent consumption and no need for complex instrumentation. The platform also demonstrated good selectivity against structurally related antibiotics and common interfering species. Practical applicability was verified using tap water and milk samples, affording satisfactory recoveries and acceptable precision without extensive sample pretreatment. These results demonstrate that the proposed MIP@ZnO-based PAD provides a low-cost, field-deployable, and effective strategy for selective monitoring of CTC residues in complex real samples.

Introduction

CTC, a broad-spectrum tetracycline antibiotic, is extensively used in veterinary practice, livestock production, and aquaculture for the prevention and treatment of bacterial infections. Despite its therapeutic effectiveness and relative stability, the excessive or prolonged application of CTC has generated growing concerns regarding food safety and environmental contamination. A considerable fraction of the administered antibiotic is excreted in an unmetabolized form, leading to its persistent release into soil and aquatic environments.1,2 Owing to its strong chelation with divalent metal ions and pronounced affinity for organic matter, CTC can persist and accumulate in diverse environmental compartments while retaining its biological activity. This prolonged persistence imposes selective pressure on microbial populations, thereby promoting the emergence and horizontal spread of antibiotic-resistance genes (ARGs) and antibiotic-resistant bacteria (ARB). Consequently, such contamination not only diminishes the therapeutic effectiveness of tetracycline antibiotics but also poses a serious threat to animal and human health. Moreover, long-term exposure to trace levels of CTC may disrupt ecological equilibrium and induce adverse toxicological effects in non-target organisms. Accordingly, the development of rapid, sensitive, and field-deployable analytical strategies for monitoring CTC residues in environmental and biological matrices is of critical importance for the protection of public health and ecological sustainability.

Given these concerns, the rapid and on-site detection of CTC residues is crucial for environmental and food safety monitoring. Conventional methods such as High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC),3–5 Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS),6–8 and Enzyme-Linked ImmunoSorbent Assay (ELISA)9 provide high sensitivity and selectivity. However, these techniques generally depend on costly instrumentation, labor-intensive sample pretreatment, and highly trained personnel, which restricts their applicability for on-site analysis. By contrast, paper-based colorimetric platforms provide a portable, cost-effective, and user-friendly alternative, allowing direct visual readout without the need for sophisticated analytical equipment.10–13

Various optical and colorimetric strategies have been reported for CTC detection, including fluorescence probes,14 metal nanocluster-based sensors,15 nanozyme systems,16 surface-enhanced Raman scattering substrates,17 and electrochemical18 methods. These methods have improved the sensitivity of CTC determination; however, some still require relatively complex probe preparation, instrumental readout, or lack sufficient selectivity in complex matrices. Colorimetric sensing based on TMB oxidation is attractive because the analytical signal can be observed visually and quantified by absorbance or image analysis. Nevertheless, for structurally similar tetracycline antibiotics, selectivity remains a key challenge. Combining a colorimetric nanozyme reaction with molecular imprinting can address this limitation by integrating catalytic signal amplification with template-specific recognition.

Green synthesis of ZnO NPs has attracted considerable attention as a sustainable alternative to conventional chemical routes because it can reduce the use of toxic reducing or stabilizing agents while providing nanoparticles with suitable surface properties for sensing applications. In plant-extract-mediated synthesis, phytochemicals such as phenolic compounds, flavonoids, alkaloids, reducing sugars, tannins, terpenoids, and other antioxidant constituents may participate in precursor conversion, nucleation, growth control, and surface stabilization of ZnO NPs. Therefore, the chemical composition of the plant extract can strongly influence the formation and surface characteristics of the resulting ZnO material.

In the present work, P. palatiferum leaf extract was selected as a green medium for ZnO synthesis because this plant is known to contain bioactive phytochemicals with reducing and stabilizing capability. These constituents are expected to facilitate ZnO formation and reduce the need for harsh synthetic reagents. The use of P. palatiferum extract therefore provides a simple, low-cost, and environmentally friendly route for producing ZnO NPs suitable for subsequent surface functionalization and colorimetric sensing.

Although ZnO nanozymes can provide catalytic signal amplification, bare ZnO does not possess intrinsic molecular selectivity toward CTC. Molecular imprinting is an effective strategy to introduce target-specific recognition sites into synthetic polymer layers. In a molecularly imprinted polymer, the template molecule interacts with functional monomers before polymerization; after polymer formation and template removal, cavities complementary to the template in size, shape, and functional group distribution remain within the polymer matrix. These recognition cavities enable selective rebinding of the target molecule even in the presence of structurally related compounds.

Surface molecular imprinting is particularly suitable for nanoparticle-based sensors because the recognition sites are located near the external surface, improving mass transfer and target accessibility. In this study, CTC was used as the template molecule, APTES was used as the functional silane monomer, and TEOS was used as the crosslinking precursor to form a siloxane-based imprinted layer on ZnO. This design combines the recognition capability of molecular imprinting with the catalytic activity of ZnO nanozymes.

ZnO was selected as the sensing material for several reasons. First, ZnO is inexpensive, chemically stable, environmentally acceptable, and readily synthesized by green methods. Second, ZnO NPs possess abundant surface hydroxyl groups, which facilitate silane functionalization and subsequent formation of an APTES/TEOS-derived imprinted layer. Third, ZnO can exhibit peroxidase-like nanozyme activity and catalyze the H2O2-mediated oxidation of TMB to blue ox-TMB, providing a simple optical signal at 652 nm. Therefore, ZnO is an appropriate support and catalytic transducer for developing a colorimetric MIP-based sensing platform.

In this work, ZnO NPs were molecularly imprinted with CTC using APTES and TEOS to fabricate MIP@ZnO. After template removal, the material provides specific recognition cavities while retaining the intrinsic peroxidase-like activity of ZnO. The nanozyme efficiently catalyzes the H2O2-driven oxidation of TMB, generating a characteristic blue oxidized TMB (ox-TMB) signal. Upon selective rebinding of CTC to the imprinted recognition sites, the catalytic process is inhibited, resulting in a concentration-dependent attenuation of color intensity. The developed platform achieved sensitive detection with a LOD of 0.35 µM, while the analytical response could be obtained within only a few minutes. This work integrates three functions in one sensing platform: CTC-specific recognition by surface molecular imprinting, catalytic signal generation by ZnO-mediated TMB oxidation, and portable readout using a paper-based colorimetric format. This combination is intended to improve selectivity while maintaining simple and low-cost operation for on-site CTC screening.19,20 Non-imprinted polymer-modified ZnO (NIP@ZnO) was prepared for comparison.

Coupling the assay with a paper-based colorimetric readout device can simplify signal visualization, reduce reagent consumption, and enable smartphone-assisted analysis without complex instrumentation. Paper substrates are attractive for field sensing because they are inexpensive, disposable, lightweight, and compatible with capillary liquid transport and visual detection. In this study, the PAD was used as a portable colorimetric readout platform for visualizing and quantifying the CTC-induced inhibition of the MIP@ZnO/TMB/H2O2 reaction.

Compared with conventional methods for CTC determination, the proposed surface-imprinted ZnO-based PAD offers several distinctive advantages, including (i) imprinting-enabled selectivity at the nanozyme–analyte interface, (ii) pump-free fluid transport through capillary action with preloaded reagents, and (iii) rapid quantitative readout within minutes, while maintaining sensitive detection at the submicromolar level. Overall process of CTC detection.

The novelty of this work lies in the integration of green-synthesized ZnO nanozymes, CTC-specific surface molecular imprinting, and a paper-based colorimetric readout format. Unlike bare ZnO or non-imprinted ZnO, MIP@ZnO provides recognition cavities that selectively rebind CTC and modulate the ZnO-catalyzed TMB/H2O2 reaction. The analytical signal is generated through binding-induced inhibition of peroxidase-like activity, allowing CTC concentration to be correlated with the decrease in ox-TMB absorbance and color intensity. This strategy provides a selective and portable sensing approach for CTC monitoring in tap water and milk samples.

Experimental section

Chemicals

CTC, APTES, TEOS, TMB, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), zinc acetate dihydrate (Zn(CH3COO)2·2H2O) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). All solutions were prepared, and all sample processing procedures were performed using ultrapure deionized water to ensure experimental consistency and minimize potential contamination.

Characterization

FTIR was acquired using an FT/IR-6600 Type A spectrometer (Jasco, Japan). UV-visible absorption measurements were performed with a Jasco V-670 UV-vis spectrophotometer (Japan). XRD patterns were recorded on an Ultima IV diffractometer (Rigaku, Japan). SEM was examined by JSM-IT200, JEOL, Japan.

Synthesis of ZnO NPs

Dried leaves of P. palatiferum (5.0 g) were placed in a 250 mL beaker containing 50 mL of ethanol and 50 mL of distilled water. The mixture was sealed and subjected to ultrasonic-assisted extraction in a water bath maintained at 30–35 °C for 60 min. After extraction, the suspension was vacuum-filtered to obtain a clear filtrate, which was subsequently used as the biogenic precursor for nanoparticle synthesis.

For ZnO NPs preparation, 2.0 g of Zn(CH3COO)2·2H2O was dissolved in 10 mL of distilled water in a 100 mL beaker, followed by heating at 60–70 °C under continuous magnetic stirring. Subsequently, 20 mL of the plant extract was added dropwise to the precursor solution while maintaining constant stirring, and the reaction was allowed to proceed for 2 h at the same temperature. The resulting mixture was then concentrated until gel formation occurred, followed by drying at 70 °C for 24 h. The dried gel was calcined in a furnace at 700 °C for 1 h, and the obtained solid product was finally pulverized into a fine powder using a ball mill to yield ZnO NPs. The calcination temperature of 700 °C was selected based on previous researches to ensure effective removal of plant-derived organic residues and formation of crystalline hexagonal wurtzite ZnO. Lower calcination temperatures may leave residual organic matter and produce less-defined diffraction peaks, whereas excessively high temperatures may promote particle sintering and reduce surface area.21–23 Therefore, 700 °C for 1 h was selected as a compromise between crystallinity, purity, and preservation of the nanoparticulate structure. The overall synthesis procedure of ZnO NPs is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Synthesis of MIP@ZnO and NIP@ZnO

ZnO was first surface-functionalized with 10 µL of APTES. Subsequently, 25 mL of 1 mM CTC solution was introduced, and the mixture was stirred for 5 min to promote the formation of a pre-polymerization complex between the template molecule and the functional silane. Thereafter, 5 mL of TEOS was added, and the reaction mixture was further stirred for 30 min to facilitate sol–gel condensation and the formation of the polymeric silica network. The resulting precipitate was collected by filtration and dried, together with the filter paper, in a desiccator at 70 °C for 2 h. The obtained solid was isolated as the MIP@ZnO. To remove the template, the precipitate was washed five times with ethanol until residual CTC was eliminated.

For preparation of the non-imprinted control, the same procedure was carried out under identical conditions but in the absence of CTC. The resulting material, denoted NIP@ZnO, was synthesized to provide a structurally comparable non-imprinted reference (Fig. 1).

In this molecular imprinting system, APTES and TEOS served as the functional monomer and crosslinking precursor, respectively, and played central roles in the generation of selective recognition sites for CTC. Specifically, APTES contains a primary amine group capable of interacting with the template molecule through non-covalent interactions, such as hydrogen bonding and electrostatic attraction, during the pre-polymerization stage. At the same time, its triethoxysilane moieties undergo hydrolysis and condensation reactions with TEOS, enabling incorporation of the template–monomer complex into the growing silica matrix. TEOS subsequently forms a rigid, porous, and chemically stable siloxane framework through sol–gel polymerization, thereby preserving the spatial arrangement of the imprinted sites. Following template removal, three-dimensional cavities complementary in size, shape, and chemical functionality to CTC remain within the polymer matrix, enabling selective rebinding during sensing. Such APTES/TEOS-based imprinting systems have been widely employed in molecularly imprinted polymers because of their high selectivity, structural robustness, and good reproducibility, particularly in selective recognition and extraction applications.24

Peroxidase-like activity of MIP@ZnO and NIP@ZnO detected by UV spectrophotometer

Peroxidase is a catalytic enzyme that facilitates the H2O2-mediated oxidation of TMB. In the present study, the peroxidase-like activities of MIP@ZnO and NIP@ZnO nanomaterials were investigated using TMB as the chromogenic substrate. The TMB working solution (1 mM) was prepared in a mixed solvent system consisting of 1 mL DMSO and 9 mL citrate-phosphate buffer (pH 4.0), while 3 wt% H2O2 was employed as the oxidizing agent.

To assess the effect of antibiotic binding on the catalytic performance of the ZnO-based materials, CTC (1 mM) was introduced into the reaction system. After incubation for 30 min, the reaction mixtures were analyzed by UV-vis spectroscopy, and the absorbance at 652 nm, corresponding to ox-TMB, was recorded as an indicator of catalytic activity.

Detection of CTC on PADs

The PADs were designed using AutoCAD 2026 with an overall diameter of 8.0 cm. The layout comprised one central circular zone surrounded by five peripheral circular zones, each with a diameter of 2.7 cm. These zones were interconnected by five straight channels extending radially from the center, with each channel measuring 0.8 × 4.1 cm. To ensure efficient liquid absorption and clear visualization of the ox-TMB color signal, qualitative filter paper with a pore size of 15–20 µm was selected as the substrate material. The patterned paper device was fabricated using a STYLECNC STJ1390-2 dual-head CO2 laser cutter (Fig. 2).

Illustration of PAD for detecting CTC.

CTC detection on the paper-based device was based on the peroxidase-like catalytic activity of MIP@ZnO. The assay procedure followed the same protocol described in Section 2.5, performed in the absence and presence of four different concentrations of CTC. After completion of the reaction, five resulting solutions were individually dispensed onto the corresponding detection zones of the paper device. The liquid was absorbed into the paper matrix, and the developed color in each circular zone was recorded using a smartphone camera. The color intensity was subsequently quantified through red green blue (RGB) analysis. For real-sample evaluation, tap water and milk samples were tested after dilution to 0.2 vol%.

Effect of various parameters on peroxidase-like activity of MIP@ZnO

Various reaction parameters were systematically optimized to establish the optimal conditions for catalytic performance. These parameters included pH (2–8), MIP@ZnO concentration (1–5 mg mL−1), TMB concentration (400–700 µM), and H2O2 concentration over the same range.

Sensitivity and selectivity analysis of CTC

The functionalized MIP@ZnO composite was employed as the recognition and catalytic element for colorimetric detection of CTC. In a typical assay, MIP@ZnO (1 mg mL−1), TMB (500 µM), and H2O2 (19.578 µM) were introduced into citrate-phosphate buffer (pH 4.0) under ambient conditions. Under these conditions, the initially colorless TMB was catalytically oxidized to blue-colored ox-TMB, which was monitored by UV-vis spectroscopy, exhibiting a characteristic absorption maximum at 652 nm. The resulting color change was also readily observable by the naked eye.

For CTC determination, MIP@ZnO was first incubated in separate reaction tubes containing different concentrations of CTC for 15 min to allow selective rebinding at the imprinted sites. Subsequently, the peroxidase-like reaction was initiated, and the absorbance spectra were recorded after 30 min, with particular attention to the signal at 652 nm. For selectivity evaluation, structurally relevant antibiotics at a concentration of 33 µM were individually introduced into the MIP@ZnO-based assay system under the same experimental conditions.

Detection of CTC in real samples

To assess the practical applicability of the proposed method in real-world settings, tap water and milk were selected as representative sample matrices. To mitigate potential matrix effects, each sample was diluted to 0.2 vol% prior to analysis.

Results and discussions

Characteristic

Fig. S1 shows that the pristine ZnO sample is composed of densely packed micron-scale agglomerates built from much finer primary nanoparticles, giving rise to a relatively uniform granular texture across the 1, 5, and 10 µm scales. After surface modification, both polymer-coated materials preserve the aggregated ZnO framework, indicating that the sol–gel treatment did not destroy the original particulate architecture. However, clear changes in surface texture are observed after coating. In the MIP@ZnO sample, the particles appear more coalesced and form larger cauliflower-like secondary aggregates with partially fused nodules, whereas NIP@ZnO retains a more finely granular appearance with more distinguishable interparticle boundaries. These morphological differences suggest that the presence of the template during polymer formation affected the local condensation and packing of the APTES/TEOS-derived siloxane network on the ZnO surface. SEM analysis further confirmed the successful surface functionalization of ZnO, indicating that the imprinted polymer layer was predominantly formed on the external surface without inducing substantial restructuring of the ZnO core.

This interpretation is consistent with previous reports showing that bare ZnO NPs inherently tend to agglomerate because of their high surface energy, while silica/silane modification changes aggregate texture by covering or bridging adjacent ZnO particles. In surface molecular imprinting, the recognition sites are generated at or near the outer layer of the support, so template-assisted polymerization can alter the compactness and heterogeneity of the surface relative to the non-imprinted control. Although individual imprinted cavities are below the resolution of the present SEM images, the distinct packing state observed for MIP@ZnO compared with NIP@ZnO is in good agreement with the expected formation of an imprinted surface layer containing accessible recognition domains after template removal.25–28

The FTIR spectra provides clear insights into the structure changes occurring during the molecular imprinting process on the ZnO support (Fig. 3). The spectrum of pure ZnO is relatively featureless in the mid-IR region, with a strong absorption below 500 cm−1 attributed to the Zn–O stretching vibration, confirming the formation of ZnO. All polymer-coated samples (MIP@ZnO) exhibit additional absorption bands compared to bare ZnO, indicating successful polymer formation on the ZnO surface. A weak band around 2360 cm−1 is observed, which becomes more pronounced in the MIP@ZnO prepared in the presence of CTC. This band can be associated with hydrogen-bonded O–H and N–H stretching, suggesting stronger intermolecular interactions between the template molecule and functional monomers during imprinting. Notably, an absorption band at approximately 1038 cm−1, absent in pure ZnO, appears in all polymer-containing samples, suggesting the formation of Si–O–Si bonds within the polymer matrix.29 This peak is attributed to C–O and C–N stretching vibrations originating from the polymer matrix, confirming the successful grafting of the molecularly imprinted polymer onto the ZnO surface. In the region of 800–1100 cm−1, clear differences are observed between MIP@ZnO with and without CTC. The MIP@ZnO synthesized in the presence of CTC exhibits stronger and broader absorption features around 788 cm−1, which can be assigned to out-of-plane bending vibrations of aromatic rings and C–H deformation modes associated with CTC structure. These features are weaker or absent in the MIP@ZnO without CTC, indicating successful template incorporation during polymerization. Furthermore, a distinct absorption band around 430–460 cm−1 appears in all polymer-modified samples but is not prominent in bare ZnO. This band is attributed to skeletal vibrations and bending modes of the polymer network, further confirming polymer coating on the ZnO surface. Overall, the FTIR results demonstrate that the molecular imprinting process successfully introduces new functional groups onto ZnO, and the spectral differences between MIP@ZnO with and without CTC confirm the effective incorporation of CTC and the formation of specific recognition sites within the imprinted polymer. The confirmation of polymer formation was based primarily on the siloxane-related bands and the comparison between ZnO, NIP@ZnO, and MIP@ZnO.

FTIR spectra of ZnO, MIP@ZnO (without CTC), MIP@ZnO (with CTC), NIP@ZnO. All spectra were baseline-corrected and normalized using the same transmittance scale.

The diffraction pattern of ZnO (Fig. 4) shows a series of sharp and well-defined peaks at 2θ values of approximately 31.7°, 34.4°, 36.2°, 47.5°, 56.6°, 62.9°, 66.4°, 68.0°, and 69.1°, which can be indexed to the (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), (103), (200), (112), and (201) crystal planes of hexagonal wurtzite ZnO (JCPDS No. 36-1451). These sharp peaks confirm the high crystallinity and phase purity of the synthesized ZnO. After coating with the molecularly imprinted polymer, the XRD pattern of MIP@ZnO retains all characteristic diffraction peaks of ZnO, indicating that the crystalline structure of ZnO remains intact during the polymerization process. However, a noticeable reduction in peak intensity is observed for MIP@ZnO compared to bare ZnO, particularly for the dominant reflections at ∼34.4° and ∼36.2°. This decrease in intensity can be attributed to the presence of an amorphous polymer layer covering the ZnO surface, which attenuates the diffracted X-ray signals. Importantly, no additional diffraction peaks corresponding to impurity phases are detected in the MIP@ZnO sample, demonstrating that the polymer coating does not introduce any crystalline by-products. The absence of peak shifting further suggests that the polymer layer is physically deposited on the ZnO surface rather than incorporated into the ZnO crystal lattice. Overall, the XRD results confirm that ZnO maintains its wurtzite crystalline structure after molecular imprinting. The reduced peak intensity of MIP@ZnO compared with bare ZnO can be attributed to the presence of an amorphous siloxane/MIP layer covering the ZnO surface.

XRD patterns of ZnO and MIP@ZnO (without CTC).

No distinct additional crystalline phase was observed in MIP@ZnO compared with pristine ZnO. The characteristic reflections of hexagonal wurtzite ZnO were retained after the imprinting process and matched the standard ZnO reference pattern, JCPDS/ICDD No. 36-1451. The absence of additional peaks indicates that no crystalline impurity or secondary phase was generated during APTES/TEOS modification. The decrease in diffraction intensity for MIP@ZnO is attributed to the amorphous siloxane/MIP layer on the ZnO surface, which partially attenuates the X-ray diffraction signal.

SEM images of ZnO, MIP@ZnO, and NIP@ZnO are provided in Fig. S1. The three samples showed aggregated particulate morphologies, and no major morphological reconstruction was observed after the APTES/TEOS sol–gel imprinting process. Because SEM mainly reflects the external aggregate morphology and does not provide direct chemical evidence for the imprinted polymer layer, the SEM images were moved to the SI. The formation of the surface polymer layer was instead evaluated primarily by FTIR spectroscopy, while preservation of the ZnO crystalline phase was confirmed by XRD.

Mechanism of sensing

Fig. 5 shows the UV-vis absorbance spectra of TMB under different experimental conditions, with the analytical signal monitored at 652 nm. Unmodified ZnO (Sample 1: TMB + H2O2 + ZnO) exhibited the highest absorbance, indicating efficient catalytic oxidation of TMB and confirming its pronounced peroxidase-like activity. This behavior can be attributed to the effective interaction of H2O2 with the ZnO surface, which facilitates the catalytic reaction. In contrast, Sample 2 (TMB + H2O2 + NIP@ZnO), in which ZnO was modified with APTES and TEOS to form a non-imprinted silica shell, displayed a marked decrease in absorbance intensity.30 This attenuation suggests that the silica layer partially restricts the accessibility of H2O2 to the ZnO surface, thereby reducing catalytic efficiency. In contrast, Sample 3 (TMB + H2O2 + MIP@ZnO), prepared using CTC as the template followed by template removal to generate imprinted recognition cavities, exhibited a clear recovery of oxidation activity. This enhanced response indicates that the imprinted structure facilitates more favorable interaction with the reactants, thereby promoting TMB oxidation. Upon the addition of CTC (Samples 4 and 5), the absorbance decreased markedly in both the NIP and MIP systems, which may be attributed to occupation of surface-active or recognition sites that interferes with H2O2 access to the catalytic interface. Notably, the MIP@ZnO + CTC system (Sample 5) retained higher catalytic activity than the corresponding non-imprinted system (Sample 4), highlighting the beneficial role of molecular imprinting in conferring target-selective recognition while preserving superior functional performance.31 The observed suppression of the peroxidase-like activity of MIP@ZnO in the presence of CTC can be primarily attributed to a binding-induced inhibition mechanism. The molecularly imprinted layer formed on the ZnO surface provides recognition cavities that are complementary to CTC in terms of size, shape, and chemical functionality, thereby enabling selective rebinding of the target molecule upon re-exposure. Such binding events partially occupy or block access to the nanozyme surface, thereby hindering its interaction with the reaction substrates, namely TMB and H2O2, and resulting in a marked decrease in catalytic activity. Accordingly, the selective recognition conferred by the imprinted polymer significantly affects the kinetics of TMB oxidation by restricting the diffusion and accessibility of H2O2 to the active nanozyme interface.32 These findings support the proposed binding-induced inhibition mechanism and underscore the critical role of the molecularly imprinted polymer in modulating catalytic activity through selective competitive binding.

Effect of various parameters

A systematic optimization of the key experimental parameters was performed to maximize both the catalytic efficiency and practical applicability of the nanozyme-based colorimetric platform, as the H2O2-mediated oxidation of TMB is highly sensitive to reaction conditions, including pH, substrate concentration, oxidant dosage, and temperature (Fig. 6). Importantly, the final working conditions were selected not merely on the basis of the highest absorbance response, but on achieving an optimal balance among catalytic performance, structural stability, and overall assay reliability. The catalytic activity exhibits a pronounced dependence on pH, as illustrated in Fig. 6a. While highly acidic conditions (pH 2.2) yield the highest absorbance, such extreme acidity may destabilize the imprinted recognition sites and promote non-selective substrate oxidation.

Effect of (a) incubation pH, (b) concentration of the TMB, (c) concentration of the H2O2, and (d) temperature on absorbance of TMB + H2O2 + MIP@ZnO.

At pH 4.0, the system still maintains a relatively strong signal, while offering a more moderate acidic environment that better preserves the structural integrity of the MIP layer and enhances assay selectivity. Consequently, pH 4.0 was chosen as the working condition, representing an optimal balance between catalytic activity and sensor robustness.33

As presented in Fig. 6b, increasing the TMB concentration enhances the absorbance response due to greater substrate availability. Although the highest signal is observed near intermediate concentrations, a higher TMB level ensures excess substrate supply and improves visual color contrast, which is particularly beneficial for paper-based or on-site detection formats. Thus, 500 µM TMB was chosen as the optimal substrate concentration, ensuring sufficient chromogenic intensity and consistent analytical performance.34,35

As shown in Fig. 6c, increasing the H2O2 concentration from 3.265 to 22.843 µM results in a gradual enhancement of the absorbance signal at 652 nm, confirming that higher oxidant availability promotes reactive oxygen species generation and accelerates TMB oxidation.

Although the strongest response appears at the highest tested concentration, excessively high H2O2 levels may lead to overproduction of radicals, which can induce non-specific oxidation and potentially compromise the stability of the nanozyme or polymeric recognition layer. Therefore, 19.578 µM H2O2 was selected as the optimal concentration, providing a strong catalytic response while maintaining controlled radical flux and operational durability.

The influence of temperature on the catalytic oxidation process is shown in Fig. 6d. The absorbance increases as the temperature rises to 60 °C, suggesting accelerated reaction kinetics at elevated temperatures. However, operation under high-temperature conditions is undesirable for portable sensing applications and may disturb hydrogen bonding interactions within the molecularly imprinted framework. Notably, the nanozyme retains sufficient catalytic activity at room temperature (25 °C), enabling convenient assay implementation without external heating. Therefore, room temperature was selected as the optimal operational condition to ensure simplicity, reproducibility, and structural stability of the sensing interface.

Detection of CTC

Fig. 7a and b present the UV-vis spectroscopic evaluation of the peroxidase-like catalytic oxidation of TMB in the presence of increasing concentrations of CTC. As shown in Fig. 10a, the characteristic absorption band of ox-TMB at approximately 652 nm exhibited an initial intensity of about 0.15 a.u. in the absence of CTC. With the gradual increase in CTC concentration from 4 to 15 µM, the absorbance at 652 nm decreased progressively, clearly indicating the concentration-dependent inhibitory effect of the target analyte on the catalytic activity of the MIP@ZnO nanozyme system.

(a) UV-vis spectroscopic analysis of TMB with varying CTC concentrations. (b) The plot of absorbance versus CTC concentration.

Within the concentration range of 0–15 µM, the absorbance decline is nearly linear, indicating a strong correlation between CTC concentration and the spectroscopic response. The corresponding linear regression equation, Y = −0.00711[C] + 0.15589 (R2 = 0.97949), where [C] represents the molar concentration of CTC and Y represents the absorbance at 652 nm, demonstrates the high quantitative reliability of the proposed sensing method. Based on the slope of the calibration curve and the standard deviation of the blank measurements, LOD was determined to be 0.35 µM.

For quantitative analysis, the absorbance at 652 nm was selected as the analytical signal because this wavelength corresponds to the maximum absorption of ox-TMB. Although slight spectral broadening can occur during TMB oxidation, all measurements were performed under fixed reaction conditions, including pH, TMB concentration, H2O2 concentration, MIP@ZnO concentration, incubation time, and temperature. The blank spectrum was used as the reference, and calibration was established only within the concentration range where the 652 nm response decreased monotonically with increasing CTC concentration. Therefore, the possible influence of peak broadening was minimized by using a fixed-wavelength signal and identical reaction conditions for all calibration and sample measurements.

The comparison data for different methods of detecting CTC are presented in alternative approaches such as fluorescence-based sensor36 (LOD of 0.876 µM) and flow injection method37 (LOD of 20 µM). Despite its relatively high cost and simple synthesis, the MIP@ZnO method provides higher accuracy than several alternative methods listed in Table 1. Overall, this TMB/MIP nanozyme sensor achieves a high precision and selective antibiotic detection over a linear range Table 1. The MIP@ZnO nanozyme method, achieving a LOD of 0.35 µM, demonstrates superior selectivity and analytical accuracy compared with alternative sensing approaches such as gold nano bipyramids-based sensor (LOD of 20.9 µM38) and flow injection potentiometry method (LOD of 20 µM).37 Overall, the TMB/MIP nanozyme-based sensor enables highly precise and selective detection of CTC over a broad linear concentration range, exhibiting an R2 value approaching unity and a biologically relevant LOD. This platform represents a compelling example of the synergistic integration of molecular imprinting and neurozyme catalyst for rapid, on-site monitoring of antibiotic residue in complex matrices, including milk and tap water.

Paper-based devices for CTC detection

Fig. 8a illustrates the colorimetric response of the PADs at different concentrations of CTC. Over the concentration range of 0–40 µM, the RGB signal exhibited an approximately linear correlation with analyte concentration, with fitting coefficients exceeding 0.942. Among them, the blue channel provides the best calibration line, y = −0.842x + 137.36 with an R2 of 0.37813 (Fig. 8b), and was therefore selected for subsequent analysis. At concentrations beyond this range, all RGB signals increase markedly due to the lighter, nearly gray color of the oxTMB layer on the paper device.

The RGB response of the paper-based device allowed visual discrimination between different CTC concentration levels. However, the linearity of the raw RGB channels was not sufficient for rigorous quantitative analysis. Therefore, the paper-based format was used as a semi-quantitative screening platform, while UV-vis spectrophotometry was retained as the quantitative method.

Selectivity of analysis method

The selectivity of the developed MIP@ZnO colorimetric assay was systematically investigated to confirm its specific recognition capability toward CTC in the presence of structurally related antibiotics and common coexisting species. Since molecular imprinting is intended to generate binding cavities with high shape and functional complementarity to the target analyte, selectivity evaluation represents a critical step in validating the sensing mechanism.

As shown in Fig. 9a, the peroxidase-like catalytic oxidation of TMB exhibits the highest absorbance signal in the absence of antibiotics, indicating maximal catalytic activity of the nanozyme system. Upon introduction of different antibiotic molecules, varying degrees of catalytic inhibition were observed. Notably, CTC induces the most pronounced suppression of the absorbance at 652 nm, demonstrating the strongest inhibitory effect on the catalytic process. In contrast, other antibiotics such as amoxicillin, ciprofloxacin, tetracycline, doxycycline, and sulfamethoxazole produce only moderate or minor decreases in absorbance under identical conditions. Although tetracycline-class antibiotics share partial structural similarity with CTC, their weaker inhibition confirms that the imprinted cavities possess preferential affinity toward CTC, likely due to the specific steric arrangement and functional group interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonding and π–π stacking) formed during the imprinting process. The enhanced response arises from molecularly imprinted cavities formed through APTES/TEOS chemistry, which are geometrically and functionally complementary to the structure of CTC.40 In contrast, other antibiotics that lack this structural complementarity interact only weakly with the imprinted sites through nonspecific adsorption. These results verify that the response originates primarily from template-specific recognition, rather than nonspecific adsorption on the ZnO surface.44–46

(a) Absorbance at 652 nm of (TMB + H2O2 + MIP@ZnO) in the presence of different antibiotics. (b) Molecular structure of various antibiotics. Condition: 10 µM for each antibiotic.

Beyond antibiotic discrimination, the robustness of the assay in complex sample environments was further assessed using a series of common inorganic ions and organic compounds, including NaCl, NH4Cl, NH4H2PO4, CaCO3, Na2SO4, CaCl2, (NH4)2SO4, ascorbic acid, urea, and glucose, as shown in Fig. 9b. When these potential interferents were introduced individually (“interferent” group), only negligible changes in absorbance were observed compared with the blank, indicating that these coexisting substances do not significantly disrupt the nanozyme catalytic oxidation of TMB. More importantly, a competitive coexistence experiment was conducted by adding CTC together with each interferent (“CTC + interferent” group). In all cases, the absorbance remained strongly suppressed, closely resembling the inhibition behavior of CTC alone. This demonstrates that the assay retains high recognition fidelity toward CTC even in the presence of excess competing species. Such resistance to interference highlights the strong binding specificity of the molecularly imprinted cavities and confirms the excellent applicability of the proposed sensor for practical detection of CTC in real environmental or food matrices. Overall, the selectivity experiments confirm that the MIP@ZnO nanozyme exhibits preferential recognition and strong inhibitory response toward CTC, while showing minimal cross-reactivity with other antibiotics and high tolerance against common ions and organic interferents. The additional “CTC + interferent” coexistence results further validate the sensor's robustness and reliability for real-sample analysis.

In most previously reported assays for antibiotic detection in milk and tap water, extensive pretreatment steps such as protein precipitation, defatting, and filtration are required to remove interfering proteins and lipids prior to analysis.44,45 Although these procedures enhance analytical precision, they also remove the native matrix effects that commonly challenge on-site detection. In contrast, the present method relies solely on simple dilution without filtration or chemical precipitation, allowing residual proteins and fats to remain and thereby offering a more realistic assessment of matrix interference. Despite this minimal sample preparation, the MIP@ZnO paper-based device delivered high recovery rates and low RSD values, demonstrating strong resistance to matrix effects. This streamlined workflow significantly improves portability and suitability for field applications by reducing analysis time and reagent usage while retaining dependable analytical accuracy.

Stability, repeatability and reproducibility of MIP@ZnO

The repeatability and reproducibility of the assay were assessed using five parallel absorbance measurements obtained from two independently prepared material batches (Fig. 10a). For Batch 1, the RSD values were 3.59 and 3.53% at 0 and 10 µM, respectively, whereas Batch 2 exhibited RSD values of 2.92 and 3.23% under the same conditions. These low RSD values demonstrate good intra-batch repeatability. In addition, comparison of the two batches showed no statistically significant difference in absorbance response, with overall RSD values of 10.31 and 11.72% at 0 and 10 µM, respectively, thereby confirming satisfactory inter-batch reproducibility.

(a) Repeatability, reproducibility, and (b) stability of the MIP@ZnO.

The long-term stability of the MIP@ZnO sensor was further evaluated by monitoring its UV-vis absorbance response during the TMB oxidation reaction over a storage period of 28 days (Fig. 10b). Only negligible variation in absorbance was observed throughout the testing period, indicating stable analytical performance during storage. The RSD values at day 0 and day 28 were 2.92 and 2.34%, respectively, further demonstrating the excellent repeatability and storage stability of the developed sensor.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we report a molecularly imprinted ZnO nanozyme coupled with a PAD for the selective colorimetric detection of CTC. The surface-imprinted polymer shell successfully generated specific recognition sites while preserving the crystalline integrity and intrinsic catalytic activity of ZnO. The sensing strategy relies on a binding-induced inhibition mechanism, whereby the selective rebinding of CTC to the imprinted cavities attenuates the peroxidase-like oxidation of TMB, leading to a quantifiable decrease in absorbance at 652 nm.

The proposed MIP@ZnO sensor achieved a low detection limit of 0.35 µM with good linearity within 0–15 µM (R2 = 0.97949) and demonstrated strong selectivity against other antibiotics and common ions. Importantly, the integration into a capillary-driven PAD enabled rapid, equipment-free visual detection suitable for field applications. Real sample analysis in tap water and milk confirmed the practical applicability of the method with acceptable recoveries (93.92 to 106.6%) and reproducibility, even with minimal sample pretreatment.

Overall, this study highlights the synergistic combination of green-synthesized ZnO nanozymes, molecular imprinting technology, and paper-based microfluidics as an effective strategy for portable antibiotic monitoring. The developed platform offers significant potential for low-cost environmental surveillance and food safety screening, and it may be extended to other target analytes through appropriate template design.
