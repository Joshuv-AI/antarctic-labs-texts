Bridging Research and Practice: A Preliminary Detailed Analysis of Current Upper Limb Occupational Therapy Dose and Content in Publicly Funded Poststroke Rehabilitation
Authors: Camille E Proulx, Manouchka T Louis Jean, Johanne Higgins, Dany H Gagnon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo characterize the dose and content of poststroke upper limb usual care during inpatient occupational therapy (OT) sessions.DesignTwo-week longitudinal observational study.SettingPublicly funded inpatient rehabilitation center.ParticipantsEight poststroke inpatients (N=8) receiving OT. No intervention groups were assigned; all participants underwent observation of usual care sessions.InterventionsNo experimental intervention was applied. The OT sessions were filmed and analyzed.Main Outcome MeasuresSession dose (frequency, time in OT, time allocated to upper-limb rehabilitation, repetition intensity) and content (rehabilitation activity types, upper-limb movement types), analyzed using a video-synchronized observational data collection system.ResultsFrom 8 participants, a total of 42 sessions were videotaped, translating into 2250 minutes of OT session dose and content. On average, 28.6±16.1 minutes (53.3% of total session time) was dedicated specifically to upper-limb movements. During this time, participants performed a mean of 209.9±175.7 upper-limb movement repetitions per session, most of which involved reaching movements (mean=111.5±97.7). Additionally, most of the time spent on upper-limb movements (mean=15.8±13.1 minutes) focused on active activities.ConclusionsThis study offered valuable insights into the current dose and content of inpatient OT sessions for upper-limb rehabilitation in a publicly funded rehabilitation center. These findings can inform strategies to optimize stroke rehabilitation outcomes, with future research focusing on adapting them to real-world clinical settings.Keywords: Motor activity, Neurorehabilitation, Occupational therapy, Rehabilitation, Stroke rehabilitation, Task performance and analysis, Upper extremity, Upper limb

Stroke is a leading cause of long-term disability worldwide, significantly impacting survivors’ ability to perform daily activities and posing substantial challenges to independence and quality of life.1 Occupational therapy (OT) plays a pivotal role in poststroke rehabilitation, focusing, among other aspects, on restoring motor function. Two critical components of effective OT interventions are the dose and the content of therapy. “Dose” refers to the intensity, frequency, and duration, whereas “content” encompasses the specific activities and movements used to target motor recovery.

Despite the recognized importance of these components, research on the typical dose and content of OT interventions for upper-limb rehabilitation remains limited. Only 2 systematic reviews, encompassing a total of 10 and 8 studies, provide insights into rehabilitation intervention dose and content,2,3 with only 4 studies in each specifically examining OT interventions. These OT-focused studies revealed that participants allocated between 6 and 28.9 minutes specifically to upper-limb rehabilitation and performed between 23 and 60 hemiparetic upper-limb movement repetitions per session. However, these studies often lack detailed intervention descriptions, making it challenging to establish evidence-based guidelines for clinical practice. Variations in therapy dose and content across professionals and settings further complicate the establishment of precise clinical practice guidelines.

This study aimed to provide a comprehensive overview of current practices in a publicly funded rehabilitation center by systematically documenting the dose (ie, absolute and relative time, number of movement repetitions) and content (ie, rehabilitation activity types and upper-limb movement types) of OT interventions dedicated to upper-limb rehabilitation in stroke survivors undergoing intensive functional rehabilitation.

Methods

Study design and ethics

A longitudinal observational study was conducted to record usual OT sessions over 2 weeks of a nonprobabilistic sample of adults who had experienced a first stroke resulting in hand sensorimotor impairments and functional disabilities. During this period, participants’ usual OT sessions were videorecorded. Sessions were delivered in their usual form, as they would naturally occur, and were planned and individualized by the treating occupational therapist according to each patient’s goals and functional level, using available equipment. They were conducted in typical therapy settings and were not specifically upper-limb focused; any upper-limb activity occurred as part of routine therapy rather than a structured program. Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Board of the CIUSSS Centre-Sud-de-l’Île-de-Montréal (2023-1822).

Participant recruitment and clinical profiles

Participants were eligible if they sustained a first stroke resulting in hand disability (1≤Fugl-Meyer Assessment-Hand scores<14), were enrolled in an inpatient intensive neurorehabilitation program, and were fluent in French, English, or Spanish. Those unable to provide informed consent were excluded. All participants were recruited between September 15, 2023, and March 15, 2024.

Sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, ethnicity) and clinical characteristics (stroke type, time since stroke onset, dominance, most affected side, Fugl-Meyer Assessment-Upper-Limb score, Action Research Arm Test score) of participants were collected by an occupational therapist during initial evaluation (table 1).

Any additional practice outside OT hours was not recorded. A structured protocol, developed and validated by 2 occupational therapists, guided the extraction of data from the videos to document the dose and content of each OT session (see below for primary outcome descriptions). The final protocol was consistently applied to all sessions by a single trained assessor. Primary outcome measures

The primary outcome measures were the dose and content of OT sessions. Dose was characterized across 4 dimensions: frequency (number of sessions per week), time in therapy (total duration of rehabilitation sessions), time specifically allocated to upper-limb exercises or activities, and intensity (number of repetitions of a specific movement). Therapy content was categorized based on activity types (active, passive, functional, sensory) defined in previous literature.4 Movement types were further classified into reaching (involving proximal joints such as the shoulder, elbow), grasping (involving distal joints such as the wrist, fingers during power grasps), and pinching (involving distal joints during precision grasps). One movement repetition was defined as a full cycle from the starting position to the target action and back—for example, reaching to an object and returning, or pinching/grasping and releasing. For each participant, we summed the total number of recorded sessions, total therapy duration, total duration per activity type, and the number of repetitions per movement type (as well as overall repetitions) across all sessions. At the group level, descriptive statistics were computed using each session as the unit of analysis to ensure equal weighting of all sessions. Group level data are reported as mean±standard deviation per session and as median (interquartile range) to account for skewed distributions.

Results

All results are presented in table 2. A total of 8814 upper-limb movement repetitions were observed across 42 OT sessions, totaling 2250 recorded minutes. Of these upper-limb movements, 4682 were reaching (53.1%), 1715 were grasping (19.5%), and 2417 were pinching (27.4%). On average, each OT session specifically focused on upper limb for 28.6±16.1 minutes (53.3% of total session duration). In terms of activity types, on average, each session incorporated 15.8±13.1 minutes of active (55.3%), 2.4±5.5 minutes of passive (8.3%), 0.4±1.6 minutes of sensory (1.5%), and 10.0±15.0 minutes of functional activities (35.2%). In terms of movement types, on average, each session included 209.9±175.7 upper-limb movement repetitions, of which 111.5±97.7 were reaching, 40.8±44.5 were grasping, and 57.5±75.8 were pinching.

Discussion

The study investigated the dose and content of upper-limb care provided during usual OT sessions for adults poststroke in an inpatient rehabilitation setting at a publicly funded center. Findings indicate that the number of movement repetitions exceeded the previously reported maximum of 60 per OT session2,3 by more than threefold. This substantial increase in repetitions, achieved without extending session duration, suggests enhanced OT efficiency—potentially reflecting the integration of research-informed strategies into clinical practice. The emphasis on active and functional tasks, which generated the highest repetition counts, further supports this interpretation. These findings align with evidence-based rehabilitation principles advocating for high-dose, task-specific practice and reinforce current clinical guidelines.5 Although the total time allocated to upper-limb therapy remained within the upper range of previously reported values (eg, 6-28.9 minutes),2,3 the observed increase in dose intensity represents a meaningful shift toward more effective, high-intensity rehabilitation delivery. Importantly, participant characteristics in the OT-focused studies included in2,3—with mean ages ranging from 54.0 to 71.3 years, time since stroke spanning 5.6-49.8 days, and impairments ranging from mild to severe, are comparable with our cohort (mean age 57.0 years; time since stroke 1.57 months; mild to severe impairments). This similarity supports that the increase in dose intensity observed here reflects a real difference in therapy delivery rather than differences in sample profile.

Despite these gains, the intensity of upper-limb rehabilitation in clinical settings remains well below the optimal dose intensity identified in preclinical models. Animal studies suggest that inducing neuroplasticity and enhancing functional recovery early poststroke requires hundreds of repetitions per session (eg, >200 in rodents; >500 in monkeys).5,6 Translating these findings to human rehabilitation remains challenging, partly due to anatomical and physiological differences, inconsistent measurement methods between human and animal studies, and variations in therapy delivery.6,7

To address the latter, a promising strategy may involve adopting an upper-limb rehabilitation model similar to gait rehabilitation, where high-repetition training is prioritized and delivered as a distinct intervention,8 better reflecting animal study protocols. Birkenmeier et al9 exemplified this model by showing that delivering task-specific training as a distinct, high-dose intervention over 6 weeks allowed individuals with chronic stroke to achieve an average of 322 repetitions per session. However, implementing such intensity in routine care remains challenging due to OT's broad scope of interventions beyond motor function, including cognitive and sensorimotor functions, as well as inter- and intrapersonal skills—along with compensatory strategies and environmental adaptations. Separating high-repetition upper-limb training from general OT, much like the evolution of gait rehabilitation in physiotherapy, which is now often delivered as a structured, standalone intervention, could facilitate the integration of research-driven intensity targets while preserving OT’s holistic role in functional recovery. In practice, such high-repetition training could be delivered by interdisciplinary teams, but should consistently remain personally meaningful, goal-oriented, and task- or activity-focused, in line with rehabilitation principles.

Study limitations

Although 42 sessions (2250 minutes) of OT content were recorded, the small sample size and the heterogeneity of the clinical profiles—high variability in participants’ level of impairment—limit the generalizability of the findings. Moreover, conducted in a publicly funded rehabilitation center, this study may not reflect the broader stroke population, and therapy practices could differ in other settings, particularly private institutions. Although we described the observed increase in dose intensity as a more effective way to deliver rehabilitation, this statement refers solely to the delivery process and does not imply that it leads to superior patient outcomes. The effect of this higher intensity delivery on functional recovery was not assessed in the present study.

Conclusions

This study provided a novel multidimensional analysis of OT dose and content, reflecting progress in aligning OT with evidence-based stroke rehabilitation. However, it also revealed a persistent gap between current practice and the higher intensity levels demonstrated to be effective in preclinical models. Future research should focus on developing scalable strategies to optimize upper-limb rehabilitation in real-world clinical settings. Adopting activity-based high-repetition models, similar to those used in gait training, may be a promising direction to further explore.
