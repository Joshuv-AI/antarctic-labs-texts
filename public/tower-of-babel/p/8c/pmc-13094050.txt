Cloning and functional analysis of the HaNAC11 transcription factor in Haloxylon ammodendron
Authors: Neng-Shuang Shen, Yi-Di Zhan, Cong Cheng, Jian-Shun Liang, Jing-Jing Ma, Yan-Ping Ren, Li Ma, Hua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe perennial desert plant Haloxylon ammodendron (H. ammodendron) faces the critical challenge of sustaining the growth and development of reproductive branches during hot and arid summers, which is essential for its seed set. Previous transcriptome analysis revealed higher expression of the NAC transcription factor gene HaNAC11 in reproductive branches compared to vegetative branches, suggesting its potential role in regulating the growth and development of reproductive branches under environmental stress.ResultsWe found that HaNAC11 exhibited higher expression in flowers and reproductive branches and responded significantly to various stressors, such as drought, high salt, temperature extremes, as well as to treatments with different plant hormones including abscisic acid (ABA), salicylic acid (SA) and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA). Subsequent characterization confirmed that HaNAC11 encodes a nuclear-localized NAC transcription factor with homodimer formation capability and transcriptional activation activity. Heterologous expression of HaNAC11 in Arabidopsis enhanced plant adaptation to high-temperature and wind stress. Further transcriptome and KEGG pathway analyses revealed that under normal conditions, glycerolipid metabolism was upregulated in HaNAC11-transgenic Arabidopsis plants. Under high-temperature stress, pathways related to glycerolipid metabolism, particularly the biosynthesis of cutin, wax, suberin, and fatty acids, were further upregulated in the transgenic plants. Consistently, triacylglycerol, the end product of glycerolipid metabolism, accumulated significantly in transgenic Arabidopsis. Substantial triacylglycerol accumulation was also observed in the reproductive branches of H. ammodendron under desert conditions. Conversely, virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) of HaNAC11 significantly reduced triacylglycerol accumulation in H. ammodendron reproductive branches.ConclusionHeterologous expression of HaNAC11 may enhance thermotolerance and promote reproductive growth in Arabidopsis by regulating glycerolipid metabolism; in its native species H. ammodendron, HaNAC11 is likely to regulate triacylglycerol synthesis through a similar mechanism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08567-x.

Background

H. ammodendron [Haloxylon ammodendron (C.A. Mey.) Bunge ex Fenzl], a super xerophytic perennial shrub tree belonging to the family Amaranthaceae, is widely distributed across desert regions of Eurasia and serves as a crucial foundation species in arid zones [1–3]. H. ammodendron exhibits remarkable tolerance to high temperature, drought, wind erosion, salinity, and nutrient-poor conditions, making it an ideal model for studying stress resistance mechanisms in desert plants. To adapt to these harsh environments, H. ammodendron has evolved special scale-like leaves, with photosynthesis primarily undertaken by its assimilating branches [4, 5]. These branches are covered with a thick cuticle and wax layer, which help to mitigate water loss through transpiration and protect against intense wind erosion under low-precipitation and high-evaporation conditions [6, 7].

In addition to these morphological adaptations, H. ammodendron has evolved unique reproductive strategies to cope with extreme summer drought and heat. In northern Xinjiang, flowering and fertilization occur in late April or early May, followed by slow embryonic development throughout June to August, with no noticeable expansion until September [8, 9]. During the developmental period, the assimilating branches differentiate into two types: vegetative branches which are responsible for photosynthesis and carbohydrate supply, and reproductive branches, which support seed development. However, under persistent high temperature and drought during these summer months, reproductive branches are prone to desiccation and abscission [10]. Therefore, maintaining the growth and physiological activity of reproductive branches is crucial for successful seed set in H. ammodendron.

In recent years, continuously updated high-quality genomic resources for H. ammodendron have laid a solid foundation for in-depth analysis of its molecular stress resistance mechanisms. In 2022, the first chromosome-level genome assembly was reported, with a size of 685.4 Mb and high contiguity (contig N50 = 23.6 Mb), where 99.6% of the sequences were anchored onto nine chromosomes [11]. With advancements in sequencing technology, another chromosome-level assembly was released in 2025. This assembly, with a size of 2.32 Gb, contains 69,844 annotated protein-coding genes [12], achieved through higher-coverage sequencing and providing a more comprehensive gene set for functional genomic studies in H. ammodendron. These two versions together reveal diverse aspects of the H. ammodendron genome.

NAC transcription factors are well-known regulators of plant stress adaptation and developmental processes [13–15]. In Arabidopsis, ATAF1 (a NAC transcription factor involved in stress response) and ANAC055 have been identified as core regulators not only for basal thermotolerance but also for the establishment of heat stress memory [16]. Concurrently, AtNAC019 interacts with the protein phosphatase RCF2 and binds directly to the promoters of heat shock factor genes HSFA1b and HSFA6b, to activate their expression, thereby enhancing thermal resilience [17]. Similarly, heterologous expression of maize ZmNAC074 in Arabidopsis activates the expression of key heat-responsive genes, including AtHSFs and AtHSPs, leading to improved heat stress tolerance [18]. NAC-mediated drought resilience has also been demonstrated in multiple species. Overexpression of OsNAC6 in rice promotes root thickening and increases crown root number, thus enhancing drought resistance [19]. In rose, RcNAC091 transcriptionally activates RcWRKY71 via an ABA-dependent pathway to improve drought tolerance [20]. Salt stress responses are also modulated by NAC transcription factors: In soybean, GmNAC181 enhances both nodulation and salt tolerance [21], while wheat TaNAC29 improves salinity resistance when expressed in Arabidopsis [22]. Several NAC genes from the halophyte Suaeda liaotungensis-including SlNAC4, 8, 10-also significantly enhance drought and salt tolerance in Arabidopsis [23–25]. And in birch plants, BpNAC012 not only increases salt tolerance but also promotes secondary cell wall deposition in stem tissues [26]. Furthermore, the root cap-specific NAC factor SMB modulates auxin distribution to fine-tune root salt-avoidance behavior in Arabidopsis [27]. In addition, NAC transcription factors participate in the regulation of organ senescence and developmental timing. For instance, ANAC092 forms homodimers that bind to senescence-associated gene promoters and accelerate leaf senescence in Arabidopsis [28]. Heterologous expression of peanut AhNAC2 in Arabidopsis delays flowering while improving salinity and drought tolerance [29]. In rice, OsNAC2 represses flowering by modulating gibberellic acid pathways [30], whereas PnNAC2 from Panax notoginseng promotes flowering via direct binding to the FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT) promoter in Arabidopsis [31].

Using homology alignment of the aforementioned genomic data combined with previous transcriptome analysis, we identified a NAC transcription factor, designated HaNAC11 (PV451625.1), which exhibited higher expression in reproductive branches than in vegetative branches. This suggests that HaNAC11 may play an important role in mediating stress adaptation and maintaining reproductive development under harsh desert conditions.

Given the absence of a stable genetic transformation system for H. ammodendron, heterologous expression in model plants has been widely employed to characterize stress-responsive genes from this species. For instance, overexpression of HaFT-1, a 14-3-3 protein, in Arabidopsis enhances thermotolerance by regulating proline and chlorophyll metabolism [32], while overexpression of HaHSFA1 improves salt stress tolerance in transgenic Arabidopsis [33]. Similarly, heterologous expression of HaNAC3 in tobacco significantly enhances tolerance to multiple abiotic stresses, including drought, heat, and salt, potentially through modulating phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and glutathione metabolism pathways [34]. Recently, heterologous expression of HaNAC12 was shown to enhance drought resistance and promote reproductive growth under water deficit conditions in Arabidopsis by regulating fatty acid metabolism [35].

In this study, we first conducted a functional analysis of HaNAC11 through heterologous expression in Arabidopsis, which indicated its potential involvement in glycerolipid metabolism. To validate these findings, we further silenced HaNAC11 expression in its native species, H. ammodendron, using VIGS. This work provides initial insights into the potential role of HaNAC11 in the adaptation of H. ammodendron to desert environments, offering a theoretical framework for investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying the ecological adaptation of desert plants.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. and Nicotiana benthamiana Domin : Seeds are preserved by the Xinjiang Key Laboratory for Ecological Adaptation and Evolution of Extreme Environment Organisms, with Hua Zhang as the curator.

Arabidopsis was grown in a growth chamber under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod, with a light intensity of 6000 lx, 60–70% relative humidity, and a temperature of 22℃. For seed sterilization, seeds were immersed in 75% ethanol for 5 min, followed by 5% sodium hypochlorite for 10 min, and then rinsed ten times with sterile distilled water. After stratification at 4℃ for 2 days, the seeds were evenly sown on solid Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium. After 5 days of culture, the seedlings were transplanted into a mixed soil substrate consisting of peat, vermiculite, and perlite (4:2:1 ratio).

Nicotiana benthamiana plants were cultivated in a growth chamber under a 16-h light/8-h dark cycle at 8000 lx, 60–70% relative humidity, and 25℃. For seed sterilization, seeds were immersed in 10% sodium hypochlorite for 4 min, then rinsed five times with sterile distilled water. The sterilized seeds were sown directly onto a mixed soil substrate of peat, vermiculite, and perlite (1:1:1 ratio). Seedlings were transplanted when they had developed four true leaves.

Haloxylon ammodendron (C.A. Mey.) Bunge ex Fenzl: Three wild H. ammodendron plants were selected at the southern edge of the Gurbantünggüt Desert in Xinjiang. Flowers were collected in May, reproductive and vegetative branches were collected from June to September at 30-day intervals, and seeds were collected in October.

H. ammodendron plants were grown in a growth chamber under the following conditions: 16-h photoperiod, light intensity of 6000 lx, relative humidity of 60–70%, and temperature of 26℃. For seed sterilization, seeds were first immersed in 75% ethanol for 30 s with vortexing, then filtered and rinsed three times with sterile distilled water. Subsequently, the seeds were soaked in a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of sodium hypochlorite (available chlorine 5%) and sterile water for 10 min with occasional agitation, then filtered. After soaking in sterile distilled water for 2 h, the seed coat was removed. Finally, the cleaned seeds were inoculated onto solid MS medium.

HaNAC11 cloning

Based on the published genome data of H. ammodendron [11, 12], the hidden Markov model (HMM) profile of the NAC transcription factor family was downloaded from the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/). A genome-wide homology search against the protein sequences of H. ammodendron was then performed using the hmmsearch program on the HMMER web server (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/hmmer/home), with an E-value threshold of < 1e-5 for preliminary candidate screening. Among the candidates, HaNAC11 (PV451625.1) was selected for further functional characterization based on its differential expression between reproductive and vegetative branches, as revealed by previous transcriptomic data.

Total RNA was extracted from the assimilating branches harvested from 21-day-old H. ammodendron seedlings using an RNA extraction kit (Magen Biotech, Guangzhou, China, Cat#. R4150-03). Subsequently, this RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China, Cat# AE311-02). To ensure cDNA integrity, an Anchored Oligo(dT)18 Primer was used for the reverse transcription reaction. Specific cloning primers (Supplementary Table S1) were designed based on the HaNAC11 coding sequence (CDS). Using cDNA as the template, the HaNAC11 CDS was amplified and subsequently ligated into the pEASY-Blunt Zero cloning vector (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China, Cat# CB501-01).

Bioinformatics analysis

The conserved domains and protein structure of HaNAC11 were analyzed using the SMART database (https://smart.embl.de/) and AlphaFold Server (based on the AlphaFold 3 model) (https://www.alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/). To determine the phylogenetic relationship of HaNAC11, the amino acid sequence encoded by HaNAC11 was subjected to BLASTP analysis via NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). NAC transcription factor amino acid sequences with high similarity were selected, and a phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbor-joining method in MEGA7.0 software (with 1000 bootstrap replicates). Multiple sequence alignment was performed using ESPript 3.0 (https://espript.ibcp.fr/ESPript/ESPript/). Amino acid homology was compared using DNAstar 11.0.

Expression pattern analysis of HaNAC11

Seeds, roots, and assimilating branches of H. ammodendron seedlings, as well as flowers collected in May and vegetative and reproductive branches collected from June to September, were sampled, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80℃.

21-day-old H. ammodendron seedlings grown in the laboratory were selected for uniformity in size and growth status and subjected to various abiotic stress and hormone treatments. Drought and High-Salt Stress: Seedling roots were immersed in 20% PEG6000 solution and 200 mmol/L NaCl solution, respectively. The containers were sealed with plastic film, and seedlings were maintained in a growth chamber at 26℃. Roots and assimilating branches were sampled at 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h post-treatment. For each time point, three biological replicates were collected, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80℃. Low-Temperature and High-Temperature Stress: Seedlings were transferred to artificial climate chambers set at 4℃ and 50℃ for sampling. Hormone Treatments: Seedling roots were immersed in 20 µmol/L IAA, 100 µmol/L ABA, 50 mg/L SA, and 100 mg/L methyl jasmonate (MeJA) solutions, respectively. Containers were sealed with plastic film, and seedlings were kept in a 26℃ growth chamber for sampling.

Total RNA was extracted from H. ammodendron samples. For cDNA synthesis, random primers were used instead of the anchored Oligo(dT)18 primer employed for gene cloning, in order to achieve more accurate gene expression analysis. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) primers for HaNAC11 (Supplementary Table S1) were designed using Primer 5.0. HaACT1 (KM886609) from H. ammodendron, identified as one of the most stable internal reference genes transcribed by RNA polymerase II, was used in this study, with its qRT-PCR primers listed in Supplementary Table S1 [36]. qRT-PCR was performed to analyze HaNAC11 expression in different tissues, during seed development, and under various stress and hormone treatments using ChamQ Universal SYBR qRT-PCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China, Cat# R711-02). The 20 µL reaction mixture contained 1 µL cDNA, 0.4 µL forward primer (10 µM), 0.4 µL reverse primer (10 µM), 10 µL 2 × ChamQ Universal SYBR qRT-PCR Master Mix, and 8.2 µL sterile nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling protocol consists of initial denaturation at 95℃ for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95℃ for 10 s and 60℃ for 30 s. Relative expression levels of HaNAC11 were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [37].

Subcellular localization and transcription-activation activity analysis

The pCAMBIA1300-35 S-EGFP vector [38] was linearized with BamH I and Sal I. The HaNAC11 CDS (without its stop codon) was then amplified using primers harboring vector homologous arms (Supplementary Table S1) and the pEASY-Blunt Zero-HaNAC11 plasmid as a template. The fusion expression construct pCAMBIA1300-35 S-HaNAC11-EGFP was assembled using the ClonExpress® II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China, Cat.# C112-01). This construct was subsequently introduced into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101. For transient expression assays, Nicotiana benthamiana leaves were infiltrated with the transformed agrobacteria. In these assays, pCAMBIA1300-35 S-EGFP (GV3101) served as a positive control, and pCAMBIA1300-Histone-mCherry (GV3101) [39] was used as a nuclear marker. Briefly, agrobacteria carrying the target plasmid were grown to an OD₆₀₀ = 0.8, harvested by centrifugation at 5,000 × g for 5 min, and resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl₂, 10 mM MES, 100 µM acetosyringone) to an OD₆₀₀ = 0.8. After incubation in the dark for 3 h, the bacterial suspensions were infiltrated into tobacco leaves. The infiltrated plants were kept in the dark for 1 day and then transferred to normal growth conditions for 2 days prior to observation. Leaf samples were imaged with a super-resolution laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM 850, Zeiss, Germany).

The full-length HaNAC11 CDS, along with its N-terminal region (containing the NAM conserved domain; 1–492 bp) and C-terminal region (493–984 bp), was cloned into the pGBKT7 vector [40] using a strategy analogous to that for the subcellular localization construct. The resulting recombinant plasmids (pGBKT7-HaNAC11/N-terminal/C-terminal) were individually transformed into yeast competent cells AH109 (Coolaber, Beijing, China, Cat.# CC300-10). Following transformation, 10 µL of denatured Carrier DNA (10 µg/µL) and 500 µL of PEG/LiAc solution were added sequentially. The cell mixture was subjected to a 30-min incubation in a 30℃ water bath, followed by a 15-min heat shock at 42℃. After centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 400 µL of 0.9% (w/v) saline. The suspension was spread onto SD/-Trp plates and incubated at 30℃ for 3–5 days. Single colonies were picked, suspended in saline, and serially diluted to OD₆₀₀ values of 0.2, 0.02, 0.002, and 0.0002. For each dilution, 5 µL was spotted onto both SD/-Trp plates and SD/-Ade/-His/-Trp plates supplemented with X-α-Gal. Transformants harboring pGBKT7-AtNAC2 (AH109) [41] and the empty pGBKT7 vector (AH109) were used as positive and negative controls, respectively.

BiFC and GST pull-down assays for detection of homodimer formation

To investigate HaNAC11 homodimer formation in vivo, we performed a bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay. The CDS of HaNAC11 was individually cloned into the pSPYCE and pSPYNE vectors [42] to generate the pSPYNE-HaNAC11 and pSPYCE-HaNAC11 constructs, respectively. These constructs were transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101. The resulting bacterial strains were co-infiltrated into leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana for transient expression, following the procedure used for subcellular localization analysis. Three negative control combinations were included: pSPYNE-empty + pSPYCE-empty, pSPYNE-HaNAC11 + pSPYCE-empty, and pSPYNE-empty + pSPYCE-HaNAC11. A known interacting pair, pSPYNE-APT1 + pSPYCE-CDPK, served as the positive control. Leaf nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a super-resolution laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM 850, Zeiss, Germany).

For the Glutathione-s-transferase (GST) Pull-Down assay to examine interactions in vitro, the HaNAC11 CDS was cloned into the pGEX-4T-1 and pET28a vectors [43], respectively. The recombinant plasmids pGEX-4T-1-HaNAC11 and pET28a-HaNAC11 were transformed into E. coli Arctic Express™. For large-scale culture, the bacterial suspension was inoculated at a 1:100 ratio into 30 mL of LB liquid medium containing the appropriate antibiotics and incubated at 37℃ 220 rpm. When the OD₆₀₀ reached 0.7, IPTG was added to a final concentration of 0.5 mM to induce fusion protein expression, and the culture was further incubated overnight at 16℃ 200 rpm. Purification of HaNAC11 fusion proteins: Bacterial cells expressing HaNAC11-GST or HaNAC11-His were lysed by sonication. The lysate was centrifuged at 4,000 × g for 10 min at 4℃, and the supernatant was collected and loaded at 0.5 mL/min onto an affinity chromatography column (GST column or Ni-IDA Sepharose CL-6B) pre-equilibrated with the corresponding binding buffer (GST Binding Buffer or Ni-IDA Binding Buffer). After loading, the column was washed with the same buffer at 0.5 mL/min until the absorbance of the effluent at 280 nm stabilized at baseline. The target protein was eluted at 1 mL/min with a specific elution buffer: GST Elution Buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 50 mM reduced glutathione, 0.15 M NaCl, pH 8.0) for HaNAC11-GST. For HaNAC11-His, the column was first washed with Ni-IDA Washing Buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 20 mM imidazole, 0.15 M NaCl, pH 8.0) to baseline, then eluted with Ni-IDA Elution Buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 250 mM imidazole, 0.15 M NaCl, pH 8.0). Protein fractions corresponding to the elution peaks were collected, dialyzed against PBS buffer, and the protein concentrations were determined.

To perform the GST pull-down assay, purified HaNAC11-GST and HaNAC11-His proteins were mixed at an equal mass ratio and incubated at 4℃ for 4 h. The mixture was applied to a Glutathione Sepharose 4B gravity flow column. The column was washed, and bound proteins were eluted with 10 mM glutathione solution. As a control, purified HaNAC11-His was incubated with GST protein alone under identical conditions. Eluates from the pull-down assay and input controls were denatured at 95℃ and separated on a 15% SDS-polyacrylamide gel. Electrophoresis was initiated at 100 V until the dye front entered the separating gel and then continued at 130 V until completion. Proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane by wet transfer at a constant current of 300 mA for 1 h. Following transfer, the membrane was washed with PBST (PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20), blocked with 5% (w/v) skimmed milk in PBST at 37℃ for 1 h, and then incubated with a specific primary antibody at 4℃ overnight. After washing with PBST, the membrane was incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at 37℃ for 1 h. Following a final wash, signals were developed using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate and captured with a chemiluminescence imaging system. Protein-protein interactions were evaluated by comparing the abundance of the target protein in the input and elution fractions. (Antibody sources, dilution ratios, and dilution buffers are provided in Supplementary Table S2.)

Biomass assay under normal growth conditions

The HaNAC11 CDS was cloned into a modified plant expression vector pCAMBIA3301-35 S (with GUS reporter removed) [35]. Arabidopsis was transformed via the floral dip method [44] using A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 harboring the recombinant construct. Primary inflorescences of 5-6-week-old wild-type (WT) plants at early flowering were used. Siliques were removed prior to infiltration. An Agrobacterium culture carrying the construct was grown to an OD600 of 1.0-1.5, harvested by centrifugation (5,000 × g, 5 min), and resuspended in infiltration medium (5% sucrose, 0.02% Silwet L-77, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM MES, pH 5.7) to a final OD600 of 0.8-1.0. The aerial parts of plants were dipped in this suspension for 1 min, then covered with plastic wrap to maintain humidity. Plants were kept in darkness for 24 h before being returned to standard growth conditions for seed set. Putative transgenic lines were screened by qRT-PCR using primers listed in Supplementary Table S1. AtACT2 (AT3G18780), a stable reference gene in Arabidopsis [45], was used for normalization. Three independent, homozygous T₃ lines with high HaNAC11 transcript levels were selected and designated HaNAC11-OE1, 2, 3. Rosette leaf development (leaf number and area) was analyzed in 3-week-old WT, HaNAC11-OE1, 2, 3 lines. Plants were subsequently grown to 7 weeks to assess post-bolting growth traits.

Mild heat stress at 27℃ and combined high-temperature and wind stress at 42℃

Five-week-old, uniformly grown WT and HaNAC11-OE1, 2, 3 plants were selected for heat stress treatments. For mild heat stress at 27℃, experimental groups were transferred to an artificial climate chamber maintained at 27℃. The initial treatment day was designated as day 0, with plant growth documented photographically at 3-day intervals. Control groups were maintained under standard growth conditions at 22℃ with a regular photoperiod.

Treatment with combined high-temperature and wind stress at 42℃ was performed as follows. Plants were subjected to stress in a controlled-environment chamber set to 42℃. Internal fans were operated to generate forced convection, rapidly and uniformly lowering the humidity to simulate natural “dry, hot wind” conditions. This setup exacerbated transpirational water loss, inducing rapid dehydration and wilting in Arabidopsis leaves. Rosette leaf phenotypes were documented after 3 h and 4 h of stress exposure. Plant recovery was assessed at 12 days and 24 days after the stress treatment. Three biological replicates were included for each plant line and time point.

Determination of malondialdehyde and proline contents

Arabidopsis leaves from control and experimental groups were collected following two stress treatments: mild high-temperature stress at 27 ℃ for 6 days, and combined high-temperature and wind stress at 42℃ for 3 h. Malondialdehyde (MDA) content was determined using a Malondialdehyde Assay Kit (Beijing Boxbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China, Cat.# AKFA013M). The results are expressed in nmol g⁻¹ FW, which denotes nanomoles of MDA per gram of fresh weight of plant tissue. Proline content was measured using a Proline Assay Kit (Beijing Boxbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China, Cat.# AKAM003M) and is reported as µg g⁻¹ FW, indicating micrograms of proline per gram of fresh weight.

Transcriptome sequencing analysis

Transcriptome sequencing (PRJCA051945) was performed separately for each of the three HaNAC11 overexpressing lines (HaNAC11-OE1, 2, 3) and WT plants following the heat stress treatments described above. Aerial tissues were collected from WT and transgenic Arabidopsis plants under three conditions: (1) five-week-old plants prior to treatment (control), (2) after 6 days of mild heat stress at 27℃, and (3) after 3 h of combined high-temperature and wind stress at 42℃. For each line and condition, three individual plants were pooled to form one biological replicate. All samples were submitted to Biomarker Technologies Corporation for transcriptome sequencing. Total RNA was extracted from quality-approved samples and used to construct sequencing libraries. After passing stringent quality control, the libraries were sequenced on a next-generation high-throughput platform. The resulting data showed a Q30 base percentage above 97.72% and alignment efficiency to the reference genome ranging from 97.5% to 99.07%, confirming high sequencing quality suitable for downstream analysis. Detailed quality metrics are provided in Supplementary Table S3. Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 [46], with significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) defined by |Fold Change| ≥ 2 and an FDR < 0.01. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of these DEGs was subsequently performed using the Kanehisa database (https://www.kanehisa.jp/).

qRT-PCR analysis of genes related to metabolic pathways

Based on transcriptome sequencing data, 22 genes involved in glycerolipid metabolism and the synthesis of cutin, wax, and suberin in Arabidopsis were selected for qRT-PCR analysis (primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S1). These genes include: glycerolipid metabolism (GPAT2, GPAT3, GLIP5, ATLT1, GDSL, ATPAP2, LPAT5, MAGL4, DGK1) [47–51]; cutin synthesis (CYP86A4, CYP86A7, CYP704B1, CYP77B1, CYP77A6) [47, 52, 53]; wax synthesis (CER1-L1, CER1, WSD3, WSD1, WSD11, CYP96A) [47, 52, 54, 55]; and suberin synthesis (FAR2, FAR3) [47, 56]. The corresponding Gene IDs and locus identifiers are listed in Supplementary Table S4.

VIGS in H. ammodendron

A 300-bp fragment corresponding to the non-conserved C-terminal region (651–950 bp) of the HaNAC11 CDS was cloned into the pTRV2 vector [57].The recombinant plasmid was then introduced into A. tumefaciens GV3101. The transformed bacteria were mixed with those carrying the pTRV1 vector [57] and resuspended in an infiltration buffer containing MS liquid medium, 10 mmol/L MgCl₂, 10 mmol/L MES, and 150 µmol/L Acetosyringone. The resulting suspension was infiltrated into tobacco leaves, followed by a 7-day cultivation period to allow for Tobacco Rattle Virus (TRV) amplification. Three experimental groups were established: TRV2:HaNAC11 served as the experimental group, TRV2:HaCLA (targeting a key chloroplast development gene in H. ammodendron, whose silencing induces a visible albino phenotype, XXJ43028.1) functioned as the positive control [58], and TRV2:00 was included as the negative control. For virus extraction, 1 g of infected tobacco leaves was homogenized in 1 mL of 0.2 mol/L sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.2), and the resulting homogenate was filtered to obtain a crude viral extract. This extract was diluted 1:40 with sterile water to prepare the final inoculation solution. Roots of 21-day-old H. ammodendron seedlings were completely immersed in the viral infection solution to allow viral uptake without wounding. The seedlings were co-cultivated in darkness for 2 days at 26℃ and subsequently transferred to MS medium for continued growth under standard conditions. Successful VIGS induction was confirmed by the appearance of the albino phenotype in the TRV2:HaCLA-treated plants, and the silencing efficiency of HaNAC11 was validated by qRT-PCR.

Determination of triacylglycerol content

Triacylglycerol levels were measured in the following plant materials: (1) leaves from control and treated Arabidopsis plants exposed to either mild heat stress (27℃ for 6 days) or combined high-temperature and wind stress (42℃ for 3 h); (2) vegetative and reproductive branches of H. ammodendron collected in June, July, August, and September; and (3) above-ground assimilating branches of H. ammodendron plants after VIGS. Triacylglycerol content was quantified using a commercial assay kit (Beijing Boxbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.; Cat.# AKFA003M) based on the acetylacetone method, with absorbance measured at 420 nm. Sample protein concentration was determined by the Bradford method for normalization. Results are expressed as mg of triacylglycerol per mg of total protein (mg mg− 1 protein).

qRT-PCR analysis of genes involved in glycerolipid metabolism in H. ammodendron

To investigate glycerolipid metabolism in H. ammodendron, homologous genes were identified using the amino acid sequences of key Arabidopsis glycerolipid metabolic genes (GPAT2, ATLT1, ATPAP2, LPAT5, MAGL4, DGK1) as queries in a BLASTP search (E‑value cutoff < 0.001). The identified orthologs were designated HaGPAT2, HaATLT1, HaATPAP2, HaLPAT5, HaMAGL4, and HaDGK1. Their corresponding original gene IDs, homology details, and sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S5. Expression levels of these genes were then analyzed by qRT‑PCR in H. ammodendron plants following VIGS‑mediated silencing of HaNAC11. The primer sequences used are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analysis of the data

The data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent biological replicates, with the sample size (n) specified in the figure legends. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Cloning of HaNAC11 of H. ammodendron

The HaNAC11 (PV451625.1) CDS was successfully amplified from cDNA derived from assimilating branches of H. ammodendron seedlings. The HaNAC11 CDS is 984 nucleotides in length and encodes a 327-amino acid protein (Fig. 1A). Conserved domain analysis showed that the HaNAC11 protein contains a typical NAM domain at its N-terminus (amino acids 19–143) (Fig. 1B, C). Homologous sequences were first identified using NCBI BLAST, and subsequent multiple sequence alignment performed with ESPript 3.0 indicated that the N-terminal NAM domain is well conserved among different NAC proteins (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Phylogenetic analysis based on the full-length amino acid sequences, conducted with MEGA7.0, demonstrated that HaNAC11 forms a distinct clade with NAC transcription factors from Amaranthaceae species, while showing more distant relationships with NAC factors from other plants (Fig. 1D). It shares the highest similarity (67.9%) with a NAC transcription factor from Chenopodium quinoa (XP_021770843.1); the similarity to an Arabidopsis NAC transcription factor (AT5G61430.2) was about 48.2% (Supplementary Fig. S1B).

To investigate the expression patterns of HaNAC11 in different tissues and developmental stages, we collected samples from H. ammodendron including seeds, seedling roots, assimilating branches, flowers of (in May), as well as reproductive and vegetative branches from June to September (Fig. 2A, B). qRT-PCR analysis revealed that HaNAC11 was expressed in all examined tissues, with the lowest expression in seeds and the highest expression in flowers (Fig. 2C). During seed development from June to September, the assimilating branches differentiate into reproductive branches (primarily responsible for seed development) and vegetative branches (mainly providing energy sources). Notably, HaNAC11 expression in reproductive branches was significantly higher than that in vegetative branches during July, August, and September. Its expression peaked in the reproductive branches in August and September, with comparable expression levels observed in these two months (Fig. 2D).

Furthermore, HaNAC11 responded to various abiotic stresses-including drought, high salt, low and high temperature in both assimilating branches and roots, though with distinct expression patterns between these tissues. Under drought stress simulated by 20% PEG, its expression in both tissues showed upward trends, peaking at 24 h. Under high salt stress (200 mmol/L NaCl), expression in assimilating branches increased initially, peaking at 24 h, and then declined at 48 h. In roots, however, it first decreased at 12 h before rising to a peak at 24 h. Under low temperature (4℃), overall expression was downregulated in both tissues, reaching the lowest point at 6 h in assimilating branches and at 1 h in roots. Under high temperature (50℃), expression in assimilating branches increased and then decreased with the highest level at 1 h, whereas in roots it exhibited a sustained upregulation, peaking at 48 h (the final time point examined) (Fig. 2E).

HaNAC11 also responded to the treatment with various hormones, such as IAA, ABA, SA, MeJA. Following 20 µmol/L IAA treatment, HaNAC11 expression was continuously increased in both tissues, peaking at 1 h in assimilating branches and at 48 h in roots after treatment. Under 100 µmol/L ABA treatment, HaNAC11 expression also showed a continuous upregulation in both tissues, respectively reaching their maxima level at 3 h in assimilating branches and 12 h in roots after treatment. With 100 mg/L SA treatment, in assimilating branches HaNAC11 expression was slightly upregulated, while in roots it showed a more pronounced response with higher expression levels at 6 h and 24 h after treatment. However, under 100 mg/L MeJA treatment, HaNAC11 expression was not obviously changed in both assimilating branches and roots (Fig. 2E).

HaNAC11 forms homodimers and functions as a nuclear-localized transcription factor with transcription-activation activity

To determine the subcellular localization of HaNAC11, HaNAC11 tagged with EGFP (enhanced green fluorescence protein) was constructed under the 35 S promoter in expression vector of pCAMBIA1300-35 S-EGFP. The generated 35 S::HaNAC11::EGFP vector was transformed into Agrobacterium and then transiently expressed in the leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana after infiltration, while the 35 S::EGFP empty vector served as a positive control and 35 S::Histone::mCherry was used as a nucleus-localized marker (Fig. 3A). Confocal microscope analyses showed that the 35 S::HaNAC11::EGFP fusion protein was exclusively localized to the nucleus, with its fluorescence signal overlapping perfectly with the nuclear marker, whereas fluorescence signals from the control 35 S::EGFP were distributed throughout the entire cell (Fig. 3A). These results demonstrated that transcription factor HaNAC11 was truly localized in nuclei.

NAC transcription factors have been demonstrated to function as dimers in species such as Arabidopsis and rice [59]. We therefore employed BiFC and GST pull-down assays to verify whether HaNAC11 can form homodimers both in vivo and in vitro. For the BiFC assay, 35 S::HaNAC11-nYFP and 35 S::HaNAC11-cYFP fusion constructs were generated and co-expressed in tobacco leaves. Yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) fluorescence was detected and showed specific co-localization with the nuclear marker DAPI. While fluorescence was observed in the positive control, no signal was detected in any of the three negative controls (Fig. 3B). This indicates that HaNAC11 can interact with itself in the nucleus of tobacco cells. For GST pull-down assays, HaNAC11-His and HaNAC11-GST fusion vectors were constructed for prokaryotic expression in E. coli and subsequent protein purification. The purified HaNAC11-GST and HaNAC11-His fusion proteins were incubated together at 4℃ for interaction analyses. Protein interactions were analyzed by affinity purification using Glutathione Beads 4FF gravity columns, followed by elution with 10 mmol/L glutathione solution. Western blot of the eluted fractions showed that both HaNAC11-GST and HaNAC11-His were present in the test sample, whereas only the GST-tagged protein was detected in the control (Fig. 3C). These results demonstrate that HaNAC11 can self-interact in vitro. Therefore, these results indicate that HaNAC11 probably forms homodimers both in vivo and in vitro.

To investigate the transcriptional activity of HaNAC11, full-length, N-terminal and C-terminal regions of HaNAC11 were respectively cloned into the pGBKT7 vector. The positive control (pGBKT7-AtNAC2), negative control (pGBKT7-Empty), and the three recombinant vectors were transformed into yeast strain AH109. On SD/-Ade/-His/-Trp medium supplemented with X-α-gal, the positive control along with transformants carrying full-length HaNAC11 and HaNAC11-C-terminal constructs grew normally and turned blue in the α-galactosidase assay, in contrast to the negative control or the HaNAC11-N-terminal transformants (Fig. 3D). These findings show that HaNAC11 possesses transcription-activation activity, which is specifically mediated through its C-terminal region.

HaNAC11 overexpression in Arabidopsis promotes plant growth and development under high temperature and wind stresses

Heterologous expression of H. ammodendron gene in Arabidopsis has been well adopted for studying gene function, since genetic transformation system has not been established for H. ammodendron plant [32, 35, 60]. We then developed HaNAC11 transgenic Arabidopsis to further explore its function. Three different HaNAC11 transgenic Arabidopsis lines were selected, in which HaNAC11 was highly expressed, they were then respectively named as HaNAC11-OE1, 2 and 3 (Supplementary Fig. S2A, B). Interestingly, all three lines exhibited slightly smaller rosette at early vegetable stage but did not show defects in growth and development at later reproductive stage (Supplementary Fig. S2C, D, E). In summary, the growth rate of HaNAC11-transgenic Arabidopsis was slightly slower than that of the WT at the early stage, but accelerated thereafter, showing no significant difference in growth morphology at later stages.

The HaNAC11-OE1, 2 and 3 lines were subjected to mild high-temperature stress. Five-week-old plants of these transgenic lines, along with WT controls, were exposed to 27℃. After 3 days of treatment, the HaNAC11-OE1, 2 and 3 transgenic lines initiated bolting and flowering, whereas WT plants did not. WT plants and HaNAC11-OE1, 2 and 3 transgenic lines grown at normal temperatures did not bolt or blossom flower, either (Fig. 4A). At day 6 after treatment, HaNAC11-OE1, 2 and 3 transgenic plants were significant higher compared to WT Col-0 plants, while both WT and HaNAC11-OE1, 2 and 3 transgenic plants without treatment started to bolt but did not show differences in plants height (Fig. 4A). These results demonstrate that HaNAC11 promotes reproductive growth in Arabidopsis under mild heat stress at 27℃.

In desert environments, H. ammodendron typically faces combined stress conditions, including drought, high temperature and strong wind erosion. We therefore designed a combined high temperature and wind stress experiment. Five-week-old WT and HaNAC11-OE1, 2, 3 transgenic plants grown at normal condition were transferred to a growth chamber at 42℃ with strong wind. After 3 h of treatment, the whole plant of WT Col-0 exhibited severe wilting, but HaNAC11-OE1, 2 and 3 transgenic lines only showed wilting in peripheral tissues (Fig. 4B). Next, these plants were grown at normal conditions for recovery. After 12 days of recovery, WT Col-0 plants had died completely, whereas HaNAC11-OE1, 2 and 3 transgenic lines regenerated new leaves. After 24 days of recovery, HaNAC11-OE1, 2 and 3 transgenic lines bolted to have flower and set seeds normally (Fig. 4B). These findings indicate that HaNAC11 significantly enhances Arabidopsis adaptation and recovery to high temperature and wind stress.

Levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and proline were measured following exposure to mild heat or combined heat/wind stress. No significant differences were detected between the HaNAC11-overexpressing lines and the WT (Supplementary Fig. S3A, B). Given that MDA and proline are established markers of oxidative stress and osmoregulation, respectively, these findings suggest that HaNAC11 likely operates via alternative mechanisms.

HaNAC11 influences glycerolipid pathway and triacylglycerol biosynthesis in Arabidopsis

To find the functional mechanisms of HaNAC11, we conducted transcriptome analyses (PRJCA051945) of WT Col-0 and HaNAC11-OE1, 2 and 3 transgenic lines with RNA-seq.

It was found that, under normal growth conditions, 277 or 57 DEGs were respectively up-regulated or down-regulated in HaNAC11 transgenic plants compared to WT Col-0 plants. After 6 days of treatment at 27℃, 1,478 and 234 DEGs were up-regulated or down-regulated in HaNAC11 transgenic plants compared to WT Col-0 plants. After 3 h of treatment with high temperature and wind stress at 42℃, 554 up-regulated or 146 down-regulated DEGs were respectively identified in HaNAC11 transgenic plants compared to WT Col-0 plants (Supplementary Fig. S4A, B, C, D). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that, under all three conditions, genes functioning in the glycerolipid metabolism pathway were consistently enriched. Additionally, after 6 days of treatment at 27℃, genes associated with cutin, wax, suberin and fatty acid biosynthesis pathways were significantly enriched. Similarly, after 3 h of treatment with high temperature and wind stress at 42℃, genes associated with fatty acid biosynthesis pathways were also significantly enriched (Fig. 5A, B, C). We analyzed 22 genes involved in glycerolipid metabolism and cutin, wax, suberin biosynthesis in transgenic Arabidopsis by qRT‑PCR. Under normal growth conditions, mild heat stress at 27℃ for 6 days, and combined high-temperature and wind stress at 42℃ for 3 h, triacylglycerol‑anabolism genes (e.g., GPAT2, GPAT3, etc.) were upregulated in transgenic lines. Similarly, triacylglycerol catabolism-related genes (MAGL4 and DGK1) were significantly upregulated under both stress conditions, consistent with the transcriptome expression profile data. This coordinated upregulation may supply substrates and energy for the synthesis of cutin, wax, and suberin synthesis. After 6 days at 27℃, genes related to cutin, wax, and suberin were also elevated in transgenic lines. These expression patterns align with transcriptome data and were further verified for glycerolipid-metabolism genes (Supplementary Fig. S5A, B, C). Together, the results indicate that HaNAC11 likely regulates glycerolipid metabolism in plants.

To further validate the role of HaNAC11 in glycerolipid metabolism in Arabidopsis, we quantified triacylglycerol content, the end product of glycerolipid metabolism, in both WT Col-0 and HaNAC11 transgenic plants. Under normal growth conditions, HaNAC11 transgenic plants exhibited significantly higher triacylglycerol levels than WT Col-0. 6 days after treatment at 27℃, triacylglycerol content was decreased substantially in both WT Col-0 and HaNAC11 transgenic plants (Fig. 5D). Analysis of downstream genes revealed that the expression of H. ammodendron orthologs involved in triacylglycerol biosynthesis-HaGPAT2, HaATPAP2, and HaLPAT5-was significantly reduced in HaNAC11-silenced plants (Fig. 6D, F, G). In contrast, the expression of orthologs related to glycerolipid transport and degradation (HaATLT1, HaMAGL4, HaDGK1) remained unchanged (Fig. 6E, H, I). This selective downregulation suggests that HaNAC11 maybe regulate glycerolipid metabolism, potentially through a pathway conserved between H. ammodendron and Arabidopsis.

Crucially, triacylglycerol quantification showed that HaNAC11 silencing led to a significant decrease in triacylglycerol content compared to the control (Fig. 6J). Furthermore, from July to September, triacylglycerol levels were consistently higher in reproductive branches than in vegetative branches, with a notable peak in September (Fig. 6K).

Together, these results demonstrate that HaNAC11 functions to promote triacylglycerol accumulation in H. ammodendron. This enhanced triacylglycerol storage likely contributes to the plant’s adaptation to harsh desert environments.

Discussion

NAC transcription factors are widely distributed in plants and play key roles in mediating responses to environmental stresses [18, 65]. HaNAC11 exhibits the typical architecture of NAC family proteins, containing a conserved N‑terminal NAM domain and a divergent C‑terminal transcriptional activation domain (Figs. 1B and C and 3D) [66] The protein localizes to the nucleus and is capable of forming homodimers (Fig. 3A, B, C). While the N‑terminal DNA‑binding region is highly conserved across NAC proteins, sequence variation is predominantly found in the C‑terminal region (Supplementary Fig. S1A). We propose that the distinct structure of its C‑terminal domain may underlie the specificity of HaNAC11 in regulating downstream targets and conferring unique physiological functions. Expression profiling in this study showed that HaNAC11 in seedlings is rapidly induced by drought, salt, high temperature (50℃), and several hormones (ABA, SA, IAA). Moreover, it maintains sustained high expression in flowers and in reproductive branches from July to September, indicating a prolonged expression platform during reproductive development. This pattern suggests that HaNAC11 expression integrates multiple stress and hormonal signals. ABA primarily triggers stress responses and balances growth with defense [67]; SA mitigates abiotic stress [68] and participates in temperature signaling [69]; and IAA coordinates organ development [70] and is crucial for seed development [71]. The coordinated action of these hormones likely enables H. ammodendron to withstand extreme desert drought and heat in July and August, supporting reproductive branches, and promotes seed maturation in September. The sustained high expression of HaNAC11 may thus be driven by combined hormonal and environmental cues, allowing the plant to cope with prolonged summer stress while maintaining reproductive growth. Collectively, these results demonstrate that HaNAC11 expression is closely linked to both stress induction and reproductive development (Fig. 2D, E).

Heterologous expression in Arabidopsis has proven effective for characterizing genes from H. ammodendron [32–35]. Transcriptomic profiling of HaNAC11-overexpressing Arabidopsis revealed a consistent enrichment of glycerolipid metabolism genes under control, mild heat (27℃), and combined heat/wind stress (42℃) conditions (Fig. 5A, B, C). These lines displayed a moderately smaller rosette during early vegetative growth (Supplementary Fig. S2). Although such an effect could be an artifact of transformation, the transcriptome data point to a possible physiological cause: the activation of lipid metabolism likely alters carbon partitioning. Glycerolipid biosynthesis culminates in triacylglycerol production. Notably, Arabidopsis lines overexpressing WRI1-a key regulator of oil synthesis and a member of the AP2/EREB domain protein family-exhibit enhanced triacylglycerol accumulation but often display slower early growth, leaf chlorosis, and delayed flowering due to the redirection of carbon flux toward storage lipids [72]. Similarly, HaNAC11 overexpression activated glycerolipid pathways, suggesting that photosynthetic carbon was preferentially channeled into triacylglycerol synthesis rather than into biomass accumulation. This may account for the early growth reduction observed. The subsequent growth recovery could then reflect the remobilization of stored triacylglycerol to fuel later development. We propose that this represents a strategic trade-off: under fluctuating stress conditions, investing carbon into protective and energy-rich triacylglycerol reserves may improve survival and reproductive success, outweighing the cost of transient growth delay.

Under mild heat stress (27℃), transgenic plants flowered earlier than controls and maintained comparatively high triacylglycerol levels, albeit lower than in non-stressed plants (Figs. 4A and 5D). This is consistent with the general trend of heat-induced triacylglycerol reduction in oil crops [73] and aligns with the high metabolic demand of reproduction. Triacylglycerol breakdown supplies energy and carbon skeletons for flowering [74, 75], and its derivatives serve as precursors for phosphatidylglycerol, which interacts with FT to promote flowering [76]. Thus, HaNAC11-induced triacylglycerol pools appear to be dynamically mobilized under mild heat to support the accelerated reproductive transition. Under severe combined heat/wind stress (42℃), transgenic lines exhibited greater tolerance alongside higher triacylglycerol content than the WT (Figs. 4B and 5E). This parallels findings in Arabidopsis overexpressing PDAT (phospholipid: diacylglycerol acyltransferase), which increases triacylglycerol and enhances thermotolerance [77], and in desiccation-tolerant species such as Craterostigma plantagineum, where leaf triacylglycerol accumulation helps maintain membrane integrity [78]. Triacylglycerol can directly stabilize membranes under stress [79] and provide fatty acids and energy for synthesizing protective cuticular waxes [80, 81]-a notion supported by the up-regulation of cutin/wax biosynthesis genes in our transgenic plants (Fig. 5B; Supplementary Fig. S5C). In summary, our results demonstrate that HaNAC11 may modulate glycerolipid metabolism to elevate triacylglycerol levels, which, under heat stress, serve a dual role: as a readily mobilized energy reserve for reproduction and as a structural component that enhances membrane stability and cuticle formation.

Building on observations in Arabidopsis, we hypothesized that HaNAC11 promotes triacylglycerol accumulation by likely regulating key glycerolipid metabolic genes, including GPAT2 and DGK1. To test whether this mechanism is conserved in H. ammodendron, we analyzed triacylglycerol levels and the expression of orthologous genes after silencing HaNAC11 via VIGS (Fig. 6D–J). The results showed significant down-regulation of Arabidopsis triacylglycerol-biosynthesis orthologs-HaGPAT2, HaATPAP2, and HaLPAT5. In the Kennedy pathway, GPAT2 catalyzes the initial committing step, LPAT5 drives the second rate-limiting step of phosphatidic acid synthesis [82], and ATPAP2 completes triacylglycerol assembly [83]. By contrast, orthologs associated with glycerolipid transport and degradation (HaATLT1, HaMAGL4, HaDGK1) remained unchanged. The specific repression of biosynthesis genes upon HaNAC11 silencing indicates that HaNAC11 participates in triacylglycerol production in H. ammodendron and supports the conservation of this metabolic pathway between the two species. Our data provide initial evidence connecting HaNAC11 to glycerolipid metabolism in H. ammodendron. HaNAC11 silencing reduced triacylglycerol content in assimilating branches, while reproductive branches-which show elevated HaNAC11 expression-consistently accumulated higher triacylglycerol levels than vegetative branches. Together, these findings support a potential role for HaNAC11 in influencing glycerolipid metabolism in H. ammodendron. Collectively, we propose that HaNAC11 influences stress adaptation not by directly regulating stress-response circuits, but primarily through its potential effect on glycerolipid metabolism. By stimulating triacylglycerol accumulation, HaNAC11 likely helps establish energy reserves and structural precursors that enable the plant to withstand environmental fluctuations, thereby potentially serving a central protective role (Fig. 7).

Future perspectives

To elucidate the direct regulatory role of HaNAC11, further molecular validation-such as EMSA and yeast one‑hybrid assays-will be required to determine whether it binds to promoters of glycerolipid metabolic genes and whether these binding sites are conserved between H. ammodendron and Arabidopsis. A notable limitation of this study is that the high‑temperature response of HaNAC11 could not be fully validated in its native host, as VIGS efficiency substantially decreases under heat stress. Consistent with this, previous reports indicate that albino positive controls may regain green pigmentation under extreme temperatures [84, 85]. Thus, establishing a stable genetic transformation system for H. ammodendron in the future will be essential to further clarify the function of HaNAC11 and its mechanistic role in enhancing plant thermotolerance and stress resilience.

Conclusions

This study shows that overexpression of HaNAC11 in Arabidopsis elevates triacylglycerol levels and improves heat tolerance, while silencing HaNAC11 by VIGS in H. ammodendron decreases triacylglycerol accumulation. Together, these findings suggest that HaNAC11 likely modulates glycerolipid metabolism. Furthermore, HaNAC11 emerges as a novel stress‑responsive gene with promising potential for breeding more resilient agricultural and forestry crops.
