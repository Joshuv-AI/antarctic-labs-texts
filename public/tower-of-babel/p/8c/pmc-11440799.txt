A broadly applicable method for quantifying cardiomyocyte cell division identifies proliferative events following myocardial infarction
Authors: Samantha K Swift, Alexandra L Purdy, Tyler Buddell, Jerrell J Lovett, Smrithi V Chanjeevaram, Anooj Arkatkar, Caitlin C O’Meara, Michaela Patterson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryCardiomyocyte proliferation is a challenging metric to assess. Current methodologies have limitations in detecting the generation of new cardiomyocytes and technical challenges that reduce widespread applicability. Here, we describe an improved cell suspension and imaging-based methodology that can be broadly employed to assess cardiomyocyte cell division in standard laboratories across a multitude of model organisms and experimental conditions. We highlight additional metrics that can be gathered from the same cell preparations to enable additional relevant analyses to be performed. We incorporate additional antibody stains to address potential technical concerns of miscounting. Finally, we employ this methodology with a dual-thymidine analog-labeling approach to a post-infarction murine model, which allowed us to robustly identify unique cycling events, such as cardiomyocytes undergoing multiple rounds of cell division.Keywords: cardiomyocyte, proliferation, endomitosis, somatic polyploidy, cell cycle, myocardial infarction, heart regeneration

Introduction

With an increased interest in stimulating endogenous cardiomyocyte (CM) cell cycle as a strategy to combat heart disease, there is a concerted interest in distinguishing true proliferation from polyploidization and hypertrophy. Many reports conflate CM cell-cycle activity with proliferation, which may lead to a major misinterpretation of results.

Existing strategies to assess cell-cycle completion have a mix of limitations.1,2 A common technique used for assessing CM proliferation is by quantification of the protein expression of common cell-cycle markers via immunofluorescence on histological sections. Many cell-cycle markers (i.e., Ki67, PCNA, and phospho-histone H3) cannot differentiate polyploidization from true proliferation. Even for proteins whose expressions are thought to mark cytokinesis specifically, proper interpretation of localization is required to accurately assess whether cytokinesis will be achieved as most are actively expressed in both binucleation and cytokinesis events. For example, multiple studies have demonstrated that even in cells that ultimately failed cytokinesis and resulted in binucleation, both aurora kinase B and anillin still transiently localize to the midbody; however, only asymmetrical positioning of specific midbody elements (i.e., anillin, RhoA, and IQGAP3) can distinguish between the two outcomes in vitro.3,4 Assessing the asymmetry of midbodies in tissue sections where CM orientation is dynamic can be challenging, although some studies have implemented additional positioning rules as an attempt to quantify successful cytokinesis.5,6,7 Nevertheless, this method is fraught with additional issues, including rare events and the inability to definitively distinguish events corresponding to CMs versus neighboring interstitial cells. Furthermore, assessment of midbody formation provides only a brief snapshot of actively dividing cells and has little use as an analysis of completed cell division over time. Finally, several past studies have employed video microscopy to capture cytokinesis as it is taking place,8,9 which, while definitive, only captures what is possible in vitro and cannot be performed in vivo nor in response to injury or disease.

Other techniques that do allow for in vivo analysis over time include transgenic mouse lines like the mosaic analysis with double markers (MADM) mouse model10,11 or the recently developed dual recombinase models.12,13 While these models have the additional benefit of being Cre/Dre-inducible, allowing analysis to be CM specific, as well as the ability to exploit a Ki67 promotor to lineage trace cells that enter the cell cycle, both pose feasibility issues. To analyze cell division by these methods, while considering unique genetic backgrounds or in combination with engineered alleles for a specific gene, would require intensive and potentially even prohibitive breeding strategies. Regarding the MADM mouse specifically, some have raised the concern that the method may be inefficient in the heart as the anticipated pairs of single-green and single-red cardiomyocytes are not usually found together.2 Clearly, there is still much work to be done to improve the accuracy and feasibility of measuring the completion of CM division.

To distinguish proliferation versus further polyploidization post-myocardial infarction (MI), we combined previously established techniques to analyze CM ploidy with thymidine analog-based labeling of DNA synthesis, to develop a methodology that allows for robust quantification of CM cell division using any genetic background and across species. We assess single-cell suspensions after thymidine analog incorporation and calculate total cellular ploidy of cycling CMs, accounting for both nucleation (i.e., how many nuclei a cell has) and nuclear DNA content (i.e., how much DNA is in each nucleus). By combining these metrics, we can conclude that any CM that has incorporated the administered thymidine analog and is mononuclear and diploid must have both entered the cell cycle and completed cell division. Here, we outline the method, address theoretical technical concerns with the method, and demonstrate an application performing a dual-thymidine-labeling experiment after adult MI. Our findings from this dual-labeling experiment provide clues as to the timing of CM cell-cycle activation and proliferation after injury, continue to point to major differences between genetic backgrounds, including dimensional changes, and demonstrate that a single CM can divide more than once following MI.

Results

Assessment of CM cell division by single-cell suspension

The method we employ here identifies cycling CMs by labeling with thymidine analogs and then assesses the DNA content (i.e., ploidy) of labeled cells to retrospectively infer whether those CMs that entered the cell cycle have successfully completed cell division. Specifically, if a CM that is positive for the thymidine analog (i.e., entered the cell cycle and undergone S-phase) and has a diploid content (2N), considering both nucleation and single nucleus ploidy, then it must have completed division. Notably, we describe the method in the context of murine models utilizing Langendorff perfusion to generate single-cell suspensions; however, the logic and method can be applied to other species with adjustments to the digestion protocol.

With this method, thymidine analog labeling can be performed in conjunction with an MI surgery to assess CM injury response,14,15 or in the absence of injury to assess development or steady-state scenarios,14 by injecting animals with the thymidine analog during a pre-established experimental window of interest. Following labeling, heart ventricles are digested by retroaortic perfusion using a Langendorff apparatus to generate single-cell suspensions of CMs. The resulting single-cell preparations are then stained for a CM marker (e.g., a sarcomeric protein), the thymidine analog, and DNA using any classic DNA dye (e.g., DAPI or Hoechst; Figure 1A). Additional markers that could be incorporated include Connexin43 (CX43) or N-Cadherin (N-Cad) for visualizing intercalated discs to confirm intact single cells, and Pericentriolar Material 1 (PCM1) or NKX2-5 to label CM nuclei specifically. Stained preparations are then spread in a monolayer across multiple slides, and the slides are scanned in their entirety under a standard fluorescent microscope for thymidine analog positive CMs. While scanning slides manually, the user will note the nucleation status (i.e., mono-, bi-, tri-, and tetranucleated) of the labeled cell and capture an image of it. Finally, captured images are used to assess the sum pixel intensity of any DNA dye within a single nucleus. Fluorescence intensity serves as a surrogate for DNA content and is normalized to a reference nucleus known to be diploid, such as nuclei from tri- and tetranucleated CMs. Ploidy analysis can be done on any software the user prefers (e.g., NIS Elements, ImageJ). By combining the quantifications of nuclear number done under the microscope and the nuclear ploidy analysis from captured images, an estimate of total DNA content for each cell can be calculated (Figure 1B). When ploidy is analyzed in conjunction with the thymidine analog, one can then infer whether cell division has taken place (Figure 1C). As stated above, pictures captured with a standard fluorescent microscope are ideal for this analysis as image acquisition is fast and the full volume of a single nucleus is conveyed into a single flattened image, resulting in clean histograms with only 0.09% of nuclei falling outside of the 2N, 4N, and 8N designated gates (Figure S1A). Confocal microscopy is less ideal due to the substantial increase in image acquisition and analysis time. Furthermore, in a small pilot experiment where ploidy analysis was instead performed by confocal microscopy (Figure S1B), the number of nuclei falling outside of a designated gate when volumetric reconstruction of 4-μM slices was used to estimate DNA content was 4.7% (Figure S1C). This error rate grew to 9.9% if only a single 4-μM plane was used (Figure S1D). Thus, confocal microscopy proved to generate less-precise histograms compared to a standard epifluorescence microscope.

While the main goal of the methodology can be achieved by ploidy analysis of the thymidine analog-labeled population, the original single-cell suspensions (Figure 1A) can be used to collect a variety of additional data that can be used to either substantiate a conclusion or add new insights. A single preparation of ventricular CMs can be used to (1) assess general CM ploidy independent of thymidine analog status (Figure 1B); (2) assess CM ploidy within the thymidine analog-labeled subpopulation to infer cell division (Figure 1C), as discussed; (3) quantify the total number of CMs, which in specific circumstances can provide additional evidence of CM expansion (Figure 1D)14; (4) quantify the total number of cycling CMs (Figure 1E) to provide context to the percentage-based data of the ploidy analysis14,15; and (5) assess CM dimensions (Figure 1F). We demonstrate the potential and the utility of this final metric in Figures 4E–4I for the first time.

Addressing potential technical concerns with the methodology

There are some foreseeable technical concerns with this methodology, which we summarize in three main points and address here experimentally. First, the unintentional inclusion of non-CM nuclei in the analysis. Second, that under-digested clump of cells might be incorrectly included as multinucleated CMs. Finally, that undetectable cell fragmentation could lead to the analysis of broken cells. We provide conclusive evidence that these potential concerns only nominally influence experimental results.

The first plausible issue regarding the presented methodology is the potential for non-CM nuclei to overlap with CM sarcomere stains, leading to incorrectly identified nuclei and inaccurate quantifications. However, unlike in tissue sections where we agree it is possible to mistake interstitial non-CM nuclei for a CM nucleus,16 in these single-cell suspensions it is quite easy to distinguish them. Collectively considering morphology, localization, and size make it relatively simple to identify a non-CM nucleus overlapping with a sarcomere stain. To experimentally address this point, we stained a C57BL/6J CM cell prep with anti-PCM1 antibody, which has a distinct perinuclear localization that is specific to CMs, thus making it a useful marker to identify CM nuclei (Figures 2A and 2A’). These preps were imaged using the standard epifluorescent imaging techniques described here. Three independent scientists first analyzed the images using only a sarcomeric stain to identify CMs and with the PCM1 fluorescent channel explicitly turned off. After identifying each perceived CM nucleus in an image, the PCM1 channel was made visible to identify any nuclei incorrectly identified by our original quantification. With this strategy, we determined that on average, across three independent observers, non-CM nuclei were incorrectly included in the analysis less than 0.3% ± 0.17% (Figure 2B) of the time. Figure 2A’ also demonstrates how easily PCM1+ CM nuclei (Figure 2A’, white arrowheads) can be morphologically distinguished from non-CM nuclei (Figure 2A’, yellow arrowheads). NKX2-5, a transcription factor specific to CMs from mid-gestation onward, can also be used in a similar fashion (Figure 2C).

Another potential limitation of the single-cell methodology is the potential of undigested cells being included in quantifications, superficially inflating the count of bi- or multinucleated cells. This we find to be the most uncommon error in our hands. With a sarcomere stain labeling the myocytes (i.e., cardiac troponin T [cTnT] or myosin light chain 2 [Myl2]), a junction between two CMs is easily identifiable (Figures 3A and 3D, yellow arrow). When we encounter one of these scenarios, we can easily distinguish them as two cells. Using CX43 or N-Cad as components of the intercalated disc (Figures 3A’–3F′), three independent observers quantified the frequency with which they had incorrectly quantified a cell as a single cell when it was in fact a doublet. Two observers never made this mistake, and a third observer made the mistake just twice in almost 400 cells analyzed. Thus, across three observers, this error occurred just 0.17% ± 0.29% of the time (Figure 3B, third column).

We employed these same markers to demonstrate that multinucleated CMs can easily be identified by contiguous sarcomeres (compare Figures 3C and 3C′ to Figures 3A and 3A′). In addition to the sarcomere stain, the alignment of nuclei can also help distinguish a situation where two cells remain attached versus one long multinuclear cell (Figures 3D and D′, yellow arrowhead points to two asymmetrically aligned cells with N-Cad in the middle; white arrowhead indicates a tetranucleated cell where the nuclei are nicely aligned as expected). Occasionally, a dense cluster of cells (either undigested or poorly distributed across the slide during coverslipping) that cannot be distinguished from one another is encountered. When these dense clusters arise, we simply exclude them from the analysis. Regarding the quantification of cycling events, digestions can be more challenging in fibrotic post-MI hearts compared to uninjured hearts. Thus, dense clusters are more common. This would simply result in an undercounting of total events, but the proportion of CM ploidy and nucleation class is expected to remain the same.

These intercalated disc stains (i.e., N-Cad or CX43) can also be used to address the final possibility—the potential inclusion of broken CMs, potentially inflating the number of mononuclear cells included in analysis. The method presented here is different from others in that the tissue is digested without prior fixation. Using this strategy, if a cell fragments due to the digestion, then it will sphere up or fray at the edges (Figures 3E–3E’), indicating cell death. This is in contrast to an intact diploid CM with intercalated disc marker evident at both ends (Figures 3F–3F’). These dead or fragmented CMs are easy to identify by morphology and are excluded from the analysis. While in some cases, fixation could help to ensure CM morphology and sarcomere integrity, we have encountered few issues keeping CMs intact when performing digestions on live, unfixed tissue, and our cell preparations consist of over 95% live and rod-shaped CMs (Figure S2A). To determine whether we are including an exorbitant number of fragmented CMs in our analysis, we again utilized the CX43 stain. As before, we imaged a preparation, and three independent evaluators quantified the images using our standard protocol with the CX43 channel turned off. After analysis was completed, the CX43 fluorescent channel was turned back on and the number of CMs with staining identifiable at both ends of the CM was assessed. We determined that across the evaluators, only 2.33% ± 0.67% of CMs included in the original quantification were potentially not intact due to the lack of visible CX43 staining at both ends of the CM. In addition, we found this level of miscounting not to be strain specific due to some unknown difference in collagen structure or digestibility, as only a rare miscall was made when analyzing a divergent strain, A/J (Figure S2B).

While the original theoretical concerns were reasonable, we have assessed their actual impact on the analysis and determined that even collectively they would only nominally impact results and interpretations. However, if users (particularly those new to the methodology) are concerned with any of these issues, the additional antibodies marking intercalated discs and CM nuclei that we have described here can be added to any analysis.

Dual-thymidine labeling paired with single-cell suspension identifies new details in CM proliferation after injury

We implemented this new methodology to answer several outstanding questions in the field regarding the time frame by which CM cell-cycle activation and completion occurs after an MI. Senyo et al. calculated the rate of CM renewal across a variety of ages, both with and without ischemic injury, using an extended thymidine analog-labeling window. This study established that CM renewal indeed occurs at a rate of <1%/year in young adults and increases specifically in the border zone after MI.17 Whether this renewal occurs gradually over time or within a specific window post-infarction remains unknown. Here, we investigated a 2-week window following MI and asked whether the number of CMs that enter the cell cycle is consistent throughout the 2 weeks and whether cell-cycle activation observed in each week preferentially results in cell division or further polyploidization. Furthermore, it remains unknown whether there is a subset of CMs that continue to proliferate throughout recovery, or, conversely, whether each CM enters the cell cycle just once before re-establishing itself as a functional myocyte. Finally, given that recent studies have demonstrated that genetic background heavily influences levels of DNA synthesis and cell-cycle completion after injury,14,15,18 we investigated the influence that genetic diversity might have on CM proliferation dynamics post-MI. To address these questions, we utilized a dual-thymidine analog-labeling strategy paired with our single-cell suspension methodology. Here, we infused mice with 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) from days 3 to 10, followed by a second infusion with bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) on days 10–17 (Figure 4A). The bioavailability of EdU following injection into rodents has been estimated to be just 2–3 h.19 Thus, any overlapping availability of the two compounds mistakenly labeling the same DNA synthesis event rather than distinct events is predicted to be nominal. At the end of the second labeling week, CMs were collected via Langendorff and ploidy was quantified in EdU+, BrdU+, and EdU+BrdU+ CMs (Figure 4B). This experiment was performed in two divergent inbred strains of mice (A/J and C57BL/6J) that are known to have differing levels of CM cell-cycle activation, completion, and recovery after MI.14,15,18

In C57BL/6J mice, we found no significant difference between the number of CMs undergoing DNA synthesis in the first week (EdU+) compared to the second week (BrdU+) (Figure 4C). Additionally, the resulting ploidy content of CMs that had undergone DNA synthesis was also similar between the two weeks, with a slight trend toward higher levels of polyploidy in the second week (Figure 4D; complete statistics provided in Table S1). During both weeks, CMs undergoing DNA synthesis in C57BL/6J mice almost exclusively demonstrated increased polyploidization, with ∼65% of CMs containing ≥8N DNA content, and only a very rare thymidine analog-positive mononuclear diploid CM (MNDCM) noted (0.4% the first week and 0.6% the second week; Figure 4D). We found fewer A/J CMs undergoing DNA synthesis during the investigative time frame compared to C57BL/6J mice, consistent with previous reports.15,18 Interestingly, significantly more A/J CMs underwent DNA synthesis during the second week of labeling compared to the first (p = 0.008; Figure 4C). Analogous to what was seen in C57BL/6J mice, the distribution of ploidy classes between the first and second week of labeling in A/J were remarkably similar (Figure 4D). Approximately 14% of thymidine analog-labeled CMs in A/J were mononuclear and diploid in both weeks, with only ∼25% of cells reaching the hyperpolyploid levels (≥8N), which was the most common ploidy class seen in C57BL/6J. A/J mice displayed significantly more (p = 3.7E−5) thymidine-analog-positive MNDCMs than C57BL/6J mice after MI (Figure 4D; Table S1), again consistent with previous reports.15

By also assessing cell dimensions on these single-cell suspensions (as in Figure 1F), we observed the area of thymidine-positive CMs is greater in C57BL/6J compared to A/J (Figure 4E). This is in concurrence with the observed increase in total DNA content of C57BL/6J thymidine-positive CMs (mostly ≥8N) compared to A/J thymidine-positive CMs (mostly ≤4N). Furthermore, C57BL/6J thymidine-labeled CMs have a lower length-to-width ratio compared to equivalent A/J CMs, perhaps indicating distinct hypertrophic responses (Figure 4F). Many have speculated that DNA synthesis without cell division could indicate hypertrophic rather than hyperplastic growth; however, this has yet to be demonstrated experimentally. To directly test the impact of DNA synthesis on cell dimension, we specifically compared thymidine-positive CMs to thymidine-negative CMs. Here, we note that nucleation also impacts CM dimensions (Figure 1F); thus, we limited this analysis to just the binucleated population. In C57BL/6J mice, thymidine-positive binucleated cells have a significantly larger area than their thymidine-negative counterparts (Figure 4G). The length of the thymidine-positive CMs remained unchanged (Figure 4H), while their width increased significantly (Figure 4I), suggesting the binucleated CMs that have undergone DNA synthesis are undergoing concentric hypertrophy specifically. An analogous analysis in A/J CMs was not possible because there were too few binucleated thymidine-positive CMs present in A/J hearts. These data confirm the prediction that DNA synthesis in the absence of cell division indeed represents a strategy to maximize the hypertrophic growth response.

When quantifying cells that had incorporated both EdU and BrdU, suggesting they had entered the cell cycle at least two times (once during each week), we found that only ∼14% of CMs in C57BL/6J that underwent DNA synthesis in the first week underwent a second DNA synthesis event during the second week (Figure 5A). Of those EdU+ CMs that did re-enter the cell cycle in the second week (i.e., were also BrdU+), approximately one-third ended up 4N CMs (Figure 5A). These 4N, double-labeled cells presumably completed at least one round of cell division, as two rounds of DNA synthesis of a 2N cell with no cell division events would instead yield an ≥8N cell (the DNA content of the remaining approximately two-thirds of double-labeled cells; Figure 5A). Conversely, 60% of A/J CMs that underwent DNA synthesis in the first labeling week (EdU+) underwent a second synthesis event in the following week. Almost 15% of EdU+ A/J CMs were both double positive and mononuclear diploid (1 × 2N), indicating that these cells underwent at least two full rounds of DNA synthesis and completed cell division. Additionally, another 33% of EdU+ CMs were double positive and 4N, meaning that they underwent at least one round of cell division. These data continue to suggest that A/J CMs are more competent to complete cell division, while C57BL/6J CMs favor endomitotic-based cycling. To determine where double-labeled cycling CMs were preferentially found, a second cohort of mice entered MI studies as in Figure 4A; however, instead of collecting for single-cell suspension, hearts were instead fixed and collected for four-chamber histology. Double-labeled CM nuclei were preferentially found on the subendocardial side of the infarct and border zones across both mouse strains (Figures 5B and 5C). Occasional double-positive CM nuclei could also be found in the remote zone only in C57BL/6J hearts; however, these remote zone double-labeled nuclei were notably larger than those found in the infarct and border zones (Figure 5C, top row), perhaps suggesting that the observed cell division is limited to the subendocardial border zone, consistent with previous a previous study.13 We note that one past study has indicated cross-reactivity of the BrdU antibody (ab6326) to recognize EdU to near fidelity20; however, in both our cell suspensions and tissue sections we observe substantial numbers of EdU+ cells (both CMs and non-CMs) that do not stain for BrdU (Figure S3A). Furthermore, we stained myocardial tissue that had been injected only with EdU and not BrdU (Figure S3B) to ask how often the BrdU antibody detected EdU incorporation, and we found that only 6.6% ± 1.0% of EdU-labeled nuclei were identified as BrdU+ by antibody stain. It remains possible that some of the double-labeled events are due to antibody cross-reactivity; however, the numbers of double-labeled cells that we report in our study are substantially higher than we observed in this negative control, and thus are not likely solely due to this technical limitation.

Discussion

Here, we describe a method that pairs thymidine analog labeling with a cardiac single-cell suspension analysis, thereby allowing for the identification and quantification of CM cell division events in a variety of contexts. Notably, adjustments to the thymidine labeling step of the protocol allow researchers to adapt the method to their own biological question of interest, and thus far, variations of the method have successfully been applied to both developmental and post-injury contexts.14,15 In the present study, we again altered the thymidine analog-labeling strategy, allowing us to address lingering questions in the field of cardiac regeneration. With this approach, we were able to confirm that both frequency of cell-cycle entry and competence to complete division are influenced by genetic background. However, beyond confirming these previously reported insights, we also report several novel findings. The observed frequency of cell-cycle events in C57BL/6J is consistent from week 1 to week 2 post-MI but in a second strain, A/J, DNA synthesis more than triples from the first to the second week. In the case of A/J mice, we know a subpopulation of CMs have a higher competence to complete cell division15; thus, the increase in DNA synthesis from the first to the second week (Figure 4C) could be explained by an expanded pool of these competent CMs following the first division event of the first week. It would be interesting to ascertain when cell-cycle activation and proliferation eventually slow in both strains; do A/J CMs continue to divide and expand in the third and fourth weeks? We also demonstrate that an additional metric, cell dimension, can be collected from the same cell preparations, which in turn provides evidence that cell-cycle activation in C57BL/6J is more indicative of hypertrophic growth, while cell-cycle activation in A/J results in smaller CMs supportive of the hyperplastic growth conclusion observed by ploidy analysis.

With our dual-labeling strategy, we were also able to ask whether any CMs undergo more than one round of the cell cycle following injury, and what the final outcomes are from these events. Indeed, we identified a population of CMs that underwent S-phase more than once. This subset of myocytes was present in both strains but was more common in A/J mice, where 60% of those CMs that incorporated EdU in the first week also incorporated BrdU in the second. Furthermore, the majority of these cells in A/J mice were 2N or 4N, indicating that they can also undergo division one or more times. In C57BL/6J mice, ∼66% of these double-labeled cells were instead 8N, likely indicating two rounds of endomitosis with no cell division. Notably, while we found no double-positive 1 × 2N cells in C57BL/6J mice, there was a small proportion of 4N double-labeled cells, which could only occur if the CM had undergone division at some point. Prior work utilizing this cell suspension methodology after labeling with only a single thymidine analog had concluded that C57BL/6J CMs were not likely capable of substantial cell division.14,15,18 The data presented here therefore identify previously unreported division events in C57BL/6J mice, a result that may be more congruent with results from the dual recombinase-based approaches, where clusters of lineage-traced cells could be found even in a predominantly C57BL/6 background.12,13

Interestingly, our follow-up experiment assessing where dual-labeled CMs were located within the myocardium identified that they were predominantly found in the infarct and border zones and preferentially on the subendocardial side, again, in agreement with past studies.13 Some double-labeled CMs could be found in the remote zone only in C57BL/6J hearts; however, these nuclei were notably larger in size, perhaps corresponding to the ≥8N population and the hypertrophic response suggested by the single-cell suspension analyses, a response that A/J hearts seemingly fail to mount. Thus, these data may indicate that cell-cycle activation at the border zone can, although not always, result in true cell division, while cell-cycle activation in the remote zone instead contributes to a non-regenerative hypertrophic response. Notably, these discoveries were only made possible by our dual-thymidine analog-labeling approach and suggest that CM cell-cycle activation and division is a much more dynamic process after MI than previously realized. Of course, even our dual-labeling strategy reported here cannot account for multiple rounds of DNA synthesis that occurred during a single labeling week.

When analyzing CM ploidy in any context, it is essential to evaluate both nucleation and nuclear ploidy together. Any analysis that separates nuclear ploidy from nucleation (i.e., by flow cytometry) would paint an incomplete picture and may result in the misinterpretation of results. The present improved methodology produces both nucleation and ploidy data from the same cell, allowing for a more complete picture of cell-cycle dynamics. Additionally, we believe single-cell preparations are advantageous over attempting to quantify ploidy in histological sections. Several studies have applied a similar logic to tissue sections using wheat germ agglutinin or another cell membrane identifier to assess nucleation status across cells. For unknown reasons, these studies tend to identify a shifted increase in the number of mononuclear and MNDCMs compared to quantifications by single-cell suspensions. Another improvement from past renditions of this method is to normalize DAPI intensity to other CM nuclei with known diploid content from the same image. In our experience, this added rigor results in remarkably clean histograms (where very few nuclei need to be excluded because they fall in between designated gates; Figure S1A) compared to when endothelial or non-CMs are used for normalization.15 Notably, the transition from 2N to 4N by this strategy is portrayed by a nearly perfect doubling of pixel intensity; however, further expansion to 8N and 16N populations are less linear and require less-stringent gates. In addition, we describe here that potential technical concerns (i.e., counting non-CM nuclei, fragmentation, and clumping) only nominally influences final results in our hands.

We report a higher efficiency of obtaining healthy, rod-shaped CMs from our Langendorff digestions than have typically been reported previously. This aids in obtaining reliable results from the proposed method, and we have therefore tried to indicate in the STAR Methods some of the factors we think are particularly important for the high efficiency of our preparations.

Limitations of the study

There is an urgent need for accurate and attainable methods for measuring true CM proliferation. Most studies fall short of demonstrating that cell-cycle-activated CMs are indeed dividing. Arguably, the best methods available (i.e., the MADM and dual recombinase mouse models) can be prohibitive when trying to pair them with gain- and loss-of-function genetic models or when assessing multiple genetic backgrounds. The MADM mouse is still quite elegant and warranted in select scenarios—for example, drug-based treatments. Again, these studies can only be performed in a single genetic background, which may pose a limitation if trying to understand how a drug may act in distinct individuals. The method we describe here does have some limitations. Here, we have been able to address many of the potential technical concerns one might have with the methodology; however, other limitations remain. Most notably, single-cell suspensions result in the loss of anatomical location data, require a second cohort of animals from those used for histological-based analyses, and are somewhat laborious; also, incomplete digestions, particularly in fibrotic scenarios, would likely result in an undercounting of DNA synthesis events. Additional troubleshooting to further digest the fibrotic scar is warranted. Furthermore, the tail end analysis can only infer cell division if a resulting thymidine analog-labeled cell is 1 × 2N; this assumption may fail to identify true division events that result in a distinct ploidy end state. Our dual-thymidine approach confirms that this limitation is real. Finally, we are utilizing a sarcomeric antibody to identify CMs; if a thymidine analog-labeled CM is actively dividing at the time of collection, the sarcomeres may be transiently disassembled and cells mistakenly excluded due to poor morphology. These limitations—incomplete digestion and failure to identify additional real events—would result in undercounting.

Additional limitations relate to the use of thymidine analogs themselves. Various thymidine analogs (i.e., BrdU, EdU, CldU) have been reported to have varying degrees of detrimental effects on cell-cycle progression.21,22 Thus, it remains possible that further division could occur in the absence of thymidine analogs. Various studies that have assessed the bioavailability of thymidine analogs following a single bolus, high-dose injection have concluded that a best estimate for the labeling window is just 2–3 h,19,23,24 which should be accounted for in the study design. To the best of our knowledge, no explicit study has examined the labeling window following administration by osmotic pump. A past report identified cross-reactivity of various BrdU antibodies with EdU.20 In our hands, we do not observe the same degree of false labeling (Figure S3); however, it remains possible that some of our double-labeled events are incorrectly included.

One might expect that thymidine analog intensity alone could be used as a metric to infer how many divisions a cell or nucleus has completed. For example, in theory, in our experiment, the EdU intensity of double-labeled (EdU/BrdU double-positive) cells should be half that of an EdU-only positive cell. However, we caution against making such inferences as various technical factors would also influence thymidine analog intensity (e.g., how many rounds of DNA synthesis a cell undergoes while the analog is present, if the bioavailability of the analogs is truly constant across the administration time course, and technical factors during the staining protocol), which results in too much noise to confidently draw such conclusions. Indeed, when we analyzed EdU intensity of single- versus double-labeled cells in our experiment, we observed a slight decrease in EdU sum pixel intensity in double-labeled cells, but this decrease was noisy and not consistent (Figure S4). A final limitation relates to nuclei labeled with thymidine analogs that had not in fact undergone DNA synthesis but had instead incorporated the free thymidine due to DNA repair. In many studies that assess DNA repair by thymidine analog incorporation, authors report more of a puncta-patterned stain, rather than the whole nucleus being brightly labeled.25,26 By microscopy-based immunofluorescence, these two staining patterns are distinguishable; thus, this limitation is likely to result in only occasional miscalling.

Limitations aside, the single-cell suspension method we describe here is powerful, with its strongest asset being the potential for very broad applicability. With a carefully designed thymidine analog administration strategy, this method can in theory be performed in any species, across any age or disease context, and in any genetic background. That said, large animal models may present feasibility issues for Langendorff-based digestions, although some labs are successfully troubleshooting alternative digestion strategies to assess ploidy by single-cell suspension even in large mammals like swine.27 The method does not require advanced equipment or technology and thus could be implemented by any lab. Furthermore, multiple layered metrics (Figure 1) can be gathered from the same sample. These attributes make it a valuable addition to the cardiac development and myocardial regeneration toolbox.

Method details

Mice

All experiments involving animals were performed following approval by the Medical College of Wisconsin’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and in accordance with recommendations by the American Veterinary Medical Association. A/J (JAX stock #000646) and C57BL/6J (JAX stock #000664) mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, Maine. MI studies were performed on 8-week-old female mice of both indicated strains.

Permanent LAD ligation and thymidine analog administration

8-week-old A/J and C57Bl/6J female mice were subjected to permanent ligation of the left anterior descending artery. Briefly, while maintained under 2–4% isoflurane, the heart was extruded from the thoracic cavity via the 4th intercostal space and the left anterior descending artery was permanently ligated with a 6-0 prolene suture.14,28,29 For continuous labeling of cycling CMs, ALZET Osmotic Pumps (ALZET, 1007D) carrying 2.5mg of EdU (Thermo, E10187) were implanted under the cutaneous layer of the interscapular region on day 3 after infarction, removed on day 10 and replaced with a second ALZET Osmotic Pump carrying 2.5 mg of BrdU (Sigma, B5002) until collection of CMs on day 17.

Single-cell ventricular suspensions

Hearts were extracted and hung retroaortically on a Langendorff apparatus; all buffers during the perfusion were maintained at 37°C. Hearts were perfused with calcium-free Tyrode’s buffer (120 mM NaCl, 4 mM KCl, 0.33 mM NaH2PO4, 1 mM MgCl2-6H2O, 10 mM glucose, 5 mM Taurine, 10 mM HEPES, 10 mM BDM, pH 7.4), followed by 50 mL of 1 mg/mL collagenase type II (Thermo Fisher, 17101015) dissolved in calcium-free Tyrode’s buffer. Following perfusion, ventricular tissue was diced with scissors, triturated with a wide bore pipette in ice-cold Kruftbruhe (KB) solution (70mM potassium aspartate, 40mM KCl, 15mM KH2PO4, 10mM glucose, 10mM taurine, 0.5mM EGTA, 10mM sodium pyruvate, 10mM HEPES, 5mM BDM, 0.5% BSA, pH 7.4), and fixed for 10 min at room temperature (RT) by adding an equal volume of 8% PFA. Following fixation, cell suspensions were spun down at 300 x g for 2 min and resuspended in Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS). In the present manuscript, we report a high efficiency (∼90–95%) of recovering healthy rod-shaped cardiomyocytes. CMs that are balled up or fraying at the ends are compromised and should be excluded from subsequent analysis. Therefore, minimizing these unhealthy spherically shaped CMs is crucial to obtaining quality data. During the Langendorff perfusion there are several steps that can help ensure the highest quality cell preparation: 1) speed from euthanasia to hung heart; with practice a user gets faster at this step; 2) correct pH and temperature of the buffers; 3) correct placement of the cannula within the aorta; if the cannula is inserted too deeply into the aorta such that it passes the first branch of the coronary artery then the perfusion buffer will simply fill the left ventricle lumen and not perfuse through the vasculature; and 4) minimizing bubbles that enter the system.

Immunostaining of single-cell suspensions

Fixed ventricular cell suspensions underwent acid hydrolysis using 2 M HCl in PBS with 0.1% Tween 20 for 30 min at RT and were washed twice in PBS with spins at 300 x g for 3 min, followed by antigen retrieval using 1x sodium citrate buffer (6.7 mM Sodium citrate dihydrate, 0.1% Triton X-100, 0.05% Tween 20, pH 6.0) for 20 min at 95°C. Cells were then blocked with 10% normal goat serum (NGS, Thermo Fisher, catalog # 50062Z) and 0.1% Triton X-100 for 1 h at RT. Cells were incubated with primary antibody for rabbit anti-myosin light chain 2 (Myl2,1:250, Abcam ab79935) and rat anti-BrdU (1:250, Abcam ab6326) in blocking solution overnight at 4°C. Cells were then washed twice in PBS and incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit and Alexa Fluor 647 goat anti-rat secondary (1:500, Thermo Fisher, A11029 and Abcam, ab150159) in PBS for 1 h at RT. After 1 PBS wash, cells were stained with Click-it EdU kit Alexa Fluor 555 (Thermo Fisher, C10339) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were then washed and incubated with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI 1 mg/mL, 1:1000) in diH2O for 10 min at RT. Cells were washed two times in diH2O, spun one final time, resuspended with Prolong Gold (Thermo Fisher, P26930), pipetted across a slide such that cells were dispersed and not overlaping, and cover slipped.

Staining procedure for intercalated disc and nuclear CM markers

Fixed ventricular cell suspensions underwent antigen retrieval using 1x Sodium Citrate Buffer for 20 min at 65°C. Cells were then blocked with 10% normal goat serum (NGS, Thermo Fisher, catalog # 50062Z) and 0.01% Triton X-100 for 1 h at RT. In the case of any staining combination incorporating the NKX2-5 antibody, blocking buffer instead consisted of 5% normal donkey serum (NDS, Jackson ImmunoResearch 017-000-121) and 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, VWR 97061-420) in 1x PBS rather than 10% NGS. CMs were incubated with primary antibodies in blocking solution overnight at 4°C. Primary antibodies included mouse anti-cTnT (1:500, Abcam ab8295), rabbit anti-Myl2 (1:250, Abcam ab79935), mouse anti-N-Cad (1:250, Invitrogen 33–3900), rabbit anti-CX43 (1:500, Abcam ab11370), rabbit anti-PCM1 (1:250, Sigma HPA023370), and goat anti-NKX2-5 (1:250, Abcam ab106923). Cells were then washed twice in PBS and incubated with an appropriate Alexa Fluor secondary (1:500) in 1xPBS for 1 h at RT. Secondaries included 555 goat anti-rabbit (Thermo Fisher A21429), 647 goat anti-mouse (Abcam ab150115), 647 goat anti-rabbit (Thermo Fisher A21245), and 488 goat anti-mouse (Thermo Fisher A11029). Cells were then washed and incubated with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI 1 mg/mL, 1:1000) in diH2O for 10 min at RT. Cells were washed two times in diH2O, spun one final time, resuspended with Prolong Gold (Thermo Fisher, P26930), pipetted across a slide, and cover slipped. For any staining combinations including NKX2-5 antibody, prior to cover slipping, but after DAPI staining, cells were incubated with 0.03% Sudan black B (SBB) dissolved in 70% ethanol for 20 min at RT to quench autofluorescence, followed by two diH2O washes.

Immunostaining of tissue sections

Hearts were extracted and hung on a Langendorff apparatus and perfused first with 5 mL of calcium-free Tyrode’s buffer followed by 5 mL of ice-cold 4% PFA. Hearts were then stored in 4% PFA overnight at 4°C (∼12-18hrs). After washing with PBS, hearts were stored in 70% EtOH until further processing took place. Briefly, hearts were dehydrated by progressive introduction of EtOH and cleared with xylene prior to being embedded in paraffin wax. Embedded tissues were sectioned in a 4-chamber orientation from ventral to dorsal side on a Thermo Microm HM 355S microtome at 4 μm thickness. 5 sections, approximately 500 μm apart were collected on a slide for each animal.

Tissue sections were deparaffinized in a 10-min xylene wash, and rehydrated using the following sequential ethanol washes for 10mins each: 100%, 90%, and 70% EtOH, followed by 10 min in H2O. A 30-min antigen retrieval was performed by incubating the slides in sodium citrate buffer with 0.1% Triton X-100 and 0.05% Tween 20, pH 6.0 heated to 95–100°C, followed by a 15-min cool down and 10-min H2O wash. Slides were blocked with 10% normal goat serum (NGS, Thermo Fisher, catalog # 50062Z) for 1 h at RT. Primary antibodies, rat anti-BrdU (1:250, Abcam ab6326) and rabbit anti-PCM1 (1:250, Sigma HPA023370) were diluted in blocking buffer and incubated on tissue sections overnight at 4°C. Slides were washed 2 × 10 min in 1x PBS and incubated with secondary antibodies Alexa Fluor goat anti-rat 647 (1:500; A21434) and goat anti-rabbit 555 (1:500; A21429) diluted in 1x PBS for 1 h at RT. Slides were washed in 1x PBS and EdU incorporation was labeled using the Click-iT EdU Alexa Fluor 488 kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, C10339) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After washing in PBS, tissues were incubated in 0.03% Sudan Black B (SBB) dissolved in 70% EtOH for 20 min at RT and washed again with PBS. Tissues were then incubated with DAPI at 10 μg/mL in PBS for 5 min at RT followed by 2 PBS washes. Slides were cover-slipped using Prolong-Gold (Thermo Fisher Scientific, P26930) and allowed to dry in the dark. All 5 sections for each strain (spanning about 2,500μm collectively) were scanned in their entirety for double-positive cells using a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 fluorescent microscope and images were captured at 20x with an ORCA Fusion Hamamatsu Camera. Locations of each discovered double-positive cell were noted by the scanner.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Ploidy analysis and assessment of cell division

Following staining, slides were manually scanned in their entirety for all EdU+, BrdU+, and double-positive CMs using a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 fluorescent microscope with 20× objective. Total thymidine-labeled CMs were quantified and nucleation status of the thymidine-labeled CMs was noted. While scanning, images of thymidine-positive CMs pictures were captured with a 10× objective and a Hamamatsu Orca camera. Nuclear ploidy was analyzed using NIS elements software using the “Binary > Define Threshold” function allowing all nuclei to be traced by setting the lower threshold of DAPI stain to a number that fully enveloped each nucleus. The “SumIntensity” object feature of thymidine-labeled nuclei along with known diploid nuclei was exported to excel. The SumIntensity of a single nucleus was normalized to the average of the SumIntensities of the known diploid population gathered from the same picture and multiplied by a factor of 2 resulting in an estimate of the nuclear ploidy of any given nucleus. Normalized values ranging from 1.25–2.85 were deemed 2N, 3.15–5.85 were deemed 4N, 5.95–11.0 were deemed 8N, and >11.95 were deemed 16N. If a nucleus falls in between two bins, it is excluded from the analysis for being ambiguous. The two independent measurements, nuclear ploidy and nucleation status were combined to calculate the total ploidy of a cell (i.e., 1 × 2N = 2N; 1 × 4N and 2 × 2N cells = 4N; etc). Statistical significance of total thymidine-analog labeled cells (as in Figure 4C) was calculated by a two-way ANOVA with Šidák’s post hoc test. Statistical significance of ploidy classes was calculated by a two-way MANOVA with LSD post hoc analysis.

Assessment of CM cell dimensions

Cell dimension analysis of thymidine negative and positive CMs were performed on the same images taken for ploidy analysis. Here, the “Binary > Define Threshold” function of NIS elements software was used to threshold or trace the cellular area of a CM based on the sarcomeric stain. Again, the lower threshold of the Myl2 stain was set to ensure that CMs were fully traced. Here, the Length, Width, and Area of the Myl2 stain were the object features selected for export. Approximately 100 binucleated thymidine negative cells and all thymidine positive cells were measured for length, width, and area. Statistical significance was calculated using a Student’s T-test.

Quantification of miscalls

CMs stained as described above were imaged using a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 fluorescent microscope with an ORCA Fusion Hamamatsu camera. Captured images were opened in NIS Elements software and nuclei were thresholded by DAPI intensity as if performing standard nucleation and ploidy analysis as described. For non-CM nuclei miscalling, three independent analyzers flagged presumed CM nuclei with only DAPI and CM autofluorescence or cTnT visible. After identifying all suspected CM nuclei, the Pcm1 or Nkx2-5 channels were made visible and nuclei that were previously flagged but proved to be negative for either nuclear marker were noted. The same strategy was employed for assessment of fragmentation and under-digestion, but with the intercalated disc markers (CX43 and N-Cad) made visible only after investigators had made their initial call. Specifically, investigators looked for cases where CX43 was not present at both ends (fragmentation) or was present in the cell body, between two or more nuclei (under-digestion).
