Our house believes that: The clipped lymph node is the true sentinel node after neoadjuvant chemotherapy in N+ patients☆
Authors: Benigno Acea-Nebril, Alejandra García-Novoa, Alberto Bouzón Alejandro, Carlota Díaz Carballada, Carmen Conde Iglesias
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAxillary staging is an important prognostic factor in breast cancer, being sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) the gold standard staging method in early stages. However, in clinically node positive (cN+) patients who converted to clinically node-negative (cN0) after primary systemic therapy (PST) the axillary staging method during surgery remains controversial. There are at least three validated methods: SLNB, targeted axillary dissection (TAD) and marking axillary nodes with radioactive iodine seeds (MARI) procedure. Our house believe that the biopsied and clipped lymph node could predict response to systemic treatment.

We have read with interest the articles by Dr. Galimberti et al. [1] and Van Hemert et al. [2] discussing the efficacy of SLNB, TAD and MARI procedure to determine axillary nodal status after neoadjuvant systemic treatment in patients with breast cancer and lymph node involvement at diagnosis. According to Galimberti [1], the efforts involved in TAD do not translate into an improved disease prognosis or a reduction in axillary recurrences. To this end, various studies including ours, with follow-up longer than 10 years, have concluded that SLNB alone in selected patients with an excellent response to chemotherapy is a rational choice and not inferior in oncological terms to axillary lymph node dissection in the medium to long-term follow-up. In contrast, Van Hemert [2] considers that the MARI technique requires only an invasive procedure before chemotherapy and a lymph node extraction following systemic therapy, while for the SLNB and TAD techniques, 2–4 lymph nodes are extracted, with increased local morbidity (Lymphedema risk) [3]. In our opinion, Galimberti [1] and Van Hemert [2] are talking about the same lymph node because the clipped lymph node is the sentinel node marked by the disease. The reality is that if we accept the sentinel node definition as the first lymph node to receive afferent lymphatic drainage from a primary tumor, therefore it is the node with highest probability of being affected by the disease, we should recognize that, for women with metastatic involvement of the axilla, the disease selected their sentinel node. This sentinel node chosen by the disease undergoes changes due to the chemotherapy but remains the sentinel node, although another marker (such as Tc99 or patent blue) can mark a different sentinel node due to the phenomena of lymphatic obstruction. Various studies using the TAD technique have demonstrated a high correlation between the clipped lymph node and the sentinel node. In our experience, from the prospective study that includes patients with cN1 axillary involvement and exceptionally cN2 patients with complete radiological response, the correlation between the clipped lymph node and the sentinel node is about 80 % [[4], [5], [6]], which shows a direct relationship between the marked lymph node and sentinel node. The absence of complete concordance between the clipped node and the sentinel node shows a possible lymphatic blockage in this group of patients (cN1).

We should therefore reconsider our position and not view the postchemotherapy axillary study as a restaging of the disease but rather as a tool to decide on the rest of the adjuvant therapies. The extirpation of the marked metastasized lymph node should predict the residual disease, which would make marking the sentinel node unnecessary. Therefore, once this lymph node (the one selected by the disease) has been identified, the important step is to determine the type of axillary therapeutic response to the chemotherapy. According to Caudle [7], the false negative rate with extirpation alone of the marked lymph node is 4.2 %, i.e., similar to that accepted for SLNB at primary surgery. This new philosophy leads us to precision surgery (remove only the marked lymph node), which is based on a biological fact: the lymph node's metastatic involvement. In contrast, the criteria for postchemotherapy SLNB is random surgery because it is based on a statistical fact for reducing the risk of false negatives using double marking and the identification of more than 2 lymph nodes, which could be the sentinel lymph nodes chosen by the isotope, but not necessarily by the disease. Most patients who undergo neoadjuvant systemic treatment are HER2 enriched or triple negative. Both subtypes have a high probability of a complete pathologic response in the axilla, and axillary treatments might be unnecessary.

The ultimate objective of postchemotherapy lymph node staging is to determine the axillary response and, if possible, prevent axillary lymph node dissection. Therefore, the important thing is not to categorize which is the best sentinel lymph node, but rather to know the effectiveness of systemic treatment on extramammary disease, to assess the rest of the adjuvant treatments. This is particularly important for systemic treatment, since lymph node radiotherapy is indicated in all cN + patients at diagnosis treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Given that we do not affect survival with an axillary lymph node dissection, our objective is to improve these patients' quality of life by suppressing axillary lymphadenectomy using a tailored axillary treatment, with the least possible morbidity. Extirpation of the biopsied and clipped lymph node, MARI procedure, could therefore be sufficient for verifying this response to systemic treatment because it is the lymph node that the disease selected as the sentinel node. This patient's overall survival will not depend on the radicality of the surgery in the axilla but rather on the efficacy of the systemic therapies. After all, the most important thing will be to confirm oncological outcome in terms of survival in each of the staging procedures.

In conclusion, the objective of the axillary study after neoadjuvant chemotherapy in patients with a clinically positive axilla at diagnosis (cN1) is to determine adjuvant treatments according to the response to NAC. Therefore, we must perform the procedure that produces the least morbidity, at the lowest cost, that allows the same overall and disease-free survival. We need more studies to confirm that removing only the clipped lymph node meets all these characteristics.
