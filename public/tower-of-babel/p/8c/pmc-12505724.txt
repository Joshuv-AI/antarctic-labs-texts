Absorbable versus non-absorbable sutures in upper eyelid blepharoplasty: a systematic review of clinical outcomes and follow-up burden
Authors: Amirabbas Safaripour, Sina Baghi Keshtan, Elahe Boumeri, Motahare Alisofi, Ali Rabiei, Shahin Dehvari, Mahmoud Reza Rahimi Barghani, Akram Soltanzadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUpper eyelid blepharoplasty is a common surgical procedure requiring meticulous wound closure. The choice between absorbable and non-absorbable sutures remains debated, with implications for clinical outcomes, patient comfort, and postoperative follow-up burden.ObjectiveThis systematic review aims to compare absorbable and non-absorbable sutures in upper eyelid blepharoplasty, focusing on clinical outcomes (scar quality, complications) and postoperative burden (follow-up visits, discomfort).MethodFollowing PRISMA 2020 guidelines, a comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. Included studies were randomized controlled trials, prospective/retrospective cohort studies, or case series directly comparing suture types. Data were extracted and synthesized qualitatively due to heterogeneity.ResultsEleven studies met inclusion criteria. Absorbable and non-absorbable sutures demonstrated equivalent scar quality and complication rates. While absorbable sutures theoretically reduce follow-up burden by eliminating removal needs, no included study provided quantitative data on visit numbers or resource utilization. A potential reduction in patient discomfort is often cited, but was also not quantitatively measured. Suture technique (running vs. interrupted) influenced early edema but not long-term outcomes. Risk factors for adverse events were technique-dependent rather than suture material-dependent.ConclusionBoth suture types are clinically viable, with absorbable sutures offering logistical advantages. The choice should consider patient preferences and surgical context. Future research should quantify follow-up burden and evaluate newer suture technologies.Keywords: Blepharoplasty, Absorbable sutures, Non-absorbable sutures, Postoperative care, Scar quality, Systematic review

Introduction

Upper eyelid blepharoplasty is a high-volume aesthetic and functional surgical procedure that relies on subtle soft tissue handling and meticulous wound closure to achieve optimal results and patient satisfaction [1, 2]. Advances in suture technology have offered surgeons a range of materials for cutaneous closure, broadly categorized as absorbable (dissolvable) and non-absorbable (removable) sutures [3, 4]. Historically, non-absorbable options such as polypropylene and nylon were considered standard owing to their reliable wound support and handling properties; however, their need for postoperative removal mandates an additional clinic visit and introduces the potential for patient discomfort [5, 6]. Conversely, absorbable sutures—including fast-absorbing catgut and newer synthetic alternatives—dissolve within days, obviating suture removal and potentially streamlining postoperative care [5, 7, 8].

The comparative efficacy and safety of absorbable versus non-absorbable sutures in upper eyelid blepharoplasty have been debated for decades. Multiple randomized, split-eyelid trials and prospective cohort studies have failed to identify significant differences in scar quality or complication rates between suture types when modern materials and protocols are used [5, 7, 8]. Nevertheless, the discrete impact of suture selection on clinical outcomes such as wound healing, patient comfort, and aesthetic appraisal, as well as on health system factors such as postoperative visit burden and resource utilization, remains incompletely synthesized. Patient discomfort associated with suture removal—while frequently cited as a rationale for absorbable suture use—is often undermeasured or variably reported [5, 8]. Furthermore, studies rarely quantify the actual reduction in follow-up workload or clinic visits enabled by absorbable materials, representing a persistent gap in the literature [5, 7, 8].

Given the increasing emphasis on patient-centered outcomes and health system efficiency, a rigorous, up-to-date synthesis of evidence is warranted [9, 10]. The landscape of blepharoplasty technique and aftercare is further complicated by variations in closure technique (e.g., running versus interrupted), the introduction of validated patient-reported and observer-graded outcome tools [7, 11, 12], and a renewed focus on the predictors of adverse healing events [13]. These factors have collectively shifted the standard for comparative evaluation beyond basic wound healing or cosmesis to include the broader patient and practitioner experience.

The purpose of this study is to systematically review the available literature comparing absorbable and non-absorbable cutaneous suture materials in upper eyelid blepharoplasty, focusing on clinical outcomes (including scar quality, complications, and patient-reported satisfaction) and postoperative follow-up burden (including theoretical implications for visit frequency, patient discomfort, and resource utilization). By synthesizing contemporary evidence, this review aims to inform best practices and highlight areas for future investigation in eyelid surgical care.

Two independent reviewers screened all titles and abstracts against the predefined eligibility criteria. Full-text articles were then obtained for all potentially eligible studies and assessed for final inclusion. Disagreements were resolved by discussion or by a third reviewer. Any uncertainties regarding study design or outcome reporting were clarified by consensus.

Summary of included studies

Comparative clinical outcomes of absorbable vs. non-absorbable sutures in upper eyelid blepharoplasty

The comparative clinical outcomes of absorbable versus non-absorbable sutures for cutaneous wound closure in upper eyelid blepharoplasty are consistently found to be equivalent in the robust studies available (Table 3). Randomized, split-eyelid and prospective comparative trials [5, 7, 8] report no meaningful differences in final scar quality, wound healing, or overall aesthetic appearance between the two suture classes when modern materials and standardized closure protocols are used. Both absorbable and non-absorbable sutures yield similarly low rates of serious complications such as wound dehiscence, infection, and granuloma. Short-term differences—such as early edema or ecchymosis—appear to be more affected by suturing technique (e.g., interrupted vs. running) than by whether the suture is absorbable or not [7]. Importantly, absorbable sutures offer the secondary benefits of obviating the need for suture removal and reducing associated discomfort, though quantitative data on this are limited.

Comparative clinical outcomes of absorbable vs. Non-Absorbable sutures in upper eyelid blepharoplasty

While some studies suggest a slight aesthetic preference or marginal reduction in removal-related discomfort favoring absorbable sutures, these effects do not alter the overall equivalence in clinically important outcomes [5, 8]. Large observational case series and additional direct comparisons [16, 17] further reinforce this consensus, although detailed outcome data are limited by lack of full abstracts. In summary, with appropriate technique and follow-up, both suture types deliver safe, effective, and cosmetically acceptable results for upper eyelid blepharoplasty.

Impact of suture type on postoperative Follow-Up burden and resource utilization

The type of suture used has a clear theoretical implication for postoperative follow-up burden, as non-absorbable sutures require a dedicated clinic visit for removal, while absorbable sutures do not. However, the available literature does not quantitatively measure this outcome. Non-absorbable sutures require patients to return for a dedicated clinic visit for suture removal—typically on postoperative day 5–7. It is therefore logical that this would increase both patient inconvenience and the utilization of clinical resources [5, 8]. Conversely, it is hypothesized that absorbable sutures, which dissolve spontaneously, could reduce follow-up demands. Notably, none of the retrieved studies provided explicit quantitative data on the number of follow-up visits, time expenditure, or associated costs. The requirement for a removal visit is inherent to non-absorbable sutures, but its quantitative impact on healthcare systems and patients remains unmeasured in the available literature on blepharoplasty.

Regarding patient discomfort, it is frequently suggested that absorbable sutures avoid the discomfort associated with suture removal, but this has rarely been measured quantitatively [5, 8]. While one prospective study [5] indicated a qualitative preference for absorbables due to less discomfort, the actual magnitude was not reported. Similarly, none of the comparative studies detailed explicit cost/resource analyses. Therefore, while the theoretical advantages of absorbable sutures are clear, this review found no quantitative evidence to confirm a reduction in follow-up burden or resource utilization (Table 4).

Influence of suture technique on aesthetic outcomes, complications, and patient satisfaction

Different suture techniques, specifically running versus interrupted closure, can have distinct impacts on early postoperative healing and short-term aesthetics in upper eyelid blepharoplasty, though these differences generally do not translate into significant variations in long-term scar quality or patient satisfaction (Table 5). For example, Aydemir et al. (2022) [7] conducted a split-eyelid prospective study and found that within both absorbable (vicryl) and non-absorbable (polypropylene) suture groups, interrupted sutures were associated with less ecchymosis and edema in the first postoperative week compared to running sutures. However, by three months postoperatively, scar quality as assessed by the Manchester Scar Scale was equivalent regardless of technique or suture type.

Impact of suture technique (Running vs. Interrupted) on outcomes in upper eyelid blepharoplasty

Other studies, including larger randomized trials focused on technique [11, 12], similarly report that while running and subcuticular (a variation of running) techniques may offer theoretical efficiency, neither technique consistently outperforms the other in patient-reported satisfaction or blinded scar outcome grading. Adverse events and complication rates, such as infection, significant edema, or poor healing, are also low and do not differ significantly between running and interrupted closures [11–13]. These findings suggest that suture technique choice can be tailored to the surgeon’s preference and clinical context, with either approach yielding safe and cosmetically satisfactory results in the long term.

Contextual findings from studies on suture technique

To contextualize the findings from the primary suture material analysis, the influence of suturing technique was examined separately. As noted, three technique-only studies provided robust evidence on this relationship [11–13]. It is important to reiterate that these studies did not compare suture materials but were included to assess technique as a potential confounder.

As shown in Table 5, Aydemir et al. [7] (a study also included in the primary analysis) and others [11, 12] consistently found that while technique (e.g., interrupted vs. running) can significantly influence short-term outcomes like edema and ecchymosis, it does not result in significant differences in long-term scar quality or patient satisfaction. For instance, Told et al. [12] and Vasović et al. [11] both used non-absorbable sutures in all patients and found no difference in outcomes between different closure techniques. It demonstrates that the suturing technique is a more significant determinant of early healing than the choice of suture material. Therefore, the equivalent long-term outcomes observed between absorbable and non-absorbable sutures in the primary analysis are likely genuine and not masked by technical variations between studies.

Predictors and risk factors for adverse outcomes in upper eyelid blepharoplasty

Adverse outcomes following upper eyelid blepharoplasty—such as significant postoperative edema, infection, or hypertrophic scarring—are uncommon, but several clinical series and recent studies have identified important predictors and risk factors (Table 6). The largest and most contemporary series, such as Park et al. (2024) [13], found that surgical technique plays a major role: specifically, blepharoplasty accompanied by mini crease enhancement or use of closure techniques incorporating the levator aponeurosis was associated with a substantially higher rate of clinically significant eyelid edema (CSEE). Logistic regression confirmed that these technical modifications, rather than suture material, were independent predictors of persistent swelling. Additionally, the study controlled for race, another variable that can influence healing and adverse event risk, but found technique to be a stronger predictor in their cohort.

Studies such as Joshi et al. (2007) [17]—an 800-case series—support that while suture material is less often implicated, surgeon experience, meticulous technique, and perioperative care are key to preventing infection, wound dehiscence, or abnormal scarring. Rare complications like granuloma or milia may also be affected by materials and closure style, though large series do not identify consistent patient-level predictors. Broader observational and qualitative evidence [6, 18] further emphasize that early suture removal can lower the risk of track marks or skin irritation, while delayed removal or poorly executed closure can increase minor adverse outcomes. Overall, patient-specific predictors are seldom highlighted, while surgical technique and perioperative management features (timely suture removal, avoidance of excessive tension, and minimizing foreign body load) predominate as identified risk factors in the literature.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesizes evidence from comparative studies on suture materials in upper eyelid blepharoplasty. The consistent finding across the literature is that, when modern materials and protocols are used, absorbable and non-absorbable sutures yield equivalent clinical outcomes in terms of long-term scar quality and complication rates (e.g., wound dehiscence, infection, granuloma) [5, 7, 8]. Studies by Scaccia et al. [5], Aydemir et al. [7], and Greene et al. [8]—utilizing robust split-eyelid and randomized designs—all reported no meaningful objective differences. It is critical to underscore, however, that this conclusion of equivalence is based on a limited, heterogeneous, and partially outdated body of evidence and should be interpreted as a tentative consensus rather than a definitive, high-strength recommendation.

The primary differentiator between suture types is their impact on postoperative logistics and the patient experience. Non-absorbable sutures necessitate a dedicated clinic visit for removal, increasing the burden on patients and healthcare systems [5, 8]. In contrast, absorbable sutures eliminate this visit, potentially reducing follow-up demands and enabling remote post-operative care. A secondary benefit is the avoidance of the specific, albeit often modest, discomfort associated with suture removal [5, 8]. It is important to note that these perceived advantages, while logically sound, are not substantiated by quantitative data in the current literature, as no included study formally measured the reduction in clinic visits, resource utilization, or patient discomfort using validated scales.

Suturing technique appears to impact early postoperative outcomes more than suture material class itself. Aydemir et al. [7] performed a unique intra-patient comparison using both interrupted and running cutaneous techniques with both suture types. Their results indicate that interrupted closure resulted in less immediate postoperative edema and ecchymosis than running sutures, particularly within the first postoperative week. Importantly, the choice of absorbable versus non-absorbable material did not significantly affect scar outcomes at 3 months. This observation suggests that, for upper eyelid blepharoplasty, meticulous attention to surgical technique may contribute more substantially to short-term aesthetic outcomes than the selection of suture material.

Other prospective and randomized studies focused on technique rather than suture class [11, 12]. Both confirmed that within non-absorbable suture cohorts, different closure strategies (e.g., running cutaneous vs. subcuticular) did not result in significant differences in patient-reported outcome measures (FACE-Q), nor did they impact adverse effects or satisfaction at early (1 week) and late (3 month) time points. These findings reinforce the role of surgical precision and proper technique as critical determinants of patient outcome.

The risk of significant adverse outcomes following upper eyelid blepharoplasty remains low and appears to be determined primarily by patient and operative factors beyond suture choice. Park et al. [13], in a large contemporary series, used multivariable analysis to examine predictors of clinically significant eyelid edema. Their findings did not implicate suture material per se as a risk modifier; rather, certain closure techniques (incorporating deep tissue planes), variations in crease formation, and potentially patient-level variables (e.g., race) were more predictive of postoperative edema. The majority of earlier prospective trials similarly reported low rates of clinically relevant complications, with no observed effect of absorbable vs. non-absorbable closure on infection or hypertrophic scarring [5, 7, 8].

The safety profile of either suture class, therefore, is well established within the paradigm of early removal (for non-absorbables) and careful technique. The low complication rates observed across studies support the ongoing acceptance of both materials in routine clinical practice [5, 7, 8, 13].

A major strength of the current literature is the repeated use of split-eyelid (intra-patient controlled), randomized designs [5, 7, 8], which minimize inter-individual confounding and increase power to detect clinically relevant differences. The application of validated outcome measures (e.g., Manchester Scar Scale, FACE-Q), blinded photographic review, and robust statistical analysis enhances confidence in observed outcomes [7, 11, 12].

However, limitations include a persistent lack of explicit, quantitative data on follow-up burden and resource use. Patient discomfort with suture removal is often qualitatively described but rarely measured with validated scales, and reporting on rare but meaningful complications (e.g., milia, granuloma) is variable. Furthermore, newer suture technologies (e.g., barbed, antimicrobial-coated absorbables) remain understudied in this context.

Collectively, these findings suggest that the choice between absorbable and non-absorbable sutures for upper eyelid skin closure after blepharoplasty should be individualized, with particular attention to patient preference for follow-up convenience and suture removal discomfort. Clinical outcomes are essentially equivalent when either material is used appropriately, and specific suture techniques tailored to minimize edema/track marks and maximize early cosmesis may be most impactful. Future research should focus on the quantification of postoperative resource utilization, the application of validated patient-reported outcome measures for discomfort, and the comparative effectiveness of novel suture materials.

The strength of the conclusion regarding suture equivalence is tempered by several factors. First, the total number of patients across all included studies remains modest, limiting the statistical power to detect small but potentially clinically significant differences. Second, significant heterogeneity was observed in study designs (RCTs, prospective cohorts, retrospective analyses), follow-up durations, and outcome measurement tools, precluding meta-analysis. Third, some influential studies [5, 8] are now over two decades old, and their findings may not fully generalize to contemporary surgical settings and modern suture formulations. While the consistency of the ‘no difference’ finding across these varied studies is reassuring, the body of evidence as a whole must be considered preliminary and indicative rather than conclusive.

Limitations

Despite its rigorous methodology, this review has several limitations. First, the heterogeneity in study designs, outcome measures, and reporting standards across included studies precluded a quantitative meta-analysis, necessitating a qualitative synthesis. Second, many studies lacked detailed quantitative data on postoperative follow-up burden, resource utilization, and patient discomfort, relying instead on qualitative descriptions. Third, the review primarily focused on older suture materials (e.g., catgut, polypropylene), with limited data on newer absorbable synthetics or advanced techniques (e.g., barbed sutures). Fourth, although our inclusion criteria initially required a direct comparison of suture materials, we made a post hoc decision to include three studies [11–13] that compared only suturing techniques. This was done to provide the necessary context for interpreting the role of technique as a confounder. While this provides a broader understanding, it introduces an element of heterogeneity into the review. However, these studies are clearly differentiated from the primary evidence base in our synthesis. Additionally, the long-term outcomes beyond three months were underreported, and potential biases in smaller or non-randomized studies may affect the generalizability of the findings. Future research must address these gaps by employing standardized outcome measures, longer follow-up periods, and direct comparisons of emerging suture technologies in contemporary, adequately powered trials. Specifically, studies should be designed to quantitatively evaluate patient-reported discomfort and resource utilization, which remain the most significant unmeasured differentiators between suture types.

Conclusion

This systematic review suggests that absorbable and non-absorbable sutures yield equivalent clinical outcomes in upper eyelid blepharoplasty. However, this finding is constrained by a limited, heterogeneous, and partially outdated body of evidence. The primary perceived advantage of absorbable sutures is the elimination of suture removal, thereby reducing logistical burden and patient discomfort; crucially, however, these benefits lack quantitative validation.

To resolve this uncertainty and inform evidence-based practice, there is a pressing need for contemporary, adequately powered, randomized controlled trials. These future studies must employ standardized outcome measures to directly compare suture types. Specifically, they should incorporate validated patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) to quantify discomfort and rigorous health economic analyses to measure resource utilization (e.g., number of follow-up visits, cost, time burden). Until such high-quality data are available, the choice between suture types should be guided by patient preference, surgical context, and surgeon expertise, with the understanding that both options remain clinically viable.
