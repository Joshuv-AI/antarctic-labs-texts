Heterogeneous and evolving epitope landscape of clinical anti-drug antibodies against multidomain biotherapeutic: a case study of TAK-186
Authors: Ruoxuan Sun, Janey Ronxhi, Mark G Qian, Zheng Zha, Bin Li, Xiaobin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe emergence of anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) poses a major obstacle in the clinical development of therapeutic proteins (TPs) such as monoclonal antibodies and their derivatives. While standard multitiered ADA assays and neutralizing antibody assays offer valuable insights into the humoral immunogenicity risks of TPs, they are not sufficient to provide in-depth knowledge such as ADA epitope specificities. For complex multidomain biotherapeutics (MDBs), ADAs targeting individual domains can elicit distinct pharmacological effects. Therefore, it is crucial to implement straightforward and reliable methodologies to deconvolute ADA epitope profiles of MDBs. Herein, we report a case study using domain specificity analysis, linear peptide scanning and bioinformatic B cell epitope prediction to unveil the clinical ADA epitope landscape of TAK-186, a multidomain T cell engager that has been discontinued from clinical development. By applying this workflow, we observed strong domain specificity variability among patient samples. Furthermore, the data showed that many patients demonstrated evolved ADA epitope specificities throughout the course of the treatment. Several potential linear epitopes were identified subsequently through experimental and computational approaches. Overall, we presented in this study a practical strategy to elucidate and potentially mitigate the immunogenicity liabilities of complex biotherapeutics.KEYWORDS: Anti-drug antibodies, bioanalysis, domain specificity, epitope mapping, immunogenicity, multidomain biotherapeutics, peptide screening

Introduction

Therapeutic proteins (TPs) have emerged as a favored category of treatments because of its high efficacy and specificity, as well as ideal tolerability. However, the administration of exogenous biologics may provoke unwanted immune responses primarily through the T cell-dependent generation of anti-drug antibodies (ADAs). ADAs elicit multidimensional clinical effects, including the reduction of efficacy,1 alteration of drug exposure, pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics (PK/PD) properties2,3 and induction of immune-related adverse events.4 Given the significant and often unpredictable implications of immunogenicity risks associated with TPs, researchers are mandated to closely monitor the immune responses induced by TPs throughout the life cycle of development by implementing risk-based immunogenicity assessment strategies.5,6

Multidomain biotherapeutics (MDBs) are a unique class of genetically engineered TPs that are composed of two or more functional domains. To date, numerous MDBs of various structural categories such as T cell engagers (TCEs), bispecific antibodies, and Fc-fusion proteins have been approved for clinical use. To achieve superior tissue specificity, some MDBs are designed as prodrugs with conditional activation mechanisms by incorporating masking elements.7 MDBs as an advantageous therapeutic modality possess intricate mechanisms that cannot be replicated by single-element agents alone; however, their underlying immunogenicity risks are significantly amplified owing to the addition of new intradomain or interdomain epitopes, potential antigen spreading and immune complex formation.8,9 Since each domain of an MDB confers unique pharmacological functions, it is foreseeable that the different domain-specific ADAs may elicit unidentical impacts on the efficacy and safety profiles of MDBs. Moreover, since MDBs contain multiple target binding moieties, which confers to more extensive epitope diversity than monoclonal antibodies, they may have more chances to form large, multimeric immune complexes with ADAs due to avidity effect, which potentially triggers amplified exposure loss and toxicity risk. In some cases, ADAs may cross-react with human endogenous proteins that share significant homology with the targeted domains of ADAs. Taken all possible negative impacts together, it is recommended by regulatory agencies to perform the fit-for-purpose, data-driven domain specificity analysis (DSA) of anti-MDB ADAs in the context of risk-based immunogenicity evaluation.5,8,9

Beyond domain specificity, high-resolution epitope information offers more direct insights enabling drug developers to pinpoint the source of risks and, more importantly, develop next-generation molecules with mitigated immunogenicity liabilities. Also, it has been realized that the clinical incidence and titer of ADAs is not always correlated with the severity of clinical implications, suggesting that additional readouts such as affinity and epitope landscape of ADAs play pivotal roles in shaping the humoral anti-therapeutic immunity.10–12 A variety of experimental approaches can be used to map the epitope profiles of polyclonal antibodies (pAbs) as reviewed in our previous literature.13 In silico epitope identification, which has undergone dramatic advancements in the past years, is increasingly utilized by researchers in drug and vaccine development. In general, a unique, integrated strategy needs to be implemented for each biotherapeutic to unmask the complicated landscape of ADA epitopes.

The MDB investigated in this study is TAK-186 (also referred to as MVC-101), a 95 kDa MDB known as a Conditional Bispecific Redirected Activation (COBRATM) TCE. Unlike other types of TCEs such as the single-chain variable fragment (scFv)-based Bi-specific T-cell engager (also termed BiTE®) or IgG form, TAK-186 is constructed by fusion of two segments tandemly (Figure 1(A)), namely the active element and the associated element. The active element contains two variable heavy domains (VHHs) of heavy chain against epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) (designated as a1 and a2 subdomain), and an active scFv against CD3ε (αCD3 scFv, composed of subdomain b and c). The associated element contains the inactive anti-CD3 domain (iαCD3 scFv, composed of subdomain d and e) with mutated complementarity-determining regions (CDRs), and a VHH against human serum albumin (HSA) for half-life extension.14 The conditionality of TAK-186 is achieved based on the local enrichment of matrix metalloproteinases (MMP) in the tumor microenvironment. Basically, the VH and VL of scFv are connected by short linkers, which prohibit the formation of Fab-like dimer with CD3 binding capacity. Under native conditions, the inactive scFv dimerize intramolecularly with the active counterpart and prohibit T cell engagement. Upon proteolytic cleavage between subdomains 4 and 5, the prodrug undergoes structural rearrangement, which enables the formation of active, dimerized TCE (containing two CD3 binding sites and four EGFR binding sites) to selectively eradicate EGFR-expressing tumor cells (Figure 1(B)).14–16 Notably, the tertiary structure of intact TAK-186 was not fully determined due to the dynamic nature and structural flexibility of the molecule. The proposed conformation, in which only the VH of αCD3 scFv (subdomain b) dimerizes with the VL of the iαCD3 scFv (subdomain d), was established based on limited experimental evidence.15 It is likely that other additional conformations of uncleaved TAK-186 also exist in solution.

The active element (subdomain a1, b, c, a2) is linked with the associated element (subdomain d, e, f) by a MMP2/9-cleavable spacer. (B) Mechanism of tumor-selective activation of TAK-186. From the first-in-human, open-labeled, Phase 1/2 study (NCT04844073), we noticed that some patients developed treatment-emergent anti-TAK-186 antibodies after multiple cycles of drug administration, but the detailed profile of ADA epitope has not yet been fully elucidated. In our case study, we introduced an integrated epitope analysis approach by sequentially conducting domain competition assay and peptide screening assay to characterize the potential linear epitopes. Additionally, immunoinformatic B cell epitope (BCE) prediction was used to gain broader insights regarding the BCE distribution. Our objective is to leverage the ADA epitope information to close the knowledge gap for a comprehensive understanding of immunogenicity characteristics of TAK-186, and potentially offer a blueprint to guide future studies involving similar therapeutic modalities.

Results

Assay design for domain specificity analysis of anti-TAK-186 ADAs

The DSA of ADA can be examined by two strategies: domain-specific total ADA assay and domain-specific competition assay. In the domain-specific total ADA assay (Figure S1), the MDB is dissected into individual domains, and a series of total ADA assays need to be implemented to measure ADAs against each domain separately. In contrast, the domain-specific competition assay (Figure 2) uses a single total ADA assay for the entire MDB. The assay is based on depleting each fraction of the domain-specific ADA, allowing for the quantification of the response against each domain. According to the study by Stubenrauch et al., the discrepancies between the two strategies are minimal, especially for samples with high ADA levels.17 While domain-specific total ADA assay offers the most straightforward view of ADAs against each domain, it can involve considerably more regent preparation and assay development work, depending on the complexity of MDB. Moreover, it is challenging to conduct quantitative analysis given the distinct dynamic ranges of each assay. Therefore, we developed our DSA strategy based on the infrastructure of the competitive ligand binding assays of bridging format.17,18

The principle and workflow of the domain-specific competition assay. As exemplified by a bi-domain protein X-Y, four groups of assays need to be performed. Group 1, screening assay of total ADA; group 2, confirmatory assay with excessive amount of X-Y added with the bridging mix (biotin-drug and SULFO Tag-drug); group 3 and 4, domain specific confirmatory groups with excessive X (group 3) or Y (group 4) included in the bridging mix. The ADAs bound to the specific domain blockers are washed away. The raw RLU data from each group was converted to % inhibition to represent the relative abundance of anti-X and anti-Y ADA. The graph was created by BioRender.com.

Using the bi-domain biologic X-Y (Figure 2) as an example, a bridging electrochemiluminescence (ECL) ADA assay was first developed with the intact X-Y as the confirmatory agent (Group 1 and 2). In parallel, domain-specific confirmatory groups were established with X or Y domain as the immunodepleting reagents individually (Group 3 and 4). All inhibitors were supplemented at sufficient concentrations to achieve the maximal ADA signal depletion, which quantitatively confers the relative ADA contribution by this specific domain. In this case, as anti-X ADA is relatively higher in the sample, it is expected to see a lower raw ECL signal from group 3, in which anti-X ADA is depleted, compared to group 4. As expected, domain X demonstrated a higher inhibition ratio (%) than domain Y. Note that this strategy is based on the assumption that there is minimal ADA epitope overlapping between X and Y. In case that domain X and Y share structure and epitope similarity, the strategy is still feasible, but the total inhibition % of X and Y may exceed 100%.

Structural analysis unveiled that the majority of TAK-186 surface area is solvent-exposed.15 In addition, all subdomains of TAK-186 are connected by glycine-rich flexible linkers, suggesting that the truncated TAK-186 fragments should retain the structure and epitope integrity of the intact parental molecule. In this regard, the DSA described above is an appropriate strategy determining the ADA binding preference on TAK-186 at the domain level. It is important to note that TAK-186 cleavage occurs exclusively within the tumor microenvironment. As such, most TAK-186 molecules elicit antigen-specific immune responses in their intact form. Therefore, this study focuses solely on the immunogenicity of intact TAK-186. By analyzing ADA epitope profiles from different patients at various treatment time points, we aim to delineate the patterns of ADA generation in response to TAK-186 in clinic, understand the root cause of the immunogenicity risk, and propose derisking strategies in potential.

A critical step in our DSA strategy is to develop a robust ECL assay detecting total ADAs against the intact TAK-186 in human plasma. The assay procedure was optimized by evaluating several assay components such as buffers, concentrations of critical reagents, acid dissociation conditions and sample dilution ratios. As the levels of drug in the samples to be tested are all below the lower limit of quantitation, the drug tolerance of the total ADA ECL assay was not evaluated. The screening cut point factor (SCPF, 95% confidence) and confirmatory cut point (CCP, 99% confidence) were estimated to be approximately 1.085 and 11.3%, respectively, by examining 40 lots of naïve human plasma (50% male, 50% female) three times (Figure S2). Pre-existing antibodies (PE-Abs) recognizing the C-end of native VHH are commonly detected in human.19,20 Due to the protection of C-terminus of each VHH domain by either linker or polyhistidine tag, we did not witness significant PE-Abs to TAK-186. The corresponding assay sensitivity was estimated to be less than 100 ng/ml (Figure 3(A)) based on a surrogate rabbit anti-TAK-186 pAb, which is sufficient for this study. As shown in Figure S3, the total ADA assay of TAK-186 demonstrated good inter-assay precision proved by the low coefficient of variation (CV) across all plates. For immunodepletion groups, we added 53 μM TAK-186 (5 μg/ml) as well as other fragments (Figure 3(B), Figure S4) to achieve maximal inhibition. Polyhistidine tag is added to each fragment on the C-end to facilitate purification. Note that the concentration of fragment 1 (F1, αEGFR VHH) was doubled to accommodate its duplicated appearance in TAK-186. Owing to the technical challenges of manufacturing the inactive αCD3 scFv, F3 (associated element) and F4 (αHSA VHH) were utilized instead to characterize the ADAs against inactivated αCD3 scFv domain (orange) in an indirect manner. The reproducibility of DSA was also examined with 5 μg/mlrabbit-derived polyclonal positive control (PC) antibody to assure data quality (Figure S5). Notably, the PC contains fractions that are specific to all domains of TAK-186, indicating its suitability to be utilized for DSA characterization. Real samples will be analyzed repeatedly, and the raw relative light unit (RLU) data (unspiked plus fragment-spiked ones) from each time will be averaged, then used for calculating the percentage inhibition.

The surrogate rabbit anti-TAK-186 pAb was used as surrogate PC. 10 μg/ml of TAK-186 (final concentration) was used in confirmatory assay. Low ADA magnitudes, which are often seen in samples from early collection points, can compromise the reliability and consistency of detection. Similarly, insufficient sample volumes necessitate dilution, which can further affect the accuracy of DSA analysis. This led us to investigate if the DSA result is solely dependent on ADA composition, regardless of ADA concentration. We thus conducted DSA using a surrogate PC gradience ranging from 370 ng/ml (signal-to-noise ratio [S/N] ~ 2.0) to 10 μg/ml (S/N ~ 30) in the naïve plasma to check whether the results remain comparable. As illustrated in Figure S6, comparable domain-specific signal depletion ratios were observed across this entire concentration range, indicating our DSA strategy could accommodate samples with a broad spectrum of ADA levels. Further, this also enabled us to set up a provisional “comfortable range” of ADA signal for DSA. Samples with limited availability can be diluted with negative control (NC) plasma as long as the ADA RLU falls within the range defined by 370 ng/ml to 10 μg/ml PC (S/N ~ 2 - 30).

Another general consideration of ADA assay development is the drug tolerability as residue drugs may interfere with the ADA detection. Assays with poor drug tolerability may lead to the underestimation of immunogenicity risks.21 Although the samples used in this study were all collected at the time point when drug concentrations were below the detection limits, we still questioned whether our DSA can be made “drug tolerant,” which will greatly expand the capability of our platform. As shown in Figure S7, the addition of 500 ng/ml drug led to negligible changes of the DSA result of 5 μg/ml PC. Higher PC and drug concentration may be tested to further substantiate the robustness of the DSA platform. Altogether, the DSA strategy has been successfully established to characterize the domain specificity of anti-TAK-186 ADAs.

The domain specificities of ADAs observed in patients treated by TAK-186

Plasma samples from five patients at multiple time points were analyzed by the DSA platform described above. The RLU S/N values of crude samples correlate strongly with the titer data documented in the clinical study (not shown), reinforcing the reliability of our unvalidated in-house ADA assay. To minimize sample consumption due to limited availability, all crude samples were diluted in neat human plasma by multiple folds based on ADA magnitudes of undiluted samples. As per our data, dilution should not elicit significant impacts on DSA results.

As shown in Figure 4(A), ADA specificity appeared to shift over the course of treatment in some patients. For example, in patient no. 3, the ADA repertoire on day 43 targeted all domains at comparable levels. However, by day 64, the relative abundance of αEGFR VHH-specific ADAs had markedly decreased, while αHSA VHH-specific ADAs became more dominant. A similar trend was observed in patient no. 5, in which most ADAs were found to target αEGFR and αHSA VHH at the late time points. In other patients, such as patient no. 1 and no. 4, ADAs favoring F2 (αCD3 scFv) showed a modest increase over time.

Each inhibition percentage was calculated with the RLU values from two independent runs. (B) The merged graph demonstrates the patient-to-patient variability of ADA domain specificity. An intriguing finding is that ADAs from most examined patients (all except no. 4) demonstrated a gradual increase in ADA preference for the αHSA VHH over time. Specifically, for patient no. 3, over 70% of ADA signal was abolished upon the addition of excessive αHSA VHH in the assay for day 107 sample, which is markedly different from approximately 25% observed on day 43. In patient no. 5, αHSA VHH accounted for about 30% of the ADA signal on day 70, but still significantly higher than the ~20% observed on day 56, despite that ~50% ADA signal was yielded consistently by the αEGFR VHH domain. Of note, the assay used cannot (and may not need to) distinguish ADAs against the two identical αEGFR VHH domains (subdomain a1 and a2), as well as ADAs against the epitopes shared by αCD3 scFv and iαCD3 scFv. Sequence analysis suggested that αHSA VHH domain has undergone humanization. Still, it appeared to be a significant ADA target in most samples, indicating that humanization may not be sufficient to mitigate the immunogenicity risk of VHHs.

Overall, while incremental ADA specificity against αHSA VHH domain was observed in some patients, it is acknowledged that those ADAs generally exhibited broad reactivity across all functional domains of TAK-186. Given the highly individualized nature of antigen-specific immune responses, diverse patterns of epitope dominance were observed across the five patients (Figure 4(B)), making it difficult to definitively answer if any particular region of TAK-186 is inherently more immunogenic than others.

Identification of linear ada epitopes on the hsa targeting moiety by peptide screening

As most of the patients included in our study demonstrated increased ADA specificity against the HSA binding domain, we sought to investigate the potential epitopes located in it. Peptide library screening is commonly applied to map immunogenic spots of TPs. Of note, by performing microarray epitope profiling, Homann et al. identified five and six linear epitopes on adalimumab and infliximab, respectively, that are recognized by patients’ sera.22,23 In order to further characterize the high-resolution ADA epitope landscape of the TAK-186 molecule, we next interrogated whether some linear epitopes can be recognized by ADAs via a plate-based peptide screening assay. Here, we focused particularly on the HSA binding domain that demonstrated increased epitope dominance by some patients. The landscapes of linear ADA epitopes of five patients are illustrated in Figure 5. Surprisingly, all patients demonstrated ADA recognition to peptide 8, which represents the CDR1-framework region 2 (FR2) junction area. Also, peptide 6 (FR1-CDR1 junction) is targeted by all patients except no. 4. Interestingly, for patient no. 5, although we noticed the ADA domain specificity change from day 43 to day 70, the linear epitope distribution remains highly stable during this period (Figure S8). Unlike the other four patients, no. 5 developed ADAs against the C-terminus of αHSA VHH. Patient no. 3 exhibited the strongest and most focused epitope reactivity within the cohort. Overall, CDR1 appeared to be the most immunogenic region located within the HSA binding domain. Overall, a wide variety of linear epitopes were observed within the αHSA VHH region of the four tested patients. Nevertheless, owing to the limited size of the current dataset, it remains unclear which individual epitope may play a dominant immunological role. More samples should be tested to elucidate the comprehensive profile of ADAs against TAK-186.

ADA epitope identification via computational strategies

In silico analysis is commonly performed for early-stage immunogenicity assessment of vaccines and biotherapeutics due to the high-throughput capacity and its cost-effectiveness.13 Compared to T cell epitope (TCE)-focused methods, BCE prediction approaches are considered somewhat premature. Nevertheless, we still performed the analysis hoping to provide a more comprehensive view of epitope landscape of TAK-186. The insufficient sample volume prohibited us from analyzing the entire molecule. Therefore, we focused on the HSA binding domain that appeared to be the dominant ADA target in several patients.

BCEs are generally classified as linear and nonlinear, and therefore the computation prediction is conducted in sequence- and structure-based manner. Both BepiPred 3.024 and SEMA 1D25 were used for sequence-based epitope identification. BepiPred 3.0 uses a neural network that incorporates protein language model (ESM-2) to predict both linear and conformational BCEs from an amino acid sequence. In contrast, SEMA 1D is a deep learning method that also uses ensembles of protein language models to infer conformational BCEs directly from the protein sequence, without needing three-dimensional (3D) structural data. Since each method generates a distinct form of output, the scores of each residue were normalized to 0–100 scale for demonstration, and the mean scores of each residue were calculated to reduce biases. The data from both methods showed high correlation (Spearman r = 0.57, p < 0.0001), and indicated that most epitopes appeared in clusters and are located near CDR2 region of the HSA-targeting VHH (Figure 6(A)).

(D) 3D illustration of epitope areas of high (red) and low possibilities (green) in (C). The 3D model of αHSA VHH was generated by PyMoL 3.1. CDR loop regions of the VHH are marked.

We next performed structure-based BCE prediction with four methods: DiscoTope 3.0,26 SEMA 3D,25 ElliPro,27 and GraphBepi.28 Although they all rely on the 3D structure, they differ slightly in terms of underlying prediction logic. DiscoTope 3.0 relies on surface accessibility and amino acid statistics, while ElliPro applies geometric protrusion with ellipsoid modeling. In contrast, SEMA 3D and GraphBepi leverage deep learning with graph neural networks, with GraphBepi further integrating language model embeddings. ESMFold29 was utilized to obtain the structure of the TAK-186 due to the lack of experimental data. As the likelihood of a specific residue of protein domain to become BCE is affected by the environment, we used the intact TAK-186 to perform the initial prediction and then focused on the detailed epitope identification of αHSA VHH.

Similar to sequence-based prediction (Figure 6(A,B)), the CDR2 loop was also considered the dominant BCE according to the structure-based models. Additionally, CDR1 and FR3 demonstrate epitope-like features according to the data (Figure 6(C,D)). Notably, the results from the four methods showed poorer correlation (Figure S9), suggesting the need for deeper understanding of computational BCE prediction.

In summary, our in silico data provided a preliminary outline of BCE distribution within the αHSA VHH of TAK-186. Regions including CDR1, CDR2 and FR2 appear to be risky. Further research in this field, and especially methodological advancement, artificial intelligence and machine learning-empowered tools, are crucial for improving the reliability and utility of computational immunogenicity assessment and biotherapeutic design.

Discussion

Fit-for-purpose characterization of ADA profiles is essential to understand the clinical consequence of immunogenicity. While clinically effective, TPs often pose significant risks of immunogenicity reflected by exposure loss or safety concerns. For antibodies or antibody-like TPs (e.g., VHH, scFv), the non-germline sequences account for immunogenicity risks by contributing both TCEs and BCEs (liner and structural).13,30 Because each domain of an MDB serves a unique pharmacological function, in complement with total and neutralizing ADA (Nab) analysis, researchers may need to implement suitable strategies to quantify ADAs in an epitope-resolved manner.8,9

In this study, we showcased an integrated strategy to identify immunogenic hotspots in MDBs, with TAK-186 as a representative molecule hereby. By running DSA analysis, researchers can readily and quantitatively pinpoint the region of high humoral immunogenicity risks. Unlike previous works that only provided qualitative domain specificity information of anti-MDBs ADA, our DSA strategy can identify the relative contribution of ADA signals to each individual domain, which presents much more valuable information. From a methodological perspective, the TAK-186 molecule used in our study is obviously more complicated than the antibody-fusion proteins reported previously. As per our exploratory analysis, an extensively diverse B cell repertoire was generated responding to TAK-186 administration in patients, resulting in ADAs targeting all domains of TAK-186. Moreover, the relative abundances of domain-specific ADAs are highly dynamic and idiosyncratic. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that the present DSA strategy is largely based on the classic ligand binding ADA assay, which is quasi-quantitative. Therefore, the relative ADA contribution calculated by raw signals cannot be directly converted to ADA concentration. Alternative assay workflows such as domain-specific pre-extraction of ADAs followed by ligand binding assay can be examined and compared with current formats to better understand the biases of each strategy. In general, there is limited guidance regarding how to characterize the domain specificity of MDB-induced ADAs. Additional questions such as how to validate and report the quantitative ADA domain specificity, and how to statistically define the significant difference of ADA domain specificity among samples, should be addressed to ensure this strategy is compliant with regulatory expectations.

It should be highlighted that we not only showed the patient-specific signatures of ADA domain specificity, more importantly, we unveiled how the specificity evolves over time. An intriguing observation of our study is that all examined patients demonstrated steady or increasing ADA specificity against the αHSA VHH, suggesting it could potentially harbor immunodominance nature, a phenomenon observed in the context of virus infection,31 within the whole molecule. This may be attributed to intrinsic characteristics that confer higher immunogenic propensity on the αHSA VHH, or to other biochemical and physiological factors that promote the enrichment of this specific population of antigen-specific B cells and ADAs. Introducing reversible or irreversible HSA engagement is a well-validated approach to optimize the half-life and efficacy of peptides and large molecule biotherapeutics.14,16,32–34 However, few studies dive deep into the immunogenicity risks elicited by the half-life extension domains, likely due to the assumption that they are not directly linked to the therapeutical mechanism of those MDBs. Considering TAK-186 binds tightly to HSA with the dissociation constant at single to low double-digit nanomolar level, the exposed FR of αHSA VHH are more likely to be the ADA binding sites, resulting in the formation of drug-HSA-ADA complexes.

In addition to the magnitude and target specificity shift, other characteristics such as ADA affinity and immune complex formation may also be considered as indicators of immunogenicity response. In the study by Joyce et al.,35 the apparent ADA affinity to TP increased during the course of treatment, aligning with the maturation status of adaptive anti-drug immune response. The formation of immune complex between TP and polyclonal ADAs was observed in animals using size-exclusion chromatography, and was found to be closely correlated with accelerated drug clearance and altered biodistribution.36,37 To better understand the clinical observations associated with immunogenicity, researchers may consider characterizing ADAs against MDB in a more comprehensive way encompassing total ADA level, Nab level, domain specificity and binding affinity data.

As a unique class of antibody originally discovered in camelids, VHH is now broadly used in designing MDBs due to its low size (about 15 kDa) and monomeric feature. More recently, the therapeutic value of VHH has been extensively recognized since the approval of caplacizumab (Cablivi, bivalent VHH) and ozoralizumab (Nanozora, trivalent VHH) for clinical uses, with more of other architectures under late-stage development. Although previously considered to have low immunogenic potential given its high homology to human germline VH sequences (particularly IGHV3 family), investigations have shown the high frequency of preexisting anti-VHH antibody19,20 as well as treatment-induced ones in humans.38,39 Humanization undoubtably reduced the immunogenicity risks of VHH-based TPs, but it cannot mitigate the risks attributed to the regions proximal to CDRs. It was found in our study that almost all patients develop over 50% ADA signals against αEGFR and αHSA VHH domains. This finding suggests the urgent need to identify derisking strategies to develop “cleaner” VHHs.

As we mentioned, the anti-drug immune response in the clinical setting is highly idiosyncratic. Beyond the drug-related factors such as sequence, aggregation, and administration route, patient-specific factors may play more critical roles outlining the immunogenicity in humans. For example, studies have shown the immune responses against TPs are closely linked with patients’ human leukocyte antigen (HLA) haplotype.40–42 Our study can potentially benefit from larger samples size along with the collection of immunogenetics data.

In conclusion, our case study of TAK-186 provides critical insights reminding MDB developers that the HSA binder, as well as other VHH-based scaffold elements, should be selected with caution to minimize the immunogenicity propensity of the entire therapeutic. In addition, MDB developers are encouraged to exploit next-generation scaffold proteins that are known to be more “human,” such as humanized VHH or human heavy chain-only antibodies, to mitigate the potential immunogenicity liabilities.43–45 TCEs are designed to act as immune stimulators, meaning they may possess amplified immunogenicity risks.10 Therefore, caution should be exercised during the drug design and efficacy/toxicity evaluation.

Following the domain specificity characterization, peptide scanning assay and in silico BCE prediction were conducted to spotlight the epitopes residing within the αHSA VHH domain that became the dominant ADA hotspot in some patients. A few CDR-related linear epitopes were identified via peptide scanning, but the high variation among patients made conclusions ungeneralizable. In terms of BCE prediction, although different methods presented unequal epitope identification results give the distinct mechanisms, they seemed to reach agreement that the CDR1, CDR2, CDR3 as well as FR3 exhibit higher risks compared to other regions. Also, the BCE predicted by the in silico methods partially overlap with the peptide screening data (Figure S10). Specifically, the high-risk spots around CDR1 and FM3 were highlighted by both methods; however, the CDR2 loop was not found to be targeted by any patient’s sample, although it was picked up by both sequence- and structure-based BCE prediction. Nonetheless, the current data is only based on EMSFold-based 3D structure prediction. As TAK-186 possesses substantial flexibility, especially the tertiary structure, it might be helpful to further check the BCE prediction using structures generated from different prediction models. To protect proprietary information, we do not disclose the specific amino acid sequence of any part of TAK-186. However, the absence of sequence information does not affect the interpretation of our data, as the structures of scFvs and VHHs are highly conserved.

Despite the aforementioned limitations of technique and sample availability, our study is the first report of adopting peptide screening to characterize immunogenic hotspots of VHH-based therapeutics to the best of our knowledge. As a frequently employed measure for epitope mapping, the data quality can potentially be further elevated by using the high-density peptide array, which not only offers improved epitope resolution but reduces the sample consumption.46,47 Some linear epitopes identified in our study were located distantly from the CDRs, meaning they are presumably engineerable in the follow-up candidates. Nevertheless, it must be acknowledged that peptide-based methods can only identify linear epitopes, which is the intrinsic limitation of this strategy. Although some linear epitopes have been identified in well-characterized immunogenic therapeutics such as adalimumab, most epitopes are believed to be conformational in nature. Some chemical approaches have been proposed to reconstruct conformational or discontinuous epitopes on solid matrixes for epitope mapping,13 but more case studies are needed to support their feasibility as a generic research tool for drug development.

In addition to peptide-based methodologies, mass spectrometry-based strategies and cryo-electron microscopy have been exploited to characterize polyclonal epitopes on pathogens, therapeutics or vaccines.48–52 However, the widespread adoption of these approaches is often challenged by the considerable labor and cost burdens, as well as the difficulties to acquire generalizable conclusions. Although it is technically feasible to acquire high-resolution epitope mapping results of pAbs, the utilization of these state-of-the-art tools is most beneficial when the representative monoclonal ADAs against domain epitopes can be identified and isolated. Nevertheless, each epitope mapping method holds certain limitations, and therefore cannot be guaranteed to offer high-quality prospective projection for any tested agent. A fit-for-purpose, integrated strategy should be developed for each specific MDB at distinctive research stages.

In summary, we demonstrated a proof-of-concept case study to outline the epitopes of ADAs on complex MDBs. Our preliminary discovery revealed the existence of ADA epitope evolution during treatment, suggesting the complexity of immunogenicity of biotherapeutics, and the necessity of multipoint samples analysis to comprehensively understand this phenomenon. At the completion of our study, sufficient data was not yet available to determine whether the occurrence of ADAs is associated with drug exposure and safety in clinic. Further investigations are necessary to achieve a more thorough understanding of the immunogenic characteristics of TAK-186, as well as MBDs of various formats.

Materials and methods

Materials

The intact TAK-186 and its truncated derivatives were expressed by Expi293 expressing system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and purified via Protein A or Ni-NTA chromatography internally.14 The molecular weights and purities of all proteins were confirmed by SDS-PAGE and Coomassie blue staining. EZ-Link Sulfo-NHS-LC-Biotin (Bt) and Blocker Casein were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. MSD GOLD SULFO-Tag (ST) NHS-Ester (#R91AO), ST conjugated anti-human and anti-rabbit antibodies (#R32AJ, #R32AB) were purchased from Meso Scale Diagnostics ([MSD] Rockville, MD, USA). Bovine serum albumin (BSA) was purchased from Millipore Sigma (#A7030, Milwaukee, WI, USA). The rabbit anti-TAK-186 pAb was produced in-house. Naïve human plasma was purchased from BioIVT (Hicksville, NY, USA). 300 mM acetic acid (pH = 3.0) and 1 M Tris (pH = 8.0) were prepared in-house.

Clinical samples

Samples were collected from an open-label, Phase 1/2 clinical trial in which all patients received intravenous TAK-186 infusion every seven days. The plasma samples for immunogenicity analysis were collected prior to the upcoming drug infusion. All samples analyzed in this study are confirmed to be ADA-positive by a validated ECL bridging assay. Informed consent was obtained from all participated subjects prior to the trial. All samples were stored at −80°C until analysis.

TAK-186 ADA assay procedure and cut point determination

A bridging-format ECL assay was developed in house as per the guideline of regulatory agency for total ADA screening assay.5 Bt-TAK-186 and ST-TAK-186 was prepared as per the instructions from the manufacturers. Samples were first acidified by nine volumes of 300 mM acetic acid (pH = 3.0) followed by neutralization with ten volumes of 1 M Tris/6% BSA neutralizing buffer (pH = 8.0) containing 1 μg/ml Bt-TAK-186 and 1 μg/ml ST-TAK-186 (bridging mix) and incubate for 1 h. The bridging complex was then transferred to streptavidin-coated MSD plates and scanned on a MESO QuickPlex SQ 120 MM reader. The ADA negative pooled human plasma was used as negative control. PC was made by diluting anti-TAK-186 pAbs in NC (pooled from individual lots of naïve human plasma confirmed as ADA-negative). For confirmatory assay, 10 μg/ml TAK-186 was spiked in the bridging mix for signal inhibition. All incubation steps were conducted at room temperature with 600 rpm orbital shaking. The parametric SCPF and CCP were calculated from three independent runs with 5% and 1% false-positive rate, respectively. Statistical outliers were excluded based on Tukey’s box plot method, and excluded for cut point calculation.

Characterization of ADA domain specificity

The domain specificity analysis was conducted based on a previously published strategy with modifications.17 In parallel with the confirmatory assay, truncated TAK-186 derivatives (F1, F2, F3 and F4) were subjected to each confirmatory group at an equal molar concentration as the intact TAK-186. The relative ADA contribution by each domain was reflected by the ECL signal inhibition rates from each corresponding truncate. The calculation of inhibition (%) was performed by the equation: Inhibition (%) = 100% × [1 - (Samplespiked - NCspiked)/(Sampleupspiked - NCunspiked)]. The RLU values were acquired from two individual runs by distinct analysts, with CV < 20%. PC standards (0, 125, 500, 2000 ng/ml) were included in each plate to evaluate inter-plate assay precision. All samples were tested in duplicate on plates.

Peptide library screening

15-mer, staggered N-terminal biotinylated peptides (5 amino acid offset, 90% purity confirmed by HPLC) derived from TAK-186 were synthesized by GenScript (Suzhou, Jiangsu, China). Peptides were suspended at 1 μg/ml and immobilized on pre-blocked streptavidin-coated MSD plates. Plasma samples (1:75 dilution) were incubated with the immobilized peptides for 2 h with 600 rpm shaking, followed by the detection with a cocktail of ST-conjugated anti-human kappa and lambda light chain antibodies (0.5 μg/ml) for 1 h. Bocker Casein buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) is used as blocking buffer and assay diluent.

Prediction of 3D structure of TAK-186

ESMFold is utilized to obtain the 3D structure of TAK-186. Briefly, the full protein sequence is parsed by biopython53 and tokenized by the Auto-Tokenizer from Huggingface. The ESMFold model is downloaded and then loaded into an AWS machine with Nvidia A10 GPU and infer_pdb function is used to predict TAK-186 structure. The availability of structure prediction tools is listed in Table S1.

CDR annotation

The CDR and FR regions of VHH are identified by ANARCI54 (via Tamarind Bio, with availability provided in Table S1) following IGMT numbering system.

Computational BCE prediction

In silico prediction of BCEs were performed using both sequence- and structure-based approaches. BepiPred 3.0 and SEMA 1D were both exploited using amino acid sequence as input for BCE identification. Discotope 3.0, ElliPro and GraphBepi and SEMA 3D were then performed to get BCE distribution data on residue level. The availability of BCE prediction tools is listed in Table S1. As each method generates output following unique rules, the scores all residues located within αHSA VHH section were isolated and normalized to 0–100 scale by Min-Max normalization approach: xnorm = 100 × (x-xmin)/(xmax-xmin)
