Complete lesion resection and early surgical intervention are favorable factors for long‐term seizure freedom in drug‐resistant epileptic spasms
Authors: Hua Li, Yao Wang, Qiang Guo, Junxi Chen, Kaihui Li, Danfang Li, Qinghua Tan, Jing Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEpileptic spasms (ES) in children carry a high risk of neurodevelopmental delay, yet predictors of long‐term surgical outcome remain incompletely defined. This study aimed to evaluate seizure outcomes following epilepsy surgery and to identify independent prognostic factors for postoperative recurrence.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 73 patients (47 males, 64%; median age at surgery: 37 months, IQR: 20–55.5) with drug‐resistant ES who underwent resective or ablative surgery at Guangdong Sanjiu Brain Hospital in China between January 2015 and September 2024, with regular follow‐up at least 12 months after epilepsy surgery. Independent predictors of recurrence were identified using multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression, and Kaplan–Meier analysis was performed to compare seizure‐free survival stratified by seizure duration (SD) and residual lesion status.ResultsSeizure‐free rates were 75.3% at 1 year and 55.1% at last follow‐up. Mean follow‐up was 61.36 ± 26.10 months (range: 12–132). Among malformation of cortical development (MCD) subtypes, focal cortical dysplasia (FCD) type II demonstrated the highest 5‐year seizure‐free rate (100%), whereas mild MCD (m‐MCD) showed the lowest (33.3%; p = 0.039). On multivariate Cox analysis, residual lesion on postoperative MRI was the strongest independent predictor of recurrence (HR = 5.13; 95% CI: 2.28–11.51; p < 0.001), followed by SD exceeding 36 months (HR = 2.49; 95% CI: 1.17–5.29; p = 0.018). Kaplan–Meier analysis confirmed a twofold difference in 5‐year seizure‐free survival between patients with duration ≤36 versus >36 months (73% vs. 36%; log‐rank p = 0.00069), and significantly better seizure‐free survival in patients without residual lesions (log‐rank p < 0.0001).SignificanceComplete lesion resection and early surgical intervention (SD ≤36 months) are independently associated with favorable long‐term seizure outcomes in children with drug‐resistant ES. Pathological subtype, particularly FCD type II, further refines prognostic stratification. INTRODUCTION

Epileptic spasms (ES) represent a distinct seizure type, typically manifesting as clusters of spasms during infancy or early childhood, accompanied by hypsarrhythmia on electroencephalography (EEG) and frequently associated with developmental delay or regression. The 2022 International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) classification unified this condition under the term infantile epileptic spasms syndrome (IESS), encompassing presentations across a broader age range and those not fulfilling the complete West syndrome triad. 1 Globally, IESS has a cumulative incidence of 58.2 per 100 000 children, with a lifetime prevalence of approximately 1.5–2 per 10 000 children, and accounts for approximately 10% of all epilepsies with onset before 36 months of age. 2 , 3 Despite first‐line pharmacological treatment, the long‐term prognosis remains guarded. A large Danish population‐based cohort (n = 326, followed to a mean age of 8.2 years) demonstrated that 76.4% of IESS patients still had active epilepsy at last follow‐up, with 44.2% experiencing severe neurodevelopmental impairment. 4 Over half of children with structural etiologies ultimately develop drug‐resistant epilepsy. 5 These findings collectively highlight the substantial neurological and socioeconomic burden of IESS and underscore the urgency of developing effective therapeutic strategies beyond pharmacotherapy.

Epilepsy surgery has emerged as a critical therapeutic modality for achieving seizure freedom and improving neurodevelopmental trajectories in children with drug‐resistant ES. Surgical interventions for ES are broadly classified into two categories: curative resective or disconnective procedures, such as focal or multiple lobectomy and hemispherectomy, which target the epileptogenic zone (EZ); and palliative procedures, principally corpus callosotomy (CC), which aim to interrupt bisynchronous ictal propagation and reduce seizure burden in patients without a discrete, surgically targetable EZ. Beyond seizure control, early surgical intervention capitalizes on heightened neuroplasticity in infancy, offering a critical developmental window to minimize irreversible cortical reorganization driven by persistent ictal activity. 6 Curative epilepsy surgery in ES yields optimal results in patients with MRI‐confirmed lesions concordant with EEG localization, particularly when intervention is undertaken early; 88% of patients who underwent surgery within 3 years of seizure onset achieved ILAE Class I outcomes, compared with substantially lower rates among those with longer preoperative disease duration. 7

Current evidence indicates substantial variability in postoperative seizure‐freedom rates across surgical modalities. A systematic review by Kolosky et al. (2024) encompassing 21 retrospective studies (n = 531) reported a pooled seizure‐freedom rate of 68.8% following resective surgery for pediatric ES, with low interstudy heterogeneity. 8 Individual patient‐level analysis (n = 360) identified two independent predictors of postoperative spasm recurrence: prolonged preoperative ES duration and nonhemispherectomy resection relative to hemispherectomy. 8 These findings were further corroborated by an individual patient data meta‐analysis (IPDMA) conducted by Cottier et al. (2024), which pooled data from 26 studies (n = 358) and demonstrated that 67.1% of patients undergoing resective surgery achieved spasm freedom, a rate markedly exceeding the 38.2% observed in the corpus callosotomy group; moreover, lesional MRI findings and hemispherectomy were independently associated with favorable postoperative outcomes. 3 Although studies reporting surgical outcomes in patients with ES have emerged from China, the existing literature remains limited by relatively small sample sizes and short follow‐up durations. 9 , 10 , 11

To address these knowledge gaps, this study retrospectively analyzed 73 children with drug‐resistant ES who underwent surgical intervention at Guangdong Sanjiu Brain Hospital between January 2015 and September 2024. With a maximum follow‐up of 10 years, it provides genuinely long‐term outcome data that are rarely achievable in existing single‐center studies, enabling characterization of the temporal trajectory of postoperative seizure recurrence.

METHODS

Patient selection

In this retrospective study, medical records of all consecutive children aged 10 years or younger who had epilepsy surgery for drug‐resistant ES at Guangdong Sanjiu Brain Hospital between January 2015 and September 2024 were reviewed. The inclusion criteria included: (1) Children who had ES, in clusters or sporadic, recorded on the video‐electroencephalogram (VEEG) prior to epilepsy surgery were included; (2) All patients met the criteria for drug‐resistant epilepsy according to the International League Against Epilepsy; 12 and (3) regular follow‐up at least 12 months after epilepsy surgery at Guangdong Sanjiu Brain Hospital. The exclusion criteria included: (1) only having undergone vagus nerve stimulation or corpus callosotomy; (2) having ES previously, but ES disappeared before surgery, presenting with other forms of seizure.

MRI assessment

Preoperative findings on brain MRI were classified into four categories: (1) normal, (2) focal abnormalities: lesions involving one or two adjacent lobes within a unilateral hemisphere, (3) multifocal abnormalities: lesions involving more than three lobes within a unilateral hemisphere or both hemispheres, and (4) hemispheric abnormalities: lesions involving the entire hemisphere. Brain MRI was performed within 6 months postoperatively to assess the presence of residual lesions. A residual lesion was defined as any structural abnormality on postoperative MRI that was present within or adjacent to the resection margin and was judged by the reviewing neurosurgeon and epileptologist to represent incompletely resected or disconnected epileptogenic tissue, based on comparison with the preoperative MRI. In patients with normal preoperative MRI findings, SEEG was performed to determine whether residual lesions exist based on whether the epileptogenic zone identified by SEEG has been completely resected. Postoperative signal changes attributable to expected surgical sequelae (e.g., encephalomalacia at the resection margin) were not classified as residual lesions unless a discrete structural abnormality consistent with the original epileptogenic substrate was identified.

VEEG assessment

All patients underwent VEEG within 3 months before surgery and within 6 months after surgery. Preoperative interictal and ictal EEG recordings were assessed for lateralizing features and the presence of hypsarrhythmia. Postoperative EEG findings were classified according to the distribution of epileptiform discharges as normal, focal, multifocal, or diffuse.

Preoperative evaluation and surgical strategy

All patients had detailed history, neurological examination, VEEG, and brain MRI; a majority also had brain fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG‐PET); some patients underwent genetic testing and neuropsychological tests. All patients had interictal and ictal VEEG recordings. Based on the above comprehensive presurgical evaluation, if the epileptogenic zone (EZ) was clearly identified, resection surgery was performed. When EZ localization remained uncertain or involved functional areas, stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG) electrodes were implanted; once localization was established, SEEG‐guided radiofrequency thermocoagulation (RF‐TC) or resection surgery was undertaken. Patients who continued to experience seizures after RF‐TC subsequently underwent further surgical resection. Patients who underwent RF‐TC without subsequent surgical resection were also included in this study for long‐term follow‐up. If lateralization of the EZ was indeterminate, corpus callosotomy was performed first, and resection surgery was subsequently carried out after lateralization/localization was clarified. Hemispherectomy was chosen for those with structural lesions involving one entire hemisphere. Results of the preoperative evaluation and surgical decision were discussed in a multidisciplinary conference. Given that the epileptogenic zone could extend beyond a single lobe, the surgical procedures were classified as lobectomy, multiple lobectomy, and hemispherectomy.

Follow‐up and seizure outcomes

All patients underwent a minimum follow‐up of 12 months, with surgical outcomes assessed annually. Outcomes were evaluated using the Engel classification system and categorized into Engel class I, II, III, and IV. In the present study, Engel class IA was defined as complete seizure freedom, including all seizure types. For patients undergoing repeat surgeries, the surgical nodes and follow‐up outcomes are calculated from the date of the last surgery.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on the normality of distribution. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Differences between groups were compared using the Student's t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the Pearson chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Postoperative seizure‐free survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, with differences assessed by the log‐rank test. To determine the optimal cutoff value for preoperative seizure duration (SD), we employed a data‐driven approach using the maximally selected rank statistics (implemented via the surv_cutpoint function in the survminer R package). This algorithm evaluates all possible cutoff points to identify the specific threshold that provides the most significant stratification of recurrence‐free survival probability (i.e., maximizing the log‐rank statistic). Consequently, patients were dichotomized into ≤36 months and >36 months groups for subsequent Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analyses.

To identify independent predictors of seizure recurrence, univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed. Variables with a p < 0.1 in the univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate model. To prevent overfitting given the total number of recurrence events (n = 31), the final multivariate Cox model was strictly limited to include a maximum of three core predictors, adhering to the standard rule of thumb of approximately 10 events per variable (EPV). Results are expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

All statistical analyses and graphics were performed using R software version 4.3.1 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

A total of 73 patients with drug‐resistant ES who underwent resective or ablative surgery were included in this study. The mean follow‐up duration was 61.36 ± 26.10 (12–132) months, with four patients lost to follow‐up. Baseline patient characteristics are detailed in Table 1. Forty‐seven patients were male (64%), and 26 patients were female (36%). The median age of seizure onset was 6.0 (2–13.5) months, and the median age at surgery was 37 (20–55.5) months. The median duration from seizure onset to resective or ablative surgery was 27 (13.5–45) months.

MRI, VEEG, PET, and genetic testing results

Abnormal findings on brain MRI were observed in 71 patients (97.3%), with 50 showing unilateral and 21 showing bilateral abnormalities. Abnormal MRI findings were categorized as focal abnormalities (n = 33, 46.5%), multifocal abnormalities (n = 20, 28.2%), and hemispheric abnormalities (n = 18, 25.3%). Preoperatively, hypsarrhythmia was present in 11 patients (15.1%), lateralization on interictal EEG was observed in 49 (67.1%), and lateralization on ictal EEG was observed in 39 (53.4%). Within 6 months after surgery, postoperative EEG findings were normal in 9 patients (12.3%), whereas 37 (50.7%) showed focal abnormalities, 17 (23.3%) showed multifocal abnormalities, and 10 (13.7%) showed diffuse abnormalities. Positron emission tomography (PET) was performed in 56 patients (76.7%); 55 showed hypometabolic abnormalities, including 38 with unilateral hypometabolism and 17 with bilateral hypometabolism. Thirty‐one patients underwent genetic testing, and pathogenic variants were identified in 11 (35.5%): TSC1 (n = 4), TSC2 (n = 6), and NPRL3 (n = 1) (Table 2).

Surgical characteristics and procedural details

Forty‐four patients (60.3%) underwent left‐sided surgery, and 28 (38.4%) underwent right‐sided surgery. Of the 9 patients who underwent SEEG, 6 (66.7%) achieved Engel class IA, 2 patients as Engel class III, and 1 patient as Engel class IV. A total of 5 patients underwent RF‐TC; of these, 3 continued to experience seizures after RF‐TC and subsequently underwent resective surgery, 1 patient with hypothalamic hamartoma achieved seizure freedom after RF‐TC, and 1 patient experienced a reduction in seizure frequency but did not undergo further resective surgery.

Lobectomy was performed in 29 patients (38.4%), multiple lobectomy in 34 (47.9%), and hemispherectomy in 8 (11.0%) (Table 3). Residual lesions were identified on postoperative MRI in 30 patients (41.1%). Among the 29 patients who underwent lobectomy, the frontal lobe was resected in 19 (67.9%), the occipital lobe in 2, the temporal lobe in 5, the parietal lobe in 1, the central region in 1, and the insular cortex in 1 patient. Excluding patients with hypothalamic hamartoma and hemispherectomy, a total of 64 cases underwent surgical resection or RF‐TC involving the following lobes: frontal lobe (n = 37), temporal lobe (n = 28), parietal lobe (n = 23), insular lobe (n = 18), occipital lobe (n = 16), and central region (n = 5). As some patients underwent multiple lobectomies, more than one cerebral lobe was involved in certain cases.

Repeat surgery

Twelve patients underwent reoperation. Eight patients initially underwent corpus callosotomy alone, followed by resection or disconnection of the epileptogenic zone in the second surgery. Two patients initially underwent corpus callosotomy combined with lobectomy, followed by extended resection or resection of remote epileptogenic foci in the second surgery. The remaining 2 patients underwent extended resection during reoperation. The interval between the two surgeries ranged from 2 to 20 months. One patient underwent three resective surgeries; the first two procedures performed at an outside institution involved resection of the right anterior frontal region, whereas the third surgery at our institution, guided by SEEG localization to the right occipital lobe, resulted in seizure freedom following resection. Among the 12 patients who underwent reoperation, 6 achieved seizure freedom at last follow‐up, 2 had no reduction in seizure frequency (Engel class IV), 3 achieved Engel class III outcome (of whom 1 experienced seizure recurrence after 18 months of seizure freedom), and 1 achieved Engel class II outcome with seizure recurrence after 5 years of seizure freedom.

Postsurgical complications and deficits

Postsurgical complications occurred in 5 patients (6.8%), including intracranial infection in 1 patient, intracranial infection with hydrocephalus in 1, cerebral infarction in 1, and subdural hematoma in 1. In the remaining patient, intraoperative oxygen desaturation occurred, followed by diffuse cerebral edema and hemorrhage detected postoperatively. This patient developed postoperative quadriplegia; after rehabilitation, muscle strength recovered to normal in the left limbs and to grade 4 in the right limbs, but cognitive and language functions remained markedly impaired. Postsurgical neurological deficits were observed in 11 patients (15.1%), including hemiplegia in 10 patients and a visual field defect in 1 patient. Among the patients with hemiplegia, 1 also showed marked cognitive and language regression.

Surgical outcome and prognostic factors

Postoperative seizure outcomes were assessed using the Engel classification system. At 1 year after surgery, 55 patients were classified as Engel class IA, 1 as Engel class II, 7 as Engel class III, and 10 as Engel class IV. At the last follow‐up, 38 patients were classified as Engel class IA, 4 as Engel class II, 14 as Engel class III, and 13 as Engel class IV (Figure 1). The seizure‐free rate at 1 year was 75.3% (55/73), followed by a gradual decline: 70.6% (48/69) at 2 years, 66.7% (40/64) at 3 years, 62.5% (30/52) at 4 years, 63.2% (24/42) at 5 years, and 52.0% (13/28) at 6 years. Follow‐up data beyond 7 years (n < 10) were insufficient for meaningful interpretation. At last follow‐up, 38 patients (55.1%) remained seizure‐free (Figure 2).

Univariate analysis was performed to identify clinical and imaging factors associated with seizure‐free outcomes at 1, 3, and 5 years postoperatively (Table 6). Older age at seizure onset was associated with seizure freedom at 1 year postoperatively (11.0 [IQR, 4.0–16.5] vs. 4.0 [2.0–6.0] months, p = 0.007), but not at 3 or 5 years. At 1, 3, and 5 years, the seizure‐free group had a shorter SD than the nonseizure‐free group (all p < 0.05). Patients with an SD ≤36 months had higher seizure‐free rates than those with a duration >36 months at 1 year (85.1% vs. 57.7%, p = 0.012), 3 years (79.5% vs. 42.9%, p = 0.009), and 5 years (80.0% vs. 30.8%, p = 0.005). Preoperative interictal EEG lateralization was significantly associated with seizure freedom only at 3 years postoperatively (78.9% vs. 45.5%, p = 0.011). Postoperative EEG findings within 6 months were significantly associated with seizure outcome at all follow‐up time points, with better seizure‐free rates in patients with normal or focal EEG findings than in those with multifocal or diffuse abnormalities (all p < 0.05). Residual lesion on postoperative MRI showed the strongest and most consistent association with poor seizure outcome. Patients without residual lesions had markedly higher seizure‐free rates than those with residual lesions at 1 year (97.7% vs. 43.3%, p < 0.001), 3 years (83.8% vs. 39.1%, p < 0.001), and 5 years (78.6% vs. 20.0%, p < 0.001) (Table 6).

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses

Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed to identify predictors of postoperative seizure recurrence. In univariate analysis, male sex was associated with a lower risk of recurrence (HR, 0.46; 95% CI, 0.23–0.93; p = 0.032), whereas longer SD was associated with a higher risk of recurrence (HR, 1.02; 95% CI, 1.01–1.04; p = 0.003). When categorized, SD >36 months was significantly associated with an increased risk of recurrence (HR, 3.27; 95% CI, 1.60–6.69; p = 0.001). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that patients with an SD ≤36 months had significantly better seizure‐free survival than those with a SD >36 months (log‐rank p = 0.00069). At 60 months the estimated seizure‐free survival probability was approximately 73% in the SD ≤36 m group versus 36% in the SD >36 m group. DISCUSSION

In this retrospective cohort study of 73 consecutively operated pediatric patients with drug‐resistant ES, we examined the influence of surgical intervention on long‐term seizure freedom and sought to delineate independent predictors of postoperative recurrence. With a mean follow‐up of 61.36 ± 26.10 (12–132) months and systematic histopathological subtype classification, the study achieved a 1‐year seizure‐free rate of 75.3%, which gradually declined to 55.1% at last follow‐up, remaining above 50% throughout the 6‐year postoperative period. Pathological subtype emerged as a significant determinant of outcome: FCD type II conferred the most favorable prognosis, with a 5‐year seizure‐free rate of 100%, whereas m‐MCD was associated with the poorest outcomes, yielding a 5‐year seizure‐free rate of 33.3% (p = 0.039). In the multivariate Cox model, residual lesion on postoperative MRI remained the strongest independent predictor of recurrence (HR, 5.13; 95% CI, 2.28–11.51; p < 0.001). Seizure duration >36 months was also independently associated with a higher risk of recurrence (HR, 2.49; 95% CI, 1.17–5.29; p = 0.018).

Our findings are largely consistent with prior literature. 3 , 7 , 8 A central point of convergence across studies is the prognostic importance of preoperative SD. Consistent with the meta‐analytic finding of Kolosky, which demonstrated a 7% increase in recurrence risk per additional year of ES prior to surgery (HR = 1.07; 95% CI: 1.03–1.12; p = 0.003), 8 and with the independent identification of prolonged SD as a significant predictor of surgical failure in the multivariate analysis reported by Chugani et al. (OR = 1.56; 95% CI: 1.07–2.28; p = 0.022). 7 Our Cox proportional hazards model similarly identified SD exceeding 36 months as an independent predictor of postoperative seizure recurrence. Chipaux et al. also found that the postoperative seizure‐free rate was 87.9% when the interval between the first seizure and surgery was less than 36 months, but it decreased to 64.7% when the interval exceeded 50 months. 13 Mechanistically, prolonged disease duration may lead to widespread epileptogenic network expansion and altered neuroplasticity, rendering focal resection insufficient to eliminate the seizure onset zone—an interpretation consistent with the “developmental and epileptic encephalopathy time window” hypothesis proposed by Ramantani and Wirrell. 14 The mechanistic basis for this relationship is increasingly well characterized: sustained ictal discharges are postulated to promote progressive synaptic remodeling, glutamatergic receptor upregulation, and the gradual recruitment of remote cortical territories into the primary epileptogenic network. These maladaptive neuroplastic processes may proceed with particular rapidity in the developing brain, owing to its inherently heightened synaptic plasticity and vulnerability to activity‐dependent circuit reorganization. 15 , 16

An important caveat when interpreting the prognostic role of SD is the potential for referral pattern bias. Patients presenting to tertiary centers after prolonged delays may have failed more antiseizure medications and harbor more extensive epileptogenic networks, factors that independently compromise surgical outcomes. Socioeconomic barriers, such as geographic distance from specialized centers and limited access to advanced neuroimaging, may further contribute to late referral in a subset of patients. Therefore, SD should be regarded with caution as a prognostic variable, as it may partly reflect disease severity or healthcare accessibility rather than the isolated effect of prolonged seizure activity. Prospective multicenter studies with standardized referral data are needed to clarify this relationship.

A major addition of the present study is the identification of residual lesion on postoperative MRI as a strong independent predictor of seizure recurrence (HR = 5.13, 95% CI: 2.28–11.51, p < 0.001). This finding is clinically important because postoperative MRI provides an objective and early assessment of surgical completeness. Although not all epileptogenic tissue is radiologically visible, a residual structural lesion often serves as a practical surrogate for incomplete resection or incomplete disconnection of the epileptogenic substrate. This concept is well aligned with prior literature showing that the completeness of seizure‐onset‐zone resection is one of the most powerful determinants of favorable postsurgical outcome. In the multivariate model of Barba et al., completeness of the seizure onset zone resection emerged as the dominant predictor of favorable outcome (OR = 0.016; 95% CI: 0.002–0.122; p < 0.001), whereas SD did not attain independent significance. 17 In children with ES, this issue may be especially relevant because epileptogenic regions are often extensive, multilobar, or only partially concordant across MRI and EEG. Accordingly, our results suggest that when a residual lesion is identified after surgery, such patients may warrant closer postoperative surveillance, earlier reassessment of antiseizure treatment, and, in selected cases, consideration of reoperation. Thus, residual lesion status is not only a prognostic marker but also a potentially actionable postoperative indicator.

Pathological subtype provided additional prognostic information. Among MCD subtypes, FCD type II had the most favorable long‐term outcome, with a 5‐year seizure‐free rate of 100%, whereas m‐MCD had the lowest rate at 33.3%. This pattern is biologically plausible and consistent with previous reports showing favorable surgical outcomes in FCD type II, likely because these lesions are relatively well‐defined, intrinsically epileptogenic, and more amenable to complete resection. 18 By contrast, m‐MCD is often subtle on imaging, less clearly demarcated histologically, and may reflect a broader or more diffuse epileptogenic cortex, all of which can hinder precise localization and complete removal. Our findings therefore support the routine incorporation of detailed histopathological subtyping into postoperative prognostic assessment and preoperative counseling whenever resected tissue is available. However, due to the limited number of cases in this study, large‐scale studies are still required to confirm the findings. Notably, although male sex demonstrated a protective effect in univariate analysis (HR = 0.46), this association was only marginally significant in the multivariate model (p = 0.084). In contrast, Cao et al. 9 found no significant association between sex and prognosis, suggesting that the observed sex effect may be confounded by other variables.

Several secondary findings also merit comment. Early postoperative EEG findings were significantly associated with seizure outcome at all follow‐up time points, with normal or focal postoperative EEG patterns indicating a more favorable course than multifocal or diffuse abnormalities. Notably, none of the nine patients with completely normal EEG findings within 6 months after surgery experienced recurrence during follow‐up. Although postoperative EEG was not retained in the final multivariable model, likely because of the limited number of recurrence events and the need to avoid model overfitting, its consistent univariate association suggests that it remains a useful adjunct marker of network‐level surgical success. Similarly, preoperative interictal EEG lateralization was associated with better seizure freedom at 3 years, which is in line with the broader concept that better lateralization reflects a more confined epileptogenic network and a higher likelihood of effective surgical targeting. 11

This study has several strengths. This study represents one of the larger single‐center surgical series of children with drug‐resistant ES in China, with enrollment of 73 patients and a mean follow‐up of 61.36 ± 26.10 months (maximum 132 months). The follow‐up duration compares favorably with most published series, allowing for the detection of late recurrences and assessment of long‐term seizure outcomes. Systematic histopathological subtyping provided the pathological resolution necessary to delineate prognostic differences between FCD Type II and m‐MCD at 3 and 5 years. Outcome analysis employed Cox proportional hazards regression with backward stepwise multivariate selection and Kaplan–Meier survival curves, appropriately accounting for censoring and temporal dynamics. Finally, the multimodal presurgical workup (scalp VEEG, MRI, FDG‐PET, SEEG where indicated, and genetic testing) is consistent with current standards at high‐volume tertiary centers, supporting the external validity of our findings.

Several limitations of the present study warrant acknowledgment. First, the single‐center retrospective design limits generalizability, and independent multicenter validation with harmonized protocols is warranted. Second, restriction of the cohort to surgically treated patients introduces selection bias toward eligible candidates, precluding extrapolation to conservatively managed populations. Third, the sample size, although relatively large for this surgical population, constrained the number of variables eligible for inclusion in the multivariable model. Fourth, completeness of epileptogenic zone resection was inferred from postoperative MRI rather than a standardized surgical metric, introducing a degree of measurement imprecision. Fifth, genetic testing was incomplete, and unmeasured molecular heterogeneity may also have influenced outcome. Sixth, neurodevelopmental outcomes were not systematically assessed; given that cognitive and behavioral trajectories are critical endpoints in this age group, future studies should incorporate standardized developmental evaluation alongside seizure outcome measures. These limitations define the interpretive boundaries of the present findings and underscore the need for prospective multicenter studies with harmonized protocols.

CONCLUSION

Overall, our results suggest that early surgery and complete lesion removal/disconnection are complementary goals in the management of drug‐resistant ES. Delayed intervention appears to permit progressive epileptogenic network consolidation, whereas residual postoperative lesions likely indicate persistent structural substrate for seizure generation. Together with histopathological subtype, these factors can improve risk stratification, refine family counseling, and help guide postoperative follow‐up strategies.
