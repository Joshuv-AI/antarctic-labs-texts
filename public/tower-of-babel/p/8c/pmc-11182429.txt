Rebound pain after interscalene brachial plexus block for shoulder surgery: a randomized clinical trial of the effect of different multimodal analgesia regimens
Authors: Tayfun Et, Betul Basaran, Aysegul Bilge, Rafet Yarımoğlu, Muhammet Korkusuz, İbrahim Tülüce
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND: Rebound pain is characterized by sudden, significant acute postoperative pain occurring after the resolution of inter-scalene block (ISB); it affects the quality of recovery postoperatively. Dexamethasone increases ISB resolution time and decreases opioid consumption and the incidence of rebound pain.OBJECTIVE: Evaluate whether multimodal analgesia including intravenous dexamethasone administration with preoperative ISB reduces the incidence of rebound pain.DESIGN: Prospective, randomized, controlled trialSETTING: Tertiary university hospitalSAMPLE SIZE: 60 patientsPATIENTS AND METHODS: Patients who underwent shoulder surgery under general anesthesia were assigned randomly to two different multimodal analgesia protocols. Thirty patients received 5 mg IV dexamethasone with non-steroid, paracetamol, and ISB with 15 mL 0.5% bupivacaine, while the control patients received the same regimen and ISB with 15 mL 0.5% bupivacaine without dexamethasone. Postoperative opioids were given to any patient on demand.MAIN OUTCOMES MEASURES: Effect of IV dexamethasone on pain score and incidence of rebound pain after ISB resolution and postoperative opioid consumption at 0–48 hours, numerical pain rating scale (NPRS) scores, sleep scale scores, and quality of recovery-15 scores (QoR-15).RESULTS: The incidence of rebound pain was lower in the dexamethasone group than in the control group (73.3% and 30%, respectively, P=.001). NPRS scores after ISB resolution were lower in the dexamethasone group (5 ([4–7]), 8 ([5.75–8]), P<.001, respectively). Those who received IV dexamethasone had less sleep disturbances (P<.001) and higher QoR-15 on day 1 (P<.001) and day 7 (P=.020) postoperatively.CONCLUSIONS: IV dexamethasone added to the ISB block resulted in a lower incidence of rebound pain. In addition, better results were obtained in postoperative sleep quality and QoR-15.LIMITATIONS: Single-center study

INTRODUCTION

Shoulder surgery requires a high amount of analgesia in the postoperative period. Single-shot injection interscalene block (ISB) is the standard in acute pain management, especially outpatient surgical procedures.1 ISB can provide many benefits, including better pain scores, less opioid consumption, and earlier hospital discharge of the patient.2 The duration of analgesia provided by the ISB differs depending on the drug used, dose concentration, use of a catheter and added adjuvant agents.2,3 ISB may provide clinically significant analgesic and opioid-protective effects lasting 12 or even 24 hours postoperatively. However, a significantly shorter duration of pain relief, not exceeding 6 to 8 hours, has been reported.2,3 In addition, patients receiving ISB may experience more pain within 24 hours postoperatively, resulting in higher analgesia requirements and/or hospital readmission. This is a hyperalgesic state defined as rebound pain following ISB resolution.4 The occurrence of rebound pain may outweigh the benefits of ISBs and may represent a more troubling problem in clinical terms.

There are many studies conducted on the single or continuous application of ISB and the addition of adjuvant agents.3 In one study, continuous ISB provided an improved analgesic profile within 12 hours postoperatively and patients did not report rebound pain in the first 24 hours.5 However, prolonged phrenic nerve palsy, accidental catheter removal, and insertion are limitations of the continuous catheter technique. Adjuvant agents are used to prevent severe and sudden rebound pain after the resolution of ISB, and the most preferred adjuvant agents are dexamethasone and dexmedetomidine.6,7 Studies report that dexamethasone increases the block duration and positively affects analgesia with IV and perineural use.6–8 In a recently published study, it was reported that the use of perineural dexamethasone prevented the development of rebound pain.9

However, perineural administration of dexamethasone may lead to a neurotoxic environment around exposed neurons and studies conducted on this subject are insufficient.10 There are many studies conducted on IV dexamethasone application with ISB, but no study has evaluated its effects on rebound pain.

Our study examined the incidence and pain score difference of low-dose IV dexamethasone in combination with single injection ISB on rebound pain before and after the block resolution in patients undergoing shoulder surgery under general anesthesia. This study also evaluated the effects of multimodal analgesia including 5 mg IV dexamethasone on postoperative opioid consumption, quality of recovery (QoR-15),11 and sleep quality.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

The Ethics Committee of Karamanoğlu Mehmetbey University approved this single-center, double-blind, randomized controlled study (decision number 01/2023–15). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients who participated in the study, in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, 2013.

We included patients between the ages of 18 and 80, with physical status I-III of the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) who were scheduled to undergo unilateral shoulder surgery between February 2023 and May 2023. Exclusion criteria were neuropathic disorder, severe cardiopulmonary disease, chronic opioid and steroid use, gastric ulcer, uncontrolled diabetes, psychological disorders, severe obesity (body mass index > 35 kg/m2), and pregnancy. The patients were informed preoperatively about the numerical pain rating scale (NPRS), sleep quality, and QoR-15 evaluations. All patients underwent open rotator cuff and acromioplasty repair by the same surgeon, and ISB was performed by an anesthesiologist with at least 10 years of experience in ultrasonography.

Randomization and blinding

Randomization sequences were generated by computer and placed in sealed opaque envelopes. A researcher not included in the study prepared 100 mL saline and added 5 mg of dexamethasone or 5 mL of saline for patients randomized to the dexamethasone group and control group. The drugs prepared after the ISB block application were infused within 15 minutes. All anesthesia and surgical team members, patients, outcome evaluators, and statisticians were blinded to the groups during the study.

Preoperative procedures

Oral cyclooxygenase-2 (celecoxib) 200 mg was given to the patients 1 hour before the surgery for pre-emptive analgesia. After standard monitoring (electrocardiogram, blood pressure, oxygen saturation) and IV access, 2 mg of midazolam was administered for sedation. The lateral face of the neck and the supraclavicular region ipsilateral to the surgical area were cleaned with an antiseptic solution. A linear ultrasound probe (6–13 MHz; Samsung RS85 Prestige, Seoul, South Korea) was placed in the interscalene space to determine the brachial plexus in the short axis under ultrasound guidance. With an in-plane approach with a 22-gauge insulated 50 mm regional needle (B. Braun), 15 ml of 0.5% bupivacaine was injected with negative aspiration after the needle tip was correctly positioned. After ISB was performed, an infusion of 100 mL of normal saline or 100 mL of normal saline with dexamethasone 5 mg was administered over 15 minutes.

İntraoperative procedures

General anesthesia was performed using 1–2 µg/kg and fentanyl IV 2 mg/kg IV propofol. Tracheal intubation was performed with 0.6 mg/kg IV rocuronium and anesthesia was maintained using 2% sevoflurane and 50% oxygen. Remifentanil (0.01–0.1 μg/kg/min) infusion was administered to keep the systolic blood pressure <100 mm Hg. After the patients were placed in the beach chair position, a surgeon performed all surgeries with an open rotator cuff and acromioplasty repair. Patients were extubated after reversing the residual muscle relaxation.

Multimodal analgesia

Pain and dermatome assessment was performed to evaluate the efficacy of ISB by a blinded investigator in the recovery room. ISB was considered unsuccessful if the NPRS was ≥3, in addition to no loss of cold sensation in the C5 and C6 sensory dermatomes.12 Patients were routinely administered paracetamol 1000 mg IV every 6 hours and celecoxib 200 mg every 12 hours orally in the ward. Oral oxycodone 5 mg rescue analgesia was administered to patients with NPRS >4. Discharge was planned after adequate analgesia (NPRS<4) and when patients were able to tolerate 50% of their range of motion targets for both elevation and external rotation.13 Analgesic management was provided with a combination of celecoxib and tramadol (37.5 mg)/acetaminophen (325 mg) as analgesia between 24–48 hours after the discharge, and follow-up was performed by phone.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the difference in pain scores and incidence of rebound pain before and after the resolution of ISB. The present study evaluated rebound pain as a measurable severe pain (NPRS ≥7) score before and after the ISB block resolution, consistent with published data.4,14,15 As a secondary outcome, time to resolution of ISB and the first opioid analgesic request, 48-hour analgesia consumption or oral morphine equivalent dose, and NPRS scores at 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 18, 24, and 48-hour were recorded. Postoperative 1 and 7 days QoR-15 values and block resolution times were also recorded. Perioperative and postoperative 24-hour glucose measurements, as well as the Likert classification, were used to evaluate the postoperative night and 6-day sleep. Patients were evaluated postoperatively for 14 days of surgical infection and 3 and 7 days of the residual block.

Sample size calculation

A pilot scheme was conducted with seven people to determine power analysis. The NPRS obtained as a result of the preliminary study was measured as mean (SD) of 5.0 (1.3). We expected a 30% decrease in the group administered IV dexamethasone to be clinically significant. The groups were planned to contain an equal number of patients. However, to compensate for possible problems, it was decided to collect 5% additional data and enroll 60 patients. We also calculated the sample size to provide a 50% decrease of rebound pain in the pilot study with a power of 80%. The required sample size for the rebound pain was 44 patients so we recruited 60 patients to cover both calculated sample sizes of the primary outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Normality distribution was evaluated by Shapiro-Wilk tests. Descriptive statistics of continuous variables were presented using mean (standard deviation) or median and interquartile range (IQR) or 25th-75th percentiles. Descriptive categorical data statistics were reported as numbers and percentages (%). The statistical significance level was evaluated as P<.05. Statistical analyses of the data obtained in our study were performed with the IBM SPSS (Version 22,0, Armonk, NY: IBM Corp) software.

RESULTS

Of 64 patients evaluated for eligibility before the study, 3 patients did not want to be included in the study, and one patient was excluded because of chronic opioid use. A total of 60 patients were included in the study; 30 patients were randomized to the dexamethasone group and 30 patients to the control group (Figure 1). Complete success was achieved in ISB in all patients. No significant differences were detected between the demographic and intraoperative data of the study participants (Table 1). After ISB resolution, the median (IQR) NPRS was 8 (1.8) in the control group and 5 (3) in the dexamethasone group (P<.001) (Figure 2).

DiSCUSSiON

In the present study, we demonstrated that IV dexamethasone effectively reduces the incidence of rebound pain in patients undergoing shoulder surgery under single-injection ISB. As well as prolonging the block time of intravenously administered dexamethasone, it also reduced opioid consumption after 12–24 hours. Also, patients who received IV dexamethasone reported better sleep quality, lower pain and nausea and vomiting scores on the night of surgery, as well as higher quality of recovery outcomes. No residual block or delayed wound healing was observed in any of the patients.

The mechanism of rebound pain that results from the resolution of a peripheral nerve block (PNB) is not fully understood, and there is no consensus on its defi-nition. Several studies with the definition of rebound pain have been increasing recently.4,16,17 Rebound pain is defined as mechanical-surgical pain caused by nociceptive inputs occurring dramatically following PNB resolution.4 In the present study, the NPRS value ≥7 after ISB resolution was taken as a criterion for evaluating rebound pain. Since it was shown that the incidence of rebound pain decreased as a result of the increased duration of the block, studies aimed at increasing the duration of the block have accelerated recently.18 In the present study, the ISB block resolution time was prolonged in the group administered IV dexamethasone. Also, an inflammatory cascade occurs in the afferent nerve as a result of surgical trauma.19 Transmission of pain signals induced by tissue damage leads to sensitization of the peripheral and central pain pathways. It has been suggested that IV dexamethasone reduces the release of inflammatory mediators, acts on ectopic neuronal discharge, and inhibits potassium channel-mediated discharge of nociceptive C-fibres.4,20–22

The effects of dexamethasone are most pronounced in the first 24–48 hours.19,22,23 For this reason, dexamethasone continues to maintain therapeutic levels throughout the postoperative period, when inflammation and postoperative pain are at their highest. The important question regarding the reduction of the incidence of rebound pain by IV dexamethasone is to prolong the duration of ISB analgesia or due to the pharmacodynamic effect of dexamethasone. Consequently, IV dexamethasone not only prolongs the duration of the local anesthetic effect but may also be preferred in the application of ISB to reduce the incidence of rebound through other unexplained mechanisms. The low incidence of IV dexamethasone rebound pain and the sensory block's prolonged duration seem to be a major factor, along with the anti-inflammatory effect. High-dose analgesics are needed to relieve the pain after rebound pain develops and after the development of rebound pain, a high need for analgesia emerges in the 12–24 hour period. The need for opioids decreased in the postoperative 12–24 hour period in patients who were administered dexamethasone in the preoperative period, and the total opioid use was lower in the patient group who received dexamethasone in the first 48 hours simultaneously.

Holmberg et al24 found that IV dexamethasone with ISB for distal radius fracture surgery effectively reduced rebound pain; however, they used high dose (16 mg) dexamethasone. In addition, the incidence of rebound pain is higher after shoulder surgery than in orthopedic interventions. In a recently published study, they interpreted IV administration of dexamethasone to be the only modifiable factor that could lead to a reduction in rebound pain.15 However, the study was heterogeneous and the use of a wide range of dexamethasone doses (4–20 mg) was evaluated. In our study, we used low-dose (5 mg) dexamethasone added to ISB block only in shoulder surgery. Consequently, the low-dose dexamethasone could be more efficacious in patients who had frequent rebound pain in the early postop period.

Dexamethasone is a potent glucocorticoid that, regardless of the route of administration, whether IV or perineural, improves block quality and prolongs time after co-administration with PNB.6,25 Studies have shown that both methods have a similar effect in controlling the duration of block, nausea and vomiting and maintaining opioid consumption beyond reduction in the incidence of rebound pain in the early postoperative period.9,24,26 However, the lack of adequate studies conducted on the long-term effects of perineural administration also remains a major concern. Furthermore, perineural dexamethasone may result in potential hazards arising from neurotoxicity or crystallization. Since its mechanism of action cannot be fully explained. Although the reparative effects of perineural dexamethasone on damaged nerve cells have been demonstrated only in in vitro studies, it has not been shown on normal nervous tissue.27 To the contrary, in an in vitro study, evidence of neurotoxicity caused by the use of high concentrations and long-term use of dexamethasone in combination with ropivacaine was reported.10 Moreover, a recently published retrospective study showed that the peri-neural dexamethasone group was 2 times more likely to have a permanent deficiency and symptoms in the postoperative 6-month following foot and ankle surgery.28 We think that IV dexamethasone administration might be preferred over perineural administration since no hazardous effects have been reported on the neural structures. However, longer follow-up and comprehensive studies are needed.

The optimal dose of dexamethasone in addition to ISB to provide postoperative analgesia is controversial. Also, the equivalence of perineural dexamethasone and IV dexamethasone has not yet been demonstrated.2,7,8,29 However, in the present study we decided that the administration of low-dose dexamethasone would be more appropriate, since there was no difference between low dose (4 mg) and high dose (10 mg) in terms of analgesic duration, and any possible side effects of high-dose dexamethasone such as hyperglycemia and perineal itching would be avoided with a low dose.30 was argued in a study that compared the effects of 4 mg IV dexamethasone and 4 mg perineural dexamethasone that 4 mg dexamethasone is too low to prolong analgesia.31 However, when evaluating this outcome, the study should have insufficient power and the differences in the outcome measures should be kept in mind. For this reason, in the present study, 5 mg perineural dexamethasone IV was administered, which effectively prolonged the duration of the block. We think the absence of a significant difference in preoperative and postoperative glucose evaluation occurs because of the use of low-dose IV dexamethasone. We believe that the low-dose IV dexamethasone used simultaneously does not affect wound healing negatively.

The QoR-15 questionnaire was used in the study to measure the quality of recovery after surgery and anesthesia. As a result of the prolonged block time after the patients were operated on in the morning, patients who were administered dexamethasone had slept comfortably the first night.9 As stated in previous studies, since surgery is usually performed during the day, many blocks wear off at night, resulting in rebound pain.9 As a result, the sleep quality of the patients deteriorates and they start to suffer from sleep problems for the following few days. Lower nausea/vomiting and better sleep values were obtained in the dexamethasone group after the postoperative 1-day QoR-15 evaluation. Accordingly, it resulted in better-resting rates and higher psychological values in the dexamethasone group. As a result of the low incidence of rebound pain, moderate and severe pain were significantly lower and made the patients feel more controlled and comfortable. We think that this effect of rebound pain on sleep quality and QoR undeniably impairs early postoperative period.

The study had some limitations. Firstly, shoulder surgery is performed openly in our clinic. However, there is no evidence that arthroscopic surgery is superior to open shoulder surgery in evaluating postoperative pain.32 Secondly, the absence of the perineural dexamethasone group and/or the catheterized group has great importance in evaluating efficacy. Thirdly, as a result of postoperatively holding the patients with a fixed shoulder strap, the evaluation of NPRS is only at rest and cannot be evaluated in motion. Finally, the postoperative drug use of the patients was evaluated by phone calls, and based on the patient statement, there was no ability to verify compliance.

In conclusion, IV dexamethasone reduces the incidence of rebound pain by achieving lower pain scores after resolution of the ISB block. It was shown that IV dexamethasone reduces postoperative 12–24-hour analgesia consumption. It has great importance in improving the postoperative sleep quality and QoR-15 of the patients most probably due to a lower incidence of rebound pain and prolonging the duration of analgesia of ISB. There is a need for studies to be conducted on the dose of IV dexamethasone on rebound pain and the risk of postoperative complications at this dose to increase the duration of analgesia of ISB and decrease the incidence of rebound pain.
