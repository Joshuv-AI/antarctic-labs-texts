2025 Obesity Fact Sheet for Korea: Prevalence of Obesity, Abdominal Obesity from 2014 to 2023 and Prevalence of Chronic Disease by Obesity Status
Authors: Esther Kim, Sinyoung Cho, Jiyoung Kim, Bongseong Kim, Ho-tak Seo, Ga Eun Nam, In Young Cho, Kye-Yeung Park, Su-Min Jeong, Kyungdo Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe 2025 obesity fact sheet provides an updated overview of the prevalence of obesity and abdominal obesity, as well as the prevalence of major chronic diseases according to obesity status in Korea.MethodsThis study analyzed data from 17,576,389 adults who underwent general health examinations provided by the National Health Insurance Service from 2014 to 2023 and 17,660 participants from the Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2019–2021).ResultsIn 2023, the prevalence of obesity and abdominal obesity was 38.4% (male 49.8%, female 27.5%) and 24.3% (male 31.3%, female 17.7%), respectively. Class II obesity was most prevalent among individuals aged 35–39 years (9.4%), and class III obesity peaked among those aged 30–34 years (2.6%). The prevalence of chronic diseases—including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hypercholesterolemia, metabolic syndrome, and hyperuricemia—was 1.5- to 3.6-fold higher in individuals with obesity than in those without obesity, with particularly large differences in young adults and female. The prevalence of obstructive sleep apnea was 4.5-fold higher in the obese group, whereas osteoporosis was less common in the obese group than the non-obese group.ConclusionThe 2025 obesity fact sheet provides updated national data on obesity, abdominal obesity, and obesity-related chronic diseases in Korea, highlighting the substantial and persistent burden associated with obesity. These findings emphasize the urgent need for timely and effective strategies for obesity prevention and management.

INTRODUCTION

The rise in obesity is a major public health challenge that affected more than 1.0 billion adult men and 1.1 billion adult female worldwide in 2021.1 Obesity increases the risk of chronic metabolic diseases such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia, which in turn lead to serious cardiovascular complications, including myocardial infarction and stroke.2 The global burden of obesity has also continued to increase during the past decades, with the age-standardized disability-adjusted life year rate attributable to excess body weight rising by 18.0% (95% uncertainty interval, 2.2 to 42.3) from 1990 to 2019.3

Obesity is associated with not only major chronic metabolic diseases, but also other conditions, including musculoskeletal diseases such as osteoarthritis and sleep-related disorders such as obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). These conditions reduce physical function, impair mental health, and lower overall quality of life. As the degree of obesity increases, the likelihood and severity of multimorbidity rise substantially, with recent evidence showing that obesity-related multimorbidity occurs more frequently at a younger age in individuals with class II and III obesity than in those who are overweight.4 This growing clustering of obesity-related diseases underscores obesity as a key contributor to multimorbidity and highlights its increasing burden on population health.

Since 2015, the Korean Society for the Study of Obesity (KSSO) has published annual obesity fact sheets to raise public awareness and provide evidence to inform national health policies on obesity prevention and management. These fact sheets have presented updates on obesity trends and associated health indicators across sex and age groups, based on nationally representative datasets. Building upon previous reports, the 2025 obesity fact sheet extends its scope to examine obesity-related chronic diseases. It describes the most recent prevalence trends in obesity and abdominal obesity from 2014 to 2023 and analyzes the prevalence of major chronic diseases according to obesity status in the Korean population.

METHODS

Data resource and study participants

This study analyzed two national datasets from South Korea: the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) Health Checkup Database (2014–2023) and the Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) VIII (2019–2021). The NHIS data were primarily used to assess trends in the prevalence of obesity and abdominal obesity. KNHANES data were analyzed to evaluate the prevalence of chronic diseases according to obesity status. In Korea, the NHIS covers 97% of the population, with the remaining 3%, representing the lowest-income group, receiving support through medical aid.5 The NHIS health screening program has long been conducted every 2 years for individuals aged 40 years and older. In 2019, the program was expanded to include non-employed adults aged 20 to 39 years, thereby extending national health checkup eligibility to all adults aged 20 years and above.

A total of 17,576,389 individuals received at least one NHIS National Health Checkup between 2014 and 2023. The checkup consists of a self-administered questionnaire on lifestyle behaviors, anthropometric assessments performed by trained personnel according to standardized protocols, and laboratory tests.6

The KNHANES, conducted by the Korean Disease Control and Prevention Agency, uses a multistage, clustered probability sampling method to represent the non-institutionalized Korean population.7 It also includes a self-reported questionnaire, anthropometric measurements, and laboratory assessments. Among the 22,559 participants in KNHANES VIII, 3,868 individuals younger than 20 years of age and 1,031 participants who were missing age, gender, or body mass index (BMI) data, were excluded, leaving a final analytic sample of 17,660 individuals.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No. E-2511-074-1692) and the NHIS review board. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the use of anonymized data and the retrospective study design. The study complies with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Definitions of obesity and abdominal obesity

BMI was calculated as body weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters. Obesity was defined as a BMI ≥25 kg/m2, in accordance with the Asia-Pacific criteria of the World Health Organization guidelines.8 Obesity classification was further categorized based on the 2022 KSSO guideline for the management of obesity as underweight (BMI <18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (BMI 18.5–22.9 kg/m²), overweight (BMI 23.0–24.9 kg/m²), class I obesity (BMI 25.0–29.9 kg/m²), class II obesity (BMI 30.0–34.9 kg/m²), and class III obesity (BMI ≥35 kg/m²).9 Abdominal obesity was defined as a waist circumference ≥90 cm for male or ≥85 cm for female.10

Definition of chronic disease

The following definitions of chronic disease were used to analyze the KNHANES data. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg, a diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg, or current use of antihypertensive medication.11 Diabetes mellitus was defined as a fasting plasma glucose level ≥126 mg/dL, glycosylated hemoglobin≥6.5%, current use of antidiabetic medication or insulin, or a physician’s diagnosis.12 Hypercholesterolemia was defined as a total cholesterol level ≥240 mg/dL or current use of lipid-lowering medication.13 Metabolic syndrome was defined according to the criteria proposed in the 2022 KSSO guidelines for the management of obesity in Korea as the presence of three or more of the following criteria:14 (1) waist circumference ≥90 cm for male or ≥85 cm for female; (2) fasting glucose ≥100 mg/dL or current use of antidiabetic medication or insulin; (3) blood pressure ≥130/85 mmHg or current use of antihypertensive medications; (4) triglyceride level ≥150 mg/dL or current use of lipid-lowering medications; and (5) high-density lipoprotein cholesterol <40 mg/dL for male or <50 mg/dL for female, or current use of lipid-lowering medication. Hyperuricemia was defined as a serum uric acid level ≥7.0 mg/dL in male or ≥6.0 mg/dL in female. Osteoarthritis, osteoporosis, and depression were defined based on a physician’s diagnosis. OSA was defined as meeting five or more of the eight criteria in the STOP-Bang (Snore, Tired, Observed, Pressure, BMI, Age, Neck, Gender) questionnaire.15 Chronic kidney disease (CKD) was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate <60 mL/min/1.73 m², calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation.16

Statistical analysis

The prevalence of obesity and abdominal obesity from 2014 to 2023 was age- and sex-standardized using 2010 Population and Housing Census data from Statistics Korea. Prevalence estimates were examined by sex and age, which was categorized into seven groups: 20s, 30s, 40s, 50s, 60s, 70s, and ≥80 years. The prevalence of chronic diseases according to obesity status was analyzed using KNHANES VIII (2019–2021) data, and obese individuals (BMI ≥25 kg/m²) were compared with their non-obese counterparts (BMI <25 kg/m²). To account for potential confounding by age, age-standardized prevalence estimates were calculated using direct standardization methods, taking the 2005 Korean census population as the standard population. Differences in prevalence between obese and non-obese groups were statistically tested, and the results are presented with 95% confidence intervals. Subgroup analyses were conducted by sex and age group (19–39, 40–64, and ≥65 years). Within the age-stratified analyses, crude prevalence estimates were compared between the obese and non-obese groups, and corresponding P-values were calculated, as age stratification itself controls for age effects. Sampling weights were applied in the analysis of KNHANES data. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.).

RESULTS

Prevalence of obesity and abdominal obesity by age and gender

From 2014 to 2023, the overall prevalence of obesity increased by 1.23-fold, from 31.1% to 38.4% (Fig. 1A). The prevalence of obesity showed a steady rise from 2014 and has remained stable at around 38.4% for the past 3 years. Among male, the prevalence continued to increase, reaching 49.8% in 2023, whereas among female, it remained relatively unchanged, from 27.8% in 2021 to 27.5% in 2023. The prevalence of abdominal obesity in the overall population increased from 20.2% in 2014 to 24.3% in 2023 (Fig. 1B), but it has remained relatively stable during the past 3 years. In male, the prevalence reached 31.3% in 2023, whereas in female, it peaked at 19.0% in 2019 and subsequently declined to 17.7% in 2023.

In 2023, the prevalence of obesity was highest among individuals in their 30s and 40s, followed by those in their 70s (Fig. 2A). In 2023, the prevalence of obesity in the total population was 32.0%, 42.0%, 42.2%, 39.4%, 38.7%, 40.1%, and 35.0% among those in their 20s, 30s, 40s, 50s, 60s, 70s, and ≥80s, respectively. Although the prevalence increased across all age groups, compared with 2014, a recent decline was observed among individuals in their 60s and 70s. The prevalence was lowest among those in their 20s. However, it markedly increased in that age group from 22.7% in 2014 to 32.0% in 2023.

Abdominal obesity was most prevalent among adults aged ≥80 years, followed by those in their 70s and 60s (Fig. 2B). In 2023, the prevalence of abdominal obesity in the total population was 16.1%, 25.3%, 26.1%, 24.0%, 27.3%, 34.7%, and 37.2% among those in their 20s, 30s, 40s, 50s, 60s, 70s, and 80s or older, respectively. The prevalence has been stable or declining across most age groups in recent years, particularly since 2019 among those in their 60s and 70s. However, it has continued to rise among individuals in their 20s, from 11.7% in 2014 to 16.1% in 2023.

Prevalence of obesity according to obesity class by age and gender

In 2023, the prevalence of class I obesity was highest among individuals aged 70–74 years (35.6%) (Fig. 3A). The highest prevalence was observed for male aged 45–49 years (44.0%) and for female aged 75–79 years (36.0%). The prevalence of class II obesity was highest among individuals aged 35–39 years in the total population (9.4%) and also in male (12.0%) (Fig. 3B). In female, the prevalence peaked in those aged 35–39 and 75–79 years (5.5%). The prevalence of class III obesity was highest among individuals aged 30–34 years (2.57%), gradually decreased with advancing age, and remained relatively stable after 60 years of age (Fig. 3C). The highest prevalence of class III obesity for male was in those aged 20–24 years (3.22%), and for female it was those aged 35–39 years (1.86%).

Prevalence of chronic disease by obesity status

Table 1 presents the age-standardized prevalence of chronic diseases according to obesity status for the overall population and by sex. Table 2 shows the corresponding prevalence stratified by age group (<40, 40–64, and ≥65 years). In 2023, the prevalence of hypertension was 32.70% in the obese group and 16.01% in the non-obese group, representing a 2-fold difference. The prevalence among obese individuals was 34.52% in male and 27.65% in female, corresponding to 1.9- and 1.8-fold higher rates, respectively, than in their non-obese counterparts. The greatest disparity was observed among adults aged 19–39 years, with a 5.1-fold higher prevalence of hypertension in the obese group (15.24% vs. 2.97%).

For diabetes mellitus, the prevalence was 15.94% in the obese group and 6.79% in the non-obese group, a 2.3-fold difference. Among obese individuals, the prevalence was 16.11% in male and 15.43% in female, with a 2.6-fold higher rate in female (6.03% vs. 15.43%). The largest difference was observed in the 19–39-year-old age group: the prevalence in the obese group was 5.61%, which was 13.0-fold higher than in the non-obese group (0.43%).

The prevalence of hypercholesterolemia was 24.93% in the obese group, 1.5-fold higher than in the non-obese group. The prevalence was 23.65% in male (1.5-fold) and 25.28% in female (1.4-fold). Among those aged 19–39 years, the prevalence was 2.1-fold higher in the obese group (11.82% vs. 5.67%).

Metabolic syndrome showed a markedly higher prevalence in the obese group across all subgroups. The overall prevalence was 51.64% in the obese group, 3.6-fold higher than in the non-obese group. The prevalence among obese individuals was 53.04% in male (3.3-fold) and 47.79% in female (3.5-fold). By age group, the prevalence in the obese group was 34.78% in those aged 19–39 years (13.1-fold), 64.12% in those aged 40–64 years (3.1-fold), and 80.18% in those aged ≥65 years (1.8-fold).

For hyperuricemia, the prevalence was 28.28% in the obese group and 10.62% in the non-obese group, corresponding to a 2.7-fold difference. The prevalence was 34.53% in male (1.9-fold) and 16.02% in female (3.1-fold). The difference was most prominent among individuals aged 19–39 years, with a 3.2-fold higher prevalence in the obese group (38.26% vs. 11.93%).

The prevalence of osteoarthritis was 7.44% in the obese group and 5.25% in the non-obese group, representing a 1.4-fold higher prevalence in the obese group. Among obese individuals, the prevalence was 3.48% in male (1.5-fold) and 11.88% in female (1.4-fold). The largest difference was observed in the 40–64-year age group: the prevalence was 8.35% in the obese group, which was 1.6-fold higher than in the non-obese group. In contrast, the prevalence of osteoporosis was lower in the obese group (3.38%) than in the non-obese group (4.21%), corresponding to a 0.8-fold difference. This difference was statistically significant in female (6.55% vs. 8.15%, P<0.05) but not in male (0.52% vs. 0.59%, P=0.66). In the age-stratified analyses, a significant difference in prevalence was observed among adults aged 40–64 years, with a lower prevalence in the obese group than the non-obese group (2.60% vs. 4.38%, P<0.05). Among adults aged ≥65 years, although the prevalence was numerically higher in the non-obese group than in the obese group (23.07% vs. 21.02%), the difference was not statistically significant (P=0.16).

For depression, the overall prevalence did not differ between the obese and non-obese groups (4.56% vs. 4.53%, P=0.94). However, among female, the prevalence was higher in the obese group (7.81% vs. 5.84%, P<0.05). The prevalence of OSA was markedly higher in the obese group (14.24%) than the non-obese group (3.16%), representing a 4.5-fold difference. The prevalence was 24.75% in male (3.6-fold) and 2.12% in female (23.6-fold). The difference was also evident across age groups, with significantly higher prevalence in the obese group among individuals aged 40–64 and ≥65 years (5.1- and 5.4-fold, respectively).

For CKD, the overall prevalence was 1.57% in the obese group and 1.02% in the non-obese group (1.5-fold higher). Among male, the prevalence was higher in the obese group (1.52% vs. 1.07%, P=0.04), and among female, the prevalence was also higher in the obese group (1.61% vs. 0.95%, P<0.05). The prevalence in the obese group was 1.28% in adults aged 40–64 years (2.3-fold) and 9.39% in those aged ≥65 years (1.3-fold).

DISCUSSION

As shown here in the 2025 obesity fact sheet, the overall prevalence of obesity has increased during the past decade, rising from 31.1% in 2014 to 38.4% in 2023, a 1.23-fold increase. The prevalence of abdominal obesity also increased, from 20.2% in 2014 to 24.3% in 2023. The prevalence of both obesity and abdominal obesity continued to increase among male, whereas a decreasing trend was observed among female during the past 3 years. The increase in obesity prevalence was more pronounced in young adults (20s) and elderly people (≥80 years old) than in middle-aged individuals. The prevalence of abdominal obesity continued to increase among young adults (20s and 40s), but decreased among those in their 60s and 70s. By obesity class, class I obesity was most prevalent among individuals aged 70–74 years (35.6%), whereas class II and III obesity were most common among those in their 30s. The prevalence of hypertension and metabolic diseases (diabetes mellitus, hypercholesterolemia, metabolic syndrome, and hyperuricemia) was consistently higher in the obese group than in the non-obese group, with the difference being most pronounced among female and those aged 19–39 years. The prevalence of osteoarthritis was higher among individuals with obesity, whereas osteoporosis was less common in that group. The prevalence of depression was similar between obese and non-obese individuals. The prevalence of OSA was 4.5 times higher, and that of CKD was 1.5 times higher in individuals with obesity than in those without obesity.

Obesity and abdominal obesity prevalence

During the past few decades, the prevalence of obesity has risen steadily worldwide and become a major public health concern. According to the World Health Organization, the global prevalence of obesity has more than doubled since 1990, with 43% of adults aged 18 years and older classified as overweight and 16% living with obesity in 2022.17 Similar upward trends have been observed across the world, although recent data suggest that obesity rates might have reached a plateau in some high-income countries.18,19

In Korea, consistent with global patterns, both obesity and abdominal obesity have increased markedly during the past decade. However, the recent data presented here indicate that the overall prevalence has stabilized during the past 3 years, particularly among female. This plateau might partly reflect the growing use of anti-obesity medications, such as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists (e.g., semaglutide and tirzepatide), which have rapidly gained popularity since 2021. According to a market-research report by Spherical Insights, the global GLP-1 receptor agonist market was valued at United States dollar (USD) 13.4 billion in 2022, and demand is projected to be fastest-growing in the Asia-Pacific region.20 Also, increased public awareness of weight management, greater access to digital health tools, and broader health screening participation might also have contributed to the recent stabilization.

Nevertheless, despite the recent plateau, the absolute prevalence of obesity and abdominal obesity remains high, suggesting that obesity continues to be a substantial burden on public health and requires ongoing preventive efforts and policy interventions.

Class II and III obesity

The prevalence of class II and III obesity was highest among individuals in their 20s and 30s, suggesting that severe obesity is increasingly affecting young people. Obesity in early adulthood is particularly concerning because it not only increases lifetime exposure to metabolic and cardiovascular risks but also tends to persist throughout life once established.21-23 In the Korean context, this pattern might, in part, reflect unhealthy lifestyle habits formed during adolescence, when many students prioritize academic achievement over physical activity and balanced nutrition during preparation for university entrance exams.24 Such behaviors, if sustained into adulthood, can contribute to the development of severe obesity and related chronic diseases at a young age.25 Additionally, adults in their 20s and 30s often face new stressors such as university studies, job preparation, and early career demands that can lead them to prioritize work or academic activities over regular exercise, balanced meals, and adequate sleep. The increased autonomy associated with this life stage could also contribute to greater reliance on convenience foods and delivery meals, further reinforcing unhealthy habits. Therefore, targeted strategies are needed to promote healthy dietary and physical activity habits from adolescence through young adulthood. School-based interventions, structured physical education programs, and health education emphasizing sustainable lifestyle behaviors could play a crucial role in preventing the early onset of severe obesity and its long-term consequences.

Prevalence of chronic disease in obesity

The present findings demonstrate that obesity is strongly associated with a higher prevalence of multiple chronic diseases, particularly hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hypercholesterolemia, metabolic syndrome, and hyperuricemia. Importantly, the relative gaps between obese and non-obese adults were largest in the 19–39-year age group, suggesting that obesity in early adulthood might accelerate the onset of metabolic disorders and lead to greater lifetime exposure to cardiometabolic risks.

In contrast to metabolic diseases, osteoporosis showed an opposite trend: osteoporosis tended to be less common in individuals with obesity in certain subgroups, particularly female and middle-aged adults, whereas osteoarthritis was more common in individuals with obesity. This pattern is consistent with previous reports that higher body weight can have a protective effect on bone mineral density through mechanical loading and endocrine factors (e.g., higher estrogen levels and leptin-related pathways), resulting in a lower prevalence of osteoporosis in obese individuals.26 Although obesity is generally associated with higher bone mineral density, that does not necessarily translate into a lower risk of fractures. In fact, several studies have reported that obesity can increase the fracture risk, suggesting that the relationship between obesity and bone health is complex and multifactorial.27-29 Also, excess body weight increases mechanical stress on weight-bearing joints, especially in female and middle-aged adults, which likely explains the higher prevalence of osteoarthritis in the obese group. This finding underscores that weight control in obese adults needs to balance bone health and joint health, rather than assuming that obesity is uniformly protective for the musculoskeletal system.

The overall prevalence of depression did not differ between obese and non-obese adults across age groups. However, in the age-standardized analyses, female with obesity had a significantly higher prevalence of depression than non-obese female, whereas no significant difference was observed among male. This finding could partly be related to gender-specific sociocultural factors, such as weight stigma, and warrants further investigation.

OSA was strikingly more prevalent in the obese group (4.5-fold overall), and the difference remained large across all age groups. This is in line with existing evidence that central obesity and upper-airway adiposity are key contributors to OSA severity.30 This suggests that primary care physicians and obesity clinicians should routinely screen individuals with obesity for snoring, witnessed apneas, and daytime sleepiness because untreated OSA worsens hypertension, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular risk.31

For CKD, the overall difference between obese and non-obese adults was modest, and the association seemed to be driven mainly by female and those aged 40–64 years. Because the association was not consistent in young adults, further analyses (e.g., stratification by abdominal obesity, diabetes status, or duration of obesity) are warranted.

Obesity is now increasingly recognized as a treatable chronic disease that requires active and sustained intervention. Recent advances, particularly the use of GLP-1 receptor agonists, have demonstrated not only substantial weight reduction but also a wide range of clinical benefits, including cardiovascular risk reduction, improved glycemic control, and improvement in OSA.32-34 The World Health Organization acknowledges that GLP-1–based therapies extend beyond weight loss and can contribute to the prevention and management of major obesity-related chronic diseases, recommending their use as part of a complementary and integrated treatment strategy that also involves lifestyle interventions.35 This paradigm shift highlights the clinical and public health importance of active obesity intervention and provides a strong rationale for prioritizing obesity management in health policy. Despite recent plateaus in obesity prevalence trends, the absolute prevalence of obesity and abdominal obesity remains high, underscoring the continued need for ongoing preventive efforts and policy interventions to reduce the burden of obesity-related chronic diseases.

This study has several limitations. Although the NHIS dataset includes a large population who voluntarily participated in the national health screening program, participants in such checkups are likely to be more health-conscious and engage in healthier behaviors than the general population. In addition, revisions to the NHIS screening eligibility criteria between 2014 and 2023 might have influenced the composition of the target population, particularly with respect to age distribution. In turn, the cross-sectional nature of the KNHANES data precludes establishing causal relationships between obesity status and chronic diseases. Furthermore, although age-standardized prevalence was used in the overall and sex-specific analyses, the age-stratified results were based on crude prevalence, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Future studies incorporating additional confounders are warranted. A notable strength of this study is the inclusion of two large, nationally representative datasets with standardized anthropometric and laboratory measurements, which allowed for robust and generalizable estimates of obesity trends and related health outcomes. In addition, this study comprehensively examined the associations between obesity status and a wide range of chronic diseases, including metabolic, musculoskeletal, psychological, renal, and sleep-related conditions, which allowed the provision of a more holistic view of the health burden associated with obesity than in previous national reports.

In conclusion, the 2025 obesity fact sheet reveals that the prevalence of obesity continues to increase in Korea, with a particularly notable rise in class II and III obesity in young adults. Furthermore, obesity is associated with a substantially higher prevalence of various chronic diseases. These findings provide an updated overview of the national obesity landscape and underscore the need for timely, population-based strategies to prevent the growing impact of obesity.
