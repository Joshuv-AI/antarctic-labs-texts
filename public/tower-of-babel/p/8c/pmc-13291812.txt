GroupStruct2: A User-Friendly Graphical User Interface for Statistical and Visual Support in Species Diagnosis
Authors: Kin Onn Chan, L Lee Grismer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStatistically defensible species diagnoses are essential for producing robust taxonomies, which underpin much of biological research. Yet, most species descriptions remain largely descriptive, often lack rigorous statistical validation, and suffer from confounding factors stemming from sampling bias, geographic variation, and intraspecific diversity. These limitations are further compounded by the steep learning curve of advanced statistical and data visualization tools, which poses a major barrier for early-career scientists and researchers in under-resourced regions who may lack programming experience or access to expert support. To address these challenges, we developed GroupStruct2, a powerful yet accessible R-based Shiny application that democratizes robust statistical analysis and data visualization for species diagnosis across any taxonomic group. GroupStruct2 is implemented through a user-friendly graphical user interface (GUI) that requires no coding experience. The user-friendly interface intuitively guides users through a comprehensive workflow, from raw data upload and outlier detection to assumption testing, adaptive statistical analyses, and the generation of highly customizable, publication-ready visualizations based on the ggplot2 architecture. It supports allometric body-size correction and widely used dimension-reduction techniques, including Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Discriminant Analysis of Principal Components (DAPC), and, critically, Multiple Factor Analysis (MFA), which enables the joint analysis of meristic, morphometric, and categorical trait data within a single integrative taxonomic framework. We showcase GroupStruct2’s capabilities using two empirical data sets, demonstrating how to conduct robust statistical analyses and produce publication-quality visualizations in just a few clicks. By lowering technical barriers without compromising analytical rigor, GroupStruct2 empowers researchers of all backgrounds working on any taxonomic group to conduct statistically sound and produce visually compelling species diagnoses, thereby advancing both the accessibility and the scientific rigor of taxonomy, species delimitation, and biodiversity research.

Although the genetic era has revolutionized and accelerated species discovery, non-genetic data such as morphological, ecological, and natural history traits continue to play an essential role in fundamental biodiversity research, management, and conservation. In practice, non-genetic data are still the primary source of information used to classify, diagnose, and identify species. Therefore, a robust and accurate characterization of key traits that differentiate one taxon from another (i.e., species diagnosis) is critical. An effective species diagnosis should not only identify how traits differ but also demonstrate that those differences are statistically significant, while accounting for confounding factors such as sample size and geographic, ontogenetic, and/or intraspecific variation. Surprisingly, even after more than a century since the advent of modern statistics, most species diagnoses remain largely descriptive, merely summarizing character states without robust statistical validation (Chan and Grismer 2021; Grismer 2025). This lack of rigor can undermine both species identification and downstream research that depends on reliable trait characterizations or species delineations (Kholia and Fraser-Jenkins 2011; Ensing et al. 2013; Borkent 2021).

Several barriers may contribute to the slow adoption of modern statistical tools for species diagnosis, including the cost of proprietary software (Hazarika 2019) and a lack of expertise or support in statistical methods (Kenneh et al. 2023). To address these challenges, we developed GroupStruct2, a powerful yet accessible R-based Shiny application that democratizes robust statistical analysis and data visualization for species diagnosis that can be broadly applied across any taxonomic group. Designed with a user-friendly graphical user interface (GUI), GroupStruct2 requires no coding experience and intuitively guides users through a comprehensive workflow—from uploading raw data and detecting outliers to assumption testing, adaptive statistical analyses, and the generation of highly customizable, publication-ready visualizations. One of the key innovations of GroupStruct2 is the implementation of Multiple Factor Analysis (MFA), enabling the joint analysis of meristic, morphometric, and categorical trait data within a single integrative taxonomic framework (Grismer 2025). Although the primary motivation of GroupStruct2 is to enhance species diagnosis, the application can be co-opted to infer group structure in any numeric or numeric + categorical data sets, even non-biological ones.

GroupStruct2 builds on the original GroupStruct package (Chan and Grismer 2022), which primarily offered allometric size correction using the allom() function and a monolithic wrapper, morpho_struct() that performs a fixed, en bloc pipeline including allometric size correction, Principal Component Analysis (PCA), and univariate statistical analyses. Although the allometric correction function in GroupStruct was widely adopted, the downstream wrapper saw limited use, likely because its rigid design reduced the flexibility and interpretability of the individual analyses and results. GroupStruct2 addresses these limitations by introducing a complete GUI, expanding the repertoire and robustness of analyses, and adopting a modular architecture to increase the flexibility and transparency of each analysis. Each analysis runs independently with configurable parameters (e.g., P-value thresholds) and reactive functions, ensuring that any change in parameter settings is reflected in the results and figures in real time. This greatly facilitates and encourages users to explore analyses using different parameter settings. Additionally, tabular and graphical outputs are downloadable in publication-ready formats. This overall design promotes transparent, flexible, and exploratory analyses without forcing users into a single, fixed pipeline, while providing highly customizable visualizations suited to the diverse and unique needs of each user and study.

We showcase GroupStruct2’s capabilities using two published empirical data sets from cryptic species complexes of geckos and fishes, demonstrating how robust statistical analyses and publication-quality visualizations can be produced in just a few clicks. By lowering technical barriers, while maintaining analytical rigor, GroupStruct2 empowers researchers of all backgrounds to conduct statistically sound and visually compelling species diagnoses, thereby advancing both the accessibility and the scientific rigor of taxonomy, species delimitation, and biodiversity research.

Materials and Methods

Application Overview

The GroupStruct2 GUI is organized into modules and nested submodules that intuitively guide users through a streamlined workflow, from raw data input to customizable, publication-ready visualizations (Fig. 1). The implementation of each module and submodule is accompanied by detailed documentation and instructions to minimize user error. The application contains three modules corresponding to three trait data types: Meristic (discrete countable traits), Morphometric (continuous measured traits), and Mixed Data (numeric + categorical traits) (Fig. 1). For the Mixed Data module, numeric data are not restricted to mensural or meristic traits and can include other types of quantitative variables that are biologically relevant for species diagnosis (e.g., bioacoustic, physiological, ecological, or environmental parameters). Frequency data from a contingency table are not supported. Each module contains a series of submodules that perform various analyses. Some submodules are shared across all data types, such as input data, summary statistics, and visualization, whereas others contain operations specific to particular data types. For example, allometric body-size correction is available only for morphometric data, and MFA is implemented exclusively for mixed data.

An overview of the GroupStruct2 analytical framework. Blue boxes (first row) = main modules, orange boxes (subsequent rows) = submodules. The overlap of the submodule boxes indicates in which module they reside (e.g., MFA is exclusive to the Mixed Data module). Blue arrows show the logical progression of workflows starting with data input and ending with visualization. These workflows are merely guides and are not fixed. Users can choose to backtrack to any submodule at any step, providing flexibility and encouraging users to explore different analytical methods and parameters.

All analytical results can be downloaded as publication-ready tables. Similarly, the Visualization submodule generates both exploratory (scatterplots, boxplots, violin plots) and diagnostic graphics (PCA, Discriminant Analysis of Principal Components [DAPC], MFA), all of which are highly customizable and available for download in JPEG or PDF formats. Analytical functions are reactive, such that any changes made within any submodule will be updated in the results and visualizations in real time. Submodules are organized in a sequential and logical order to intuitively guide users through a streamlined workflow starting with raw data upload, followed by outlier detection, summary statistics, and then proceeding to more robust analyses such as allometric correction (morphometric data only), inferential statistics (meristic and morphometric data), MFA (mixed data), and visualization.

The GUI of GroupStruct2 is clean and easy to navigate. Modules and submodules are accessed through a static left-side panel, whereas analyses, results, and visualizations are shown on the right (Fig. 2). Every analysis is accompanied by a brief explanation of the analysis and how to perform it that should be read before the operation proceeds. When the Visualization module is accessed, options for global graphical customizations that apply to all plots are shown on the left panel. These include graphical themes, color palettes, axes, and legend text sizes. Other customizations that are specific to certain plots, such as point size/shape outline and confidence ellipses/convex hulls for PCA/DAPC/MFA, are conditionally shown on the right panel when those plots are activated (Fig. 2). Below, we provide detailed descriptions of each submodule.

Input Data

Users may input raw trait data in comma-separated (.csv), tab-separated (.tsv), or plain-text (.txt) format with traits arranged in columns and individual samples in rows. The first column must be the operational taxonomic unit (OTU) grouping variable (e.g., species), followed by other trait variables that do not have to be in any particular order (Supplementary Table S1). The ordering of the original input data is preserved throughout all relevant analyses and output tables, ensuring easy tracking of unique, individual data points. Several statistical analyses, such as calculating mean and allometric correction, require more than one sample per group, as such, singletons (OTUs with only a single sample), and missing data are not allowed in the data set. Because trait data often contain erroneous entries (e.g., user transcription errors), this submodule includes an outlier detection analysis using the interquartile range (IQR) method, where values below Q1 − 1.5 × IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 × IQR are flagged as outliers. Detected outliers are printed on the screen for user verification but are not removed from the data set. In the mixed data module, the outlier detection analysis is only performed on numeric variables.

Summary Statistics

Once the input data set has been uploaded and validated, the Summary Statistics submodule automatically calculates the mean, standard deviation (SD), minimum (min), and maximum (max) values for each trait, aggregated by OTU. The results are presented in a publication-ready summary table formatted as mean ± SD (min–max) and can be downloaded in Excel format (.xlsx). Categorical traits, if present, are excluded from this analysis and are not shown in the summary table because they cannot be summarized using these metrics.

Allometric Correction

The Allometric Correction submodule removes the influence of body size on trait measurements, thereby isolating shape variation from overall size effects. This correction is optional and should only be applied to morphometric traits that exhibit allometric growth and is thus only available in the Morphometric and Mixed Data modules. This submodule implements the allometric body-size correction formula outlined by (Thorpe 1975):

where Xadj = size-corrected trait; X = raw/uncorrected trait; b = regression coefficient or slope of the relationship between log(X) and log(BL); BL = standard measurement of body length/size (e.g., snout–vent length); BLmean the mean of BL across all individuals within a species. All logarithmic transformations are performed at base 10. This equation corrects for ontogenetic variation by removing all effects related to size, scales individual traits to a standardized size (represented by species-specific BLmean), and adjusts their shape to the standardized size (Thorpe 1983; Lleonart et al. 2000). Because non-adult samples such as hatchlings and juveniles can skew the calculation of BLmean, they should be excluded from the data set. There is no generally accepted method for determining this cut-off, and we suggest that researchers exclude juveniles using an approach suitable for their focal group.

Two methods of allometric correction are available. For the “multispecies” method, the OTUs in the input data set are assumed to represent separate species or putative species, whereas the “multipopulation” method assumes that OTUs represent different populations of a single species (e.g., geographic populations of a single species). The only difference in calculation between the two methods is that in the former, a separate BLmean is calculated for each OTU/species, whereas in the latter, a single BLmean is calculated across all OTUs (= populations). In both multispecies and multipopulation options, a separate slope (b) is calculated for each OTU. Therefore, in the multispecies approach, where each OTU represents a species, different populations are combined under a single OTU name and a pooled slope is calculated. This assumes that the slopes of different populations of the same species are not significantly different. If variation among populations is large (population slopes differ significantly), the multipopulation approach should be used on that species. However, the multipopulation approach only handles one species at a time and hence, different species will have to be analyzed as separate data sets. Therefore, it is crucial that users understand how to group and analyze their data sets appropriately based on their knowledge of populations and species.

Allometric correction is also available in the Mixed Data module. Because this module handles mixed data types that can include non-morphometric data, there is an additional “Trait Selection” step to ensure that only morphometric traits are selected for allometric correction. Numeric traits are automatically detected, and users select the morphometric traits that should be subjected to allometric correction. Non-morphometric traits, which should not be selected, will not be corrected. A summary table of the entire data set can also be downloaded, with morphometric traits allometrically corrected, whereas other traits are left unchanged. The allometric analysis was adapted from the original GroupStruct R package (Chan and Grismer 2022). There is also an option to bypass allometric correction and use raw measurements for variables that do not scale allometrically or are non-biological in nature (e.g., ecological or environmental data). This option is also useful to compare and visualize how allometric correction can affect downstream results. Because these functions are reactive, we recommend that users toggle back forth between the different options and examine the changes in the corresponding plots and inferential tests results.

Inferential Statistics

This submodule is implemented in the Meristic and Morphometric modules. Four types of analyses can be performed: Univariate, Multivariate (Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance, PERMANOVA), PCA, and PCAtest. Univariate analyses are performed using R base functions to determine whether the means of traits are (or are not) statistically different between OTUs. The type of univariate analysis is automatically and adaptively selected depending on the number of OTUs (e.g., t-test for two OTUs or ANOVA for more than two OTUs, followed by the appropriate post hoc tests) and whether the data violate the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance (parametric vs. non-parametric tests). Because parametric tests can be robust to assumption violations, there is also an option to overwrite assumption violations and perform parametric tests on all traits. Results can be downloaded separately for each trait or as a single publication-ready summary table of all traits. If users have a combined meristic + morphometric data set, inferential statistics can be performed by analyzing them as separate data sets.

The PCA uses default settings of the prcomp base function in R. A summary of the results is shown (standard deviation, proportion of variance, cumulative proportion), including the loadings for each variable. The PCAtest analysis performs permutation-based tests to evaluate the overall significance of a PCA, the significance of each principal component (PC) axis, and the contributions of each trait to the significant axes. This analysis aids in the interpretation of the PCA results and determines which axes are informative for downstream analysis. The PCAtest analysis is based on the PCAtest R package (Camargo 2022).

The PERMANOVA analysis uses the adonis2 function from the vegan R package (Oksanen et al. 2022) to test whether multivariate centroids of OTUs are significantly different based on a distance matrix. This is the non-parametric analog of MANOVA (Multivariate Analysis of Variance), which utilizes permutations instead of relying on distributional assumptions. Users select the number of permutations to perform, and the method for calculating the distance matrix (e.g.,. Euclidean, Bray-Curtis, and Manhattan). There is also an option to use PCA scores for the PERMANOVA, which is highly recommended to accompany a PCA to determine if OTU clusters in a PCA are significantly different. Results from the PCA, PCAtest, and PERMANOVA analyses can be downloaded as publication-ready tables.

Multiple Factor Analysis

This submodule is specific to the Mixed Data module and is performed after the Allometric Correction step. The goal of MFA is to integrate different data types that describe the same observation and can include numeric and categorical variables in a single data set, thus allowing a nearly total evidence morphological analysis (Grismer 2025). Users must first define custom trait groups based on biological relevance (e.g., meristic, morphometric, body shape, and color pattern). Each group can only comprise a single data type (either numeric or categorical; groups with numerical + categorical data are not allowed), though multiple groups of the same type are permitted (e.g., multiple meristic or categorical groups; see Grismer 2025, table 1). MFA outputs include eigenvalues, group contributions (the proportion of variance each trait group contributes to each dimension), quantitative variable contributions (for numeric traits), and individual coordinates in reduced dimensional space used for plotting. Similar to PCA, a PERMANOVA can be performed on MFA dimension scores to evaluate whether multivariate centroids differ significantly among OTUs. The MFA uses the FactoMiner R package (Lê et al. 2008).

Visualization

The Visualization submodule is based on the ggplot2 architecture (Wickham 2016) and offers a suite of highly customizable and interactive plotting tools for exploratory and inferential data visualization. A global customization panel provides unified control over graphical parameters such as ggplot theme, color palette selection, axis labeling, and legend formatting. Users can choose from a broad range of built-in color palettes including viridis (Garnier et al. 2024), RColorBrewer (Neuwirth 2022), and colorblind-safe palettes (Wong 2011). There is also an option for users to manually create their own custom color palette. To avoid clutter, specialized plotting parameters specific to certain plots are handled separately as conditional functions that are only shown in those plots (e.g., ellipses for PCA plots).

Across all modules, scatterplots, boxplots, and violinplots are generated to explore trait distributions and relationships, with reactive controls to select which variables and OTUs to plot. The Meristic and Morphometric modules also include PCA and DAPC graphics, which separately support detailed aesthetic controls, such as point size and shape, the inclusion of confidence ellipses, convex hulls, group centroids, and transparency (alpha) settings. The DAPC plot also allows users to define the number of retained PCs and discriminant axes on a manual sliding scale, allowing observation of changes in real time. To choose the number of PCs to retain for a DAPC, we recommend that users perform the PCA (Inferential Statistics submodule) and retain the number of PCs that contribute 80–90% of the variation. The DAPC analysis is performed using the adegenet R Package using default settings (Jombart 2008). Finally, all plots support manual adjustment of output dimensions (height and width). All these functions provide highly granular control over many graphical parameters, allowing users to produce and export publication-ready figures tailored to their aesthetic and formatting needs.

Results

We illustrate the utility and user-friendliness of GroupStruct2 using two empirical data sets from morphologically cryptic species complexes of Gekko lizards (Grismer et al. 2022) and Hampala fishes (Tan and Grinang 2025). The Gekko data set includes four species, 11 meristic, 15 morphometric, and five categorical traits. The species boundaries of this group have been independently validated using genetic data (Grismer et al. 2022). Similarly, the Hampala data set includes four species, seven meristic, 21 morphometric, and one categorical trait (Panitvong and Hui 2025; Tan and Grinang 2025).

To evaluate the differential contributing power of the various data types to species structure, we partitioned the two empirical data sets into four subsets of traits and analyzed them separately: (1) Meristic-only, (2) Morphometric-only, (3) Meristic + Morphometric, and (4) Meristic + Morphometric + Categorical. The first two subsetted data sets were analyzed using the Meristic and Morphometric modules, respectively, whereas the latter two subsetted data sets were analyzed using the Mixed Data module. All example data sets are available in the application’s GitHub repository. The Gekko data sets are additionally embedded in the application and can be loaded through the Input Data submodule. Every graphic presented in the Results section was generated from the Visualization module and subsequently assembled in Photoshop with minimal adjustments.

Gekko Data Set

The Gekko data set comprises four species that are distributed across the Sunda Shelf in Southeast Asia: Gekko smithii, G. hulk (Malay Peninsula), G. albofasciolatus (Borneo and the Natuna Islands), and G. albomaculatus (Sumatra, Bangka Belitung Islands, and Mentawai Islands). Gekko hulk was previously lumped with G. smithii but was recently described as a distinct species based on morphological, genetic, and geographic data (Grismer et al. 2022).

The Meristic-only data set failed to detect clear separations among the four species (Fig. 3). In contrast, the importance of performing allometric body-size correction is clearly demonstrated in the Morphometric-only and Meristic + Morphometric data sets. When using raw morphometric data, all species overlapped significantly in the PCA and DAPC plots. However, after applying allometric correction, Gekko hulk and G. smithii showed strong separation. In the Meristic + Morphometric + Categorical data set, the MFA inferred clear species-level separations among all four taxa, with markedly tighter clustering of within-species data points. Notably, even when the morphometric data were not allometrically corrected, the MFA still recovered relatively strong species separation. This is likely due to the intraspecific invariability of the categorical traits, and to a lesser extent, the meristic data which contributed significant amounts of variation to the overall structure of the data (Fig. 4). The importance of allometric size correction is recapitulated in the group and trait contribution graphs, which show that the relative contribution of morphometric data significantly increases after allometric correction.

Hampala Data Set

The Hampala fish data set was analyzed to demonstrate the broad applicability of GroupStruct2 across different taxonomic groups and measurement systems. Fishes have a streamlined, fusiform body-plan optimized for aquatic locomotion, which is fundamentally different from tetrapods. Hampala fishes in particular are morphometrically conservative, characterized by a wedge-shaped head, wide gape, streamlined body, and a deeply forked caudal fin. Consequently, most diagnostic characters relate to meristic and color-pattern characters (Tan and Grinang 2025). This data set includes four species: Hampala siamensis from Thailand and H. bimaculata, H. lupar, and H. katibas from Borneo. The latter two species were previously confused with H. bicamulata and only recently described (Tan and Grinang 2025). Geographic populations within each species were pooled to boost sample size.

The PCA on meristic data failed to separate the four species, whereas the DAPC separated H. siamensis but not the remaining three species (Supplementary Fig. S1). PCA and DAPC analyses on uncorrected morphometric data showed no separation, but all four species were clearly distinguished after allometric correction. Notably, even though the allometric correction successfully removed the effects of size (confirmed by regression analyses), variation was almost entirely concentrated along the first axis/dimension in both PCA and DAPC. This result indicates that the morphometric traits covary along a single, dominant shape gradient, which is expected from Hampala fishes that have a conservative streamlined and wedged shape form.

When morphometric, meristic, and categorical data were integrated via MFA, the three Bornean species (H. bimaculata, H. katibas, and H. lupar) were separated along Dimension 1, whereas H. siamensis from Thailand was separated from Bornean Hampala along Dimension 2 (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Although morphometric traits collectively contributed most to Dimension 1 (Supplementary Fig. S2B), individual meristic and categorical traits contributed significant variation as well (Supplementary Fig. S2C). Together, these results highlight how an integrative framework that jointly analyzes different data types can reveal species-level structure that single data classes alone may miss.

Discussion

Elucidating and characterizing group structure is an integral aspect of taxonomy and systematics. In species delimitation, these patterns are typically evaluated alongside other complementary methods/datatypes within a multistep framework (Carstens et al. 2013; Hillis et al. 2021). However, phenotypic clusters are not synonymous with species, although they may be consistent. Biological processes (e.g., polymorphism, geographic variation) and non-biological artifacts (e.g., sampling bias) can generate group structures that are not associated with species boundaries. It is also important to recognize that aside from the allometric correction analysis, GroupStruct2 implements purely statistical and ordination methods that are not modeled after biological or evolutionary processes and are thus intended to generate, not validate hypotheses.

As such, GroupStruct2 results should be interpreted within a more robust and explicit biological framework that integrates other independent lines of evidence (e.g., genetics, behavior, and ecology), and not be used as a standalone method for species delimitation. Despite being optimized for phenotypic trait data, GroupStruct2 can also be applied to any data set that includes numeric or numeric + categorical variables associated with group structure, including environmental, ecological, paleontological, physiological, or even non-biological data. In mixed data sets where morphometric data are absent (e.g., meristic + environmental data), users can simply bypass the allometric correction step by selecting “No correction.”

Our re-analysis of the Gekko and Hampala data sets using the GroupStruct2 workflow highlights the importance and power of integrating multiple types of data into the inferential statistical framework to delimit group structure. In many systematic studies, statistical analyses are typically performed on morphometric data only, while meristic and categorical data are excluded from the inferential framework and are only used for often anecdotal descriptive comparisons (Grismer 2025). The Mixed Data module in GroupStruct2 addresses this limitation by providing a powerful, flexible, and user-friendly workflow that analyzes both numerical and categorical data simultaneously—unlocking this often-overlooked cache of data that can significantly enhance the resolution of group structure (Figs. 3 and 4; Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2).

Although other R packages and wrapper functions can perform some of the individual analyses available in GroupStruct2 (e.g., Šlenker et al. 2022), assembling them into a robust analytical workflow that includes multiple statistical analyses and advanced publication-quality graphics typically requires a relatively high proficiency in R programming. The key innovations of GroupStruct2 lie in its integration of complex functions into a flexible, interactive, and user-friendly GUI and the use of MFA for the analysis of mixed data types. The entire application can be installed, launched, and used with just four lines of R code:

> install.packages("remotes")

> install.packages("remotes")

> remotes:: install_github("chankinonn/GroupStruct2")

> remotes:: install_github("chankinonn/GroupStruct2")

> library(GroupStruct2)

> library(GroupStruct2)

> groupstruct2()

> groupstruct2()

The first line installs the “remotes” R package, which enables the installation of R packages from GitHub. The second installs GroupStruct2, the third loads the GroupStruct2 library, and the fourth launches the application. Once launched, users can navigate the app intuitively through the interface without requiring prior coding experience. Because GroupStruct2 is built entirely in R, it is cross-platform compatible and runs on all major operating systems.

Internally, the application uses reactive programming to pass data between submodules, enabling a seamless analytical workflow. Analyses and visualizations update in real time in response to user input, allowing for easy comparisons between methods and parameter choices. For example, users can compare PCA results before and after allometric correction of morphometric data (Fig. 3) or assess the impact of including or excluding specific traits or trait groups in an MFA. To our knowledge, GroupStruct2 is the first GUI-based application that combines robust, multistep statistical analyses with highly customizable, publication-ready visualizations for species diagnosis without requiring proprietary software or coding experience. This significantly lowers technical barriers and empowers researchers from all backgrounds to conduct rigorous, reproducible, and statistically defensible species diagnoses, which are lacking nowadays in many species descriptions.
