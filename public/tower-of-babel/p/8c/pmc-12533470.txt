The correlation between smoking and preoperative deep venous thrombosis after hip fracture in geriatric patients: a propensity score-matched study
Authors: Wanyun Tang, Runzhuo Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to investigate the association between smoking and the incidence of preoperative deep venous thrombosis (DVT) after hip fracture in geriatric patients.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 1,190 geriatric patients who underwent surgery for hip fractures. Patients were categorized into smokers and non-smokers based on their smoking history. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to estimate adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for preoperative DVT, controlling for potential confounders. Propensity score matching (PSM) was employed to minimize confounding variables, including age, sex, and comorbidities. Additionally, a dose–response analysis was performed to assess the relationship between the intensity of smoking and the probability of preoperative DVT.ResultsThe incidence of preoperative DVT was significantly higher in smokers compared to non-smokers before PSM (27.6% vs. 11.9%, p < 0.001) and after PSM (26.3% vs. 11.4%, p < 0.001). Smoking was an independent predictor of increased preoperative DVT risk, with an adjusted OR of 3.68 (95% CI 2.23–6.08, p < 0.001) after multivariable regression and 2.75 (95% CI 1.47–5.14, p < 0.001) after PSM. A dose–response relationship was observed between smoking intensity and preoperative DVT risk.ConclusionsSmoking is an independent risk factor for preoperative DVT after hip fracture in geriatric patients. Moreover, a dose–response relationship was observed, with higher smoking intensity corresponding to a greater risk of DVT.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06014-5.Keywords: Smoking, Deep venous thrombosis, Hip fracture, Geriatric

Introduction

Hip fracture is one of the most important public health problems worldwide, especially among the elderly population [1–5]. Deep venous thrombosis (DVT) is a prevalent and potentially life—threatening complication among geriatric patients with hip fractures [6–8]. The immobility associated with hip fractures, combined with the physiological changes of aging, significantly increases the risk of DVT [9]. This condition can lead to pulmonary embolism, a major cause of morbidity and mortality in this patient population [10–12]. Identifying modifiable risk factors for preoperative DVT in geriatric hip fracture patients is crucial for implementing preventive strategies and improving perioperative outcomes [13–15].

Smoking is a well—known risk factor for various cardiovascular and thrombotic diseases [16–18]. It has been associated with endothelial dysfunction, hypercoagulability, and platelet activation [17, 19, 20]. However, the relationship between smoking and preoperative DVT in geriatric patients with hip fractures remains incompletely understood. Some studies have suggested an association between smoking and DVT, but the evidence is inconsistent, especially in the context of geriatric hip fracture patients [21–25].

Previous research on the topic has produced mixed results. For example, while some studies have found a significant link between smoking and DVT [26–29], others, such as the study by Zhang et al. failed to identify a significant association between smoking and postoperative DVT [30]. These discrepancies may be due to differences in study populations, methodologies, and the presence of confounding factors.

In the specific population of geriatric hip fracture patients, there is a lack of comprehensive studies that accurately assess the impact of smoking on preoperative DVT. This knowledge gap is particularly important because geriatric hip fracture patients are a high—risk group for DVT, and any modifiable risk factor that can be identified could have a significant impact on patient care.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the association between smoking and the incidence of preoperative DVT in geriatric patients with hip fractures. By using a propensity score—matched design and controlling for potential confounders, we aim to provide more robust evidence on this relationship. Understanding this association could help in developing targeted smoking cessation programs as a preoperative intervention to reduce the risk of DVT and improve the overall prognosis of geriatric patients undergoing hip fracture surgery.

Methods

Data sources and patients

This retrospective study was conducted using patient data from Dandong Central Hospital between November 2011 and October 2024. The study included geriatric patients aged 65 years or older who were admitted with a diagnosis of hip fracture and subsequently underwent surgical treatment. Patients were identified from the institutional database, and their medical records were reviewed to obtain relevant clinical data including demographics, comorbidities, surgical factors, laboratory results, and complications.

To be eligible for the study, patients were required to have undergone routine preoperative screening for deep venous thrombosis (DVT) using Doppler ultrasonography. Those with a confirmed diagnosis of DVT prior to surgery were included in the analysis. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) incomplete medical records (n = 63), including missing smoking status data (n = 28), absent preoperative Doppler ultrasonography results (n = 22), or incomplete laboratory values (n = 100). This resulted in the exclusion of 150 patients (12.6% of initially screened participants), leaving 1,190 eligible patients for final analysis.

Exposure and outcome

The primary exposure variable in this study was smoking status, which was obtained from patient medical records and patient self-reports documented at the time of hospital admission. (1) Regular smokers: Patients who smoked at least one cigarette daily for continuous or cumulative periods of ≥ 6 months. (2) Occasional smokers: Patients who smoked > 4 times weekly but averaged < 1 cigarette daily. (3) Non-smokers: Patients who never smoked or smoked < 100 lifetime cigarettes. Both regular and occasional smokers were categorized as "smokers" in our primary analysis.

In addition to smoking status, data on other potential risk factors for DVT were collected, including age, sex, and comorbid conditions (such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease).

The primary outcome of interest was the occurrence of preoperative DVT. DVT was defined as the presence of a thrombus in the deep veins of the lower extremities, as confirmed by venous Doppler ultrasonography. Preoperative DVT screening was routinely performed on all patients upon admission to identify those at risk of thromboembolic complications and to guide perioperative management. The ultrasonography was conducted by board-certified attending radiologists with specialized training in vascular imaging (minimum 4 years of post-residency experience in Doppler ultrasonography), who were blinded to the patients' smoking status to minimize detection bias.

To enhance the robustness of our outcome assessment, we also reviewed clinical signs and symptoms suggestive of preoperative DVT, such as unilateral leg swelling, pain, and erythema, in conjunction with ultrasonographic findings. However, only cases confirmed by ultrasonography were considered positive for DVT in the final analysis. This approach ensured that our outcome measure was both specific and objective, minimizing the risk of misclassification bias.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics and baseline characteristics

For descriptive analysis, categorical variables were presented as percentages and compared using chi-square tests. Continuous variables underwent normality testing (Shapiro–Wilk). Normally distributed continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with group comparisons between two groups using independent t-tests and across multiple groups via one-way ANOVA. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were described using median (Q1, Q3) and compared between two groups with the Mann–Whitney U test or across multiple groups with the Kruskal–Wallis test.

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis

Initially, a univariate logistic regression was conducted to examine the unadjusted relationship between smoking and preoperative DVT in the propensity score-matched cohort, using smoking status as the independent variable and preoperative DVT as the dependent variable. Results were reported as OR with 95% CI.

Covariates demonstrating statistical significance (p < 0.05) in univariate analysis were then included in a multivariate logistic regression model to evaluate the independent effect of smoking on preoperative DVT risk. Adjusted OR with 95% CIs were used to summarize these results, highlighting the association after adjusting for confounders.

Propensity score matching

To minimize the potential confounding effects of covariates, we employed propensity score matching (PSM). PSM was performed using 1:1 nearest neighbor matching without replacement, with a caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score to ensure optimal balance while minimizing bias, aiming to maximize the similarity between the groups while preserving an adequate sample size. The effectiveness of the matching was assessed by examining standardized mean differences (SMDs) for all baseline covariates before and after matching. An SMD less than 0.1 was considered indicative of a good balance between groups, ensuring that the matched sample closely resembled a randomized trial, thereby reducing bias from confounding variables. The propensity score model incorporated all measured covariates, including: age, sex, drinking history, hypertension, diabetes, COPD, cardiovascular disease, stroke history, dementia, intracranial hemorrhage, hepatic disease, kidney disease, tumor history, ASA grade, history of deep vein puncture, chronic steroid use, WBC, RBC, HGB, PLT counts, BUN, Cr, albumin, cholesterol, TG, FIB, D-Dimer, PT, APTT, previous DVT.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Supplement Fig. 1 is a detailed flowchart of this article. The study enrolled 1,190 geriatric patients (median age: 75 years, IQR: 65–82) undergoing hip fracture surgery, with 966 never smokers and 224 current smokers. Table 1 highlights marked demographic and clinical disparities between groups. Current smokers were younger (median: 70 vs. 76 years, p < 0.001), more likely male (86% vs. 29%, p < 0.001), and had higher alcohol use (49% vs. 4%, p < 0.001). Never smokers had higher hypertension (53% vs. 37%, p < 0.001), diabetes (26% vs. 14%, p < 0.001), and cardiovascular disease (32% vs. 21%, p = 0.002). Current smokers demonstrated higher COPD prevalence (17% vs. 10%, p = 0.008) and lower hemoglobin (128 vs. 120 g/L, p < 0.001) and creatinine levels (66 vs. 60 μmol/L, p = 0.001). Notable laboratory differences included elevated white blood cell counts (8.8 vs. 8.4 × 10⁹/L, p = 0.002) and triglycerides (1.08 vs. 1.18 mmol/L, p = 0.008) in smokers (Table 1). Among smokers, those in the DVT group had a notably higher median smoking intensity compared to those in the Non-DVT group (Supplement Fig. 2).

Multivariate analysis

Table 2 presents the univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses for DVT risk factors. Univariable analysis identified significant associations between DVT and age (OR = 1.01, 95%CI = 1.00–1.04, p = 0.036), smoking (OR = 2.83, p < 0.001), drinking history (OR = 1.91, p = 0.003), hypertension (OR = 1.91, p < 0.001), diabetes (OR = 1.71, p = 0.002), COPD (OR = 1.69, p = 0.021), dementia (OR = 2.46, p = 0.011), history of DVT (OR = 5.97, p < 0.001), Elevated D-Dimer (OR = 1.12, p < 0.001), and PT (OR = 1.07, p = 0.021). Multivariable analysis comprised smoking (OR = 3.68, 95%CI = 2.23–6.08, p < 0.001), hypertension (OR = 1.49, p = 0.042), diabetes (OR = 1.65, p = 0.015), dementia (OR = 3.70, p = 0.001), history of DVT (OR = 4.96, p < 0.001), and Elevated D-Dimer (OR = 1.12, p < 0.001) as independent risk factors, while adjusting for other covariates. Drinking history, COPD, BUN, albumin, and PT lost significance in the multivariable model. These findings highlight smoking and D-Dimer as robust predictors of DVT after controlling for confounding variables.

Propensity score matching

PSM with greedy 1:1 matching and a 0.2 SD caliper reduced selection bias, creating 148 matched pairs. Table 3 shows the baseline characteristics of non—smokers and smokers before and after PSM. Before PSM, there were significant imbalances in variables such as age (SMD = 0.351), male gender (85.7% vs. 29.1%, SMD = 1.397), drinking history (49.1% vs. 3.7%, SMD = 1.200), hypertension (36.6% vs. 52.8%, SMD = 0.330), and hemoglobin levels (SMD = 0.443). After PSM (n = 148 per group), all critical variable SMDs were below 0.1, showing successful balance. Improvements were seen in male gender (78.4% vs. 82.4%, SMD = 0.102), drinking history (24.3% vs. 20.3%, SMD = 0.098), hypertension (39.9% vs. 45.9%, SMD = 0.123), and hemoglobin (SMD = 0.037). Balanced distributions of laboratory parameters like white blood cell count, albumin, and D—dimer also confirmed effective covariate adjustment. This validates PSM's ability to minimize bias and ensure comparable groups.

Dose–response relationship

Table 5 demonstrates a dose-dependent relationship between smoking intensity and DVT risk, with never smokers (11.9% DVT incidence) serving as the reference. Compared to never smokers, smokers with < 10 pack-years showed no significant risk increase in any model (unadjusted OR = 0.95, p = 0.920; multivariable OR = 1.48, p = 0.447; PSM OR = 1.45, p = 0.542). However, risk of preoperative DVT increased significantly with higher exposure: < 20 pack-years had unadjusted (OR = 1.74, p = 0.038) and multivariable-adjusted (OR = 3.05, p = 0.002) significance, while < 30 pack-years (OR = 6.47, p < 0.001) and ≥ 30 pack-years (OR = 15.62, p < 0.001) showed marked unadjusted risks. After multivariable adjustment, ≥ 30 pack-years retained significance (OR = 9.92, p < 0.001), and PSM-adjusted analysis confirmed robustness for ≥ 30 pack-years (OR = 5.46, p = 0.006). A significant linear trend (p < 0.001) across all categories underscores the cumulative thrombotic risk of prolonged smoking, with heavy smokers (≥ 30 pack-years) facing a 67.8% observed DVT rate. Figure 2a (before PSM) and Fig. 2b (after PSM) display the association between smoking pack-years and DVT risk, presenting the observed rate (orange dots with error bars) and predicted probability (blue line with 95% CI shaded area). Across both figures, as smoking pack-years increase, both the observed DVT risk rate and the predicted probability exhibit an upward trend, indicating a positive correlation between cumulative smoking exposure (measured by pack-years) and DVT risk. The consistent upward trajectory in both plots underscores that higher smoking pack-years are associated with elevated DVT risk probabilities. Discussion

In this study, we found that smoking was independently associated with an increased risk of preoperative DVT in elderly patients with hip fractures. A dose–response relationship between smoking intensity and increased risk of DVT was noted, providing additional evidence supporting the role of smoking as a prognostic factor for adverse perioperative outcomes. These findings align with existing literature on smoking and thromboembolic events while offering novel insights into the geriatric hip fracture population.

For instance, Sweetland et al. reported a 38% increased risk of DVT in smokers, with a dose–response relationship between cigarette consumption and thrombotic risk [31]. Similarly, a systematic review of 32 observational studies confirmed a pooled relative risk of 1.23 (95% CI 1.14–1.33) for DVT in current smokers, further strengthened by adjustment for BMI (RR = 1.30) [32]. These findings reinforce the biological plausibility of smoking-induced endothelial dysfunction, hypercoagulability, and platelet activation as mechanisms driving thrombotic events.

However, discrepancies exist in the literature. The study by Khaled et al. encompassing diverse surgical procedures, found no significant association between smoking and postoperative DVT [33]. This contrast may stem from methodological differences: our study focused specifically on geriatric hip fracture patients—a high-risk subgroup with prolonged immobilization and inflammatory responses—while Khaled’s analysis included heterogeneous surgeries with varying thromboembolic risk profiles. Additionally, our use of propensity score matching (PSM) minimized confounding, whereas the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program database’s (NSQIP’s) broad design might have obscured smoking’s specific effects in orthopedic contexts.

Notably, our findings diverge from a systematic review that failed to identify smoking as a significant predictor of preoperative DVT in hip fractures. This discrepancy may arise from differences in study inclusion criteria or statistical power, as our larger sample size and rigorous PSM approach enhanced the detection of smoking’s independent role. Furthermore, our dose–response analysis revealed that heavy smokers (≥ 30 pack-years) faced a 9.92-fold increased DVT risk after adjustment, aligning with Cecilia et al.’s observation that heavy smoking (> 20 pack-years) significantly elevated provoked DVT risk (HR = 1.75).

The Danish cohort study by Severinsen et al. highlighted sex-specific DVT risks in smokers, with women exhibiting higher susceptibility (HR = 1.52 vs. 1.32 in men) [34]. While our study did not stratify by sex, the predominance of male smokers in our cohort (86%) suggests potential interactions between smoking, gender, and thrombosis that warrant further exploration.

Importantly, our findings complement recent meta-analyses. A 2023 meta-analysis of 9,823 hip fracture patients identified smoking as a significant DVT risk factor (OR = 1.45) [35], while another study emphasized smoking’s role in calf muscle vein thrombosis (CMVT) via endothelial damage and hypercoagulability [36]. These collective results underscore smoking cessation as a critical preoperative intervention for mitigating thrombotic complications in geriatric orthopedic patients.

The pathophysiological mechanisms linking smoking to DVT in the lower extremities primarily involve endothelial dysfunction, hypercoagulability, and hemodynamic disturbances. Smoking induces oxidative stress through reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reduces nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, impairing endothelial integrity and promoting platelet activation [31, 37]. Additionally, nicotine-mediated vasoconstriction exacerbates venous stasis, a critical factor in thrombus formation [38, 39]. Studies have demonstrated that smokers exhibit prolonged forced midexpiratory time, indicating compromised peripheral airflow dynamics, which may indirectly reflect venous flow abnormalities in immobilized patients [40]. Dose–response analyses further reveal that cumulative smoking exposure (≥ 30 pack-years) correlates with a 9.92-fold increased DVT risk, underscoring the cumulative toxic effects of tobacco on vascular health [41].

Moreover, smoking alters coagulation-fibrinolysis balance by elevating fibrinogen levels, enhancing platelet aggregation via nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, and suppressing fibrinolysis through increased plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 [42]. Chronic inflammation driven by tobacco smoke upregulates pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α), fostering a thrombogenic microenvironment [43]. Genome-wide association studies suggest that smoking-related polymorphisms, such as those regulating IL15, may modulate inflammatory pathways, potentially exacerbating endothelial injury [44]. These mechanisms collectively highlight smoking as a multifactorial risk factor for DVT, necessitating preoperative cessation interventions in high-risk populations such as geriatric hip fracture patients.

The particularly heightened thrombotic risk in geriatric hip fracture patients who smoke likely results from several synergistic mechanisms: age-related endothelial dysfunction that amplifies smoking-induced vascular damage [18]; fracture-induced inflammatory responses that compound smoking-related hypercoagulability [45, 46]; and immobilization-related venous stasis that exacerbates smoking-impaired microcirculation. This triad of risk factors creates a perfect storm for DVT development in this vulnerable population [47].

Our findings suggest that preoperative smoking cessation strategies could potentially reduce postoperative complications, and thus their implementation may be worth considering in clinical practice. Healthcare providers might benefit from incorporating smoking cessation support, such as brief counseling or nicotine replacement therapy, into preoperative pathways, though further evidence may be needed to confirm optimal approaches.

This study employed a robust research design with PSM to minimize confounding variables, ensuring a balanced comparison between smokers and non-smokers. The inclusion of a large sample size enhanced statistical power, while the use of Doppler ultrasonography for objective DVT diagnosis reduced detection bias. The dose–response analysis further validated the cumulative thrombotic risk associated with smoking intensity, providing mechanistic insights into the observed association. Multivariable regression models adjusted for key covariates, including age, sex, and comorbidities, strengthened the evidence for smoking as an independent risk factor.

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. As a retrospective analysis, it relies on self-reported smoking status and medical records, potentially introducing recall bias or incomplete data. Additionally, the single-institution design may limit generalizability to populations with differing demographics or healthcare practices. Although PSM reduced baseline imbalances, residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., physical activity, diet) cannot be fully excluded. The focus on preoperative DVT alone precludes assessment of long-term thrombotic outcomes, and the absence of gender-stratified analysis may obscure sex-specific interactions. Future prospective studies with standardized smoking metrics and multicenter collaboration are warranted to address these gaps.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that smoking is an independent risk factor for preoperative DVT in geriatric patients with hip fractures, with a dose–response relationship observed between smoking intensity and DVT risk.
