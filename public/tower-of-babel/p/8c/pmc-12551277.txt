Longitudinal trends in mortality and its causes among migrant HIV/AIDS patients undergoing antiretroviral therapy compared to their counterparts: a cohort study, 2016–2022
Authors: Jing Li, Xiaozhen Zhang, Jun Liu, Jinglin Wang, Binghui Wang, Zirui Zhang, Xiaomin Kang, Hanwen Fu, Mingmei Zhang, Xueshan Xia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTo assess the impact of immigration status and other risk factors on mortality among HIV/AIDS patients receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART).MethodsWe conducted a cohort study (2016–2022) of 3,795 HIV/AIDS patients aged ≥ 18 years in Yunnan, China. The migrant population was defined as individuals whose household registration and current residence were located in different provinces. Mortality was analysed from patients inclusion to death or censored. Descriptive analysis results were obtained using chi-square tests, Fisher’s Exact test, t-tests, and Poisson regression models were conducted to estimate mortality outcomes().ResultsThe total follow-up time for this cohort was 14,263.0 years, with an overall mortality rate of 7.7% (294/3,795). Target population was predominantly male (68.7%) aged 25–49 years (63.3%). After adjusting for demographic and clinical characteristics, there were several variables with significances by Poisson regression, and they were migrant status, age, gender, time to ART initiation, opportunistic infections, viral load, serum creatinine level, and CD4 cell count. It revealed that migrants had a lower risk for mortality (aIRR 0.64, 95% CI: 0.40–0.97), although those 25–49 (aIRR 2.53, 95% CI:1.21–6.49) and ≥ 50 years (aIRR 7.25, 95% CI:3.48–18.55) of age exhibited a higher mortality rate, and women were at lower risk than men (aIRR 0.43, 95% CI:0.31–0.58). Delayed initiation of ART > 30 days increased the risk for mortality (aIRR 1.31,95% CI:1.04–1.67). Mortality was higher among patients with opportunistic infections (aIRR 1.58, 95% CI:1.22–2.05), as well as those with viral load ≥ 50 copies/mL (aIRR 2.77, 95% CI:2.07–3.79) and abnormal serum creatinine levels (aIRR 1.58, 95% CI:1.08–2.26). CD4 cell count ≥ 200 cells/µL were associated with lower mortality ( aIRR 0.62, 95% CI:0.46–0.82). The causes of death were dominated by respiratory diseases, cardiovascular diseases, and other non-AIDS-related diseases.ConclusionsBeing migrant was associated with a lower mortality as compared with local natives or other long-settled population. The main factors influencing the risk for mortality among HIV/AIDS patients were migrant status, age, gender, time to ART initiation, opportunistic infections, viral load, serum creatinine level, and CD4 cell count.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24546-7.Keywords: HIV/AIDS patients, Antiretroviral therapy, Mortality, Cause of death, Migrant

Introduction

The global effort to combat human immunodeficiency virus/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (HIV/AIDS) has achieved substantial progress, particularly with the widespread implementation of antiretroviral therapy. An estimated 39.9 million people globally were living with HIV, and around 630,000 deaths in 2023 were linked to AIDS-related illnesses [1, 2]. In China, the fatality rate dropped drastically from 39 deaths per 100 person-years in 2002 to 3.7 per 100 person-years in 2019 [3]. Similarly, Yunnan Province saw a 66% reduction in AIDS-related mortality rate, dropping from 3.2% in 2013 to 1.1% in 2021 [4].

The advent of ART has significantly improved life expectancy and reduced mortality among HIV/AIDS patients. However, as this population ages, the epidemiology of HIV/AIDS-related deaths has shifted, potentially influenced by age-related declines in immune function and progressive liver/kidney impairment. Studies indicate that male sex [5] and older age [6] are associated with an elevated risk of mortality, which may be mediated by high viral loads [7], low CD4 cell counts [8], and opportunistic infections [9]. Liver-related diseases represent a major contributor to morbidity and mortality in HIV/AIDS patients, accounting for 13 to18% of all-cause deaths [10]. Similarly, renal dysfunction exacerbates adverse health outcomes in this population [11]. Despite these advances, significant disparities persist in health outcomes across different patient subgroups, particularly between migrant and non-migrant populations.

The prevalence of HIV among migrant populations exhibits substantial geographical variation, shaped by a complex interplay of socioeconomic disparities, migration patterns, and demographic risk factors. In the European Union and European Economic Area (EU/EEA), migrants represented 48% of all newly diagnosed HIV cases in 2022, highlighting their disproportionate burden in the epidemic [12]. Consistent with Santoso et al.'s findings, international migrants demonstrate elevated HIV/AIDS rates relative to non-migrant groups, potentially attributable to healthcare system barriers and sociocultural exclusion [13]. In China, mobile populations—particularly rural-to-urban migrants—constitute a high-risk group. A 2020 study of 1,155 newly diagnosed men revealed that 86.23% were non-local residents, highlighting how population mobility facilitates viral spread [14, 15]. The Chinese migrant population is concentrated among those 20 to45 years of age, predominantly males, with relatively low literacy and income, which collectively contribute to the potential for HIV/AIDS transmission [16].

Human mobility is a critical driver of infectious disease transmission, as exemplified by the global spread of Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) [17, 18]. Similarly, migration patterns significantly influence HIV epidemiology, with multiple transmission models highlighting the role of population movement in disease dissemination [19]. Migrants experience compounded HIV risk factors: disrupted social networks, familial dislocation, risky sexual practices, and inadequate disease awareness. These factors not only increase infection risk but also create barriers to healthcare access, perpetuating a cycle of poor health outcomes among migrant populations [20]. Studies in the northern hemisphere have documented delayed HIV diagnosis and suboptimal care engagement among both international and internal migrants [21]. These disparities necessitate the immediate implementation of tailored programs to enhance clinical outcomes, especially for migrant HIV/AIDS patients.

Accumulating evidence indicates that international migrants face a disproportionately high burden of HIV compared to the general population [13, 22, 23]. Surveillance data from Europe revealed that 44.4% of men who have sex with men (MSM) diagnosed with HIV in 2023 were migrants, underscoring their heightened vulnerability [24]. Three vulnerable subgroups— asylum applicants, resettled refugees and undocumented individuals - manifested the most elevated infection risks [13]. Despite their elevated risk, migrants with HIV/AIDS experience delayed diagnosis [25], higher AIDS incidence [26], increased virological failure rates [27], and elevated mortality compared to native-born populations [26]. Population mobility has been established as a distinct determinant of HIV acquisition, though the independent effect of migrant status on ART outcomes requires further elucidation.

Building upon existing evidence of health disparities among migrant populations, we hypothesized that migration status independently predicts ahigher mortality risk among HIV/AIDS patients receiving ART, even after accounting for key confounding factors. The purpose of our study was to characterize the migrant population among HIV/AIDS patients undergoing long-term ART and to explore associations between mobility and mortality. We adjusted for various demographic and clinical characteristics to better understand this relationship, with the ultimate goal of enhancing the quality of care, particularly in terms of improving treatment adherence.

Methods

Study settings and period

This study was conducted from January 1, 2016 to December 31, 2022 in the infectious disease department of the largest hospital in Kunming, the capital of Yunnan Province. The hospital was a specialized infectious disease institution, in which there were the highest number of HIV/AIDS patients in its department.

Study design and participants

This study was a single-center, healthcare-based cohort study. It should be noted that the enrollment time did not always align with the initiation time of ART. According to our data, the median interval between HIV detection and ART initiation was 54 days. The rate of ART initiation within 30 days of diagnosis among newly diagnosed HIV-infected individuals is less than 75% in China [28]. The term “HIV/AIDS patients” was uniformly used to refer to all target populations eligible for treatment. The participants were divided into two groups: (1) Migrant population and (2) Non-migrant population. The majority of participants were followed through the entire observation period, and dichotomized as either experiencing an event (death) or not (called censored). The cohort comprised most patients with HIV/AIDS in Yunnan province. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Enrolment period: January 1, 2016 to December 31, 2022; (2) Age ≥ 18 years at the time of ART initiation. ( The exclusion criteria were: (1) Missing baseline information; (2) Patients lost to follow-up; (3) Individuals younger than 18 years. All participants in this study were Chinese citizens. Consecutive sampling was adopted to recruit participants. The average follow-up frequency was once per year, with variations in individual follow-up timelines: the first follow-up was conducted 365 days after enrollment, and subsequent follow-ups were performed every 365 days thereafter. The follow-up period lasted from enrollment until December 31, 2022. Our data were derived from baseline recruitment visits, which included both newly diagnosed HIV/AIDS patients and those who had received prior treatment.

The loss to follow-up rate was 3% overall. The majorities of patients lost follow-up were those who stopped taking medicine on their own due to side effects of the drug. Approximately 1% of the patients in our loss to follow-up had transferred to other hospitals for continuous treatment. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality among HIV/AIDS patients, defined as death from any cause. The cause of death was derived from the national death register; the diagnosis was made by an accredited health-care professional. For patients lost to follow-up (i.e., no contact for > 3 months), death status was verified through local community health center reports and, when possible, by contacting family members. Patients who were lost to follow-up, transferred out, or failed to complete follow-up were excluded from the analysis. Survival time was measured from the start of enrolment to the end of follow-up or the censoring point.

Measurement and variables

Due to potential collinearity between HIV/AIDS clinical stages and opportunistic infections, the variable selection process was carried out and finally opportunistic infections were ultimately selected for entering the modeling. The dependent variable in this study was mortality (i.e., not deceased, deceased), and the independent variables included age (18–24, 25–49, ≥ 50 years), sex (male, female), marital status (unmarried/divorced/widowed/unknown, married/have a spouse), and migrant population (no, yes), was defined as individuals whose registered domicile and current residence were in different provinces, indicating inter-provincial movement. In contrast, non-migrants were those who were local residents, including long-term settled migrants, whose registered domicile (hukou) and current residence were within the same province, representing individuals with no history of inter-provincial migration. Route of infection (sexual transmission, blood transmission), interval between diagnosis and treatment (≤ 30 days, >30 days). Opportunistic infections(OIs) were conditionallydefined as which pathogenic bacteria did not easily disease when colonizing the human body, but they can cause infection and disease in people with weakened immune systems. When opportunistic infections happened, patients had been in the AIDS stage (no, yes). CD4 cell count (< 200 cells/µL, ≥ 200 cells/µL), HIV RNA viral load (VL;< 50 copies/mL, ≥ 50 copies/mL), white blood cell count (WBC; 4.0–10.0 × 109/L, < 4.0 or >10.0 × 109/L); platelet count (PLT; 100–300 × 109/L, < 100 or >300 × 109/L), serum creatinine (SCR; 44–133 µmol/L, < 44 or >133 µmol/L), triglycerides (TG;< 1.7 or ≥ 1.7 mmol/L), total cholesterol (TC;3–5.2 mmol/L,<3 or >5.2 mmol/L), blood glucose (GLU; 3.9–6.1, <3.9 or >6.1 mmol/L), total bilirubin (TBIL; 3.4–17.1 µmol/L, < 3.4 or>17.1 µmol/L), alanine aminotransferase (ALT;< 40 U/L or ≥ 40 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (AST; < 40 U/L or ≥ 40 U/L), and CD4/CD8 ratio (< 0.3 or ≥ 0.3) (The cutoff values for CD4/CD8 were the means taken). When the CD4 cell count was below 200 cells/µl, the patient had entered the AIDS stage. Additionally, when the CD4 cell count was between 200 and 350 cells/µl simultaneously with opportunistic infections, the patient was also considered to be in the AIDS stage. In our study, the proportion of patients who developed opportunistic infections with 200–350 cells/µl was relatively low at approximately 1.8%. Therefore, we used the threshold cut-off point of 200 CD4 cells/µL. All viral load tests were conducted at ART initiation and annual follow-up visits. The viral load of ART initiation was used for analysis. Serum creatinine was routinely monitored to assess renal function impairment during ART treatment so that medication could be adjusted in a timely manner.

Statistical analysis

Data were double-entered using EpiData version 3.1 (www.epidata.dk), and analyzed by SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) and R-Studio version 4.3.1 (R Core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). For descriptive statistics, categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and proportions, while continuous variables—including viral load and CD4 cell count—were described using mean and standard deviation. Baseline characteristics of HIV/AIDS patients were stratified by mobility status. Chi-square tests were used to compare categorical variables between groups, and Fisher’s exact test was used to compares two groups of causes of death. Viral load and CD4 cell count were analyzed as continuous variables using t-tests. All univariate analyses used a two-sided significance level of P < 0.05. The mortality variable (count data) met the criterion for Poisson distribution, with the mean (0.07747036) approximating the variance (0.07148754). A likelihood ratio test showed no significant deviation, indicating that the fit of the Poisson distribution was nearly equivalent to that of the negative binomial model; the P-value of 0.15354 confirmed no significant difference between the two models. Thus, Poisson regression (performed in RStudio 4.3.1) was used to estimate mortality outcomes. We conducted a two-stage analytical approach to examine factors associated with mortality: 1) Univariate analysis: all candidate variables were initially assessed using univariate Poisson regression models; 2) Multivariate analysis: variables showing significant association (P < 0.05) in univariate analyses were included in the final multivariate Poisson regression model.

Results

Mortality rate during our follow-up and demographic characteristics of included participants

The total follow-up duration for this cohort was 14,263.0 person-years. Among the 3,913 HIV/AIDS patients included in the cohort, 3% (118/3,913) were lost to follow-up or transferred to other medical institutions for continued treatment. A total of 3,795 patients were included in the analysis, of whom 7.7% (294/3,795) died. The majority of participants were male (68.7%, 2,606/3,795) and aged 25–49 years (63.3%). Of these, 13.5% (514/3,795) were migrants, and 86.5% (3,281/3,795) were non-migrants (Supplementary Materials Table 2).

Predictors of mortality in Poisson regression

As shown in Table 2, multivariate Poisson regression analysis revealed that the risk of death was significantly lower in the migrant population (adjusted incidence rate ratio [aIRR] = 0.64, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.40–0.97). Additionally, individuals with higher CD4 cell counts (≥ 200 cells/µl) had a reduced risk of death (aIRR 0.62, 95% CI: 0.46–0.82). In contrast, older age, male sex, ART initiation > 30 days after diagnosis, presence of OIs, abnormal SCR levels, and higher VL were associated with an increased risk of death (all P < 0.05). As shown in Fig. 2, when comparing crude incidence rate ratios with adjusted ratios, the effects of adjusted variables—including age, presence of OIs, and VL—became more pronounced.

Causes of death during follow-up

The primary causes of death were respiratory diseases, cardiovascular diseases, and other non-AIDS-related conditions. The results indicated differences in cause-specific mortality patterns between migrant and non-migrant populations. Notably, non-migrants had higher mortality rates from respiratory diseases and malignant neoplasms, whereas migrants had higher mortality rates from AIDS-related illnesses, external causes (including suicide), and deaths due to external factors (Fig. 3). However, the distribution causes of death between the two groups was not differ statistically significantly (Supplementary Materials Table 4). The number of deaths decreased annually, with no deaths observed among the migrant population in 2022 (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This single-center cohort study identified predictive factors for mortality among HIV-positive patients receiving ART. The total follow-up duration was 14,263.0 person-years, with an overall mortality rate of 7.7% . In our cohort, migrant HIV/AIDS patients receiving ART were predominantly male and aged 25–49 years. After adjusting for confounding factors, multivariate analysis revealed that migrant status, age, sex, interval from diagnosis to ART initiation, presence of OIs, VL, SCR levels, and CD4 cell count were significant predictors of mortality in this population.

This study identified significant differences in age and sex distribution between migrant and non-migrant populations, with both groups predominantly consisting of males aged 25–49 years. By 2023, there were approximately 110 males for every 100 females [29], indicating an imbalanced sex ratio. A previous study noted thatin 2018, the age distribution of China’s migrant population was predominantly in the 25–39 years age group, accounting for 52.2% of the total. This cohort—born after 1980 and often referred to as the “new generation of migrant workers”—represents the most demographically active segment of the migrant population [30]. Multivariate Poisson regression revealed that age was significantly associated with mortality risk: individuals aged ≥ 50 years had a higher risk of death compared to those aged 18–24 years. Ningjun et al. reported a standardized mortality ratio of 0.59 among geriatric male HIV/AIDS patients (50–79 years), indicating age-associated disease progression [31]. Since China’s 2004 free ART initiative markedly extended survival durations, transitioning AIDS mortality patterns from acute to chronic disease trajectories [32]. In our study, female patients had a significantly lower mortality risk than male patients, which aligns with findings from previous research. Chinese data revealed evolving sex disparities in HIV/AIDS outcomes, with male-to-female mortality ratios escalating from 1.98 (2001) to 2.99 (2016) [33]. Similarly, data from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Preventionform 2010-2018 showed that the total number of deaths among HIV-infected males (12,256) was higher than among females (3,994) [34]. Potential contributing factors include accelerated disease progression, suboptimal treatment response (immunological/virological), and clinical engagement challenges in male patients [35, 36].

In our study, the loss-to-follow-up rate among migrant populations was slightly higher than that among non-migrant populations. Kunming, as an open and inclusive city with specialized HIV clinics and experienced medical personnel, may have mitigated some challenges—evidenced by the relatively low overall loss-to-follow-up rate. However, migrant patients still remained at higher risk of dropout. Social discrimination against HIV/AIDS patients in their hometowns may exacerbate this disparity: migrant populations may delay or avoid follow-up care in their places of origin, opting instead to seek treatment in Kunming. Yet, they may struggle to maintain access to medical services due to transportation barriers, mobility-related interruptions (e.g., work commitments), or limited social support. A previous study reported that migrants had higher risks of virological failure, immunological failure, missed visits, and treatment interruption compared with non-migrants [37]. In contrast, our analysis revealed that migrant patients receiving ART had a 36% lower risk of death than non-migrant patients. A systematic review by Segala et al., which assessed mortality across 4 studies, reported a slightly lower mortality risk among migrant HIV/AIDS patients compared with non-migrants (relative risk [RR] 0.88, 95% CI: 0.75–1.04; P = 0.13), though this difference was not statistically significant [37]. Notably, in-hospital deaths were most common among local patients (12.3%), exceeding rates among both inter-provincial (7.0%) and intra-provincial migrants (5.9%) [38]. Majority of HIV/AIDS patients those who received ART across provinces have significantly strong desire for treatment due to HIV/AIDS stigma in local clinic. In comparison, a portion was organized by local medical institutions for treatment based on the Chinese policy of “95%-detection and 95%-treatment” among local HIV/AIDS patients without strong desire of ART. Notably, our definition of migrant populations—individuals whose household registration and current residence are in different provinces—focuses on inter-provincial mobility. In other studies, “external migrants” are often defined as individuals who leave the study area during follow-up, encompassing broader mobility patterns. Further research is needed to standardize the definition of migrant populations, integrate socioeconomic and health factors across different mobility types, and fully clarify the impact of mobility on HIV-related mortality.

Given our findings that migrants achieve better HIV treatment outcomes than to non-migrants, we propose several policy enhancements to optimize healthcare for mobile populations. First, establishing centralized care hubs in major migration destination cities (e.g., Kunming) could ensure continuous access to ART and minimize disruptions to follow-up care. Secondly, strengthening inter-city referral systems with shared electronic medical records would facilitate seamless care transitions for migrant . Additionally, tailored outreach programs should be implemented to address migrant-specific barriers, such as offering flexible clinic hours, mobile health reminders, and peer navigation services. These strategies could extend Kunming’s successful model nationwide. Future research should further explore the socioeconomic and structural factors underlying these outcome disparities to inform more targeted interventions.

In this study, we found that ART initiation > 30 days after diagnosis, the presence of OIs, high VL, and abnormal SCR levels were risk factors for mortality in this population. A CD4 cell count > 200 cells/µl, in contrast, was a protective factor against death in HIV/AIDS patients. Timely ART cement is associated with a higher likelihood of a favorable prognosis [39], and immediate ART (initiated within 30 days of diagnosis) offers the strongest protection against mortality [40]. Most HIV/AIDS patients requiring hospitalization have concurrent OIs, which correlates with higher mortality rates [41]. Additionally, mortality risk increases with rising VL [42], highlighting the critical role of viral suppression in HIV management. Conversely, higher CD4 cell counts significantly reduce the risk of AIDS-related death, whereas low CD4 cell counts are an independent predictor of mortality in this population [43]. With the widespread adoption and optimization of combination ART regimens, AIDS-related deaths and OIs have declined significantly, and patient survival has improved. This has shifted the clinical spectrum of HIV, with renal involvement becoming relatively common—affecting up to one-third of patients [44]. SCR, a biomarker of renal function and damage, is used to identify and grade acute kidney injury (AKI) [45]. AKI is defined based on absolute or relative increases in SCR levels. Studies have shown that severe AKI (e.g., SCR elevations > 300% above baseline) is associated with a higher risk of death [46]. While abnormal SCR levels may correlate with an increased mortality risk, they are not a direct cause of death. Early ART initiation during acute HIV infection rapidly reduces VL [47], whereas the incidence of OIs increases as CD4 cell counts decline [48]. Moreover, CD4 cell counts are significantly negatively correlated with SCR levels [49]. In summary, early ART initiation, control of OIs, VL reduction, preservation of renal function, and improvement in CD4 cell counts are key factors in reducing AIDS-related deaths. These factors—which influence patients’ immune status and overall health—must be prioritized in the management of HIV/AIDS.

Limitations of the study

The present study effectively tracked the survival of HIV/AIDS patients receiving ART and provided dynamic insights into potential causal relationships. However, this study has several limitations. First, the socioeconomic status of patients was not adequately considered. Second, selection bias may exist as it is predominantly composed of young adults. Third, loss-to-follow-up rates differed between the two groups. Additionally, confounding factors— such as treatment adherence, specific route of sexual transmission, past medical history, and body mass index (BMI) were not included in the analyses, these unmeasured factors may have influenced the observed survival outcomes.

Conclusions

This study identified key factors influencing mortality in HIV/AIDS patients, including migrant status, age, sex, interval from diagnosis to ART initiation, presence of OIs, VL, SCR levels, and CD4 cell count. Contrary to our initial hypothesis, mobility was found to be associated with lower mortality in HIV/AIDS patients due to strong desire for treatment and avoidance of the potential HIV/AIDS stigma that possibly arose from local treatment. Future research should prioritize multi-center studies to expand the sample size and validate the generalizability of these findings. Additionally, studies should integrate socioeconomic and health factors across diverse mobility patterns to further clarify the mechanisms underlying these associations.
