A cross‐national cost‐effectiveness analysis of brivaracetam focusing on psychiatric and behavioral adverse effects
Authors: Yasushi Maruyama, Hiroki Kimura, Takuji Nishida, Shiori Ogawa, Kohei Ninomiya, Masashi Ikeda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveLevetiracetam (LEV) has been widely used as an antiseizure medication (ASM), but is associated with psychiatric and behavioral adverse effects (PBAEs). Brivaracetam (BRV) has demonstrated better psychiatric tolerability, albeit at a higher cost. This study aimed to evaluate the cost‐effectiveness of initiating BRV versus LEV therapy with a subsequent switch to BRV in the event of PBAEs across the US, Australia, and Japan.MethodsWe developed a decision tree model with a 10‐year time horizon comparing two strategies across the US, Australia, and Japan: initial BRV therapy versus initial LEV therapy with a switch to BRV upon the occurrence of PBAEs. We utilized clinical trial data and regional cost estimates to conduct base‐case and probabilistic sensitivity analyses.ResultsThe results of the base‐case analyses indicated that initial BRV therapy was cost‐effective only in Australia. By contrast, in the US, the risk of PBAEs needed to exceed 76% to achieve a comparable cost‐effectiveness probability.SignificanceWhile the initial BRV strategy consistently demonstrated cost‐effectiveness in Australia, it failed to do so in Japan and the US. INTRODUCTION

Levetiracetam (LEV) is a widely prescribed antiseizure medication (ASM) known for its broad spectrum of efficacy across various seizure types, favorable pharmacokinetic profile, and minimal drug interactions, making it a highly practical treatment option. According to the epilepsy guideline published by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), LEV has been recommended as the first‐line treatment for focal epilepsy across all age groups. 1 However, LEV has also been associated with a higher incidence of psychiatric and behavioral adverse events (PBAEs), occurring in ~15% of cases, compared with an overall average incidence rate of 8.4% observed across all ASM monotherapies. These PBAEs are commonly observed in clinical practice and may lead to treatment discontinuation. 2 , 3

Brivaracetam (BRV) is a relatively new ASM that was first introduced in 2016, obtaining approval in the US and Australia in the same year. Since then, it has gradually been approved in various countries around the world, including Japan in August 2024. BRV is a selective analog of LEV that demonstrates higher affinity for synaptic vesicle protein 2A (SV2A) 4 and exhibits a notable profile regarding PBAEs. In addition, the superior psychiatric tolerability of BRV has been confirmed based on real‐world evidence. The EXPERIENCE study (n = 1644) reported finding psychiatric adverse events in 6.4% of patients receiving BRV at 3 months, 5 , 6 while Yates et al. 5 , 6 reported 93.1% improvement when switching from LEV to BRV.

Given its clinical advantage in reducing PBAEs, the economic implications of BRV use—particularly in the context of differing health‐care systems and drug pricing—warrant careful evaluation. The pricing structure of BRV shows marked international variation, with the cost in the US significantly exceeding that in other nations. These price differences largely reflect the distinct characteristics of different health‐care systems. For example, the US employs market‐based pricing mechanisms, whereas Japan and Australia utilize government‐regulated pricing systems within universal health‐care frameworks. Although BRV is expensive, costing ~5 to 20 times more than LEV according to Medicare pricing databases and international pharmaceutical benefit schemes, its potential to reduce the occurrence of adverse events suggests its cost‐effectiveness.

Previous cost‐effectiveness analyses of ASMs in epilepsy, such as Hu et al., 7 who analyzed 11 ASMs for newly diagnosed focal epilepsy, have primarily focused on comparing efficacy and overall costs. However, these analyses have not specifically addressed the trade‐off between drug costs and psychiatric adverse effects as a primary focus. Considering the significant clinical impact of psychiatric adverse effects on treatment adherence and quality of life (QOL), it is important to evaluate the economic implications of these differences.

Based on this background, in the present study, we aimed to evaluate the cost‐effectiveness of initial BRV versus initial LEV therapy with a subsequent switch to BRV in the event of a PBAE across three distinct health‐care systems: the US, Japan, and Australia, representing diverse regulatory and economic contexts. Additionally, we aimed to examine whether BRV could demonstrate favorable cost‐effectiveness in populations at high risk for LEV‐induced PBAEs.

METHODS

Cost‐effectiveness analysis methodology

Cost‐effectiveness analysis is a systematic method for evaluating health‐care interventions by comparing their costs and health outcomes to guide optimal resource allocation. The analysis calculates the incremental cost‐effectiveness ratio (ICER), which represents additional costs divided by additional health benefits, typically measured in quality‐adjusted life years (QALYs).

Cost‐effectiveness analysis can be conducted using various approaches, including model‐based analyses that employ mathematical frameworks such as Markov models to simulate patient transitions between health states over time, or trial‐based analyses conducted alongside clinical trials. In this study, we implemented a model‐based approach using decision tree and Markov frameworks to track associated costs and effects.

Cost calculations include direct medical expenses such as treatment costs and medications. For effectiveness measurement, utility values are derived from standardized health‐related QOL instruments like EQ‐5D, which quantify health states on a 0–1 scale where 0 represents death and 1 represents perfect health. These utility values are combined with the observation period to calculate QALYs.

Sensitivity analyses, including one‐way parameter variation and probabilistic Monte Carlo simulations with thousands of iterations, test the robustness of results under uncertainty. The calculated ICER is compared against willingness to pay (WTP) thresholds that vary by country, with interventions below these thresholds considered cost‐effective.

Model structure

We aimed to evaluate two distinct treatment strategies for newly diagnosed focal epilepsy in the US, Japan, and Australia using a comprehensive decision analysis approach.

Strategy 1: The initial BRV strategy involves using BRV as ASM. If patients experience PBAEs or inadequate seizure control, then the treatment transitions to second‐line ASMs.

Strategy 1: The initial BRV strategy involves using BRV as ASM. If patients experience PBAEs or inadequate seizure control, then the treatment transitions to second‐line ASMs.

Strategy 2: The initial LEV strategy begins with LEV as first‐line therapy. In cases where seizure control is achieved but PBAEs emerge, the treatment transitions to BRV. Alternatively, if seizure control is inadequate, then treatment shifts to second‐line ASMs.

The three countries were selected to represent distinct health‐care systems and regulatory environments. The US represents a market‐based pricing system with private insurance dominance, Japan a universal health‐care system with government price regulation and recent BRV approval, and Australia a government‐regulated pricing model through the Australian Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme. The pricing structure of BRV shows marked international variation, with costs in the US significantly exceeding those in other nations. These price differences reflect the distinct characteristics of health‐care systems, and this selection enables comprehensive assessments of how these different characteristics influence cost‐effectiveness outcomes for innovative ASMs.

Although BRV monotherapy is not currently approved in Australia, we included it in our analysis for two key reasons. First, Australia represents a unique pricing environment with government‐regulated costs. The Australian Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme provides an important comparator for government‐regulated pricing systems. Second, regulatory approval for monotherapy often follows adjunctive therapy approval by several years in most countries, and this assumption enables prospective economic evaluations that can inform future policy decisions when monotherapy approval becomes available.

To ensure comprehensive and transparent reporting, this cost‐effectiveness analysis followed the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards checklist. 7 To analyze these strategies, we developed a decision tree model using TreeAge Pro Healthcare 2024 (TreeAge Software, Williamstown, MA) (Figure 1). The model employs a cycle length of 1 year to adequately capture the time required for efficacy assessment and adverse effect monitoring. The analysis spans a 10‐year time horizon, chosen to reflect the anticipated period before generic BRV availability or significant price revisions. The model represented a group of Japanese patients with an average age at epilepsy onset of 40 years, based on the observed average onset age of 35–42 years. 8

The analysis was conducted from the health‐care system perspective, with costs expressed in USD and effectiveness measured in QALYs. To facilitate direct comparisons across health‐care systems, all costs were standardized to USD using exchange rates as of December 2024 (1 USD = 150 JPY = 1.56 AUD). The WTP thresholds were similarly converted to USD, ensuring that our cost‐effectiveness conclusions would remain robust against exchange rate fluctuations, as both costs and thresholds are expressed in the same currency.

The WTP thresholds of the US, Japan, and Australia were set at 50 000 USD, 33 333 USD (=5 000 000 JPY), and 32 051 USD (= 50 000 AUS), respectively, consistent with health technology assessment standards. Strategies yielding an ICER below this threshold were considered cost‐effective within the health‐care context of each country.

This analysis applied annual discount rates to both costs and effects, which were the annual rates used to convert future costs and effects into present values, reflecting the decreasing value of resources over time. Common rates are 3% in the US, 2% in Japan, and 5% in Australia. 9 , 10 , 11

Epilepsy treatment

To ensure analytical consistency, our model assumed the potential approval of monotherapy in Australia.

The 1‐year seizure suppression rate for LEV monotherapy was derived from previously reported comparative trials. 12 Although no monotherapy studies for BRV were available, we hypothesized equivalent monotherapy efficacy based on the similar seizure response rates observed in combined therapy studies involving LEV and BRV. 13 The model assumed that patients who achieved seizure freedom for 1 year would maintain this state permanently. Although seizure suppression for 1 year does not preclude the potential for recurrence, we simplified the model to facilitate a cost‐effectiveness analysis focused on PBAEs.

For second‐line ASMs, we considered a hypothetical drug representing the average cost and effectiveness of recommended agents from the epilepsy guideline of NICE for focal epilepsy, excluding LEV and BRV 1 : these included carbamazepine, lacosamide, lamotrigine, topiramate, zonisamide, perampanel, and valproate. Treatment intensity varies based on seizure control status, with patients achieving adequate seizure control receiving an average of 1.23 second‐line ASMs, 14 whereas those with inadequate control receive an average of two ASMs. Response probabilities for this hypothetical second‐line treatment were imputed based on epidemiological data indicating that ~70% of patients with epilepsy (PWE) eventually achieve complete seizure control, regardless of the ASM used. 15

Drug prices were determined based on Medicare's public database of annual per‐person drug costs by medication, as Medicare represents the national public health insurance system for the US, where prices fluctuate according to market conditions, and the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme in Australia and the Medical Fee Point System and the Japanese drug price standard established by the Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare in Japan, from which we calculated the cost per prescription.

For the US analysis, we used current originator BRV pricing throughout the 10‐year horizon. While generic BRV is anticipated to enter the market within this time frame, the exact timing remains uncertain. Given that patent expiration and generic entry can significantly alter cost‐effectiveness outcomes, our analysis represents a conservative scenario using current pricing.

Psychiatric and behavioral adverse effects (PBAEs)

PBAEs represent significant treatment‐related complications manifesting as mental health and behavioral disturbances, including depression, mania, impulsivity, and irritability. 2 , 16 For modeling purposes, we incorporated PBAEs based on available clinical evidence and necessary assumptions, including both their clinical impact and associated health‐care utilization, as follows. For LEV, we adopted a PBAE incidence rate of 14.1% from Josephson et al., 16 a large study specifically assessing psychiatric adverse effects in 1173 patients. While SANAD II reported higher rates (26%–30%), our choice reflects the larger sample size and primary focus on psychiatric outcomes in Josephson et al. 17 , 18 As direct monotherapy data for BRV‐associated PBAEs were not available, we applied data from add‐on therapy studies to estimate the incidence rate; the model incorporated a 93.1% PBAE improvement rate when transitioning from LEV to BRV, based on Yates et al., 5 a prospective study specifically examining patients switching as a result of psychiatric adverse effects. This represents the best available evidence for this clinical scenario despite being from a single study.

The assumption of no psychiatric adverse effects with second‐line ASMs represents a simplification necessary for model tractability. In clinical practice, second‐line treatment represents a heterogeneous group of multiple ASMs, and when psychiatric adverse effects occur, clinicians typically switch to alternative agents within this group, making such effects transient.

The use of bipolar disorder type II utility values to estimate the impact of ASM‐induced psychiatric adverse effects represents the best available approximation given the lack of specific utility data for drug‐induced psychiatric symptoms. 19 Bipolar disorder type II was selected because it encompasses similar symptomatology (depression, irritability, mood instability) to ASM‐induced PBAEs without the severe manic episodes of bipolar I disorder.

The economic impact of PBAEs was incorporated through outpatient psychiatric care costs for affected patients. Because distinguishing between drug‐induced symptoms and psychiatric comorbidities common in epilepsy requires specialist evaluation in many care settings, we included psychiatric consultation costs for patients experiencing PBAEs.

Mortality

In our model, mortality considerations incorporate both the natural age‐specific mortality rates of the population of each country and the increased mortality risk associated with epilepsy. We applied a standardized mortality ratio (SMR) of 1.42 for PWE, as established in previous epidemiological studies. 20

Sensitivity analysis

To address parameter uncertainty and validate the robustness of our findings, we employed both probabilistic and one‐way sensitivity analyses. The probabilistic sensitivity analysis utilizes Monte Carlo simulation methodology, where model parameters are varied according to their respective probability distributions across 100 000 iterations to generate 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for both costs and QALY outcomes. Additionally, we conducted one‐way sensitivity analyses by systematically varying each key model parameter individually within its plausible range to assess the impact on model results and identify influential variables.

To characterize the relationship between the risk of LEV‐induced PBAEs and the economic feasibility of BRV implementation in clinical practice, we evaluated whether BRV could be used cost‐effectively when combined with pre‐assessments of LEV‐induced PBAEs. We conducted probabilistic sensitivity analyses by fixing the risk of LEV‐induced PBAEs at 1% increments while simultaneously varying other parameters to determine the cost‐effectiveness probability at each risk level.

Parameter ranges for sensitivity analysis were determined using ±2 standard deviations when literature‐based CIs were available, from −50% to +200% variation around base‐case values for cost parameters, and clinically plausible ranges for probability parameters. When data from the literature were insufficient, we applied conservative ranges based on standard health economic modeling conventions and expert judgment. RESULTS

Base‐case analysis

To evaluate the incremental cost‐effectiveness of initial BRV therapy compared with LEV as a first‐line ASM, we conducted a base‐case analysis using country‐specific model parameters for the US, Japan, and Australia. The model parameters used in our analysis are presented in Table 1. The cost‐effectiveness results of the base‐case analysis comparing initial BRV and LEV strategies across the US, Japan, and Australia are summarized in Table 2. The incremental costs of the BRV strategy were 63 832.58 USD in the US, 13 404.70 USD in Japan, and 4799.56 USD in Australia, corresponding to incremental QALY gains of 0.39, 0.40, and 0.39, respectively. These yielded ICERs of 161 670 USD, 33 949 USD, and 12 156 USD per QALY. When compared with each country's WTP threshold, BRV was cost‐effective only in the Australian context.

Sensitivity analyses

To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we conducted probabilistic sensitivity analyses using 100 000 Monte Carlo simulations. The results showed that the mean ICERs were 156 801.2 USD (95% CI: 156 475.5–157 126.9), 34 926.7 USD (95% CI: 34 857.7–34 995.7), and 13 772.9 USD (95% CI: 13 746.7–13 799.0) per QALY in the US, Japan, and Australia, respectively. The initial BRV strategy had a 0%, 49.6%, and 99.99% probability of being cost‐effective in the US, Japan, and Australia, respectively (Figure 2, Figure S1).

In Japan, with the base‐case ICER approximating the threshold value, the ICER fluctuated above and below the WTP threshold depending on parameter variations. Upon examining the parameter pertaining to the rate of LEV‐induced PBAEs, it became evident that values exceeding 15.1% may fall below the WTP threshold. We additionally conducted a one‐way sensitivity analysis focusing on the annual cost of BRV in the US. The findings revealed that BRV would become cost‐effective if the annual cost fell below 4263 USD, compared with the base‐case cost of 13 469 USD. Therefore, a 68% cost reduction would be required for BRV to achieve cost‐effectiveness in the US health‐care system.

To clarify the specific impact of PBAE risk on cost‐effectiveness outcomes, further probabilistic sensitivity analyses were conducted with a fixed rate of LEV‐induced PBAEs while allowing other parameters to vary stochastically in the US and Japan. When the PBAE incidence rate was set to 24%, ~70% of iterations were cost‐effective in Japan, suggesting the increased economic value of the initial BRV strategy in populations at higher risk of PBAEs (Figure 4A). In the US, the initial BRV strategy demonstrated cost‐effectiveness with 70% probability when the risk of LEV‐induced PBAEs exceeded 76% (Figure 4B).

DISCUSSION

Our cross‐national cost‐effectiveness analysis contributes to the growing literature examining the economic implications of differences in ASM tolerability. Although psychiatric adverse effects have not been the primary focus, previous cost‐effectiveness studies in epilepsy, such as Hu et al., 21 who compared 11 ASMs for newly diagnosed focal epilepsy, have shown that tolerability profiles significantly influence economic outcomes. The present study extends this work by specifically examining psychiatric tolerability as a key economic driver across different health‐care systems.

The economic rationale for accepting higher drug costs to prevent adverse effects has been established in epilepsy management. Kinderen et al. 22 demonstrated that the adverse effects caused by ASM impose a substantial economic burden, with annual societal costs reaching 20 751 EUR (~$28 429 at March 2014 rates) per patient. This evidence base reinforces our finding that the superior psychiatric tolerability profile of BRV can justify its higher cost in specific clinical and economic contexts.

The present cross‐national comparison revealed distinct variations in the cost‐effectiveness of initial BRV strategies across health‐care systems. The US showed more definitive results, with BRV failing to achieve cost‐effectiveness in both base‐case and one‐way sensitivity analyses across all parameter variations. Japan presented an intermediate scenario, with results near the WTP threshold in the base‐case analysis, suggesting potential cost‐effectiveness under certain parameter conditions. However, in the Australian health‐care system, the initial BRV strategy demonstrated favorable cost‐effectiveness, with sensitivity analyses consistently validating the findings across multiple parameters. These differences primarily stem from drug pricing variations: compared with Australia, BRV costs more than four times as much in Japan and nearly 10 times as much in the US, which largely explains the disparity in cost‐effectiveness between countries.

However, our one‐way sensitivity analysis revealed that BRV would achieve cost‐effectiveness in the US if the annual cost were reduced from the current 13 469 USD to 4263 USD, representing a 68% cost reduction. This finding has important implications for future generic BRV availability. Generic medications typically achieve an 80% cost reduction compared with their originator counterparts, suggesting that generic BRV entry could potentially make the initial BRV strategy cost‐effective in the US health‐care system. 23 This represents a significant shift from our findings and warrants a future reassessment following generic market entry.

Our analyses also identified the need for a more selective approach to BRV prescriptions. The implementation of risk stratification for LEV‐induced PBAEs prior to treatment initiation could identify scenarios where BRV becomes cost‐effective. Our one‐way sensitivity analysis revealed that BRV may achieve cost‐effectiveness when the risk of LEV‐induced PBAEs exceeds 15.1%. However, demonstrating robustness across the entire population remains essential. Specifically, probabilistic sensitivity analysis for Japan indicated that when the risk of LEV‐induced PBAEs exceeded 24%, the probability of BRV being cost‐effective rose above 70%, maintaining stable cost‐effectiveness. This finding aligns well with the prediction tool of LEV‐induced PBAEs developed by Josephson et al., 16 which established four key risk factors: female gender, history of depression, anxiety disorders, and recreational drug use. Their research suggests that patients positive for three of these four factors have a predicted PBAE risk exceeding 30%, aligning closely with our cost‐effectiveness threshold. By employing this analytical tool to assess the risk of LEV‐induced PBAEs, the present study demonstrates the potential for implementing the initial BRV strategy with cost‐effectiveness in the Japanese population.

By contrast, in the US, when the probability surpasses 76%, the initial BRV strategy can be considered consistently cost‐effective at the population level. However, it would be clinically unacceptable to prescribe LEV to patients with such a high risk of PBAEs. Therefore, from a clinical perspective, the initial BRV strategy cannot be cost‐effective, even after assessing LEV‐induced PBAEs in the US population.

The integration of these economic and clinical findings suggests a potential approach for optimizing BRV utilization. The integration of validated risk prediction tools in clinical practice allows health‐care providers to identify patients at high risk of PBAEs, who are likely to benefit most from initial BRV therapy, leading to both better clinical outcomes and cost efficiency. This selective approach offers a more refined strategy compared with universal policies of either routinely prescribing or avoiding BRV. However, it is important to note that in the US, our one‐way sensitivity analysis indicated that even with this risk assessment approach, BRV cannot achieve cost‐effectiveness.

This study has several important limitations. First, as BRV has only recently entered the market, long‐term monotherapy data remain limited, and our model assumes sustained efficacy based only on short‐term studies. Second, our assumption of equivalent efficacy between LEV and BRV is based on adjunctive therapy studies rather than direct monotherapy comparisons. Third, our model employs simplifying assumptions, including no psychiatric adverse effects with second‐line ASMs and permanent seizure freedom after 1 year of control, which may not fully capture the clinical complexity. Fourth, using bipolar disorder type II utilities to approximate LEV‐induced PBAEs may not accurately reflect the QOL impact of drug‐induced psychiatric symptoms, representing a gap in ASM‐specific QOL research. Fifth, we focused on direct medical costs and may have underestimated indirect costs such as lost productivity and caregiver burden. Additionally, key parameters such as the 93.1% improvement rate when switching from LEV to BRV are based on limited evidence. Finally, our analysis focuses specifically on the economic optimization of BRV use rather than on establishing its overall position in epilepsy therapy, and the results should be interpreted within this context.

CONCLUSION

While the initial BRV strategy consistently demonstrated cost‐effectiveness in Australia, it failed to do so in Japan and the US. However, Japan presents a unique opportunity where BRV can be used cost‐effectively when combined with pre‐assessments of the LEV‐induced risk of PBAEs.

Implementing prior risk assessment for LEV‐induced PBAEs through risk stratification across different health‐care systems offers a more economically viable approach to BRV administration. This strategic approach to patient selection holds promise for optimizing BRV implementation while maintaining economic sustainability, regardless of variations in regional drug pricing.
