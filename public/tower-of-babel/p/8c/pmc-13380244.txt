Genomic Characterization of SARS-CoV-2 NB.1.8.1 and PQ.2 from the Infants and Young Children with Gastrointestinal Symptoms
Authors: Xianyan Zhang, Xuemin Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study investigated the viral genomic characteristics of infants and young children who presented to our hospital with gastrointestinal symptoms during a local COVID-19 epidemic and were confirmed to have SARS-CoV-2 infection.Patients and methodsBetween May and August 2025, pharyngeal swab samples were collected from four infants and young children who presented to the outpatient department of Meizhou People’s Hospital in Meizhou, with gastrointestinal symptoms. Nucleic acid testing and whole-genome sequencing were performed. The viral mutation profile was analyzed, and the potential impact of mutations on protein function was predicted.ResultsPangolin typing identified the NB.1.8.1 variant in three patients and the PQ.2 variant in one patient. Genome sequences from three of the four viral variants displayed varying degrees of mutation. The nonsynonymous mutations for both variants were concentrated in the spike protein. A comparison with the parental XDV.1.5.1 lineage revealed 14 specific mutations, with 7 nonsynonymous sites conserved across all gene sequences. Five of these mutation sites, NSP12: D284Y, ORF3a: L46F, ORF3a: F207C, N: Q9H, and N: Q384H, were predicted to be functionally deleterious and structurally destabilizing.ConclusionSARS-CoV-2 variants from specimens obtained from four infants and young children exhibited varying degrees of mutation, providing evidence for the ongoing evolution of emerging variants in pediatric patients. However, monitoring genomic changes of circulating variants requires further clinical specimens, which contributes to understanding the dynamic changes at mutation sites, thereby supporting epidemic prevention and control.

Introduction

In May 2023, the World Health Organization declared that Coronavirus disease (COVID-19) no longer constituted a Public Health Emergency of International Concern, marking the start of the post-pandemic era.1,2 However, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) has not been conquered.2,3 The virus continues to circulate worldwide and has steadily evolved into a series of novel viral lineages.3 Ongoing mutations and evolution of Omicron and its subsequent lineages have enhanced viral fitness and immune evasion, rendering the virus a persistent threat in the post-pandemic era.3,4 COVID-19 affects multiple organ systems, including the gastrointestinal tract.5 Gastrointestinal symptoms such as diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain occur in approximately 13–60% of infected individuals, with some patients presenting with these manifestations as the initial or sole sign of infection.6–8 The virus invades gastrointestinal tissues via the angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor, which is highly expressed on intestinal epithelial cells.9 The persistent detection of viral RNA and live virus in patient stool samples demonstrates that SARS-CoV-2 replicates within the gastrointestinal tract and is shed in feces.10,11 Infants and young children are a distinct population with respect to SARS-CoV-2 infection, because they often present with atypical clinical features and have a higher frequency of gastrointestinal manifestations than those of older children and adults.12,13 Their immature immune systems, combined with an intestinal ACE2 expression pattern that differs from that of adults, may contribute to the lower incidence of COVID-19 in this age group.12,14 Patients with gastrointestinal involvement may experience prolonged viral shedding, which is associated with an increased risk of fecal-oral transmission and may be associated with disease severity.15 Consequently, gastrointestinal involvement in COVID-19 not only influences the individual prognosis but has public health implications for disease control.

In infants and young children, compared with the Delta variant, infections with Omicron strains are more likely to present with upper respiratory and gastrointestinal manifestations.16,17 With the sequential emergence of Omicron variants, the adverse effects on infected pediatric patients have become increasingly apparent, including reduced vaccine-induced protection, an elevated risk of co-infection with other respiratory viruses, and prolonged hospitalization.18–20 The NB.1.8.1 variant, which emerged and became prevalent in 2025, has subsequently become a variant of concern.21 NB.1.8.1 descends from the XDV lineage, a recombinant of XDE and JN.1. Relative to JN.1, NB.1.8.1 carries seven amino acid substitutions in its spike protein and 23 in the non-spike regions.22 These mutations collectively augment the capacity of the variant for immune evasion and enhance its transmissibility.22 Furthermore, PQ.2, a descendant strain of NB.1.8.1 that appeared in the second half of 2025, has also been circulating in several countries.23,24 Although news reports have indicated that the emerging variant NB.1.8.1 may cause gastrointestinal symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea, the impact of this variant and the PQ.2 variant on the pediatric population remain unclear.25 In the post-pandemic era, continued genomic surveillance of new variants and close monitoring of mutations with the potential to drive epidemic resurgence remain important for maintaining public health emergency preparedness.26

During the summer 2025 COVID-19 epidemic in Meizhou in southern China, we performed whole-genome sequencing analysis of samples from infants and young children with SARS-CoV-2 who presented with gastrointestinal symptoms. The aim was to understand the genetic characteristics of the NB.1.8.1 and PQ.2 variants in a pediatric cohort to inform regional COVID-19 prevention and control measures.

Methods

Patients and Sample Collection

This study enrolled four infants and young children (P1–P4) who presented to the outpatient department of Meizhou People’s Hospital in Meizhou, Guangdong Province, China, with SARS-CoV-2 infection between May and August 2025 during a local COVID-19 epidemic. All patients had at least one concurrent gastrointestinal symptoms, including nausea, vomiting, diarrhea and abdominal pain.12,27 Throat swab samples were collected from each participant for subsequent analysis. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Meizhou People’s Hospital (approval number: MR-44-21-014309). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all children prior to study commencement.

Nucleic Acid Extraction and Detection

Viral RNA was extracted from throat swab samples using a magnetic bead-based kit (Zybio Inc., Chongqing, China). SARS-CoV-2 RNA was subsequently detected using the Novel Coronavirus (2019-nCoV) Nucleic Acid Detection Kit (Fluorescence PCR) (Shanghai BioGerm Medical Technology Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. This assay specifically targets the ORF1ab and N genes of SARS-CoV-2. We performed real-time quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) using a SLAN-96S real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR system (Shanghai Hongshi Medical Technology Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China). The cycle threshold (Ct) values for the ORF1ab and N genes were recorded independently. A sample was defined as SARS-CoV-2 positive only if the Ct values for both the ORF1ab and N genes were ≤40.

Whole-Genome Sequencing of Viruses

Samples confirmed as SARS-CoV-2 positive by real-time PCR with Ct values for the ORF1ab and N genes below 30 were selected for genome sequencing. Total RNA extracted from these samples underwent whole-genome capture and amplification using an RNA Pathogen Microbe Library Construction and Capture Kit (iGeneTech Bioscience Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Double-stranded circular DNA libraries were then constructed according to the protocol for the MGIEasy RNA Library Prep Set (MGI, Shenzhen, China). Whole-genome sequencing was performed on a DNBSEQ-T7 sequencer (MGI, Shenzhen, China). Raw reads were filtered using Fastp (version 0.20.0) to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads. Host genomic reads were eliminated using Bowtie2 (version 2.4.5), and the remaining non-host target reads were retained. Subsequently, the pathogen-mapped reads were de novo assembled into contigs using MEGAHIT (version 1.2.9). These assembled contigs were then aligned against a pathogen database using blastn (version 2.13.0). The sequence exhibiting the highest similarity at the species level to the pathogen database was selected as the reference sequence (Wuhan-Hu-1 strain, MN908947). Finally, the high-quality reads were mapped to this reference genome using BWA (version 0.7.12), and a consensus sequence was determined employing iVar (version 1.3.1), thereby generating the final viral consensus sequence.

Sequence Alignment and Phylogenetic Analysis

Viral lineage and clade analyses were conducted using the online tools Pangolin (version 4.3) (https://pangolin.cog-uk.io/) and Nextclade (version 3.18.1) (https://clades.nextstrain.org/). Four SARS-CoV-2 consensus sequences, along with the parental genealogical lineage XDV.1.5.1 sequence (GISAID accession number: EPI_ISL_19230297), were submitted to the Nextclade online platform (https://clades.nextstrain.org/) for the identification of nucleotide and amino acid mutations.22,28 Subsequently, multiple sequence alignment was performed using the Clustal Omega module in SnapGene (version 6.0.2) to verify the mutation sites identified by Nextclade. The Wuhan-Hu-1 strain (MN908947) was used as the reference sequence for alignment. All aligned sequences were normalized for position relative to this reference sequence. Homology comparison analysis was conducted using MEGA 12. Based on the typing results, we selected representative sequences of the BA.2, BA.2.86.1, JN.1, XDV.1.5.1, NB.1.8.1, and PQ.2 variants from the GISAID database (https://gisaid.org/) for phylogenetic analysis alongside the genomic sequences obtained in this study. The phylogenetic analysis was performed using PhyloSuite (version 2).29 Sequence alignment was conducted with the MAFFT module, and poorly aligned positions and divergent regions were removed using gBlocks.30,31 The ModelFinder module was used to identify the optimal nucleotide substitution model (TN+FO+I).32 We reconstructed a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree using the IQ-TREE module, with branch support evaluated from 1,000 standard bootstrap replicates.33 The Wuhan-Hu-1 strain (MN908947) was used as the outgroup for the phylogenetic tree. The phylogenetic tree was visualized using the ChiPlot online platform (https://www.chiplot.online).

Analysis of the Impact of Mutations on Viral Protein Function

We compared the sequencing data from four patients with the parental lineage XDV.1.5.1 (GISAID accession number: EPI_ISL_19230297) to identify nonsynonymous nucleotide mutations.22 The impact of these nonsynonymous mutations on viral protein function was assessed using the online server PredictSNP (https://loschmidt.chemi.muni.cz/predictsnp1/).34 Sequences of NSP3, NSP9, NSP12, S, ORF3a, and N proteins derived from the Wuhan Hu-1 strain (MN908947) were used for subsequent analyses. The web server of PredictSNP integrates six top-performing prediction tools (MAPP, PhD-SNP, PolyPhen-1, PolyPhen-2, SIFT, and SNAP) and provides an accurate prediction confidence score for mutations classified as deleterious or neutral. The confidence score, representing the percentage of expected accuracy, ranges from 0 to 100%.

Analysis of the Impact of Mutations on Viral Protein Stability

Three different protein stability prediction tools, I-Mutant 2.0, MUpro and SAAFEC-SEQ, were used to analyze the impact of nonsynonymous mutations on protein stability. I-Mutant 2.0 is a support vector machine (SVM)-based server that estimates changes thermodynamic potential as Gibbs free energy (ΔΔG) to predict stability alterations.35 MUpro uses SVM and neural network classifiers trained on extensive mutation datasets.36 SAAFEC-SEQ predicts ΔΔG by leveraging the physicochemical properties of mutation sites, sequence features, and evolutionary data.37 These tools were used to predict protein stability changes upon mutations using the NSP12, ORF3a, and N protein sequences from the Wuhan Hu-1 strain (MN908947). All three tools classify mutations according to the predicted change in ΔΔG. A positive ΔΔG value indicates a stabilizing mutation, whereas a negative value indicates a destabilizing mutation.

Data Analysis

Data were plotted using GraphPad Prism version 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Figures were assembled using Adobe Illustrator CC2017 (Adobe Systems, San Jose, CA, USA).

Viral Mutation Analysis

Compared with the Wuhan-Hu-1 strain, the SARS-CoV-2 sequences obtained in this study contained 165 mutated, deleted, or inserted nucleotide sites. Of these, 148 variant nucleotide sites were present in at least three viral gene sequences. These 148 nucleotide variations resulted in mutations or deletions at 135 amino acid sites within the viral genome. Among these amino acid variant sites, 93 (68.9%) were nonsynonymous, 36 (26.7%) were synonymous, and 6 (4.4%) were deletions (Supplementary Figure 1). The nonsynonymous mutations occurred primarily within the S protein region (60.2%, 56/93) (Supplementary Figure 1).

NB.1.8.1 and PQ.2 are sublineages of XDV.1.5.1.22 The SARS-CoV-2 genome sequences from the four patients exhibited variant sites largely identical to those of XDV.1.5.1 (Figure 2). Compared with XDV.1.5.1, sequences in the viral strains from the four patients contained 14 unique nonsynonymous mutations, of which 7 (NSP3: I1891V, NSP9: P57S, NSP12: D284Y, S: T22N, S: F59S, S: A435S, and S: Q493E) were present in all four patients. Moreover, mutations at various positions were also observed in three of the four viral variant genome sequences. The PQ.2 strain derived from Patient P1 additionally carried ORF3a: W193R and N: Q384H; the NB.1.8.1 strain from Patient P2 additionally carried S: D1084A and N: Q9H; and the NB.1.8.1 strain from Patient P4 additionally carried NSP3: A1183T, ORF3a: L46F, and ORF3a: F207C nonsynonymous mutations. Discussion

SARS-CoV-2 is a single-stranded positive-sense RNA virus with the capacity for rapid mutation and recombination.38 The high mutation rate of RNA viruses is closely associated with their ability to modulate virulence and evolve, which facilitates enhanced viral adaptability and transmissibility.38 This study presents whole-genome sequencing of the viruses isolated from four infants and young children with SARS-CoV-2 infection and gastrointestinal symptoms during the summer 2025 COVID-19 epidemic in Meizhou. Three samples were classified as belonging to the NB.1.8.1 lineage, and one sample belonged to the PQ.2 branch lineage of NB.1.8.1. These assignments align with the predominant lineages circulating in China at that time.23 The nonsynonymous mutations for both variants were concentrated primarily in the spike (S) protein region. Fourteen specific mutations were identified relative to the parental lineage XDV.1.5.1, with seven nonsynonymous sites consistently observed across all patient sequences, of which five were predicted to have deleterious effects on protein function and to destabilize the protein. Furthermore, variations at mutation sites were detected among the SARS-CoV-2 variant genomes from four pediatric patients, suggesting ongoing evolution of the viral variants.

The S protein is a homotrimeric glycoprotein and a highly variable region of the SARS-CoV-2 genome, where it plays a crucial role in viral evolution.39 Its receptor-binding domain (RBD) mediates infection by recognizing the ACE2 receptor, a process that influences viral cell tropism and makes the RBD a key target for vaccines and therapeutic antibodies.39 The Omicron variant harbors a substantial number of mutations relative to earlier variants, most of which are located in the S protein. These genetic changes enhance both transmissibility and immune evasion.40 Although Omicron variant infection can cause acute illness, the clinical manifestations are generally mild.40,41 The presentation of NB.1.8.1 infection is similar to that of other Omicron variant infections, with patients primarily seeking medical attention for mild clinical manifestations such as sore throat, fever, and cough.41 Alignment of sequenced NB.1.8.1 and PQ.2 strains with the Wuhan reference revealed that the nonsynonymous amino acid mutations were predominantly concentrated in the S protein, accounting for 60.2% of the total. The clinical manifestations of Omicron variant infection in infants and young children are generally mild and are managed in outpatient settings, consistent with previous reports.40,41

Evolutionary mutations in the SARS-CoV-2 genome can not only enhance its infectivity and immune evasion capability but can also alter its pathogenic potential. Single amino acid substitutions can induce structural changes that directly affect protein function. For example, the D614G substitution in the S protein, which was identified in early 2020, induces a conformational change in the cleavage site loop, increasing its accessibility to furin and promoting more efficient cleavage between the S1 and S2 subunits, thus facilitating more effective viral transmission and replication.42 Additionally, deleterious mutations that reduce protein stability may contribute to increased pathogenicity in SARS-CoV-2 variants.43,44

Q493E and A435S are key mutation sites in the S protein of the NB.1.8.1 lineage.22 The Q493E mutation, potentially acting in synergy with F456L, may enhance viral binding affinity to ACE2 and increase infectivity,45 whereas the A435S mutation reduces the effectiveness of neutralizing antibodies targeting conserved epitopes and may simultaneously strengthen interactions with ACE2, potentially improving the efficiency of host cell entry.22,41 Located in the N-terminal domain of the S protein, the T22N and F59S mutations may alter hydrophobic interactions between adjacent residues, affecting both the conformational stability of the S protein and its susceptibility to antibody neutralization; thereby potentially enhancing viral immune evasion and infectivity.46 Non-structural protein (Nsp)3 is critical for viral replication and host immune suppression, as it hydrolyzes ubiquitin and the ubiquitin-like interferon-stimulated gene 15 protein to interfere with the host immune response.47 Nsp9 and Nsp12 participate in forming the replication and transcription complex, mediating viral genome replication, and contributing to viral proliferation and host immune regulation.47 Predictions using various tools in this study suggest that the D284Y mutation may have deleterious effects on the Nsp12 protein and reduce its stability. However, Rodriguez et al48 found that the antiviral drugs remdesivir and obeldesivir retained activity against NB.1.8.1 carrying the mutations NSP3: I1891V, NSP9: P57S, and NSP12: D284Y; therefore, further research is required to investigate whether the D284Y mutation influences viral fitness by affecting Nsp12 function through alternative pathways.

The primary function of the N protein is to bind to the genomic RNA and form ribonucleoprotein complexes, which are involved in viral replication, assembly, and infection.49 The structure of the N protein comprises an N-arm (aa 1–48), an N-terminal domain (aa 49–174), a C-terminal domain (aa 247–364), a flexible linker region (aa 175–246), and a C-tail region (aa 247–419).47,49 The full-length structure of the N protein remains unresolved due to the high flexibility of its intrinsically disordered N-arm and C-tail regions, the complexity of its oligomeric assembly, and the absence of well-defined structural information.47,49 The Q9H mutation is situated in the N-arm region, whereas Q384H resides in the C-tail. In a clinical study, Alsuwairi et al50 found that mutations in these regions were associated with disease severity, and that C-tail mutations were associated with an increased risk of intensive care unit (ICU) admission, whereas N-arm mutations were associated with a reduced risk. The specific mechanisms through which the Q9H and Q384H mutations influence SARS-CoV-2 pathogenicity, however, require further investigation.

The ORF3a protein consists of an N-terminal ectodomain (aa 1–39), three transmembrane domains (TM1–TM3; aa 40–128), eight β‑strands (β1–β8; aa 145–235), and a C‑terminal region (aa 239–275).51 ORF3a functions as a viroporin and a key virulence factor, contributing to viral entry, replication, release, and pathogenesis.51 Specific mutations in ORF3a are associated with the clinical severity in COVID-19.52 For example, the Q57H and G251V variants are associated with an increased risk of ICU admission or severe outcomes, whereas the S253P mutation is associated with an increased case fatality rate.52 In this study, we identified a W193R mutation in the ORF3a protein of the PQ.2 variant from patient P1, which is a defining characteristic of this lineage.53 This mutation disrupts the ORF3a-VPS39 interaction, impairing lysosomal exocytosis and viral release.54 We identified the L46F and F207C mutations in ORF3a in the NB.1.8.1 variant from patient P4. The L46F mutation resides in the TM1 region near Q57H, but its functional impact on NB.1.8.1 has not been established. The Q213K mutation within the A*01:01-restricted CD8+ ORF3a epitope FTSDYYQLY207-215, located in the β7 segment, abolishes cytotoxic T-cell reactivity.55 The potential for the F207C mutation to assist the virus in immune evasion requires experimental confirmation.

Based on whole-genome alignment and in silico predictions, this study analyzed SARS-CoV-2 variants from four pediatric patients. Among 14 specific mutations identified, five were predicted to be deleterious, potentially affecting protein stability. This finding suggests mutation patterns that are potentially relevant to protein stability, which may guide future functional studies. However, the primary limitation of this study is the small sample size, which restricts the generalizability of the mutation site findings. Further validation with larger sample collections is needed in future studies. In addition, infection with NB.1.8.1 or PQ.2 variants was detected in pediatric patients presenting with gastrointestinal symptoms, which are common and nonspecific responses observed in SARS-CoV-2 infection. Notably, gastrointestinal symptoms have been reported in patients infected with the NB.1.8.1 variant;25 however, similar symptoms have not yet been documented for the PQ.2 variant. In this study, stool testing was not performed; therefore, the presence of gastrointestinal symptoms in patients could not rule out as being caused by other enteric viral infections.

Conclusion

In summary, we systematically analyzed the SARS-CoV-2 genomes of samples from infants and young children infected with the NB.1.8.1 and PQ.2 SARS-CoV-2 Omicron variants who presented with gastrointestinal symptoms, and computationally predicted that these lineages carry multiple specific deleterious mutations. The emerging variants identified in pediatric patients exhibited differences in mutation sites, providing valuable observational data into the ongoing evolution of these variants. However, this study is limited by a small sample size of only four patients, which restricts the generalizability of these findings. Future investigations will involve the collection of larger samples to monitor the dynamic evolution of mutation sites in emerging variants. This information could be used to support the public health response to outbreaks driven by these SARS-CoV-2 variants in the post-pandemic era.
