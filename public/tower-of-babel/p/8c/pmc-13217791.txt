From lipid modification to cell death: the diverse roles of S-palmitoylation in cell fate decisions
Authors: Yue Zhu, Chunyu Ma, Liping Zhong, Tingting Wang, Weiwei Liu, Zishu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractS-palmitoylation is a dynamic, reversible lipid modification that plays a crucial role in the regulation of various types of cell death. However, systematic insights into how S-palmitoylation modifications integrate and mediate crosstalk among diverse cell death pathways, including apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, and cuproptosis, to ultimately determine cell fate remain elusive. We propose the concept of the “palmitoylation code,” which posits that S-palmitoylation affects substrate function and signaling pathways through site specificity, kinetic dynamics, and spatial distribution, ultimately regulating the cell’s decision between survival and death. This review describes the regulatory principles of this code in major cell death pathways and provides a comprehensive overview of palmitoylation-targeting strategies and pharmacological inhibitors through an examination of how targeting the “palmitoylation code” can modulate cell fate transitions.Keywords: S-palmitoylation, Cell death, Apoptosis, Ferroptosis, Pyroptosis, Targeted therapy

Introduction

Cell death is a vital biological process [1, 2] that ranges from classical apoptosis [3–5] to more recently discovered processes such as ferroptosis [6–8], cuproptosis [9, 10], and pyroptosis [11], all of which participate in life signaling and disease progression. Although the molecular mechanisms that underlie these death pathways have been extensively studied, a core question remains unresolved: How does a cell precisely select a specific death modality under different stimuli? Accumulating evidence suggests that posttranslational modifications (PTMs) play a significant role in this decision-making process [12, 13]. Among these modifications, S-palmitoylation, as a dynamic and reversible lipid modification, may function as a critical “molecular switch” that determines the cell death modality through its unique regulatory mechanisms [14, 15].

Palmitoylation occurs in three major forms: S-, N-, and O-palmitoylation. S-palmitoylation is a reversible modification in which palmitate is attached to cysteine residues via a labile thioester bond, which allows cycles of acylation and deacylation that respond quickly to cellular signals. This reversible and dynamic nature of S-palmitoylation contributes to its role in protein localization and function [16, 17]. In contrast, N-palmitoylation involves the attachment of palmitate to an N-terminal glycine or cysteine residue through a stable amide linkage and is generally irreversible [18]. In contrast, O-palmitoylation occurs on serine or threonine residues through stable ester linkages, as seen in the processing of Wnt ligands [19, 20]. The following sections focus on S-palmitoylation, whose reversibility underpins its role in cell death signaling. DHHC family protein acyltransferases (PATs) transfer palmitoyl groups to specific cysteine residues on substrate proteins [21, 22]. Depalmitoylating enzymes, such as acyl protein thioesterases (APT1/2) [23, 24], palmitoyl-protein thioesterase 1 (PPT1) [25] and members of the α/β hydrolase domain-containing 17 family (ABHD17A/B/C) [26], participate in a dynamic regulatory cycle [27, 28]. Their dynamic and reversible nature enables rapid responses to intra- and extracellular signals that regulate cell death decisions by altering the membrane-binding capacity, stability, and interactions of substrate proteins [29]. Interestingly, the same DHHC enzyme can play diverse roles in different types of cell death. For instance, zinc finger DHHC-type palmitoyltransferase 5 (DHHC5) promotes receptor-interacting serine/threonine-protein kinase 1 (RIPK1) transactivation to induce apoptosis [30], while DHHC5 also maintains autophagy homeostasis by modifying Beclin 1 [31], a key autophagy regulatory protein. These divergent outcomes suggest that S-palmitoylation may determine cell death modes via a specific “modification code”.

According to the literature and known S-palmitoylation modification patterns, we propose that S-palmitoylation determines cell death modes through three principles (Fig. 1): (1) Site specificity: Modification at different sites on the same protein results in distinct functions. (2) Kinetic properties: The timing of palmitoylation–depalmitoylation affects signal transduction. (3) Spatial distribution: The distinct subcellular localization of DHHC enzymes produces differential effects. We refer to this framework as the “palmitoylation code,” which can explain the paradox wherein the same modifying enzyme (e.g., DHHC5) promotes both survival and death in varying contexts. Here, we clarify the regulatory principles of S-palmitoylation in major cell death pathways and explore how targeting the “palmitoylation code” can alter cell fate decisions.

Characteristics of S-Palmitoylation—the modification code

Site-specificity

The specificity of S-palmitoylation predominantly involves the selection of cysteine residues for modification; palmitoylation at different sites on the same protein can result in significantly different biological outcomes. For example, the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) cysteine residues Cys1025 and Cys1122 in the C-terminal tail can both be palmitoylated. Studies indicate that S-palmitoylation at Cys1025 primarily anchors the EGFR C-terminal tail to the plasma membrane, which reduces its binding to Grb2 and thereby attenuates downstream signaling. In contrast, S-palmitoylation at Cys1122 is increased upon EGF stimulation, which accelerates EGFR endocytosis and degradation and thus limits prolonged signaling activation [32–34]. Additionally, S-palmitoylation sites near the EGFR kinase domain, such as Cys775, Cys781, and Cys797, primarily regulate EGFR trafficking from the Golgi apparatus to the plasma membrane. Therefore, S-palmitoylation at different sites on EGFR induces distinct functional outcomes, such as the modulation of signaling and protein localization [35].

Similarly, during NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation, the S-palmitoylation of NLRP3 at Cys130 occurs in the early priming stage before activation. This modification promotes NLRP3 activation and inflammasome assembly [36, 37]. In contrast, S-palmitoylation at Cys844 promotes NLRP3 degradation via the lysosomal pathway, which terminates signaling in the later stages of inflammasome formation and prevents excessive inflammation [38, 39]. Thus, S-palmitoylation at different sites on a substrate protein can produce distinct biological effects that regulate multiple stages of different signaling pathways.

Kinetic properties

The kinetic properties of S-palmitoylation depend on the coordination between palmitoylating and depalmitoylating enzyme activities [40, 41]. For example, S-palmitoylation of the human Ras protein is catalyzed by the Golgi‑localized DHHC9–GCP16 complex [42]. Newly synthesized Ras must reach the Golgi and undergo S-palmitoylation to acquire strong hydrophobicity before its packaging into transport vesicles and delivery to the plasma membrane. Ras on the plasma membrane is then depalmitoylated by specific enzymes, after which it dissociates from the membrane and diffuses back to the Golgi apparatus for remodification [43–45]. This palmitoylation–depalmitoylation cycle enables Ras to shuttle between the plasma membrane and the Golgi apparatus, thereby modulating downstream signaling [46, 47].

Pyroptosis is also dynamically regulated by palmitoylation–depalmitoylation. During pyroptosis, the cleavage and oligomerization of gasdermin D (GSDMD) are controlled by palmitoylation [14]. GSDMD is palmitoylated at Cys191, which promotes caspase‑1‑mediated cleavage and the release of the active N-terminal fragment (GSDMD‑NT). This modification increases hydrophobicity and enables membrane targeting. Depalmitoylation occurs after GSDMD-NT reaches the plasma membrane, where APT2 removes the palmitoyl group, exposes the Cys191 site, and triggers oligomerization and pore formation [48, 49]. Thus, the cyclical mechanism involving palmitoylation and depalmitoylation ensures that GSDMD-NT oligomerizes only at the correct location, which prevents premature or aberrant pore formation. This dynamic modification and temporal control help maintain precision in biological processes and prevent excessive or premature signal activation.

Spatial distribution

S-palmitoylation is not a random event but is controlled by the specific subcellular localization of PATs. Consequently, the compartment in which a substrate encounters its specific PAT critically determines the functional consequence of S-palmitoylation. For example, in fatty acid uptake mediated by CD36, DHHC4 and DHHC5 cooperate at distinct locations to control the S-palmitoylation of the fatty acid translocase CD36 [50–52]. DHHC4 catalyzes the initial S-palmitoylation of CD36 at the Golgi apparatus, which promotes its trafficking to the plasma membrane. In contrast, DHHC5 maintains the palmitoylated state of CD36 at the plasma membrane, ensuring its stable membrane anchorage and fatty acid binding. Upon fatty acid binding, the kinase LYN phosphorylates and inactivates DHHC5. This inactivation triggers APT1-mediated depalmitoylation of CD36, which leads to its endocytosis and subsequent fatty acid transport into cells [16]. Thus, “spatial distribution characteristics” provide “geographic coordinates” for S-palmitoylation, enabling compartmentalized regulation and functional specificity of signaling pathways. Notably, the continuous cycle of modification–demodification, which is reliant on a specific spatial architecture, itself constitutes a unique kinetic model.

Decoding S-palmitoylation in major cell death pathways

On the basis of the fundamental rules of the “palmitoylation code” outlined above, we now apply them to major cell death pathways to decipher how these pathways are precisely regulated.

Apoptosis and necroptosis

Apoptosis, the canonical form of programmed cell death [53, 54], involves the tightly regulated activation of the core executioner caspase protease family [55, 56] and is governed by the mitochondrial pathway (intrinsic pathway) and the death receptor pathway (extrinsic pathway) [57, 58]. S-palmitoylation plays crucial roles in the initiation, amplification, and termination of apoptotic signaling by precisely regulating the stability, membrane localization, and protein interactions of key proteins involved [59]. In contrast, necroptosis is another form of regulated cell death that is characterized by typical morphological features of necrosis, such as plasma membrane rupture and the release of cellular contents [60]. It is caspase-independent, and its core execution involves RIPK1, RIPK3, and mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL). When apoptosis is inhibited, necroptosis serves as an alternative cell death mechanism [61]. Research on the role of S-palmitoylation in necroptosis is still in its early stages, but its role is beginning to emerge (Fig. 2).

TNFR1: Depalmitoylation by APT2 facilitates receptor endocytosis and survival signaling activation. Activation of the extrinsic apoptotic pathway begins with the oligomerization of death receptors (e.g., Fas/CD95 and tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 (TNFR1)) on the cell membrane [62, 63]. With the Fas receptor as an example, a specific cysteine residue, Cys199, within its intracellular segment is catalyzed by the palmitoyl acyltransferase DHHC7, which is localized to the Golgi apparatus [64]. This modification exemplifies typical spatial distribution characteristics: newly synthesized Fas is palmitoylated in the Golgi apparatus, enabling its stable targeting to lipid raft microdomains in the plasma membrane [65, 66]. Lipid rafts, which are enriched in sphingolipids and cholesterol, facilitate the formation of SDS-stable high-molecular-weight aggregates of palmitoylated Fas. Fas then efficiently recruits Fas-associated death domain (FADD) protein and procaspase-8 to form the death-inducing signaling complex (DISC), which amplifies the apoptotic initiation signal [67, 68]. In TNF signaling, which is part of the extrinsic pathway, the adaptor protein RIPK1 is a key molecular switch that determines cell fate toward survival (through nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) signaling) or death (apoptosis or necroptosis) [69–71]. Research has shown that upon TNF stimulation, RIPK1 is palmitoylated at Cys256 within its kinase domain in TNFR1 signaling complex I, which is catalyzed by DHHC5. This process facilitates its autophosphorylation, which activates its kinase activity and thereby promotes apoptosis or necroptosis [30]. In the context of a metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) model, fatty acids increase the expression of DHHC5 through the JNK/c-Jun pathway, which subsequently increases the S-palmitoylation level of RIPK1. This leads to massive hepatocyte death and exacerbated liver injury [30]. Moreover, the S-palmitoylation of the TNFR1 receptor itself is dynamically balanced. TNF signaling activates the depalmitoylating enzyme APT2 to remove palmitate from TNFR1, a critical step that promotes receptor internalization and subsequent NF-κB signaling activation [72]. This dynamic reversible modification finely balances the survival and death signals mediated by TNFR1.

The core event of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway is mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP) [73]. Bcl-2-associated X protein (BAX) is localized within the cytoplasm under resting conditions [74, 75]. Upon apoptotic stimulation, its Cys126 site is palmitoylated by multiple DHHC enzymes (e.g., DHHC3, 7, 11, 12, and 21) [76]. This modification drives the targeting and firm anchoring of BAX to the mitochondrial outer membrane. Palmitoylated BAX is more prone to conformational changes and oligomerization, ultimately leading to MOMP [76]. In addition to the direct regulation of effector proteins, S-palmitoylation can indirectly influence the intrinsic pathway. For instance, in germinal center B-cell-like diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (GCB-DLBCL), G protein subunit alpha 13 (GNA13) undergoes S-palmitoylation at the N-terminal Cys14 and Cys18 residues. Palmitoylated GNA13 negatively regulates the expression of the antiapoptotic protein B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL2). Impairment of GNA13 through mutation or inhibition of its S-palmitoylation reduces cellular sensitivity to specific BCL2 inhibitors such as Venetoclax [77]. Furthermore, given the crucial role of the tumor suppressor p53 in apoptosis induction [78, 79], palmitoylation critically affects p53 functionality. p53 must be modified by the palmitoyltransferase DHHC1 at Cys135, Cys176, and Cys275 to achieve nuclear translocation and initiate the transcriptional program of its downstream proapoptotic genes [80]. Notably, in many cancers, epigenetic silencing of DHHC1 through promoter hypermethylation constitutes a nonmutational mechanism that inactivates p53 and enables cancer cells to evade apoptosis [80].

Ferroptosis

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent, lipid peroxidation-driven form of regulated cell death [81]. Recent studies have revealed that S-palmitoylation plays a key role in determining cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis, as it precisely regulates core proteins in the ferroptosis pathway [82, 83]. Its regulatory network focuses primarily on two major defense hubs: glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and the system Xc⁻ subunit solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) (Fig. 3) [84, 85].

S-palmitoylation in ferroptosis. DHHC8: Catalyzes the S-palmitoylation of both SLC7A11 and GPX4 to synergistically inhibit lipid peroxidation. SLC7A11: S-palmitoylation promoted by DUXAP8 prevents its lysosomal degradation; independently, S-palmitoylation catalyzed by DHHC8 inhibits its ubiquitin-mediated degradation. GPX4: S-palmitoylation by DHHC8 and DHHC20 promotes its correct subcellular localization and suppresses its ubiquitination-mediated degradation, respectively. TfR1: DHHC5-mediated S-palmitoylation stabilizes TfR1 at the plasma membrane by reducing its hyper-endocytosis, whereas TfR1 hyper-endocytosis or overexpression causes dysregulated iron uptake and ferroptosis

As the key enzyme responsible for clearing lipid peroxides [86], GPX4 undergoes site-specific palmitoylation at Cys75 and Cys66, which convey distinct molecular instructions related to “localization” and “stability,”, respectively, collectively determining whether cellular ferroptosis will occur. The palmitoyltransferase DHHC8 catalyzes the palmitoylation of GPX4 at Cys75 [87]. This modification does not alter its enzymatic activity but serves as a spatial targeting signal that promotes the localization of GPX4 to membrane structures such as the endoplasmic reticulum where lipid peroxidation occurs; this enables GPX4 to eliminate lipid peroxides at their source [83]. The Cys66 site of GPX4 is modified by the palmitoyltransferase DHHC20. The primary function of this modification is to protect GPX4 from ubiquitin‒proteasome degradation, thereby extending its half-life and enhancing its stability [84, 88]. The reversibility of this pathway is controlled by the depalmitoylating enzyme APT2. Inhibition of APT2 can counteract ferroptosis and exert protective effects in models such as hepatic ischemia‒reperfusion injury [84].

The stability of the system Xc⁻ subunit SLC7A11 is also influenced by palmitoylation [89]. DHHC8 has been shown to catalyze SLC7A11 palmitoylation at Cys327 [90]. This modification, by competing with ubiquitination, inhibits SLC7A11 degradation and maintains its stable expression on the membrane. In glioblastoma, upstream AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activates DHHC8 via phosphorylation, resulting in the formation of a positive AMPK/DHHC8/SLC7A11 signaling axis that promotes tumor growth [90]. Additionally, the long noncoding RNA DUXAP8 directly binds SLC7A11 and promotes its palmitoylation at Cys414, which prevents its degradation by lysosomes [91] and thereby confers sorafenib resistance in hepatocellular carcinoma cells [92]; this reveals the complexity of multiple molecular layers that contribute to the same stability code.

Other key proteins in the ferroptosis regulatory network are also affected by palmitoylation. The functionality of transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1), for instance, is critically dependent on palmitoylation [93, 94]. In oligodendrocytes, the DHHC5-mediated palmitoylation of TfR1 at Cys98 is essential for maintaining its membrane localization and preventing iron overload. Neonatal sevoflurane anesthesia downregulates DHHC5 expression, which leads to increased TfR1 endocytosis and dysregulated iron uptake, and ultimately triggers oligodendrocyte ferroptosis [95]. In castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC), DHHC2-mediated palmitoylation of ubiquitin-specific protease 19 (USP19) attenuates its interaction with acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), which promotes ACSL4 degradation via the ubiquitin‒proteasome pathway and consequently suppresses lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis [96]. These findings collectively highlight palmitoylation as a promising regulatory axis that controls ferroptosis.

Pyroptosis

Pyroptosis is a lytic form of programmed cell death mediated by the gasdermin protein family [97] and plays a central role in host defense and inflammatory diseases [98]. A wealth of recent evidence indicates that S-palmitoylation is not only a key regulator of NLRP3 inflammasome activation but also a critical step for membrane pore formation and the execution of cell lysis by the GSDM proteins themselves (Fig. 4) [99, 100].

S-palmitoylation in pyroptosis. NLRP3: S-palmitoylation by DHHC1/5/7 promotes its localization to the Golgi apparatus and its subsequent activation; furthermore, NLRP3 is palmitoylated by DHHC5, which enhances the NLRP3-NEK7 interaction and drives inflammasome assembly; conversely, NLRP3 may be palmitoylated by DHHC12, which promotes its protein degradation. GSDMD: S-palmitoylation by DHHC5/7/9 facilitates the targeting of GSDMD-NT to the plasma membrane, while depalmitoylation by APT2 promotes pore formation, revealing a critical dynamic regulatory cycle. GSDME: Palmitoylated by DHHC7/14, thereby promoting the dissociation of its N- and C-terminal fragments following proteolytic cleavage

NLRP3, the core sensor protein of the inflammasome, activates caspase-1 to cleave GSDMD and trigger pyroptosis [101, 102]. The cysteine residues of NLRP3 undergo palmitoylation, which regulates its localization. Specifically, palmitoylation at the Cys130 site (Cys126 in mice), which is catalyzed by PATs such as DHHC1/5/7, is required for the targeting of NLRP3 to the Golgi apparatus and its response to activation signals [103–105]. The drug disulfiram has been shown to inhibit NLRP3 palmitoylation by covalently binding to this site, thereby blocking inflammasome activation [36, 105]. Further mechanistic studies revealed the complexity of NLRP3 palmitoylation: DHHC1-mediated modification of NLRP3 at Cys958 is involved in the regulation of localization during the inflammasome “priming” phase [104]; DHHC5-mediated palmitoylation at Cys837/838 enhances the interaction between NLRP3 and NIMA-related kinase 7 (NEK7) during the activation phase, which drives inflammasome assembly [106]; and DHHC12-catalyzed palmitoylation at Cys844 acts in the late activation phase to terminate signaling by promoting NLRP3 protein degradation [38].

During the pyroptosis execution stage, S-palmitoylation directly regulates the membrane localization and activation of GSDM proteins. Multiple studies have confirmed that Cys191 in human GSDMD (Cys192 in mice) is the key site for its functional palmitoylation. This modification is catalyzed by the palmitoyltransferases DHHC5, DHHC7, and DHHC9 [14, 107, 108]. This process is positively regulated by reactive oxygen species (ROS) and is crucial for the targeting of GSDMD-N-terminal fragment to the plasma membrane and its subsequent oligomerization [14, 100, 109]. Notably, under specific conditions, palmitoylation is sufficient to partially relieve the autoinhibition mediated by the C-terminal domain, enabling full-length, uncleaved GSDMD to form functional plasma membrane pores [110]. These findings reveal an alternative activation pathway that acts independently of proteolytic cleavage [14, 111]. A critical concept here is the “palmitoylation–depalmitoylation relay,” which drives pore formation through a clear sequence. S-palmitoylation first delivers GSDMD-NT to the membrane, after which APT2-mediated depalmitoylation exposes the Cys191 residue. This exposure promotes its oxidation, leading to final oligomerization and pore formation [48]. In chemotherapy-induced pyroptosis, gasdermin E (GSDME) is palmitoylated at Cys407 and Cys408 within its C-terminal domain. Unlike GSDMD, this modification does not affect GSDME cleavage by Caspase-3 but promotes the dissociation of the cleaved N-terminal and C-terminal fragments, thereby releasing active GSDME-NT [112]. Through binding to the C-terminal domain of GSDME and inhibiting its palmitoylation, circPDIA3 facilitates C-terminal/N-terminal domain interactions that suppress pyroptosis, which leads to chemotherapy resistance in colorectal cancer [113].

Cuproptosis

Cuproptosis is a recently identified modality of regulated cell death [114, 115] characterized by its induction through copper ion overload and its fundamental dependence on mitochondrial respiratory function [116, 117]. Current research indicates that S-palmitoylation influences copper homeostasis by regulating the activity and localization of the copper transporter solute carrier family 31 member 1 (SLC31A1) (Fig. 5). Polystyrene nanoplastics activate the palmitoyltransferase DHHC14 in macrophages, catalyzing S-palmitoylation of the copper uptake protein SLC31A1 at Cys189. This modification competitively inhibits the ubiquitination and degradation of SLC31A1, which increases its membrane stability and expression, promotes copper influx, and induces cuproptosis [118]. In synovial M2 macrophages, DHHC5-mediated palmitoylation of P2X purinoceptor 7 (P2 × 7) at Cys377 is required for its membrane targeting and suppression of cuproptosis. Degradation of DHHC5 via tripartite motif-containing protein 7 (TRIM7)-mediated ubiquitination disrupts P2 × 7 palmitoylation, leading to its mislocalization, impaired copper efflux, and subsequent cuproptosis [119]. Notably, while this study revealed that palmitoylation is essential for P2 × 7 membrane localization and copper export, the molecular mechanism through which this transmembrane cation channel [120, 121] facilitates copper efflux remains unclear and warrants further investigation. Furthermore, in clear cell renal cell carcinoma, DHHC18 expression confers resistance to cuproptosis. Depletion of DHHC18 downregulates the expression of the cuproptosis suppressors cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2 A (CDKN2A) and metal-regulatory transcription factor 1 (MTF1) [122, 123]; however, the role of the enzymatic palmitoylation activity of DHHC18 in this pathway remains to be determined [124].

S-palmitoylation in cuproptosis. SLC31A1: S-palmitoylation by DHHC14 inhibits its ubiquitin-mediated degradation, thereby sustaining copper influx and promoting cuproptosis. P2X7: S-palmitoylation by DHHC5 increases its ability to target the plasma membrane and thus inhibits cuproptosis

Autophagy

Autophagy, a vital homeostatic process, generally functions as a protective mechanism for cell survival [125, 126]. However, dysregulation or excessive activation of autophagy can paradoxically lead to cell death [127, 128]. The regulation of autophagy relies on the ordered assembly of core protein complexes and membrane trafficking [129], a process in which S-palmitoylation serves as a critical coordinator (Fig. 6) [130].

ATG9A and Beclin 1: DHHC5 is the palmitoyltransferase for both ATG9A and Beclin 1 and promotes ATG9A trafficking and VPS34 complex assembly, respectively, thereby facilitating PI3P production. ATG16L1: DHHC7 catalyzes the S-palmitoylation of ATG16L1, accelerating the lipidation of LC3. ATG2A: Dynamic palmitoylation and depalmitoylation by DHHC11 and APT1 regulate its association and dissociation from the ER membrane, thereby mediating ATG2A-dependent lipid transfer

During the autophagy initiation phase, S-palmitoylation regulates the activity and localization of core complexes. DHHC13-mediated palmitoylation of unc-51-like autophagy-activating kinase 1 (ULK1) at Cys927 and Cys1003 is a key step in the recruitment of the ULK1 complex to the autophagosome formation site [131]. This modification drives the phosphorylation of its downstream substrate, autophagy-related protein 14 L (ATG14L), which activates the VPS34 complex and initiates autophagy [132, 133]. In parallel, upon autophagy induction, DHHC5 is initially localized to the plasma membrane and is recruited to the endomembrane system via clathrin-mediated endocytosis. DHHC5 then catalyzes the palmitoylation of autophagy-related protein 9 A (ATG9A) at Cys155 and Cys156 [134], which drives the trafficking of ATG9A vesicles from the trans-Golgi network to the autophagosome nucleation site, providing the essential membrane source for the PI3P platform generated by the VPS34 complex [135]. Concurrently, DHHC5 also catalyzes Beclin 1 palmitoylation at Cys137, which enhances its hydrophobic interactions with ATG14L and vacuolar protein sorting 15 (VPS15) to specifically promote the assembly and activation of the proautophagic VPS34 complex [31]. During autophagosome formation, DHHC7-mediated palmitoylation of autophagy-related protein 16-like protein 1 (ATG16L1) at Cys153 specifically enhances its binding to WD repeat domain phosphoinositide interacting 2B (WIPI2B) and RAB33B, a member of the RAS oncogene family‌ (RAB33B) [15], which ensures efficient recruitment of the microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3) lipidation machinery to the phagophore [136, 137]. Furthermore, the lipid transfer function of autophagy-related protein 2 homolog A (ATG2A) is dynamically regulated by palmitoylation. DHHC11 and APT1 coregulate the palmitoylation status of its C-terminal residues (Cys1704, Cys1713, and Cys1714), which controls its anchoring to and release from the ER and thereby mediates lipid delivery to the expanding phagophore [138].

In selective autophagy, palmitoylation can function as a “recruitment signal” or a “protective shield.” On the one hand, DHHC19-mediated palmitoylation of the selective autophagy receptor p62 at Cys289 and Cys290 enhances the affinity of p62 droplets for the autophagosomal membrane, which promotes cargo degradation [139]. On the other hand, palmitoylation can spatially hinder the recognition of substrates by autophagy receptors. For instance, DHHC5- and DHHC2-mediated palmitoylation of nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-containing protein 2 (NOD2) and B-raf proto-oncogene serine/threonine-protein kinase (B-RAF)/Raf-1 proto-oncogene serine/threonine-protein kinase (C-RAF), respectively, impedes their binding to the cargo receptors SQSTM1/p62 or optineurin (OPTN)/toll-interacting protein (TOLLIP) [140–142]. This inhibits their degradation via selective autophagy, thereby stabilizing inflammatory signals and pro-survival signaling pathways. Additionally, palmitoylation serves as a critical negative feedback mechanism that drives the autophagic clearance of specific signaling molecules. A prime example is the DHHC12-catalyzed palmitoylation of NLRP3, which increases its affinity for the chaperone heat shock cognate 71 kDa protein (HSC70) and promotes its degradation via chaperone-mediated autophagy, thereby preventing excessive inflammation [38, 143].

Cross-talk in S-palmitoylation and cell fate decision-making

We have decoded the regulatory principles of the “palmitoylation code” within individual cell death pathways. However, cellular fate is not the sum of these pathways, but rather, it emerges from a complex and integrated network [144, 145]. The power of this code lies in its capacity to integrate diverse stimuli and mediate cross-talk between distinct death modalities. This raises the following question: How can a limited repertoire of PATs respond to a vast array of signal combinations? The answer lies in the inherent network properties of the system, primarily the broad substrate specificity of individual PATs and their counteracting depalmitoylases [146, 147]. By concurrently regulating key components across multiple death pathways, these enzymes function as “molecular arbiters” that integrate upstream signals to ultimately determine cellular fate [148, 149].

DHHC5: a palmitoyl transferase at the crossroads of cell death

DHHC5 exemplifies how S-palmitoylation regulates crosstalk between cell death pathways. This enzyme can palmitoylate RIPK1 to unlock its kinase activity, which promotes apoptosis and necroptosis [30] and also modifies Beclin 1 to sustain autophagic flux and promote cell survival [31]. Concurrently, DHHC5 mediates the S-palmitoylation of GSDMD to execute pyroptosis [14, 107] and modifies TfR1 to regulate its membrane localization, thereby interfering with ferroptosis [95]. Therefore, the regulatory role of DHHC5 is not simply to turn a specific death pathway on or off. For instance, under inflammatory conditions, DHHC5 influences both pyroptosis and the initiation of apoptosis/necroptosis. The final cellular outcome is determined by the dynamic balance of the death signals integrated through DHHC5.

DHHC8 coordinates a dual-pathway defense against ferroptosis

DHHC8 orchestrates a dual-pathway defense against ferroptosis by regulating both the localization of GPX4 [87] and the protein stability of SLC7A11 [90], which synergistically enhances cellular antioxidant capacity. This ability to concurrently regulate multiple key nodes within a single death modality establishes DHHC8 as a central hub for maintaining intracellular redox homeostasis. Its activity critically influences the cellular threshold for ferroptosis [150], which highlights its potential as a therapeutic target for the induction of ferroptotic cell death.

APT2: dynamic integration of multiple death signals

In the S-palmitoylation regulatory network, depalmitoylases are as critical as acyltransferases, as they provide an essential counterbalance [151–153]. APT2 plays a role in this process, as this protein dynamically influences cell death pathways. For example, in the TNF-α pathway, APT2 depalmitoylates TNFR1, which is required to promote receptor internalization and NF-κB signaling [72]. During pyroptosis, APT2 removes palmitate from GSDMD-NT, which is necessary for membrane pore formation [48]. In ferroptosis, APT2 positively drives cell death by the depalmitoylation of GPX4 at Cys66, thereby targeting it for degradation [84]. Therefore, the role of APT2 transcends mere “de-modification”. The function of APT2 emerges from the integration of its enzymatic activity, specific localization, and substrate selection, thereby regulating the balance among cell survival, apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis.

Therapeutic targeting and challenges

A deep understanding of the “palmitoylation code” marks a critical step from basic science to precision medicine applications. The pharmacologic enhancement of or interference with this code holds promise for the fine-tuned regulation of cell death decisions, which may offer novel therapeutic strategies for cancers [154, 155], neurodegenerative diseases [156, 157], autoimmune disorders [158], infectious diseases [159–161], and other conditions. According to our understanding of the various dimensions of the “palmitoylation code”, corresponding intervention strategies are evolving from traditional enzyme activity inhibition to more precise and multifaceted approaches.

Direct targeting of enzyme activity modifications

This is currently the most intuitive strategy, as it aims to interfere directly with the “writers” (PATs) and “erasers” (APTs) of the code using small molecules.

DHHC enzyme inhibitor 2-bromopalmitate (2-BP) is the most classic and widely used broad-spectrum PAT inhibitor [162, 163]. It is metabolized intracellularly to 2-BP-CoA [164], which forms an irreversible covalent bond with the cysteine residue in the active site of PATs via its bromoalkyl group, thereby inhibiting the autopalmitoylation and catalytic activity of the enzyme [165–167]. 2-BP has been extensively used in various disease models [168, 169], but its poor selectivity and associated toxicity limit its clinical translation and application [165, 166, 170]. As an optimized version of 2-BP, cyano-myracrylamide (CMA) is less readily converted to the CoA form and is less cytotoxic, and thus it is a potent alternative to 2-BP [170, 171]. More promising is the development of selective PAT inhibitors. For example, artemisinin was identified as a specific inhibitor of DHHC6, as it effectively disrupts N-Ras palmitoylation and oncogenic signaling [172]. PF-670,462 induces lysosomal degradation of DHHC8, which indirectly inhibits the palmitoylation of its substrate GPX4, sensitizes cells to ferroptosis and synergizes with immunotherapy [87, 173].

APT Enzyme Modulators: The activity of depalmitoylases is equally critical in shaping cell fate, as demonstrated by emerging chemical tools. For instance, ML349, an inhibitor of APT2 [174], produces context-dependent outcomes. In antiviral immunity, APT2 inhibition increases mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein (MAVS) palmitoylation, which strongly amplifies the interferon response and antiviral defense [175]. According to previous cardiovascular research, the inhibition of APT2 exacerbates palmitic acid-induced pyruvate kinase isozyme M2 (PKM2) palmitoylation and endothelial injury [176]. This contrast highlights how functional output depends critically on substrate specificity. Selective pharmacological inhibition of APT1 with ML348 enhances the palmitoylation and membrane stability of estrogen receptor α (ERα), which confers neuroprotective effects and improves functional recovery in a Parkinson’s disease model [177]. Furthermore, broad-spectrum depalmitoylase inhibitors such as Palmostatin B/M, which covalently target both APT1 and APT2 [178], were initially characterized for their ability to disrupt the S-palmitoylation cycle of oncogenic Ras [179, 180]. However, their observed antitumor efficacy may partly stem from the inhibition of other depalmitoylases, including members of the ABHD17 family [181, 182].

Targeting substrate code sites

Targeting S-palmitoylation sites on substrates offers a more precise strategy. Instead of acting directly on palmitoyltransferases, this approach involves competitive binding to substrate modification sites through covalent interactions or steric hindrance [183]. For instance, nitrofuran derivatives such as C-176 and H-151 covalently bind to Cys91 of stimulator of interferon genes (STING), thereby preventing its S-palmitoylation and subsequent multimerization, a mechanism exploited for the treatment of autoinflammatory disease [184, 185]. Similarly, the compound NU6300, which features a vinyl sulfone warhead, covalently modifies Cys191 on GSDMD to directly block its palmitoylation and suppress pyroptosis [186]. Furthermore, several inhibitors (e.g., DC-TEADin02 and K-975) that target the autopalmitoylation pocket of the TEA domain (TEAD) of transcription factors occupy this hydrophobic cavity, where they competitively inhibit TEAD autopalmitoylation and consequently disrupt its oncogenic transcriptional activity [187, 188]. This strategy is typically mediated by small molecules that bind to S-palmitoylation sites on protein substrates and that demonstrate improved toxicity profiles compared with broad-spectrum enzyme inhibitors.

Disrupting enzyme–substrate interactions

Targeting the enzyme‒substrate interaction focuses on the disruption of specific recognition between a DHHC enzyme and its substrate, often using competitive peptide inhibitors [189, 190]. These peptides, designed to mimic the S-palmitoylation site sequence of the substrate protein and fused with a cell-penetrating peptide (CPP), can enter cells and act as “molecular decoys” to competitively occupy the active site of PATs [191]. A key example is the peptide CPP-S1 (also called PD-PALM). This peptide contains the sequence around the Cys272 palmitoylation site of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1). CPP-S1 enters cells and competes with PD-L1 for binding to its cognate palmitoyltransferase, DHHC3. This competitive binding blocks PD-L1 palmitoylation and results in the failure of the unmodified protein to anchor stably at the plasma membrane. Consequently, nonpalmitoylated PD-L1 is subjected to increased ubiquitination and subsequent lysosomal degradation, which ultimately remodels the anti-tumor immune response [168]. Similar strategies validate this concept. For instance, CPP-S1 competes with octamer-binding transcription factor 4 A (Oct4A) for binding to DHHC17, reducing glioma stem cell self-renewal [192]. Another peptide, apelin receptor (APLNR)-S1, competes with the APLNR for binding to DHHC9, which leads to receptor degradation and reduced morphine tolerance [193]. The RAC-alpha serine/threonine-protein kinase (AKT1)-C224 penetrating peptide mimics the AKT1 modification site to occupy DHHC24, thereby inhibiting AKT signaling [194]. Moreover, the concept of competitive inhibition also extends to small molecules. For example, the natural compound salvianolic acid B (SalB), binds directly to the substrate pocket of DHHC5. This binding inhibits claudin-4 (CLDN4) palmitoylation and reverses chemoresistance in hepatocellular carcinoma [195].

Indirect strategies

Indirect inhibition of S-palmitoylation has emerged as an alternative strategy. This approach suppresses protein S-palmitoylation by limiting the supply of its substrate, palmitic acid [196, 197]. Fatty acid synthase (FASN) catalyzes de novo lipogenesis to generate the palmitic acid essential for this modification [17, 198, 199]. FASN inhibitors such as ASC40 (TVB-2640) and TVB-3166 therefore reduce the palmitoylation levels of multiple disease-related proteins, including signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) and tubulin, via the inhibition of palmitate synthesis [200, 201]. This pathway, which acts through indirect suppression of palmitoylation, has demonstrated promising efficacy in tumor and osteolysis models.

Conclusion and perspective

Recent in-depth research on protein S-palmitoylation has revealed its roles in various physiological and pathological processes. Its function in the regulation different cell death pathways has attracted particular attention. However, the same protein substrate can exert strikingly different biological effects after S-palmitoylation, a phenomenon that prompts further reflection. In this review, we use the concept of the “palmitoylation code” to frame this complexity. By this, we refer to the integrated regulatory logic by which site specificity, kinetic dynamics, and the spatial distribution of S-palmitoylation events collectively determine cell death decisions. This framework has been applied throughout the review to decode how S-palmitoylation participates in diverse cell death pathways, which demonstrates that a limited set of DHHC enzymes, by modifying distinct substrates at specific sites with precise timing and in defined subcellular compartments, can generate context-dependent outcomes in apoptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, and autophagy. For example, site specificity determines whether NLRP3 undergoes inflammasome activation or lysosomal degradation, which is based on whether Cys130 or Cys844 is modified. Kinetic dynamics govern GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis, in which a timed cycle of palmitoylation and depalmitoylation controls oligomerization and pore formation. Spatial distribution is exemplified by DHHC5, which promotes apoptosis through RIPK1 modification, sustains autophagy via Beclin 1 palmitoylation, and regulates ferroptosis by maintaining TfR1 membrane localization. This highlights how the same enzyme can exert distinct effects depending on the subcellular context in which it acts.

Currently, translating the “palmitoylation code” into clinical therapies still faces major challenges. Specificity is the primary obstacle: most DHHC enzymes have multiple substrates, and broadly inhibiting their activity may lead to unforeseen off-target effects. Therefore, targeting specific enzyme–substrate pairs will be a key future research direction. Another challenge is targeted delivery: the efficient delivery of drugs to diseased tissues or specific subcellular compartments is crucial for improved efficacy and reduced toxicity. With advances in technologies such as high-throughput screening and artificial intelligence, drug development that targets this important modification is expected to open new avenues for the treatment of a variety of major diseases.
