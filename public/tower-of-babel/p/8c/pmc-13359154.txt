Congenital myasthenic syndrome type 2C in a neonate: Redefining the phenotype of CHRNB1‐related myasthenic syndromes
Authors: Zurisadai Gonzalez, Simon Kayyal, Neda Zadeh, Julian Thomas
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe present a neonate with generalized weakness due to autosomal recessive congenital myasthenic syndrome type 2C (CMS2C) resulting from a compound heterozygous mutation in the CHRNB1 gene.Patient descriptionOur patient was determined by multiple methodologies to have a diagnosis of CMS2C (OMIM #616314). Whole‐genome sequencing revealed two distinct variants in the CHRNB1 gene (OMIM *100710): a maternally inherited 2 kb pathogenic microdeletion on chromosome 17p13.1 and a paternally inherited intronic deletion (c.1218‐9_1218‐7) that was reported by the laboratory as a variant of unknown significance.ConclusionsCMS2C is a rare autosomal recessive genetic condition associated with early‐onset muscle weakness. Our patient had a paternally inherited deletion in CHRNB1 (c.1218‐9_1218‐7) that was initially described as a variant of unknown significance. We suggest this finding is “likely pathogenic,” as this aberration has not been commonly described. He also had a partial deletion of CHRNB1 in the maternally inherited allele, which provides further evidence that partial gene deletions may be a more common molecular mechanism than previously known for this condition. The combination of the clinical presentation and electrophysiologic data allowed us to understand the molecular findings and ultimately diagnose CMS2C in our patient.Keywords: case report, CHRNB1, congenital myasthenic syndrome, weakness

Background

Congenital myasthenic syndromes (CMS) are a heterogeneous group of genetic neuromuscular conditions caused by variants in various genes known to be associated with the organization, maintenance, and function of the motor endplate. 1 , 2 CMS can present with a variety of symptoms, ranging from mild exercise intolerance and muscular weakness to severe life‐limiting forms in neonates. 3

To date, pathogenic variants in 32 genes are known to be associated with CMS: three autosomal dominant, 23 recessive, both patterns in four genes, and unknown in two. CMS can also be classified according to the mutated protein: eight proteins are associated with presynaptic CMS, four with synaptic CMS, 15 with postsynaptic CMS, and five with glycosylation defects. 1 , 4

CHRNB1 encodes the β‐subunit of the nicotinergic, postsynaptic acetylcholine receptor. Pathogenic variants in this gene are associated with both autosomal dominant and recessive forms, although only a few patients have been documented. 5 Here, we describe a patient with biallelic CHRNB1 variants and describe the clinical presentation, molecular findings, electrophysiologic results, and treatment response.

Patient description

This baby was born at 37 weeks' gestation via emergency C‐section secondary to breech presentation, umbilical cord prolapse, and nonreassuring fetal heart tones. Apgar scores were 6 and 7 at 1 and 5 min, respectively, and he developed respiratory failure requiring early intubation and mechanical ventilation. Neurological examination was notable for microretrognathia, tented upper lip, bilateral ptosis, ophthalmoparesis, elbow and knee contractures, camptodactyly, absent gag reflex, global hypotonia, and generalized weakness. His prenatal history was significant for polyhydramnios.

Continuous video electroencephalography, done to diagnose seizures as a cause for apnea, was recorded for 24 h without evidence of seizures. A broad diagnostic evaluation was then initiated to elucidate the etiology of the severe weakness and hypotonia. His newborn screening panel was negative for organic acid, fatty acid oxidation, and amino acid disorders. Metabolic testing included creatinine kinase 900 U/L (57–374 U/L), ammonia 39 (20–75), pyruvate, acylcarnitine, and plasma amino acids, as well as evaluation for congenital disorders of glycosylation, all of which were nondiagnostic. Molecular testing for myotonic dystrophy type 1 and assessment for chromosomal copy number variants (i.e., microarray) was normal. Brain magnetic resonance imaging showed small areas of T2 signal abnormality in parietal white matter bilaterally, consistent with hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy, but it did not explain the patient's severe clinical manifestations. Given the severity of his clinical menifestations and his uninformative prior investigations, whole‐genome sequencing was performed in an effort to obtain a genetic diagnosis that could alter medical management and determine the prognosis. Informed and disclosure consent was obtained from parents.

Genetic studies

Whole‐genome sequencing was performed on day 20 of life, and the results were obtained two weeks later. Two variants in the CHRNB1 gene were found. The maternally inherited exon 8 deletion (arr[GRCh37]17p13.1(7356766_7358046)x1) has been previously reported as pathogenic. Deletion of this exon is predicted to result in frameshifting and premature stop codon, likely leading to nonsense‐mediated decay. 2 , 5

The paternally inherited three‐base‐pair deletion c.1218‐9_1218‐7, located nine base pairs upstream of the intron 9 splice acceptor site, was reported by the laboratory as a variant of unknown significance. However, this variant has been reported by Freed et al. as “likely pathogenic” and is predicted to disrupt the splice acceptor site at the end of intron 9. If this leads to inappropriate splicing of exon 9, it will likely lead to a premature truncating codon and nonsense‐mediated decay similar to what has been described on the maternal exon 8 deletion. 5

A review of the literature identified a few reports describing CMS due to biallelic variants in CHRNB1. 2 , 5 , 6 , 7

Electrophysiologic studies

Repetitive nerve stimulation of the left ulnar and median nerve at 3 Hz demonstrated >30% decrement in weak muscles.

Based on the genetic results, the patient was started on oral pyridostigmine on day 21 of life and up‐titrated based on clinical response and side effect tolerance (5 mg/kg/day). His condition improved and he was able to be extubated and transitioned to room air. His motor exam also notably improved with antigravity movements, and he developed a weak suck and gag reflex; however, he required G‐tube placement. Postdischarge follow‐up (6 and 9 months of age), he was on room air, was G‐tube fed, could roll to his sides, and could bring hands to midline, as well as hold objects. Overall, despite his improvement he continued to have significant weakness and delays.

Discussion

We describe a newborn boy with severe weakness and respiratory failure due to a compound heterozygous or biallelic mutation in the CHRNB1 gene leading to a CMS type C. The paternal intronic deletion c.1218‐9_1218‐7, which was previously classified as a variant of uncertain significance by the laboratory, is predicted to reduce the quality of the natural splice acceptor site in intron 9, allowing abnormal splicing. 1 , 2 , 5 This three‐pair deletion has been found in a patient who presented with congenital myasthenia syndrome and features of fetal akinesia deformation sequence, and it was reported as likely pathogenic. 5 The maternally inherited 2 kb pathogenic microdeletion at 17p13.1 includes the 3′ portion of the CHRNB1 gene and has been associated with exon 8 skipping or deletion leading to a frameshift and truncated protein. This microdeletion has been found in patients with severe autosomal recessive CMS.

Based on the literature review, both variants would likely lead to a premature truncating codon and nonsense‐mediated decay, and ultimately reduce muscle nicotinic acetylcholine receptor. 5

To date, there are only four reported instances of biallelic mutations on CHRNB1 leading to CMS. 2 , 5 , 6 , 7 Those patients presented with fetal akinesia deformation sequence or severe CMS and were associated with partial deletions. This provides further evidence that partial gene deletions may be a more common molecular mechanism than previously known for this condition.

Our patient illustrates the challenges in the diagnosis of CMS and the use of electrophysiologic studies to confirm suspected neuromuscular junction transmission disorders and clarify the genetic data. Prompt diagnosis facilitates timely appropriate treatment. 8
