The association between body temperature parameters and blood culture positivity in the inpatient setting: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yue Zhai, Yu Du, Xiaohua Xu, Bingzhe Li, Zhixun Wang, Yuxia Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeBlood cultures (BC) are the gold standard for diagnosing bloodstream infections, yet their diagnostic yield is low, leading to misdiagnoses and increased mortality among hospitalized patients. Previous studies have shown mixed results regarding the role of body temperature in predicting BC positivity. We aimed to explore the relationship between body temperature parameters and BC positivity in hospitalized patients.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using data from a 5000-bed tertiary hospital between June 2022 and September 2025. The nearest temperature before BC collection and temperature fluctuation over the previous 24 h were analyzed in relation to BC results via multivariate logistic regression adjusted for potential confounders identified through directed acyclic graph, and inverse probability of treatment weighting.ResultsThe study included 4,481 episodes of BC collection corresponding to 2796 patients, with 1,281 positive and 3,200 negative results. A U-shaped relationship was observed between both body temperature parameters and BC positivity. Subgroup analyses showed significant interactions between body temperature parameters and antibiotic exposure, and infection sources in relation to BC positivity.ConclusionBody temperature parameters, particularly extremes and minimal fluctuations, are associated with blood culture positivity. These findings emphasize the importance of considering body temperature trends alongside other clinical factors such as immunosuppression, infection site, and prior treatment with antibiotics or antipyretics when considering BC collection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13700-0.Keywords: Bloodstream infections, Body temperature, Blood culture, Fever, Immunosuppression, Antimicrobials, Retrospective studies

Introduction

Blood cultures (BC) remain the gold standard test for diagnosing bloodstream infections (BSI). However, the yield of BC is low, leaving a significant proportion of patients with BSI being undiagnosed [1]. The timing of BC order and sample collection is a key factor for improving BC positivity and avoiding a missed diagnosis of BSI [2].

Fever is a coordinated systemic reaction to an immunological threat, e.g., bacterial infection [3]. Therefore, it is common practice to collection BC specimens in patients with suspected BSI when the body temperature is elevated or close a fever spike, especially in the inpatient setting [2, 4]. However, previous studies have mixed conclusions regarding the value of fever at the time of BC collection in increasing culture yield [5–8]. The conflicting evidence are possibly due to inconsistent and imprecise selection of confounding variables during model construction, various timing or value threshold of body temperature used, and inclusion of sample of heterogenous conditions and infection sources. Instead of a single body temperature value, body temperature fluctuation may better reflect individuals’ immunological response to bacterial infection. Lu et al. [9] found that compared with fever at different thresholds, an elevation of basal temperature greater than 1 °C is a better predictor of positive BC (PBC) and another study found lower maximum body temperature around the BC episode, which may signify a blunted febrile response, was associated with higher mortality [10].

In recent years, data-driven methodologies have been employed to predict BC positivity among a large number of clinical and demographic variables [11]. However, there is a lack of well-designed causal inference studies to elucidate the relationship between various body temperature parameters and PBC. We aim to explore whether body temperature parameters, among other clinical variables, are associated with PBC.

Methods

Design

We conducted a quantitative causal-inference study using retrospective data from the electronic health record between June 2022 and September 2025 in a 5000-bed metropolitan tertiary hospital, where relevant data sets were extracted, cleansed and analyzed. This study was reported in alignment with the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data (RECORD) statement.

Clinical trial number

Not applicable.

Population

All patients with at least one BC result in the inpatient setting were potentially eligible for inclusion. We excluded patients with the following active diagnoses which may lead to hyperthermia: malignant hyperthermia, anticholinergic toxicity, serotonin syndrome, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, salicylate poisoning, pheochromocytoma, Guillain-Barré syndrome, heat stroke, heat exhaustion. For patients with multiple PBCs, only the index PBC (the first positive culture within the past seven days) were included, trying to capture the real picture of novel infection onset [12]. Cultures from the same patient collected ≥ 8 days after the index PBC were considered new episodes. BC episodes without adequate body temperature data availability or outliers were also excluded: no body temperature measurement two hours before collection, less than three measurements within the past 24 h of collection, timespan across all measurements less than 12 h.

Outcome

The outcome is BC positivity. In our institution, most BCs were taken in two sets (four bottles), and positivity denotes the presence of at least one positive bottle, after ruling out suspected contamination [13] as reported by the microbiology lab.

Exposures

The primary exposures were two body temperature parameters (1) the nearest body temperature taken prior to BC (T nearest), (2) body temperature fluctuation within the 24-hour period before BC collection (ΔT 24 h = T max-T min). Time interval between BC collection and the highest temperature (Tmax) within the previous 24-hour period was calculated as the secondary exposure.

Covariates

Potential confounders were determined guided by the evidence synthesis for constructing directional-acyclic diagram (ESG-DAG) framework [14], consisting three stages: mapping, transforming and integration. Factors associated with the exposure variable or the outcome variable were first screened from literature review in PubMed. The causal relationships between variables were determined by temporality and biologic plausibility [15], which was an iterative process of literature review and DAG construction until consensus was reached within the research team. Refer to supplementary material 2 for detailed process of confounder selection process. The finalized DAG following integration has been published on dagitty.net (https://dagitty.net/m3xK9WcUz). The finalized DAG contained one minimal adjustment set: age, BMI, Charlson comorbidity index (CCI), immunosuppression, possible source of infection, prior antibiotic exposure, antipyretic exposure, gender.

Definitions of certain covariates and concepts are summarized in supplementary material 3.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as proportions, and continuous variables as mean (SD) or median (IQR). Two-way logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with BC results, with missing data were excluded. Since a single patient could contribute more than one blood culture episode, the assumption of independent observations could be violated. We applied inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) at the episode level and accounted for within-patient clustering when estimating standard errors [16]. This procedure produces robust “sandwich” standard errors that are valid under arbitrary within-cluster correlation and is equivalent to a generalized estimating equations (GEE) approach with an independent working correlation structure [17]. We applied Restricted cubic splines (RCS) and Generalized Additive Models (GAM) to visualize non-linear relationships. Analyses were conducted in R 4.5.1, with statistical significance set at 0.05.

Results

The raw data returned 3864 positive and 19,019 negative BC episodes, in which 3612 episodes of positive and 16,744 episodes of negative BC were collected in the inpatient setting. After ruling out cultures collected within seven days of the previous positive episode, 2192 and 9471 remained in the cohort, respectively. We excluded 4350 episodes with non-physiological temperature (< 33 °C or > 43 °C) (N = 572) or limited temperature data availability (N = 3778). 491 episodes have missing data for CCI, BMI, immunosuppression status, resulting to a total of 4481 episodes (1281 positive, 3200 negative) corresponding to 2796 patients included in the regression analysis (Fig. 1).

Subgroup analysis

To delineate the probable heterogenous relationship between body temperature and PBC among patients with different clinical characteristics, we further stratified the cohort by antibiotic use, antipyretic use, immunosuppression, age, gender and infection sites, all adjusted for covariates. We only found significant interaction between antibiotic use, infection sites and body temperature parameters (Table 4; Figs. 4 and 5, Supplementary Fig. 2–12).

Antibiotic exposure significantly modified the association between temperature parameters and BC positivity. In antibiotic-naïve patients, both higher temperature and greater temperature fluctuation were associated with increased probability of positivity. In antibiotic-exposed patients, higher temperatures and larger fluctuations were associated with lower PBC probability, while minimal fluctuation remained associated with the highest risk (Fig. 3). A dose–response gradient was evident, with more pronounced patterns at higher antibiotic doses (Supplementary Fig. 2–3).

Finally, we explored the relationship between body temperature parameters and PBC for infection stratified by suspected sources, with differentiated patterns identified for respiratory and IE/endovascular infections (Fig. 5, Supplementary Fig. 12).

Sensitivity analysis

In this section, we excluded episodes with: (1) suspected culture contamination for PBCs, (2) acute neurological conditions, (3) active cancer, (4) autoimmune/autoinflammatory diseases. The findings showed concordance with the main analyses (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

The aim of this causal inference study was to explore the association between body temperature parameters and probability of PBC, employing the DAG-ESG framework. Visualized using RCS or GAMs with splines, we demonstrated a U-shaped relationship between both body temperature and its fluctuation and BC positivity, with highest risk observed when the body temperature was below 36 °C or above 40 °C and when the temperature fluctuation was minimal (regardless of absolute body temperature) or greater than 5 °C within a 24-hour period. The trend remained mostly consistent in subgroup analyses. Our finding is consistent with previous findings that the presence of fever alone is not associated with PBC. Among the published predictions rules for BC positivity, the most extensively validated one is developed by Shapiro et al. [18], which utilized a high threshold for body temperature (> 39.4 °C) as a major criterion for ordering BC. Similarly, Lee et al. adopted a more stringent threshold of < 35 °C or > 40 °C for ruling in BC collection [19]. The threshold used in the more recent rule introduced by Julián-Jiménez et al. [20] was 38.3 °C; however, the core indicator in their model with the maximal scoring weight was procalcitonin rather than body temperature.

Previous investigations have mixed findings regarding body temperature variation and BSI, probably due to distinct study population and timing of measurement, i.e., already diagnosed with sepsis versus no suspicion of sepsis [21, 22]. Another perpective is the presence of sub-phenotypes of sepsis according to temperature trajectories, suggested by Bhavani et al. [23], where large and lack of body temperature fluctuation represent two subtypes of infected patients.

Immunosuppression is among the strongest risk factors for positive BC. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 10, for immunocompetent individuals, the probability of PBC drastically rises when body temperature reaches 39℃, signifying adequate response to bacterial infection. However, the immunocompromised are more likely to present as afebrile during a BSI episode. Fever has a protective effect during BSI, mediated by inflammatory responses signifying underlying stronger host immunity, or a beneficial effect of fever per se [24]. Impaired immune function may blunt or modify the fever response [25]. Factors such as end-stage renal disease, advanced liver disease and corticosteroid use are linked to afebrile bacteremia [26–28]. According to our findings (Supplementary Fig. 5), patients with true BSI seem more likely to be initiated on antipyretics before a culture is drawn. However, for critically ill patients, fever may be one of their limited immune defense mechanisms [29] and indiscriminate antipyresis might hinder infection clearance [30].

It is widely acknowledged that antibiotics may decrease the yield of BC, as supported by our findings. Patients receiving antibiotics exhibited distinct temperature patterns in relation to PBC, suggesting that antibiotic treatment may alter fever responses and influence diagnostic outcomes. For those who are antibiotic-exposed, both elevated body temperature and greater body temperature fluctuation were associated with lower BC positivity. We are unsure about the underlying mechanism behind this phenomenon. It is plausible that effective antimicrobial therapy will result in gradual normalization of body temperature while persistent febrile status signifies ineffective antimicrobial therapy or fever from a non-infective origin. However, a possible reverse association between body temperature and antibiotic exposure may exist according to Schuttevaer et al. [31] who found that among emergency patients with suspected BSI and a BC taken, those who are hyper- or hypothermic are more likely to be initiated with antibiotics.

We also explored the relationship between body temperature parameters and PBC stratified by source of infection. We found similar patterns across most suspected infection sites, with the exception of endovascular/IE and respiratory group. Due to the persistent nature of bacteremia in infective endocarditis, BCs may be collected regardless of febrile status, probably explaining the weaker correlation between body temperature and PBC. The relatively low probability of PBC for suspected endovascular infection/IE in our study may be due to the fact that these conditions are strong indications for BC and are liable to persistent bacteremia and therefore both initial and follow-up BCs are recommended [32], leading to more BCs being sent compared to other infection sources. Our analysis excluded samples sent within seven days of the index PBC, which may have underestimated BC positivity. Regarding respiratory infection, a review on indications for BC [33] concluded that both healthcare and community acquired pneumonia are associated with a low pretest probability of bacteremia (< 10%), possibly explaining the relatively low probability of PBC for respiratory infection in our cohort (Fig. 5, Supplementary Fig. 12). Similar to endovascular infection/IE, body temperature and its fluctuation seem to play a minor role in BC results in respiratory infection. Yo et al. [34] showed that pneumonia was among the independent predictors of afebrile bacteremia. We strongly speculate that there is an interaction between immunosuppression and infection source. Compared to other infection sites, respiratory infection, with fungal infections included in the analysis, is more likely to arise in the context of immunosuppression, thereby blunting the febrile response.

A number of limitations need to be considered. First, determining the infection source is difficult in some BSI episodes. We analyzed IE /endovascular infection as a whole, although there is biological continuity between catheter-related bacteremia and IE, they follow different fever patterns, which may mask the observed temperature-positivity relationship in this subgroup. Second, we calculated previous antibiotic and antipyretic exposure as aggregated doses rather than keeping them dichotomous, which improved validity, it failed to capture the heterogeneity of spectrum, route, or susceptibility of various regimens. Third, CCI was used as a surrogate for chronic health burden, but it is not necessarily parallel with acute severity; therefore, unmeasured confounding still exists. Finally, this study was designed for causal effect estimation, intended to deepen understanding of the heterogeneity in febrile responses so that findings cannot be translated directly into a clinical prediction rule.

Conclusion

This study highlights that both extremely low and high body temperatures, as well as minimal temperature fluctuations, are associated with increased risk of positive blood cultures. In practice, aside from being febrile, it is important to consider temperature patterns, combined with clinical factors such as immunosuppression, infection site, and antibiotic or antipyretic exposure when considering BC collection.
