Exploring differences in mindfulness and mindful eating between adults with varying weight status
Authors: Christian E. Preissner, Dennis de Ruijter, Hein de Vries, Anke Oenema
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMindfulness is increasingly being investigated as a relevant factor for weight management. It is currently unclear how and which underlying dispositional mindfulness and eating behavior-specific mindfulness processes are related to body weight. The purpose of this study was to investigate how dispositional mindfulness and mindful eating facets differ between adults with lower weight, overweight, and obesity.MethodsDutch adults (N = 1118; Mage = 52.1) completed a cross-sectional assessment of dispositional mindfulness, mindful eating, and body mass index (BMI). Multivariate analyses were used to examine differences between BMI groups (i.e., participants with lower weight, overweight, and obesity) on dispositional mindfulness and mindful eating facets, controlling for demographics and meditation experience.ResultsThere were no differences between the BMI groups in dispositional mindfulness facets. In terms of mindful eating, participants with obesity showed a heightened awareness of the effects of environmental cues on their eating behavior, and a lower capacity to refrain from reactive responses and judgment toward their eating, in comparison to participants with a lower weight.ConclusionResults suggest that behavior-specific mindful eating may be a relevant factor to address to promote healthy weight management in the general, non-treatment-seeking population. These explorative findings need to be replicated and explored further through studies utilizing more robust, longitudinal research designs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40519-026-01848-2.

Differences in mindfulness and mindful eating facets between adults with varying weight status

Overweight and obesity are major public health concerns that are associated with an increased risk of developing serious health problems such as type 2 diabetes, heart disease, and stroke [1]. Despite action plans to reduce the burden of non-communicable diseases associated with excessive weight, rates of overweight and obesity have been steadily increasing [2]. In the European Union, about 60% of adults are living with overweight or obesity [2]. In 2021, half of the Dutch population aged 18 years and over were reported to be overweight [3]. Continuing efforts in health promotion research are needed to investigate new potential intervention targets aimed at preventing weight gain and promoting healthy weight maintenance. Increasingly, mindfulness is being investigated as such a target point [4–8].

Mindfulness (a non-judgmental and non-reactive awareness of the inner body and environmental experiences; Preissner et al. [9]1) has gained attention in health promotion as a potential determinant and strategy for healthy weight management in both treatment-seeking and non-treatment-seeking populations [5, 6, 8, 11, 12]. In the field of health and health-related behaviors, different forms of mindfulness are distinguished, including, i.e., dispositional mindfulness and behavior-specific mindfulness [9]. Dispositional mindfulness (an inherent level of mindfulness that differs between individuals and across conditions and time; Sala et al. [13] is a general skill that a person can learn to apply to multiple facets of their life, including food-related environments. Individuals with greater dispositional mindfulness may be more conscious of their momentary eating experiences (e.g., paying attention to their hunger and satiety, rather than eating while distracted or without awareness of inner and environmental cues to eat). This might allow a person to, e.g., consume smaller portion sizes rather than overeating. Behavior-specific mindfulness is mindfulness specifically directed at a behavior, such as eating. This has also been termed “mindful eating behavior” (ME) [14], a non-judgmental and non-reactive awareness of inner body (e.g., physical and emotional) and external (i.e., environmental) sensations while eating or in a food-related environment [15]. The underlying processes of ME include: (i) awareness of the sensory properties of food; (ii) awareness of homeostatic cues of hunger and satiety; (iii) awareness of triggers to eat; and (iv) fostering a non-judgmental and non-reactive awareness in experiences related to food and eating [16]. Such ME processes may promote: (a) the attentiveness to a person’s current eating habits; (b) their awareness of external cues to eat (i.e., cues outside of the individual’s homeostatic hunger and satiety); and (c) decreasing an individual’s reactivity to negative thoughts and emotions when attempting to change unhealthy eating habits [17].

Deriving from the potential benefits of mindfulness on dysregulated eating behaviors (e.g., binge eating, emotional eating, external eating) [5], several studies have investigated the effects of mindfulness-based interventions on eating behaviors and weight status [7, 18, 19]. Reviews of such interventions have shown promise in lowering binge eating, emotional eating, and body weight among individuals with overweight and obesity [6, 7, 18]. Research investigating mindfulness as a determinant of weight management in the general population found individuals with greater dispositional mindfulness to be more mindful eaters and that ME may mediate the negative association between dispositional mindfulness and serving size [20]. Three cross-sectional studies suggested that a lower body mass index (BMI) was associated with greater ME [21–23]. Prior research has also shown both participants with underweight and those with obesity to report less ME behavior than those with a “healthy”2 BMI [24]. However, in two studies among college students, Anderson et al. [25] and Taylor et al. [26] found no significant correlation between ME and BMI. In addition, in a preliminary study in a general population, Carriére et al. [15] found a higher body weight to be associated with greater levels of ME-related awareness and lower levels of non-reactance and non-judgment. In contrast, Demirbas et al. [27] reported higher attentional scores for women with a lower weight in contrast to those living with obesity. In sum, the previous studies provide initial evidence that dispositional mindfulness and ME might be related to weight status and obesity-related behaviors, making these factors potential determinants for weight management. However, considering the lack of consistent findings and of research separately investigating the underlying mindfulness processes (i.e., awareness, non-judgment, non-reactance), further evidence is needed regarding the relationship between mindfulness, ME processes, and weight status.

This study aims to investigate whether and which mindfulness processes might be associated with weight status by investigating differences in dispositional mindfulness and ME processes between people with a lower body weight, overweight, and obesity. In line with preliminary research distinguishing between awareness and non-evaluative processes (i.e., non-reactance and non-judgment) of ME [15], we expected individuals with overweight or obesity versus those with lower body weight to score: (i) higher on dispositional and behavior-specific awareness (e.g., internal and external cues to eat) and (ii) lower on dispositional and behavior-specific non-evaluative processes.

Methods

This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study received ethical approval from the institutional review ethics board (FHML-REC/2022/129). This paper reports on cross-sectional analyses described as exploratory analyses in the protocol, available in Open Science Framework (OSF), corresponding to the overarching project: https://osf.io/uqazb.

Participants and recruitment

A representative sample of Dutch-speaking adults regarding gender, education, and province was collected from the members of an online research panel (Flycatcher). In total, 2614 individuals were invited to participate, with 1260 responding (response percentage 48.5%) to the survey. As we aimed to study mindfulness in a non-treatment-seeking population, we excluded individuals with severe neurodegenerative diseases (e.g., Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease) as well as severe mental health conditions (e.g., major depression), and those currently undergoing treatment for a diagnosed eating disorder (e.g., bulimia nervosa). These conditions were noted as exclusion criteria in the informed consent form and assessed via self-report at the beginning of the questionnaire. Of the 1260 participants responding to the survey, 1118 provided informed consent and met the eligibility criteria of the study. Participants were reimbursed with a ticket for a Flycatcher-internal lottery and points that can be exchanged for gift vouchers.

Measures

We assessed demographic variables, including age, gender, the highest completed level of formal education (including no education, primary and secondary school, Dutch levels of high school, vocational training, and higher professional education at university; recoded to low, medium, and high), employment status (employed, self-employed, temp or on-call employment, retired or unemployed), formal meditation experience (yes/no), and current engagement in meditation practice (“yes, regularly”, “yes, irregularly”, “no”). Height (in meters) and weight (in kilograms) were assessed to permit the calculation of a BMI score. Weight categories were created based on recommended cutoffs (< 18.5 for having underweight, 18.5 to < 25 indicating a lower body weight, 25 to < 30 for having overweight and > 30 for having obesity; [28]. Further, participants were asked to indicate whether they had maintained their current body weight (± 3 kg) over the past year. Participants were grouped into maintainers (‘yes’) and non-maintainers (‘no’, having gained or lost ± 3 kg over the past year).

Dispositional mindfulness

Dispositional mindfulness was assessed using the Dutch short-form Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire [29, 30], an 18-item self-report questionnaire that examines five dispositional mindfulness dimensions: observing (3 items, α = 0.67), describing (5 items, α = 0.82), acting with awareness (3 items, α = 0.71), non-judging of inner experience (3 items, α = 0.55), and non-reactivity to inner experience (4 items, α = 0.66). Participants were asked to indicate their agreement with each statement on a scale of 1 (never or very seldom true) to 5 (very often or always true). Negatively worded items were reverse scored before calculating subscale scores. Subscale sum scores were converted from ordinal to interval-level scores in line with Medvedev et al. [30] for the 18-item short-form to increase the accuracy of measurement. In line with the processes described in the underlying operational definition of mindfulness (i.e., relating to one’s internal and external awareness with a non-judgmental and non-reactive attitude), the separate subscales were used in the analyses. The Dutch items can be found in Online Resource 1.

Mindful eating

To assess ME, we utilized a Dutch version of the validated 29-item Four Facet Mindful Eating Scale (FFaMES; Carrière et al. [15]). The Dutch version of the FFaMES was developed in line with recommended forward-and back-translation procedures by independent, bilingual translators [31]. The FFaMES measures two awareness processes, external awareness (the ability to observe the effects of environmental factors on one’s eating; 6 items, α = 0.84) and internal awareness (the ability to observe the effects of internal processes on one’s eating; 6 items, α = 0.90), as well as two underlying non-reactive processes, non-reactance (the ability to maintain a mental distance from immediate eating needs; 9 items, α = 0.94) and non-judgment (the ability to accept one’s eating behaviors without negative self-judgment; 8 items, α = 0.92). Items are scored on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (never) to 5 (very often). Non-reactance and non-judgment items were reverse-coded, with higher scores indicating a greater ability to act in a non-evaluative manner. Composite scores for mindful eating facets were calculated based on the mean of responses to the corresponding subscale items. In line with the scale authors’ recommendations (Carrière et al. [15]), we included the separate subscales in the analyses. The Dutch items can be found in Online Resource 2.

Analysis plan

Preliminary analyses and bivariate correlations were conducted using SPSS v. 28.0. Outliers on mindfulness and ME were identified and removed from subsequent analyses using Mahalanobis Distance (n = 12). Missing values were below 1% for all variables, with the exception of height and weight (9.0%), as participants could skip sensitive items. Information on BMI was available for 1054 participants. To assess differences in demographic variables between the BMI groups, one-way ANOVAs and Chi-square tests were performed. To examine the association between study variables, we ran bivariate correlations for BMI, dispositional mindfulness, and ME. Next, we conducted two MANCOVAs to examine potential differences between the BMI groups on dispositional mindfulness (FFMQ) facets and ME (FFaMES) facets, controlling for significantly different demographic variables and meditation-related variables between the groups [32]. Univariate ANCOVAs and Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons were conducted for significant FFMQ or FFaMES constructs.

Results

Participant characteristics

The sample consisted of 1118 healthy, predominantly middle-aged participants with a medium level of education and low meditation experience. About half of the participants identified as male. The majority of participants had maintained their body weight over the past year. Fifteen individuals were classified as being in the category of underweight (1.42%); the majority (n = 474; 44.97%) had a lower body weight. Of the remaining participants, 377 (35.77%) were classified as having overweight and 151 (14.32%) as having obesity. The weight groups significantly differed on all socio-demographic variables, with individuals with overweight and obesity being of higher age, indicating the least full-time employment, meditation experience, meditation engagement, and weight maintenance. Results of the first MANCOVA indicated no statistically significant difference between the BMI groups on the dispositional mindfulness facets after controlling for gender, age, education, meditation experience, and meditation engagement, F(15, 2976) = 1.46, p = 0.110, Pillai’s Trace = 0.022, η2 = 0.007).4 In contrast, the second MANCOVA showed a significant difference in the ME facets between the BMI groups after controlling for gender, age, education, as well as meditation-related variables, F(12, 2979) = 6.89, p < 0.001, Pillai’s Trace = 0.081, η2 = 0.027). Univariate ANCOVAs with Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons indicated a significant difference in BMI groups on external awareness (F(3, 994) = 5.99, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.018). Participants with a lower body weight and overweight had lower external awareness than those with obesity, yet only those with overweight scored significantly lower than those with obesity (p = 0.002). The BMI groups did not differ in their internal awareness (F(3, 994) = 0.918, p = 0.432, η2 = 0.003). In terms of non-evaluative processes, BMI groups significantly differed in their non-judgment (F(3, 994) = 14.38, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.042). Participants with a lower weight showed a significantly higher ability to non-judge their eating as opposed to those with overweight (p < 0.001) and obesity (p < 0.001). Participants with overweight showed a greater ability to non-judge than those with obesity (p = 0.031). Lastly, BMI groups differed significantly in their non-reactance (F(3, 994) = 20.41, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.058). Specifically, participants with a lower body weight showed significantly higher ability to non-react than those with overweight (p < 0.001) and obesity (p < 0.001). Participants with overweight showed higher non-reactance scores than those with obesity (p < 0.001). In general, effect sizes for all comparisons are small.

Discussion

This study explored potential differences in dispositional mindfulness facets and behavior-specific ME among individuals with diverse weight statuses. No significant differences were observed between the BMI groups concerning dispositional mindfulness. However, individuals with obesity showed significantly greater eating-specific external awareness and both lower non-judgment and non-reactance when compared to individuals with lower BMI.

Our first finding with regard to no differences in dispositional mindfulness adds to the mixed findings in the literature. To date, there is no solid evidence regarding the relationship between mindfulness and weight [4]. For example, whereas a general population-based study reported all FFMQ subscales to be associated with weight status [33], other research in people with diabetes found neither total FFMQ scores nor the individual subscales to predict changes in body weight [34]. A first methodological interpretation is that no differences between the groups were detected because of generally low scores in dispositional mindfulness and little variation between the BMI groups. If there is little variation in dispositional mindfulness scores, it becomes challenging to discern meaningful distinctions between the BMI groups. The finding that dispositional mindfulness was low may be due to the low level of prior and current meditation engagement in our sample and, in turn, a lack of full comprehension of mindfulness due to limited practice exposure. As noted by van Dam [32], greater familiarity with mindfulness may influence perceptions of its value and, in turn, influence responses on self-report mindfulness questionnaires [35]. While this raises a broader methodological question as to whether self-report mindfulness measures demonstrate sufficient validity in mindfulness-naïve populations, a thorough examination of measurement invariance in experienced and non-experienced samples was beyond the scope of the present paper.

A second interpretation of our findings is that dispositional mindfulness may have limited relevance for weight status in the general population. Further longitudinal research is needed to establish the role of dispositional mindfulness as a determinant of weight management in non-treatment-seeking adults. Additionally, it should also be noted that our study suggests a weakness in the internal consistency of several facets of the 18-item short-form FFMQ [36]. As the low reliability may have introduced noise or variability into the measurement, it is difficult to distinguish true differences from random fluctuations or measurement error. This may have limited the detection of potential differences between BMI groups and dispositional mindfulness processes.

Our second finding showed the expected differences between BMI groups and behavior-specific ME facets. We found a higher BMI to be associated with higher external awareness and lower scores on non-evaluative processes. With respect to awareness processes, individuals with obesity showed a greater ability to observe how external (i.e., environmental) cues influence their eating behavior. On the one hand, this may indicate heightened cue-reactivity, meaning that individuals are more sensitive to environmental food cues, which may increase vulnerability to overeating [37]. On the other hand, it may also suggest an enhanced capacity to recognize and reflect upon the impact of environmental food cues on one’s behavior. Higher external awareness on its own is therefore neither good nor bad. Rather, its functionality likely depends on the broader self-regulatory context, including the individual’s non-evaluative capacities (in other words, a synergy between mindful eating constructs; [37]).

This study demonstrated that individuals with obesity also showed a lower ability to maintain a mental distance from impulsive reactions to eating and to avoid negative self-judgment regarding their eating behavior. This finding is in line with prior research demonstrating a significant difference in ME between individuals with lower body weight and those with obesity [15, 21, 23, 24]. As indicated by our differing findings per ME facet, although a person may be aware of the thoughts, emotions, or physical sensations that accompany their eating, they may be unable to successfully decenter from habitual patterns of reaction and negative self-judgment [38, 39]. This difficulty in maintaining a mental distance from feelings of distress or self-judgment has been suggested to be particularly problematic for people (of any weight status) who struggle with emotional eating or binge eating, as it may increase their risk of compulsive overeating [37, 40, 41]. Taken together, present-moment awareness may not be sufficient on its own to reduce dysregulated eating behaviors [16, 37]. Heightened awareness without corresponding non-evaluative skills may even amplify distress or reactivity if individuals become more conscious of cravings or external food cues without possessing the regulatory skills to relate to them adaptively [37]. The results of this study suggest that teaching non-evaluative ME skills in combination with addressing heightened external awareness may have some merit for weight management in the general, non-treatment-seeking population. To encourage healthy weight management, future research on mindful eating should continue to separately investigate and apply both awareness and non-evaluative processes that underlie behavior-specific mindfulness [37]. In addition, studies should explicitly investigate: (i) the potential overlap and distinction of ME with dysregulated eating behaviors such as emotional eating to clarify shared and unique mechanisms [42] and (ii) the potential bidirectionality of these associations.

In sum, this study aimed to investigate whether and which dispositional and behavior-specific mindfulness processes might be associated with weight status. The lack of findings on dispositional mindfulness and the identified differences in ME facets emphasize the need to delve further into investigating the role of behavior-specific mindfulness processes instead of dispositional mindfulness, as ME might be a more proximal factor associated with eating behaviors and weight management [20]. Our findings indicate that there may be variations in behavior-specific ME across different BMI groups, such that individuals with higher body weight score lower in non-evaluative abilities and higher in external awareness. Despite these significant differences, correlations and group comparisons for ME demonstrated small effect sizes in our general population sample. Therefore, the present findings are a first step and need to be replicated and explored further through studies utilizing more robust research designs to arrive at implications for practice. Studies are needed to investigate the longitudinal development of specific ME skills in relation to eating behaviors and weight status to determine causal relationships between these concepts.

Strengths and limitations

This study was based on a large, representative sample from the general Dutch population. Notably, our study adds to the existing literature by addressing limitations seen in prior research, such as calculating a total mindfulness score, overlooking the underlying mindfulness processes, and excluding non-evaluative mindfulness features [15, 16]. Through the utilization of specific subscales of dispositional mindfulness and ME, we gained detailed insight into the differing mindfulness facets among diverse BMI groups. As can be seen from the bivariate correlations among mindful eating facets, the inverse relationship between awareness and non-evaluative skills demonstrates the necessity of future research incorporating and measuring the separate underlying processes to better understand their role as a potential determinant and strategy of health-related outcomes. Despite these strengths, we recognize several limitations of the present study.

First, the chosen research design possesses certain limitations with respect to our objective of investigating mindfulness as a potential determinant of weight status. As this study was based on cross-sectional evidence, we were unable to investigate a temporal relationship between mindfulness and BMI and establish causality of mindfulness mechanisms. Although our study offers initial insights into potential associations, we recommend longitudinal studies to further explore this relationship. Second, our study had the limitation of directly associating mindfulness with weight status without considering the influence of weight-related dietary and physical activity behaviors. Future studies should longitudinally investigate the link between underlying ME facets and health behavioral outcomes in the general population. Such a study is currently in progress by our team. Third, as noted, the dispositional non-reactance and non-judgment subscales of the FFMQ exhibited a Cronbach’s alpha lower than anticipated. We utilized the Dutch 18-item short-form version that showed higher reliability than other short forms [30] to mitigate the response burden. It is plausible that these subscale items may not be the most appropriate for a population with limited mindfulness and/or meditation experience. This resulted in the inclusion of separate subscales in our analyses and hence lower statistical power. In addition, we converted the ordinal FFMQ scores to an interval scale, which is common practice but not undisputed. This could have contributed to the finding of no association between dispositional mindfulness and weight status in our study by potentially not capturing the nuances of the different mindfulness skills. Future research on mindfulness should delve into investigating the most suitable questionnaire content for the general population with limited practice experience. Fourth, variables assessed in our study relied on self-report, which may have introduced bias. Where possible, future studies should aim to include more objective measures of sensitive variables such as weight, and of difficult-to-estimate variables such as mindfulness, physical activity or diet. In this context, Ecological Momentary Assessment studies might be valuable to better understand emotional states as predictors of eating and weight-related outcomes [43]. Although it is difficult to model the relationship between all relevant weight-related variables (i.e., environmental factors, social and cultural components, stigma, introspective tendencies), incorporating further inner-individual and environmental factors into future work can refine our understanding of weight and eating regulation. Fifth, our scope was limited to the application of mindfulness in non-treatment-seeking populations. Results may differ in treatment-seeking samples and intervention settings such as Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), where structured skills training may influence mindfulness-related processes. Future research should therefore examine whether the relationships identified in the present study generalize to clinical and treatment contexts.

Conclusion

Participants from the general population with lower weight, overweight, or obesity did not differ in dispositional mindfulness processes. However, individuals with obesity showed a heightened awareness of external factors regarding their eating behavior and a lower capacity to refrain from reactive responses and judgment towards their eating-related experiences. The findings from our cross-sectional study suggest some association between a higher body weight and a lower ability to respond to eating-related experiences with a non-reactive and non-judgmental attitude. These results offer explorative insights about the potential of mindful eating training as a strategy for weight management in the general, non-treatment-seeking population. However, further longitudinal investigations are needed to: (i) explore the role of dispositional versus behavior-specific mindfulness processes as determinants and strategies of weight management and (ii) separately investigate awareness and non-evaluative mindfulness processes to determine if and which essential mindfulness skills are responsible for changes in eating behaviors and weight status over time.
