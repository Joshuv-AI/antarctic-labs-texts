Unusually slow progression of a high-grade intramedullary oligodendroglioma in a dog: serial magnetic resonance imaging findings and clinical course over two years
Authors: Robert Wise, Lillian R Miller, Molly E Church, Wilfried Mai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 7.5-year-old spayed female French Bulldog presented for back pain that did not respond to conservative management after the dog had jumped off a couch. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) identified an intramedullary mass that was T2 hyperintense, T1 iso- to hypointense, short tau inversion recovery (STIR) hyperintense, strongly rim-enhancing, and centrally suppressed on post-contrast T2-fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images. Despite a suspected glial cell neoplasm, clinical signs initially improved with palliative treatment. Follow-up MRI 2 years later showed progressive enlargement of the lesion, with newly noted multiseptated regions. Histopathology indicated a high-grade oligodendroglioma.Keywords: cystic neoplasm, glial cell neoplasia, intramedullary neoplasia, spinal glioma

Signalment, history, and clinical findings

A 7.5-year-old spayed female French Bulldog initially presented for back pain after jumping off a couch but failed initial conservative management that included carprofen, methocarbamol, and gabapentin.

The dog was normal on physical examination and had a history of bilateral total ear canal ablations related to chronic ear infections. Neurologic examination identified markedly delayed hopping and proprioceptive positioning in the right pelvic limb, and absence of hopping and proprioceptive positioning in the left pelvic limb, consistent with a T3-L3 myelopathy. A CBC and serum biochemistry panel identified mild neutropenia, decreased serum creatinine concentration, increased aspartate aminotransferase activity, and increased serum albumin concentration.

Thoracic and spinal radiographs identified moderate L7-S1 and mild T12-T13 and T13-L1 intervertebral disc disease, as well as multifocal thoracic and thoracolumbar vertebral anomalies (ie, hemivertebrae). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was recommended for further evaluation of the T3-L3 myelopathy.

Imaging, diagnosis, and outcome

An MRI study of the thoracolumbar spine (T3-L3) was obtained using a 1.5 T scanner (Signa Explorer, GE Healthcare) with the patient in dorsal recumbency using a 6-channel spine cervical-thoracic-lumbar coil. Sequences included transverse plane pre- and post-contrast T1-weighted (T1W), T2-weighted (T2W) fast spin echo (FSE), and post-contrast T2W fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (T2-FLAIR); sagittal plane T2W FSE, short tau inversion recovery (STIR), and single-shot fast spin echo (SSFSE) myelogram.

An ovoid, slightly left-sided intramedullary mass occupied the majority of the spinal cord at the level of T13-L1 (Figures 1 and 2). The mass was T2 hyperintense, T1 iso- to hypointense, STIR hyperintense, strongly rim-enhancing, and centrally suppressed on post-contrast FLAIR images. The spinal cord was swollen, resulting in attenuation of the ventral and dorsal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) signals on the 3-dimensional myelogram series. Mild dilatation of the central canal and intramedullary T2 hyperintensity extended cranially and caudally from the mass, consistent with perilesional edema. Additional minor findings included multifocal non-compressive intervertebral disc protrusion at T9-T10, T10-T11, L2-L3, L3-L4, and L4-L5, as well as congenital vertebral anomalies (ie, hemivertebrae in the mid-thoracic spine with secondary focal kyphosis at T9-T11).

Given the imaging findings, the primary differential for the spinal cord lesion was intramedullary neoplasia with secondary perilesional edema and syringomyelia. A glioma was considered most likely, considering the ring enhancement and lateralized location, rather than other intramedullary neoplasms such as ependymoma, lymphoma, or nephroblastoma. Non-neoplastic diseases, such as granulomatous meningomyelitis (GME) or infectious myelitis or abscess, were considered less likely.

Cerebrospinal fluid was collected, and cytologic interpretation was consistent with moderate mononuclear lymphocytic pleocytosis. Based on these results, differential diagnosis also included viral, bacterial, fungal, protozoal, parasitic, or rickettsial infections; necrotizing encephalitis; granulomatous meningoencephalitis (GME); corticosteroid-responsive encephalitis; neoplasia; trauma and degenerative conditions. No infectious agents were identified. Abdominal ultrasonography was performed because of the concern for neoplasia, and was normal. Palliative care was elected with conservative treatment including gabapentin, prednisone, and omeprazole.

Over the next 2 years, palliative treatment for the neurologic signs was continued along with medical management of lower urinary tract infections. The neurologic signs varied throughout this time period. Initial improvement occurred 2 months after the initial MRI, and the dog was reported to be fully ambulatory, had regained lower motor neuron function of the urinary bladder, and had only residual absence of cutaneous trunci reflex at L1-L2 bilaterally. The dog was repeatedly evaluated for recurrent lower urinary tract infections and cystolithiasis, with several positive urine cultures for Proteus mirabilis and a single positive culture for Enterococcus faecalis. The dog responded to antibiotics (ie, amoxicillin-clavulanate, amoxicillin) and a dissolution diet (Royal Canin Urinary S/O). Neurologic signs recurred intermittently and were managed using long-term administration of prednisone and short-term treatments with cytarabine, gabapentin, and cyclosporine. Tapering of prednisone was attempted at multiple time points (10 mg per 24 h initially to 5 mg per 24 h, and then finally to 2.5 mg per 24 h), but it was never completely discontinued.

Over the 2 years, multiple additional diagnostic tests were performed, including CBCs, serum biochemistry panels, urinalyses, urine cultures, thoracic radiographs, and abdominal ultrasonography. Complete blood count abnormalities included mild lymphopenia, eosinopenia, and thrombocytopenia. Serum biochemistry abnormalities included slightly increased serum glucose, low creatinine, low phosphorus, low globulin, and increased albumin concentrations as well as increased alkaline phosphatase and alanine aminotransferase activity. Chloride concentration was also periodically low. Serial thoracic radiographs showed no evidence of metastatic neoplasia, and multifocal thoracolumbar congenital, breed-associated vertebral anomalies were stable with concurrent degenerative intervertebral disc disease also noted. Abdominal ultrasonography identified recurrent cystitis with bladder calculi that resolved after feeding a dissolution diet. A right-sided adrenal nodule was also noted.

After 3 months, neurologic signs progressed, and the patient became nonambulatory paraparetic. A repeat MRI was performed. Sequences included transverse plane T1W and T2W FSE, T2W FLAIR; sagittal plane T2W FSE, STIR, and SSFSE; and post-contrast transverse T1W with fat saturation.

On repeat MRI (Figures 3 and 4), the intramedullary mass was larger (4.1 cm length, previously 2.2 cm length), extending from T12 to L1, and occupying the majority of the spinal cord’s diameter. The mass was heterogeneously T2 hyperintense (but less hyperintense than on the initial MRI) and with isointense regions, T1 iso- to hypointense, and homogeneously STIR hyperintense. On T2-FLAIR images, the previous suppression of the hyperintense signal seen on T2W FSE images was not as pronounced as on the first MRI. On post-contrast series, a strongly contrast-enhancing rim was noted, with a small caudal portion of the mass slightly but homogeneously enhancing with newly noted areas of enhancing internal septations. Secondary findings (including spinal cord swelling, perilesional hyperintensity, and syringomyelia) also were progressive.

Given the progressive imaging features of the intramedullary mass, neoplasia remained the primary consideration (slow-growing glioma or ependymoma). Non-neoplastic causes (eg, GME) were considered less likely. Other minor findings included multifocal thoracolumbar degenerative intervertebral disc disease and marked focal muscle atrophy of the hypaxial and epaxial musculature.

Palliative treatment was continued using cyclosporine and prednisone. The dog’s neurologic signs continued to worsen at home, and the dog was euthanized 2 weeks after the follow-up MRI.

On necropsy, a 5.3 cm segment of spinal cord, extending from approximately T11 to L1, was swollen and slightly softer than the surrounding parenchyma. The spinal cord in this region was mottled light gray to blue with prominent meningeal vessels over its dorsal aspect (Figure 5A). Histologically, the mass consisted of sheets and packets of neoplastic oligodendrocytes that effaced the central gray matter, compressed and invaded the white matter, and focally extended into the subarachnoid space. Within the center of the mass were multiple broad foci of necrosis (Figure 5B). Neoplastic cells had distinct cell borders, abundant lightly amphophilic to clear cytoplasm, and round, hyperchromatic nuclei (Figure 5C). Scattered karyomegalic cells were present, and mitotic figures were rarely observed. Throughout the neoplasm, tortuous vascular channels lined by reactive endothelial cells were observed, consistent with microvascular proliferation. In the surrounding parenchyma, myelin sheaths were frequently dilated and contained myelinomacrophages. Similar degeneration was observed within the sacral nerves and in the dorsal and dorsolateral funiculi of the cranial segments of the spinal cord. Immunohistochemistry for oligodendrocyte transcription factor 2 (OLIG2) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) was performed on sections of the spinal cord expanded by the neoplasm. More than 90% of the examined neoplastic cells expressed strong nuclear immunoreactivity for OLIG2 (Figure 5D). Approximately 60% of the neoplastic cells demonstrated moderate to strong cytoplasmic immunoreactivity for GFAP. Evidence of necrosis, mitotic activity, cellular pleomorphism, and microvascular proliferation satisfied the criteria for high-grade glioma.1 These findings, in conjunction with immunohistochemical reactivity, supported the diagnosis of high-grade oligodendroglioma.1,2 The changes in the surrounding spinal cord parenchyma were consistent with white matter degeneration and attributed to compression from the adjacent neoplasm.

Discussion

Our case demonstrates that high-grade spinal gliomas may unexpectedly exhibit indolent clinical behavior, with implications for clinical decision-making.

Intramedullary neoplasms of the spinal cord are relatively rare in dogs, representing up to 16% of spinal cord neoplasms when compared with extradural (61%) and intradural-extramedullary (23%) neoplasms.3 Among intramedullary neoplasms in dogs, the most commonly reported are ependymomas, followed by other glial cell neoplasms, with astrocytomas being more common than oligodendrogliomas.3,4

Gliomas are common neoplasms of the central nervous system (CNS) in dogs, particularly in Boxers and other brachycephalic breeds.5 Their MRI appearance has been more thoroughly documented for intracranial tumors. Intracranial oligodendrogliomas are more common in the cerebrum and thalamus and are often closely associated with the lateral ventricles or the cerebral surface.6,7 In contrast, astrocytomas are more commonly located in the caudal fossa.6,7 The overall MRI characteristics of oligodendrogliomas and astrocytomas overlap substantially. However, certain characteristics are suggestive of oligodendroglioma, such as pre-contrast T1 hypointensity or contact with the lateral ventricles.5,6 Recently, the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign was reported to be a potential MRI biomarker of intracranial oligodendroglioma.8 It is characterized by a homogeneous hyperintense signal on T2W images and a hypointense signal with a hyperintense peripheral rim on T2-FLAIR images.8 Certain signal characteristics may be more common with high-grade glial cell tumors, such as the presence of cystic regions, admixed signal voids, intratumoral hemorrhage, contrast enhancement, ring enhancement, necrosis, and ventricular contact.5–7,9 Other CNS diseases also have been reported to mimic intracranial gliomas, including focal GME, cerebrovascular accidents, and abscesses.10–12

Glial cell neoplasia of the spinal cord is uncommon in dogs, typically affecting older dogs and the cervical or thoracic region.4 Among glial cell neoplasms, astrocytomas are more common than oligodendrogliomas in dogs and humans.3,13,14 Oligodendrogliomas can present as solitary spinal cord lesions or multifocal lesions, such as mucinous oligodendrogliomas and meningothelial oligodendrogliomatosis.3,4,15,16 The MRI features of intramedullary gliomas in dogs are less well described than their intracranial counterparts, but, similar to their presentation in humans, they are usually well-marginated, eccentrically located, round or ovoid masses that are T1 iso- to hypointense, T2 iso- to hyperintense relative to gray matter, and accompanied by variable degrees of perilesional edema and enhancement (either homogeneous enhancement or ring enhancement).3,13,14 Differential diagnoses for intramedullary gliomas in dogs include other glial cell neoplasms (eg, ependymoma) and non-neoplastic lesions (eg, GME or intramedullary cysts, such as neuroenteric or epidermoid cysts). Other neoplasms also have been reported, but more commonly in human medicine, such as hemangioblastomas.14,17–20

In our case, a glioma initially was suspected because of the MRI features of an intramedullary mass with a well-defined contrast-enhancing rim and lateralized location. Other differential diagnoses, such as ependymoma, also were considered possible, because these have been reported occasionally to contain cystic components (T2-hyperintense, T1-hypointense, and T2-FLAIR-suppressing). Other features, however, were not supportive of ependymoma, such as the eccentric location of the mass in the spinal cord (although lateralized location also has been reported with these tumors18) and lack of T2 hypointense hemosiderin caps.13,14

Although they could have represented central cystic changes, the MRI features on the initial examination (ie, marked diffuse T2-hyperintensity and central T2-FLAIR suppression with a hyperintense rim) were suggestive of the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign recently reported in intracranial oligodendrogliomas in dogs.8 Additional studies are needed to determine whether this sign could also serve as a biomarker of spinal cord oligodendrogliomas in dogs.

Because the clinical signs initially improved and stabilized for up to 2 years, the initial suspicion of glioma was reconsidered, and the condition was thought to represent focal GME.

The mean survival time (MST) of dogs with glial cell neoplasms is variable, and the MST of dogs with astrocytomas has been reported to be longer than that of dogs with oligodendrogliomas.21 However, because of the lack of comprehensive estimates for these tumor types and variability in the timing of diagnosis and in the delivery of standardized care, current data on MST in dogs with gliomas remain highly variable.7 A survival time of 1155 days was reported in a Yorkshire Terrier with a low-grade astrocytoma22 and a survival time of 1505 days was reported in a 9-month-old Cavalier King Charles Spaniel with a high-grade oligodendroglioma,7 both of which were treated palliatively. Our case appears to be unique in that no such survival times have been reported in dogs with intramedullary glial cell tumors receiving only palliative treatment.

Spinal cord biopsy is a valuable diagnostic procedure in human medicine for patients with progressive symptomatic lesions23; but biopsy is not commonly performed in veterinary medicine because of its high cost, technical requirements, and potential complications, particularly for intramedullary lesions. In addition, given the potential cystic component of the lesion in our case (as indicated by MRI signal characteristics), with a potential for low biopsy sample yield, biopsy was not attempted. Therefore, confirmation of the exact nature of the lesion at the time of the first MRI scan was not possible. Histopathology performed at necropsy 2 years after the initial presentation identified a high-grade oligodendroglioma. It is unlikely that a dog with high-grade oligodendroglioma would have remained stable for such an extended time period. Thus, the lesion potentially could have initially been a low-grade oligodendroglioma that eventually progressed to high-grade, as has been suggested in a previous case.7 In humans, up to 70% of diffuse low-grade gliomas have been reported to progress to high-grade gliomas within a decade.24 Alternatively, the oligodendroglioma may have arisen from an initially cystic lesion, as has been postulated in human medicine, such as in cases of posttraumatic oligodendrogliomas, although this correlation previously was considered indirect.25 There was no historical or histopathological evidence of a prior traumatic event in our case. If the initial presentation were that of an oligodendroglioma, it would represent the longest reported survival time for a dog with a spinal cord oligodendroglioma treated palliatively, which emphasizes the potentially indolent behavior of these tumors.

In conclusion, we demonstrated an extremely long survival time for a dog with high-grade oligodendroglioma. This case emphasizes the difficulty in properly diagnosing spinal cord mass lesions based solely on MRI and CSF analysis, and the importance of biopsy to select appropriate treatment. This case also emphasizes the usefulness of MRI follow-ups at short intervals, without spinal cord biopsy, to assess spinal cord lesions better and determine the most appropriate clinical management.
