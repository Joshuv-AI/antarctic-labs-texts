Clinical characteristics and histopathologic features of movable inflammatory laryngeal masses in five dogs
Authors: Aki FUJIWARA-IGARASHI, Sho GOTO, Yuta NAKAZAWA, Takafumi OHSHIMA, Haruyoshi TETSU, Kenichi INABA, Yukino MACHIDA, Masaki MICHISHITA
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInformation on inflammatory laryngeal masses in dogs remains extremely limited. We aimed to describe the clinical and histopathological features and outcomes of five dogs with bilateral, movable inflammatory laryngeal masses. Stridor was a common clinical sign, followed by dysphonia and snoring, all of which were mild. The presence of movable masses was confirmed in all dogs through fluoroscopic or echolaryngographic evaluation. Laryngeal endoscopy identified two masses in each dog, consistent with everted laryngeal saccules. Surgical excision was performed in all dogs, and no recurrence was observed during a median follow-up of 13 months. Histopathological analysis revealed chronic laryngitis characterized by fibrosis, lymphoplasmacytic infiltration, edema, and lymphangiectasia, consistent with findings reported in laryngeal saccules associated with brachycephalic airway syndrome in dogs.Keywords: canine laryngeal mass, inflammatory laryngeal disease, laryngeal saccules, laryngitis, Yorkshire Terrier

Laryngeal masses in dogs and cats arise from both neoplastic and inflammatory conditions. Inflammatory laryngeal diseases and laryngeal tumors occur more frequently in cats than in dogs. In cats, inflammatory laryngeal conditions that can be difficult to distinguish from neoplastic lesions have been documented [4, 8, 9, 11, 14,15,16, 23, 24]. These inflammatory laryngeal masses may cause life-threatening upper airway obstruction; however, favorable outcomes have been reported following appropriate surgical or medical intervention. By contrast, laryngeal masses in dogs—whether neoplastic or inflammation-related—are uncommon. Primary laryngeal tumors in dogs, including squamous cell carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, mast cell tumor, plasmacytoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, chondroma, myxochondroma, malignant melanoma, and lymphoma, have been described primarily in case reports or small case series [1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 10, 15, 17, 18, 20, 21, 26]. Information on inflammatory laryngeal masses in dogs remains limited. In a previous study of dogs with various laryngeal conditions treated by partial laryngectomy, six were diagnosed with proliferative, ulcerative, or granulomatous laryngitis, with lesions that grossly resembled neoplasms. Five dogs showed favorable outcomes following adjunctive prednisolone therapy. By contrast, one dog that did not receive adjunctive medical therapy failed to improve and was subsequently euthanized [9]. A separate case report described a dog with granulomatous laryngitis presenting with dysphonia and gagging, in which a mass originating from the unilateral laryngeal saccule was identified. The condition was successfully managed with ventral laryngotomy and adjunctive prednisolone therapy [19]. Another report described a tumor-like granulomatous mass caused by Leishmania spp. infection in a dog presenting with respiratory distress [25]. In a study involving 15 dogs with infiltrative laryngeal lesions, 13 were diagnosed with inflammatory disease. The identified histopathological types included granulation tissue, neutrophilic, septic neutrophilic, eosinophilic, lymphocytic/plasmacytic, and mixed or unclassified inflammation, with some cases presenting as mass lesions [7]. Among the 13 dogs with inflammatory disease treated surgically or medically, three died during hospitalization, and five of the ten discharged dogs experienced recurrence of respiratory signs. Inflammatory mass lesions were observed on laryngeal examination in nine of these dogs. All but one of these lesions was unilateral; the exception involved bilateral, partially pedunculated masses located caudal to the arytenoid cartilages. A recent case report described inflammatory polyps in the larynx of miniature dachshunds [22]. One dog presented with dyspnea due to a laryngeal inflammatory polyp, which was successfully treated with surgical resection. The present study reports the clinical and histopathological features and outcomes of five dogs with bilateral, movable inflammatory laryngeal masses originating from the laryngeal ventricles, a presentation not previously reported in canine laryngeal disease.

Medical records from the Veterinary Medical Teaching Hospital at Nippon Veterinary and Life Science University and Nagoya Minami Animal Hospital−Animal Respiratory Clinic were reviewed for the period 2021–2023. Five dogs with laryngeal masses associated with inflammatory laryngitis were included in this study. The clinical features and outcomes for each dog are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 11 years (range, 7–16), and the median body weight was 3.50 kg (range, 2.85–5.38). Three of the five dogs were Yorkshire Terriers, two of which were related as parent and offspring. At the time of initial referral, clinical signs included stridor (n=4), dysphonia (n=3), snoring (n=3), cough (n=2), and retching (n=1). These signs were mild and intermittent in all dogs. The median duration of clinical signs prior to presentation was 30 days (range, 3–1,095). One dog had an elevated C-reactive protein level (5.2 mg/dL), while no significant abnormalities were detected in the other four dogs.

Lateral cervical radiography and fluoroscopy were performed in all dogs, revealing a soft tissue density within the laryngeal lumen, consistent with a displaceable laryngeal mass that shifted cranially during expiration (Figs. 1 and 2). The median maximum diameter of the masses was 1.7 cm (range: 1.3–2.0 cm) as observed on lateral cervical radiographs. Fluoroscopy suggested dynamic collapse of the cervical trachea in four dogs (dogs 1–4). Echolaryngographic evaluation was conducted in three dogs (dogs 1–3); in all cases, heterogeneous, hyperechoic, smooth, and movable masses were identified at the glottis during expiration (Fig. 3). No abnormalities were noted in adjacent structures, including the thyroid and arytenoid cartilages. Endoscopic examination of the larynx was performed under sedation using intravenous propofol (Propofol 1%; Fresenius Kabi Japan K.K., Tokyo, Japan) at a dose of 3 mg/kg, administered over 1 min to effect, while maintaining spontaneous respiration. The examination revealed two pale pink to reddish, pedunculated, well-defined soft tissue masses with intact, non-ulcerated mucosal surfaces within the glottis that moved craniocaudally with respiration in all dogs. Each dog exhibited two masses originating from the laryngeal ventricles (Fig. 4). Following endoscopic evaluation of the larynx, all dogs were endotracheally intubated, and anesthesia was maintained using isoflurane in oxygen. En bloc excision of the masses was not feasible owing to their small size and the limited working space within the larynx. Therefore, the lesions were resected under laryngoscopic guidance by incrementally transecting them into small fragments until they detached at the base, and histopathological evaluation was subsequently performed. Adjunctive treatment with prednisolone was administered in three dogs. Following excision, respiratory signs resolved rapidly in all dogs. Lateral cervical radiography performed postoperatively at 7–10 days in dogs 1–3 and at 1 month in dogs 4 and 5 confirmed the absence of laryngeal masses. The median follow-up was 13 months (range, 6–27), and no recurrence of clinical signs suggestive of laryngeal mass regrowth was observed.

Histopathologic examination showed that all laryngeal masses were consistent with chronic laryngitis. Lymphocyte and plasma cell infiltration with accompanying fibrosis was noted in the lamina propria of all five dogs. Edema and lymphangiectasia were observed in four dogs, and neutrophil infiltration in two. As the masses were considered to represent everted laryngeal saccules, a comparative histopathologic assessment was performed using laryngeal saccules resected from a 1-year-old French Bulldog treated for brachycephalic airway syndrome. Infiltration of plasma cells, lymphocytes, neutrophils, and macrophages was observed, along with fibrosis, edema, and lymphangiectasia in the lamina propria. These findings were similar to those observed in the five dogs with laryngeal masses associated with chronic laryngitis (Fig. 5). With reference to previous reports describing the histopathologic features of laryngeal saccules in brachycephalic airway syndrome [2], the histopathologic findings in the five dogs with laryngeal masses in this study were evaluated using the following scoring system. For lymphoplasmacytic infiltration, scoring was performed by two pathologists (Y.M. and M.M.) according to the number of infiltrating cells per × 40 field, referencing the criteria established for inflammatory cell quantification in canine gastrointestinal disease by the WSAVA Gastrointestinal Standardization Group [6]. The clinical signs observed in the present study, including stridor and dysphonia, were similar to those reported in dogs with inflammatory laryngeal masses in previous studies [7, 9, 19, 22, 25]. However, clinical signs in the dogs in this study were mild and intermittent, and none showed severe respiratory compromise. In all dogs, the masses were movable and displaced cranially during expiration, which may have prevented severe upper airway obstruction. To date, bilaterally movable inflammatory laryngeal masses have not been documented in the veterinary literature. Previously reported masses were primarily associated with granulomatous inflammation, often of infectious origin. In contrast, the inflammatory laryngeal masses described here were bilateral and showed histopathologic features of chronic inflammation without granulomatous change, suggesting a pathogenesis distinct from that described in earlier studies. All dogs recovered without complications or recurrence after surgical excision, as determined by long-term clinical monitoring and short-term radiographic follow-up, regardless of adjunctive prednisolone administration. These findings suggest that inflammatory, movable masses originating from the laryngeal ventricles may carry a favorable prognosis following excision. However, owing to the absence of postoperative laryngoscopic evaluation, the possibility of subclinical recurrence of the laryngeal mass cannot be entirely excluded. In all dogs that underwent echolaryngographic or fluoroscopic evaluation, mass mobility was detected, indicating the potential value of these modalities as minimally invasive diagnostic tools for evaluating inflammatory laryngeal masses in dogs. Notably, echolaryngography has also been shown to be effective for detecting laryngeal masses in cats [14] and is generally more accessible than fluoroscopy in veterinary practice, supporting its use as a practical diagnostic tool. Although collapse of the cervical trachea was suspected in most dogs, it was considered a secondary change related to the presence of laryngeal masses and the mild severity of respiratory signs such as stridor. Therefore, endoscopic evaluation of the trachea to confirm a definitive diagnosis of tracheal collapse was not performed.

In all cases, histopathological examination revealed features consistent with chronic inflammation; however, no clear association between histological findings and clinical presentation could be assessed owing to the limited number of cases. Further analyses, such as immunohistochemistry or PCR-based lymphocyte clonality testing, were not performed. Therefore, although an underlying lymphoproliferative disease is considered unlikely based on morphology findings, it cannot be entirely excluded. The morphologic characteristics of the laryngeal masses observed in this study closely resembled those of everted laryngeal saccules in dogs with brachycephalic airway syndrome (BAS), suggesting a potentially similar underlying pathophysiology [13]. Evaluation of collagen, elastic, and reticular fiber composition using special histochemical stains was not feasible in this study, as the masses were excised in fragmented portions rather than as en bloc. Further investigation is warranted to determine whether the firmness and mobility of the masses are associated with the relative abundance and distribution of these connective tissue components. One possible explanation is that chronic laryngitis may have caused edema, which could have gradually drawn the affected laryngeal saccules outward under repeated inspiratory negative pressure. In non-brachycephalic breeds, where the aditus laryngis provides greater anatomical space than in brachycephalic dogs, this persistent traction might contribute to the formation of pedunculated, movable masses, allowing for gradual enlargement without causing marked respiratory signs until middle to old age. A previous study reported that Norwich Terriers are predisposed to laryngeal disorders—including laryngeal collapse and eversion of the laryngeal saccules—conditions typically associated with BAS, even in the absence of clinical signs [12]. In the present case series, three of the five affected dogs were Yorkshire Terriers, two of which were a parent–offspring pair. These findings raise the possibility of a genetic predisposition contributing to the development of subclinical chronic laryngeal inflammation and subsequent mass formation. However, the precise mechanisms involved remain unclear, and further investigation in a larger cohort is warranted to elucidate these processes.

In conclusion, this case series suggests that bilateral, movable inflammatory laryngeal masses arising from the laryngeal ventricles may carry a favorable prognosis following surgical excision. Echolaryngography and fluoroscopy may serve as useful, minimally invasive tools for evaluating the mobility of these lesions. Histopathological features of the excised masses were comparable to those of everted laryngeal saccules in dogs with BAS, supporting the possibility that a similar pathophysiological process may contribute to mass formation, even in non-brachycephalic breeds.
