Identification of a G Protein-Coupled Receptor for Buccalin-Type Peptides in the Mollusk Aplysia: Evolutionary Insights into Neuropeptide Signaling
Authors: Ju-Ping Xu, Xue-Ying Ding, Qing-Chun Jin, Yi-Long Zhang, Jian-Hui Chang, Hui-Min Jiang, Ya-Dong Li, Ping Fu, Yan-Chu-Fei Zhang, Cui-Ping Liu, Rui-Ting Mao, Cheng-Yi Liu, Fan Li, Shao-Qian Wu, Elizabeth C. Cropper, Guo Zhang, Jian Jing
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Neuropeptides play a crucial role in modulating behavior, with certain signaling systems, such as those for Neuropeptide Y and Cholecystokinin (CCK), being clearly conserved across protostomes and deuterostomes. However, evolutionary relationships for other signaling systems are less evident. Buccalin (Buc) and allatostatin A (AstA) were independently discovered in the mollusk Aplysia californica and the arthropod Diploptera punctate and are now recognized as homologous. These peptides share a conserved C-terminal Y/F-X-F-G-L/I-amide motif. Bioinformatic evidence further suggests homology between this signaling system and the deuterostome galanin (GAL) and kisspeptin (KISS) signaling systems. Nonetheless, uncertainties persist, particularly due to the limited characterization of Buc/AstA receptors in lophotrochozoans, the superphylum that encompasses mollusks and annelids. To date, only one AstA receptor has been experimentally characterized in the annelid Platynereis dumerilii, while no receptors have been characterized in Aplysia. In this study, we combined bioinformatics, molecular biology, and a cell-based assay to identify an Aplysia buccalin receptor, designated as apBuc/AstA-R. The 19 mature buccalin peptides encoded by the buccalin precursor activated apBuc/AstA-R in a dose-dependent manner, with EC50 values ranging from 23 to 320 nM. Importantly, the potencies of buccalin A and B corresponded to their previously characterized physiological effects. To assess cross-activity, we tested whether Drosophila AstAs and human KISS/GAL can activate apBuc/AstA-R. While receptor activation was observed with Drosophila AstAs and human KISS, human GAL was not active. Phylogenetic and chromosomal gene analyses reinforced the idea that the protostome Buc/AstA signaling system is orthologous to the deuterostome GAL systems and paralogous with the KISS system. Additionally, bioinformatic data suggest the presence of a KISSR-like receptor in Aplysia, though it was not activated by either human KISS/GAL or by Aplysia and Drosophila Buc/AstAs. Overall, our study provides the first comprehensive characterization of the Buc/AstA signaling system in Aplysia and provides further evidence for its close evolutionary relationship with the deuterostome GAL and KISS signaling systems.

Introduction

Neuropeptides are critical modulators of a wide range of behaviors across bilaterian animals. − A key question in evolutionary biology is how the peptide signaling systems in protostomes and deuterostomes are related. For some systems, such as those involving Neuropeptide Y, vasopressin/oxytocin and Cholecystokinin (CCK), relationships are evident due to striking similarities in both the mature peptides and their receptors. − However, elucidating evolutionary relationships between other signaling systems has proven more challenging. These relationships are becoming clearer as peptide precursors and crucially, their receptors are identified or predicted in a broad range of species.

Here, we study the signaling system for the buccalins (Bucs), a family of peptides originally characterized in the mollusk Aplysia californica. Three buccalins, referred to as buccalin A, buccalin B and buccalin C, were purified from neuronal elements in the accessory radula closer (ARC) neuromuscular system of the feeding network. − They are synthesized in both of the motor neurons, B15 and B16, that innervate the ARC and function as modulatory cotransmitters that reduce acetylcholine release from neuronal terminals, thereby reducing the size of motor neuron-induced muscle contractions. − Additionally, the buccalins are expressed in neurons that innervate various other peripheral tissues associated with feeding, digestion, circulation, and reproduction. Subsequently the buccalin precursor in Aplysia was cloned and it became apparent that there are more than three buccalins. The precursor protein encodes 21 short peptides, 11 of which have been verified via mass spectrometry (Figure S1)

Over 20 years later, it became apparent that the buccalins are homologues of a type of allatostatin (Ast) peptide. In general, the allatostatins are widely present in arthropods, where they rapidly and reversibly inhibit juvenile hormone (JH) synthesis, playing key roles in insect development, molting, reproduction, programmed cell apoptosis and reconstruction. , Based on conserved motifs, the allatostatin family is classified into three types: allatostatin A, B, and C. In particular, allatostatin A (AstA) is the buccalin homologue. AstA was first isolated from Diploptera punctata in 1989, , and all arthropod genomes, except beetles, encode an AstA precursor gene. , The buccalins and AstAs share a conserved C-terminal Y/F-X-F-G-L/I-amide motif.

AstA receptors (AstA-Rs) in arthropods ( D. melanogaster ) , and nematodes (C. elegans) have been identified and it was suggested that they are homologous to receptors that were characterized in deuterostomes, namely galanin (GAL) receptors. , Bioinformatic analyses later provided additional evidence supporting the evolutionary relationship between the protostome AstA signaling system and the deuterostome GAL − and KISS signaling systems. AstA and KISS peptides all share a conserved C-terminal FGL-amide sequence which is critical for receptor activation in both insects and vertebrates. − This sequence is not present in GAL peptides (Figure and Table S1).

Despite extensive research on the Buc/AstA signaling system, relatively little is known about its receptors (Buc/AstA-Rs) in mollusks or even in lophotrochozoans in general. To date, only a single receptor has been experimentally characterized in the annelid Platynereis dumerilii. While molluscan receptors have been included in phylogenetic analyses, these studies have relied exclusively on predicted sequences. To address this gap, we aimed to experimentally characterize the buccalin receptor in Aplysia.

Several phylogenetic analyses ,,, have shown that AstA-Rs cluster with GAL receptors (GALRs), which in turn cluster with KISS receptors (KISSRs). This suggests orthology between AstA-Rs and GALRs, and paralogy between AstA-Rs and KISSRs. However, other phylogenetic analyses, including one incorporating predicted molluscan sequences, have proposed that AstA-Rs may first cluster with deuterostome KISSRs instead. A key objective of our study was to resolve this conflict.

We cloned four putative buccalin receptors and identified one, apBuc/AstA-R, that was activated by the 19 mature buccalin peptides, each containing a C-terminal Y/F-X-F-G-L/I-amide motif and derived from a previously characterized precursor protein. While previous bioinformatic studies have predicted the existence of KISSR-like receptors in mollusks, ,, our data provide further evidence for a KISSR-like receptor in Aplysia, though we were unable to identify its corresponding ligand. Our phylogenetic, chromosomal homologous gene and synteny analyses all support the hypothesis that the protostome Buc/AstA system is orthologous to the deuterostome GAL system but paralogous to the deuterostome KISS system. Overall, our study completes the characterization of the Buc/AstA neuropeptide signaling system in Aplysia, and provides further evidence for its relationship to the deuterostome GAL/KISS signaling system.

Materials and Methods

Subjects and Reagents

Experiments were performed on Aplysia californica (100–350 g) obtained from Marinus, California, USA. Aplysia are hermaphroditic (i.e., each animal has reproductive organs normally associated with both male and female sexes). Animals were maintained in circulating artificial seawater at 14–16 °C and the animal room was equipped with a 24 h light cycle with light period from 6:00 am to 6:00 pm. All chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich unless otherwise stated.

Bioinformatic Analysis of Buc/AstA Receptors

We first used NCBI to search for specific sequences of interests. In addition, we also searched AplysiaTools databases (Dr. Tomas Abrams, University of Maryland, USA) to obtain additional sequences for comparison. The latter databases (http://aplysiatools.org) include Aplysia transcriptome and genome data. The open reading frames (ORFs) of the Aplysia putative Buc/AstA receptor full-length cDNA sequences were obtained using ORF Finder (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/orfnder/).

For putative Aplysia Buc/AstA receptors, we predicted transmembrane domains using TMHMM Server v2.0 (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM/). For proteins difficult to annotate through BLAST, we utilized the Pfam database (http://pfam-legacy.xfam.org/) to determine protein class. We constructed phylogenetic trees for various species using the maximum likelihood method in MEGA X (https://www.megasoftware.net/), with 1000 bootstrap replicates. The different models we used were based on preliminary MEGA analysis.

RNA Extraction and Reverse Transcription

Animals were anesthetized by injecting 30–50% of their body weight with 333 mM MgCl2. Aplysia cerebral, pleural-pedal, buccal and abdominal ganglia were dissected out and maintained in artificial seawater containing the following (in mM): 460 NaCl, 10 KCl, 55 MgCl2, 11 CaCl2, and 10 HEPES buffer, pH 7.6, in a dish lined with Sylgard (Dow Corning). RNA was prepared from Aplysia ganglia using the TRIzol reagent method. Specifically, the dissected ganglia were placed into 200 μL TRIzol (Sigma, T9424) and stored at −80 °C until use. Frozen ganglia were thawed, homogenized with a plastic pestle, then TRIzol was added to a total volume of 1 mL. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 10 min and 200 μL of chloroform was added. The resulting solution was mixed thoroughly by shaking, and placed on ice for 15 min. It was then centrifuged (12,000 × g, 4 °C, 15 min), and the supernatant was added to an equal volume of isopropanol. The tube was shaken gently by hand and let stand at −20 °C for 2 h. It was then centrifuged (12,000 × g, 4 °C, 15 min) again, the supernatant was discarded, 1 mL of 75% ethanol/water was added, and the centrifuge tube was shaken gently by hand to suspend the pellet. It was centrifuged (12,000 × g, 4 °C, 10 min), the supernatant was discarded, and the precipitant was dried at room temperature for 5–10 min. Finally, 30 μL of nuclease-free water was added to dissolve the RNA pellet, and the RNA concentration was determined with a Nanodrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Using the extracted RNA as a template, cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription using PrimeScript RT Master Mix Kit (Takara, RR036A) and then stored at −20 °C until use. The synthesized first-strand cDNA served as a template for subsequent PCR.

Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR)

Synthesized cDNA was used as a template for PCR. Each pair of specific primers was designed (Table S2) in Primer Premier 6 and Oligo7, based on protein coding sequences for putative receptors. The PCR reaction was performed with 98 °C/2 min predenaturing, 98 °C/10 s denaturing, 60 °C (depending on the specific primers: see Table S2) /15 s annealing, 72 °C/30 s extension and 72 °C/5 min re-extension for 35 cycles. During the PCR reaction, a high-fidelity polymerase named T3 DNA Polymerase was used. The PCR products were subcloned into vector pcDNA3.1­(+) and sequenced to ensure the sequence was correct.

IP1 Accumulation Immunofluorescence Assay

Inositol monophosphate (IP1) accumulation assay measures the concentration of IP1, which is hydrolyzed from the second messenger, inositol triphosphate (IP3). IP3 is generated by Gαq pathway when a G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) expressed in CHO-K1 cells is activated by an appropriate ligand. To express the Aplysia putative receptors transiently in CHO-K1, the cDNA was cloned into the mammalian expression vector pcDNA3.1­(+). CHO-K1 cells (Procell, CL-0062) were cultured in F-12K medium (Gibco, 21,127–022) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Genial, G11–70500) at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Transfection experiments were performed when the cells were grown to 70–90% confluence. In preliminary experiments, for each dish (60 mm diameter), 2 μg of the putative receptor plasmids [in pcDNA3.1­(+)] and 2 μg of the promiscuous Gαq plasmids (also known as Gα16) , [in pcDNA3.1­(+)] were cotransfected in CHO-K1 cells, mixed with 200 μL of Opti-MEM (Gibco, 11,058,021), followed by the addition of 6 μL of Turbofect (Thermo Fisher Scientific, R0531).

CHO cells with the reagents added above were mixed gently and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. The DNA/Turbofect mixture was then added to the dish in drops, and cells were incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 overnight. The next day, cells were trypsinized and reseeded in 384-well tissue culture-treated plates (Corning, 3570) at a density of 20,000 cells/well in F-12K and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 overnight.

On the third day, the activation of the putative receptor was detected by monitoring IP1 accumulation using an IP1 detection kit (Cisbio, 62IPAPEB) in Tecan Spark. Specifically, FBS-containing media were aspirated prior to peptide treatment, though trace amounts of FBS (and associated proteases) may remain due to the lack of extensive washing steps. While the use of heat-inactivated FBS during cell culture and additional washing steps could have further minimized protease carryover, these measures were not employed in the current study due to experimental constraints. Neuropeptides, serially diluted in 1× stimulation buffer, were added at final concentrations ranging from 10–12 to 10–4 M (14 μL per well) and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Following incubation, 3 μL each of 0.5× Tb cryptate-labeled IP1 antibody and 0.5× d2-labeled IP1 (prepared in lysis and detection buffer) were added to each well and incubated at room temperature for 1 h in the dark. Excitation intensities were measured at 620 and 665 nm using a Tecan Spark reader.

It should be noted that since protease inhibitors were not employed in this study, the measured responses may reflect both receptor-mediated activity and peptide degradation. This limitation is particularly relevant for nonamidated peptides, which are more susceptible to proteolysis. Particularly, peptidases originating from cellular secretion or surface expression may have contributed to peptide degradation, as no specific peptidase inhibitors were used to mitigate their effects. Peptides were synthesized by Guoping Pharmaceutical (Figure S2) and were aliquoted in 50 nmol Eppendorf tubes and stored at −20 °C until use.

Cluster Analysis of Sequences (CLANS) of Bilaterian Rhodopsin γ Receptors

We performed a cluster-based analysis of receptor sequences using CLANS with the BLOSUM62 scoring matrix and linkage clustering at an e-value threshold of 1 × 10–5 to identify coherent clusters. Initially, we constructed a data set comprising kisspeptin-type receptors, galanin receptors, buccalin/allatostatin-A-type receptors, and other Rhodopsin γ receptors, including melanin-concentrating hormone receptors (MCHR1/2), urotensin-2 receptors (UTS2R), somatostatin receptors (SSTR), allatostatin-C receptors (AstC-R), and opioid receptors (Opioid-R). To this data set, we subsequently added our cloned apKISSR-like receptor and other additional predicted KISSR-like sequences from lophotrochozoans to generate the final data set used for clustering analysis, as detailed in Data S1 and S2. The clustering was first performed in three dimensions, and then the map collapsed to two dimensions (2D) to enable generation of the diagrams shown in Figures.

Chromosome Homologous Gene Analysis across Protostomes and Deuterostomes

The sequence of the Magallana gigas chromosome containing the putative buccalin precursor gene (chromosome 5; NCBI accession no. NC_088857.1) was analyzed to investigate the occurrence of genes with putative orthologs located on the chromosomes that contain the KISS precursor gene (chromosome 1; NCBI accession no. NC_000001.11) and the GAL precursor gene (chromosome 11; NCBI accession no. NC_000011.10) in Homo sapiens , respectively. Additionally, the sequence of the Magallana gigas chromosome containing the putative Buc/AstA-R gene (chromosome 7; NCBI accession no. NC_088859.1) was analyzed to investigate the occurrence of genes with putative orthologs located on the chromosomes that contain the KISSR gene (chromosome 19; NCBI accession no. NC_000019.10), the GALR1 receptor gene (chromosome 18; NCBI accession no. NC_000018.10), the GALR2 receptor gene (chromosome 17; NCBI accession no. NC_000017.11), and the GALR3 receptor gene (chromosome 22; NCBI accession no. NC_000022.11) in Homo sapiens , respectively (Table S3). Similar comparison was made with Mus musculus, as well as with Biomphalaria glabrata and Drosophila melanogaster to ensure a comprehensive cross-species analysis (Table S3). BLAST and reciprocal BLAST (TBtools version 2.142), with the e-value set to 1 × 10–20 and the number of hits restricted to one, were used to compare the sequences of proteins encoded by genes located on the target chromosomes mentioned above. Protein pairs identified as putative orthologs were curated to combine data for predicted proteins encoded by the same gene and to match results from reciprocal BLAST analysis. For visualization, these results were mapped onto circular plots generated using the Circlize package in R programming language.

Synteny Analysis of Genomic Regions across Protostomes and Deuterostomes

The information for chromosome localization of orthologs/paralogs of neighboring genes was provided from the Ensembl Genome Browser. Conserved genes flanking Buc/AstA-Rs across protostomes were used to establish synteny with human KISSR and GALR1/2 genome regions. The identity and evolutionary relatedness of the flanking genes identified was confirmed using sequence similarity searches against the mollusks (Magallana gigas and Biomphalaria glabrata ), ecdysozoans ( Drosophila melanogaster and Caenorhabditis elegans), echinoderms (Asterias ruben) and vertebrates ( Homo sapiens ) genome assemblies. Additionally, orthologs of the genes identified in the human KISSR and GALR2 paralogon were mapped to protostome genomes.

Results

Identifying Putative Receptors for Buc/AstA in Aplysia californica

To identify putative Buc/AstA receptors in Aplysia, we first used the sequence AKQ63073.1 as a query to perform a BLASTn search of NCBI database AKQ63073.1 was previously identified as a receptor for annelid Platynereis dumerilii Buc/AstA neuropeptides. Our search identified one related sequence (XP_005097945.1). We then searched the NCBI database using the query “Aplysia allatostatin A receptor” and retrieved 12 putative receptor sequences, including the sequence XP_005097945.1 (Table S4). By performing BLAST in the AplysiaTools database with these sequences and using NCBI Conserved Domain Search and TMHMM sever 2.0 to predict GPCR structures, we identified seven sequences with seven transmembrane domains that appeared to be complete GPCRs (Table S5). One of these sequences was XP_005097945.1.

Based on the seven GPCR sequences, we designed specific primers (Table S2) and successfully cloned four receptors: XP_005097945.1 (Figure A,B, see Figure S3B for the complete gel) and three other sequences, TRINITY_GG_20747_c3_g1_i4 in the Aplysia transcriptome database, TRINITY_DN899_c2_g1_i16 in the Aplysia transcriptome database and XP_012945008.1 (Figure S3B). We then compared the four putative receptors using BioEdit (Figure S4), which showed that the sequences in the 7 transmembrane domains are highly conserved.

(B) PCR products of the putative Buc/AstA receptors, apBuc/AstA-R (sequence accession: XP_005097945.1) with a length of 1152 bp. The cloned mRNA sequence has been verified by sequencing. M, Marker.

To assess whether the four cloned GPCRs could serve as Buc/AstA receptors, we performed BLAST searches against NCBI protein databases of Caenorhabditis elegans, Drosophila melanogaster , Danio rerio and Mus musculus (Table S6). For XP_005097945.1, we obtained several hits which were annotated as AstA receptors or KISS/GAL receptors with low E-values (<9 × 10–55). Therefore, we tentatively named this receptor apBuc/AstA-R. For TRINITY_GG_20747_c3_g1_i4, we obtained one hit with a low E-value (<5 × 10–57), a KISS1 receptor in [ Danio rerio ]). We tentatively designated this receptor as apKISSR-like. For TRINITY_DN899_c2_g1_i16 and XP_012945008.1, we did not obtain any hits with low E-values. TRINITY_DN899_c2_g1_i16 was most similar to Cholecystokinin receptor-like proteins and KISS receptors in Danio rerio (with the lowest E-value of 7 × 10–33). XP_012945008.1 was most similar to a “G protein-coupled receptors family 1 profile domain-containing protein [Caenorhabditis elegans]” (E-value of 4 × 10–9). Based on classification methods of Pfam (http://pfam-legacy.xfam.org/), both are categorized as Class-A GPCRs (rhodopsin γ family), and were tentatively named Class-A GPCR3 and Class-A GPCR4, respectively.

We compared our cloned sequences to those available in AplysiaTools (see Figure S5). For apBuc/AstA-R (Figure S5A) and apKISS-R (Figure S5B), we identified two single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) relative to the AplysiaTools sequences; however, these did not alter the amino acid sequences. For Class-A GPCR3 (Figure S5C), our cloned sequence is 15 nucleotides shorter than the database sequence, resulting in the absence of five amino acids. We confirmed this result across multiple independent cDNA preparations from different ganglia, consistently observing this deletion. These five amino acids are located in the third intracellular loop and are unlikely to be involved in ligand binding. Additionally, there is a SNP that does not result in an amino acid change. For Class-A GPCR4 (Figure S5D), three SNPs were identified, none of which altered the amino acid sequence. Thus, except for the five amino acid truncation in Class-A GPCR3, our cloned sequences are identical to those in the database.

To better understand the phylogenetic relationship of the four cloned proteins, we took advantage of a preexisting phylogenetic tree that includes a number of GPCRs from Lottia gigantea and Magallana gigas. We added the four cloned Aplysia sequences to the tree (see Table S6). We also added a putative Buc/AstA-R sequence of Magallana gigas derived from NCBI BLAST. We found that apBuc/AstA-R clustered with M. gigas Buc/AstA-R (Figure S3C), supporting its identity as the Aplysia Buc/AstA receptor. apKISSR-like was positioned close to this cluster. Class-A GPCR3 and Class-A GPCR4 were phylogenetically more distant, suggesting that the latter two are unlikely to be Buc/AstA-Rs.

Activation of Putative Receptors by Buc/AstA Peptides

To evaluate the functional activity of the four putative Aplysia GPCRs, we cloned them into pcDNA3.1­(+) vectors and transiently expressed them in Chinese hamster ovary-K1 (CHO-K1) cells. Using an inositol monophosphate (IP1) accumulation assay, we tested their responses to 19 Aplysia buccalins and two acidic peptides (BGPAP-A and BGPAP-B) (Figure S1A). In all experiments, we cotransfected receptors with a promiscuous Gαq-family protein (Gα16), which binds to most GPCRs. This setup ensures that a receptor will respond if there is a ligand interaction regardless of the downstream signaling pathways it normally activates.

We first screened receptor responses using two different peptide concentrations (10–10 and 10–5 M). At 10–10 M, peptides minimally activated receptors, serving as a control. apBuc/AstA-R was robustly activated by all 19 Buc/AstA peptides (Figure A). The apKISSR-like, the Class-A GPCR3, and the Class-A GPCR4 were unresponsive (Figure S6). None of the four receptors responded to the two acidic peptides.

To further characterize apBuc/AstA-R, we constructed dose response curves using peptide concentrations that ranged from 10–12 to 10–4 M (Figure B–F). apBuc/AstA-R was most sensitive to buccalin-K, (EC50 = 23 nM, n = 3), and least sensitive to buccalin-D (EC50 = 320 nM, n = 3), suggesting that sequence variations influence potency. The C-terminal motif of buccalin-D does differ from the Y/F-X-F-G-L/I-amide motif shared by the other buccalins (Figure G). Statistical analyses of log [EC50] values are summarized in Table S7.

Cross-Activity with Insect ( D. melanogaster ) and Vertebrate ( H. sapiens ) Peptides

Given the homology between lophotrochozoan buccalins and ecdysozoan AstA neuropeptides, we determined whether Drosophila AstA peptides could cross-activate the apBuc/AstA-R. This approach aimed to assess the functional conservation of these signaling systems across distantly related species. Additionally, we tested these peptides on apKISSR-like in an attempt to deorphanize the receptor.

We selected four Drosophila AstA peptides, known for their well-characterized activity, and assessed their ability to activate apBuc/AstA-R. Although apBuc/AstA-R responded to Drosophila AstA peptides (Figure A), their potency (Figure B,C) was lower than that of native Aplysia buccalins. Specifically, the EC50 values for Drosophila AstA peptides were significantly higher than those observed for endogenous buccalins (Figure D,E), suggesting that while functional conservation exists, species-specific adaptations have fine-tuned receptor–ligand interactions. In contrast, none of the four Drosophila AstA peptides activated apKISSR-like.

We further examined cross-activity of Aplysia receptors with human KISS and GAL neuropeptides. Human KISS peptides activated apBuc/AstA-R but had no effect on the apKISSR-like (Figure A,C), whereas human GAL peptides failed to activate either receptor (Figure A,B). These findings underscore the functional divergence and species-specificity of neuropeptide-receptor interactions, even within conserved signaling systems. Together with our results from Aplysia buccalins (Figure S7), these data indicate that none of the tested ligands activated apKISSR-like, which remains an orphan receptor.

In the experiments shown above, several other peptides, including human galanin and BGPAP-A and BGPAP-B, were tested and showed no activity on apBuc/AstA-R, effectively serving as negative controls. Additionally, other putative receptors (apKISSR-like, Class-A GPCR3, and Class-A GPCR4) showed no response to Buc/AstA peptides, further supporting the specificity of apBuc/AstA-R.

Cluster Analyses of KISSR-like Receptors in Lophotrochozoans

In addition to apKISSR-like, previous phylogenetic analyses ,, proposed the existence of KISSR-like in other lophotrochozoans. To explore the relationships between apKISSR-like and other putative KISSRs, we performed an analysis of Rhodopsin γ receptor relationships using the CLuster ANalysis of Sequences (CLANS) method. Our analysis revealed that apKISSR-like and other previously identified lophotrochozoan KISSR-like sequences , all clustered together with known deuterostome KISSRs (Figure and Data S1). The BLOSUM62 cluster map further demonstrated a high degree of similarity between the protostome Buc/AstA signaling system and the deuterostome KISS and GAL signaling systems at the receptor level. Notably, Buc/AstA receptors exhibited a closer evolutionary relationship with GAL receptors than with KISS receptors.

Phylogenetic Relationships between the Buc/AstA, KISS, and GAL Signaling Systems in Bilaterians

The identification of the Aplysia Buc/AstA receptor (apBuc/AstA-R) and the putative orphan KISSR-like receptor allowed us to further investigate the phylogenetic relationships among Buc/AstA, KISS, and GAL signaling systems. To this end, we constructed a phylogenetic tree incorporating the newly identified apBuc/AstA-R, along with multiple Buc/AstA receptors from other protostomes, as well as KISS and GAL receptors from deuterostomes (Figure , see Table S8 for information on these sequences). The resulting tree showed that apBuc/AstA-R clusters with other molluscan and annelid Buc/AstA receptors, exhibiting the closest homology to the receptor from Lymnaea stagnalis. Additionally, lophotrochozoan receptors formed a clade with arthropod AstA receptors. Notably, protostome Buc/AstA receptors clustered within the same branch as deuterostome GAL receptors, suggesting they are orthologs, whereas KISS receptors formed a separate, paralogous branch. These findings support the hypothesis that Buc/AstA and GAL signaling systems share a common evolutionary origin, while the KISS system represents a distinct yet related lineage.

A previous study predicted numerous putative molluscan KISSRs and constructed a phylogenetic tree incorporating predicted sequences and deuterostome GALRs, KISSRs, and protostome Buc/AstA-Rs. Their findings suggested that protostome Buc/AstA-Rs are more closely related to deuterostome KISSRs, which contradicts our results (Figure ) and those of other studies. ,,, To resolve this discrepancy, we first replicated their phylogenetic tree using their sequence data set. However, we noted that their tree lacked an outgroup. We therefore added Drosophila RYamide receptor as an outgroup and reran the tree. These results aligned with ours, showing that Buc/AstA-Rs are more closely related to GALRs (Figure S8). This finding highlights the importance of careful sequence validation in phylogenetic analyses and resolves the inconsistency between studies.

To further elucidate the phylogenetic relationships between these signaling systems, we performed comparative genomic analyses of the chromosomes containing precursor and receptor genes (Table S3). Given the limited annotated gene information in chromosomes (referred to as scaffolds) in Aplysia, we selected two other molluscan species, Magallana gigas and Biomphalaria glabrata , both of which have well-annotated chromosomal gene information for comparisons with Homo sapiens and Mus musculus.

First, we identified homologous genes in M. gigas and B. glabrata using the Aplysia proteins as queries. Similar to Aplysia, the putative buccalin precursors and receptors for Buc/AstA and KISS were all located on different chromosomes in both species (Table S3).

Next, we compared the gene repertoires of M. giga chromosomes 5 (MgChr5), which contains the buccalin gene, with H. sapiens chromosomes 1 (HsChr1) containing KISS gene and chromosomes 11 (HsChr11) containing GAL gene. This chromosome homologous gene analysis aimed to provide further insights into the genomic context and evolutionary connections between these signaling systems. Our analysis revealed that MgChr5 shares 241 putative orthologs with HsChr1 (KISS gene) and 215 putative orthologs with HsChr11 (GAL gene). While the absolute numbers of orthologs are similar, the substantial difference in chromosome length between protostome and deuterostome prompted us to normalize the data and calculate the relative density of orthologous genes. Interestingly, after normalization, the relative density between MgChr5 and HsChr1 (KISS gene) was 0.97, while the density between MgChr5 and HsChr11 (GAL gene) was 1.59 (Figure A,B, Table S3). A similar pattern was observed in M. musculus (Figure S9A,B, Table S3), suggesting that, at the level of gene density, molluscan buccalin peptides share a closer evolutionary relationship with deuterostome GAL peptides. This trend was also consistent in B. glabrata (Table S3).

We extended this analysis to chromosomes containing the receptor genes. We compared the gene repertoire of M. gigas chromosome 7 (MgChr7), which harbors the Buc/AstA-R gene, with those of H. sapiens chromosomes 19 (HsChr19, KISS1R gene), 18 (HsChr18, GALR1), 17 (HsChr17, GALR2), and 22 (HsChr22, GALR3) (Figure C,D, Table S3). A similar analysis was performed for M. musculus (Figure S9C,D, Table S3). Our results showed that MgChr7 shares a low number of homologous genes and exhibits low gene density with HsChr18 (GALR1). In contrast, the homology and gene density between MgChr7 and HsChr17 (GALR2) were higher. Furthermore, MgChr7 exhibited greater homology with HsChr17 (GALR2) than with HsChr19 (KISS1R). These findings further support, at the receptor level, the conclusion that the protostome Buc/AstA-R and deuterostome GALR are orthologs, while the relationship to KISSR is paralogous. This pattern was also observed in B. glabrata (Table S3).

To assess whether this pattern extends beyond mollusks, we analyzed Drosophila melanogaster as a representative ecdysozoan, focusing on its AstA signaling system in relation to human and mouse GAL and KISS signaling systems (Table S3). Consistently, there were more putative orthologs in the GAL comparison, reinforcing our findings across protostome lineages.

Finally, we conducted a chromosomal gene synteny analysis to examine the genomic regions surrounding the Buc/AstA-R, GALR, and KISSR genes across diverse taxa, including mollusks (Magallana gigas and Biomphalaria glabrata ), ecdysozoans ( Drosophila melanogaster and Caenorhabditis elegans), echinoderms (Asterias ruben) and vertebrates ( Homo sapiens ). We analyzed the synteny of the human KISSR and GALR2 genes by comparing the distribution and localization of neighboring genes across these evolutionary lineages. Our analysis revealed significant gene synteny conservation between the genomic region containing Buc/AstA-R in protostomes and the region harboring GALR2 in deuterostomes, with 19 conserved gene pairs were identified (Figure E,F, Table S9). In contrast, synteny between the Buc/AstA-R region and the KISSR-containing region was less pronounced, with only 9 conserved gene pairs (Figure E,F, Table S9). Thus, the synteny block containing Buc/AstA-R in mollusks exhibited a higher degree of conservation with the GALR2 region in humans than with the KISSR region. This finding further supports the hypothesis that Buc/AstA-Rs and GALRs share a closer evolutionary relationship, consistent with our phylogenetic and chromosomal homology analyses.

Discussion

In this study, we combined bioinformatics and experimental approaches to identify the buccalin receptor in Aplysia. The potency of two buccalins on the receptor correlated with their known physiological effects. Since the buccalin peptides are homologues of the arthropod allatostatin A peptides, we refer to this as an apBuc/AstA receptor. Additionally, we performed bioinformatic analyses that elucidate the evolutionary relationships between the protostome Buc/AstA signaling system and deuterostome GAL and KISS signaling systems. Lastly, our data support the idea that KISSR-like receptors are present in lophotrochozoans including Aplysia, despite the fact that ligand for these receptors has not been identified.

Protostome Buc/AstA Precursors and Their Encoded Peptides

The Aplysia buccalins were first identified through biochemical methods, ,, with buccalin-A being the first Buc/AstA-like peptide identified in protostomes. Subsequent study identified a single precursor gene encoding these peptides in Aplysia. However, Cardoso et al. proposed the presence of two precursor genes based on their reinterpretation of the earlier data, predicting the generation of 8 and 30 putative buccalins, respectively. Upon reevaluating their findings, we determined that Cardoso et al. misinterpreted a partial clone as a complete precursor, confirming that only one complete gene encoding the Buc/AstA precursor exists in Aplysia. This conclusion is consistent with observations in other mollusks, which also possess a single predicted Buc/AstA precursor. Similarly, most annelids have a single precursor, ,, although Platynereis dumerilii might be an exception with three predicted precursors.

In arthropods, most have only one Buc/AstA precursor gene. Beetles, however, are an exception, as they lack both the precursor and receptor. In nematodes, two AstA homologous precursors (nlp-5 and nlp-6) have been identified in C. elegans, whereas other nematodes show variable numbers of precursors, ranging from none to two.

The number of Buc/AstA neuropeptides encoded by the characterized precursors varies significantly across species. In Aplysia, the Buc/AstA precursor is 505 amino acids long and encodes 29 mature Buc/AstA neuropeptides, including 19 unique species (excluding two acidic peptides lacking the conserved C-terminal motif) (Figure S1). Some of these peptides are present in multiple copies, i.e., this is the case for buccalin A, B, C, J, and L (Figure S1A). Other mollusks, such as Saccostrea glomerata, produce fewer (i.e., nine) neuropeptides. Similar variability is observed in arthropods. For example, the Buc/AstA precursor in Drosophila melanogaster encodes four types of Buc/AstA neuropeptides (seven peptides in total), while Diploptera punctata produces 13 distinct peptides.

This variability in precursor length and neuropeptide diversity highlights the evolutionary plasticity of the Buc/AstA signaling system across protostomes. Further studies are needed to elucidate the functional implications of these differences and their role in species-specific physiological processes.

Buc/AstA Receptors in Lophotrochozoans and Ecdysozoans

In this study, we experimentally characterized the first Buc/AstA receptor (NCBI accession: XP_005097945.1) in mollusks. The receptor we identified, apBuc/AstA-R, demonstrates high sensitivity to Buc/AstA peptides, with EC50 values ranging from 23 to 317 nM. Previous studies established the physiological activity of buccalin A ,− and buccalin B, with buccalin B being more potent. Consistently, buccalin B exhibited a lower EC50 values (35 nM) than buccalin A (58 nM) (Figure G), suggesting that apBuc/AstA-R mediates these physiological effects. Notably, buccalin K showed the highest receptor affinity (EC50 = 23 nM). This pattern of receptor sensitivity mirroring their physiological potency is also observed in insect Buc/AstA signaling systems, i.e., Rhodnius prolixus. ,

The relatively low EC50 values with apBuc/AstA-R contrast with those reported for the annelid P. dumerilii, which exhibits significantly higher EC50 values (up to 12–15 μM). This discrepancy may stem from the limited number of native ligands tested in P. dumerilii. As more ligands are identified and tested, and additional receptors are characterized in annelids, this issue may be resolved.

In arthropods, such as Drosophila melanogaster and Anopheles coluzzii, two receptor subtypes have been identified. ,,, In Drosophila, the receptors DAR-1 and DAR-2 are activated by its endogenous AstA neuropeptides with low EC50 values of 19.7 and 14.5 nM, respectively. , In general, the EC50 values observed in arthropods are comparable to those in Aplysia, reinforcing the classification of apBuc/AstA-R as a bona fide Buc/AstA receptor. Notably, while Drosophila AstA peptides activate apBuc/AstA-R (Figures and ), their EC50 values exceeded 1600 nM, suggesting functional divergence in ligand–receptor interactions despite the evolutionary conservation of the Buc/AstA signaling system. The low activity of Drosophila peptides on apBuc/AstA-R likely results from differences in N-terminal sequences despite conservation of the C-terminal FGLamide motif. The Drosophila DAR receptors, in contrast, appear to have evolved binding sites optimized for Drosophila AstAs, explaining its higher affinity. Additionally, human KISS peptides, which share some features with buccalins, exhibited weak activation of apBuc/AstA-R, whereas Human GAL, which is much longer and shares fewer sequence similarities, had no effect (Figure ).

Although multiple Aplysia sequences have been annotated as AstA receptors in the NCBI database (Tables S4 and S5), our bioinformatic and experimental approach determined that only oneapBuc/AstA-Ris a functional Buc/AstA receptor in Aplysia (Figures and ). This finding underscores the limitations of bioinformatic predictions, which often overestimate receptor diversity. For example, four Buc/AstA receptors have been predicted in mollusk Lymnaea stagnalis, but sequence alignments reveal that these differ by fewer than five amino acids (Figure S10), suggesting they are likely sequencing variants resulting from single nucleotide polymorphism or artifacts rather than distinct receptors.

In lophotrochozoans, only two Buc/AstA receptors have been experimentally validated: one in Aplysia and one in the annelid Platynereis dumerilii. This contrasts sharply with the inflated receptor numbers often predicted by bioinformatics (see Table S4 for a list of related molluscan sequences that have been clarified by our current work). In contrast, the Buc/AstA signaling system is better characterized in ecdysozoans, particularly arthropods. Following the identification of AstA ligands in multiple species, − , their corresponding receptors have been extensively studied. ,, Some dipteran insects, such as Drosophila and Anopheles, possess two receptor subtypes, ,, while others, like Carausius morosus, Bombyx mori, and Periplaneta americana, have only one. ,, This well-documented diversity in arthropods highlights the need for rigorous experimental validation in lophotrochozoans to clarify the true complexity or simplicity of their neuropeptide signaling systems.

The diverse effects of a large number of peptides tested in our study (Figures and ) provide valuable insights into how specific residues or motifs within the ligands contribute to receptor activation. To explore the relationship between sequence variation and potency, we performed a sequence alignment using BioEdit (Figure ), arranging the peptides by their potency (EC50). This analysis suggests that C-terminal amidation is critical, as peptides lacking this modification (e.g., BGPAP-A, B, and HsGAL) failed to activate apBuc/AstA-R. The C-terminal FGL motif also appears to play a role, as Buc-D, which lacks this motif, exhibited the weakest potency among the Bucs. Additionally, the N-terminal MD residues seem to influence activity, potentially explaining why Drosophila AstAs, which have divergent N-terminal sequences, are less potent despite sharing the C-terminal FGLamide motif. Future mutagenesis studies will be valuable for further validating these structure–activity relationships. It is also worth noting that all functional studies were conducted in mammalian cell lines (CHO cells), which may not fully capture Aplysia-specific receptor dynamics. Future studies employing protostome-derived cell lines, such as the Drosophila SK1 cell line, could provide additional validation.

Phylogenetic Relationships between Protostome Buc/AstA and KISS Signaling Systems and Deuterostome KISS and GAL Signaling Systems

The evolutionary relationships between the protostome Buc/AstA signaling system and the deuterostome KISS and GAL signaling systems remain a subject of significant interest. Most studies suggested a closer relationship between AstA and GAL signaling systems, ,,, while others favor a stronger link between AstA and KISS systems. , Our phylogenetic analysis (Figure ) is consistent with the former idea. ,,, More importantly, our analyses with chromosomal homologous gene and synteny analyses (Figure ) provided additional support for the former hypothesis. Specifically, the Buc/AstA system in protostomes and the GAL system in deuterostomes are orthologs, while the relationship between the Buc/AstA system and the KISS system is paralogous. Our phylogenetic tree revealed that protostome Buc/AstA receptors cluster within the same branch as vertebrate GAL receptors, indicating orthology, while KISS receptors form a distinct paralogous branch (Figure ). Notably, our reanalysis of the phylogenetic tree by Cardoso et al. (Figure S8) suggests that the previous clustering of protostome Buc/AstA receptors with deuterostome KISS receptors might have resulted from the absence of an appropriate outgroup.

Comparative genomic analyses (chromosome homologous gene analysis) further corroborate these findings (Figure A–D, Table S3). At the precursor level, the protostome Buc/AstA genes showed greater gene density and syntenic conservation with vertebrate GAL genes than with KISS genes. Similarly, at the receptor level, genomic regions surrounding Buc/AstA-R share more homology with GALR2-containing regions than with KISSR-containing regions. These patterns were largely consistent across multiple protostome species, including lophotrochozoans (Magallana gigas, Biomphalaria glabrata ) and ecdysozoans ( Drosophila melanogaster ).

Synteny analyses further highlight the conserved genomic organization between the Buc/AstA and GAL systems (Figure E,F, Table S9). For instance, 19 conserved gene pairs were identified between the Buc/AstA-R region in protostomes and the GALR region in deuterostomes, compared to only 9 conserved pairs with the KISSR region. This pattern underscores the deep evolutionary roots shared by the Buc/AstA and GAL systems, while emphasizing the distinct evolutionary trajectory of the KISS system. Taken together, these findings support the idea that Buc/AstA and GAL systems share a common evolutionary origin, whereas the KISS system represents a later divergence within deuterostomes. ,,,

Based on a bioinformatic analysis of three putative receptors in the mollusk L. gigantea (http://neuroevo.org), Mirabeau and Joly were the first to hypothesize the existence of KISSR-like receptors in lophotrochozoans. Subsequent studies supported this hypothesis, suggesting that both Buc/AstA and KISS receptors are present in lophotrochozoan. , For instance, Cardoso et al. predicted two KISSRs in Aplysia, three KISSRs in Lottia gigantea, three KISSRs in Crassostrea gigas , two KISSRs in Pinctada fucata, and four KISSRs in Mytilus galloprovincialis. While our data support the presence of KISS receptors in lophotrochozoans, we found no evidence for such a high number of KISSRs (Figures and S7, Table S4). Specifically, only some of the predicted KISS receptors in mollusks, including the putative Aplysia KISS receptor (apKISSR-like), and additional molluscan and annelid receptors we found by BLAST, cluster with vertebrate KISS receptors in a BLOSOME cluster map (Figure ). This apKISSR-like clusters with apBuc/AstA receptor in a phylogenetic tree (Figure S3C) but did not respond to any of the buccalins (Figure S6A). Curiously, this receptor did not respond to human KISS or GAL nor to Drosophila AstAs (Figure A,B). Presumably, changes in key residues within the peptide-binding site have rendered this receptor nonresponsive to known ligands, raising the possibility that KISS receptors in lophotrochozoans are orphan receptors. However, we cannot exclude the existence of as-yet-unidentified ligands that may be characterized in future studies. In addition, future studies using AI-based protein structural prediction and ligand docking could help elucidate why apKISSR-like does not respond to buccalins, unlike apBuc/AstA-R (Figure ).

In summary, the experimental identification of the first molluscan Buc/AstA receptor represents a significant advance in our understanding of peptide signaling evolution. The presence of both Buc/AstA receptors and KISSR-like receptors in lophotrochozoan provides critical insights into the evolutionary divergence and conservation of these systems. Our findings highlight the close evolutionary relationship between protostome Buc/AstA and deuterostome KISS/GAL systems. Given the well-characterized peptidergic signaling systems ,− in Aplysia, particularly in feeding and locomotor circuits, ,− our study establishes a foundation for future investigations into the functional roles of Buc/AstA signaling in motivated behaviors.
