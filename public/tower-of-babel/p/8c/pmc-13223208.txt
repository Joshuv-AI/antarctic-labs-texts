Intraoperative video-based artificial intelligence model exceeding surgeon accuracy for predicting severe fibrosis in minimally invasive liver surgery
Authors: Namkee Oh, Bogeun Kim, Sunghyo An, Eunjin Lee, Hayeon Do, Jiyoung Baik, Suk Min Gwon, Jinsoo Rhu, Jongman Kim, Inwoo Hwang, Sang Yun Ha, Myung Jin Chung, Hakje Yoo, Gyu-Seong Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate evaluation of liver fibrosis is essential for surgical planning in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), as advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis can significantly alter resection strategy and postoperative outcomes. However, preoperative assessments using blood-based indices or imaging often fail to accurately reflect the true degree of fibrosis observed intraoperatively. This study aimed to develop and evaluate a deep learning model for video-based diagnostic analysis for of severe liver fibrosis (F3–F4), compared to surgeons’ visual assessment and conventional non-invasive scores. In this single-center retrospective study, we included 103 patients who underwent minimally invasive liver resection for HCC between December 2019 and March 2022. Intraoperative video frames were extracted and labeled according to METAVIR fibrosis grades. A DenseNet-121 architecture pretrained on ImageNet was fine-tuned to classify severe (F3–F4) vs. non-severe (F0–F2) fibrosis using five-fold cross-validation. The model’s performance was compared against five experienced liver surgeons’ visual estimations and two commonly used indices: APRI and FIB-4. Diagnostic metrics included area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), sensitivity, specificity, and F1-score. The deep learning model achieved a mean (± SD) AUROC of 0.927 (± 0.039), with sensitivity of 0.918 (± 0.110) and specificity of 0.910 (± 0.032). Surgeons exhibited lower AUROCs (0.844 for more experienced surgeons and 0.808 for less experienced), primarily due to lower specificity. APRI and FIB-4 also showed inferior discriminative capabilities, with AUROCs of 0.680 and 0.670, respectively. A deep learning approach using intraoperative liver surface images demonstrated superior performance in detecting severe liver fibrosis compared to surgeons’ assessments and standard non-invasive indices. These findings suggest a potential to provide objective reference data to determine intraoperative surgical strategies for HCC. Prospective multicenter validation is warranted to confirm generalizability and clinical impact.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47518-2.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the sixth most common malignancy and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, often developing in the setting of chronic liver disease and varying degrees of fibrosis1,2. Accurate evaluation of liver fibrosis is crucial for surgical planning because advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis (F3-F4) increases the risk of postoperative liver failure and may necessitate modifications to the resection strategy3,4. Despite its clinical importance, the degree of fibrosis is frequently underestimated prior to surgery when using conventional laboratory-based indices such as the Child-Pugh-Turcotte score, aspartate aminotransferase to platelet ratio index (APRI), and fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index5–8. Radiological approaches, including magnetic resonance elastography and contrast-enhanced imaging, can offer additional preoperative information but can be subject to technical variability, patient-related factors, and institutional resource constraints9,10.

In current surgical practice, intraoperative findings can substantially deviate from these preoperative assessments; the surgeon may discover more advanced fibrosis during the operation. Such unexpected diagnoses pose a significant clinical dilemma-overlooking severe fibrosis can lead to overly extensive resections and heightened morbidity, whereas overestimating fibrosis may prompt unnecessarily conservative approaches4,11–13. Yet, the conventional intraoperative evaluation of liver fibrosis typically relies on subjective visual inspection by the surgeon, which is prone to variability based of experience and the complexity of the procedure.

Recent advances in deep learning have shown promise in medical image classification, segmentation, and real-time decision support14–16. By leveraging convolutional neural networks to analyze intraoperative images or videos, it is now possible to extract visual cues related to tissue texture, color, and microarchitectural patterns that may correlate with underlying fibrosis10. However, few studies have addressed the feasibility of using deep learning for direct intraoperative evaluation of liver fibrosis in HCC, and none have compared these models against both surgeons’ assessments and common non-invasive indices.

Here, we present a deep learning approach that analyzed laparoscopic intraoperative liver surface images to predict severe (F3-F4) vs. non-severe (F0-F2) fibrosis. We hypothesized that a model trained on retrospective surgical videos could provide a more accurate and objective assessment than surgeons’ visual evaluations alone. Accordingly, the objectives of this study were to: (1) develop and validate a deep learning model for intraoperative fibrosis prediction, (2) compare its performance to experienced surgeons’ assessments, and (3) benchmark its diagnostic accuracy against conventional APRI and FIB-4 scores.

Methods

Ethics statement

This single-center, retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Samsung Medical Center (IRB No. 2024-03-122-001). The need for informed consent to participate was waived by the Institutional Review Board of Samsung Medical Center as the study was retrospective. All patient data were anonymized prior to analysis to ensure confidentiality.

Patients

This retrospective study included patients who underwent laparoscopic liver resection for HCC at Samsung Medical Center between December 2019 and March 2022. Inclusion criteria were: (1) availability of complete surgical videos, (2) histologically confirmed HCC, and (3) adequate tissue for background liver evaluation. Clinical characteristics such as age, sex, body mass index (BMI), hepatitis B viral infection, hepatitis C viral infection, alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), platelet count, bilirubin, albumin, international normalized ratio (INR), and histologic grade for fibrosis were extracted from clinical data warehouse of the Samsung Medical Center. Histopathologic evaluation of background liver

All resected liver specimens were reviewed by two experienced pathologists for the diagnosis of HCC. Slides were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s Trichrome stain, and the fibrosis of background areas outside the HCC lesion was also evaluated. The histological fibrosis grading was performed using the meta-analysis of histological data in viral hepatitis (METAVIR) scoring system, which defines fibrosis stages as follow: F0 for no fibrosis, F1 for periportal fibrosis, F2 for septal fibrosis, F3 for bridging fibrosis (incomplete cirrhosis), and F4 for cirrhosis17. To establish the ground truth labels, the pathologists independently reviewed the slides. In cases of initial disagreement, a final grade was determined through consensus via joint review using a multi-head microscope. Due to this consensus-based approach for establishing the final diagnosis, inter-rater reliability metrics for the initial independent assessments were not derived. For analysis, we dichotomized fibrosis severity into non-severe (F0-F2) versus severe (F3-F4) categories.

Video preprocessing and labelling

All procedures were recorded using a high-definition laparoscopic camera (1920 × 1080 pixels, 30 frames per second [fps]). From each of the 103 videos, we extracted the “inspection phase” during which the liver surface was systematically visualized before parenchymal transection. Subsequently, image frames were systematically extracted from these selected video segments at a rate of 3 fps. To ensure data quality, frames were manually reviewed and excluded based on specific quality criteria to establish baseline model performance. The exclusion criteria included: (1) significant occlusion by surgical instruments (> 50% of the liver surface), (2) visual artifacts such as motion blur, surgical smoke, or blood, and (3) poor illumination or glare. Each remaining frame was labeled with the corresponding patient’s final METAVIR fibrosis score, ranging from F0 to F4 (Fig. 1). Because the histopathologic diagnosis was obtained from the resected segments, we acknowledge that hepatic fibrosis can vary regionally; however, for this study, all liver surface frames from a given patient were assigned the same ground-truth label.

Deep learning model

We implemented a DenseNet-121 architecture pretrained on the ImageNet in Python 3.8 using Pytorch (version 1.9.0)18. The prediction of fibrosis was performed via binary classification as severe fibrosis (F3-F4) versus non-severe fibrosis (F0-F2). The study employed five-fold cross-validation (5CV) at the patient level, ensuring that all frames from a single patient were confined to either the training set or the validation set within a given fold. In each fold, the model was trained on four-fifths of the patient data and validated on the remaining one-fifth. After training, we used Grad-CAM to visualize model attention maps for interpretability.

Human (surgeon) evaluation

Five attending liver surgeons at our institution were recruited to visually assess whether each patient had severe (F3–F4) or non-severe (F0–F2) fibrosis. Three surgeons had experience in more than 1,000 liver resection or transplantation cases (“experienced group”), whereas two surgeons had participated in approximately 500 cases (“less experienced group”). All surgeons were blinded to patients’ histopathology results, laboratory indices (APRI, FIB-4), and the DL model’s predictions. Each surgeon made an independent judgment of “severe” vs. “non-severe” fibrosis and rated their confidence on a three-point Likert scale (1 = low, 2 = moderate, 3 = high). No group discussions were allowed to minimize rater bias.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.8 with NumPy, Pandas, and scikit-learn (version 1.0.2). Descriptive statistics for continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median [interquartile range], depending on data distribution. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the independent t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages and analyzed with the Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test.

Diagnostic performance was evaluated via AUROC, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and F1-score. Where possible, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using the DeLong method for AUROC. Logistic regression models were built for APRI and FIB-4 to predict severe fibrosis; their optimal cutoffs were identified through the clinical utility index (CUI + and CUI-). The McNemar test was used to compare misclassification patterns between the DL model and surgeons. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Fleiss’ kappa for overall agreement and Cohen’s kappa for pairwise comparisons. To compare confidence scores between correct and incorrect classifications, we applied the Mann-Whitney U test; for multiple comparisons involving confidence scores, two-way ANOVA with post-hoc Bonferroni correction was conducted.

All reported p-values are two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 103 patients met the inclusion criteria, of whom 67 (65.0%) were classified as having non-severe fibrosis (F0-F2) and 36 (35.0%) as having severe fibrosis (F3-F4). Table 1 summarizes their baseline demographics and clinical variables. There were no significant differences in age, sex, BMI, or viral hepatitis prevalence between the two groups. However, patients with severe fibrosis exhibited higher INR (1.02 [0.97, 1.06] vs. 0.99 [0.94, 1.03], p = 0.012), APRI (0.49 [0.37, 0.77] vs. 0.36 [0.28, 0.49], p = 0.002), and FIB-4 (2.39 [1.85, 3.08] vs. 1.81 [1.15, 2.37], p = 0.005), as well as a lower platelet count (133.5 [117.75, 164.0] vs. 180.0 [154.0, 228.5], p < 0.001). In the non-severe fibrosis group, all patients were classified with a Child Pugh score of 5 (67 of 67, 100%), whereas in the severe fibrosis group, scores were distributed as follows: score of 5 (33 of 36, 91.7%), score of 6 (2 of 36, 5.5%) and score of 7 (1 of 36, 2.8%). Among the non-severe fibrosis group, 57 (85.1%) were staged F2, 6 (8.9%) were F1, and 4 (6.0%) were F0. In contrast, the severe fibrosis group mostly comprised stage F4 (23 of 36, 63.9%), with the remainder 13 (36.1%) being stage F3.

Deep learning model performance

The deep learning model achieved mean AUROC of 0.927 (± 0.039) in the binary classification of severe (F3-F4) from non-severe (F0-F2) fibrosis. Mean F1-score was 0.877 (± 0.060), with a mean sensitivity of 0.918 (± 0.110) and specificity of 0.910 (± 0.032) across the five folds (Table 2, see supplementary for the detailed result from each fold). Figure 2 illustrates representative ROC curves for each fold, highlighting the model’s robust discriminative capability. Grad-CAM visualization of both non-severe and severe fibrosis examples (Fig. 3) revealed that the network predominantly focused on regions of uneven liver surface contour and color differences suggestive of fibrotic changes.

Surgeons’ visual assessments

Overall, the experienced group achieved a mean AUROC of 0.844 (± 0.014), accuracy of 0.819 (± 0.006), sensitivity of 0.954 (± 0.045), and specificity of 0.678 (± 0.031). The less experienced group showed a mean AUROC of 0.808 (± 0.027), accuracy of 0.767 (± 0.042), sensitivity of 0.959 (± 0.016), and specificity of 0.611 (± 0.049) (Table 2, see supplementary for the detailed result of each surgeon).

Fleiss’ kappa for all five surgeons was 0.725, indicating substantial agreement in severe vs. non-severe classification. Within-group agreement (experienced vs. less experienced) showed Fleiss’ kappa values of 0.754 and 0.739, respectively. Pairwise Cohen’s kappa between individual evaluators ranged from 0.69 to 0.82 (Fig. 4). Surgeons’ confidence scores correlated moderately with classification accuracy, especially in the experienced group. Among the three experienced surgeons, confidence levels were significantly higher for correctly classified cases than for misclassified ones (A: 2.70 vs. 2.26, p = 0.013; B: 2.39 vs. 1.94, p = 0.027; C: 1.78 vs. 1.30, p < 0.001). However, in two less experienced surgeons, one showed a difference in confidence score (D: 2.18 vs. 1.71, p = 0.020), while another did not show a significant difference (E: 2.59 vs. 2.45, p = 0.215).

Comparison with APRI and FIB-4

Logistic regression models for APRI and FIB-4 were built to predict severe fibrosis (F3–F4). The AUROC for APRI was 0.680, and its optimal threshold of 0.40 yielded a sensitivity of 0.69, specificity of 0.61, and F1-score of 0.58. FIB-4 demonstrated an AUROC of 0.670, with an optimal threshold of 2.09 resulting in a sensitivity of 0.67, specificity of 0.66, and F1-score of 0.58 (Table 2).

Discussion

In this single-center, retrospective study, we developed and validated a deep learning (DL) model using intraoperative liver surface images to predict severe fibrosis (F3–F4) in patients undergoing laparoscopic liver resection for HCC. Our findings indicate that this model outperforms both experienced and less experienced surgeons, as well as conventional non-invasive indices (APRI and FIB-4), in distinguishing severe from non-severe fibrosis. Specifically, the DL model achieved an AUROC of 0.927, surpassing the performance metrics of surgeon assessments and traditional scoring systems. These results suggest that deep learning techniques can reliably extract clinically meaningful information from intraoperative images, offering an objective tool to address the limitations of subjective visual inspection.

Our model’s high sensitivity (0.918) and specificity (0.910) underscore its potential to guide intraoperative decisions. High sensitivity helps prevent underestimating advanced fibrosis—a scenario that can lead to overly aggressive resections and increased postoperative liver failure—while high specificity curbs the risk of undertaking unnecessarily conservative operations. By contrast, surgeons in our study demonstrated higher sensitivity but lower specificity. This discrepancy might reflect a cognitive bias toward erring on the side of caution to avoid missing severe fibrosis. In clinical practice, many surgeons adopt a conservative approach: missing a case of advanced fibrosis carries significant morbidity and mortality risks, so a bias toward over-calling severe disease is not unusual19,20. However, such an approach can also lead to under-treatment in cases where the liver is healthier than assumed, resulting in potentially suboptimal oncologic outcomes. An AI-assisted model that maintains both high sensitivity and specificity could help balance this trade-off, providing an objective estimate to refine surgical decision-making.

By contrast, commonly used indices such as APRI and FIB-4 showed only modest performance in our cohort (APRI: sensitivity 0.69, specificity 0.61; FIB-4: sensitivity 0.67, specificity 0.66), reflecting their well-known variability across patient demographics and disease etiologies. FIB-4 is notably less specific in older adults, with specificity reportedly dropping below 30% in those over 65. Non-fibrotic factors (e.g., alcohol use, thrombocytopenia) can also inflate these scores, which were originally validated in narrow cohorts7,8. A comprehensive review further indicates that APRI and FIB-4 often yield indeterminate results in 30–50% of patients21. Although these tests are quick and non-invasive, their limitations underscore the need for more precise intraoperative assessment methods—an unmet need our intraoperative deep learning model aims to address.

While the model consistently outperformed conventional indices, a key question is how it compares to the expertise of surgeons who visually inspect the liver. Our approach was specifically designed to provide an objective, data-driven complement to surgical judgment rather than to replace it entirely. By offering quantitative feedback on likely fibrosis severity, the model can reduce inter-observer variability—particularly among less experienced surgeons—and potentially serve as a tiebreaker in borderline cases22,23. Surgeons remain integral to the final decision-making process, but AI-driven predictions may streamline intraoperative workflows by flagging higher-risk cases. The incorporation of interpretability tools (Grad-CAM) further enhances clinical acceptance24. By visually highlighting the regions most responsible for the model’s classification, surgeons gain additional insight into the morphological features linked to significant fibrosis. This transparency is essential for building trust in AI-assisted interventions and integrating novel technologies smoothly into established surgical procedures.

Despite these promising results, several limitations warrant discussion. First, the study’s retrospective, single-center design and relatively small sample size (n = 103) may limit the generalizability of our findings. Although our patient-level cross-validation scheme aimed to mitigate overfitting, external validation on independent cohorts from multiple institution is essential to confirm the model’s robustness across differing patient demographics and surgical environments. Second, to establish baseline performance, we utilized manually curated frames, explicitly excluding those with significant occlusion, motion blur, or surgical smoke. Consequently, the reported performance metrics may overestimate the model’s accuracy compared to an unedited, continuous intraoperative vide stream which inevitably contains visual noise. Future deployment will require automated frame-selection algorithms to handle raw surgical video feeds in real-time. Third. While we benchmarked the model against widely used blood-based indices (APRI and FIB-4), a direct comparison with standard preoperative radiological elastography, such as magnetic resonance elastography (MRE), was not performed due to data unavailability in this retrospective cohort. Future studies should aim to compare the diagnostic accuracy of intraoperative video analysis against these advanced imaging modalities. Fourth, regarding the ground truth, regional heterogeneity in hepatic fibrosis is not fully captured when assigning a single histopathological label (F0–F4) to all frames from a given patient. Furthermore, as the final fibrosis stage was determined via consensus between pathologists, inter-rater reliability metrics for the initial independent assessments were not calculated. Finally, this study was an in silico diagnostic accuracy analysis using recorded videos. The model was not deployed in a live surgical setting; therefore, practical challenges such as inference speed, hardware integration, and the model’s actual influence on intraoperative decision-making and surgical outcomes remain to be verified in prospective clinical trials.

In conclusion, our study underscores the feasibility and accuracy of a deep learning–based model for intraoperative liver fibrosis assessment in HCC patients. By leveraging standard laparoscopic videos, this approach addresses key limitations of both laboratory indices and subjective surgical judgment. Further multi-center prospective studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate these findings and assess the model’s generalizability.
