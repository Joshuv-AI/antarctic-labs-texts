Beyond phagocytosis: Co-expressing IL-21 to arm CAR-macrophages for systemic anti-tumor immunity
Authors: Yizhao Chen, Wei Wei, Jiajie Tu
Published: 2026

Main text

Following the groundbreaking progress of chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapy in the treatment of hematological malignancies, this adoptive cell therapy continues to demonstrate therapeutic potential in various diseases, including fibrotic diseases, autoimmune disorders, and infections, supported by clinical research. However, in the field of solid tumors, it has not yet achieved the expected outcomes.1 Only limited studies have shown improved clinical results in cancers such as gastric cancer and glioma, and these experiences have not been successfully replicated in other solid tumors. Extensive research has identified key factors contributing to this limited efficacy: poor tumor infiltration, the complex immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), a high degree of tumor heterogeneity, and the harsh physicochemical conditions within solid tumors, which collectively severely impair CAR-T cell survival and function.2

In this context, given the direct immunostimulatory role of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) signaling, innate immune cells, particularly macrophages, which are prevalent in the solid TME, have emerged as promising candidates for CAR-based engineering. Unlike T cells, macrophages exhibit intrinsic tumor-homing capacity, allowing them to infiltrate solid tumors; their phenotypic plasticity provides opportunities to remodel the immunosuppressive tumor niche; and their specialized phagocytic and antigen-presenting functions can bridge innate and adaptive anti-tumor immunity. Engineering macrophages with CAR constructs endows them with the ability to specifically recognize tumor cells while controllably activating macrophage effector functions. Through phagocytosis and trogocytosis, CAR-macrophages (CAR-Ms) can directly eliminate tumor cells and, via secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and presentation of tumor antigens, orchestrate adaptive immune responses.3 This multimodal anti-tumor network supports the potential of CAR-M therapy as a novel cellular treatment strategy for solid tumors.

Despite advances in pre-clinical studies, CAR-M therapy continues to face challenges in achieving significant efficacy against solid tumors. First-generation CAR-M therapies (e.g., CT-0508 and SY001) have demonstrated only limited anti-tumor activity in clinical trials (NCT04660929 and NCT06562647).4,5 To enhance CAR-M function and potency, novel intracellular domain-activated, enhanced-potency CAR-M cells have been developed, and key genes supporting CAR-M persistence have been identified. However, an engineering strategy widely used in CAR-immune cell platforms and already advanced to the clinical stage, cytokine co-expression, has not been explored or validated in CAR-M therapy.

Addressing this gap, our team, in the January issue of Communications Biology, extending prior work,6 reported a strategy to enhance engineered CAR-M cell therapy through IL-21 co-expression.7 In this work, we emphasized the importance of integrating cytokines to endow CAR-M with the ability to autonomously improve the microenvironment and enhance the endogenous capacity of adaptive immune response in future development. We systematically designed and evaluated the anti-tumor activity of second-generation anti-CD47 CAR-M cells engineered to co-express IL-21. They demonstrated that IL-21 co-expression potentiates CAR-M and T cell functions via synergistic activation of the AKT/ERK and STAT signaling pathways. This enhanced the phagocytic capacity of CAR-M cells, promoted their polarization toward an M1-like phenotype, and robustly activated cytotoxic T lymphocytes. Consequently, tumor growth was suppressed through integrated direct and indirect mechanisms.

A paramount challenge for CAR-M therapy lies in sustaining the inflammatory phenotype of engineered cells within the profoundly immunosuppressive TME, thereby preventing their detrimental reprogramming. To combat this, cytokine co-expression has emerged as a potent synthetic biology strategy. This approach directly engineers a self-sustaining, pro-inflammatory feedback loop into the CAR-M itself. While other engineering tactics, such as incorporating innate immune signaling domains (such as TIR-CD3ζ),8 can enhance intrinsic activation, cytokine co-expression offers a distinct, systems-level advantage: it enables CAR-Ms to act not only as autonomous effectors but also as local microenvironmental orchestrators. By secreting factors like IL-21 or IFN-γ, CAR-Ms can resist local suppression, actively polarize toward an M1 state, and, crucially, recruit and re-activate bystander immune cells such as cytotoxic T lymphocytes. Thus, this strategy transcends merely maintaining CAR-M function; it leverages the cells’ inherent antigen-presenting capacity to initiate and amplify a broader, adaptive anti-tumor immune response, addressing the immunosuppressive TME at its core.

It should be noted that cytokines, which excessively potentiate CAR-M activity and systemic distribution, may carry risks of off-target toxicity or cytokine release syndrome, necessitating stringent dose control or the incorporation of logic-gated safety switches. Moreover, artificially introduced potent signaling pathways may disrupt the intrinsic signaling networks of macrophages, potentially interfering with their normal metabolism, differentiation, and memory functions. Furthermore, whether sustained cytokine stimulation can durably maintain an M1 phenotype within the harsh TME requires further investigation.9 Future research in this field will likely focus on refining cytokine-enhanced CAR-Ms through more sophisticated engineering, such as logic gates and inducible switches, tighter targeting control using TME-specific promoters, and improved manufacturing paradigms like in vivo editing or CAR-monocyte approaches. These strategies aim to ensure that cytokine-augmented CAR-M therapies operate within a safe and controllable window, thereby facilitating their clinical translation.
