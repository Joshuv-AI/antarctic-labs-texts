Vasopressor Use in Acute Burn Resuscitation: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Kristine Knappskog, Kostiantyn Botnar, Julia Kleinhapl, Juquan Song, Amina El Ayadi, Henning Onarheim, Anne Berit Guttormsen, George Golovko, Steven Wolf, Stian Kreken Almeland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundVasopressors are often introduced in severe burn patients who respond poorly to fluid resuscitation to maintain adequate blood pressure and avoid fluid creep. This study aims to characterise these patients and explore potential correlations between vasopressor use and patient outcomes.MethodsMedical records from 2010 to 2023 for patients aged ≥ 18 years with burns ≥ 20% of the total body surface area (TBSA) were extracted from the TriNetX Research Network, a global federated health research platform aggregating de‐identified electronic health records. The patients were divided into two groups: those who received vasopressors within the first 2 days of hospitalisation and those who did not. Statistical analysis included Cramér–von Mises and χ 2 test, with significance set at p < 0.05. Propensity score matching (PSM) in a 1:1 ratio was applied for the variables age, sex, pre‐existing comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, chronic heart disease, chronic lung disease, chronic kidney disease, and alcohol or opioid addiction), presence of inhalation injury, %TBSA burned, and burn injury year. Odds ratios (OR) for outcomes were calculated, with p‐values adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg correction.ResultsOf 6872 included patients, 17.7% received vasopressors within the first two days of hospitalisation. These patients were older, had more comorbidities and sustained more severe burns. After PSM we included 1206 patients in both cohorts (VP+ and VP−). Acute kidney injury occurred within 3 days in 51.0% of the VP+ group and 28.4% in VP− (OR 2.62, 95% CI: 2.20–3.11). VP+ patients also had higher rates of sepsis (30.8% vs. 22.9%, OR 1.50, 95% CI: 1.25–1.81) and higher mortality (45.1% vs. 32.7%, OR 1.69, 95% CI: 1.43–2.01) within 30 days following hospitalisation.ConclusionWhile vasopressors are frequently administered as part of burn resuscitation, their use was associated with higher rates of early acute kidney injury and an increased 30‐day mortality rate.Editorial CommentPotent vasopressor use to support the circulation is commonly combined with fluid resuscitation in modern intensive care, and this is a prominent aspect of care in major burn cases. In this retrospective analysis of a commercial, de‐identified health care dataset (primarily from US hospitals or health systems), matched cases over 14 recent years were analysed by early vasopressor use (or not) and then for important outcomes including mortality. Vasopressor use was associated with some complications and negative outcomes, though there are likely many factors which may contribute to negative outcomes which were not included in this analysis.

Introduction

Severe burns trigger a series of systemic inflammatory changes, adding the risk of developing burn shock [1, 2]. The aetiology of burn shock is complex and holds features of hypovolemic, distributive, and cardiogenic shock, making it challenging to treat [3]. Increased microvascular permeability leads to substantial plasma loss and reduced preload, while accompanying cardiovascular changes, including myocardial depression and a compensatory rise in systemic vascular resistance, further diminish cardiac output, resulting in hypotension and impaired end‐organ perfusion [4].

Rapid initiation of fluid resuscitation to replenish intravascular volume is the mainstay treatment during the initial burn shock, aiming to balance fluid volumes to provide adequate systemic perfusion while avoiding over resuscitation and its potential adverse effects. When patients respond poorly to fluids alone during the burn resuscitation phase, vasopressors are commonly added to the fluid therapy [5]. Indications for vasopressor initiation during resuscitation are reported to be in response to hypotension or decreased urine output despite adequate fluid volumes with either crystalloids alone or after adding colloids to the intravascular volume resuscitation [6, 7, 8]. However, the effects of vasopressors during resuscitation of burn injury patients remain inadequately investigated in the literature [9, 10].

Vasopressors' immediate effects can be lifesaving, by preventing hypotensive shock development, but may portend later complications. Still, potentially harmful effects on vital organs, due to the peripheral vasoconstrictive effects of these drugs, remain unknown. Concerns include possible compromise of the circulation of the critical zone of stasis, which may contribute to burn wound progression [11, 12]. Further reductions in renal perfusion may also exacerbate early acute kidney injury, a frequent complication in patients with severe burn injuries [13, 14]. Additionally, norepinephrine has been associated with immune dysregulation, potentially increasing susceptibility to sepsis [15, 16, 17]. Moreover, vasopressor administration was described as one of the signs indicating a failed resuscitation in burn patients [18], and the use of these drugs has been associated with more severe outcomes and increased mortality [13, 19, 20].

Internationally accepted consensus guidelines are consistently vague or fail to mention the issue of how to proceed in acute burn resuscitation when intravascular volume alone is insufficient to maintain systemic perfusion measured by blood pressure and urinary output. Hence, the timing, dosage, and choice of vasopressors vary between burn centres [6, 7, 8, 13, 21, 22].

This study aimed to investigate the use of vasopressors during the initial resuscitation phase of adult patients with severe burn injuries involving ≥ 20% of the total body surface area (TBSA) and to characterise those receiving vasopressors to establish potential correlations between vasopressor use and patient outcomes within this group.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective cohort study used the TriNetX Research Network, a global federated health research platform aggregating de‐identified electronic health records from more than 120 healthcare organisations (HCOs) across 19 countries. The data was obtained from the US Collaborative Network within TriNetX, which included 80 HCOs. The platform aggregates data types, including diagnoses, procedures, and medications, using ICD‐10‐CM and ICD‐9‐CM for diagnoses, RxNorm for medications, and CPT, ICD‐10‐PCS, and ICD‐9‐PCS for procedures. The analysis was conducted with the support of The Institute for Translational Sciences at the University of Texas Medical Branch (UTMB), and the study was approved as exempt by the UTMB Health Institutional Review Board (#20‐0085) and approved by the Regional Committees for Medical and Health Research Ethics Western Norway (REK Vest) (#603929). This study was performed according to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines [23].

Patient records from 2010 to 2023 were accessed through TriNetX to extract data from the first hospital stay following burn injury of adult patients ≥ 18 years of age with burn injuries ≥ 20% TBSA. The data extraction was done on 11/21/23. Cases with multiple TBSA values on the same day were resolved by selecting the highest value. Patients with a prior burn injury recorded within the past 6 months, or who received vasopressors or had any of the predefined outcomes within 30 days before the burn incident were excluded.

Data extraction followed a predefined form (Appendix S1) with variables collected for demographic data (age, sex, and race), pre‐existing comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, chronic heart disease, chronic lung disease, chronic kidney disease, and alcohol or opioid addiction), %TBSA burned, and inhalation injury. The vasopressors included in the search were epinephrine, norepinephrine, phenylephrine, ephedrine, vasopressin, and dopamine, in addition to procedure codes for vasopressor administration (Appendix S1). The cohort was categorised into two groups: those who received vasopressors within the first 2 days of hospitalisation (day 0–1) and those who did not within the first 2 days of hospitalisation. TriNetX defines days as midnight‐to‐midnight intervals, and timing is not available at the hourly level. Because of this limitation, we chose the first 2 days of hospitalisation to best represent the acute burn resuscitation phase. Outcomes are likewise reported only at the daily level, without information on the exact timing within each day. Variables for predefined outcomes were collected for each group, including the development of acute kidney injury (AKI), continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), operative burn treatment, development of sepsis, and mortality (Appendix S1).

Statistical Analysis

Comparisons between those receiving vasopressors and those who did not were performed using the Cramér–von Mises test for numerical parameters and χ 2 test for categorical variables, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Continuous variables with a non‐normal distribution were summarised using median and interquartile range (IQR). To adjust for potential confounding factors, propensity score matching (PSM) was conducted using Python's Causal Inference library (version 0.1.3). A priori propensity matching criteria were selected based on feature importance analysis using multiple machine learning models (Random Forest, Extra Trees, Decision Tree, AdaBoost, Gradient Boosting, LightGBM, and XGBoost). Feature ranks were averaged across models, supplemented by recursive feature elimination and feature permutation approaches, resulting in the selection of 11 top confounders to be used as propensity matching criteria, including age, sex, pre‐existing comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, chronic heart disease, chronic lung disease, chronic kidney disease, and alcohol or opioid addiction), presence of inhalation injury, %TBSA burned, and burn injury year. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression, and a 1:1 matching without replacement and without a caliper. Post‐matching balance was assessed by comparing confounder distributions between groups using χ 2 tests for categorical variables and the Cramér–von Mises test for continuous variables. No statistically significant differences were observed after matching (Table S1).

Subsequently, outcome odds ratios (OR) were calculated, and p‐values were adjusted using Benjamini‐Hochberg correction. All data processing and statistical analyses were conducted in Python (version 3.9), with pandas (version 2.1.4) [24], SciPy (version 1.11.4) [25], scikit‐learn (version 1.4.0) [26], seaborn (version 0.13.1) [27], and EHRchitect (version 1.0.0) [28].

Results

We identified 6872 adult patients with burns ≥ 20% TBSA. The median age was 47 years (IQR 33–61), and other demographic details are shown in Table 1. 67.9% of the patients had burns below 50% TBSA, while 11.5% had burns above 80% TBSA. Within the first 2 days (day 0–1) of hospitalisation, 1213 patients (17.7%) received vasopressors, with the most frequently used drugs listed in Table 2. Patients who received vasopressors were older, with a median age of 50 years (IQR 35–64), compared to those who did not receive vasopressors with a median age of 47 years (IQR 32–60). We found no significant difference in gender or race between the groups except for those with race registered as unknown. The vasopressor patients had significantly more pre‐existing comorbidities across all categories assessed, with hypertension being the most common for both cohorts (Figure 1). Additionally, the vasopressor group was coded (ICD‐9‐CM/ICD‐10‐CM) for more extensive %TBSA burns (Figure 2) and had a higher incidence of inhalation injury (64.3% vs. 19.8%, p < 0.0001).

Propensity matching for age, sex, pre‐existing comorbidities, presence of inhalation injury, %TBSA burned, and burn injury year resulted in 1206 patients in both the two study groups, defined as patients receiving vasopressors within the first 2 days of hospitalisation (VP+) and patients not receiving vasopressors in the same period (VP−). Among the matched patients, 1121 (93.0%) of the VP+ group experienced one or more of the predetermined outcomes listed in Table 3 within the first 30 days of hospitalisation compared to 792 (65.7%) in the VP− group. Early AKI, defined as AKI within the first 3 days of hospitalisation, occurred in 615 patients (51.0%) in the VP+ group, while 343 patients (28.4%) in the VP‐ group developed early AKI (OR 2.62, 95% CI: 2.20–3.11). CRRT was started in 103 patients (8.5%) in the VP+ group and 31 patients (2.6%) in the VP‐ group (OR 3.54, 95% CI: 2.33–5.52) within the same period. Within 30 days of hospitalisation, 51.6% in the VP+ cohort developed AKI, compared to 29.6% in the VP− cohort (OR 2.53, 95% CI 2.13–3.01), and CRRT was started in 12.9% and 5.1% of patients, respectively (OR 2.79, 95% CI: 2.03–3.86). For both cohorts, the mean time of AKI diagnosis occurred within the first day of hospitalisation (day 0.16 ± 1.08 and 0.48 ± 1.90).

More VP+ patients underwent operations for wound closure both within 3‐ and 30‐days following hospitalisation, with 50.0% vs. 26.5% (OR 2.78, 95% CI: 2.34–3.31) and 62.4% vs. 40.2% (OR 2.47, 95% CI: 2.10–2.92) undergoing burn surgery procedures in the VP+ and VP− cohorts respectively. For the 30‐day period, the VP+ patients also had a higher incidence of sepsis (OR 1.50, 95% CI: 1.25–1.81). Three‐day mortality was low and similar between the groups (1.8% vs. 1.8%), but at 30 days after hospitalisation, the VP+ patients' mortality was 45.1%, compared to 32.7% in the VP− patients (OR 1.69, 95% CI: 1.43–2.01) (Table 3).

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate an association between vasopressor use during the acute burn resuscitation phase and higher rates of adverse outcomes. Compared with a propensity‐matched VP− cohort, the VP+ patients had higher rates of early acute kidney injury, more procedures for wound closure, increased incidence of sepsis, and higher mortality rates. Half of the VP+ patients developed AKI within the first 3 days of hospitalisation, and 45% died within the first month. This was much higher than for the VP− cohort, where 28% developed AKI and 33% died within the same periods.

Vasopressors have long been considered controversial in burn resuscitation. However, our data suggest that their use is relatively common, with 18% of severe burn injury patients having received vasopressors during resuscitation. Reports indicate that in patients with severe burn injuries, the use of vasopressors ranges from 18% to 65% [13, 20, 29]. Although consensus guidelines for the use of vasopressors in burn resuscitation are lacking, these drugs are often used as a strategy to limit fluid administration and reduce the risk of over‐resuscitation [8]. Several burn centres report that they introduce rescue therapies, such as colloids and vasopressors, when the projected 24‐h fluid volumes exceed 6 mL/kg/%TBSA [6, 7, 21]. A mean arterial pressure below 65–55 mmHg, despite increasing crystalloid volumes or starting colloid infusions, typically prompts vasopressor initiation [6, 8, 13]. Unfortunately, information on fluid volumes and haemodynamic monitoring is not available in the TriNetX database.

A concern with administering vasopressors during burn resuscitation is that these might contribute to burn related kidney injury [13, 29]. For burn patients, burn severity is known to be the main driver of AKI development [30]. Early AKI after burn is often attributed to acute changes occurring during the resuscitation phase, such as hypovolaemia and cardiac dysfunction. Patients with severe burn injuries remain relatively hypovolaemic despite fluid resuscitation until the increased capillary leak starts to normalise, usually around 12 h post burn, likely resulting in a transient renal hypoperfusion [31]. The massive release of neurohormonal mediators, like catecholamines, vasopressin, and angiotensin II, increases systemic vascular resistance. In hypovolaemic patients, this contributes to suboptimal end‐organ perfusion [32, 33]. In theory, the additional vasoconstrictive effects of vasopressors could further reduce renal perfusion, potentially aggravating damage to kidneys already compromised by the burn injury. Our results can be interpreted to support this notion, demonstrated by a higher incidence of AKI in the vasopressor treated cohort, with the majority of AKI cases occurring within the first 3 days of hospitalisation. However, given the retrospective nature of our study, it is not possible to determine the underlying causes of AKI, as well as the indication for starting CRRT. Because the cases of early AKI were, on average, diagnosed on the first day of hospitalisation following burn injury, it remains unclear whether vasopressors directly contributed to AKI development or were administered in response to underlying haemodynamic instability and organ dysfunction, which themselves may have driven renal injury. In an intensive care setting, AKI may be classified broadly and as such may not always meet the criteria for a formal diagnosis code, potentially excluding some cases from this search [14].

Burn care providers are concerned that vasopressors contribute to burn wound progression and impair graft take and wound healing by reducing skin microvasculature perfusion. Many commonly used vasopressors, including epinephrine, norepinephrine, phenylephrine, ephedrine, and dopamine, are sympathomimetic agents, inducing contraction of the microvasculature, including those of the skin, through activation of α₁‐adrenergic receptors [34, 35, 36]. A further reduction in perfusion within the potentially salvageable zone of stasis may contribute to the progression of irreversible tissue necrosis in this vulnerable area of the burn wound [37]. While challenging to evaluate in a retrospective study, the higher proportion of patients who underwent operations for wound closure could serve as an indirect marker of burn wound progression. In our study, the VP+ patients underwent burn surgery twice as often as the VP− patients within the first 3 days of hospitalisation. However, it is important to note that the dataset lacked information on burn wound depth, the main factor predicting early excision and grafting. This limits our ability to draw firm conclusions from this association. Furthermore, the rather low proportion of patients undergoing burn surgery in either cohort may reflect limitations inherent to coding practices, potentially resulting in underreporting. Still, given that the effects of vasopressors on the skin microcirculation in burn patients remain unexplored, we cannot dismiss vasopressors as a contributing factor in burn wound progression.

Vasopressor use has previously been associated with adverse outcomes in septic burn patients and burn patients with AKI [19, 38]. Some studies, including ours, report higher mortality rates in patients with burn injuries receiving vasopressors during acute resuscitation [20, 29], though the available data are limited and inconclusive [10, 13]. Patients receiving vasopressors are often more critically ill at presentation, which can skew outcomes [20, 29]. It is difficult to determine whether the vasopressor use itself, or the underlying clinical severity prompting their use, explains the observed association with poorer outcomes in the VP+ patients. In our study, with propensity score matching, we controlled for factors known to worsen prognosis in burn patients, such as age, comorbidities, inhalation injury, and %TBSA burned, all more common in the VP+ group before matching. Despite this, the VP+ patients were associated with worse outcomes, including higher 30‐day mortality.

This study reflects burn patient treatment across both specialised and non‐specialised burn centres in the U.S. Burn care practices, including thresholds for vasopressor use, fluid resuscitation protocols, and ICU admission criteria, can vary across countries and healthcare systems. Initial burn resuscitation in non‐specialised centres may be less standardised, potentially involving suboptimal fluid resuscitation and inappropriate use of vasopressors. The importance of resuscitating patients adequately with crystalloids before initiating vasopressors has been stressed [21], and is particularly important in the treatment of burn shock, where fluid loss is severe. Administering vasopressors with inadequate intravascular volumes may cause end‐organ hypoperfusion.

The distribution of vasopressors used in the study's patient population is broadly consistent with the limited literature on vasopressor use in burn resuscitation. Norepinephrine, epinephrine, and the combination of norepinephrine and vasopressin were the three most frequently used vasopressors, although the common use of epinephrine was unexpected [6, 7, 13, 20, 22]. The database does not provide information on dosing or infusion duration. As a result, agents typically given as boluses, such as ephedrine, could not be distinguished from those administered via continuous infusion and may have introduced heterogeneity in the exposure group. Additionally, we could not assess vasopressor use prior to admission, such as in the prehospital setting or at referring institutions not registered in TriNetX. This limitation may affect interpretation of outcomes, particularly with regard to early resuscitation and timing of interventions.

Due to data limitations, the specific reasons for initiating vasopressors were unavailable. To mitigate the risk of including patients treated with vasopressors for reasons other than burn‐related hypotension, such as part of intraoperative care or as a tumescent injection with epinephrine before skin harvesting, we examined the data from patients who had vasopressors and general anaesthesia within the first 2 days of hospitalisation. No vasopressor type or combination of vasopressors had a higher frequency of use in this period. Additionally, there was a great variation in the number of patients having local anaesthesia, both for those receiving epinephrine and those receiving other vasopressors; thus, no systematic predictions could be made, and no patients were excluded from the VP+ cohort.

TriNetX harmonises core clinical domains, like diagnoses, procedures, and medications, through universal terminologies to ensure interoperability, performance, and patient privacy. While these codes enable large‐scale analyses, this standardisation precludes the inclusion of heterogeneous chart‐level data and introduces potential biases due to variability in coding accuracy. This limited our ability to assess factors such as burn depth, palliative care status, fluid resuscitation volumes, vasopressor dose and duration, and haemodynamic monitoring. The similar three‐day mortality between the two cohorts makes an overrepresentation of palliative care patients in either cohort less likely. However, the absence of detailed resuscitation data remains a key limitation, as delayed or inadequate fluid resuscitation volumes may have contributed to poor outcomes. The lack of information on vasopressor dosing and duration also prevented evaluation of dose–response relationships. Moreover, the lack of invasive monitoring data or non‐invasive measures of volume responsiveness restricted our ability to evaluate the quality of early haemodynamic management. While these constraints may affect the precision of patient identification and outcome assessment, the large sample size helps mitigate some of the potential impact of misclassification. Future studies aiming to capture precise resuscitation volumes should consider on‐site ETL (Extract, Transform, Load) exports or custom data extracts to capture resuscitation parameters more accurately.

The use of vasopressors in burn resuscitation is an under‐investigated topic, considering the common use of these drugs. Details on fluid volumes, haemodynamic monitoring, and vasopressor dose and duration will be essential to further understand the mechanisms contributing to the observed association of adverse outcomes in burn injury patients who received vasopressors. If our results can be validated by other burn patient registries, considerations should be made to revise treatment strategies for burn patients with inadequate fluid responses. Possibly, reducing the use of vasopressors and returning to more stringent resuscitations with crystalloids alone or considering other fluid sparing options for hypotensive burn patients as a consequence. Furthermore, permissive hypotension for a time might also be considered, but variability during the dynamic resuscitation phase should be analysed first to define when recovery might be expected in the course [39]. While vasopressors share many similar properties, they represent a diverse group of drugs with varying mechanisms of action. Certain drugs and their combinations might be more suitable in this patient population, but specific information on the most effective treatment is not currently available and remains to be elucidated.

Conclusion

In this study, patients who received vasopressors during acute burn resuscitation were associated with adverse outcomes, including early acute kidney injury and an increased 30‐day mortality. The findings indicate that while vasopressors are frequently administered as part of burn resuscitation, their use may adversely affect the clinical condition of burn patients. Whether the adverse outcomes were caused by vasopressors, individual factors in the patients receiving the drugs, or perhaps a combination remains unclear. Addressing these questions would rely on data sources capturing real‐time haemodynamic parameters, precise fluid volumes, vasopressor dose and duration, renal biomarkers, and microcirculatory measurements, ideally obtained prospectively. This information will be essential to investigate the isolated effects of vasopressors and inform recommendations on timing of initiation and dosing thresholds.
