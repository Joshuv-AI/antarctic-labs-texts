A Diagnostic Dilemma in Hepatic Cystic Lesions: Radiological Mimicry of Biliary Cystadenoma and Hydatid Cyst in a Histologically Confirmed Simple Hepatic Cyst
Authors: Aayush Bist, Nishant Pandey, Mukund Raj Joshi, Mukund Raj Joshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHepatic cystic lesions pose diagnostic challenges due to overlapping imaging features, as simple hepatic cysts may mimic biliary cystadenoma or hydatid cysts. We report a 43‐year‐old female with chronic right lower abdominal pain, initially diagnosed with hydatid cyst on ultrasonography and later suspected as biliary cystadenoma on CT and MRI. She underwent laparotomy with cyst excision and cholecystectomy, but histopathology confirmed a simple hepatic cyst. This case highlights the limitations of radiology in differentiating hepatic cystic lesions, the crucial role of histopathology—particularly the absence of ovarian‐like stroma—in establishing diagnosis, and the importance of imaging‐pathology correlation for accurate management and avoiding inappropriate treatment.

Introduction

Cystic liver disease includes a broad spectrum of fluid‐filled lesions within the liver parenchyma, increasingly detected due to widespread use of imaging modalities such as ultrasonography (US), CT, and MRI [1]. These lesions are broadly classified as simple or complex cysts, accurate differentiation of which is essential for diagnosis and treatment planning [1]. Simple hepatic cysts are usually congenital, thin‐walled, and lined by cuboidal epithelium, and may enlarge gradually to cause nonspecific symptoms like abdominal pain or early satiety [1]. In contrast, complex cysts exhibit features such as internal septations, mural thickening, or nodularity, which may raise suspicion for neoplastic conditions like biliary cystadenoma or biliary cystadenocarcinoma [1].

Biliary cystadenoma, a rare hepatic neoplasm, typically presents as a multilocular cyst lined by cuboidal to non‐ciliated columnar epithelium and is most often located intrahepatically [2, 3]. Hydatid cysts are focal liver lesions due to echinococcus granulosus infection where humans are accidentally infected on consuming food contaminated with echinococcal eggs or meat from infected animals such as sheep or cows [4]. On ultrasonography (USG), small hydatid cysts may resemble simple hepatic cysts [4]. Diagnosis of simple hepatic cysts is challenging due to overlapping imaging features with other lesions such as hydatid cysts or biliary cystadenoma [1, 2]. However, imaging plays a pivotal role, as biopsy may risk tumor spread; CT and MRI are particularly helpful in identifying multilocular architecture and septations suggestive of biliary cystadenoma [5, 6, 7]. Differentiating these lesions preoperatively remains a diagnostic dilemma.

Case Presentation

Patient Information

A 43‐year‐old female initially presented to the gynecologist with complaints of chronic dull aching pain localized in the right lower abdomen for the past year. There was no history of fever, nausea, abdominal distension, or burning micturition. She neither smokes nor consumes alcohol but is a known case of hypothyroidism under Tab. Thyronorm—25 mcg once daily.

Clinical Findings

Clinically there was no tenderness. Typical features of liver pathology such as vomiting, loose stool, fever, icterus, and pruritus were absent.

Diagnostic Assessment and Interpretation

The abdominal ultrasonography revealed a localized cystic lesion involving both lobes of the liver showing minimally enhancing thin wall septations within the lesion and is in close relation and abutting both right and left portal venous branch for which she was given a provisional diagnosis of biliary cystadenoma by a radiologist. However, a diagnosis of a hydatid cyst was made based on the presence of a cystic space occupying lesion with a detached membrane and internal septation in the right lobe of the liver measuring 93*49 mm. She was prescribed albendazole twice a day for 6 weeks. After taking the medication for a month, she was asked to perform abdominal CECT.

CECT (Contrast Enhanced Computed Tomography) of abdomen and pelvis findings revealed lobulated cystic lesion of size 7.8 × 7.7 × 5.6 cm (CC × T × AP) expanding in both lobes of liver involving segment V, VIII, IVa and IVb seen in close relation to and abutting both right and left portal venous branches with minimally enhancing thin‐walled septations noted within the lesion. Right and left hepatic arteries were seen coursing posterior to the cystic lesion. The cystic lesion had no enhancing solid component, and no calcification was appreciated (Figure 1). Based on this, she was again given a diagnosis of biliary cystadenoma.

CECT showing lobulated cystic lesion (black arrows) with thin septations (yellow arrows) abutting portal branches, without solid component or calcification, suggestive of biliary cystadenoma.

MRI was planned which revealed correlating findings of abdominal CECT scan showing a lobulated T1 low, T2/T2 fat‐saturated high signal intensity lesion (~8.5 × 7.1 × 5.5 cm) noted in segments V, VII, IVa, and IVb of the liver. The lesion had T2 low signal intensity thin septation within and abuts the right and left branches of the portal vein without luminal narrowing suggesting Biliary Cystadenoma (Figure 2) Tumor markers were negative for Alfa feto‐protein (AFP), CEA, CA 19–9.

MRI (T1W left, T2W right) shows a lobulated (white arrows) cystic liver lesion with thin septations (black arrows) abutting the right and left portal venous branches, suggestive of biliary cystadenoma.

Therapeutic Intervention

Laparotomy and excision of cyst was planned. Given the preoperative suspicion of biliary cystadenoma with malignant potential and its proximity to major portal structures, an open approach was chosen to ensure safe complete excision and intraoperative oncologic assessment. Abdomen was opened with Modified Makuuchi incision. Liver was mobilized, inflow control was taken, cholecystectomy was performed. Cyst was dissected outside its capsule carefully and mobilized from liver parenchyma and vascular interface. Cyst could be removed in total without any untoward effects.

Cyst of about 7*8*4 cm was located at the undersurface of liver abutting segment 4A, 4B, 5 of liver and gallbladder and portal vein bifurcation. It was well capsulated, containing clear fluid. Her postoperative was uneventful. Drain was removed on third day and advised discharge on 4th post op day. All vitals were stable at the time of discharge. The postoperative course was uneventful, with no complications observed, corresponding to Clavien‐Dindo Grade I [8]. Excisional biopsy of the specimen was sent measuring 9.5 × 6.5 × 4 cm. Cut section showed multi‐lobulated cystic spaces measuring 3.6 cm containing watery fluid. The histo‐pathological diagnosis revealed simple cyst. Gall bladder was 5.5 × 2.5 cm. Cut section showed green velvety mucosa (Figure 3).

Post‐laparotomy specimen showing a multilobulated hepatic cyst containing clear fluid and a gallbladder with green velvety mucosa without stones.

The cyst wall was flattened to cuboidal epithelium. Subepithelium showing fibrocollagenous tissues with multiple congested blood vessels, areas of hemorrhage, numerous macrophages, and mild lymphoplasmacytic infiltration. Fibrosis was noted in the cyst wall. No atypical or malignant cells were seen. Gall Bladder mucosa was lined by tall columnar epithelium. Lamina propria showed a few chronic inflammatory cells comprising lymphocytes and plasma cells (Figure 4).

H&E‐stained section showing cuboidal epithelial lining with fibrocollagenous stroma and mild inflammatory infiltration.

Follow‐Up and Outcomes

Upon 1 week, 1 month and 6 months follow up, the patient had no significant issue. She is doing well and the wound site is healthy (Figure 5).

Complete wound healing observed after 1 month.

Patient Perspective

“I was anxious when I heard I might have a liver tumor, but I'm thankful everything turned out to be simple and I'm back to normal.”

Discussion

Simple hepatic cysts are thin‐walled, fluid‐filled cavities lined by epithelium, arising from aberrant bile duct cells [9]. They are often detected by using imaging modalities like US, CT, and MRI [9]. Though usually asymptomatic and left untreated, large hepatic cysts may cause abdominal pain, distension, or compression of adjacent organs, warranting treatment in symptomatic cases [9]. Biliary cystadenoma is a rare hepatic lesion characterized by multilocular cysts lined by cuboidal or nonciliated columnar epithelium [2]. It is seen more commonly in the age group of 16–77 years (mean: 48 years) with a female preponderance of 4:1 approximately [10].

Simple hepatic cysts typically range in size from less than 1 cm to as large as 30 cm and are more commonly located in the right lobe of the liver; the presence of more than 20 hepatic cysts is defined as polycystic liver disease (PLD) [9]. Biliary cystadenoma show large multiloculated with internal septa on gross appearance and a dense cellular fibrostroma surrounds them [11]. The size of cysts ranges between 3.5 and 16 cm (mean: 10.6 cm) [12].

Most of the patients with biliary cystadenoma and hepatic cysts are asymptomatic [9, 13]. Even if the patients show symptoms, they are mostly non‐specific, like abdominal pain, abdominal distension, and abdominal mass [14, 15, 16]. These symptoms usually arise when a cystic tumor compresses surrounding tissue [17]. In this case, the female patient also exhibited similar mild abdominal pain occasionally.

Simple hepatic cysts require no further management unless found to be symptomatic, as per GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) system for grading evidence and strength of recommendations, unlike the biliary cystic neoplasms which may be malignant and demand surgical excision [4]. Thus, it is crucial to accurately differentiate simple hepatic cyst from biliary cystic neoplasm for appropriate management. However, it becomes difficult for a consultant to differentiate between simple hepatic cysts and biliary cystadenomas on the basis of radiological findings only because the findings of two disease entities may overlap. Despite this, four CT findings i.e., upstream bile duct dilatation, coexistence of fewer than three other cysts, presence of perilesional THAD (Transient Hepatic Attenuation Difference) and lesion location in left hepatic lobe can significantly predict the biliary cystadenoma thus ruling out the possibility of simple hepatic cyst. Abdominal CECT of this patient demonstrated a lobulated cystic lesion of liver which was non‐calcified and exhibited thin‐walled septations with minimal enhancement. It was noted to be in close proximity to and abutting both the right and left portal venous branches. The right and left hepatic arteries were observed coursing posterior to the cystic lesion. No solid components or areas of significant enhancement were identified within the lesion. Although imaging suggested biliary cystadenoma, features such as thin septations and multilobulated morphology can overlap with complex simple hepatic cysts, contributing to diagnostic ambiguity. The lesion's proximity to portal venous branches further increased suspicion for neoplastic pathology. This overlap highlights the risk of overinterpretation based on structural imaging alone and underscores the importance of multidisciplinary correlation.

On microscopic examination, biliary cystadenomas are characterized by a lining of cuboidal to columnar epithelium with dense cellular ovarian‐like stroma, but may have undergone changes like flattening, denudation, or pseudo‐stratification at some foci, with some areas of squamous metaplasia [18]. In contrast, simple hepatic cysts lack this mesenchymal component and are lined by a single layer of cuboidal or columnar epithelium resembling biliary epithelium, with an outer wall composed of thin fibrous tissue [9]. Alongside, simple hepatic cysts gradually enlarge due to fluid secretion by the epithelial lining, which can eventually lead to symptoms or complications when the cysts become sufficiently large [9]. In our case, the cyst wall showed flattened cuboidal epithelium with underlying fibrocollagenous tissue, vascular congestion, and inflammatory infiltration—findings consistent with a simple hepatic cyst and notably lacking the ovarian‐like mesenchymal stroma characteristic of biliary cystadenoma.

While histopathology remains the gold standard for definitive diagnosis, percutaneous biopsy has practical limitations in suspected biliary cystic neoplasms. Sampling error may occur due to heterogeneous cyst architecture, and invasive sampling carries potential risks such as cyst rupture, infection, or tumor dissemination. For these reasons, biopsy is often avoided preoperatively when neoplastic pathology is suspected, and diagnosis relies on clinicoradiological assessment followed by definitive surgical excision [1].

In low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), limited access to advanced imaging such as CT and MRI may further contribute to diagnostic errors, leading to unnecessary treatments—as seen in our case where the patient was empirically started on albendazole for suspected hydatid cyst. This underscores the importance of optimizing available diagnostic pathways and applying imaging resources judiciously in resource‐constrained settings, particularly when imaging characteristics are interpreted without sufficient consideration of overlapping benign features.

Strength and Limitations

Limitations include being a single case with short follow‐up, initial misdiagnosis, and lack of molecular analysis. A strength of this report is the comprehensive imaging‐pathology correlation with complete surgical and histological documentation.

Conclusion

This case highlights the diagnostic challenge in differentiating hepatic cystic lesions, where imaging suggested biliary cystadenoma but histopathology confirmed a simple hepatic cyst. Accurate diagnosis requires careful radiological‐pathological correlation to guide appropriate management and avoid unnecessary treatment.
