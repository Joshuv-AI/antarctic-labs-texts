Precise antibody delivery to the brain via nanobubble-actuated focused ultrasound alleviates depression
Authors: Wenjing Li, Yifan Feng, Zhouyang Xu, Xing Xu, Kexin Yang, Xinya Yao, Jia-Ji Pan, Hengxiang Cui, Ji Hu, Bingbing Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceBrain disorders pose significant challenges for precise treatment due to the brain’s inherent heterogeneity. Here, we introduce a nanobubble (NB)-actuated focused ultrasound (FUS) strategy for highly precise blood–brain barrier (BBB) opening and targeted monoclonal autoantibody delivery. By leveraging the superior acoustic properties of NBs, our approach significantly enhances delivery accuracy while minimizing precision loss. We precisely transported antibodies to the lateral habenula in depression-like mice, effectively achieving sustained alleviation of depressive symptoms. This noninvasive method offers the longest-lasting and most targeted antibody delivery among existing therapies, presenting a promising alternative to conventional antidepressants. Moreover, with near-zero detectable hemorrhage, our strategy ensures a high level of safety, reinforcing its potential for clinical translation.

Targeting specific brain regions with high precision holds great promise for achieving therapeutic success in treating brain disorders while minimizing side effects. Recently, the lateral habenula (LHb) has emerged as a key brain region in the regulation of mood and motivation and serves as a promising target for treating depression (1–3). Ketamine has been demonstrated to achieve long-term antidepression effects by blocking N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors (NMDAR) signaling (4–6), and the LHb was highlighted as the initial site for this mechanism (3). Notably, in the human brain, both medial and lateral habenula together occupy approximately 72 mm3, constituting around 0.6‱ of the overall brain mass (7, 8). Given its diminutive size and the complexity of its connections, precision in targeting the LHb is essential for the effective modulation of its activity in both research and clinical interventions (9).

Compared to traditional antidepressant compounds, antibodies possess highly specific targets, making them promising candidates for potential therapeutics. Human-derived anti-NMDAR monoclonal antibodies (HuMAbs) have been experimentally validated for their specificity against a singular antigen. They effectively attach to NMDARs, leading to their reversible endocytosis (10). However, antibodies are difficult to pass the blood–brain barrier (BBB) (11). The BBB protects the brain from potential toxins and pathogens in the bloodstream, but also restricts the delivery of therapeutics to the brain (11). Current strategies for augmenting drug permeation through the BBB, encompassing both physical and chemical approaches (12–15), often face challenges such as increased intracranial pressure, invasiveness, and/or limited absorption.

Focused ultrasound (FUS) combined with microbubbles (MBs) has been demonstrated to open the BBB in a noninvasive, localized, and reversible manner (16–19). Preclinical studies have demonstrated that therapeutic agents such as genes, peptides, or other pharmacological agents can be delivered into the brain for the treatment of neurodegenerative disease (20–23). Several ongoing clinical studies are investigating the safety and efficacy of FUS-MB-induced BBB opening in patients of brain disorders (24–27). However, achieving precise control over the extent and location of BBB opening still remains a challenge. Our previous studies demonstrated nanobubbles (NBs) have promising acoustic properties in terms of stability, circulation time, and persistence under ultrasound exposures compared to traditional MBs (28–30). The FUS frequency used for BBB opening is usually less than 2 MHz (20–23, 26–32). The resonant frequency of NBs (~20.5 MHz for a 280 nm NB) is much higher than that of MBs (~2.3 MHz for a 2.5 μm MB) according to the Newtonian rheology (33). Based on the assumption that bubbles with a resonant frequency further away from FUS frequency respond less intensely, we hypothesize that NBs can achieve a more precise BBB disruption (smaller volume) compared to traditional MBs under the same ultrasound exposures. This precision is due to NBs having a smaller region responding to the FUS exposure compared to MBs. Therefore, these acoustic profiles suggest that FUS-NB-mediated BBB opening could achieve superior precision in drug delivery while minimizing side effects, positioning it as a promising candidate for clinical translation requiring high-resolution neuromodulation.

This study aims to precisely and noninvasively deliver the antibodies to the specific region via NB-assisted FUS BBB opening, enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of macromolecular therapy while minimizing side effects. The enhanced precision of our FUS-NB approach is supported by both numerical simulation and in vivo experiments. We characterized the delivery efficiency for drugs with various molecular weights (MW), opening–closure time, and multitarget capability of NB-induced BBB opening. Drug delivery with this approach showed precise coverage and sustained HuMAbs level in the targeted LHb region, which successfully reversed the depression-like behaviors in a chronic restraint stress (CRS) mouse model.

Results

NBs Have Superior Acoustic Properties for FUS BBB Opening.

The schematic diagram illustrates the composition of custom-made NBs and commercial MBs (Fig. 1A). Particle size distribution curves show that the average diameter of NBs is 273.2 ± 12.91 nm with a polydispersity index of 0.274 ± 0.062, while the average diameter of MBs is 994.8 ± 71.3 nm (Fig. 1B). To analyze the acoustic response of bubbles, NBs and MBs were injected into a tube phantom immersed in degassed water (Fig. 1C). Acoustic signals collected by the hydrophone indicated that the acoustic emission amplitudes for NBs (1.04 ± 0.04) and MBs (1.04 ± 0.41) were similar at a focal pressure of 0.35 MPa. However, when the focal pressure was exceeded 0.5 MPa, NBs exhibited significantly stronger acoustic emissions compared to MBs (P < 0.0001). Moreover, NBs presented a more stable acoustic performance than MBs, with a smaller SD of measured acoustic signals (Fig. 1D).

NB-Actuated FUS BBB Opening Enables Precise Brain Delivery.

The schematic diagram in Fig. 1E illustrates the numerical simulation geometry utilized for acoustic simulation. The simulated ultrasound field showed that the FUS focal size was 0.9 × 0.9 × 3.93 mm (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The mean relative peak radius change (∆R) of both NBs and MBs was calculated under different FUS pressures at a fundamental frequency of 1.5 MHz (Inset in Fig. 1F). The simulated response area was defined as the region that NBs or MBs influence when subjected to acoustic stimulation, and it was determined by the mean relative peak radius change. NBs exhibited a smaller simulated response area and mean peak radius change compared to MBs (Fig. 1F). When the peak negative pressure at the focus of the simulated ultrasound field was 0.35 MPa, the simulated response area of NBs was calculated and integrated with the Allen Mouse Common Coordinate Framework (34). The corresponding MB response area is 340.26% larger than that of NBs in the coronal plane and 639.52% larger in the transverse plane (Fig. 1G).

The schematic diagram depicts the experimental setup of FUS BBB opening used in this work (Fig. 2A). To evaluate the delivery precision of FUS BBB opening actuated by NBs/MBs, comparative experiments were conducted following the animal experimental timeline shown in Fig. 2B. Coronal and transverse planes targeting the ventral hippocampus (vHPC) were selected, with focal pressures of 0.28, 0.35, or 0.7 MPa applied. In vivo Evans Blue (EB) extravasation assay was performed to measure the BBB permeability after FUS BBB opening. The fluorescent images demonstrated that the EB leakage was confined to the FUS focal region in the NB group, while additional EB leakage, indicative of side-lobe BBB opening, was observed in the MB group at 0.35 MPa (Fig. 2 C and D). Precision loss was calculated to quantify the delivery precision, with a small value representing high precision (details in Materials and Methods). The statistical analysis of fluorescence intensity in the brain sections (Fig. 2 E and F) indicated that precision loss was significantly lower in the NB group compared to the MB group, achieving drug delivery precision improvements of up to fourfolds (coronal: 3.26 ± 2.51 vs. 12.64 ± 0.81 P = 0.004; transverse: 1.83 ± 0.8 vs. 7.19 ± 0.52, P = 0.001). When the focal pressure was reduced by 20% below conventional therapeutic levels, the side-lobe BBB opening effect caused by MBs diminished, but the precision loss remained lower than that of NBs (3.73 ± 1.69 vs. 7.04 ± 1.72, P = 0.08). At 0.7 MPa, which is twice our pressure setting, both NBs and MBs exhibited side-lobe BBB opening effects, with no significant difference in precision loss between the two groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S2).

These results were further validated in the anterior pretectal nucleus (APtN) of mouse brains (Fig. 2 G and H), revealing a 2.72-fold improvement in precision compared to MBs. NB-Actuated FUS BBB Opening Precisely Delivered Macromolecules with Multitarget Capability.

The FITC-Dextran of different MW (70, 150, and 500 kDa) was used to assess the delivery efficiency of FUS-NB BBB opening. As shown in Fig. 3B, less FITC-Dextran was delivered to the targeted vHPC with a larger FITC-Dextran MW. The normalized optical density of leaked FITC-Dextran at the FUS target decreased by 41.06% when the FITC-Dextran MW increased from 70 to 150 kDa (Fig. 3C, 2.07 ± 0.42 × 107 vs. 1.22 ± 0.15 × 107, P = 0.02). For the 500 kDa FITC-Dextran, FUS-NB-mediated BBB opening only achieved its delivery to the targeted brain region in a sparse and scattered manner with a normalized optical density of 0.16 ± 0.06 × 107.

The BBB opening–closure time was evaluated at right vHPC by quantifying the EB leakage at different administration time points after the FUS treatment (Fig. 3 A, Right Bottom). The fluorescence intensity of EB in the treated region decreased with the delay of its administration (Fig. 3D). At 6 h, almost no EB fluorescence signal was observed. As shown in Fig. 3E, the normalized optical density significantly reduced with the time increase (0 h: 17.14 ± 1.41 × 107; 2 h: 8.64 ± 0.76 × 107; 6 h: 0.66 ± 0.56 × 107). Based on Fig. 3E, we estimated that the half-life of the FUS-NB BBB opening was approximately 2 h.

To assess the effectiveness of multitarget BBB opening with a single bolus injection of NBs, representative fluorescence images of EB leakage were captured (Fig. 3F). Six brain targets, symmetrically distributed across both hemispheres to ensure a consistent gray-white matter composition, were randomly selected. The FUS-BBB opening treatments were performed in the order of the target numbers, and obvious EB leakage was observed in all six targets (Fig. 3G). The normalized optical density of leaked EB in the target regions decreased gradually following the order of FUS treatment (Fig. 3H, target 1: 21.77 ± 15.39 × 107; target 4: 8.42 ± 8.57 × 107; target 2: 17.22 ± 9.75 × 107; target 5: 4.82 ± 3.76 × 107; target 3: 10.06 ± 5.24 × 107; target 6: 4.76 ± 5.03 × 107), indicating the clearance of NBs from the mouse brains.

FUS-NB BBB Opening Precisely Delivered Antibodies into LHb.

To determine the appropriate delivery strategy of HuMAbs for treating depression-like mice, experiments with three different FUS-NB BBB opening protocols were conducted (Fig. 4A). After a single intravenous injection of HuMAbs, the amount of HuMAbs delivered to the LHb (Fig. 4B). On the contrary, the isotype antibodies, mouse-derived anti-NMDAR monoclonal antibodies (MoMAbs), remained at a low level in the three FUS-treated groups (Fig. 4B). The level of HuMAbs in the mouse brain in the three-time FUS-treated group, measured by the normalized optical density, showed an approximately fourfold increase compared to the one-time FUS-treated group (LHb: 5.45 ± 1.26 × 106 vs. 1.36 ± 0.33 × 106, P = 0.0001) and twofold increase compared to the two-time FUS-treated group (5.45 ± 1.26 × 106 vs. 2.56 ± 0.90 × 106, P = 0.003) (Fig. 4C). The FUS-treated brain tissues generally appeared to be unaffected, except for a few very minor red blood cells (RBCs) extravasation in the treated hemisphere (Fig. 4D). Quantitative analysis revealed that the largest hemorrhage area in one slide of the LHb region was approximately 2.1 mm2, accounting for only 0.9‱ of the calculated total area. Furthermore, no significant differences in RBC leakage areas were observed among one-time, two-time, and three-time FUS treatments (Fig. 4E).

LHb Antibody Delivery Sustainably Mitigated Depression-Like Behaviors in Mice.

A CRS-induced depression-like mouse model was established after 14 d and validated by behavioral testing (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). Tail suspension test (TST) and forced swim test (FST) showed that CRS mice increased the immobile duration significantly compared to naïve mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B, TST: 146.1 ± 25.49 s vs. 117.8 ± 22.82 s, P = 0.03; FST: 177.2 ± 19.66 s vs. 150.7 ± 21.09 s, P = 0.01). In addition, the sucrose preference test (SPT) indicated a significantly reduced preference in the CRS mice compared to the naïve mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B, 72.1 ± 13.3% vs. 85.6 ± 9.57%, P = 0.03).

Six groups of mice were evaluated: CRS, Naïve, CRS+FUS, CRS+FUS+MoMAbs, CRS+HuMAbs, and CRS+FUS+HuMAbs, to eliminate the influence of BBB opening, isotype antibodies administration, and HuMAbs injection. The therapeutic effect of a single dose of HuMAbs combined with three-time FUS-NB BBB opening was evaluated in the CRS mice (Fig. 5A). Fluorescence images of the brain slices obtained 10 d after the antibodies injection demonstrated that the HuMAbs were successfully delivered to the bilateral LHb regions and remained in the brain for at least 10 d (Fig. 5 B and C).

Behavioral study showed that CRS+FUS+HuMAbs mice had reduced the immobile duration in the TST [F (5, 52) = 8.7984, P < 0.0001] and FST [F (5, 52) = 7.12652, P < 0.0001], and increased the sucrose preference in the SPT [F (5, 52) = 8.53782, P < 0.0001] compared to the CRS group. In contrast, mice receiving only HuMAbs, FUS-NB BBB opening alone, or mice receiving MoMAbs with FUS-treated exhibited clear depression symptoms compared to the naïve mice. Additionally, compared to the CRS+FUS+HuMAbs group, CRS+FUS and CRS+FUS+MoMAbs mice exhibited longer immobility in the TST, CRS+FUS+MoMAbs mice had prolonged immobility in the FST, and sucrose preference remained unchanged in the CRS+FUS+MoMAbs and CRS+HuMAbs groups in the SPT (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, we examined the duration of HuMAbs retention and the time course of therapeutic effect decline (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). Specifically, the normalized optical density of HuMAbs in the brain was significantly lower on day 15 compared to day 10 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B, P = 0.04), suggesting progressive antibody clearance. Notably, CRS+FUS+HuMAbs mice still maintained significant behavioral improvements in helplessness tests on day 15 compared to the CRS group (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C, TST: 107.38 ± 32.25 vs. 150.39 ± 20.48, P = 0.007; FST: 132.13 ± 36.72 vs. 172.67 ± 29.06, P = 0.03). Improvement in anhedonia-like behavior was also observed at this time point (SPT: 73.66 ± 6.80 vs. 63.36 ± 7.31, P = 0.06). By day 25, these behavioral benefits were no longer evident, indicating that the therapeutic effects of HuMAbs combined with FUS-NB BBB opening were transient and correlated with antibody presence in the brain (SI Appendix, Fig. S5D). These findings indicate that HuMAbs combined with three-time FUS-NB BBB opening effectively alleviated the depression-like behaviors in CRS mice at least 2 wk after HuMAbs administration.

Discussion

In this study, we leveraged the unique acoustic properties of NBs and demonstrated that FUS-NB-mediated BBB opening can precisely, noninvasively, and safely deliver antibodies into the key brain area for depression treatment. This targeted delivery system is designed to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of antibody treatments while minimizing side effects. Multiple FUS-NB BBB opening in LHb combined with antibodies i.v. administration effectively reversed the depression-like behaviors in CRS mice for at least 2 wk.

LHb has emerged as a pivotal brain region implicated in the pathophysiology of depression, with its hyperactivity being closely associated with the onset and maintenance of depressive symptoms (1, 3, 4). Precise drug delivery to the LHb holds significant potential for treating depression. By targeting the LHb specifically, we can maximize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing side effects on other brain regions, thereby preserving vital cognitive and motor functions (35). Given the tiny size of the LHb, achieving precise targeting for therapeutic intervention has been a formidable challenge. Here, we have successfully navigated this complexity through the development of a noninvasive approach utilizing FUS combined with NBs. Previously, we have demonstrated that NBs have longer in vitro persistence, in vivo circulation time, and more consistent harmonic emissions, compared to MBs (28, 29). In this study, we further illustrated that NBs can produce stronger in vitro acoustic emissions compared to MBs under the condition of the same gas volume (Fig. 1). The numerical simulation (Fig. 1) and in vivo experiments (Fig. 2) supported our hypothesis that NBs can achieve a more precise BBB disruption pattern within the ultrasound beam. Notably, MBs exhibited significant side-lobe BBB opening, leading to unintended drug delivery beyond the targeted region (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). This effect was not only at the therapeutic pressure in our study but also at 0.28 MPa, a commonly used lower limit in previous studies (36). In contrast, NBs maintained precise BBB opening across different pressure conditions, further demonstrating their advantage in targeted drug delivery (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). One possible explanation is that the resonant frequency of NBs is further away from the FUS driving frequency, therefore having a smaller responsive region to FUS exposure compared to MBs. Our results are also aligned with a previous study, in which the authors reported that MBs can generate cavitation activity in the sidelobes of the acoustic field with reduced ultrasound pressure (37). The mechanical force induced by the bubble cavitation activity was believed to be the main mechanism for FUS-mediated BBB opening (38). Compared to MBs, our FUS-NB approach ensured a more precise BBB opening at the FUS target with less side effect drug delivery and similar opening uniformity (Figs. 1 and 2 and SI Appendix, Figs. S2 and S3).

The HuMAbs, a specific NMDAR antibody, effectively induce the reversible internalization of NMDARs, holding great promise for the depression treatment. In conventional treatments for CRS-induced depression in mice, at least 2 wk of continuous administration is typically required, with antidepressant effects that are short-lived (4), uncertain in duration (39–41), or accompanied by multiple side effects (42). In 2018, Yang et al. demonstrated that the administration of NMDAR antagonists, such as ketamine and AP5, significantly reverses depression-like behavior, offering a new route for treating depression (1). Traditional NMDAR antagonists often affect multiple targets and carry various risks associated with their complex mechanisms of action (1, 43). In contrast, HuMAbs is a highly specific NMDAR antibody with a longer half-life compared to small molecule drugs, maintaining its effects for at least 5 d (44). While promising, its MW is around 150 kDa, preventing it from entering the brain parenchyma due to the presence of the BBB. Our data showed that the FUS-NB BBB opening approach is able to deliver drugs with MW ranging from 70 to 500 kDa with a half-life of 2 h (Fig. 3). Numerous studies have demonstrated that multiple deliveries of anti-NMDAR drugs can effectively reverse depressive symptoms (45–47). We demonstrated that three-time FUS-NB BBB openings combined with only one-time HuMAbs i.v. injection could achieve optimal drug accumulation in the LHb region (Fig. 4) and successfully alleviate the depressive symptoms in mice (Fig. 5). Unsurprisingly, the residual fluorescence intensity of HuMAbs after each FUS treatment was significantly higher than that of the MoMAbs. Our data further demonstrated that following delivery via FUS-NB BBB opening, the HuMAbs successfully remained in the brain for at least 10 d.

Behavioral improvements were observed through the assessment of helplessness behaviors (TST, FST) and anhedonia behavior (SPT) after multiple FUS exposures with one-time HuMAbs administration. Enhanced delivery of HuMAbs significantly elicited antidepressant effects (TST: P < 0.05, FST: P < 0.01, SPT: P = 0.16; Tukey’s post hoc test) in CRS mice. Notably, our experiments confirmed that neither BBB opening alone, HuMAbs administration alone, nor the delivery of isotype antibodies after the same FUS treatment did not alleviate depression symptoms, further validating the efficacy of our treatment (Fig. 5D). The absence of significant differences between some control groups and the Naïve group in the FST may be attributed to transient BBB permeability changes induced by circadian rhythms or cavitation, allowing limited entry of molecules like brain-derived neurotrophic factor or HuMAbs to influence behavior (24, 48–50). To further investigate the duration of the antidepressant effect, we conducted behavioral assessments at 15- and 30 d posttreatment. Significant antidepressant effects were observed on day 15 following the initial FUS treatment, as evidenced by improved outcomes in both the TST and FST, along with partially improved SPT performance (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 C and D), highlighting the sustained therapeutic potential of this approach compared to traditional methods (4, 39–41). Consistent with the behavioral findings, we observed a significant reduction in surface NMDAR expression in the LHb region following FUS+HuMAbs treatment, with NMDAR density restored to levels comparable to those in naïve mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Electrophysiological recordings further demonstrated that the proportion of burst-type neurons decreased from 24.0% in CRS mice to 5.3% after treatment, accompanied by a reduction in burst-mode spikes and normalization of interspike intervals (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Additionally, we confirmed that the delivered antibodies were nearly undetectable in the brain 15 d after administration (SI Appendix, Fig. S5), supporting the observed decline in therapeutic effects over time. Our results align with previous studies (1), which demonstrated that blocking NMDAR with antibodies prevented its function, thereby interrupting the bursting activity of LHb neurons and alleviating excessive inhibition on downstream reward regions. This restored the mice’s survival instinct and ability to experience pleasure.

We also demonstrated that the FUS-NB BBB opening is safe and well-tolerated. We did not observe any edema or hemorrhage occurrence following one-time or three-time FUS-NB BBB opening, except for a few very minor RBC extravasation (Fig. 4). The brain’s immune-privileged status and limited immune response to cross-species antibody delivery, together with our behavioral and survival data showing unaffected general locomotion (SI Appendix, Fig. S8), suggest a low risk of neurological immune-related adverse events and minimal confounding by locomotor activity on immobility measurements. In addition, we collected acoustic emissions from stimulated NBs during the FUS BBB opening treatment with a hydrophone inserted within the therapeutic transducer. From our acoustic feedback signal analysis for the FUS treatment, no broadband emission (indicator of potential tissue damage) was observed in NB-assisted BBB opening, which further supported the safety of our technique (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). This safety profile, coupled with the enhanced drug delivery precision and efficiency, positions our approach as a promising strategy for the treatment of depression as well as other brain disorders, with clinical translation potential.

In summary, our study leverages capabilities of NBs and FUS, addressing the complexities of targeted drug delivery to the important but small brain regions, which lays the foundation for precise and noninvasive therapies for brain disorders.

Materials and Methods

Animals and Ethics Statement.

Adult male C57BL/6J mice (7 to 8 wk old) weighing 20 to 22 g were purchased from Shanghai Jihui Laboratory Animal Care Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China. All rodents were housed in a temperature-controlled facility with a 12:12-h light–dark cycle. Food and water were supplied ad libitum. All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of ShanghaiTech University (ethical approval number: 20220630001). Animal experiments were conducted strictly following the ARRIVE guidelines. Notably, female mice were excluded from the animal experiments for consistency. All possible efforts were made to minimize the incidence of animal infection and alleviate suffering.

NB Synthesis and Activation.

NBs were synthesized based on our previously published protocol with minor modifications (28). Briefly, DPPC, DPPA, DPPE, and DSPE-mPEG were dissolved in 10 mL chloroform with a mass ratio of 5:1:2:1 in a glass vial. The sample was kept in the shaker at 42 °C for 10 to 20 min. Then, 1 mL of the lipid solution was transferred into 3 mL vials and placed in a vacuum chamber for 24 h to ensure complete solvent evaporation. Then 1 mL of 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.6 mg mL−1 Pluronic L61 was added to each vial. The vials were capped, sealed, and heated at 80 °C for 30 min. After hydration, the air was removed by suction with a syringe needle, and then replaced it with C3F8 gas. To activate the bubble, the vials were shaken at 4,500 rpm for 45 s. Then they were put in the centrifuge upside-down at 50 g force for 5 min. Finally, the NB solution was carefully drawn out from the bottom of the vial using a 27-gauge syringe.

Bubble Size Measurement.

The particle size distribution of the NBs and MBs was characterized using a DLS instrument (ZETASIZER LAB, Malvern Panalytical, Worcestershire, UK). The saline used to dilute the NB solution was prefiltered using a 0.22 μm mixed cellulose ester syringe filter (02036322-TYLQ-0005, Shanghai Titan Scientific Co., Ltd). 1 mL of diluted NB solution (dilution factor: 500) was loaded into the 10 × 10 plastic cell (DTS0012, Malvern Panalytical). Measurements were conducted at 25 °C with an equilibration time of 1 s. The general-purpose analysis model was selected with side scatter detection to enhance the stability of the measurements. The particle size distribution of the MBs was measured in a similar manner.

Acoustic Field Characterization.

A tube phantom was designed to investigate the acoustic response of both NBs and MBs under ultrasound exposures at different pressures. A silicon tube with an inner diameter of 0.58 mm and an outer diameter of 0.99 mm was placed in the middle of a plastic water container. The NBs were diluted with a factor of 3 to reach the same gas volume of MBs (51). The bubbles were infused using an infusion pump (Longer, Hebei, China) at a fixed rate of 20 μL min−1 to refresh the bubble population within the focal volume of the ultrasound after each pulse. During pressure sweep, the focal pressure was increased from 0.3 to 1.1 MPa with a step size of 0.05 to 0.1 MPa. At each pressure level, three waveforms were acquired and analyzed. The area under the curve (AUC) values were calculated by summing the amplitude at the ultraharmonic frequency (0.75 MHz) within a 300 kHz bandwidth. The averaged AUC of three waveforms represented the acoustic emission at this specific pressure level.

Simulation.

To simulate the FUS propagation in the mouse brain, the Helmholtz equation was used Eq. (1):[1]∇·-1ρ∇pt-qd-keq2ptρc=Qm,keq2=ωcc2, cc=ωc, k=ωc-iα, ρc=ρc2cc2,

where ρ and c are the density and the speed of sound of the medium, respectively. pt is the acoustic pressure, ω is the angular frequency, i is the imaginary unit, α is the attenuation constant of the medium, and qd and Qm are the dipole domain source and monopole domain source, respectively. qd and Qm were set to 0 in this simulation. The acoustic pressure on the surface of the transducer was calculated by pt = PFUS sin(2π fFUS t). The transducer parameters were 24.112 mm radius, 34.92 mm diameter, 1.5 MHz center frequency (fFUS), and 9.4 kPa driving pressure (PFUS), while the medium parameters are listed in SI Appendix, Table S1. The spherical transducer with a diameter of 34.92 mm and curvature radius of 24.112 mm was placed at the bottom of the model (Fig. 1E). A layer of water (light blue) was placed in between the transducer and the brain tissue (red region) for acoustic coupling. The outermost layer of the model is the perfect matching layer (gray).

MBs were assumed to maintain spherical shape during oscillations. Rayleigh–Plesset model was implemented to describe the changes in the bubble radius under ultrasound exposures (52) Eq. (2):[2]ρRR¨+32ρR˙2=P0+2σR0-PVR0R3κ-4μR˙R-2σR+PV-P0-PACt,

where ρ is the density of the medium, R = R(t) is the instantaneous radius of bubbles, and R˙ and R¨ are the first and the second-order derivative of R. R0 is the initial radius of bubble agents, P0 and PV are the hydrostatics and saturated water vapor pressure, PAC(t) is the acoustic pressure at the bubble location, κ is the polytropic exponent, μ is blood viscosity, and σ is the surface tension coefficient of bubble agents. The parameters of the bubble agents are shown in SI Appendix, Table S2.

A series of independent bubble dynamics simulations was implemented using varying amplitudes of PAC(t) to determine the response of MBs and NBs. The amplitudes were tested from 0.01 to 0.4 MPa, with a step of 0.01 MPa from 0.01 to 0.04 MPa, and a step of 0.05 MPa from 0.05 to 0.4 MPa. For each acoustic pressure condition, the peak values of the time-varying relative radius change were identified. The mean value of these peaks, specific to each pressure and bubble type, characterized the level of bubble oscillation. The response regions of MBs and NBs were determined by the threshold of the mean peak values of the relative radius change.

FUS BBB Opening.

FUS BBB opening was performed using a stereotactic-guided FUS system (RK50, FUS Instruments, Toronto, ON, CA). Acoustic parameters of FUS BBB opening were set as follows: peak negative pressure = 0.35 MPa, pulse length = 10 ms, pulse repetition frequency = 1 Hz, and number of pulses = 120. The properties of concave ultrasonic transducer were described as follows: diameter = 35 mm, radius = 24.5 mm, and center frequency = 1.5 MHz. Acoustic emissions from FUS-stimulated bubbles were monitored and collected by a hydrophone integrated into the middle of the transducer (resonant frequency 0.75 MHz).

During FUS BBB opening, mice were maintained in anesthesia with 1.5% isoflurane in a 30% O2/70% N2 mixture. A temperature-controllable heating pad was used to maintain the mouse body temperature at 37 ± 0.5 °C during the FUS treatment. The hair of mice was removed using depilation cream (Nair, Church & Dwight Co., Inc.), and the skull was exposed to identify the bregma and lambda. Following the insertion of a custom-built 27-gauge intravenous catheter into the lateral tail vein, the mice were transferred and secured onto a stereotaxic apparatus. FUS targets were localized utilizing landmark registration to a mouse atlas, after which MBs (2.5 μL g−1) or NBs (1.55 μL g−1) were intravenously administered to the mice while simultaneously receiving a 2-min FUS exposure. The infusion rate for all FUS BBB opening experiments was calculated based on the bubble dosage.

In Vivo Evaluation of FUS BBB Opening.

In group I, the right APtN and vHPC were selected as FUS targets to compare the BBB opening efficacy of NBs and MBs. An In vivo EB leakage assay was conducted to evaluate BBB permeability. In brief, 2% EB (3.33 mL kg−1) was injected into mice via the tail vein after the FUS exposure and allowed to circulate for 30 min (28). The frozen sections of mouse brain tissues were used for fluorescence imaging of EB leakage.

In group II, FUS-NB BBB opening delivered 70, 150, and 500 kDa FITC-Dextran (2 mg diluted in 100 μL saline, Shanghai Titan Scientific Co., Ltd) to the right vHPC of mouse brains (53). Mice were killed 30 min after the FUS treatment to quantify the delivery efficiency of different MW substances. To verify the duration of FUS-induced BBB opening, mice that received FUS treatment targeting the right vHPC were intravenously injected with 2% EB immediately. Mouse brain tissues collected at 0, 2, and 6 h after FUS exposure were evaluated. To evaluate the capability of multitarget FUS BBB opening with a single bolus injection of NBs, mice received FUS exposures at six randomly selected brain regions. EB leakage was measured to assess the BBB permeability.

In group III, the FUS target was the right LHb. All mice received MoMAbs/HuMAbs (2 mg mL−1, 50 μL per mouse) via the tail vein only after the first FUS treatment on day 1 (44). They were assigned to received a total of one-, two-, or three-time FUS exposures, with the additional exposures performed on days 3 and 5 (Fig. 4A) (54). Mice were killed 1 d after the last FUS treatment to evaluate the residual drugs. H&E staining was performed to assess the safety of FUS-NB BBB opening.

Depression-Like Mouse Model.

For modeling depression-like behaviors in rodents, adult male C57BL/6J mice were subjected to a 2-wk CRS (55). Briefly, individual mice were placed in a customized 50 mL plastic tube with adequate ventilation. Mice were kept completely immobile in these tailored tubes to induce restraint stress. The CRS mice received a random 3 h restraint stress daily between 11 AM and 7 PM, followed by returning to their housing conditions. The control mice underwent the same operation, but without receiving restraint stress. After 2 wk, depression symptoms of the CRS mouse were evaluated by behavioral testing.

FUS Intravenous Delivery of Antibodies to CRS Mouse Brains.

The HuMAbs and MoMAbs were generously provided by Shujia Zhu’s laboratory at the Institute of Neuroscience, Center for Excellence in Brain Science and Intelligence Technology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. For HuMAbs, Fluorescence-Activated Cell Sorting was used to isolate single immune B cells from anti-NMDAR encephalitis patients. Monoclonal antibodies against NMDARs were cloned, identified, and recombinantly expressed in HEK293T human embryonic kidney cells. CRS mice received three-time FUS exposures on alternate days, targeting bilateral LHb with a single bolus injection of NB solution (Fig. 5A). Immediately following the first FUS BBB opening, HuMAbs (2 mg mL−1, 50 μL per mouse) were intravenously injected into CRS mice. Subsequently, behavioral tests were conducted to assess anhedonia and helplessness 3 d after the final FUS exposure. For MoMAbs, BALB/c mice were actively immunized with tetrameric NMDAR proteins, and the monoclonal antibodies were obtained using the hybridoma technique and live-cell-based immunostaining. These MoMAbs were administered following the same surgical protocol as HuMAbs to ensure procedural consistency. Designed to bind NMDARs without triggering receptor internalization, these antibodies served as an isotype antibody control. Sham-operated mice were either received the same FUS exposures without drug delivery procedures or received HuMAbs without FUS exposure. Naïve mice, which were not subjected to CRS or anesthesia, served as the normal control group. After completing the behavioral tests, the mice were killed the following day to evaluate the residual drug distribution.

Behavioral Testing.

All behavioral testing was conducted by an investigator blinded to experimental manipulations. TST, FST, and SPT were performed to assess depression-like behaviors in CRS mice according to standard protocols (56).

For the TST, mice were gently secured by fastening their tails to a rail positioned 50 cm above the ground using adhesive tape, preventing any additional contact or climbing during the test. Following the tape application, a 6-min video recording was initiated, and the duration of immobility in the final 4 min was measured.

For the FST, mice were familiarized with swimming 1 d prior to the test. They were placed in a 5,000 mL beaker filled with approximately 2,500 mL of water maintained at 27 °C. A 6-min video recording commenced, and the duration of immobility in the last 4 min was recorded.

For the SPT, mice were individually housed and provided with a single water bottle 4 d prior to the experiment. On the second day, a second bottle containing a 2% sucrose solution was introduced. To control for spatial memory effects, the positions of the two bottles were swapped on the third day. Subsequently, the mice were water-food-deprived for 24 h and then exposed to one bottle of 2% sucrose solution and one bottle of water for 2 h. An hour later, the positions of the bottles were switched. The sucrose preference ratio was calculated by dividing the consumption of sucrose by the total consumption of both bottles.

Histology and Image Analysis.

Anesthetized mice were transcardially perfused with saline and 4% paraformaldehyde (30525-89-4, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent). Mouse brains were postfixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, followed by dehydration in 30% sucrose (57-50-1, Adamas) for about 24 h. OCT-embedded brain tissues were sectioned using a cryostat (Leica CM3050S, Germany). To evaluate BBB permeability after FUS treatment, mice were intravenously injected with EB (314-13-6, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent) or FITC-Dextran (60842-46-8, Adamas) immediately after FUS BBB opening. Then, 40-μm-thick brain sections were collected at the FUS target regions. To evaluate the level of FUS-delivered antibodies in mouse brains, 40-μm-thick coronal sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-human (1:1,000, ThermoFisher) for 1 h at room temperature. Coverslips were mounted on slides using antifade mounting medium with DAPI (041045257, Adamas) for fluorescence imaging. To identify whether FUS BBB opening induced tissue damage, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) solution (60524ES60, Yeasen) was used to stain 10-μm-thick brain sections following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, VS120, Japan). Regions of interest (ROIs) around left (control) and right (treated) APtN and vHPC (4 × 4 mm region and 1 mm diameter circle or 4 × 1 mm oval target region, 795 × 783 mm/pixel resolution) were selected using Image J 1.54f (NIH, USA), the white and yellow dashed area shown in Fig. 2. The ROIs around bilateral LHb on each section were manually outlined (Figs. 4 and 5). The opening uniformity was quantified by calculating the average ratio of the variance to the mean of effective values surrounding the target regions across two to three brain slices. A value was considered effective when the variance around the target region exceeds twice the variance around nontarget regions (57). The NOD was the sum of the target region minus the sum of the nontargeted region. According to the ultrasound focal region and the actual area of BBB opening, the precision loss was defined and calculated using Eq. 3:[3]Precision loss=1n∑i=1nAreatheoretical-Areaactual2,

where n is the number of brain slices, Areatheoretical is the target region, and the Areaactual is the actual BBB opening area.

The fluorescence intensity of various brain regions (group I) was calculated by Pmod4.3 (PMOD Technologies LLC). The fluorescence intensity in distinct brain regions was calculated automatically following the manual selection of the target section.

To quantify RBC leakage, brain sections were imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, VS120, Japan). ROIs centered on the LHb were manually selected for analysis using MATLAB (version R2020b, MathWorks) to detect and quantify extravasated RBCs. Images were converted to the LAB color space, and intensity thresholds were applied to isolate RBC regions. For each ROI, masks were generated by combining thresholding of the L (luminance) and B (blue-yellow) channels to identify RBC-specific pixels. The total area of RBC extravasation was then calculated by summing the pixel area within the masks. This automated approach ensured precise and reproducible quantification of RBC leakage in the LHb regions.

Statistical Analysis.

All data were analyzed with OriginPro software (version 2023b, Northampton, MA). Data were presented as means ± SD. Gaussian distribution of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. For data with Gaussian distribution, two-tailed Student’s t test was used for comparison of two groups of data, while one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for comparison of multiple groups of data. The acoustic response amplitudes of two bubble agents at various ultrasonic pressures were analyzed using two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The sample sizes, specific statistical tests used, and main effects of our statistical analyses for each experiment are reported in SI Appendix, Table S3.
