DNA methylation status of SHATI/NAT8L promoter in the blood of cigarette smokers
Authors: Naotaka Izuo, Hajime Miyanishi, Daisuke Nishizawa, Takuma Fujii, Junko Hasegawa, Naomi Sato, Fumihiko Tanioka, Haruhiko Sugimura, Kazutaka Ikeda, Atsumi Nitta
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsCigarette smoking is a preventable risk factor for various diseases such as cancer, ischemic stroke, cardiac stroke, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Smoking cessation is of great importance not only for individual smokers but also for social health. Regarding current cessation therapies, the effectiveness of nicotine replacement is limited, and the cost of varenicline medication is considerable. Thus, a method for screening smokers who are responsive to cessation therapy based on the therapeutic effectiveness is required. Peripheral biomarkers reflecting smoking dependence status are necessary to establish a method for achieving effective cessation therapy.MethodsMethylation status of smokers' blood DNA was evaluated focusing on SHATI/NAT8L, an addiction‐related gene. Eight CpG sites in SHATI/NAT8L were quantified by pyrosequencing.ResultsThere was no difference in the methylation status of this gene between smokers (n = 129) and non‐smokers (n = 129) at all CpG sites. No correlations between the methylation status of SHATI/NAT8L and indicators of smoking dependence were found.ConclusionsAlthough the present study found no significance in the DNA methylation of SHATI/NAT8L among smokers, the exploration of predictable peripheral biomarkers for the effectiveness of smoking cessation therapy is required.Keywords: cigarette smoker, DNA methylation, pyrosequencing, SHATI/NAT8L, tobacco dependence screener

INTRODUCTION

Since cigarette smoking is associated with diverse diseases, such as cancers, ischemic stroke, cardiac stroke, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, smoking cessation is a serious issue from the perspectives of both individuals and society worldwide. Although smoking carries risks of diseases, quitting smoking requires considerable effort. Among cessation therapies, the succession rate of nicotine replacement is lower than 40%, 1 whereas the use of varenicline, approved by the FDA in 2006 for smoking cessation therapy, has been an economical burden because of its low cost‐effectiveness. 2 The future prevalence of current digital medicine programs to administer varenicline will increase economic concern. 3 , 4 , 5 In such situations, it is necessary to determine who should receive cessation therapy with priority based on the predicted sensitivity to such therapy. Peripheral biomarkers associated with smoking dependence can be used for this purpose.

Shati/Nat8l was identified as an upregulated molecule in the nucleus accumbens (NAc) of mice that received methamphetamine (METH), an addictive drug. 6 Our genetic studies on Shati/Nat8l using a mouse model have revealed the significant contribution of this molecule to psychiatric and neurological diseases 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 including METH‐induced addictive behaviors. 6 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Global deletion or NAc‐specific reduction of Shati/Nat8l enhanced METH‐induced hyperlocomotion, 6 , 11 whereas overexpression of this gene in the NAc or prefrontal cortex attenuated such behavioral alterations. 12 , 14

Current pyrosequencing technology, applicable to the high‐throughput quantification of DNA modifications, is a strong tool for exploring biomarkers of various diseases. 15 , 16 This method enables the identification of alterations in the methylation status of DNA in the peripheral blood of patients with schizophrenia, 17 , 18 major depressive disorder (MDD), 19 and bipolar disorder. 20 The methylation levels of the brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) promoter, measured in the peripheral blood by pyrosequencing, increased in patients with schizophrenia 17 and decreased in those with MDD. 19 In addition, we detected changes in methylation at CpG sites of the SHATI/NAT8L promoter in the blood of METH users using pyrosequencing. 21 These suggest the potential of the DNA methylation on SHATI/NAT8L as a peripheral biomarker reflecting the alteration of the central nervous system (CNS) in the METH dependence. The expanded application of measuring the peripheral methylation status of the SHATI/NAT8L gene from METH users to smokers highlights its significance as a general dependence marker.

Here, to examine the potential of SHATI/NAT8L as a peripheral biomarker of smoking dependence, we analyzed the association between the dependent status of smokers and the methylation status of CpG sites in the promoter region of SHATI/NAT8L in the peripheral blood DNA of cigarette smokers using pyrosequencing.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and subjects

DNA samples were obtained from the blood of 129 current smokers (male: 120, female: 9, age: 65–86 years) and 129 non‐smokers who have no smoking experience (male: 114, female: 15, age: 65–93 years) (Supplementary table S1), who visited Iwata City Hospital in Japan. The inclusion criteria for this study were being Japanese, ambulatory, able to communicate orally, and aged 60 years or older. Smokers completed a questionnaire consisting of various questions about their lifestyle, including alcohol consumption, smoking, diet, and cancer history. Peripheral blood samples were collected from these individuals for DNA methylation analysis. Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants, with a focus on smoking behaviors, including the Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence (FTND, a test that yields a continuous measure of nicotine dependence), 22 Tobacco Dependence Screener [TDS, a screening questionnaire for tobacco/nicotine dependence according to the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th revision (ICD‐10), Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 3rd edition, revised (DSM‐III‐R), and DSM‐IV], 23 and the number of cigarettes smoked per day (CPD), 24 were as shown in Table 1.

Bisulfate conversion

Genomic DNA was used in bisulfite reactions, in which unmethylated cytosine residues were converted to uracil residues, using an EpiTect Bisulfite Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's guidelines and published methods. 21 , 25 , 26 DNA (200 ng) was used in the experiments.

PyroMark PCR and pyrosequencing

To prepare a single‐stranded PCR product for subsequent pyrosequencing, one PCR primer was labeled with biotin at its 5′ mark. PCR primers were designed using PyroMark Assay Design Software 2.0 (www.qiagen.com). PyroMark PCR was performed using PyroMark PCR Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's guidelines and published methods. 21 , 25 , 27 Pyrosequencing was performed according to the PyroMark Q24 User Manual and previously published reports. 21 , 25 , 28 Samples failed in the first measurement of methylation were re‐tried to measure.

Statistical analyses

All data are shown as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Differences between the two groups were analyzed with an unpaired Student's t‐test. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Cohen's d was used as the measure of effect size. Cohen defined effect sizes as “small, d = 0.2,” “medium, d = 0.5,” and “large, d = 0.8.” Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to determine the utility of pyrosequencing as a biomarker of MDD for predicting group status. The best possible cutoff value in the ROC curve was identified with the highest Youden index to determine sensitivity and specificity. Pearson's correlation analysis was conducted between the methylation ratio and smoking dependence indices (FTND, TDS, and CPD). All statistical analyses were performed using Prism, version 7 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

RESULTS

No alteration of methylation rates in cigarette smokers

Methylation rates at CpG sites in the promoter region of SHATI/NAT8L in DNA from cigarette smokers and non‐smokers were measured (Supprementary table S2). Eight CpG sites (−1714, −1700, −1696, −1532, −1509, −1492, −1482, and, −1480 bp) from transcription start site (TSS), whose methylation patterns were altered in patients with psychiatric disorders, 29 were analyzed by pyrosequencing. There were no differences in the methylation ratio at eight CpG cites analyzed here between smokers and non‐smokers from TSS [−1714 bp (df = 256, p = 0.851, t = 0.188), −1700 bp (df = 255, p = 0.603, t = 0.521), −1696 bp (df = 255, p = 0.166, t = 1.39), −1532 bp (df = 254, p = 0.462, t = 0.736), −1509 bp (df = 254, p = 0.8977, t = 0.129), −1492 bp (df = 253, p = 0.458, t = 0.743), −1482 bp (df = 248, p = 0.695, t = 0.393) and −1480 bp (df = 248, p = 0.630, t = 0.482)] as shown in Table 2. All effect sizes at eight CpG sites were low [−1714 bp (Cohen's‐d = 0.02), −1700 bp (Cohen's‐d = 0.07), −1696 bp (Cohen's‐d = 0.17), −1532 bp (Cohen's‐d = 0.09), −1509 bp (Cohen's‐d = 0.02), −1492 bp (Cohen's‐d = 0.09), −1482 bp (Cohen's‐d) = 0.05, −1480 bp (Cohen's‐d = 0.06)]. For further investigation, we sought to determine the correlation between DNA methylation ratio and indicators of smoking dependence. No correlations were found between FTND, TDS, and CPD at the eight CpG sites (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

Peripheral biomarkers reflecting alterations in molecules related to psychiatric functions in the CNS are of great importance for the diagnosis, evaluation of disease status, and monitoring of treatment effects in patients. 30 , 31 Here, we studied the peripheral biomarkers linked to the status of dependence on cigarette smoking, which could be beneficial in predicting the effectiveness of smoking cessation therapy. DNA methylation of SHATI/NAT8L, which is involved in psychological diseases, 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 was previously reported to be altered in patients with MDD 25 and METH users. 21 However, in this study, DNA methylation at eight CpG sites measured by pyrosequencing exhibited no difference between cigarette smokers and nonsmokers and no correlation with indicators of smoking dependence. The CpG sites focused on in this study showed alterations in methylation in the peripheral blood DNA of patients with schizophrenia in our previous study. 29 These sites include those where the methylation status changes in patients with MDD 25 and METH users. 21 Considering that another 28 additional methylation sites were analyzed in the literature, 29 the methylation status could be changed at these sites; however, we did not investigate this in the smoking group. The donors of the samples used in this study do not accompany the history data of MDD or METH use. The possibility that participants' status of psychiatric diseases including other substance use disorders affected the DNA methylation of SHATI/NAT8L is unignorable.

A Rotterdam study revealed that plasma levels of BDNF increased in current smokers. 32 Given that BDNF, whose expression is in both the CNS and peripheral tissues, is affected by various factors other than the alteration of its expression in the brain, there is no conflict between the epidemiological study mentioned above and the present study on SHATI/NAT8L, which is expressed in the CNS upstream of BDNF. By seeking microRNAs in blood exosomes, some groups reported a decreased expression of miR‐137, associated with schizophrenia, in METH users. 33 , 34 Exploring biomarkers in neuron‐derived exosomes (NDE) from the blood of patients has been successful in identifying schizophrenia‐related genes. 35 , 36 Considering that the methylated DNA detected in circulating exosomes reflects the methylation status of the corresponding DNA of their parental cells, 37 , 38 investigation of SHATI/NAT8L in the NDE extracted from the blood could monitor alterations related to this gene, reflecting the status of dependence on cigarette smoking or the effects of cessation therapy.

Considering that the pathology of smoking dependence is caused not only by the pharmacological effects of contains but also by the complicated interaction of factors such as behavioral patterns to reach smoking and sensory inputs during smoking. 39 , 40 , 41 Peripheral biomarkers associated with the psychological status of smokers are necessary for the development of cessation therapy. As such biomarkers could be applicable to predict smokers' sensitivity to cessation therapy, their exploration is of great significance from the perspective of health economics. Although the present study did not find significant alterations in the DNA methylation of SHATI/NAT8L in the blood of smokers, it is meaningful to focus on this dependence‐related gene. The exploration of peripheral biomarkers for smoking dependence should continue in the future.
