Multilocus sequence typing of Candida albicans isolates from wild and farm animals from southern Italy
Authors: Wafa Rhimi, Letterio Giuffrè, Jairo A. Mendoza-Roldan, Younes Laidoudi, Roberta Iatta, Orazio Romeo, Vanessa Barrs, Domenico Otranto, Ana Alastruey-Izquierdo, Claudia Cafarchia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Candida albicans is the leading cause of invasive candidiasis in humans and animals and is recognized as a critical priority pathogen by the World Health Organization. However, despite the growing attention paid to this pathogen, few studies have investigated its genetic diversity, population structure, and evolution in non-human hosts.We report, for the first time, genotyping data from C. albicans isolates recovered from wild and farm animals in Italy, highlighting the potential role of animal hosts in C. albicans evolution and transmission.Twenty-seven Candida albicans isolates collected from faecal samples of laying hens (n = 15), wild boars (Sus scrofa) (n = 9), and three from different lizard species (Tarentola mauritanica, Chalcides ocellatus, Podarcis filfolensis) were characterized by multilocus sequence typing (MLST), based on seven housekeeping genes (i.e., AAT1a, ACC1, ADP1, MPIb, SYA1, VPS13, and ZWF1b).Of 27 diploid sequence types (DSTs) identified, 22 (81.5 %) were novel. DNA mutations were most frequent in the VPS13 gene while little genetic variation was observed in the ACC1 gene. The C. albicans DSTs from laying hens, lizards and wild boars clustered with clinical isolates from humans and domestic and wild animals of different geographical provenance.The phylogenetic tree analysis reveals intricate and interconnected relationships, highlighting the complex evolutionary dynamics of C. albicans. Global optimal eBURST analysis revealed 16 genotypes (59 %) were classified as singletons, while the remaining 11 genotypes (41 %) were placed in 6 different clonal complexes, none of which were exclusively composed with isolates of animal origin. Our data showed a close genetic relationship between human clinical and animal isolates suggesting potential cross-transmission of C. albicans between animals and the community.

Introduction

Fungal zoonotic diseases are increasingly reported due to a plethora of factors related to significant changes in the ecosystem dynamics [1,2], eventually leading to an intensification of human-animal interactions, especially at the interface with wildlife [3,4]. Indeed, over 70 % of emerging zoonotic diseases originate from wildlife [4,5], which may be reservoirs of emerging fungi including Candida auris, a multidrug-resistant yeast suspected to have environmental reservoirs [6] and Histoplasma spp., associated with bats and soil mixed with their guano [7]. Wildlife contribute substantially to the maintenance and spreading of fungal diseases through various complex pathways. For example, the consumption of wildlife as “bushmeat” can serve as a conduit for the introduction of fungal pathogens such as Sporothrix schenckii and Talaromyces marneffei, associated to armadillo meat in southern Brazil [8] and bamboo rats in Southeast Asia [9], respectively. Also, reptiles, which are often maintained in suboptimal hygienic conditions, may play a role in the dissemination of fungal pathogens in association with traditional practices involving their handling and utilization for dietary, medicinal, or cultural purposes [10].

In recent years, human fungal diseases associated with wildlife have increased in their prevalence because of many factors, including altered transmission routes, and expanded at-risk populations [4]. For example, various mammalian hosts (i.e., bears, monkeys, and wild boars), reptiles and amphibians have been identified as reservoirs for zoonotic fungi (e.g., genus Candida, Trichosporon, Saccharomyces, and Geotrichum) [ [[11], [12], [13], [14]]]. Analogously, wild birds also harbour diverse yeast species in their cloacae, and their excreta facilitating transmission to humans and other mammals including Rhodotorula, Cryptococcus, Trichosporon, and Candida spp. [15,16].

As the burden of fungal infections has increased, so too has the number of immunocompromised people, who are particularly vulnerable, due to changes in their immune status at the population level [17,18]. This latter phenomenon has resulted in a resurgent interest in fungal diseases including the discovery of novel pathogens of public health concern [17,18].

The intricate interactions between fungi and their respective hosts are regulated by biotic and abiotic factors, including the environmental microbiome [19,20]. Refined investigations are needed to determinate the intricate interplays among all components [21]. For example, multilocus sequence typing (MLST) has proven to be an effective tool for analysing fungal populations, enabling more accurate understanding of these complex ecological interactions [21,22].

Candida albicans, is a commensal organism residing in the gastrointestinal tracts and oral cavities of healthy animals and humans. It is also as the predominant etiological agent of human candidiasis [23]. Various domestic animals (e.g., cattle, horses, pigs, cats, dogs, and avian species) are susceptible hosts to Candida infections [24,25], raising questions about their role in the transmission of these yeasts to humans, especially immunocompromised individuals [26]. Despite the existence of numerous case reports detailing candidiasis in animals [24,25], our understanding of the precise taxonomic identity and evolutionary origins of these infecting isolates remains limited, as does our knowledge of the genetic relationships that exist among C. albicans isolates from both human and animal reservoirs [27,28]. This gap in knowledge highlights the urgent need for comprehensive research in the field of medical mycology to clarify the intricate interactions between animals and humans in the spread of Candida spp. Therefore, in this study the genetic diversity of C. albicans isolates from wild and farm animals was characterized by MLST and their relationship with other isolates of different human, animal and geographical origins was also assessed.

Identification procedures

The identification of isolates was performed based on colony morphology, microscopic and biochemical features and by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionisation time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS; Vitek MS-bioMerieux, France, knowledge Base V3.2). However, before genotyping analysis, the identity of all C. albicans isolates was confirmed by amplification and direct sequencing of the ribosomal ITS1–5.8S-ITS2 region using the ITS1 (5′-TCCGTAGGTGAACCTGCGG-3′) and ITS4 (5′-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3′) primers according to our previous study [30].

Genomic DNA was extracted from each fungal isolate using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, Italy), following the manufacturer's instructions.

Genotyping of animal C. albicans isolates by multilocus sequence typing (MLST) analysis

Genetic diversity of animal C. albicans isolates was examined at 7 protein-coding loci using the official MLST scheme developed for this species [31]. Seven housekeeping genes (i.e., AAT1a, ACC1, ADP1, MPIb, SYA1, VPS13, and ZWF1b) were partially amplified and bidirectionally sequenced using specific oligonucleotide MLST primers as reported by previous studies [32,33].

For each fungal isolate, PCRs were carried out for each MLST locus in a total volume of 50 μL comprising 0.5 μL of AmpliTaq DNA polymerase (5 U/μL) (Applied Biosystems Italy), 5 μL of PCR buffer, 8 μL dNTP mix (1.25 mM), 5 μL of MLST forward and reverse primer (10 μM each), 5 μL of genomic DNA template (5 ng/μL) and 26.5 μL H2O using previously described PCR conditions [33].

Subsequent to conventional agarose gel electrophoresis (1,5 % w/v) and purification of PCR products with a QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, Italy), amplicons were sequenced in both directions at Genomics unit at the National Center for Microbiology, Instituto de Salud Carlos III, Madrid, Spain, using standard Sanger sequencing and the same MLST locus- specific primers used for the initial PCR amplification.

Raw sequencing data were examined using the FinchTV chromatogram viewer ver. 1.4 (GeoSpiza Inc., Seattle, WA) to detect base-calling errors and potential heterozygous sites displayed as two overlapping peaks in the same position.

For each MLST locus, a consensus sequence was generated by matching forward and reverse sequencing traces and then converted into FASTA format using the single-letter nucleotide code of the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) nomenclature.

The resulting DNA sequences were used to query those deposited in the official C. albicans PubMLST database (http://pubmlst.org/calbicans; last accessed: 14/01/2025) for assigning allelic numbers and define a diploid sequence type (DST) for each fungal isolate [33]. Any allelic sequence not assigned by the database (i.e. new allele), and/or new allelic combinations (i.e. novel DSTs), were submitted to the curator of the international C. albicans PubMLST database: Prof. Marie Elisabeth Bougnoux.

Phylogenetic analysis and prediction of clonal complexes using MLST data

For each C. albicans isolate, the DNA sequences of the seven MLST loci were concatenated into a contiguous sequence of 2883 bp and edited as described by Odds et al. (2007) [34] to mark homozygous and heterozygous polymorphic positions to allow the analysis of diploid sequences using MEGA X software [35]. The final dataset contained the concatenated sequences of all animal fungal isolates in this study and 10–15 representative DSTs for each of 19C. albicans clades described by Gong et al. (2018) [36].

The phylogenetic tree was constructed in MEGA X [35] using the UPGMA algorithm with a p-distance model of sequence evolution supported by a bootstrap of 1000 replicates.

Clonal complexes (CCs) were predicted with PHYLOViZ 2.0 [37] using the global optimal e-BURST (goeBURST) algorithm at the single locus variance (SLV) level. Two isolates were considered as belonging to the same CC if their DSTs differed only by one MLST gene. The final set of isolates used for analysis included all those examined in this study and an additional 5759 validated isolates available in the C. albicans PubMLST database (last accessed: 14/01/2025).

Results

Partial sequencing of the coding region of the seven MLST housekeeping genes yielded a total of 2883 aligned nucleotides for each isolate. Among the analyzed genes, a total of 86 alleles were found, of which 29 (∼34 %) have never been reported before (Table 1).

The SYA1 gene was the most informative genetic marker with the highest number of alleles detected (19/86; 22 %), followed by MPIb (15/86; 17 %), VPS13 (13/86; 15 %), ADP1, AAT1a and ZWF1b (11/86; ∼13 % each) and ACC1 (6/86; ∼7 %). Moreover, a total of 79 polymorphic sites were identified among all sequenced MLST loci, corresponding to a ratio of 2,74 % (79/2.883 nucleotides). The VPS13 locus exhibited the highest number of variable sites (17/79) while the ACC1 locus showed the lowest (6/79).

By combining all seven allele numbers for each isolate, a total of 27 unique DSTs (or MLST genotypes) were obtained, of which 22 were novel (22/27; ∼81.5 %) and submitted to the international C. albicans PubMLST database (Table 1). Only five C. albicans isolates showed DSTs already deposited in the central MLST database (DSTs: 461, 1363, 1706, 1902 and 2907): two isolates (W1400 and W1407) obtained from laying hens and three isolates (W1602, W1619 and W1637) were recovered from wild boars (Table 1).

Phylogenetic analysis among our fungal isolates and several reference DSTs representing the MLST clades of C. albicans phylogeny currently known, revealed that our 27 isolates grouped in 6 distinct clades and 1 singleton (Table 1; Fig. 1). Most of the isolates clustered in clade 10 (14/27; ∼52 %), followed by clade 17 (4/27; ∼15 %), clade 11 (3/27; 11 %), clade 7 (3/27; 11 %), clade 4 (1/27; ∼4 %) and clade 1 (1/27; ∼4 %) (Fig. 1). Five isolates (W1369, W1398, W1400, W1407 and W1769) were grouped into CC16 (5/27, 18.5 %) (Table 1; Fig. 2) which contained 30 DSTs and from 53 isolates, of which 14 were of animal origin, Two DSTs from wild boars (DST 1902 and 4048) clustered into CC13 (2/27, ∼7.4 %) together with 35 MLST genotypes and 58 isolates, including two from the West Indian manatee (Trichechus manatus) and one from the Eurasian badger (Meles meles). Another DST from wild boars (DST 4070) was grouped into CC62, a minor cluster with only one human isolate containing 4 DSTs from 8 isolates, of which 6 were from different birds: Buteo buteo, Columba palumbus, Falco tinnunculus and Streptopelia decaocto.

Moreover, DSTs (2907 and 4068) from wild boars clustered into CC37 (1/27, 3.7 %) and CC76 (1/27, 3.7 %), which have been primarily associated with human isolates. Finally, CC6 (1/27, ∼3,7 %) was the largest clonal cluster identified in this study, containing 90 DSTs and 136 isolates mainly of human origin (Fig. 2), including DST 461 from wild boar (Table 1) which was identified as the putative founder genotype of this cluster.

Discussion and conclusions

The MLST analysis of C. albicans isolates from wild animals (boars) and farmed animals (laying hens) in this study revealed the presence of known DSTs, as well as DSTs closely related to human and other animal isolates, suggesting a high potential for zoonotic transmission. In contrast, the identification of distinct DSTs, some of which are unique to wild boars, laying hens, and lizards, indicates the presence of host- or region-specific genotypes in these animals.

Analysis of the 27C. albicans clinical isolates collected from various animals revealed high genetic diversity among DSTs, probably due to the heterozygosity inherent in the diploid genome of C. albicans, which significantly promotes genetic recombination events [27,38]. The latter indicates that C. albicans from different geographical locations and hosts display distinct DSTs, also due to the ability of MLST systems to detect minor genetic alterations. The observed variations in the genotype population in this study, aligns with previous findings where clinical C. albicans isolates from different animal samples were analyzed [39]. The high number of novel DSTs (81.5 %) in this study is similar to that reported in China and Tanzania, where the differences in the C. albicans population structure exceeded 70 %. [38,40].

Of the five C. albicans DSTs (461, 2907, 1902) in this study previously deposited in the PubMLST database, three were found in wild boars. These DSTs are shared with healthy humans, hospitalized patients (e.g., with cirrhosis, tuberculosis, or aplastic anemia) from various regions worldwide, and wild animals such as the Amazonian manatee (Trichechus inunguis) [21,22,39,40]. A similar finding was observed for the laying hen genotype DST 1363 which has been identified mainly in human clinical samples (vaginal swabs and blood) according to the PubMLST database. This finding highlights the potential zoonotic role of wild boars and laying hens, as possible reservoirs and spreaders of C. albicans through faecal contamination of the environment, as previously reported [13,30]. Our data and other studies show no evidence of host-specific genotypes or of complete genetic separation between human and animal isolates [27,28]. In fact, we found that human and animal genotypes were mixed in various ratios within each clonal cluster detected in this study (Fig. 2), confirming a previous assumption that animal isolates may be evolutionarily closely related to human isolates, just as human isolates are to each other [28].

DNA mutations were most frequent in the VPS13 gene (encoding a putative vacuolar protein sorting-associated protein) while only little genetic variations were observed in the ACC1 locus (essential in fatty acid biosynthesis) (www.candidagenome.org). This is in agreement with previous studies that have suggested that variation in MLST genes can be significantly influenced by their chromosomal location [21]. In fact, the ACC1 gene is considered the least variable as it maps on chromosome 3 which is known to be devoid of genomic rearrangements and repetitive sequences [21]. In contrast, the VPS13 gene is located on chromosome 4 which is highly unstable and prone to chromosomal aberrations in some C. albicans isolates [41].

MLST has been proved to be effective tool for determining the genetic diversity and population structure in C. albicans [21]. In this study, MLST delineates phylogenetic relationships, revealing complex and interconnected evolutionary pathways among C. albicans isolates from different animal species in Italy, as well from humans and animals across different geographical regions.

The clustering distribution observed in the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1) indicates a high degree of genetic similarity among C. albicans isolates from laying hens, lizards, and those recovered from animals and humans in different regions of the world. In particular, the co-clustering of clade 10C. albicans isolates from laying hens (Table 1), clinical isolates from humans, and domestic and wild animals (DSTs 304 and 1363) (Fig. 1) highlights the remarkable adaptability of C. albicans to various host environments. This flexibility in adapting to various changing conditions may be facilitated by common ecological niches or interactions among different species, therefore highlighting the zoonotic potential and interspecies transmission of C. albicans [22,42]. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting similar clustering patterns and suggesting that certain strains can infect multiple host species through interspecies transmission and adaptation [40,43]. A similar trend was observed for Candida tropicalis, which showed close genetic relationships between those from humans and animals [44,45]. Nevertheless, other C. albicans genotypes herein isolated, such as the DST4070 (W1672 isolate from wild boar), showed genetic difference with distinct polytomy from other clinical and animal DSTs. These findings suggest that specific DSTs of C. albicans can undergo divergent evolutionary paths, potentially driven by geographical and host-specific factors, as documented in previous studies [22,28,46]. Similarly, the geographical locations of C. glabrata were shown to affect its genetic population structure, as assessed by MLST [46].

In our study, the high variability in branch lengths observed in the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1) indicates complex evolutionary relationships with other animal and human isolates suggesting that animals can serve as reservoirs for C. albicans, and humans could also act as reservoirs and spreaders, facilitating interspecies transmission and adaptation. In this context, mutation events contribute to the microevolution of C. albicans, enhancing its ability to change phenotype, increase virulence factors, and survive in diverse environments [42].

The identification of 22 novel DSTs among C. albicans isolates from wild and farmed animals in this study, suggests that a high genetic variation occurs in animal isolates. In addition, based on phylogenetic clustering, some DSTs of C. albicans from Italian laying hens, wild boars, and lizards showed a great adaptability to colonize or infect humans and other animal species. Future studies could investigate the mechanisms of genetic host adaptation and transmission dynamics, in order to decipher the risk that C. albicans infections may have in public health.
