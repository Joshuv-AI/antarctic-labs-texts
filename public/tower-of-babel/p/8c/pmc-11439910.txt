Age affects the association of red blood cell indices with efficacy of remote ischemic conditioning in patients with acute moderate ischemic stroke
Authors: Qiong Wu, Yi-Na Zhang, Nan-Nan Zhang, Quan-Ying Liu, Ji-Ru Cai, Hui-Sheng Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe conducted a post hoc analysis of Remote Ischemic Conditioning for Acute Moderate Ischemic Stroke (RICAMIS) to investigate whether red blood cell (RBC) indices are associated with efficacy of remote ischemic conditioning (RIC), and whether the association is affected by age. In this post hoc analysis, patients with RBC indices at admission were enrolled. RBC indices including RBC count, hematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), hemoglobin (HB), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC) were analyzed. According to the median of these RBC indices, eligible patients were divided into high and low groups, which were further subdivided into RIC and control subgroups. Primary endpoint was excellent functional outcome defined as a modified Rankin Scale score of 0–1 at 90 days, which was used to evaluate RIC efficacy. RIC efficacy as well as effect of age on RIC efficacy were analyzed across the high and low groups of different RBC indices, and the interaction effects of RBC indices on RIC efficacy were evaluated. A total of 1640 patients were enrolled in the final analysis. In overall patients, no significant interaction effects of RIC intervention by all RBC indices were found, although there was a trend in interaction effect of RIC intervention by MCH (p = 0.116). However, we found an effect of age on the association of MCH with RIC efficacy. In patients over 60 years old, MCH significantly affected RIC efficacy (p = 0.006) and RIC significantly produced a higher proportion of primary outcome in high MCH (72.6% vs. 59.1%, P < 0.001) vs. low MCH group (61.2% vs. 62%, P = 0.829), which was not identified in patients under 60 years old. Furthermore, RIC efficacy decreased with increasing age in patients with low MCH with significant interaction effect (p = 0.012), while RIC efficacy increased with increasing age in patients with high MCH although no significant interaction (p = 0.126). No significant interaction effects of RIC intervention by RBC count, HCT, MCV, HB, and MCHC were found regardless of age. This secondary analysis of RICAMIS suggested that RIC exhibited more obvious benefit in AIS patients over 60 years old with high MCH compared with those with low MCH group, but RBC count, HCT, MCV, HB, and MCHC were not associated with the efficacy of RIC treatment regardless of age. Introduction

Intravenous thrombolysis and endovascular therapy are the only proven interventions for acute ischemic stroke (AIS), but due to the limited time window and the requirements of equipment and skilled operators, only a small number of patients can receive these treatments1,2. It has been a hot research topic to improve clinical outcomes in AIS, however, almost all cerebroprotective strategies have not been successfully translated into human3.

Remote ischemic conditioning (RIC) refers to a repetitive, transient and sublethal ischemic stimulation applied to an organ or tissue, for example, the limb as the optimal regimen for clinical scenarios4,5, to induce endogenous tolerance and protect distant target organs or tissues against subsequent prolonged ischemic threats6. RIC can be classified as remote ischemic preconditioning, remote ischemic perconditioning, and remote ischemic postconditioning7according to the given time of intervention. The neuroprotection mechanisms of RIC have been extensively explored, involved in reducing edema, inflammation, oxidative stress, excitotoxicity, blood-brain-barrier disruption; enhancing hemodynamics, oxygenation, neurogenesis, mitochondrial function, and extracellular vesicles released8–10. Besides, some novel insights into RIC complex signal transduction mechanisms have been raised, including the activation of the vago-splenic axis in RIC’s signal transfer11,12, and the involvement of circulating soluble plasma factors13, or circulating platelets as cellular transmitters of RIC’s cardioprotection14. Translating to the clinical trials, the feasibility and safety of RIC have been demonstrated, but the benefit was inconclusive15–17. The challenges of clinical transformation may be attributed to heterogeneity of populations10, different RIC strategy such as intervention procedures, start time, and duration4, and patient compliance18. Recently, the Remote Ischemic Conditioning for Acute Moderate Ischemic Stroke (RICAMIS) trial found that RIC with 2 weeks duration initiated within 48 h from symptom onset increased the likelihood of excellent neurologic function at 90 days in AIS patients who cannot receive intravenous thrombolysis or endovascular therapy compared with Usual Care (67.4% vs. 62%)19, although the finding was not confirmed by the recent RESIST trial20.

Preclinical studies found that RIC promotes the glutamate release from injured brain tissue to blood21,22and increases the glutamate uptake capacity of red blood cell (RBC), with the result of reducing neurotoxicity23. Besides, RIC was found to enhance the oxygen-carrying capacity of hemoglobin (HB) and oxygen delivery to ischemic tissue24. In addition, RIC stimulates RBC to release circulating vesicles25, which can bypass the blood-brain barrier and deliver their cargo to the brain26,27. Collectively, these results suggest the potential association of RBC indices with the efficacy of RIC. Furthermore, our recent study showed that RIC efficacy may be attenuated with increasing age in moderate AIS patients28. Given the close relationship of age to stroke outcomes and treatment effect29–31, and the relationship of age to RBC structure and function32, we hypothesize that the association of RBC indices with the efficacy of RIC may be affected by age, which has not been investigated so far. The data from the RICAMIS trial provide the opportunity to investigate whether RBC indices are associated with RIC efficacy, and whether the association is affected by age.

Methods

Patients and study design

The post hoc analysis of the RICAMIS study followed the CONSORT guideline and was in accordance with the ethical principles that have their origins in the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Details about the design and methods of RICAMIS trial have been previously published19. Briefly, the RICAMIS trial aimed to assess the efficacy of two weeks of RIC in patients with moderate AIS within 48 h from symptom onset, and a total of 1776 patients were enrolled. The trial and data collection were approved by Ethics Committee of General Hospital of Northern Theater Command (approval number: k 2018[43]). All participants or their representatives provided written informed consent. The unique identifier was NCT03740971.

In this secondary analysis, eligible patients with available RBC indices were enrolled. RBC indices included RBC count, hematocrit (HCT), and hemoglobin (HB), respectively referring to the number, volume of RBC and the amount of hemoglobin per liter of blood, which are commonly used to assess the presence of anemia; mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), respectively referring to the average volume, amount of hemoglobin, concentration of hemoglobin of a single RBC, which are commonly used to determine the cause and type of anemia.

According to the median of these six RBC indices, patients were grouped into high and low groups, which were further grouped into RIC and control subgroups. Furthermore, these patients were divided into ≥ 60 years and < 60 years groups.

Procedures

The cuff of a pneumatic electronic auto-control device (Patent Number: ZL201410834305·2; Device Model: IPC-906; Beijing Renqiao Cardiocerebrovascular Disease Prevention and Treatment Research Jiangsu Co., Ltd), placed around the bilateral upper limbs within 48 h of symptom onset, was used to deliver the RIC protocol: 5 cycles of cuff inflation (200 mmHg for 5 min) and deflation (for 5 min), for a total procedure time of 50 min, twice daily for 10–14 days. Further detail of RIC treatment has been described in a previous report.15 Neurologic status, measured with NIHSS score, was evaluated at admission, 7 days, and 12 days after randomization. Follow-up data were collected at 7 days, 12 days (or at hospital discharge if earlier), and 90 days after randomization.

Clinical data and functional outcome

From Electronic Data Capture system, baseline demographics and clinical data were collected, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), current smoke status, current drink status, hypertension, diabetes, chronic heart disease, atrial fibrillation, hyperlipidemia, previous stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA); clinical data included time from the onset of symptom to RIC treatment (OTT), duration of RIC, baseline National Institute of Health stroke scale (NIHSS), pre-stroke modified Rankin Scale (mRS); and Trial of Org 10,172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST). The blood samples were collected on admission day or next morning.

In line with the RICAMIS trial, the primary outcome was excellent functional outcome, which was defined as a score of 0 to 1 on the mRS at 90 days.

Statistical analysis

The continuous variables were presented as the median with the interquartile range (IQR). Mann Whitney U was used to test the significance between two groups. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and compared by Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Binary logistic regression analyses were performed for mRS 0–1 at 90 days, and the treatment effects were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence interval (CI). Adjusted ORs and 95% CIs were calculated after adjusting confounding factors (listed in the footnotes of Tables), which were identified according to the difference in baseline characteristics between high and low groups of RBC indices or between RIC and control subgroups across different RBC indices with p<0.05 (Table S1-S12). The interaction effects of RIC treatment assignment with the RBC indices were evaluated in logistic models (categorical variable) or generalized linear models (continuous variable). In addition, the association of RBC indices with RIC efficacy and their interaction effects were further analyzed according to age stratification (≥ 60 vs. < 60 years). Statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS 26.0 software (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL) for Windows. The figure of interaction effect was drawn by R language. A p value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate a significant difference (two-tailed).

Results

Patient characteristics

As shown in Fig. 1, of 1776 patients enrolled in the RICAMIS trial, 136 patients without data of RBC indices at admission were excluded. A total of 1640 patients were included in the final analysis, including 785 in RIC group and 855 in control group, and 1175 patients with ≥ 60 years and 465 patients with < 60 years. Discussion

In the secondary analysis of RICAMIS trial, we found that all RBC indices did not affect the efficacy of RIC in overall population, but MCH affected the efficacy of RIC and better benefit was identified in patients with high vs. low MCH in patients over 60 years old. Furthermore, we found that RIC efficacy decreased with increasing age in patients with low MCH, with significant interaction effect of RIC intervention by age (p = 0.012). Collectively, these results suggest that MCH is significantly associated with the efficacy of RIC and the association is affected by age. However, this finding was not identified with regard to other RBC indices such as RBC count, HCT, MCV, HB, and MCHC.

Prior studies have suggested the close association of RBC with ischemic stroke, for example, RBC was involved in thrombus formation and stabilization, as well as hypoxia and damage mechanisms after stroke33,34, and RBC was also found to be associated with clinical outcomes in stroke35–37. In addition, RBC was also found to be involved in the formation of ischemic tolerance through multiple mechanisms. First, RIC promotes RBC to uptake glutamate via activating the excitatory amino acid transporters to reduce neurotoxicity23. Second, RIC may enhance oxygen supply to ischemic brain tissue via elevating 2,3-biphosphoglycerate level in RBC to improve oxygen-carrying capacity24, and via enhancing RBC deformability to make it easier to pass through microcirculation to transport oxygen38–40. Third, RIC can stimulate RBC to release circulating vesicles25, containing proteins as well as mRNAs, miRNAs, and DNAs, which mediate intercellular communication and may participate in the transduction of protective signals41,42. Finally, RBC can regulate the metabolism of nitric oxide (NO)43,44, which is a key circulating mediator of RIC and involves in increased cerebral blood flow and cytoprotection45.

In this study, we find that MCH is significantly associated with the efficacy of RIC. Given that MCH is defined as the average amount of HB in a single RBC, and only a limited number of RBC can enter the microcirculation of ischemic tissue, it is reasonable to speculate that MCH may better reflect the actual capacity of oxygen supply, antioxidant, and regulation of NO metabolism in ischemic tissue than other indices of RBC. RIC was reported to increase the level of 2,3 biphosphoglycerate in RBC46, which interacts with HB and causes HB to undergo a conformational change allowing for bound oxygen prone to be dissociated from HB, thus increasing oxygen release in ischemic brain tissue24,47. In particular, RBC releases NO-bioactivity under deoxygenated conditions of HB, with consequent capillary and postcapillary venules dilatation and inhibition of platelet reactivity48. Besides, RIC was found to increase the level of haptoglobin, which binds HB and functions as antioxidants49. Thus, we speculate that high MCH may magnify the neuroprotection of RIC by improving oxygen supply, maintaining microcirculation, and enhancing antioxidant effect in ischemic brain tissue. These findings deserve to be further investigated.

Our recent study showed a trend that RIC efficacy may be attenuated with increasing age in AIS patients, although no significant interaction was found28. Whether age affected the protective effects of RIC was inconsistent in previous cardiovascular studies50,51. In this study, the association of MCH and RIC efficacy was affected by age, which may be attributed to the increased proportion of aged RBC in the elderly population, given the changes of structural and functional properties of aged RBC, such as decreased deformability, oxygen-carrying capacity, and antioxidant capacity52,53. Furthermore, stroke itself may cause the decrease in RBC deformability, and severe morphological changes within 48 h after stroke, which may lead to impaired oxygen transport capacity40. This phenomenon should be more serious in the elderly population given high proportion of aged RBC. The impaired ability of RBC may be reversed to some extent by RIC intervention. Some studies found that RIC may influence RBC deformability to improve exercise performance38,39. Besides, physical exercise was found to increase RBC deformability, RBC turnover, and a greater proportion of young RBC to improve oxygen transport and release in the microcirculation54. Given the overlapped mechanisms of physical training and RIC55, RIC may exert more obvious neuroprotection by increasing RBC deformability, RBC turnover, and young RBC in elderly patients, compared with young patients.

It is worthy to note that we only investigated the relationship between RBC indices and RIC efficacy in this study. Recently, we also investigated the association of neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, and systemic immune inflammation index, determined based on white blood cell and platelet, with RIC efficacy, and the results showed the potential benefit of RIC in patients with high inflammation status56. The effect of these blood cells on RIC efficacy warrants further investigation given their potential interaction with stroke as well as RIC. Besides, we also found that, diabetes did not affect the neuroprotective effect of RIC in acute moderate ischemic stroke, although diabetes was independently associated with functional outcomes57. Given the possible effect of diabetes on RBC indices58, diabetes was adjusted as a confounding factor when investigated the relationship between RBC indices and RIC efficacy. Collectively, these results may reflect the complex of neuroprotective mechanisms underlying RIC.

The main strength of this study is the first report that MCH may affect the neuroprotection of RIC in patients over 60 years old based on the RICAMIS trial. However, several limitations should be highlighted. First, given that this is not a pre-defined subgroup analysis, thus the bias effect cannot be excluded. Second, the intrinsic flaw of retrospective study made the potential confounding factors inevitable, although these known imbalance of baseline characteristics between groups has been adjusted. Third, there was imbalanced and reduced sample size between groups due to this retrospective subgroup analysis, especially the small sample size in patients under 60 years old, which rendered the statistical analysis unpowered. Fourth, given Chinese participants in the RICAMIS trial, the generalizability of the findings should be further validated in other cohorts. Finally, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the exploratory nature of this post-hoc analysis, which needs to be confirmed in the future.

Conclusion

This secondary analysis of RICAMIS suggested that RIC exhibited more obvious benefit in AIS patients over 60 years old with high MCH compared with those with low MCH group, but RBC count, HCT, MCV, HB, and MCHC were not associated with the efficacy of RIC treatment regardless of age.
