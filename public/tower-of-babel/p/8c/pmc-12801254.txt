Prenatal organophosphate ester exposure and epigenetic changes at birth: a characterization of the methylome in the ECHO cohort
Authors: Rose Schrott, Jennifer L Ames, Emily S Barrett, Deborah H Bennet, Lisa A Croen, Sarah D Geiger, Kurunthachalam Kannan, Zorimar Rivera-Nunez, Jiwon Oh, Alicia K Peterson, JRichard Pilsner, Rebecca J Schmidt, Anne P Starling, Yeyi Zhu, Christine Ladd-Acosta, ECHO Cohort Consortium
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Prenatal exposure to organophosphate esters (OPEs) affects multiple child health domains. Alterations to the DNA methylome are a plausible mechanism through which these changes occur. This study characterized DNA methylation signatures at birth associated with prenatal OPE biomarkers.Methods:We included 736 mother-infant pairs from 7 sites in the Environmental influences on Child Health Outcomes (ECHO) Cohort. Five OPE biomarkers were quantified in maternal urine samples collected during the second and third trimesters and modeled as log2-transformed continuous variables. Using covariate-adjusted linear regression, we tested associations between OPE biomarkers and locus-specific, regional, and global cord blood DNA methylation changes measured by Illumina 450 K and EPIC arrays, and gestational epigenetic age measured by the Knight gestational age epigenetic clock generated with measures from the 27 K, 450 K, and EPIC arrays. When feasible, we examined relationships by sex.Findings:Global hypomethylation at multiple regions was associated with BDCPP concentrations (p = 0.003 to 0.02, coef = −0.002). Differentially methylated regions annotated to PCDHGB1 and SLC43A2 were associated with BDCPP and DPHP concentrations, respectively (FDR q < 0.05). In sex-specific analyses, global hypomethylation was associated with prenatal BDCPP (p = 0.006 to 0.03, coef = −0.0003 to −0.0002) and DBUP_DIBP (p = 0.01, coef = −0.0007 to −0.0006) concentrations in females; and global hypermethylation was associated with DBUP_DIBP concentrations in males (p < 0.05, coef = 0.0004). BCETP concentrations were significantly associated with decelerated epigenetic aging at birth in females (p < 0.05, coef = −0.05).Interpretation:Prenatal exposure to OPEs impacts child methylation at birth, suggesting a potential mechanism for the association between prenatal OPE exposure and child health outcomes.Keywords: DNA methylation, Epigenome, Pregnancy, Gestational age, Child health

INTRODUCTION

Organophosphate esters (OPEs) are a prevalent class of flame retardants that rose in abundance in response to the phase-out of polybrominated diphenyl ethers and have since become ubiquitous. (Doherty et al., 2019; Phillips, 2018) As additive flame retardants, OPEs are added to polyurethane foams, textiles, furniture, and other consumer products, from which they can volatilize and leach, entering the human environment. (Doherty et al., 2019; Phillips, 2018) In addition to their use as flame retardants, OPEs have been commonly used as plasticizers in consumer products and construction materials. (Li, 2019) Consequently, OPE exposure is pervasive among pregnant people and people of reproductive age. (Castorina, 2017; Percy, 2020; Hoffman, 2017) This is especially concerning given that the prenatal period is a time of exquisite sensitivity and vulnerability to harmful exogenous exposures. (Varshavsky, 2020).

Prenatal exposure to OPEs has been associated with a suite of adverse pregnancy, birth, and child health outcomes. (Hoffman, 2018; Oh, 2024; Peterson, 2024) OPEs have been detected on the fetal side of placental tissues in chorionic villi and cord blood, demonstrating the ability for these chemicals to move across the maternal-fetal barrier during pregnancy. (Hernandez-Castro, 2023; Guo, 2025; Wang et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2017) The precise mechanism for how OPEs in pregnancy impact offspring health remains to be determined; however, human observational and animal model studies have demonstrated that OPEs detected in cord blood are primarily derived from maternal transplacental transfer. (Guo, 2025; Wang et al., 2021) This process—either active or passive—and the biotransformation of OPEs, is dependent on the physicochemical properties of the OPE, and their potential binding affinity to transporter proteins. (Guo, 2025; Wang et al., 2021) Exposure to OPEs in utero has been associated with preterm birth, low birthweight, (Hoffman, 2018) and adverse neurodevelopment (including impaired fine motor skill development and deficits in early language abilities (Doherty, 2019), metabolic dysregulation, thyroid dysfunction and thyroid hormone imbalances, and altered immune responses (Shahin et al., 2024) in childhood. In addition, OPEs are a potential obesogen and have been associated with higher birthweights in the Environmental influences on Child Health Outcomes (ECHO) Cohort. (Oh, 2024) Thus, prenatal OPE exposure is adversely associated with a large range of child health outcomes.

The epigenome is responsive to the environment, is particularly labile during early-life development, and has been associated with health outcomes across the lifespan. (Mitchell et al., 2016) A recent study from China identified DNA methylation changes in the placenta following prenatal OPE exposure. (Chen, 2024) This study focused on and identified DNA methylation changes at a select number of genes involved in the PPAR signaling pathway, demonstrating the potential for prenatal OPE exposure to impact the methylome. A study in zebrafish identified altered DNA methyltransferase expression and aberrant global DNA methylation changes following OPE exposure. (Negi et al., 2024) Environmental exposures and pregnancy conditions have previously been shown to impact epigenetic aging at birth, (Ladd-Acosta, 2023) which reflects an individual’s cellular, or biological, age relative to their chronological age, and prior work has demonstrated that prenatal OPE exposure is associated with shortened gestational length and preterm birth. (Oh, 2024) Thus, understanding how prenatal OPE exposure may impact DNA methylation outcomes is of interest. However, to our knowledge, no studies have investigated the effect of prenatal exposure to OPEs on genome-scale DNA methylation changes or epigenetic age outcomes at birth. This study examined urinary OPE biomarker measures collected during pregnancy and genome-scale DNA methylation data from cord blood in the ECHO Cohort. The overarching goal of this project was to characterize DNA methylation signatures at birth that are associated with prenatal OPE biomarker exposure.

METHODS

Study design

ECHO is an observational cohort study of children recruited at 69 sites across the United States (U.S.) and is dedicated to understanding the role of the environment in child health and development. (Blackwell et al., 2018; Bush, 2020; Jacobson et al., 2018; Gillman and Blaisdell, 2018; Knapp, 2023) In total, 736 mother-infant pairs with DNA methylation and OPE biomarker data were eligible for inclusion in this study. Pairs were included in the epigenetic age analysis if children had DNA methylation assessment via the Infinium 27 K, 450 K, or EPIC arrays in cord blood samples collected at birth and if mothers had OPE concentrations measured in urine samples collected at least once in the second trimester (68 %) or third trimester (32 %). Mother-infant pairs were restricted to those with 450 K or EPIC data (n = 577) for the global DNA methylation and locus-specific analyses. This study used data available through the ECHO data repository as of the data lock on March 31, 2023. The study protocol was approved by the single ECHO institutional review board (IRB). Original data collection from each observational site, prior to the launch of the ECHO Cohort, was approved by their local IRBs, and all study participants (parents) provided informed consent. Child participants included in the analysis were born between 2008 and 2020 and were recruited from 7 participating ECHO sites: The Healthy Start Study, MADRES (Maternal And Developmental Risks from Environmental and Social Stressors), New Hampshire Birth Cohort Study, CANDLE (Conditions Affecting Neurocognitive Development and Learning in Early Childhood), Vitamin C, EARLI (Early Autism Risk Longitudinal Investigation), and Fair Start.

OPE biomarker measures

The urinary OPE biomarker analysis was performed as previously described in detail in Oh et al. 2024. (Oh, 2024; Hernandez-Castro, 2023) Briefly, urine samples were collected and shipped on dry ice from each site to the Wadsworth Center — Human Health Exposure Analysis Resource (WC-HHEAR) laboratory. (Oh, 2024) Solid-phase extraction, followed by high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry, was used to identify and quantify OPEs. (Oh, 2024) A Kinetex hydrophilic interaction lipid chromatography column with a Betasil C18 guard column was used to separate OPE biomarkers and isotopically labeled internal standards. (Oh, 2024).

Quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC) procedures were previously described in detail in Oh et al. 2024. (Oh, 2024) Briefly, QC samples included synthetic and urine pool samples that were spiked with 1 ng of the native standard and 1 ng of the internal standard that were analyzed alongside study samples with every batch. (Oh, 2024) To account for trace levels of OPE biomarkers detected in reagent blanks, OPE biomarker concentrations in study samples were subtracted from the corresponding reagent blank values. (Oh, 2024).

Of the 9 OPE biomarkers included in the panel, we selected the following 5 OPE biomarkers to include in our study given that they had the highest detection frequencies: bis(butoxyethyl) phosphate (BBOEP), bis(2-chloroethyl) phosphate (BCETP), bis(1,3-dichloro-2-propyl) phosphate (BDCPP), a composite of dibutyl phosphate and di-isobutyl phosphate (DBUP/DIBP), and diphenyl phosphate (DPHP). DBUP/DIBP is reported as a composite sum of both analytes because they coeluted and could not be quantified individually. (Oh, 2024).

Biospecimen collection and DNA methylation assessment

Sites collected cord blood biospecimens through approved site-specific protocols. DNA methylation was measured using the Infinium HumanMethylation27 (27 K) BeadChip array, Infinium Human-Methylation450 (450 K) BeadChip array, or Infinium MethylationEPIC (EPIC) v1 array (Illumina, San Diego, USA). iDAT files containing raw DNA methylation data from each ECHO site with available data were imported into the minfi processing pipeline in R to calculate ‘beta’ values for each probe and sample; beta values (estimating the proportion of DNA methylation at each locus from 0 to 1) were calculated as previously described. (Ladd-Acosta, 2023) Sample and probe level filters were applied to the data as follows. (Ladd-Acosta, 2023) Briefly, samples were removed if discrepancies were detected between reported and predicted sex, a low overall signal intensity was observed, duplicates were present, >1% of probes for the sample had a detection p-value > 0.05 (compared with the background signal), or > 1 % of probes had a bead count < 3. Probes were removed if > 1 % of samples had a detection p-value > 0.05, >1% of samples had a bead count < 3, probes were cross-reactive, or the queried CpG site had a known SNP. Using the noob function in minfi, background correction and within-sample normalization were performed on the filtered data sets separately for each methylation platform. (Ladd-Acosta, 2023) Additional sample filters were applied to the noob-corrected data sets based on age and tissue-type discrepancies to achieve clean data sets for each platform for analysis. (Ladd-Acosta, 2023) Among those with BBOEP, BCETP, BDCPP, DPHP, and DBUP_DBIP concentrations, 395 cord blood biospecimens had DNA methylation data from the 450 K array (Healthy Start, n = 274; New Hampshire Birth Cohort Study, n = 91; EARLI, n = 30); 182 cord blood biospecimens had DNA methylation data from the EPIC array (MADRES, n = 128; Vitamin C, n = 30; Fair Start, n = 24); and 159 cord blood biospecimens had DNA methylation data from the 27 K array (CANDLE).

Confounders and technical covariates

We created a directed acyclic graph (DAG) to show the main effect we are testing and the relationship between other variables that could influence these effects to determine a set of covariates to adjust for in our statistical models. As shown in Fig. S1, we test the effect of OPE levels, measured in maternal urine samples from pregnancy, and neonate cord blood DNA methylation measures. We examined 3 units of DNA methylation: global methylation, the epigenetic aging pathway, and methylation at individual CpGs. We considered factors that could influence associations between our exposure and outcome, both technical and non-technical, and were not postulated to be in the causal pathway (i.e. confounders). We decided to include maternal age, education, smoking, parity, and body mass index (BMI) based on prior literature showing associations with neonate DNA methylation levels. We included methylation measurement batch and blood cell heterogeneity as technical covariates because differences in the frequency of exposure and/or outcome levels related to these factors could influence the detection of associations. Finally, we examined child sex as a modifier to evaluate whether the effects of OPEs on child methylation levels differ by sex given prior reports of differences in OPE effects on health outcomes by sex. (Oh, 2024) Covariates harmonized across sites included maternal age at birth, maternal education (less than high school; high school or General Educational Development [GED]; some college, no degree, and above), maternal pre-pregnancy BMI in kg/m2, prenatal smoking (yes, no), parity (0, greater than 0), child sex assigned at birth (male, female), DNA methylation measurement batch, and cell composition estimates. Demographic data were obtained from participant questionnaires or via electronic medical records.

Statistical approach

OPE measures and urinary dilution

The limit of detection (LOD) of the target analytes ranged from 0.01–0.04 ng/mL. For this analysis, we included OPEs where 70 % or more of our study samples had measures above the LOD. Five OPEs met this criteria. For those samples with concentrations below the LOD, we imputed values based on an analyte-specific LOD. (Oh, 2024) When mothers had multiple OPE biomarker measures (n = 4), we used the concentration based on the sample collected at the earliest maternal gestational age. Given that urinary biomarker concentrations are affected by hydration status, we applied an approach described by Kuiper et al. to account for hydration status. (Oh, 2024; Kuiper et al., 2021; Kuiper, 2022) Urinary dilution was measured using either specific gravity or creatinine levels, depending on the site. For each site, the median creatinine or specific gravity value was calculated and the OPE biomarker concentrations were multiplied by the ratio of the site-specific median dilution value to the mother’s dilution value. For specific gravity, values were first subtracted by 1. (Oh, 2024; Kuiper et al., 2021; Kuiper, 2022) We modeled OPE biomarkers as log2-transformed, dilution-standardized concentrations to mitigate the influence of outliers, achieving comparable or slightly improved Akaike Information Criterion values compared with models using untransformed concentrations, consistent with other ECHO analyses. (Oh, 2024).

Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all continuous and categorical variables across all samples for each site. For OPE biomarker concentrations, the median, interquartile range (IQR), minimum, and maximum for each analyte were calculated, and we examined correlations across individual OPE biomarkers. When missingness was present among demographic variables and was found to be unrelated to the exposures, we applied the following imputation method. Using the first step of multiple imputation for categorical variables with missingness, the distributions of the complete data were defined, and random samples were drawn from these distributions, as previously described. (Petroff, 2023; Schrott, 2025) This method utilizes the first step of multiple imputation, but it does not run the analysis multiple times. Any imputation of the major category is by chance. (Petroff, 2023; Schrott, 2025) For continuous variables with missingness (e.g., maternal BMI), we imputed using the site-specific mean. R version 4.2 was used for statistical analyses unless otherwise specified.

Cell composition estimates

Given that cord blood is a bulk tissue sample comprised of multiple cell types, there is a need to adjust for differences in the proportion of each cell type present in the cord blood samples collected from different individuals. The estimateCellCounts() function from the minfi Bio-conductor package was used to generate cell composition estimates for each individual, and the compositeCellType argument was set to “cord blood.” Cell types for cord blood samples include granulocytes, monocytes, natural killer cells, B cells, nucleated red blood cells, CD4 cells, and CD8 cells.

Global DNA methylation analysis

For each sample with 450 K or EPIC array data, we derived five global DNA methylation measures. The first measure is called ‘all’ and was derived by taking the mean beta value for all overlapping CpG sites measured on the 450 K and EPIC arrays. Four additional global methylation measures were derived using Illumina gene annotation information that defined whether a CpG measured by a probe on the array was located in a (i) CpG island, (ii) shelf, (iii) shore, or (iv) open sea. (Balduzzi et al., 2019) Data from the 27 K array were not included given that it would meaningfully limit the number of CpGs that could be included in the global methylation measure. Then, within each cohort, we performed linear regression for each global methylation measure and for each OPE biomarker, adjusting for maternal age, maternal education, maternal BMI, prenatal tobacco use, parity, child sex, cell composition estimates, and DNA methylation measurement batch, which captures genome-scale shifts in methylation values that can result from processing samples at different times and in different laboratories. We performed a random-effects meta-analysis across cohorts using the meta package in R. (Balduzzi et al., 2019) To determine the impact of OPEs on global DNA methylation in a sex-specific manner, we also performed secondary analyses stratified by sex, where sex was removed from the covariate adjustment set. We were not sufficiently powered to perform a meta-analysis by cohort for sex-specific analyses, and instead, mega-analyzed across cohorts within an array (i.e., EPIC or 450 k) and then meta-analyzed across arrays using meta. This ensured that we were still accounting for potential technical differences between the two arrays. To support the validity of this approach, we first ran the total population analysis using this method and compared the results with the cohort meta-analysis approach where we had grossly similar main effect findings (Fig. S2).

Epigenome-Wide association testing

Epigenome-wide association tests between prenatal OPE biomarkers and DNA methylation were restricted to children with 450 K and EPIC array data, and analyses were performed separately for each site. Children with 27 K data were not included given that it would meaningfully limit the number of CpGs we could assess. For individual CpG analyses, we used a linear regression model of the M methylation value (logit-transformed beta methylation value) at each CpG site as the dependent variable and the OPE biomarker as the independent variable, and we adjusted for covariates, including maternal age, child sex, maternal education, maternal BMI, prenatal tobacco use, parity, DNA methylation batches, and cell composition estimates. We implemented this modeling using the lmFit() function from the limma R package for each DNA methylation probe that passed QC. (Andrews, 2018) After completing association testing for each site individually, we meta-analyzed data across the 6 sites using an inverse variance-weighted approach with the METAL software on the 361,610 probes that were in common across the 450 K and EPIC arrays. Lambda values were calculated for the meta-analyzed results to ensure reasonable control of genomic inflation. (Willer et al., 2010) The false discovery rate (FDR) was computed using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, (Korthauer, 2019) and CpG sites were annotated to genes using the genome assembly GRCh37/hg19 of the human genome browser. This analysis was not powered to perform an epigenome-wide association study (EWAS) stratified by sex.

ipDMR

Differentially methylated region (DMR) analyses were performed using the meta-analysis summary statistic results output from our epigenome-wide association testing of single CpGs (see 2.5.4.) and the ipDMR() function from the ENmix R package. (Xu et al., 2021) Using summary statistics from each probe tested, the ipDMR function calculates a p-value for the interval bordered by two adjacent CpG sites within the specified value of 1,000 base pairs (bp). After performing the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure on the interval p-values, we selected those with an FDR ≤ 0.1. All nearby significant intervals and CpGs less than 1,000 bp apart were joined, and a p-value for the combined region was recalculated using the original p-value for all CpGs in the region. A final FDR correction was performed on the region p-values to obtain the FDR-adjusted p-value. (Xu et al., 2021) We filtered for DMRs that consisted of three or more consecutive CpG sites in a 1,000-bp region, and a FDR < 0.05 was considered significant. This study was not powered to perform DMR analyses stratified by sex.

Epigenetic age Calculation and analysis

Epigenetic age analyses, which included the full sample (n = 736), were performed as previously described. (Schrott et al., 2024) We used the Knight gestational epigenetic clock based on: 1) its robust previous performance in the ECHO Cohort, 2) the availability of data from multiple Illumina array platforms (27 K, 450 K, and EPIC), and 3) the wide range of gestational ages in this study. (Knight, 2016) We first estimated epigenetic age within a platform (e.g. all samples on 27 K were run through the Knight clock algorithm together) to minimize practical and potential batch technical differences in our data. We then took the estimated epigenetic age (e.g. this is a single derived epigenetic age variable per person) and regressed this on chronologic age to generate residuals for downstream association testing between each OPE and accelerated/decelerated epigenetic aging. For these regression models, we combined the derived epigenetic age variables across platforms. Extrinsic age acceleration (EAA) was calculated using a linear regression model with newborn chronological gestational age at birth (in completed weeks) as the independent variable and newborn epigenetic age as the dependent variable. Extracted residuals from the linear regression model represent individuals’ EAA. Intrinsic age acceleration (IAA) was calculated using a linear regression model with newborn chronological gestational age at birth as the independent variable and newborn gestational epigenetic age as the dependent variable, adjusted for estimated cell proportions based on tissue type. We included both EAA and IAA as independent outcomes in statistical models—two commonly used epigenetic age measures—given that IAA captures cellular age without confounding by blood cell proportions, which are known to change, whereas EAA incorporates intrinsic measures and blood cell proportion estimates. (Smith, 2019) The residuals extracted from the models represent the individuals’ IAA. Linear regression models were fitted with prenatal maternal OPE exposure levels as the exposure. EAA and IAA were tested independently as outcomes in crude and fully adjusted models. Fully adjusted models included the covariates site, maternal age, maternal education, maternal BMI, prenatal tobacco use, parity, and child sex. Point estimates, 95 % confidence intervals (CIs), and p-values are reported, with a 2-sided p-value < 0.05 considered a significant association between OPE exposure and newborn gestational epigenetic age. Analyses were stratified by sex, and sex was removed from the covariate adjustment set.

Results

Study Demographics

A total of 736 mother-infant pairs had prenatal OPE biomarker measures and DNA methylation data measured in cord blood across one of the three Illumina arrays (Fig. 1). Demographic information for the included participants can be found in Table 1. The mean maternal age was 29.04 years, and most infants were born at full term with a mean gestational age of 39 weeks. Most mothers had more than 1 child (53.4 %); were non-smoking (88.6 %), non-Hispanic (72.3 %), and White (71.6 %); and had an education level of some college or more (57.3 %). This study included an even number of male (51.1 %) and female (48.9 %) children. For global and epigenome-wide analyses, we restricted our analysis to 577 mother–child pairs with child DNA methylation data measured on only the EPIC v1 and 450 K arrays, although the demographic information was nearly identical to that of the full analytic sample (Table S1).

OPE biomarkers

Overall, 736 mother–child pairs had BBOEP, BCETP, BDCPP, DPHP, and DBUP_DIBP concentrations measured in maternal urine during pregnancy, of which 234 were taken from the second trimester and 502 from the third trimester. The concentrations of OPEs were similar across the two trimesters (Table S2). The detection frequencies and distributions of dilution-standardized urinary OPE biomarker concentrations are presented in Table S3a, and Fig. S3 shows the distribution of dilution-standardized urinary OPE biomarker levels by year and cohort. The detection frequencies for BBOEP, BCETP, BDCPP, DPHP, and DBUP_DIBP were all ≥ 70 %. The highest median concentration was observed for DPHP (0.96 ng/mL), followed by BDCPP (0.88 ng/mL), BCETP (0.70 ng/mL), DBUP_DIBP (0.17 ng/mL), and BBOEP (0.05 ng/mL). OPE biomarkers were weakly correlated with each other (corr −0.007 to 0.410, Table S3b).

Global DNA methylation findings

We identified significant differences in global DNA methylation related to prenatal exposure to BDCPP (Fig. 2 and Table S4), and point estimates with 95 % confidence intervals for each cohort are shown in Fig. S4. Specifically, we observed significant hypomethylation at ‘all’ (coef = −0.00015, p = 0.005), ‘open sea’ (coef = −0.00018, p = 0.009), ‘shelf’ (coef = −0.00018, p = 0.021), and ‘shore’ (coef = −0.00020, p = 0.003) regions in association with higher concentrations of BDCPP. No significant associations between global methylation and OPE biomarkers were observed for the other OPEs assessed.

Meta-analysis findings

EWAS (n = 361,610 CpG sites) findings for each OPE biomarker are presented in Sup. Tables S5–S9. The lambda values for the meta-analyses were as follows: BBOEP = 1.07, BCETP = 0.88, BDCPP = 1.28, DBUP_DIBP = 1.12, and DPHP = 0.92. Following FDR correction (q < 0.05), no CpG sites were significantly associated with prenatal OPE biomarkers.

Differentially methylated regions

We identified significant associations between regional DNA methylation levels and prenatal OPE biomarkers. After applying the ipDMR function and filtering for DMRs that contained three or more CpG sites in a 1,000-bp region, we identified 1 DMR associated with BDCPP and 1 DMR associated with DPHP (Table 2). The DMR associated with BDCPP was annotated to the gene PCDHGB1 (FDR q < 0.05), consisted of 3 CpG sites, and was located in a North Shore relative to a CpG island. The DMR associated with DPHP was annotated to the gene SLC43A2, contained 3 CpG sites, and was located in a CpG island.

Epigenetic age findings

We did not identify any significant associations between prenatal OPE biomarkers and newborn gestational epigenetic age for any OPE biomarker (Table S10).

Sex-Specific findings

Global DNA methylation findings

Global methylation analyses were performed as described, with males (n = 304) and females (n = 273) analyzed separately in both crude and fully adjusted models, adjusting for the covariates listed above. In adjusted models, we detected significant hypermethylation at ‘shore’ regions in relation to DBUP_DIBP in males (Table S11 and Fig. 3; coef = 0.0004, p = 0.048), and point estimates with 95 % confidence intervals for each platform are shown in Fig. S5. We detected significant differences in global DNA methylation related to BDCPP and DBUP_DIBP in adjusted models in females (Fig. 4 and Table S12), and point estimates with 95 % confidence intervals for each platform are shown in Fig. S6. Specifically, we observed a significant decrease in global DNA methylation at ‘shore’ (coef = −0.0002, p = 0.007), ‘open sea’ (coef = −0.0002, p = 0.03), and ‘all’ (coef = −0.0002, p = 0.03) regions in relation to increased prenatal BDCPP concentrations. We observed a significant decrease in global DNA methylation at ‘all’ (coef = −0.0007, p = 0.01), ‘shelf’ (coef = −0.0008, p = 0.02), ‘shore’ (coef = −0.0006, p = 0.01), and ‘open sea’ (coef = −0.0007, p = 0.01) regions in relation to increased DBUP_DIBP concentrations.

Epigenetic age

In sex-stratified epigenetic age analyses, prenatal BCETP concentrations were significantly associated with decelerated epigenetic aging at birth in females in crude and adjusted EAA models and adjusted IAA models (Table 3; coef = −0.05, p < 0.05). The relationship was not significant in males; however, the direction of the estimated effect was opposite than that observed in females in crude and adjusted EAA and IAA models (Table 3). No other significant associations were detected. A summary of epigenetic age by sex can be found in Table S13. No substantive differences were observed between male participants (mean = 38.5 weeks, standard deviation = 1.36 weeks) and female participants (mean = 38.8 weeks, standard deviation = 1.5 weeks).

Discussion

This study is among the first to examine associations between prenatal OPE exposure and epigenetic outcomes at birth. We investigated whether BBOEP, BCETP, BDCPP, DBUP_DIBP, or DPHP concentrations in prenatal maternal urine were associated with global DNA methylation, locus-specific and regional DNA methylation, and newborn gestational epigenetic age changes at birth, representing several approaches for analyzing DNA methylation data. In our total sample analysis, we identified two DMRs that were associated with prenatal OPE biomarkers; one was associated with BDCPP and one was associated with DPHP. Global analyses revealed hypomethylation at ‘all,’ ‘open sea,’ and ‘shelf’ regions in relation to prenatal BDCPP concentrations. Additional findings in our total sample analyses were non-significant. We identified sex-specific associations between prenatal OPE biomarkers and global methylation and epigenetic age outcomes, with effects more pronounced in females than males. Global methylation changes were identified in females in response to prenatal BDCPP concentrations – hypomethylation at ‘shore,’ ‘open sea,’ and ‘all’ regions – and DBUP_DIBP concentrations – hypomethylation at ‘shore,’ ‘shelf,’ ‘open sea,’ and ‘all’ regions. Conversely, higher concentrations of prenatal DBUP_DIBP were associated with hypermethylation in males at ‘shore’ regions. We further found that prenatal BCETP concentrations were associated with decelerated epigenetic age at birth in females. We did identify interesting, opposing patterns of effect between males and females in epigenetic age analyses beyond those that were significant. This is the first large-scale study to address genome-scale epigenetic changes at birth in relation to prenatal OPE exposure; therefore, these findings should be replicated in other populations. In addition, the methylation changes we identified should be investigated for their functional impact on gene expression, pathways, and phenotypes through experimental models and potentially human observational studies where appropriate. Our results, linking prenatal OPE exposure to epigenetic outcomes, fill a large gap in the literature surrounding the physiologic effects of this prevalent class of flame retardants. This is important given that epigenetic mechanisms are hypothesized to be a conduit linking the prenatal exposome to a broad diversity of child health outcomes. (Doherty et al., 2019; Shahin et al., 2024; Zhao, 2022).

OPE exposure during pregnancy has been associated with a broad suite of adverse health outcomes both for the pregnant person and their child. Many of these outcomes stem from the putative endocrine-disrupting functions of this class of chemicals. For example, impaired fertility, thyroid hormone disruption, and cancers have all been linked to exposure to OPEs. (Doherty et al., 2019; Shahin et al., 2024; Zhao, 2022) Beyond endocrine disruption, OPE exposure has been associated with alterations in fetal growth, impaired neurodevelopment, metabolic outcomes, and immune system dysfunction. (Shahin et al., 2024) Whether other maternal characteristics, such a maternal BMI, may interact with OPE exposure in ways that either amplify or mitigate the effects of either exposure on both DNAm and child health outcomes is an important area for future investigation.

The global hypomethylation that we observed at multiple regions in response to multiple OPEs is an important finding given that hypomethylation is broadly associated with negative health outcomes, including cancer, immune system dysregulation, and aging. (Ehrlich, 2009; Hon, 2012; Unnikrishnan, 2018) Methylation functions, in part, to silence genes and regulate gene activity; however, global dysregulation of DNA methylation can have unintended effects, some of which may be associated with the health outcomes shown to be vulnerable to OPE exposure. (Ehrlich, 2009; Hon, 2012; Unnikrishnan, 2018) In our total sample analysis, we identified less DNA methylation, on average, in all, shore, shelf, and open sea regions among individuals with increased prenatal exposure to BDCPP but no significant changes in DNA methylation for CpG island regions. No prior studies have examined global methylation changes in OPEs. Three recent studies (Chen, 2024; Simner, 2017; Mancia Leon, 2020) have examined the association between OPE levels and DNA methylation but only assessed single genes, CpGs, or differentially methylated regions, including two where DNA methylation was measured in the placenta and one in cord blood. Only one of these prior studies (Simner, 2017) found that placenta methylation in the DIO3 and TTR genes was associated with intermediate BDCPP levels. The lack of findings in prior studies compared with ours could be due to the limited sample sizes and scope of methylome examined, differences in exposure variability, and differences in biospecimen source for two of those prior studies. Our BDCPP hypomethylation findings may have biologic implications particularly because we observed changes outside of CpG island regions but not within islands. CpG islands are located near gene promoter regions where they are directly responsible for regulating gene expression while shores, shelves, and open sea regions typically consist of other types of regulatory elements (e.g. enhancers and chromosomal stability). (El Hajj, 2016; Guetg, 2015) This indicates that the changes in methylation that we observed for BDCPP may have more broad regulatory biologic function than controlling any one specific gene.

The genes we identified in our DMR analyses are involved in these processes as well, including fetal growth, (Simner, 2017) neurodevelopment, (Mancia Leon, 2020; El Hajj, 2016) thyroid hormone production, (Guetg, 2015; Zevenbergen, 2015) and cancer. (Vega-Benedetti, 2019) We identified hypomethylation at the gene PCDHGB1 in relation to higher BDCPP concentrations. The protein encoded by this gene is involved in the establishment and maintenance of neural circuit formation in the developing brain and is a member of the protocadherin gamma gene cluster. (Mancia Leon, 2020; El Hajj, 2016) Epigenetic dysregulation of this gene cluster has been identified in the developing cortex of individuals with Down syndrome compared with those without. (El Hajj, 2016) More broadly, dysregulation of protocadherin expression has been associated with tumor progression in multiple types of cancer. (Vega-Benedetti, 2019) Interestingly, the expression of protocadherins is under epigenetic control, and often, clustered protocadherins are hypermethylated and subsequently silenced in cancers. (Vega-Benedetti, 2019).

The second DMR we identified was annotated to the gene SLC43A2. We observed hypomethylation at this region, located in a CpG island, in response to higher DPHP concentrations. SLC43A2 is a member of the L-amino acid transporter-3 family of transporters that plays important roles in mediating transport of amino acids, including the transfer of amino acids across the placenta to the fetus during pregnancy. (Simner, 2017) Slc43a2 knockout mice displayed intrauterine growth restriction and low levels of amino acids present in amniotic fluid, suggesting defects in transplacental amino acid transport. (Guetg, 2015) Further, these mice had reduced postnatal growth and increased rates of premature death, suggesting that Slc43a2 is necessary for early-life development. (Guetg, 2015) Studies suggest that SLC43A2 may indirectly influence thyroid hormone production given that it regulates transport, and subsequent availability, of amino acids that are necessary for thyroid hormone synthesis. (Guetg, 2015; Zevenbergen, 2015) Thus, dysregulation of this gene may lead to altered thyroid hormone production. Dynamic methylation changes at this gene have been identified in response to therapeutic intervention in individuals with a history of panic disorder, suggesting that perhaps other exposures may similarly be able to elicit changes in methylation, as we see here. (Ziegler, 2019) In cancer, SLC43A2 has been shown to alter T cell methionine metabolism, resulting in decreased histone methylation and reduced anti-tumorigenic activity of T cells, suggesting that this gene is also implicated in cancer, metabolism, and immune system regulation. (Bian, 2020).

In our sex-stratified epigenetic age analyses, we identified decelerated gestational epigenetic age in females in association with prenatal BCETP concentrations in both EAA and IAA adjusted models. When considering gestational epigenetic age in relation to chronologic age at birth, it is plausible that decelerated gestational epigenetic age may reflect a biologically/cellular preterm state. Interestingly, previously work in the ECHO Cohort found that prenatal exposure to higher levels of BCETP was associated with a higher odds of being born preterm and a shorter gestational age among females. (Oh, 2024) This suggests that decelerated epigenetic age may be a biological mechanism through which OPE exposure acts prenatally to influence birth outcomes, and as such, the relationship between OPEs, epigenetic age, and gestational length requires further investigation. Interestingly, other notable sex differences in child health outcomes have been observed following prenatal OPE exposure. In a recent review, the authors demonstrated that across studies, increased OPE exposure was associated with a number of birth outcomes in females in particular, including a shorter gestational duration, increased risk of preterm birth, lower birth weight, shorter birth length, and lower abdominal circumference. (Oh, 2024; Gan et al., 2023; Ouidir, 2020) The potential for greater sensitivity to prenatal OPE exposure in female fetuses both at the molecular and phenotypic levels is further supported by the global methylation findings in this study, which were more pronounced in females than males. Future studies should investigate whether DNA methylation changes may be mechanistically implicated in mediating exposure-outcome relationships in a sex-specific manner and should consider other epigenetic analyses, such as histone modifications and chromatin accessibility.

We note several limitations of this analysis. Our sample sizes were relatively small, which may have limited our ability to detect all significant associations, and our ability to perform sex-stratified analyses at the locus- or region-specific levels. Further, our sex-specific global DNAm analysis may have been limited in our ability to detect all significant associations because the mega-analysis approach could have removed meaningful variability in exposures across cohorts. Despite this, we identified several relationships between prenatal OPE exposure and DNA methylation outcomes at birth. Second, given that we had DNA methylation measures across the 450 K and EPIC (v1) platforms, we limited the number of CpG sites that we could include in our EWAS analyses to those sites that were present on both arrays. Thus, it is possible that there are additional CpG sites that may be associated with prenatal OPE exposure that we did not examine. The maternal OPE measures were generated from samples collected at a single timepoint; thus, they may only represent a snapshot in time and may not reflect the extent of OPE exposure over the course of an entire pregnancy. While there are no precisely known half-life values for each of the OPEs included in this study, the half-life of OPEs is generally on the order of hours to days and thus their presence in urine is thought to primarily reflect a recent exposure. Third, there may be differences in epigenetic clock performance by self-reported racial and ethnic groups. (Fang, 2023) Additionally, this study was not powered to perform trimester-stratified analyses to identify sensitive exposure windows during pregnancy. We included all data available, which were collected from women during the second and third trimesters. We did not have OPEs measured from specimens collected in the first trimester. While this limitation bears no impact on the DNAm differences related to OPE levels in the second and third trimesters, future studies could determine whether specific methylation patterns are related to trimester-specific critical windows of OPE exposure. Only one other recent study, to our knowledge, has investigated this relationship; however, their epigenetic outcomes were limited to placental CpG sites in 14 genes involved in PPAR signaling in 327 individuals. (Chen, 2024) Two specific OPEs overlapped between our study and this previous study: BBOEP and BDCPP. Chen et al (Chen, 2024) found methylation associations in one gene for BBOEP and none for BDCPP. We did not find any methylation changes in this gene among our list of the top 1000 probes (based on p-value) for any of the five OPEs examined here. There are several possible reasons for this difference in methylation. The previous methylation finding could be a false positive or placenta-specific (we examined cord blood and not placenta), or the specific CpG(s) may not have been measured on EPIC or 450 K arrays. Finally, while we included a robust set of confounders in our statistical models, it is possible that there were additional unknown sources of confounding, which we did not adjust or account for in our models.

These limitations, however, are balanced out by several notable strengths of this study. This is among the first studies to analyze the effect of prenatal OPE exposure on DNA methylation outcomes at birth. We characterized the effect of prenatal OPE exposure on multiple DNA methylation units of measurement – global DNA methylation, locus- and region-specific DNA methylation, and epigenetic age. Additionally, we were able to include 5 OPEs in this analysis, allowing us to make meaningful contributions to the field about the health impact of multiple chemicals from this broader chemical class. Finally, the ECHO Cohort represents a population with diverse racial, ethnic, geographic, and educational backgrounds in the U.S., which allows these results to have increased generalizability to the broader U.S. population.

Conclusions

We found that prenatal exposure to certain OPEs was associated with global hypomethylation in shore, shelf, and island regions, and differential methylation in two genes instrumental to early development. Further, we identified several sex-specific differences with respect to global and gestational epigenetic age outcomes. This is the first time this relationship has been investigated at this scope and scale, thus contributing to a major gap in the literature. The genes we identified have functions that are relevant to child health outcomes that have been associated with prenatal OPE exposure. Further, we detected decelerated epigenetic aging in females in response to OPE exposure that has previously been associated with premature birth in females. Our findings contribute to the broader literature, suggesting that DNA methylation is a biologic target of a wide variety of prenatal chemical exposures, and we define the specific molecular effects of prenatal OPE exposure. Future studies should investigate whether these DNA methylation changes, or other epigenetic markers, are mechanistically implicated as mediators of adverse child health outcomes associated with prenatal OPE exposure. An improved understanding of this relationship may allow us to reduce the burden of chemical exposures on children’s health.
