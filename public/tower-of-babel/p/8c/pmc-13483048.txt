Molecular imaging-guided discovery of a potent natural FAPα inhibitor for liver fibrosis therapy
Authors: Wenze Zhang, Jiwei Li, Lei Sun, Chaoran Li, Kaiyu Zhang, Zhiying He, Guangyi Yang, Boyang Yu, Fengguo Xu, Haijun Xu, Xianchuang Zheng, Jiangwei Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiver fibrosis can progress to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, yet effective antifibrotic therapies remain limited. Fibroblast activation protein α (FAPα) is a promising biomarker and therapeutic target, but existing probes are constrained by limited tissue penetration and single-modality imaging, and effective FAPα-targeted antifibrotic agents remain lacking. Here we show that Cs-FAP, a hemicyanine-based near-infrared fluorescence/photoacoustic dual-modal probe, enables real-time and noninvasive visualization of FAPα activity in fibrotic liver tissue. Using a Cs-FAP-guided screening platform integrating high-content imaging and chromatographic purification, we screened 95 medicinal herbs and identified sappanchalcone (Sap) as a potent low-toxicity FAPα inhibitor. Sap markedly attenuated fibrosis progression and improved liver function in multiple liver fibrosis models using male mice. Mechanistically, Sap suppressed hepatic stellate cell activation through inhibition of the FAPα–PI3K–AKT signaling pathway. These findings establish Cs-FAP as a versatile platform for fibrosis imaging and drug discovery and identify Sap as a promising antifibrotic candidate.Subject terms: High-throughput screening, Molecular imaging

Introduction

Hepatic fibrosis is a common pathological consequence of chronic liver injury and inflammation and represents the pivotal intermediate stage that drives the progression from chronic liver disease to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma1–4. Although early fibrosis is reversible, sustained fibrogenesis leads to irreversible architectural distortion and liver failure. A 2025 report by the Global Liver Institute states that 844 million people worldwide currently suffer from chronic liver disease, causing 2 million deaths each year and generating a huge health and economic burden. Current clinical management focuses on treating underlying etiologies, however, once fibrosis is established, patients continue to experience substantial morbidity and mortality despite etiologic control5–8. Reliable tools for early and accurate detection remain lacking: non-invasive serological or imaging-based assessments provide only limited diagnostic precision, whereas liver biopsy, the clinical gold standard, is invasive and unsuitable for longitudinal monitoring9. Therapeutic options are equally limited. Currently, only two antifibrotic agents, resmetirom and semaglutide, have been approved for clinical use. However, their broader therapeutic implementation remains constrained by factors such as cost considerations, treatment tolerability, and a relatively narrow indication10,11. These limitations underscore an urgent unmet need for sensitive, minimally invasive diagnostic strategies and mechanism-driven antifibrotic interventions capable of intercepting fibrogenesis at its early and potentially reversible stage.

Fibroblast activation protein α (FAPα) is a serine protease that is minimally expressed in healthy tissues but markedly induced in activated hepatic stellate cells (HSCs)12. Through its roles in extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, HSCs activation, and the amplification of inflammatory signaling, FAPα functions as a key molecular driver of hepatic fibrosis13,14. Its restricted expression in pathological fibroblasts, together with its strong linear association with fibrosis severity, highlights FAPα as a quantitative and disease-relevant biomarker that surpasses conventional indicators such as α-SMA, hyaluronic acid, and collagen15,16. Although multiple analytical techniques, including RT-PCR, immunohistochemistry, and mass spectrometry, can detect FAPα, optical imaging offers distinct advantages for biological interrogation owing to its sensitivity, spatiotemporal resolution, and compatibility with in vivo applications. Nevertheless, existing FAPα-responsive fluorescent probes are constrained by short emission wavelengths and single-modality readouts (Supplementary Table 1)17–19. Integrating photoacoustic (PA) imaging—characterized by deep-tissue penetration and three-dimensional resolution—with near-infrared (NIR) fluorescence could overcome these limitations20–22. Therefore, the development of FAPα-activatable NIR probes capable of dual PA/FL imaging is essential for resolving FAPα dynamics in liver fibrosis, improving diagnostic accuracy, and enabling real-time evaluation of antifibrotic interventions.

Although a broad spectrum of FAPα inhibitors has been developed, most have been evaluated predominantly in tumor diagnosis and therapy rather than in fibrotic diseases23–25. Emerging evidence indicates that suppressing or downregulating FAPα mitigates HSC activation, dampens pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and interrupts profibrotic signaling cascades, establishing FAPα inhibition as a compelling strategy to restrain fibrosis progression26–28. Despite this promise, no FAPα inhibitor has yet been purposefully designed or optimized for liver fibrosis. A few candidates, such as UAMC1110, BR103354 and AZD2389, have demonstrated preliminary antifibrotic efficacy, but their potency, selectivity, and translational reliability remain insufficient for therapeutic deployment (Supplementary Table 2)28–31. Collectively, these limitations highlight a critical unmet need for next-generation FAPα inhibitors with improved pharmacological performance and biocompatibility tailored specifically to fibrotic liver disease.

Building on these considerations, we developed a FAPα-responsive NIR FL/PA dual-modal small-molecule probe, Cs-FAP, featuring a hemicyanine scaffold with intracyclic sulfur substitution to balance radiative and nonradiative decay pathways, and incorporating the FAPα-specific substrate peptide benzyloxycarbonyl-glycine-proline (Cbz-Gly-Pro) for precise monitoring of FAPα activity during fibrosis (Fig. 1a). Subsequently, we employed Cs-FAP in a high-throughput screening approach utilizing a hepatoprotective plant extract pool comprising 95 medicinal herbs with potential anti-fibrotic properties. Among these, sappanchalcone (Sap), derived from Sappan Lignum, was identified as an efficient and low-toxicity FAPα inhibitor. Leveraging the powerful dual-modal imaging capabilities of Cs-FAP, we conducted comprehensive assessments of the anti-fibrotic effects of Sap across cellular, 3D liver microtissues, and multiple liver fibrosis mouse models. Furthermore, we investigated the pharmacokinetics of Sap and, through transcriptomic analysis, uncovered its potential mechanism of action. Specifically, Sap appears to inhibit FAPα/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway to suppress HSCs activation, proliferation, and migration, thereby mitigating liver fibrosis (Fig. 1b).

Results

Construction of FAPα-responsive PA/FL probe

The synthesis commenced with the commercially available IR-780 dye. Based on reported reaction32 conditions with subsequent optimization, a dual-mode molecular probe scaffold Cs bearing both FL and PA signals was successfully constructed. To confer FAPα-specific responsiveness, the FAPα-specific substrate peptide Cbz-Gly-Pro was conjugated to the optical probe core Cs via a one-step amidation reaction, yielding the target probe designated as Cs-FAP (Fig. 2a). Cbz-Gly-Pro was selected as the recognition motif as it represents an optimal FAPα-responsive sequence that balances enzymatic specificity, catalytic efficiency, and imaging compatibility. The N-terminal benzyloxycarbonyl (Cbz) group enhances hydrophobic interactions within the substrate-binding pocket of FAPα, thereby improving molecular recognition while effectively suppressing cross-reactivity toward related prolyl peptidases commonly associated with Gly–Pro-based substrates. Importantly, FAPα-mediated cleavage of Cbz-Gly-Pro proceeds with rapid kinetics, enabling efficient signal activation and supporting its suitability for sensitive in vivo imaging applications33,34. Detailed synthetic procedures and characterization data are provided in the Supporting Information (Supplementary Fig. 1-3). The response principle of this probe is illustrated in the Fig. 2b. Upon reaction with FAPα, Cs-FAP undergoes enzymatic cleavage at the amide bond between the proline residue and the Cs core, as FAPα specifically recognizes and hydrolyzes its substrate peptide. This cleavage event releases the optical core, leading to the activation of FL/PA signals.

The spectroscopic response of the Cs-FAP in the presence of FAPα was investigated in vitro. Upon treatment with FAPα, Cs-FAP exhibited a pronounced red shift in its absorption spectrum, with the emergence of a new absorption peak at 740 nm (Fig. 2c). Upon excitation at 740 nm, a significant enhancement in fluorescence intensity was also observed (Fig. 2d). Following the confirmation of Cs-FAP’s response to FAPα, the catalytic reaction conditions were systematically optimized, including incubation time, pH, and temperature (Supplementary Fig. 4). Under the optimized reaction conditions, the response range and detection limits of Cs-FAP toward FAPα were systematically evaluated. As the concentration of FAPα increased, the fluorescence intensity of the reaction solution gradually enhanced (Fig. 2e), exhibiting a good linear relationship within the range of 0–200 ng/mL at the maximum emission wavelength of 765 nm (Fig. 2f). The limit of detection (LOD) was calculated to be 0.667 ng/mL. Furthermore, photoacoustic phantom experiments demonstrated a corresponding increase in PA signal intensity with increasing FAPα concentration (Fig. 2g), displaying a linear PA response in the range of 0–0.8 μg/mL (Fig. 2h), with a LOD of 0.19 μg/mL. To highlight the advantages of Cs-FAP as a dual-mode probe, the related probe FQCy7 was synthesized (Supplementary Fig. 1, 5)19. Notably, upon enzymatic activation, Cs-FAP exhibited a significantly higher nonradiative decay rate (knr) than FQCy7 (12 × 108 s−1 vs 7 × 108 s−1), indicating a more efficient conversion of absorbed optical energy into heat. Consistent with this photophysical advantage, photoacoustic measurements revealed that FQCy7 displayed a markedly weaker signal enhancement upon FAPα activation compared with Cs-FAP (Supplementary Fig. 6). These results indicate that Cs-FAP possesses high sensitivity for dual-mode FL/PA detection of FAPα. Moreover, the Michaelis constant (Km) and the catalytic rate constant (kcat) for FAPα using Cs-FAP as a substrate were determined as shown in Fig. 2i, the kinetic parameters were determined to be Km = 7.16 μM and kcat = 0.12 s⁻¹, resulting in a catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km) of 1.7 × 10⁴ M⁻¹·s⁻¹. The relatively low Km value indicates a high binding affinity between FAPα and Cs-FAP, while the catalytic efficiency further confirms that FAPα can effectively catalyze the cleavage of Cs-FAP, thereby triggering a robust optical signal response. Selectivity studies (Fig. 2j) demonstrated that Cs-FAP exhibited a significant signal response exclusively in the presence of FAPα, while showing negligible responses to other biologically relevant ions, reactive species, and enzymes. These results confirm the high specificity of the probe toward FAPα.

To validate the response mechanism of Cs-FAP, high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was first employed to compare the retention times of Cs-FAP before and after reaction with FAPα, alongside that of the optical core Cs (Fig. 2k). The product after enzymatic cleavage exhibited the same retention time as Cs, indicating that Cs-FAP was fully converted to Cs under the catalytic action of FAPα. Time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) calculations further revealed that the S₁ → S₀ transitions of Cs-FAP and Cs occurred at the LUMO → HOMO levels (Fig. 2m, Supplementary Data 1, 2), with energy gaps of 2.77 eV and 2.07 eV, respectively. Electrostatic potential (ESP) surface analysis (Fig. 2l) indicated intramolecular electron redistribution in Cs-FAP upon conjugation of the Cs core with the recognition moiety. This electron rearrangement accounts for the significant fluorescence quenching observed in Cs-FAP compared to the free core. Molecular docking (Fig. 2n) demonstrated that Cs-FAP could effectively enter the active site of FAPα and adopt a favorable orientation within the catalytic cavity, aligning toward the catalytic center. Further receptor–ligand interaction analysis showed that the key catalytic residue Ser 624 forms a hydrogen bond with Cs-FAP, while His 734 participates in π–π stacking interactions. These interactions promote the activation of Ser 624, enabling a nucleophilic attack on the amide bond, leading to enzymatic hydrolysis and release of the free Cs core. This process underlies the OFF–ON signal activation for FAPα detection.

In the cellular activity assay of FAPα

Before employing Cs-FAP to detect FAPα activity in cells, its cytotoxicity was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay in hepatic stellate cells (LX-2 and JS-1), hepatocytes (AML-12 and HepRG), and the murine macrophage cell line RAW 264.7. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 7a, Cs-FAP exhibited minimal cytotoxicity in all tested cell lines over the concentration range of 0–50 μM. Cellular uptake, analyzed by flow cytometry (Fig. 3b) and confocal laser scanning microscopy (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 7b), showed that LX-2 cells efficiently internalized Cs-FAP after a 30-min incubation. This duration was then chosen as the optimal time for imaging. Photostability tests (Fig. S7c) confirmed that Cs-FAP retained its fluorescence emission under continuous laser exposure, highlighting its suitability for prolonged intracellular imaging. FAPα is a type II transmembrane serine protease predominantly localized on the cell surface. Upon engagement at the plasma membrane, Cs-FAP was rapidly internalized, triggering the release and intracellular accumulation of the fluorescent core CS (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig 8a)35. The efficacy of Cs-FAP for detecting cellular FAPα activity was evaluated using LX-2 cells stimulated with TGF-β to induce FAPα overexpression36,37. As shown in Fig. 3d, f a gradual increase in fluorescence intensity in the Cs-FAP channel was observed with increasing TGF-β concentrations, indicating a dose-dependent upregulation of FAPα expression induced by TGF-β. Consistent with its internalization behavior, colocalization studies (Supplementary Fig. 8b) revealed that Cs-FAP was distributed across multiple subcellular organelles, highlighting its potential for imaging FAPα activity at the subcellular level. To verify the specificity of Cs-FAP for imaging FAPα in cells, we examined FAPα expression in TGF-β-treated cells by flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig. 9a, c) and Western blot (Supplementary Fig. 9b, d). As the TGF-β concentration increased, FAP levels rose accordingly. Moreover, FAPα expression was knocked down via siRNA during TGFβ induction (Fig. 3e, g). As expected, the TGFβ + siRNA group exhibited markedly reduced fluorescence compared with the TGFβ-only group. This result was further supported by RT-qPCR analysis of FAPα mRNA expression (Fig. 3h). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Cs-FAP enables efficient, accurate, and stable detection of FAPα activity in living cells.

FAPα activity assay in HIRI model

Hemocompatibility and in vivo biocompatibility are pivotal considerations for the utilization of molecular probes within living organisms. The hemolysis assay (Supplementary Fig. 10a) outcomes demonstrated that the hemolysis rate of Cs-FAP remained below 5% across all concentrations examined, signifying its remarkable hemocompatibility. Following this, mice received intravenous injections of either Cs-FAP (100 μM, 100 μL/10 g, 5% CH3OH in saline) or vehicle via the tail vein. After a seven-day period, no notable differences in body weight (Supplementary Fig. 10b) were discernible between the two groups. Moreover, serum markers for liver and kidney function (Supplementary Fig. 10c-f), including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (Cr), together with histological assessments of major organs using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Supplementary Fig. 10g), showed no abnormalities compared to the control group, further confirming the excellent biocompatibility of Cs-FAP in vivo. After verifying its biocompatibility, we investigated the tissue distribution (Supplementary Fig. 11a, b) and best optimal imaging time (Supplementary Fig. 11c, d) of Cs-FAP in the liver. The results showed that Cs-FAP predominantly accumulated in the liver, with the fluorescence signal in the hepatic region peaking at 30 minutes post-injection.

To establish the in vivo feasibility of Cs-FAP prior to screening applications, we first established a progressive hepatic fibrosis model induced by hepatic ischemia–reperfusion injury (HIRI). This model triggers pronounced oxidative stress and rapid activation of hepatic stellate cells during the reperfusion phase, which are key early events in fibrogenesis and lead to robust FAPα activation within a short time window38. Therefore, HIRI is well-suited for evaluating FAPα-activatable probes at the early stages of fibrosis. In this model, 70% hepatic ischemia was maintained for 90 min, followed by reperfusion for 1, 3, 5, and 7 days (Fig. 4a). ALT and AST are commonly used serum biomarkers for liver injury. Our results (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b) showed that their levels peaked on the first day after modeling and then gradually declined, yet remained significantly higher than those in the sham group on day 7. This suggests that ALT and AST may not be suitable for evaluating the progression of liver damage during HIRI-induced fibrosis. In contrast, the hepatic hydroxyproline (HYP) content (Supplementary Fig. 12c) increased steadily with the duration of reperfusion, indicating progressive liver fibrosis over time. Activation of hepatic stellate cells and the expression of associated markers play a pivotal role in liver fibrogenesis. To this end, we isolated primary HSCs (p-HSCs, Fig. 4b) from mice at various time points after ischemia-reperfusion and evaluated FAPα activity using Cs-FAP. Confocal microscopy (Fig. 4c, g) and flow cytometry (Fig. 4d) analyses consistently showed a time-dependent increase in FAPα activity in HSCs. Furthermore, both 2D and 3D in situ intravital imaging (Fig. 4e, h) revealed the same trend, with increasing fluorescence signal density of Cs-FAP around blood vessels over time. This perivascular accumulation is likely related to the secretion of cytokines and growth factors by aHSCs, which contribute to vascular remodeling and fibrotic tissue proliferation39. Subsequently, we employed an in vivo small-animal fluorescence imaging system and a super-resolution photoacoustic imaging platform to monitor FAPα expression in the liver at different time points following HIRI. As shown in Fig. 4f, i, j, both fluorescence and photoacoustic signals in the liver increased significantly with the number of reperfusion days, indicating elevated FAPα activity over time. To evaluate the universality of the probe in different liver fibrosis models, we further tested the visualization performance of Cs-FAP in mice with bile duct ligation (BDL)-induced and carbon tetrachloride (CCl₄)-induced hepatic fibrosis (Supplementary Fig. 13). The fluorescence and photoacoustic signals of the probe gradually increased with the progression of fibrosis. Consistently, Masson’s trichrome staining of the corresponding liver tissues showed an increasing trend in collagen deposition over time, which generally agrees with the probe signals, supporting the capability of Cs-FAP to reflect fibrosis progression. To exclude interference from circulating free FAPα, we assessed Cs-FAP activation in plasma and liver homogenates from Sham and Model mice. Cs-FAP showed negligible fluorescence turn-on in both groups’ plasma but exhibited markedly higher activation in liver homogenates from Model mice, indicating that the in vivo signal mainly originates from FAPα activity in fibrotic liver tissue (Supplementary Fig. 14). These results support the use of Cs-FAP for bimodal fluorescence/photoacoustic imaging of FAPα in vivo.

Alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) is a well-established marker of HSCs activation40. Therefore, we examined α-SMA expression post-HIRI using immunofluorescence staining, while simultaneously assessing FAPα expression to validate the imaging results. Additionally, we employed Masson’s trichrome and Sirius Red staining to evaluate the extent of liver fibrosis. As shown in Fig. 4k, the expression levels of both α-SMA and FAPα (Supplementary Fig. 15a, b) increased in a time-dependent manner following HIRI modeling. The pattern of FAPα expression was consistent with previous imaging and biochemical results, further supporting the accuracy of Cs-FAP in capturing dynamic changes in FAPα levels in vivo. Masson’s trichrome staining, which distinguishes collagen (blue) from muscle fibers (red), showed a progressive increase in blue-stained collagen fibers with longer reperfusion durations, indicating the development of liver fibrosis (Supplementary Fig. 15c). Sirius Red staining (Supplementary Fig. 15d), another classic collagen-specific staining technique, revealed increasing red-stained collagen deposition with HIRI progression. Under polarized light, type I collagen appeared yellow or red, while type III collagen appeared green. The polarized light imaging results were consistent with the trends observed in Masson’s and Sirius Red staining. Quantitative analysis using ImageJ software (Supplementary Fig. 15e, f) demonstrated that neither type I nor type III collagen showed significant changes during the first three days post-HIRI. However, both increased significantly on days 5 and 7, with type III collagen exhibiting a more pronounced increase. Correlation analysis, a powerful tool to explore the relationships among variables, was conducted using R software. We performed pairwise correlation analyses of all measured parameters, with a particular focus on the relationship between modeling day (DAY) and other indicators. As shown in Fig. 4l, FAPα expression had the highest correlation with DAY, followed by α-SMA expression. These findings further substantiate that Cs-FAP is a reliable probe for detecting FAPα activity and can serve as an effective tool for evaluating the degree of fibrosis in HIRI-induced liver fibrosis models.

High-throughput screening of FAPα activity-inhibiting compounds

Natural products have long been recognized as a valuable source of inspiration for drug discovery41. Notably, nearly 60% of FDA-approved drugs are either small-molecule agents derived directly from natural products or synthetic compounds inspired by their structural frameworks42. However, efficiently identifying truly pharmacologically active constituents remains a significant challenge. Conventional activity-based screening methods often suffer from limited sensitivity, throughput, and mechanistic resolution, while affinity-based and virtual screening approaches are prone to false positives and may not reflect functional inhibition in complex biological systems43. In contrast, spectrum-effect correlation strategies directly link chemical features with pharmacological responses, enabling more reliable identification of active components. Building on this concept, we developed optical probe-based platforms that provide activity-dependent, real-time readouts across cellular, tissue, and whole-organism levels, and integrated them into high-throughput screening workflows to enhance both efficiency and functional relevance44,45. Based on this premise, we systematically selected 95 medicinal herbs with reported hepatoprotective properties (Supplementary Data 3, 4). Following lyophilization, we constructed a hepatoprotective plant extract pool (HPEP) and employed a high-throughput screening strategy to identify FAPα inhibitors using a Cs-FAP-based assay, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 16.

In the first stage, an in vitro enzyme activity-based screening was conducted by co-incubating individual HPEP extracts with recombinant FAPα, followed by the addition of Cs-FAP. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader. As shown in Fig. 5a, 16 extracts exhibited significant inhibition of FAPα enzymatic activity. These candidates were further evaluated in a secondary screening using a high-content analysis system in TGF-β-stimulated LX-2 cells. Most extracts showed consistent FAPα inhibition at the cellular level, whereas several exhibited apparent agonistic effects, underscoring the necessity of cellular-level validation (Supplementary Fig. 17). For extracts displaying comparable inhibitory activity in the two-dimensional cell model (including H56, H78, and H79), a further round of screening was performed using a 3D liver microtissue model under prolonged TGF-β stimulation to achieve more robust and sustained HSC activation. Under these more stringent and physiologically relevant conditions, extract H56 (Sappan Lignum) consistently produced the most pronounced suppression of Cs-FAP fluorescence signals, indicating superior inhibition of FAPα activity (Fig. 5b). Sappan Lignum was subsequently subjected to preparative high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with UV detector, yielding 14 fractions (F1–F14, Fig. 5c). After lyophilization and reconstitution to a standardized concentration, these fractions were re-evaluated using both in vitro enzymatic assays (Fig. 5f, Supplementary Fig. 18a) and high-content cellular screening (Fig. 5d, e, Supplementary Fig. 18b). Among them, F12 consistently demonstrated the strongest inhibitory activity against FAPα in both assay platforms.

To elucidate the chemical composition of F12, we performed structural characterization using HPLC coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-QTOF-MS/MS) (Supplementary Fig. 18c). A total of six compounds were tentatively identified based on mass spectral comparison with reference46,47 (Supplementary Table 3), of which five were commercially available and obtained for further evaluation (Fig. 5g). These compounds were tested for FAPα inhibition through enzyme-based assays (Supplementary Fig. 18d), as well as in TGFβ-stimulated LX-2 cells and 3D liver microtissue models (Fig. 5h-j). Compounds C2 and C5 emerged as the most active inhibitors. Multiple cell viability assays and LDH cytotoxicity tests (Supplementary Fig. 19) demonstrated that C5 exhibited lower cytotoxicity compared to C2. Further acute (Supplementary Fig. 20) and chronic toxicity (Supplementary Fig. 21) studies of C5 were conducted in mice, and the results showed that both a high-dose single injection (200 mg/kg) and long-term low-dose administration (20 mg/kg/day) caused no significant damage to major organs. Complete blood count analysis (Supplementary Figs. 22, 23) also indicated that neither high-dose nor prolonged administration affected the immune or hematopoietic systems. Therefore, C5 (Sappanchalcone, Sap) was selected as the lead compound for further investigation.

Sap attenuates HSCs activation by targeting FAPα

The binding of Sap to FAPα is fundamental to its inhibitory action. Therefore, we investigated the binding interaction between Sap and FAPα both in vitro and in cells using surface plasmon resonance (SPR) and cellular thermal shift assays (CETSA). SPR results (Fig. 6a) showed that the binding affinity of Sap to FAPα was high, with an equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) value of 3.88 ± 2.50 μM. The CETSA (Fig. 6d) indicated that, compared to the control group, the FAPα band in the Sap-treated group maintained similar intensity at 43 °C and 50 °C as at 37 °C, suggesting that Sap binding enhanced the thermal stability of FAPα. This also confirmed that Sap binds to FAPα in LX-2 cells. Further investigation using molecular docking (Fig. 6b) and molecular dynamics simulations (Fig. 6c, Supplementary Fig. 24) revealed that the small molecule Sap binds to FAPα mainly through hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds (Supplementary Tables 4, 5). Additionally, Glu 203 and Tyr 625, key residues in the FAPα substrate binding pocket48, may play an important role during Sap binding. The interaction between Sap and FAPα involves multiple hydrogen bonds, especially with the GLU 202 residue, indicating that Sap may occupy the substrate binding pocket of FAPα, thus interfering with its normal substrate recognition and catalytic function. Molecular dynamics simulations further supported the stability of this binding conformation. This binding model may inhibit FAPα from interacting with its natural substrate via a competitive inhibition mechanism, thereby affecting its enzymatic activity and providing theoretical support for the application of Sap as a FAPα inhibitor.

In the TGFβ-activated LX-2 cells, we used confocal microscopy to evaluate the inhibitory effect of Sap on FAPα and its anti-fibrotic properties. The experimental results (Fig. 6e, Supplementary Fig. 25a, b) showed that, after TGFβ stimulation, the model group exhibited significantly enhanced Cs-FAP and α-SMA signals, indicating that TGFβ successfully induced LX-2 cell activation and fibrosis. In contrast, the drug-treated groups exhibited decrease in both signal intensities, suggesting that Sap inhibits FAPα activity and alleviates LX-2 cell activation to some extent. Notably, in groups treated with different concentrations of Sap, the inhibitory effect on FAPα was superior to that of the colchicine (Col) group, and at the same concentration (Sap-h vs. Col), Sap exhibited a more significant downregulation of α-SMA, indicating its greater anti-fibrotic potential compared to the control drug. In the TGFβ-activated 3D liver microtissues, we also used confocal microscopy to assess the inhibitory effect of Sap on FAPα and its anti-fibrotic properties. Experimental results (Fig. 6f, Supplementary Fig. 25c, d) showed that, after TGFβ stimulation, the model group exhibited significantly enhanced Cs-FAP and α-SMA signals, indicating that TGFβ successfully induced activation and fibrosis of the 3D liver microtissues. Compared to the model group, the drug-treated groups showed a decrease in both signals, suggesting that Sap effectively inhibits FAPα activity and suppresses stellate cell activation. Particularly, in terms of FAPα inhibition, the control drug Col showed no significant difference from the model group, while all concentrations of Sap displayed stronger inhibitory effects. Furthermore, in terms of α-SMA downregulation, Sap exhibited superior inhibitory effects, even at lower concentrations compared to Col, further highlighting its anti-fibrotic potential. Consistent with these imaging results, the regulatory effect of Sap on FAPα was further examined at the protein expression level by Western blot analysis in TGF-β-activated LX-2 cells. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 26, TGF-β or Ang II stimulation markedly upregulated FAPα protein expression relative to the control group. In contrast, Sap treatment reversed this upregulation, partially restoring FAPα expression toward basal levels. These results demonstrate that Sap not only inhibits FAPα enzymatic activity but also effectively downregulates its protein expression under fibrotic conditions.

The migration and invasion of activated hepatic stellate cells (aHSCs) to damaged areas are considered critical initial steps in wound healing and tissue remodeling49,50. Once these cells enter the damaged site, they promote further proliferation, secrete extracellular matrix, and recruit more myofibroblasts. Excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix disrupts the structure and function of surrounding tissues51. Therefore, enhanced HSCs proliferation, migration, and invasion accelerate fibrosis and exacerbate organ dysfunction. We evaluated cell proliferation status in various experimental groups using 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) staining and flow cytometry. The experimental results (Fig. 6g) showed that the positive signal in normal LX-2 cells was only 4.16%, whereas in TGFβ-activated LX-2 cells, the positive signal significantly increased to 22.4%. All drug-treated groups displayed inhibition of LX-2 cell proliferation, with the Sap groups demonstrating a stronger inhibitory effect, superior to the Col group. We assessed the effects of Sap on LX-2 cell migration and invasion abilities using scratch assays and invasion assays. The results showed that LX-2 cells in the normal state exhibited some migratory ability, which significantly increased after TGFβ activation, with a migration rate of about 80% within 12 h (Fig. 6h; Supplementary Fig. 27). The Col group had no significant impact on cell migration, whereas all Sap-treated groups significantly inhibited the migration of activated LX-2 cells. Invasion assays (Fig. 6i, j) showed that LX-2 cells in the resting state had weak invasion ability, which significantly increased after TGFβ stimulation. Sap treatment at various concentrations significantly suppressed the invasion ability of activated cells, with fewer invasive cells compared to the Col group, which showed weaker inhibitory effects. These results suggest that Sap effectively inhibits the migration and invasion of aHSCs, demonstrating excellent anti-fibrotic potential.

To further validate the regulatory effects of Sap on FAPα and related fibrosis genes in activated hepatic stellate cells, we conducted RT-qPCR experiments. The results (Supplementary Fig. 28) showed that TGFβ stimulation significantly induced the expression of FAPα, while Sap treatment effectively suppressed its upregulation in a dose-dependent manner. In contrast, the Col group did not significantly affect FAPα expression levels. This result further confirms that Sap functions as a targeted inhibitor of FAPα. Additionally, Sap significantly downregulated the expression of fibrosis-related genes ACTA2, COL1A1, and COL3A1, indicating its potential to inhibit HSCs activation and collagen synthesis, with stronger inhibitory effects than the Col group. Based on the above results, Sap effectively attenuates HSCs activation by markedly inhibiting FAPα activity and expression, thereby reducing HSCs proliferation, migration, and invasion, and downregulating fibrosis-associated genes.

Sap in vivo antifibrotic effect

Considering the complexity of liver fibrosis pathogenesis, three different mouse models of liver fibrosis were established to systematically evaluate the in vivo antifibrotic efficacy of Sap.

In the carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)-induced liver fibrosis model (Fig. 7a), fluorescence and photoacoustic signals (Fig. 7b) in the liver were significantly enhanced in the model group, indicating marked upregulation of FAPα activity. Signal intensities decreased across all treatment groups. The Sap-L group showed an inhibitory effect comparable to that of the Col group but slightly weaker than that of the UAMC1100 group (UAMC), whereas the Sap-M and Sap-H groups exhibited more pronounced inhibitory effects. Liver index analysis (Supplementary Fig. 29a) showed reduced liver mass in the treatment groups, though without statistical significance compared to the model group, except for the Sap-H group. Serum ALT and AST levels (Supplementary Fig. 29b, c) were significantly elevated in the model group and were dose-dependently reduced following Sap treatment, with the Sap-H group nearly restoring levels to normal. Liver HYP content (Supplementary Fig. 29d) also decreased in a dose-dependent manner, further confirming the antifibrotic effects of Sap. Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated reduced collagen deposition across treatment groups, with pseudolobule structures almost disappearing in the Sap-H group (Fig. 7c). Sirius Red staining and polarized light analysis (Fig. 6c, Supplementary Fig. 29e-g) showed marked increases in type I and III collagen in the model group; while the Col group had limited effects, Sap treatment exhibited dose-dependent inhibition of collagen deposition. H&E staining revealed disrupted lobular architecture, steatosis, hepatocyte necrosis, and inflammatory infiltration in the model group, whereas these pathological changes were progressively ameliorated with increasing Sap doses, with the Sap-H group demonstrating the most pronounced recovery. Immunofluorescence staining (Supplementary Fig. 29e, h, i) indicated that α-SMA and FAPα protein expression were upregulated approximately 15-fold in the model group compared to the Sham group, predominantly localized around the vasculature. Sap treatment resulted in more effective suppression of both markers compared to the UAMC and Col group.

In the bile duct ligation (BDL)-induced cholestatic fibrosis model (Fig. 7d), in vivo fluorescence and photoacoustic imaging (Fig. 7e) showed a significant increase in hepatic FAPα activity after modeling, which was dose-dependently suppressed by Sap treatment. Liver index (Supplementary Fig. 30a), serum ALT, AST, TBIL, and liver HYP (Supplementary Fig. 30b-e) measurements consistently supported the protective effects of Sap. Masson staining (Fig. 7f) showed extensive collagen deposition around bile ducts in the model group, which was significantly reduced following Sap treatment, particularly in the Sap-M and Sap-H groups. Sirius Red staining and polarized light analysis (Supplementary Fig. 30f-h) indicated that Sap groups more effectively suppressed type I and III collagen expression compared to the Col group. H&E staining revealed portal tract expansion, periductal fibrosis, disorganized hepatocyte arrangement, and inflammatory infiltration in the BDL model group, all of which were alleviated in a dose-dependent manner by Sap treatment. Immunofluorescence results (Supplementary Fig. 30f, i, j) demonstrated significant upregulation of α-SMA and FAPα proteins in the BDL model, both of which were effectively downregulated by Sap, with superior efficacy compared to the Col group, particularly in inhibiting FAPα expression. In contrast, UAMC treatment did not result in significant therapeutic benefits in the BDL model, with minimal improvement in hepatic FAPα activity and fibrosis-related parameters, consistent with previous reports in cholestatic fibrosis28.

In the HIRI-induced fibrosis model (Fig. 7g), in vivo imaging (Fig. 7h) showed markedly enhanced fluorescence and photoacoustic signals on day 7 post-surgery, which were progressively reduced by Sap treatment in a dose-dependent manner. Liver index measurements (Supplementary Fig. 31a) were significantly increased in the model group and reduced following Sap or UAMC treatment, while Col treatment showed limited improvement. Serum ALT, AST, and HYP content (Supplementary Fig. 31b–d) results were consistent with imaging findings, indicating a dose-dependent protective effect of Sap. Masson’s trichrome staining and Sirius Red staining (Fig. 7i, Fig. 31e-g) revealed significant collagen accumulation in the model group, which was attenuated by Sap treatment, with a more pronounced reduction in type III collagen. H&E staining (Fig. 7i) showed severe disorganization of hepatocytes, necrosis, and inflammatory infiltration in the model group, all of which were gradually reversed by Sap treatment, especially in the Sap-H group, where hepatic architecture resembled that of the Sham group. Immunofluorescence analysis (Supplementary Fig. 31e, h, i) revealed that both α-SMA and FAPα were significantly upregulated in the model group. Sap treatment induced a dose-dependent reduction in their expression, with the high-dose group exhibiting stronger inhibitory effects than both the UAMC and Col groups.

Across the three liver fibrosis models and during Sap treatment, both fluorescence and photoacoustic signals of the Cs-FAP probe showed significant positive correlations with histopathological fibrosis markers, including Sirius Red and Masson staining, as well as immunofluorescence-based FAPα expression in liver tissues (Supplementary Fig. 32). Collectively, these findings underscore the therapeutic potential of Sap as a FAPα inhibitor for liver fibrosis and affirm the utility of Cs-FAP-based imaging for dynamically monitoring efficacy, fibrosis progression, and prognostic outcomes.

Pharmacokinetics and antifibrotic mechanisms of Sap

Sap has demonstrated potent antifibrotic activity across multiple liver fibrosis models, but its in vivo pharmacokinetic characteristics and underlying molecular mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated. To investigate the pharmacokinetics of Sap, a HPLC coupled with triple quadrupole tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-QQQ-MS/MS) method was established and applied to assess its plasma concentration following intraperitoneal administration (10 mg/kg, Fig. 8a). Pharmacokinetic parameters (Supplementary Table 6) were calculated using PKSolver software52, with the two-compartment model providing the best fit, as indicated by the green line in the Fig. 8b. Under the two-compartment model, Sap exhibited a rapid absorption rate, characterized by a short absorption half-life (t1/2ka = 0.04 h) and a peak plasma concentration (Cmax) of 201.71 ng/mL achieved at 0.11 h, suggesting a rapid onset of action. The volume of distribution (V/F = 30 mL/ kg) was relatively low, indicating that Sap was primarily confined to the plasma or specific tissues. Regarding elimination, Sap demonstrated a relatively high apparent clearance rate (CL/F = 70 mL/h/kg), suggesting efficient removal from the systemic circulation. The distribution phase half-life (t1/2Alpha = 0.16 h) was brief, indicating rapid initial tissue distribution, while the elimination phase half-life (t1/2Beta = 3.39 h) was comparatively longer, implying a degree of retention that could sustain therapeutic efficacy. The area under the plasma concentration–time curve (AUC0-inf = 143.29 ng/mL·h) reflected substantial systemic exposure, while the mean residence time (MRT = 2.68 h) suggested that Sap could maintain pharmacological effects for a moderate duration. Tissue distribution studies (Fig. 8c) showed that Sap accumulated rapidly in the liver (blue line), with an early Tmax and high Cmax, suggesting a favorable liver-targeting profile. Moreover, the slow clearance from the liver implied prolonged retention, which may benefit therapeutic outcomes in liver diseases. Additionally, plasma protein binding studies indicated that Sap exhibited a moderate binding rate (~50.15%) across different concentrations without significant variation (Supplementary Table 7), suggesting concentration-independent plasma protein binding, further supporting its rapid systemic distribution and clearance properties.

The CCl4-induced liver fibrosis model, one of the most widely used animal models for studying the pathogenesis of liver fibrosis and evaluating antifibrotic therapies53, was employed to further investigate the mechanisms of action of Sap using transcriptomic approaches. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on |LogFC | ≥ 1 and P ≤ 0.05 thresholds and visualized using volcano plots. Compared to the vehicle group, 1377 genes were significantly upregulated and 401 genes downregulated in the model group (Fig. 8d). In comparison, 284 genes were upregulated and 760 genes were downregulated in the Sap-treated group (drug group) relative to the model group (Fig. 8e). Principal component analysis (PCA, Fig. 8f) revealed distinct clustering among the Vehicle, Model, and Drug groups. Samples from the Sap-treated group (red circles) clustered in the lower-left quadrant, while samples from the Model group (blue triangles) were distributed on the right side of the plot. Vehicle group samples (green squares) were located on the left side. PC1 clearly separated the model group from the other two groups, while PC2 indicated some degree of separation between the drug and vehicle groups, although to a lesser extent. Heatmap analysis of fibrosis-related genes (Fig. 8h) further demonstrated that Sap treatment significantly reversed the expression of most fibrosis-associated genes, showing a gene expression pattern closely resembling that of the vehicle group. Venn diagram analysis (Fig. 8g) comparing DEGs identified 774 overlapping genes between the Vehicle vs. Model and Drug vs. Model comparisons. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of downregulated DEGs (Fig. 8i) revealed significant suppression of ECM remodeling-related biological processes, including “extracellular matrix organization” and “collagen-containing extracellular matrix,” indicating that Sap may exert its antifibrotic effects by inhibiting ECM deposition and tissue fibrosis. Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis showed that the downregulated DEGs were significantly enriched in immune response and signaling pathways, with the phosphoinositide 3-kinase/ protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) signaling pathway exhibiting the highest number of affected genes and the smallest p-values (Fig. 8j). Moreover, the PI3K/AKT pathway was closely related to other enriched pathways, such as osteoclast differentiation. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) (Fig. 8k) confirmed significant inhibition of the PI3K/AKT pathway in the Sap-treated group compared to the model group.

Based on the transcriptomic analysis, previous literature54,55, and experimental findings that Sap suppressed LX-2 cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, we hypothesize that Sap exerts its antifibrotic effects by inhibiting FAPα activity, leading to downregulation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Typically, activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway depends on phosphorylation events that mediate downstream signaling to alleviate liver fibrosis56. Western blot analysis (Fig. 8l-n) revealed that Sap treatment significantly reduced the phosphorylation levels of PI3K and AKT in liver tissues compared to the model group, suggesting that Sap inhibits hepatic stellate cell activation and exerts antifibrotic effects through suppression of the FAPα/PI3K/AKT axis (Fig. 8o).

Discussion

Our study introduces Cs-FAP, a FL/PA molecular probe specifically designed to target FAPα, a key biomarker involved in fibrotic disease. Cs-FAP exhibits excellent sensitivity and selectivity toward FAPα, enabling reliable detection both in vitro and in vivo. Notably, the probe operates at a longer excitation wavelength and supports both fluorescence and photoacoustic signal outputs, enabling complementary imaging readouts in complex biological environments. Mechanistic investigations, including HPLC analysis, TDDFT calculations, and molecular docking simulations, demonstrated that FAPα-mediated enzymatic cleavage of the peptide sequence leads to the release of the optically active core, resulting in simultaneous activation of fluorescence and photoacoustic signals. The utility of Cs-FAP was validated in multiple biological contexts, ranging from cellular systems to complex animal models. In TGFβ-activated HSCs, Cs-FAP effectively visualized FAPα activity with minimal cytotoxicity and good photostability. In three distinct liver fibrosis models (CCl4, BDL, and HIRI-induced), Cs-FAP enabled dynamic monitoring of liver fibrosis progression with strong correlation to disease stage compared to conventional biomarkers. The consistent performance across different fibrosis models further supports its applicability under diverse pathological conditions. This non-invasive imaging capability represents an advancement over traditional histological assessments that require tissue sampling.

Leveraging the high-throughput screening capabilities of Cs-FAP, we identified Sap from Sappan Lignum as a potent FAPα inhibitor. Sap effectively attenuated HSCs activation by targeting FAPα, as evidenced by decreased cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and downregulation of fibrosis-associated genes. The antifibrotic properties of Sap were further validated in three distinct liver fibrosis models (CCl4, BDL, and HIRI-induced), where it consistently outperformed colchicine in ameliorating histopathological alterations and reducing collagen deposition. Mechanistically, transcriptomic analysis and subsequent validation experiments revealed that Sap exerts its antifibrotic effects by inhibiting the FAPα/PI3K/AKT signaling axis. The favorable pharmacokinetic profile of Sap, characterized by rapid hepatic accumulation and sustained retention, coupled with its low toxicity, positions it as a promising therapeutic candidate for liver fibrosis.

Looking forward, this study opens several avenues for future research. First, the molecular design strategy utilized in developing Cs-FAP could be further optimized to enhance sensitivity, tissue penetration, and pharmacokinetic properties for FAPα detection, improving its translational potential for clinical applications. In particular, further optimization of dual-modality signal balance and optical properties may improve performance in deeper or more heterogeneous tissue environments. Second, structure-activity relationship studies on Sap could yield derivatives with enhanced potency and pharmacokinetic properties. Third, given the promising antifibrotic efficacy of Sap in liver fibrosis models, its therapeutic potential could be expanded to address other fibrotic disorders where FAPα plays a critical role, such as cardiac fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and renal fibrosis. This would broaden the clinical impact of this potent FAPα inhibitor. Finally, the translational potential of both Cs-FAP and Sap should be evaluated in large animal models and eventually in clinical settings, where they could impact the diagnosis and management of fibrotic diseases.

In conclusion, this work makes two significant contributions to fibrosis research. First, Cs-FAP represents a powerful dual-mode molecular imaging tool that enables sensitive and specific detection of FAPα activity in vitro and in vivo, offering a robust platform for non-invasive fibrosis monitoring and high-throughput screening of antifibrotic agents. By integrating activatable fluorescence and photoacoustic imaging within a small-molecule probe specifically adapted for liver fibrosis, this work complements and advances current FAP-targeted optical imaging strategies. Second, the identification of Sap as a potent FAPα inhibitor provides not only a promising therapeutic candidate but also a new chalcone-based scaffold for future FAPα inhibitor development. The unique structural features of Sap, particularly its chalcone backbone with specific hydroxyl and methoxy substituents, could serve as a valuable starting point for medicinal chemistry efforts aimed at developing more potent and selective FAPα inhibitors. The integration of Cs-FAP diagnostics with Sap-based therapeutics establishes a comprehensive platform for addressing liver fibrosis, representing a paradigm shift toward precision medicine in fibrotic diseases. This theranostic approach centered on FAPα targeting holds great promise for both fundamental research and clinical translation in this challenging medical domain.

Methods

Animals

All animal experimental procedures and care were conducted in accordance with the guidelines outlined in the “Regulations on the Administration of Laboratory Animals” issued by the Ministry of Science and Technology of China and were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of China Pharmaceutical University (approval number: 2024-06-100). Male ICR mice (6 weeks old) used for in vivo experiments were purchased from Huachuang Sino (Jiangsu, China). Mice were housed under specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in groups of up to five per cage, with free access to water and a standard laboratory chow diet (Jiangsu Qinglongshan Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China; catalog no. SN612238361432136). Animals were maintained in a controlled environment (temperature, 20–25 °C; humidity, 40–70%) under a 12 h light–dark cycle. We strictly adhered to all relevant ethical regulations for animal research and experimentation.

Spectral responsiveness of Cs-FAP

Spectroscopic analyses of Cs-FAP (10 μM) in response to varying concentrations of FAPα (Sino Biological, 10464-H07H, China) were performed in a buffer solution consisting of 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.4) containing 1 mg/mL BSA. Absorption spectra in the range of 400–800 nm was recorded before and after the reaction using a UV–Vis–NIR spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, UV-3600Plus, Japan). Fluorescence emission spectra were acquired with a fluorescence spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, RF-6000, Japan) under an excitation wavelength of 740 nm. Photoacoustic spectral responses were measured using a super-resolution small-animal ultrasound/photoacoustic imaging system (Fujifilm, Vevo LAZR, Japan) operated in spectral scanning mode. Imaging parameters were set as follows: frequency, 21 MHz; frame delay, 4; PA gain, 30 dB; B-mode gain, 18 dB; wavelength range, 680–960 nm; imaging depth, 18 mm; and PA acquisition mode, Spectro.

Theoretical calculation and molecular docking

Geometry and energy optimization of the Cs-FAP molecule were carried out using the ORCA software (5.0.4) at the B3LYP/def2-SVP level of theory. Following optimization, the electronic structure, including HOMO/LUMO orbitals and excitation properties, was analyzed using Multiwfn57,58. The corresponding energy levels of the HOMO and LUMO were extracted, and orbital visualizations were generated using Avogadro. ESP analysis57,59 on the van der Waals surface was also conducted with Multiwfn, and the ESP distribution was mapped onto the electron density isosurface using VMD (1.9.3). Molecular docking between Cs-FAP and FAPα (PDB ID: 1Z68) was performed using AutoDock Vina (1.1.2). The grid box was set to 120 × 120 × 120 to fully encompass the binding pocket. The top-ranked docking conformation was then uploaded to the Protein–Ligand Interaction Profiler (PLIP) web server60 for interaction analysis between Cs-FAP and FAPα. The optimized structures of Cs-FAP and Cs have been provided in XYZ format in the Supplementary Information.

Cell culture

Human hepatic stellate cells (LX-2) were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and cultured in DMEM (KeyGEN, KGL1206-500, China) supplemented with 2% FBS (Gibco, A5669701, USA). Mouse hepatocytes (AML-12) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were purchased from KeyGEN Biotech (Jiangsu, China) and maintained in DMEM with 10% FBS. Human hepatocyte-derived cells (HepaRG) were acquired from Thermo Fisher Scientific and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (KeyGEN, KGL1501-500, China) supplemented with 10% FBS. All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and passaged upon reaching 80–90% confluency.

RT-qPCR

LX-2 cells (1 × 10⁵/well) were seeded in six-well plates and treated as control, TGFβ model, Col (10 μM), or Sap (1/5/10 μM) groups for 24 h. Total RNA was extracted and reverse-transcribed (1 μg RNA). qPCR was performed using SYBR Mix under standard cycling conditions (40 cycles), followed by melting curve analysis. Data were obtained from triplicate samples, normalized to ACTB, and calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 8.

In vivo safety evaluation of Cs-FAP

To assess the in vivo biocompatibility of Cs-FAP, ICR mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 3 per group) based on body weight and administered with either physiological saline or Cs-FAP (100 μM, 5% CH3OH in saline) via tail vein injection. Body weight was monitored daily for 7 consecutive days post-injection. On day 7, mice were euthanized and blood samples were collected for serum biochemical analysis. Liver function indicators, including AST and ALT, and kidney function markers, including BUN and Cr, were measured using commercial assay kits (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, C010-2-1, C009-2-1, C013-3-1, C011-2-1, China). Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were harvested, fixed, and subjected to H&E staining. Histological sections were scanned using a slide scanner (Hamamatsu, NanoZoomer S60, Japan) and examined for pathological changes to evaluate the potential systemic toxicity of Cs-FAP.

Cellular uptake of Cs-FAP

LX-2 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well in 4-well confocal dishes (Cellvis, D35C4-20-1.5-N, China) when they reached 80–90% confluency, and incubated overnight to allow for cell attachment. The medium was then removed, and cells were washed three times with PBS. Cs-FAP probe solution (10 μM, diluted in serum-free DMEM) was added at designated time points and incubated at 37 °C. 15 min before the end of incubation, Hoechst 33342 (Solarbio, C0031, China) was added to each well and incubation was continued until the endpoint. Cells were then washed and incubated in fresh DMEM for live-cell confocal imaging (Leica, STELLARIS 5, Germany). Hoechst 33342 was excited at 405 nm with emission collected at 420–550 nm; Cs-FAP was excited at 685 nm with emission collected at 730–800 nm. Fluorescence intensity was quantitatively analyzed using Fiji (ImageJ). For flow cytometry, after incubation with Cs-FAP for the indicated time, cells were harvested by trypsinization, centrifuged, and resuspended in serum-free DMEM. Cell suspensions were transferred to 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes, gently pipetted to obtain a single-cell suspension, and analyzed using a flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, CytoFLEX, USA). Cs-FAP fluorescence was detected in the APC channel.

Cellular uptake assay of Cs-FAP in LX-2 cells

LX-2 cells were digested and resuspended in serum-free DMEM to a final concentration of 5 × 104 cells/mL when they reached 80–90% confluency. After gentle pipetting, 2 mL of cell suspension was seeded into each well of a six-well plate and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a cell culture incubator. The next day, the medium was aspirated, and cells were treated with various concentrations (0, 5, 10, 20 ng/mL) of TGFβ (ABclonal, RP01458, China) diluted in serum-free DMEM, and cultured for an additional 24 h. Afterward, the medium was aspirated, and cells were washed with PBS. Cs-FAP and Hoechst 33342 solution were added and incubated for 30 minutes. Confocal microscopy imaging was performed to assess cellular response.

SiRNA-mediated FAPα silencing in LX-2

LX-2 cells were seeded in confocal imaging dishes one day prior to transfection to reach approximately 30–50% confluency at the time of transfection. Before transfection, the culture medium was replaced with fresh serum-free medium and cells were serum-starved for 2 h. siRNA (Hycyte, China, Sense GGUGGAUUCUUUGUUUCAACATT, AntiSense UGUUGAAACAAAGAAUCCACCTT) lyophilized powder was briefly centrifuged and reconstituted in DEPC-treated water (Biosharp, BL510B, China) to a final concentration of 20 μM as a stock solution, aliquoted and stored at –20 °C. For transfection, siRNA was diluted to a final concentration of 70 nM in antibiotic- and serum-free DMEM (Opti-DMEM), and mixed with 2.3 μL transfection reagent (Abbkine, BMU111-CN, China) in 50 μL Opti-DMEM. The mixture was incubated with cells at 37 °C for 6 h. The medium was then replaced with fresh complete medium and cells were incubated for an additional 18 h. After transfection, TGFβ (10 ng/mL) was added to each well, followed by a 24-h incubation. Cs-FAP and Hoechst 33342 were then added and incubated for 30 min. Confocal imaging was performed to evaluate fluorescence signals.

Establishment of a mouse HIR-induced fibrosis model

All male ICR mice (6 weeks old) were fasted for 24 h prior to surgery with free access to water. A small animal heating pad was preheated to maintain a constant temperature. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (MCE, HY-A0134, USA), and abdominal hair was shaved. The mice were positioned supine with limbs secured to fully expose the abdomen. After disinfecting the surgical site with povidone-iodine, a midline incision was made along the linea alba from the xiphoid process downward to expose the peritoneal cavity. The hepatic artery and portal vein were carefully isolated and clamped using atraumatic microvascular clips to induce approximately 70% hepatic ischemia. Successful ischemia was confirmed by a visible color change of the left and middle lobes from reddish to pale gray or yellow. A hemostatic clamp was applied to temporarily close the abdominal cavity during the ischemia period. After 90 min of partial hepatic ischemia, the vascular clips were removed to initiate reperfusion. The abdominal wall and skin were closed with absorbable sutures in layers. Mice were maintained on a warming pad until full recovery from anesthesia before being returned to their cages.

Isolation of primary HSCs from mice

p-HSCs were isolated following a modified protocol described by Ingmar et al.61. Briefly, mice subjected to HIR or sham operation were anesthetized, and in situ liver perfusion was performed via the inferior vena cava using sequential pre-warmed buffers: pre-perfusion solution (D-Hanks, Biosharp, BL559A, China), Pronase (Sigma-Aldrich, P5147-1G, USA), and Collagenase Ⅳ (Gibco, 17104019, USA). The digested liver was excised, minced, and filtered. Hepatocytes were removed by low-speed centrifugation, and primary HSCs were enriched using a 15%/11.5% Optiprep (BasalMedia, R714JV, China) density gradient. The interphase was collected, washed, and resuspended in DMEM (20% FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin) and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

In situ liver FAPα fluorescence imaging

HIRI models were established on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 prior to imaging, following the previously described procedure. For in situ hepatic FAPα imaging, mice were intravenously injected with the Cs-FAP (100 μM, 50 μL/10 g) and retro-orbitally injected with Alexa Fluor 594-labeled anti-CD31 antibody (50 μL/mouse, Abcam, ab307336, USA). After anesthesia with isoflurane, the liver was exposed, and the appropriate lobe was carefully mounted onto a customized imaging slide according to the IVIM system guidelines. Imaging was performed using a small-animal intravital microscopy (IVIM Technology, IVM-M, Republic of Korea) for dynamic in situ analysis.

In vivo liver FAPα fluorescence and photoacoustic imaging

Male ICR mice (6 weeks old) were intravenously injected with Cs-FAP (100 μM, 50 μL/10 g) via the tail vein. After 30 min, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and fluorescence imaging was performed using a small-animal in vivo imaging system (Tanon, ABLX6, China), with the Cy7 channel selected to detect Cs-FAP fluorescence signals. Photoacoustic imaging was conducted using a high-resolution small-animal ultrasound/photoacoustic imaging system with the following parameters: frequency, 21 MHz; frame delay, 4; photoacoustic gain, 30 dB; B-mode gain, 18 dB; wavelength, 725 nm; imaging depth, 18 mm.

Hypoxyproline assay

Hydroxyproline content in liver tissue was quantified following the manufacturer’s instructions (Solarbio, BC0255, China). Approximately 20 mg of liver tissue was weighed, homogenized in 200 μL of 1:1 (v/v) 37% HCl and ultrapure water, and hydrolyzed at 100°C for 3 h. After cooling, the pH was adjusted to 6–8 with 10 mM NaOH and the volume was brought to 400 μL. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 21,000 × g for 20 min and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. A 20 μL aliquot of the filtrate was mixed with reagents I and II and ultrapure water as instructed, reacted at 60 °C for 20 min, then cooled to room temperature. Absorbance was recorded at 560 nm using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, SpectraMax iD3, USA).

In vitro FAPα enzyme activity inhibition assay of 95 herbal extracts

To evaluate the inhibitory effects of herbal extracts on FAPα activity, reactions were conducted in black, clear-bottom 96-well plates (Absin, abs7245, China). Each well received HEPES buffer (50 mM, pH 7.4), FAPα (1 μg/mL), Cs-FAP (10 μM), and 1 μL of herbal extract stock solution to reach the final reaction volume. All reactions were performed in a strictly identical HEPES buffer system, and the added volume of each component (including extracts) was minimized to avoid perturbation of buffer conditions. Control wells contained the same components except for the herbal extract, and their fluorescence intensity was designated as 1 (100% FAPα activity). Herbal extracts were prepared in DMSO, and an equal volume of DMSO was maintained across all wells to exclude solvent effects. After thorough mixing, the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Fluorescence intensity was then recorded using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher, Varioskan LUX, USA) to calculate inhibition ratios and assess the FAPα-inhibitory potential of each extract.

High-throughput screening based on LX-2

For high-content screening, LX-2 cells (1 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded into 96-well imaging plates (PerkinElmer, 6055302, USA). After adherence, cells were treated with 10 ng/mL TGFβ for 24 h to induce FAPα expression. Following medium removal and PBS washing, cells were incubated with serum-free DMEM containing 0.5 μL herbal extract stock solution (final concentration: 25 μg/mL) for 2 h. The volume of extract added was kept minimal and consistent across all groups to ensure negligible disturbance to the cellular microenvironment. After washing, cells were stained with serum-free DMEM containing Cs-FAP and Hoechst 33342, and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Fluorescence images were acquired and analyzed using a high-content imaging system (PerkinElmer, Opera Phenix, USA) after final PBS washing and addition of fresh serum-free DMEM. A detailed summary of the high-throughput screening workflow, assay validation, library characteristics, hit criteria, and follow-up analyses is provided in Supplementary Data 4.

Construction of 3D liver microtissue model

3D liver microtissue model was constructed following the method described in reference62. Briefly, HepaRG, LX-2, and HUVEC cells were cultured separately in T75 flasks (LABSELECT, 13212, China) until reaching 80–90% confluency. After digestion, each cell type was resuspended at 1 × 105 cells/mL. Cells were mixed in a ratio of 4:2:1 (HepaRG: LX-2: HUVEC), and a total of 8000 cells per well were seeded into U-bottom ultra-low attachment 96-well plates (Corning, 7007, USA). Cells were briefly centrifuged to facilitate aggregation and cultured for 7 days to enable spheroid formation.

In vivo acute and chronic toxicity studies of Sap

For acute toxicity assessment, healthy male ICR mice (n = 5 per group) were intraperitoneally injected once with Sap (Chemfaces, China, 200 mg/kg, formulated in 5% DMSO, 35% PEG-300, and 60% saline). Control animals received an equivalent volume of vehicle. Body weights were monitored every other day. After 14 days, mice were euthanized, and blood samples were collected for serum biochemical analysis, including ALT, AST, BUN, and creatinine. Major organs were harvested and subjected to H&E staining for histopathological evaluation. For chronic toxicity assessment, mice (n = 5 per group) were administered Sap (20 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injection every other day for 30 days. Control mice received vehicle alone. Body weights were recorded throughout the experiment. On day 30, mice were sacrificed, and the same panel of serum biochemical and histological analyses was performed.

SRP assay

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) was performed on a Biacore S200 instrument (Cytiva, USA) evaluate the interaction between Sap and FAPα. FAPα protein was diluted to 50 μg/mL in pH 4.0 acetate buffer and immobilized onto a CM5 chip using standard amine coupling (900 s contact time), with EDC/NHS activation and ethanolamine blocking. Sap was diluted to various concentrations in PBS-P containing 0.4275% DMSO, ensuring a final DMSO concentration of 0.5% in all samples. The binding analysis was performed using the LMW kinetics mode (contact time: 60 s; dissociation time: 120 s). All consumables and reagents were provided by Cytiva (USA). Data analysis was carried out using Biacore S200 Evaluation Software.

Cell thermal shift assay

The cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) was performed following standard protocols63. LX-2 cells were cultured in multiple 100 mm dishes and stimulated with TGFβ (10 ng/mL) for 24 h to induce FAPα expression once they reached 80–90% confluence. Cells from each dish were collected into centrifuge tubes by trypsinization, centrifuged at 93 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The experimental group was incubated with Sap (10 μM) diluted in serum-free DMEM, while the control group received serum-free DMEM containing only DMSO. After thorough mixing, cells were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Following incubation, cells were pelleted, resuspended in PBS (100 μL per sample), and transferred into PCR tubes on ice. Each group (Sap-treated and control) was divided into six temperature conditions (37, 43, 50, 57, 63, and 70 °C) and heated in a PCR thermal cycler (Bio-Red, T100, USA) for 5 min. After heating, samples were centrifuged and the supernatants were discarded. The remaining cell pellets were analyzed by Western blot to assess FAPα protein stability across different temperatures.

EdU cell proliferation test

EdU cell proliferation assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (APExBIO, K1077, USA). Briefly, LX-2 cells (1 × 105 cells/well) were seeded into six-well plates and divided into groups: control (serum-free DMEM), model (10 ng/mL TGFβ), positive control (TGFβ + 10 μM Col), and Sap-treated (TGFβ + 1, 5, or 10 μM Sap). After 24 h incubation, 20 μM EdU was added and cells were incubated for an additional 3 h. Cells were then harvested, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, G1101, China), and permeabilized. Click reaction was performed using the freshly prepared labeling solution. Finally, cells were washed and analyzed by flow cytometry using the PE channel to assess EdU incorporation.

Cell migration

Wound healing assays were performed using LX-2 cells seeded at 2 mL/well (1 × 105 cells/mL) in six-well plates. Upon reaching full confluence, a scratch was made perpendicularly across the well using a 200 μL pipette tip. Detached cells were removed by washing with PBS three times. Cells were then treated with serum-free DMEM (control), TGFβ (10 ng/mL, model), TGFβ + 10 μM Col, or TGFβ with Sap at 1, 5, or 10 μM. Images of the scratch width were captured at 0 and 12 h using an inverted microscope. Cell migration rates were quantified using ImageJ software.

Transwell invasion assay

Transwell chambers (NEST, 725321, China), 24-well plates, and consumables (e.g., pipette tips and tubes) were sterilized, sealed, and pre-cooled at −20 °C. Matrigel (Mogengel Biotech, 082706, China) was diluted with serum-free DMEM (1:6) and 60 μL of the mixture was evenly coated on the inner membrane of each Transwell insert. Inserts were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h to allow gelation, followed by hydration with 100 μL DMEM for 30 min. LX-2 cells in logarithmic phase were resuspended in serum-free DMEM at 2.5 × 10⁵ cells/mL. The lower chambers of a 24-well plate were filled with 500 μL of treatment media (control: serum-free DMEM; model: TGFβ 10 ng/mL; Col: TGFβ + 10 μM Col; Sap: TGFβ + 1, 5, or 10 μM Sap). Inserts were gently placed into wells, and 100 μL of cell suspension was added to the upper chambers. After 24 h incubation, inserts were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (20 min), and stained with 0.5% crystal violet (20 min, Aladdin, C110703, China). Non-migrated cells on the upper surface were removed with cotton swabs. The membranes were air-dried and imaged under an inverted microscope. Migrated cells were quantified using ImageJ software.

CCl4-induced mouse liver fibrosis model

Male ICR mice (6 weeks old) were randomly divided into six groups (n = 8 per group): Sham, Model, UAMC, Col, Sap-L, Sap-M, and Sap-H. A 25% (v/v) carbon tetrachloride (CCl4, Macklin, C805329, China) solution was prepared in olive oil (Aladdin, O108686, China). Mice in the Model and treatment groups received intraperitoneal injections of 25% CCl4 (1 mL/kg) twice per week for six consecutive weeks to induce liver fibrosis, while mice in the Sham group received equal volumes of olive oil at the same frequency. From the fifth week onward, drug administration was initiated and performed every other day. The specific treatments were as follows: Sham and Model groups received vehicle only (5% DMSO, 35% PEG-300, and 60% saline); the UAMC group received UAMC1110 (50 mg/kg); the Col group received colchicine (1 mg/kg); Sap-L, -M, and -H groups received Sap at 1, 5, and 10 mg/kg, respectively, all formulated in the same vehicle.

Bile duct ligation-induced liver fibrosis model

Male ICR mice (6 weeks old) were randomly divided into six groups (Sham, Model, UAMC, Col, Sap-L, Sap-M, and Sap-H; n = 8 per group). Following anesthesia, the mice underwent abdominal shaving and disinfection with iodophor, and were then placed on a heating pad. A midline laparotomy was performed, and the bile duct was carefully isolated using saline-moistened cotton swabs. The common bile duct was ligated with non-absorbable sutures to establish the model, whereas in the Sham group, the bile duct was only exposed without ligation. After surgery, 100 μL of normal saline was injected into the peritoneal cavity, and the incision was closed using absorbable sutures. Mice were kept warm until recovery before being returned to their cages. Drug administration began on days 1 and 3 after surgery via intraperitoneal injection, following the same protocol as in the CCl4-induced liver fibrosis model.

Transcriptomic analysis

Male ICR mice (6 weeks old) were randomly divided into three groups (n = 3 per group): the Vehicle group (solvent control), the Model group (CCl4), and the Drug group (Sap 10 mg/kg in 5% DMSO, 35% PEG-300, and 60% saline). Liver fibrosis was induced in the mice using CCl4, followed by drug administration. After the treatment period, the mice were euthanized, and the liver tissues were collected for RNA extraction using Trizol. Transcriptomic sequencing was performed by Biozeron Biotech Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China).

Molecular dynamics simulation

All MD simulations were performed using GROMACS 2020.6. The topology of FAPα was generated with the Amber99sb-ildn force field, and the ligand Sap was parameterized using Sobtop (Tian Lu, 1.0). The complex was placed in a cubic box with a 10 Å buffer from the edges and solvated with water. Counter-ions were added to neutralize the system. Energy minimization, followed by NVT and NPT equilibration, was conducted prior to a 100 ns production run. Structural stability and flexibility were analyzed via RMSD (gmx rms) and RMSF (gmx rmsf), respectively. Hydrogen bonds between FAPα and Sap were evaluated using gmx hbond. The free-energy landscape was constructed with gmx sham. The initial and final conformations of the trajectories are provided in the Supplementary Data 5 and 6 for reproducibility.

Statistics and reproducibility

All in vitro cell culture experiments were performed using at least three biologically independent samples and yielded similar results. For imaging experiments, representative images are shown, and quantitative analyses were performed using all independent samples. Animal experiments were performed once for each condition unless otherwise indicated. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using one-way analysis of variance (One-way ANOVA) with GraphPad Prism 9.5. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. Significance levels are denoted as follows: P < 0.05 (*), P < 0.01 (**), P < 0.001 (***), and P < 0.0001 (****); “ns” indicates no significant difference. Unless otherwise specified, all statistical analyses in this study were conducted using this method.
