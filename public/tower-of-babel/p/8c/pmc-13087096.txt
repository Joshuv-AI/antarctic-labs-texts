Oncologic Outcomes of Cytoreductive Radical Prostatectomy in Patients With Synchronous Metastatic Castration‐Sensitive Prostate Cancer: A Retrospective Analysis
Authors: Kento Morozumi, Kenji Numahata, Masaaki Oikawa, Masato Konno, Hiroki Kusumoto, Kuromoto Akito, Yuki Katsumata, Masahiko Sato, Yasuhiro Kaiho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesLocal therapy for metastatic castration‐sensitive prostate cancer (mCSPC) remains controversial. This study aimed to assess the feasibility, safety, and oncologic outcomes of cytoreductive radical prostatectomy (cRP) in patients with synchronous mCSPC, with a particular focus on pathological findings.MethodWe retrospectively analyzed 61 patients with mCSPC who underwent cRP following neoadjuvant androgen deprivation therapy (ADT). Clinical, perioperative, and pathological parameters were reviewed. Survival outcomes were assessed in relation to biopsy Gleason grade (GG), metastatic burden, PSA dynamics, and pathological treatment effect (pTE).ResultsThe 5‐year CRPC‐free survival and cancer‐specific survival (CSS) rates were 50.8% and 77.0%, respectively. Biopsy GG 5 was associated with adverse outcomes, whereas pTE grade ≥ 2 was significantly associated with favorable outcomes, including improved CSS. Biopsy GG 5 was the sole preoperative factor associated with pTE. The rate of major perioperative complications (Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ 3) was 1.6%, underscoring the safety of cRP in this setting.ConclusionCytoreductive radical prostatectomy (cRP) appears to be a feasible and safe local treatment option in selected patients with mCSPC. In addition to cytoreductive benefit, cRP provides critical pathological information—particularly pTE—that correlates strongly with survival outcomes but cannot be predicted preoperatively. These findings support the incorporation of cRP as a diagnostic and therapeutic component within a multimodal treatment strategy for mCSPC.Keywords: cytoreductive surgery, metastasis, prognosis, prostate cancer, radical prostatectomy

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the most common solid malignancy among men [1]. In 2024, approximately 299,010 new cases were diagnosed and 35,250 deaths were attributed to PCa in the United States [2]. While the overall 5‐year survival rate for PCa exceeds 97%, synchronous metastatic castration‐sensitive prostate cancer (mCSPC) remains an aggressive subtype, characterized by a high risk of progression to castration‐resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) and a 5‐year survival rate of only 30% [3].

In recent years, local therapy has been proposed as a potential strategy to improve outcomes in selected patients with mCSPC [4, 5, 6]. Although current guidelines do not routinely recommend local therapy in the metastatic setting, increasing evidence supports its benefit in carefully selected patients [7, 8]. The proposed mechanisms underlying these benefits include reduction of total tumor burden, modulation of the immune microenvironment, and prevention of local complications such as hematuria, urinary retention, or obstructive uropathy [4, 6]. From a biological perspective, the seed‐and‐soil hypothesis provides a conceptual framework for cytoreduction, whereby the primary tumor may act as a continuous source of metastatic seeding and systemic signaling that supports distant tumor growth. Removal of the primary lesion may therefore disrupt metastatic self‐seeding and tumor–microenvironment interactions, which have been proposed as a potential mechanism for influencing systemic disease progression [9, 10].

To date, most studies have focused on radiotherapy as the modality of local treatment, and the role of cytoreductive radical prostatectomy (cRP) remains less well defined [11]. In particular, data regarding its oncologic efficacy, perioperative safety, and pathological outcomes in the metastatic setting are limited. Moreover, the pathological therapeutic effect (pTE) of androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) in mCSPC, and its association with clinical outcomes, has not been comprehensively investigated.

In this study, we conducted a retrospective analysis of mCSPC patients treated at a single facility to evaluate the feasibility, safety, and prognostic utility of cRP, with a particular focus on the pathological response following neoadjuvant ADT.

Patients, Materials, and Methods

A total of 108 patients were diagnosed with mCSPC at Yamagata Prefectural Central Hospital between 2015 and 2018. Of these, 61 patients who underwent cRP and orchiectomy after receiving 6 months of ADT were included in this retrospective analysis. The remaining 47 patients did not undergo cRP due to poor general condition (n = 32, 68.1%), presence of comorbid malignancies that precluded surgery (n = 5, 10.6%), or refusal of surgery (n = 10, 21.3%). All patients initially received ADT alone, consisting of luteinizing hormone‐releasing hormone (LHRH) agonists or LHRH antagonists. cRP was performed on the patients who were fully informed and gave their consent for cases of mCSPC with surgical feasibility.

Clinical information collected included patient's age, performance status, International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) grade of biopsy specimen, presence of visceral and bone metastases, initial PSA level, PSA kinetics, pathological diagnosis and clinical outcomes. Metastatic burden at the diagnosis was categorized as high volume (HV) or low volume (LV) based on the criteria from the CHAARTED trial [12]. PSA elevation was defined as an increase in serum PSA levels immediately before surgery compared to the nadir PSA level achieved during neoadjuvant ADT [13]. Castration‐resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) was defined according to the PCWG3 criteria as disease progression during ADT, indicated by one or more of the following: a confirmed rise in PSA levels, the appearance of new lesions on imaging, or measurable progression of soft tissue disease [14]. pTE of ADT was evaluated based on the extent of residual viable tumor in the prostate specimen, using a four‐grade system adapted from previously reported criteria; grade 0: No response; no histologic evidence of tumor regression, grade 1: Minimal response; viable tumor cells constitute > 50% of the tumor area, grade 2: Partial response; viable tumor cells constitute 5%–50% of the tumor area, grade 3: Near‐complete response; viable tumor cells constitute < 5% of the tumor area [15]. For survival analyses, pTE grades 2 and 3 were grouped together to represent a biologically meaningful pathological response characterized by substantial tumor regression (≤ 50% viable tumor), whereas grades 0–1 were considered poor responders. pTE was evaluated independently by two genitourinary pathologists, discrepancies resolved by consensus.

Prognosis was assessed by reviewing the patients' medical records. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and statistical significance was assessed with the log‐rank test. Predictors of CRPC‐free survival were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression analysis. Multivariable Cox regression for CRPC‐free survival was performed with a limited number of prespecified covariates, including metastatic volume, biopsy Gleason Grade 5, perioperative PSA elevation, and pathological treatment effect (pTE), to minimize the risk of model overfitting. In contrast, for CSS, only univariate Cox regression analysis was performed because of the limited number of cancer‐specific death events, and multivariable analysis was not conducted. Based on this analysis, we also performed Kaplan–Meier analysis stratified by Gleason Grade (GG) on biopsy, metastatic volume, Nadir PSA, PSA elevation, LN metastases and pTE. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using BellCurve for Excel, version 4.08 (Social Survey Research Information Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Because this was a retrospective exploratory study, no formal sample size calculation was performed. The sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients treated at our institution during the study period, and survival analyses were conducted with careful consideration of event numbers to minimize the risk of model overfitting.

Results

Clinical and Pathological Variables

Table 1 summarizes patient characteristics and clinicopathological findings. Among the 61 patients analyzed, the median follow‐up duration was 55.0 months (range: 21.0–150.0 months). The median age was 70 years (range: 44–83 years), and all patients had a performance status of ≤ 1. The median initial PSA level was 58.0 ng/mL (range: 5.7–2149.4 ng/mL). GG 5 on biopsy was observed in 32 patients (52.5%). Bone metastases with an extent of disease (EOD) score of 2–3 were present in 32 patients (52.5%), and visceral metastases were observed in 5 patients (8.2%). Based on the CHAARTED criteria, 34 patients (55.7%) were classified as having HV disease, and 27 patients (44.3%) as having LV disease.

Perioperative Outcomes and Pathological Findings

The perioperative and pathological outcomes are summarized in Table 2. The median preoperative PSA level was 1.02 ng/mL (range: 0.006–22.66), and the median postoperative nadir PSA was 0.025 ng/mL (range: 0.01–29.0). The median operative time was 230 min (range: 108–1205 min), and the median amount of intraoperative blood loss was 607 mL (range: 57–2830 mL). Among the 61 patients, 58 (95.1%) underwent RP alone, while 3 (4.9%) required additional rectal resection due to direct tumor invasion [16]. Postoperative complications occurred in 42.5% of patients; however, no Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ 4 complications were observed. There were no patients with urinary symptoms such as hematuria or urinary retention during follow‐up periods.

Residual tumor was identified in 95.1% of cRP specimens, with 70.5% of patients exhibiting locally advanced disease (≥ pT3). Regarding surgical GG, GG 5 was the most common and was observed in 40 patients (65.6%). A total of 22 patients (36.1%) had pathological lymph node metastases, with a median of 11 lymph nodes resected (range: 1–23). pTE was graded as follows: grade 0 in 17 patients (27.9%), grade 1 in 21 patients (34.4%), grade 2 in 20 patients (32.8%), and grade 3 in 3 patients (4.9%).

During the follow‐up period, 30 patients (49.2%) progressed to CRPC, and 14 patients (23.0%) had died by the end of the observation period.

Survival Analysis

Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that the 5‐year CRPC‐free survival rate was 50.8%, and the 5‐year cancer‐specific survival (CSS) rate was 77.0% for the entire mCSPC cohort (Figure 1A,B). Multivariable Cox regression analysis for CRPC‐free survival, including metastatic volume, biopsy GG 5, perioperative PSA elevation, and pTE, revealed that biopsy GG 5 (HR 2.35; 95% CI, 1.40–3.95; p = 0.002), perioperative PSA elevation (HR 1.90; 95% CI, 1.35–2.70; p < 0.001), and pTE ≥ 2 (HR 0.42; 95% CI, 0.22–0.78; p = 0.001) were independently associated with CRPC‐free survival (Table 3). For CSS, pTE ≥ 2 was significantly associated with improved CSS in univariate analysis (HR 0.46; 95% CI, 0.33–0.77; p < 0.001). Table 4 shows that the only independent adverse factor for pTE was GG 5 on biopsy (OR 0.25; 95% CI, 0.07–0.83; p = 0.0024).

Figure 2 shows that pTE grade ≥ 2 was significantly associated with improved outcomes (5‐year CRPC‐free survival rate: 66.7% vs. 38.7%, p = 0.037; 5‐year CSS rate: 93.3% vs. 64.5%, p = 0.0065), and was significantly associated with improved CSS in univariate analysis. Patients with GG 5 on biopsy had significantly worse CRPC‐free survival rate compared to those with GG 1–4 on biopsy (5‐year CRPC‐free survival rate: 28.9% vs. 87.0%, p < 0.001), although there was no significant difference in CSS rate. PSA kinetics during ADT were also prognostically relevant: patients with a nadir PSA ≥ 0.5 ng/mL showed worse CRPC‐free survival rate than those with nadir PSA < 0.5 ng/mL (44.4% vs. 81.8%, p = 0.0078). Additionally, PSA elevation before cRP was associated with poor prognosis, with a 5‐year CRPC‐free survival rate of 21.7% in patients with elevation versus 68.4% in those without (p < 0.001). The presence of LN metastases was associated with significantly worse prognosis (5‐year CRPC‐free survival rate: 64.1% vs. 38.7%, p < 0.001; 5‐year CSS rate: 89.7% vs. 59.0%, p = 0.0058). Discussion

Local therapy for patients with mCSPC has been proposed to improve prognosis and reduce the incidence of local complications [4, 5, 6]. This study demonstrates that cRP combined with orchiectomy is feasible in selected mCSPC patients and may contribute to improved oncologic outcomes. In our cohort, the 5‐year CRPC‐free survival rate was 50.8%, and the 5‐year CSS rate was 77.0%. In contrast, previous Japanese cohort studies have reported less favorable outcomes with ADT alone: Narita et al. reported a 5‐year CSS rate of 55.6%, [17] while Miyazawa et al. reported a 5‐year CRPC‐free survival rate of approximately 25% and a CSS rate of around 60% [18]. These findings underscore the limitations of systemic therapy alone and suggest that adding local therapy such as cRP may confer meaningful clinical benefit. However, it should be emphasized that all patients in the present study were treated with ADT alone, without upfront treatment intensification using ARSIs or chemotherapy, which now represents the standard of care for mCSPC. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted within the context of the ADT alone era. In the contemporary treatment landscape, cRP should not be considered a substitute for intensified systemic therapy, but rather a potential complementary approach that may provide local disease control and pathological information to guide subsequent treatment decisions in carefully selected patients.

Previous retrospective studies have evaluated the role of cRP in mCSPC, particularly in patients with LV metastases. Heidenreich et al. reported that cRP in patients with LV mCSPC led to improved local control and delayed progression to CRPC [6]. Similarly, Sooriakumaran et al. demonstrated that cRP in mCSPC was associated with acceptable perioperative outcomes and a potential survival benefit, especially in those with LV disease [19]. With the increasing use of prostate‐specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography (PSMA PET/CT), staging accuracy for metastatic prostate cancer has improved, raising important considerations regarding stage migration and patient selection for local therapy [20]. A recent multicenter European study including 116 patients who underwent cytoreductive radical prostatectomy following PSMA‐PET staging reported encouraging intermediate‐term oncologic outcomes, with 2‐year CRPC‐free survival and overall survival rates of 85.8% and 98.9%, respectively [21]. However, the stage migration phenomenon associated with PSMA‐PET complicates direct comparisons between contemporary PSMA‐staged cohorts and earlier series based on conventional imaging, such as the present study. Further prospective studies incorporating molecular imaging are warranted to better define the optimal candidates for cytoreductive surgery in mCSPC.

The pathological findings in our cohort underscore the persistence of locally advanced and biologically aggressive disease despite 6 months of ADT. The predominance of ypT3b tumors, the high incidence of surgical GG 5 (65.6%), and the notable rate of lymph node metastases (36.1%) suggest that conventional ADT alone may be insufficient to induce adequate tumor regression for the majority of patients with mCSPC. Moreover, only 4.9% of patients achieved a pathological complete response (ypT0), further highlighting the limited tumoricidal efficacy of ADT in high‐risk diseases [22]. Importantly, these pathological features cannot be reliably assessed by imaging or serum markers alone and therefore provide diagnostic information beyond that obtained from systemic therapy alone [23]. Given the high prevalence of GG 5 and other adverse pathological features in our cohort, upfront treatment with ARSIs should be essential to achieve optimal disease control in patients with mCSPC [24, 25, 26, 27]. In this context, cRP serves not merely as a local treatment but as a diagnostic procedure that enables direct assessment of treatment response and residual tumor aggressiveness, thereby informing the need for early systemic treatment intensification. The pathological information obtained through cRP not only provides prognostic insight and offers a biologically grounded framework for tailoring subsequent systemic treatment strategies based on individual tumor biology, particularly in patients with high‐grade or treatment‐resistant features.

Our results further emphasize the prognostic significance of the pTE in response to ADT. Patients with a pTE grade ≥ 2 exhibited significantly improved outcomes, with approximately two‐thirds achieving long‐term CRPC‐free survival. To our knowledge, this study is among the first to demonstrate the potential prognostic value of pTE in the mCSPC setting [28]. These findings suggest that there may be a favorable‐risk subgroup of selected patients for whom cRP combined with ADT may represent an effective component of multimodal treatment rather than a sufficient treatment strategy alone. Notably, pTE grade ≥ 2 showed a significant association with improved CSS, whereas traditional clinical markers such as metastatic volume and PSA response did not demonstrate a significant association with CSS [29, 30]. Furthermore, no pre‐treatment clinical parameters, other than GG 5 on biopsy, were associated with favorable pTE, underscoring the inability of conventional metrics to be a reliable predictor of pathological response. Thus, cRP not only provides a local therapeutic effect but also serves as a critical tool for evaluating biological response to systemic therapy, offering pathological insights that cannot be captured through imaging or serum markers alone [17, 31] More importantly, we further observed a significant inverse association between GG 5 on biopsy and favorable pTE: patients with GG 5 on biopsy disease were significantly less likely to exhibit a pTE grade ≥ 2 (OR 0.18, 95% CI: 0.04–0.61, p = 0.040). This finding supports the notion that high‐grade tumors may harbor intrinsic resistance to ADT and are less likely to undergo meaningful pathological regression. Therefore, the presence of GG 5 could potentially serve as a negative predictor of favorable treatment effects. Taken together, these findings underscore the dual therapeutic and diagnostic value of cRP in mCSPC. Because pTE cannot be reliably predicted preoperatively, surgical removal of the primary tumor provides critical biological insight into tumor responsiveness. In this regard, cRP may function not only as local therapy but also as a potential biological indicator of systemic treatment sensitivity, potentially enabling more personalized post‐operative management.

Radiotherapy has been proposed to exert systemic immune‐mediated effects, including the abscopal effect, through radiation‐induced immunogenic cell death. In contrast, the potential systemic effects of cytoreductive surgery remain less clearly defined. Rather than inducing immune activation, surgical cytoreduction may reduce immunosuppressive tumor burden and eliminate a source of continuous metastatic seeding. These mechanistic differences suggest that surgery and radiotherapy may influence systemic disease control through distinct biological pathways, and their relative roles warrant further investigation.

Local complications such as hematuria and urinary retention occur in approximately one‐third of patients with mCSPC [32, 33]. Several studies have suggested that local therapy targeting the primary tumor may not only improve prognosis but also mitigate the risk of local complications. Tsuboi et al. reported that local treatment in mCSPC reduced the incidence of local complications, thereby decreasing the need for surgical interventions [32]. Wang et al. similarly demonstrated that combining ADT with radiotherapy improved outcomes in this population [33]. In our cohort, no patients developed local symptoms during follow‐up, indicating excellent local control. These findings further indicate the possibility of cRP having clinical value not limited to oncologic control.

In terms of safety, cRP was well tolerated. Only one patient (1.6%) experienced a Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ 3 complication, reflecting the overall feasibility of cRP. This aligns with previous reports by Sooriakumaran et al., who reported a major complication rate of 7.8% and no perioperative mortality, [19] and Heidenreich et al. who also observed a low complication rate [6]. Tilki et al. further demonstrated that RP for high‐risk and locally advanced PCa carries acceptable morbidity and is oncologically safe [34]. Although cRP in the metastatic setting is often considered high‐risk, our findings demonstrate otherwise. Notably, the three patients who required rectal resection were preoperatively identified to have tumor invasion, and multidisciplinary surgical planning was implemented accordingly. All patients recovered without mortality, and the procedures were performed with informed consent emphasizing surgical complexity and risk. These findings underscore that, with careful patient selection, multidisciplinary planning, and experienced surgical teams, cRP can be safely and feasibly incorporated into multimodal care, even in anatomically complex cases.

In this study, orchiectomy was performed concurrently with cRP to provide immediate and definitive androgen deprivation without ongoing medical therapy. This approach may offer advantages in terms of cost‐effectiveness, patient compliance, and potentially more complete testosterone suppression compared to LHRH analogs, particularly in resource‐limited settings or in elderly patients [35, 36].

From a clinical perspective, the findings of the present study may have implications for treatment decision‐making in patients with mCSPC. In particular, the pathological response observed in the primary tumor following neoadjuvant ADT may provide additional biological information that cannot be captured through conventional imaging or serum biomarkers. Such information may help identify patients who require early treatment intensification with modern systemic therapies, including androgen receptor signaling inhibitors or chemotherapy. However, this study has several limitations. First, the study was a retrospective analysis and is therefore subject to selection bias. In particular, patient selection for cRP was based on surgical feasibility, physician judgment, and patient consent, which may have resulted in selection bias favoring patients with better general condition and fewer comorbidities, thereby limiting the generalizability of the results. Second, treatment decisions, including the use of antiandrogens and bone‐modifying agents, were left to the discretion of individual physicians and may not reflect current clinical guidelines. More importantly, none of the patients received upfront combination therapy with ARSIs or docetaxel, and access to these agents was limited following progression to mCRPC. Additionally, disease assessment was based on conventional imaging (MRI, CT, and bone scintigraphy), which may be less sensitive compared to current imaging modalities. In particular, PSMA PET/CT, which has been increasingly reported to improve staging accuracy in patients with mCSPC considered for local therapy, was not utilized in this cohort because it was not routinely available during the study period and remains not universally reimbursed in Japan. As a result, the metastatic burden may have been underestimated, and occult micro‐metastatic disease could not be fully excluded. Future studies incorporating modern imaging modalities such as PSMA PET/CT are warranted to refine staging accuracy and patient selection. Finally, the number of cancer‐specific death events was limited, which precluded robust multivariable survival modeling for cancer‐specific survival and may limit the generalizability of prognostic inferences.

Despite these limitations, our findings suggest that cRP with orchiectomy is a feasible and potentially effective treatment option for selected patients with mCSPC, particularly as part of a multimodal treatment strategy. In addition to providing local tumor control, cRP enables acquisition of valuable pathological insights that cannot be obtained through imaging or serum markers alone. Among these, the degree of pTE following neoadjuvant ADT was significantly associated with CRPC‐free survival and CSS. Notably, patients with a pTE ≥ 2 showed significantly better oncologic outcomes. Furthermore, biopsy Gleason grade 5 (GG5) was inversely associated with favorable pTE, suggesting that high‐grade tumors may be less responsive to neoadjuvant ADT. These observations highlight the potential of surgical pathology to inform prognosis and guide subsequent systemic treatment decisions. However, because this study did not include a direct comparison with patients treated with ADT alone or ADT combined with radiotherapy, the survival outcomes should be interpreted cautiously, and no definitive conclusions regarding the comparative benefit of cRP can be drawn.

Conclusions

cRP may represent a feasible component of multimodal treatment strategies in carefully selected patients with mCSPC, providing both local tumor control and valuable pathological information that may help guide subsequent systemic therapy.
