Little things matter: Unveiling hidden invaders in marine coastal communities
Authors: James T. Carlton
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The burgeoning abundance of a microscopic marine invader on the US Atlantic seaboard may signal the scale of overlooked non-native species in the sea.

In coastal waters around the world, small invaders go largely unnoticed. Place a sediment sample from a bay or estuary under a microscope and a rich and busy world of tiny creatures present themselves: ostracod and copepod crustaceans, nematodes, flatworms, ciliated protists, and foraminifera—“forams,” ubiquitous, shelled and often beautifully sculptured eukaryotes. The same estuaries often support diverse communities of non-native macroscopic invertebrates and seaweeds, transported by ships around the world for centuries—an anthropogenic global bioflow that continues robustly today.

However, science usually falls silent about whether all the “small stuff” also includes non-native species.

In this issue of Science Advances, Goetz et al. (1) announce the first detection of an introduced microscopic benthic species in Long Island Sound, one of North America’s iconic estuaries. Although only recently identified, the foraminiferan Ammonia confertitesta, native to the Northwest Pacific Ocean (coastal China), became abundant in the 1970s in shallow waters east of New York City. Ships taking up ballast water in New York then transported A. confertitesta in the 1980s to Europe. Independently, A. confertitesta was found in 2022 in the Northeast Pacific, in British Columbia, completing its circuit around the Global North (2).

To understand when A. confertitesta may have arrived in the Western Atlantic from the Western Pacific, Goetz et al. studied archived museum specimens of foraminifera from Long Island Sound as well as recent collections. Critically, they took sediment cores, dated by standard 210Pb/137Cs and 14C methodology. The cores, dating back thousands of years, revealed forams identifiable to species level. In addition, and in a welcome and too rare exploration, they asked questions about the maritime history of the New York area, history that could provide insight into the hub and spoke model of invasions (3)—with the potential home range of A. confertitesta being the hub and one of the spokes leading to New York City.

But, a surprise turned up—thanks to the fine-grained analysis permitted by carefully analyzed cores. Although the first modern record of A. confertitesta in Long Island Sound is 1961, it also appeared in a core dated to about 1820, not far from New York Harbor—140 years earlier. How did the Western Pacific come to the Western Atlantic at the turn of the 19th century?

The American Revolution ended on 3 September 1783, with the signing of the Treaty of Paris. Only 172 days later, on 22 February 1784, the first US merchant vessel, The Empress of China, departed for China—returning May 1785, marking the start of what became known as the Old China Trade. The period lasted until 1844 (4). The paths created by wars have often been related to new invasion tracks (3); the cessation of wars, rarely so. In addition to this little Asian foram, it may be that the Asian red seaweed Melanothamnus harveyi arrived in the same era in Long Island Sound. First collected in the Sound in 1847 (5), M. harveyi is a small filamentous alga that would have easily escaped earlier detection, albeit not escaping the common fate of description as a native species (6). We know of few other arrivals on the North American Atlantic coast from the Western Pacific before the mid-19th century, but it seems likely that the discovery reported by Goetz et al. signals that the early histories of other invaders may be overlooked as well—or perhaps are still hidden in the fauna and flora of New England as pseudoendemics (6).

Had this Asian foraminiferan been in Long Island Sound all along, in small numbers, followed by a long lag until a resurgence nearly a century-and-a-half later? Or was its earlier arrival, followed by an extinction, the ghost of invasions future? With the surge in late 20th century globalization, the North Atlantic Ocean saw a concomitant surge in species arrivals from the Western Pacific (7), providing a modern conveyor belt for A. confertitesta from Asia. Goetz et al. leave this question open for future work.

Regardless, the presence of an Asian marine species in early 19th century New York reminds us that introductions, mediated by global human-mediated dispersal, began long before most biological surveys commenced. Sailing ships long transported species on their hulls, including in the deep interstices of wooden ships, and in their rock and shore ballast. After the 1880s, ships traded solid ballast for water ballast while continuing to carry species in numerous niche spaces on their hulls, in sea chests, and in seawater pipe systems. A. confertitesta likely rode on both of these early and later phases of vessel-mediated transport.

Two hundred years after A. confertitesta first arrived near New York City, opportunities continue to abound for the arrival and novel colonization of foraminifera and many other marine species—unfortunately, more so than ever, and with increasing frequency. Goetz et al. report that yet another species of allochthonous Ammonia has joined A. confertitesta in Long Island Sound—the warm-water American A. advena, likely traceable to the burgeoning phenomenon known as Caribbean Creep (8), the climate-induced poleward movement of countless, low-latitude species along the Western Atlantic shore. An additional anthropocene dispersal infusion is another global phenomenon: the growing sea of rafting plastic marine debris (9), now providing expansive prospects for the long-distance coastal and transoceanic transport of marine life, including foraminiferans.

The informal “smalls rule” of biogeography, an assumption that tiny species are typically naturally cosmopolitan (10), has led to a multicentennial obfuscation of the true scale of the history and richness of invasions. Non-native macroscopic marine species have been reported in astounding numbers around the world (10). Most notable among the many regions that support hundreds of introduced marine species: the Mediterranean Sea, San Francisco Bay, California, and the Hawaiian Islands. Were we to increase the biogeographic resolution of many of the little species in these same communities, the very same taxa that are often invoked in marine ecology classes as key components of the structure and function of benthic communities, would invasion richness be recognized as vastly greater?
