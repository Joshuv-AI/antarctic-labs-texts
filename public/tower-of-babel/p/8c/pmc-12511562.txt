Pyridine-assisted solvent engineering for high-quality narrow-bandgap perovskites in efficient tandem modules
Authors: Jinglin Sun, Qiushi Tian, Chao Yu, Jie Zhou, Yang Shi, Haibao Jin, Zhibin Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
All-perovskite tandem photovoltaic modules represent a promising technology for enhancing power conversion efficiency. However, a significant challenge remains in preparing high-quality, large-area tin-lead mixed narrow-bandgap (NBG) perovskite films for these tandem modules. Here, we develop a method to prepare large-area, compact, and uniform NBG perovskite films by modulating the crystallization process through solvent engineering. Specifically, we introduce pyridine, a solvent with strong donor coordinating ability and high saturated vapor pressure, into the solvent system. The strong coordination ability of pyridine facilitates better formation of intermediate phases during the crystallization process of tin-based NBG perovskite films, while its high saturated vapor pressure enables the solvent to evaporate quickly and reduce solvent residue at the bottom of the films. By combining this strategy with a vacuum annealing method, the all-perovskite tandem photovoltaic mini-modules exhibited an average efficiency of 22.0 ± 0.4% with an aperture area of 10.4 cm2, demonstrating their promising potential for future commercialization.

Introduction

The solution processable all-perovskite tandem solar cells exhibit great potential as future photovoltaic (PV) technique with their power conversion efficiency (PCE) gradually exceeding 30%1–3. However, most reported high-efficiency tandem devices were realized in small device area (~0.1 cm2) by the spin-coating technique. Only several studied explored the techniques for large-scale production of all-perovskite solar modules4–8. Till now, high-throughput solution processing techniques, such as blade coating9, roll-to-roll coating10, slot-die coating11, etc., have been widely studied and applied in single-junction perovskite solar cells (PSCs) with a bandgap of approximately 1.5 eV12–15. However, their solvent systems cannot be directly transferred into the fabrication of narrow-bandgap (NBG) or wide-bandgap (WBG) perovskite films in the tandem devices due to the different crystallization dynamic process16,17. Both Sn/Pb mixed NBG perovskites and I/Br mixed WBG perovskites have their own unique properties and should develop proper solvent systems for high-quality perovskite film preparation. Especially, the Sn/Pb mixed NBG perovskite suffers from their fast crystallization rate when using the commonly used N, N’-dimethylformamide (DMF)/dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) solvent system during large-scale preparation with solution processing. Although the crystallization process of pure Pb- or Sn-based perovskite systems has been widely investigated and modulated individually14,15. It is a fundamentally more complex challenge in controlling the crystallization process of Sn/Pb mixed perovskites, which arises from the significant disparity in crystallization kinetics between Sn- and Pb-based perovskite components.

The commonly used solvent systems for preparing perovskite films mainly consist of noncoordinating and coordinating solvents. The coordinating capability of solvents can be roughly described by donor number (DN), which refers to the bond strength between the solvents and perovskite precursors18,19. The DN of I- ions is measured to be 28.9 kcal/mol in 1,2-dichloroethane and therefore severed as a boundary between noncoordinating and coordinating solvents20. Noncoordinating solvents commonly act as great mediums to dissolve the perovskite precursors with little interaction, such as DMF, 2-methoxyethanol and acetonitrile, etc. However, due to the intrinsic fast crystallization property of Sn/Pb mixed NBG perovskites, volatile solvents including 2-methoxyethanol and acetonitrile are not good choices as noncoordinating solvents4,19. In addition, Cs- or Sn-based salts have low solubility in these solvents, which further limits their application4. Therefore, DMF still act as main noncoordinating solvent in the Sn/Pb mixed NBG perovskite precursors. As another important part, the coordinating solvents, such as DMSO and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP), are broadly used to enhance the crystallinity and morphology of Pb-based perovskite films by forming an intermediate phase through coordination interactions21–23. Unfortunately, it was found that the crystallization process of Sn/Pb mixed NBG perovskites cannot be finely modulated by forming a DMSO or NMP-induced intermediate phase due to the insufficient coordination strength16. Comparing to Pb2+ ions, Sn2+ ions have a lager ionic radius due to lanthanide shrinkage as well as smaller electropositivity, resulting in weaker coordination ability with DMSO and NMP molecules24. In addition, those solvents with low saturated vapor pressure are easily trapped at the buried perovskite interface upon annealing and form voids after annealing, ultimately leading to a decline in device performance12,25,26. In summary, it is still a big challenge to achieve the fabrication of high-quality NBG perovskite films due to the asynchronous crystallization behavior between Sn- and Pb-based perovskites. Therefore, introducing a coordinating solvent with strong Sn²⁺-binding affinity (higher DN value) and high saturated vapor pressure is crucial for optimizing the solvent system and fabricating high-quality NBG perovskite films.

In this work, we successfully prepare high-quality and large-area NBG perovskite films by solvent engineering for their application in all-perovskite tandem solar modules. Among different coordinating solvents, pyridine, with a higher DN value and saturated vapor pressure compared to DMSO, was found to be an ideal coordinating solvent for blade coating high-quality NBG perovskite films. Benefiting from the strong electron-donor capability, pyridine can effectively coordinate with the Sn2+ ions to form an intermediate phase, thereby suppressing the rapid heterogeneous nucleation in the crystallization process. Meanwhile, the high saturated vapor pressure of pyridine can accelerate the solvent extraction together with a vacuum annealing (VA) process. Thus, the crystal growth process is both thermodynamically regulated by the formation of a strong electronic donor-accept complex and kinetically modulated by phase conversion rate during the crystallization process, leading to homogeneous and compact NBG perovskite films. As a demonstration, we fabricate all-perovskite tandem solar mini-modules and achieve an average efficiency of 22.0 ± 0.4% with an aperture area of 10.4 cm2.

Results and discussion

Preparation of large-area and high-quality NBG perovskite films

In this study, NBG perovskite films with a composition of FA0.8Cs0.2Pb0.5Sn0.5I3 were prepared by a blade-coating method with a vacuum assistant annealing as shown in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1. The control NBG perovskite precursor was prepared with a commonly used DMSO/DMF mixed solvent system. Due to the low saturated vapor pressure of DMSO and DMF, a nitrogen knife was applied after the blade coater to accelerate the drying process of the wet perovskite films. To achieve a high-quality NBG perovskite film, we aimed to modulate the crystallization process by solvent engineering.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of blade-coating NBG perovskite films via solvent engineering.

Schematic illustration of blade-coating NBG perovskite films via solvent engineering.

Considering that the commonly used coordinating solvent DMSO has a relatively low DN value of 29.8 kcal mol−1 and a low saturated vapor pressure of 0.049 KPa, which results in little formation of intermediate phase and a slow drying process of the blade-coated NBG perovskite films. Two solvents pyridine (DN of 33.1 kcal mol−1 and saturated vapor pressure of 1.50 KPa) and 1,2-Diaminopropane (DP, DN of 52.5 kcal mol−1 and saturated vapor pressure of 1.07 KPa) with higher DN values and saturated vapor pressures were introduced into the solvent system to modulate the crystallization process (Figs. 1, 2a). For a comparative analysis of the coordination ability of solvents with different DN values, 1.0 mol/L PbI2 and SnI2 powder were added into solvents DMSO, pyridine and DP, respectively. As shown in Fig. 2b–d and Supplementary Fig. 2, the PbI2 and SnI2 were directly soluble in DMSO, while white PbI2·pyridine and yellow SnI2 ·pyridine adducts were formed due to the strong coordination originating from their high DN values. Similar phenomena were observed in the solvent of DP. With the presence of amine ions in the precursor, the perovskite can be fully dissolved in these solvents based on their coordination. After undergoing an N2-assisted blade coating process, the NBG perovskite film, prepared from a precursor with DMSO/DMF solvent, rapidly crystallized and directly converted into the black perovskite phase prior to annealing, as shown in Fig. 2e. The absence of significant formation of intermediate phase was primarily attributed to the weak coordination between DMSO and Sn2+ ions. Upon incorporating pyridine with high DN value into the solvents system, a homogeneous brown film was formed after the blade-coating process, which corresponded to a pyridine-based intermediate phase (Fig. 2f). When DP with a higher DN value was introduced into the solvent system, a light-yellow film was obtained with a much stronger intermediate phase (Fig. 2g). After blade-coating, a VA process was applied to promote the rapid phase conversion. As shown in Fig. 2h, some voids were observed at the bottom of the perovskite films that prepared from the DMSO-based precursor. This was attributed to DMSO molecules being trapped at the bottom interface and escaping during the annealing process, ultimately causing damage to the films27. When pyridine was added into the solvent system, a compact and uniform NBG perovskite film with highly textured columnar was obtained as shown in Fig. 2i. That was because the crystallization process was effectively slowdown by the formation of an intermediate phase. Similarly, there is no voids formed in the bottom of NBG perovskite films that prepared from DP-based precursor as shown in Fig. 2j. However, according to the cross-sectional and top-viewed SEM images presented in Fig. 2j and Supplementary Fig. 3, the DP-based NBG perovskite film exhibits a small crystal size with a non-dense grain-packing structure. This could be attributed to the formation of an excessive number of nucleation sites, which is likely caused by the overly strong coordination between DP and Pb2+/Sn2+ ions28. Subsequently, the X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectroscopy was conducted to understand the crystallinity of different NBG perovskite films, as shown in Fig. 2k. In comparison to the control sample, pyridine based NBG perovskite films lead to an obvious intensity increase of all XRD specific peaks and are further enhanced with VA operation, which indicates that both pyridine and VA operation has a positive effect on improving the crystallinity of NBG perovskite films.Fig. 2NBG Perovskite Films prepared with different coordinating solvents.a DN and Saturated vapor pressure (at 20°C) of DMSO, pyridine and DP. b–d Photographs of SnI2 solubility in DMSO (b), pyridine (c) and DP (d). e–g Photographs of the blade-coated perovskite films prepared with DMSO (e), Pyridine/DMSO (f) and DP/DMSO (g) based perovskite precursors before annealing. h–j Cross-sectional SEM images (h–j) and XRD spectra (k) of NBG perovskite films prepared with DMSO (h), Pyridine/DMSO (i) and DP/DMSO (j) based perovskite precursors after annealing. l Photographs of a blade-coated NBG perovskite film with dimensions of 15 cm × 10 cm.

Based on the above founding, pyridine was selected as the coordinating solvent in the perovskite precursor for the preparation of high-quality NBG perovskite films in this study. The volume ratio of pyridine in the solvent system was systematically optimized by evaluating the morphology and crystallinity of the resulting perovskite films using a 3D optical surface profiler and XRD spectroscopy, respectively. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, as the volume ratio of pyridine increases from 0 vol% to 10 vol%, the surface of the perovskite films gradually becomes smoother with the arithmetic mean roughness (Ra) decreasing from 26.2 nm to 3.7 nm. However, the Ra increases to 18.0 nm when the pyridine ratio is further increased to 16 vol%. In the XRD study (Supplementary Fig. 5), all NBG perovskite films exhibit similar XRD patterns with specific peaks located at 14.2° and 28.5°, which correspond to the (110) and (220) crystal planes of the NBG perovskite. The intensities of all specific XRD peaks are gradually enhanced as the pyridine ratio in the precursors increasing from 0 vol% to 10 vol%, and then significantly decreased when the pyridine ratio is further increased to 16 vol%. This trend is similar to the observed changes in film roughness. Based on the results of the morphologies and XRD studies, it can be concluded that a 10 vol% ratio of pyridine in the precursor solvent system achieves the best quality of NBG perovskite film. Overall, high-quality NBG perovskite films with smooth surface morphology, compact interior and high crystallinity can be realized by combining a pyridine-based solvent engineering and vacuum annealing process. As a demonstration, a compact and uniform NBG perovskite film with a size of 15 cm × 10 cm can be easily prepared by a blade-coating process as shown in Fig. 2l.

Sequentially, photoelectric properties of NBG perovskite films prepared with different coordinating solvents were investigated by scanning Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM), conductive atomic force microscopy (C-AFM) and photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 3a,b, the control DMSO-based NBG perovskite films exhibit a broad contact surface potential distribution (CPD) of ~40 mV and a wide leakage current distribution (LCD) of ~4.2 pA, accompanied with a high leakage current of ~12.0 pA. This highly heterogeneous distribution of surface charge and leakage current are mainly due to the presence of a high density of active and reactive defects within the perovskite films29. Conversely, upon the incorporation of pyridine into the film preparation process, the CPD, LCD and leakage current of the perovskite film are notably decreased to approximately ~20 mV, ~3.5 pA and ~7.2 pA, respectively, as illustrated in Fig. 3c, d. This implies that the use of pyridine can improve the quality of NBG perovskite films with a lower defect density. Furthermore, when compared to the control NBG perovskite film, the pyridine-based perovskite film displays a notably enhanced intensity of steady-state PL peak, indicating suppressed nonradiative carrier recombination due to improved crystallinity (Fig. 3e). To further investigate the area-dependent charge-carrier dynamics, time-resolved PL mapping was also conducted, as shown in Fig. 3f. The PL lifetimes of the pyridine-based NBG perovskite films exhibit a longer and more uniform distribution than those of the control samples, indicating a reduced defect density after modulating the crystallization process with pyridine.Fig. 3Photoelectric properties of NBG perovskite films.a, c the contact surface potential mapping and distribution from KPFM study of NBG perovskite films prepared with DMSO (a) and Pyridine/DMSO (c) based solvents. b, d the leakage current mapping image and distribution recorded from C-AFM of NBG perovskite films prepared with DMSO (b) and Pyridine/DMSO (d) based solvents. e and f Steady-state PL spectroscopies (e) and TRPL mapping image (f) of NBG perovskite films prepared with DMSO and Pyridine/DMSO-based solvents.

Mechanism study of pyridine based intermediate phase

Based on the critical role of pyridine in the preparation of high-quality NBG perovskite films, we attempt to gain a deeper understanding of its underlying mechanism. Considering the coordination solvent has an electron donor-acceptor (EDA) interaction with Pb2+ and Sn2+ ions in the perovskite precursor, The binding energies between DMSO/Pyridine and PbI2/SnI2 were carefully calculated by density function theory (DFT) (Fig. 4a, b and Supplementary Fig. 6). PbI2 and SnI2 respectively own intermediate solvated phases of PbI2·2DMSO30 and SnI2·3DMSO31 according to previous reports. Based on our DFT calculations, the binding energies of PbI2·2DMSO and SnI2·3DMSO are 26.67 and 45.54 kcal/mol, respectively. In contrast, when coordinating with pyridine, the binding energy of PbI2·2pyridine and SnI2·3pyridine is enhanced to 29.24 and 46.94 kcal/mol, respectively. These calculated results confirmed that the pyridine has a stronger binding energy with PbI2 and SnI2. For coordinating solvent DMSO, the electron cloud density of lone-pair electrons on the O atoms (two lone-pair electrons) of DMSO decreases due to n-Π conjugation. Since the N atoms of pyridine are not involved in the conjugation of the pyridine ring, a lone-pair electron from these N atoms possesses a high electron cloud density, which favorably forms strong N-Pb/Sn bonds. The EDA interaction between Pyridine/DMSO and Pb2+/Sn2+ ions is further experimentally examined by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR). As shown in Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 7a, the absorption peak at 1042 cm−1 is attributed to the stretching vibration of S = O (ν(S = O)) in the DMSO32, which is also observed in the FTIR spectra of DMSO·PbI2 and DMSO·SnI2, owing to the presence of DMSO solvent in these compounds. In accordance with harmonic motion principles for the diatomic model, it is known that the frequency of vibration is proportional to the square root of the force constant, which suggests that a reduction in the S = O stretching frequency implies a decrease in the force constant22,32. For PbI2·2DMSO and SnI2·3DMSO, the ν(S = O) peaks shift from 1042 to 1014 cm−1 and from 1042 to 994 cm−1, respectively. This indicates a decrease in bond strength between the sulfur and oxygen atoms, resulting from the coordination between DMSO and the Pb2+/Sn2+ ions. In contrast to pyridine presented in Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 7b, the absorption peak associated with the stretching vibration of C-N (ν(C-N)) in pyridine shifts from 1068 cm−1 to 1064 cm−1 in both spectra of PbI2·3pyridine and SnI2·3pyridine. Furthermore, new signals at 1003 cm−1 and 1006 cm−1 were detected, which corresponds to the bending vibrations of the pyridine skeleton, induced by the coordination between pyridine and the Pb2+ and Sn2+ ions. These findings confirm that pyridine exhibits a stronger EDA interaction with Sn2+ ions compared to DMSO.

To further understand the crystallization process with the presence of pyridine in the precursor, the intermediate states were also investigated by XRD spectroscopy. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 8, both compounds of PbI2·2DMSO and PbI2·3pyridine exhibits new XRD peaks compared to PbI2 pattern indicates an existence of intermediate phase. While the SnI2-DMSO system formed a homogeneous solution without any detectable solid adducts, the SnI2-pyridine combination resulted in the formation of stable solid adducts, as evidenced by the appearance of multiple characteristic XRD peaks (Fig. 4e). This result also indicates that there is a stronger coordination interaction between pyridine and SnI2, leading to the formation of intermediate phase products. Upon the occurrence of the coordination effect, the positive charges of Pb2+ and Sn2+ reduce the electronic cloud density of the pyridine ring, thereby decreasing the shielding effect. This leads to a chemical shift in the carbon atom of pyridine, which can be detected by 13C NMR as shown in Supplementary Fig. 933.

Photovoltaic performance of all-perovskite tandem mini-modules

After optimizing the NBG perovskite films by solvent engineering, we systematically studied their impact on the photovoltaic performance of NBG PSCs with a structure of glass/Indium tin oxide (ITO)/poly(3,4-ethylene dioxythiophene): poly(styrenesulfonate)(PEDOT: PSS)/FA0.8Cs0.2Pb0.5Sn0.5I3/C60/bathocuproine (BCP)/Ag. As shown in Fig. 5a and Table S1, the typical control NBG PSCs fabricated by the blade-coating method shows a PCE of 18.5% with a VOC of 0.814 V, short-circuit current (JSC) of 30.2 mA cm−2 and FF of 75.2%. With the ratio of pyridine increases from 0 to 10 vol% in the mixed solvent system, all three photovoltaic parameters, VOC, JSC and FF, show notably enhancement. Upon incorporating 10 vol% pyridine into the DMSO-DMF solvent system, we achieve a best PCE of 22.0% with a VOC of 0.853 V, JSC of 32.5 mA cm−2 and FF of 79.2%. However, the excessive presence of pyridine, with its ratio further increasing to 13 vol% and 16 vol% in the precursors, drives suboptimal photovoltaic performance of the NBG PSCs. This could be attributed to the progressive deterioration of the crystallization and morphology of the perovskite films (Supplementary Figs. 4, 5). The JSC of the optimized device were further confirmed by the integrated current density from external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra with a deviation of ~4% (Supplementary Fig. 10). To assess the universality of this approach, we introduced pyridine into the perovskite precursor solution for spin-coated devices. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 11, the control spin-coated NBG PSCs achieved a PCE of 22.3% (VOC of 0.875 V, JSC of 31.9 mA cm−2, and FF of 79.8%). Devices incorporating pyridine in the mixed solvent system exhibited a comparable PCE of 22.9% (VOC of 0.885 V, JSC of 32.0 mA cm−2, and FF of 81.0%). However, pyridine addition yielded no significant performance enhancement. Subsequently, NBG perovskite mini-modules were fabricated as shown in Fig. 5b. The widths of each sub-cell and dead area in the mini-module are designed to be 6.7 and 0.35 mm, thus corresponding to a geometric factor of 94.7% (Supplementary Fig. 12). By covering a shadow mask with an aperture area of 10.4 cm2 during JV measurement, the NBG perovskite mini-module exhibits a high PCE of 16.3% with a VOC of 3.25 V, a ISC of 72.6 mA and a FF of 71.7% under reverse scan and shows neglectable hysteresis (Fig. 5c and Table 1). The optimized single-junction WBG perovskite mini-module exhibits a PCE of 15.9% with a VOC of 5.13 V, an ISC of 40.6 mA and a FF of 79.0% (Table 1). Moreover, Both NBG and WBG mini-modules exhibit excellent reproducibility with average PCE of 15.9 ± 0.17% and 15.7 ± 0.16%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 13). After completing the optimization of both NBG and WBG perovskite mini-modules, monolithic all-perovskite tandem mini-modules with a structure of ITO/NiOx/WBG perovskite/C60/SnO2/Au/PEDOT:PSS/NBG perovskite/C60/BCP/Ag were fabricated as demonstrated in Fig. 5d. The cross-sectional SEM image of a tandem device is presented in Fig. 5e, where a 900-nm-thick NBG perovskite film together with a 480-nm-thick WBG perovskite films are used for current match. The fabricated all-perovskite tandem mini-module with an aperture area of 10.4 cm2 exhibits an average efficiency of 22.0 ± 0.40% with excellent reproducibility (Fig. 5f). The champion mini-module exhibits a high PCE of 22.9% under reverse scan, with a VOC of 8.10 V, an ISC of 37.0 mA and a FF of 79.5% (Fig. 5g, Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 16 and Table S2). Among the reported tandem modules as summarized in Table S3, our device demonstrates a competitive efficiency level within the current state-of-the-art. The remarkable FF of the final tandem mini-module is predominantly governed by the superior FF of the WBG sub-module, which serves as the limiting element in the tandem architecture and consequently dictates the overall FF characteristics of the integrated device34. The EQE spectra of the NBG and WBG sub-cells in the tandem mini-module were also investigated, as shown in Fig. 5h. The integrated JSC from EQE spectra for NBG and WBG sub-cells are 14.8 and 14.7 mA cm−2, respectively, indicating an excellent current matching. Finally, the photostability of encapsulated all-perovskite tandem mini-modules were investigated under light-emitting diode (LED) illumination in ambient conditions with a relative humidity of 30–40%. The tandem module exhibited minimal variation in PCE at an MPP voltage of 6.40 V during the initial 4800 s (Supplementary Fig. 17) and maintained approximately 87% of its initial value after 1000 h of illumination (Fig. 5i), demonstrating excellent photostability.

In summary, we developed an approach to preparing large-area and high-quality NBG perovskite films by modulating the solvent coordination in the perovskite precursor. After comparison, the coordinating solvent pyridine with stronger electron-donor capability and higher saturated vapor pressure enables better formation of the intermediate phase for tin-based NBG perovskite thin films, thereby suppressing the rapid heterogeneous nucleation in the crystallization process. Based on that, we successfully prepared large-area compact and uniform NBG perovskite films for module fabrication. The all-perovskite tandem photovoltaic mini-modules based on the above-optimized NBG perovskite films exhibit an average efficiency of 22.0 ± 0.4% with an aperture area of 10.4 cm2. This study developed an approach for preparing high-quality NBG perovskite films, which facilitates the commercialization of all-perovskite tandem modules.

Methods

Materials

All solvents and chemical materials were used as purchased without further purification. FAI (99.999%) was purchased from GreatCell Solar Materials. CsI (99.9%) was purchased from Thermo Scientific. PbI2 (99.99%, trace metals basis), SnI2 (99.999%, trace metals basis), SnF2 (99%), DMF (99.8% anhydrous), and DMSO (99.8% anhydrous) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Pyridine (99.8% anhydrous) and 1,2-Diaminopropane (99.9%) were purchased from TCI. PEDOT: PSS aqueous solution (Clevios™ P VP AI 4083), C60 (99.7%), and BCP (99.6%) were purchased from Xi’an Polymer Light Technology. PbBr2 (99.999%, trace metals basis) was purchased from Energy Chemical. NiOX (99%) was purchased from Shanghai Weizhu New Material Technology. Ag (99.999%) and Au (99.999%) were purchased from ZhongNuo Advanced Material Technology.

Perovskite precursor solution. The 2.1 M NBG perovskite (FA0.8Cs0.2Pb0.5Sn0.5I3) precursor solutions were prepared by dissolving 289.0 mg FAI, 109.2 mg CsI, 391.1 mg SnI2, 484.0 mg PbI2, 16.0 mg of SnF2 and 5.2 mg Pb(SCN)2 in a mixed solvent of DMF/DMSO/pyridine with a volume ratio of 85:5:10. 1.6 M WBG perovskite (FA0.7Cs0.3PbI1.8Br1.2) precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 192.6 mg FAI, 124.8 mg CsI, 295.0 mg PbI2 and 352.3 mg PbBr2 in a mixed solvent of DMF and DMSO with a volume ratio of 6:4. All perovskite precursor solution was stirred at room temperature for 3 hours and filtered through a 0.22 μm polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membrane before use.

Fabrication of NBG PSCs and mini-modules

Patterned ITO substrates were cleaned sequentially by using ultrasonication with deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol for 15 min, respectively. PEDOT:PSS solution was spin-coated onto clean ITO substrates at 5000 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 150 °C for 15 min in air. For the blade-coating of NBG perovskite film, the gap between the blade-coater and substrates, the coating speed, and the pressure of N2 knife are set as 60 μm, 1 mm s−1, and 60 psi, respectively. The correlation between film thickness and both the precursor concentration and the blade-substrate gap is systematically summarized in Supplementary Fig 18. The blade-coated NBG perovskite films were vacuum annealed at 100 °C with pressure of 10–100 Pa for 10 min. Then, 20 nm C60, 5 nm BCP, and 120 nm Ag were subsequently deposited on the resulted substrates by thermal evaporation. For the fabrication of NBG perovskite photovoltaic mini-modules, the ITO electrode, PEDOT: PSS/NBG perovskite/C60/BCP and Ag electrode were respectively ablated by a laser scriber (GT-LS150-G) to form P1, P2 and P3 patterns. The widths of the active area, P1, P2, and P3 are 6.7 mm, 30 μm, 120 μm, and 100 μm, respectively. For the P2 and P3 laser scribing processes, environmental humidity must be maintained below 30% RH to prevent moisture-induced degradation of the perovskite layer.

Fabrication of all-perovskite tandem mini-modules

NiOx nanocrystal (10 mg mL−1 in deionized water) was spin-coated onto ITO substrates at 4000 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 150 °C for 15 min in air. Then, the WBG perovskite precursor was blade-coated on the NiOx-covered substrates and annealed at 105 °C for 10 minutes in a N2 glove box. The gap between the blade-coater and substrates, the coating speed, and the pressure of N2 knife are set as 30 μm, 3 mm s−1, and 60 psi, respectively. After thermal evaporating a 20-nm-thick C60 layer, the substrates were transferred to the ALD system (NCE-200R) to deposit 15 nm compact SnO2 film at temperatures of 95 °C using tetrakis(dimethylamino) tin(iv) and deionized water as precursors. After that, 1 nm Au clusters were deposited on the SnO2 layer, and then PEDOT:PSS solution (Diluted with IPA with a volume ratio of 1:2) was blade-coated onto the above layers at a speed of 5 mm s−1 with a substrate temperature of 45 °C, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 10 min in air. Eventually, a complete all-perovskite tandem mini-module is acquired by repeating the fabrication process of NBG perovskite mini-modules.

Device characterizations

The J–V curves of all devices were tested under the illumination of AM 1.5 G (100 mW cm−2) by a Keithley 2400 source meter together with a 3 A solar simulator (CME-Sol 8040-3 A, Microenerg Beijing Technology Co., Ltd). The areas of the small cells and mini-modules were determined by a shade mask with an aperture area of 6.84 mm2 and 10.4 cm2, respectively. The light intensity of solar simulator was calibrated with a 2 × 2 cm2 reference mono silicon cell (Oriel PN 91150 V, Newport, USA., calibrated by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory). The EQE measurement was conducted on a TRACQ-BASIC system applying a lock-in amplifier with a monochromator and 500 W Xenon lamp, which is checked with a calibrated silicon detector (PRL-12, Newport, USA) with a known photo-response. Bias illumination from highly bright LEDs with emission peaks of 850 and 460 nm was used for saturating the other junction to measure the WBG and NBG perovskite sub-cells, respectively. The photostability measurements were implemented under home-built LabVIEW-based LED illumination with an intensity equivalent to 1 sun in ambient conditions with humidity of 30–40%. The spectrum of the LED light source, ranging from 350 to 1100 nm, is presented in the Supplementary Fig. 19. The spectral mismatch factor (SMF) of the LED lamp is calculated to be 1.03 from 300 to 1020 nm, demonstrating excellent spectral matching. The measured mini-modules were encapsulated by a cover glass with ultraviolet epoxy.

DFT calculation

The DFT calculation for all molecules was performed using Gaussian 16 (Revision C.01. Wallingford CT (2016)). All the molecules were optimized with the PBE0 functional combined with the basis set of def2-SVP. And the single point energy was calculated with the basis set of def2-TZVP. The dispersion corrections were carried out through the DFT-D3BJ method. The solvation effect was concerned by a solvation model based on density (SMD)method, and the solvent was defined as DMF. The binding energy is defined as the difference between the electron energy of the complex and the sum of the electron energies of the individual molecules in the free state, ignoring the thermodynamic correction.

Other characterizations

SEM images were collected via a RISE-MAGNA microscope with an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. X-ray diffraction patterns were obtained using a D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer using monochromatized Cu-Kα radiation as the X-ray source with a scan rate of 6° min−1. The 13C-NMR spectra were recorded on d6-DMSO using a Bruker 500 MHz instrument. UV-vis absorption spectra were carried out with a Shimadzu spectrometer model UV-1800 with films on the quartz plates under N2 atmosphere. The FTIR spectra were conducted by a Spectrum 100 spectrophotometer in reflective mode. The Steady-state PL was characterized by an FLS1000 spectrophotometer with an excitation wavelength of 688 nm. TRPL was measured using a Horiba Fluorolog-3 time-correlated single photon counting system. The samples were excited using a pulsed laser with a wavelength of 688 nm from the glass side. The PL decay curves were fitted with biexponential components to obtain a fast and a slow decay lifetime. The surface morphology, contact potential, and dark current of the perovskite films were recorded on an atomic force microscope (FastScan Bio). The plane-view samples were scanned in at least three random locations to ensure reliable measurements. The optical transmission photos are characterized by optical microscopy (Leica Microsystems CMS Co., Ltd., LEICA DM2700M).
