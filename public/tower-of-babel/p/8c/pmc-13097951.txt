Analgesic efficacy of liposomal bupivacaine adductor canal block combined with periarticular infiltration after total knee arthroplasty: a prospective, double-blind, randomized controlled trial
Authors: Zhiping Yin, Jun Chen, Tianchi Zhao, Yongyan Chen, Benfu Wang, Jianfeng Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative pain after total knee arthroplasty remains substantial despite multimodal analgesia (MMA); however, whether adding liposomal bupivacaine provides clinically meaningful benefits over conventional long-acting local anesthetics within contemporary regional anesthesia–based pathways remains unclear.MethodsIn this single-center, double-blind, randomized controlled trial, 72 patients undergoing unilateral primary TKA were randomized into three groups: levobupivacaine alone (ZB), liposomal bupivacaine alone (LB), and a combination of liposomal bupivacaine and levobupivacaine (LZB). All patients received a standardized MMA protocol including an adductor canal block (ACB) and a periarticular injection (PAI). Primary outcomes were postoperative pain intensity at rest and during activity, measured by the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS). Secondary outcomes included knee range of motion (ROM), patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCA) utilization, sensory disturbances within the saphenous nerve distribution, postoperative complication rates, length of hospital stay, and patient satisfaction.ResultsAmong the 62 patients who completed the study, resting pain scores showed no significant differences between groups. However, activity-related pain scores were significantly lower in the LB and LZB groups than in the ZB group between 36 and 72 h postoperatively (p < 0.05). Additionally, the time to first PCA request was significantly longer in the LB and LZB groups than in the ZB group. The LB group exhibited a higher incidence of sensory disturbances in the saphenous nerve distribution than the ZB group at 48 and 72 h postoperatively (p < 0.05). No significant differences emerged regarding ROM, opioid consumption, complication rates, length of hospital stay, or patient satisfaction.ConclusionsIn patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty, incorporating liposomal bupivacaine into an analgesic regimen involving adductor canal block and periarticular infiltration provides prolonged analgesia and superior control of activity-related pain. This may facilitate early postoperative mobilization, thereby promoting early recovery. These findings support the potential of this approach in accelerating the recovery process.Trial registrationThis study was prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400084619) on May 21, 2024.Keywords: Total knee arthroplasty, Liposomal bupivacaine, Multimodal analgesia, Adductor canal block, Periarticular injection, Postoperative pain

Background

TKA is one of the most effective surgical interventions for end-stage knee osteoarthritis, significantly improving function and quality of life [1]. Despite its overall success, more than 50% of patients experience moderate to severe acute pain during the first 72 h postoperatively, with pain typically peaking on postoperative day 2, which coincides with the highest opioid consumption [2–4]. Uncontrolled postoperative pain not only impairs patient comfort but also delays functional recovery and negatively affects health-related quality of life [5, 6]. Notably, a one-point increase in resting pain scores on postoperative day 3 is associated with a 52% higher risk of developing chronic postsurgical pain at ≥ 3 months [7]. These findings underscore the importance of effective early postoperative pain control to optimize recovery outcomes.

Multimodal analgesia, which integrates systemic analgesics and regional anesthesia techniques, is the current standard approach for postoperative pain management. It effectively reduces opioid use and enhances functional recovery [6]. Among these techniques, the combination of ACB and PAI is recommended as first-line analgesia for TKA by Procedure-Specific Postoperative Pain Management (PROSPECT) guidelines, owing to its analgesic efficacy and preservation of quadriceps function [8, 9].

Liposomal bupivacaine, a sustained-release local anaesthetic providing analgesia up to 72 h, has shown potential for reducing postoperative pain and opioid use [10]. However, evidence regarding its superiority over conventional long-acting local anaesthetics, such as bupivacaine or ropivacaine, remains inconclusive [11, 12].

This single-center, double-blind, randomized controlled trial aimed to evaluate the efficacy and feasibility of liposomal bupivacaine within an ACB and PAI protocol following TKA. Specifically, it compared levobupivacaine, liposomal bupivacaine, and their combination in terms of analgesic effectiveness, functional recovery, and patient satisfaction. The goal was to provide evidence-based guidance for optimizing MMA strategies in TKA to enhance recovery and minimize complications.

Methods

This single-center, double-blind, randomized controlled trial was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Approval No. 2024-K-006-02) and was prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400084619). The study protocol was developed in accordance with the SPIRIT 2013 guidelines, which were current at the time of trial initiation [13], and reporting adhered to the CONSORT 2025 statement for randomized trials [14]. The study was conducted from June 2024 to January 2025 at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University, Wenzhou, China.

Participants

Eligible participants were adults aged 60–80 years with American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–III who were scheduled for elective unilateral primary TKA. For participants with diabetes mellitus, only those with stable and acceptable glycemic control on preoperative assessment were enrolled. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, who were required to be able to understand and use the VAS for pain assessment.

Exclusion criteria included allergy to morphine or local anesthetics; chronic analgesic use (> 2 weeks); history of gastrointestinal ulcers or peripheral neuropathy; body mass index (BMI) > 40 kg/m2; platelet count < 80 × 109/L; coagulation abnormalities; infection at the planned block puncture site or ipsilateral lower-limb skin or soft tissue infection; evidence of systemic infection (e.g., fever, bacteremia, or sepsis); psychiatric illness; history of lower limb thrombosis; poor compliance; or participation in concurrent clinical trials. Patients with suspected or confirmed infection of the operative knee were not candidates for elective TKA at our institution and were therefore not enrolled.

Study protocol

Patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio to three study groups using a computer-generated randomization sequence and opaque, sequentially numbered envelopes. Group allocations were as follows:

ZB Group: ACB with 0.375% levobupivacaine 20 mL (75 mg) plus PAI with 150 mg levobupivacaine diluted to 60 mL;

ZB Group: ACB with 0.375% levobupivacaine 20 mL (75 mg) plus PAI with 150 mg levobupivacaine diluted to 60 mL;

LB Group: ACB with liposomal bupivacaine 20 mL (133 mg) plus PAI with 266 mg liposomal bupivacaine diluted to 60 mL;

LB Group: ACB with liposomal bupivacaine 20 mL (133 mg) plus PAI with 266 mg liposomal bupivacaine diluted to 60 mL;

LZB Group: ACB with 0.375% levobupivacaine 20 mL (75 mg) plus PAI with 266 mg liposomal bupivacaine diluted to 60 mL.

LZB Group: ACB with 0.375% levobupivacaine 20 mL (75 mg) plus PAI with 266 mg liposomal bupivacaine diluted to 60 mL.

Only the anaesthesiologist performing the nerve block was unblinded to group allocation; patients, data collectors, and outcome assessors were blinded to group assignment.

Anaesthetic protocol

All patients received ultrasound-guided ACB performed by the same experienced regional anaesthesiologist before induction of anaesthesia, with intraoperative confirmation of block efficacy via temperature sensation testing. PAI was performed intraoperatively before wound closure via a medial parapatellar approach by injecting the periarticular soft tissues using a 60 mL solution containing the study drug, epinephrine (final concentration 1:200,000), and dexamethasone (5 mg). The PAI additives were chosen per our institutional multimodal analgesia pathway and kept identical across groups to minimize confounding, as no universally accepted “standard” PAI cocktail exists [15]. All patients included in the analysis of this study underwent surgery performed by the same primary surgeon.

General anaesthesia was induced with intravenous propofol (1.5–2.5 mg/kg) or etomidate (0.2–0.3 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.3–0.5 μg/kg), and cisatracurium (0.15–0.2 mg/kg). Anaesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane and remifentanil, titrated based on bispectral index (BIS) and hemodynamic parameters. Additional cisatracurium was administered as needed.

Postoperative analgesia

All patients received intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) consisting of hydromorphone (8 mg) combined with dexamethasone (5 mg) for 48 h postoperatively. Rescue analgesia was provided using patient-initiated boluses of hydromorphone (0.16 mg) via the PCA device, with a lockout interval of 15 min.

Primary and secondary endpoints

Data collection was conducted by a single anesthesiologist who had received standardized training and was blinded to group allocation. Assessments began 12 h postoperatively and were conducted at six prespecified time intervals: 12–24 h, 24–36 h, 36–48 h, 48–60 h, 60–72 h, and beyond 72 h postoperatively.

To standardize activity-related pain assessment, predefined tasks were performed under assessor supervision: active ankle pumps within 36 h postoperatively and, thereafter, a supine unassisted active heel-slide to 60° knee flexion without external passive force [16]. Knee flexion angle for the heel-slide task and knee range of motion (ROM) were measured using smartphone images analyzed with the Angle Meter application (end position held ≥ 5 s; camera aligned parallel to the knee joint center) [17, 18].

The primary outcome was postoperative pain intensity at rest and during activity using a 100-mm VAS (0 = no pain, 100 = worst pain) [19]. Activity-related pain was evaluated using ankle pump exercises within 36 h and knee flexion to 60° thereafter.

Secondary outcome measures included:

Knee range of motion (ROM): measured at 48–60 h and 60–72 h postoperatively during unassisted active flexion and extension in the supine position using smartphone images analyzed with the Angle Meter application.

Knee range of motion (ROM): measured at 48–60 h and 60–72 h postoperatively during unassisted active flexion and extension in the supine position using smartphone images analyzed with the Angle Meter application.

PCA usage: Time to first demand, total and effective button presses within 48 h.

PCA usage: Time to first demand, total and effective button presses within 48 h.

Sensory disturbances: Evaluated via temperature sensation in the saphenous nerve distribution at 24, 48, and 72 h postoperatively [20, 21].

Sensory disturbances: Evaluated via temperature sensation in the saphenous nerve distribution at 24, 48, and 72 h postoperatively [20, 21].

Postoperative complications: Incidence of nausea, vomiting, wound bleeding, injection-site infection, nerve injury, and falls.

Postoperative complications: Incidence of nausea, vomiting, wound bleeding, injection-site infection, nerve injury, and falls.

Length of hospital stay and patient satisfaction: Length of hospital stay was recorded. Patient satisfaction with postoperative analgesia was assessed at 72 h postoperatively using a 5-point Likert scale by a blinded assessor [22].

Length of hospital stay and patient satisfaction: Length of hospital stay was recorded. Patient satisfaction with postoperative analgesia was assessed at 72 h postoperatively using a 5-point Likert scale by a blinded assessor [22].

Sample size

The sample size calculation was performed using PASS 15.0 software for a repeated-measures design with one between-group factor (group: ZB, LB, and LZB) and one within-group factor (time: 24 h, 36 h, 48 h, 60 h, and 72 h). The planning parameters were informed by an internal pilot dataset (n = 14; not included in the final trial analysis). In the pilot data, the group means were 32.25 (ZB), 25.80 (LB), and 24.73 (LZB), with a standard deviation of 6.46. Based on these pilot estimates, the standardized effect size (Cohen’s f) was 0.514 for the main effect of group and 0.483 for the group × time interaction; these effect sizes were used as assumptions for sample size planning, recognizing that effect size estimates from small pilot samples may be imprecise. With a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05 and a target power of at least 80%, the required sample size for a repeated-measures ANOVA (with Greenhouse–Geisser correction specified for potential violations of sphericity) was 19 participants per group (total N = 57). To account for potential attrition (e.g., surgery cancellation or ineligibility), a 20% dropout rate was anticipated, and therefore 72 participants (24 per group) were planned for recruitment.

Data analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 27.0; IBM Corp.) and R (version 4.5.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. Sphericity was evaluated with Mauchly’s test, and the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied when violated. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR), and categorical variables as counts (%).

For repeated-measures outcomes (VAS at rest and during activity), the primary analysis used a two-way mixed-design repeated-measures ANOVA (group × time) with Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons where appropriate; when sphericity was violated, Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. As a sensitivity analysis, we additionally fitted linear mixed-effects models with fixed effects of group, time, and group × time and subject-level random intercepts and random slopes for time (1 + time | subject), estimated by REML with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom; estimated marginal means and between-group differences at each time point were reported with Holm-adjusted p values.

For non-normally distributed repeated-measures data, the Friedman test with Dunn’s post hoc test was used. Knee ROM was compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Sensory disturbance data were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. Time to first PCA press was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank tests, and Cox regression was used to explore independent predictors. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 177 patients were screened for eligibility. Of these, 86 were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria or for meeting the exclusion criteria, 16 declined to participate due to personal reasons, and 3 were excluded for other reasons. Ultimately, 72 patients were enrolled, and 10 were subsequently withdrawn after randomization due to intraoperative block failure or postoperative complications, such as deep vein thrombosis, joint swelling, or other adverse events. A total of 62 patients completed the trial (Fig. 1).

Secondary outcomes

There were no significant between-group differences in knee range of motion (ROM) at 48–60 h (p = 0.44) or 60–72 h (p = 0.87) postoperatively. However, all groups exhibited significant within-group improvements in ROM between 48–60 h and 60–72 h (Fig. 4). Evaluation of sensory disturbances in the saphenous nerve distribution revealed a significantly higher incidence of numbness in the LB group than in the ZB group at both 48 and 72 h postoperatively (adjusted p < 0.05) (Fig. 5).

Incidence of sensory disturbances in the saphenous nerve distribution across three groups postoperatively. Asterisks (*): Indicate statistically significant differences in the incidence of sensory disturbances at 48 and 72 h postoperatively between the ZB and LB groups (P < 0.05)

Kaplan–Meier survival curve analysis of time to first press of the patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCA) pump after surgery demonstrated significant differences among the three groups (log-rank test, p = 0.013). The median time to first PCA demand was significantly shorter in the ZB group (12.0 h; 95% CI: 3.0–46.0 h) compared with both the LB and LZB groups, which each had a median time of 48.0 h (Fig. 6). Cox proportional hazards regression analysis showed that the LZB group had a significantly lower hazard ratio (HR = 0.50; 95% CI: 0.27–0.93; p = 0.0281) for time to first PCA activation compared to the ZB group. Although the LB group also showed a reduced hazard ratio, the difference did not reach statistical significance (HR = 0.57; 95% CI: 0.30–1.06; p = 0.0739). Moreover, no statistically significant differences were found among the three groups in either actual or effective postoperative PCA button-press counts (p > 0.05).

Kaplan–Meier survival curves illustrating the time to first activation of PCA across the three groups. Asterisks (*): Indicate significantly earlier first patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCA) activation in the ZB group compared with the LZB group (P < 0.05)

The incidence of postoperative complications was 14.3% in the ZB group, 20.0% in the LB group, and 19.0% in the LZB group, with no statistically significant differences among the groups (p > 0.05). Additionally, there were no significant differences in hospital length of stay, patient satisfaction scores, or postoperative supplemental opioid consumption (oral morphine equivalents) among the three groups (p > 0.05).

Discussion

In this study, resting pain scores did not differ significantly among the three groups at any postoperative time point, consistent with findings reported by Aurora et al. [23–25]. Pain intensity remained within the range of “no pain” to “mild pain,” supporting the efficacy of MMA in managing postoperative pain. Although resting pain scores approached statistical significance over time (p = 0.05), post hoc analyses did not reveal meaningful between-group differences. However, significant differences emerged in the time to first patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCA) activation, with the ZB group requiring analgesia significantly earlier than the LB and LZB groups. This finding supports the prolonged analgesic duration provided by liposomal bupivacaine.

For activity-related pain, the ZB group exhibited significantly higher VAS scores between 48 and 72 h postoperatively compared to both the LB and LZB groups, further supporting the extended analgesic effect of liposomal bupivacaine. In our cohort, the magnitude of between-group differences in activity-related pain scores appeared to narrow toward the ≥ 72 h postoperative interval. Because early postoperative knee ROM is multifactorial, this temporal pattern may have contributed to why we did not observe significant between-group differences in knee range of motion (ROM) at 48–60 h or 60–72 h despite within-group improvements over time. The discrepancy with the meta-analysis by Yu et al., which reported superior ROM outcomes with liposomal bupivacaine, may be related to differences in study design, analgesic protocols, dosing and administration techniques, ROM assessment methods and timing, and rehabilitation pathways [26]. Further analysis showed that although liposomal bupivacaine produced statistically significant analgesic effects, its overall clinical advantage was relatively modest, as indicated by the area under the curve (AUC) of pain scores [27]. This may reflect the multifactorial nature of postoperative pain, which includes mechanical, inflammatory, and neuropathic components [28]. Thus, a single-agent local anaesthetic may not sufficiently address the full spectrum of postoperative pain. Postoperative functional recovery is also influenced by additional factors such as muscle strength, neuromuscular coordination, surgical technique, psychological state, and individual pain tolerance [29–31]. Consequently, analgesic efficacy alone may inadequately reflect the overall clinical benefit of LB during early postoperative recovery.

Interestingly, our findings showed that liposomal bupivacaine effectively alleviated activity-related pain for up to 72 h, in contrast to previous literature that generally reported durations of less than 48 h [25]. This discrepancy likely arises from the higher dose of liposomal bupivacaine (266 mg/60 ml) used periarticularly in our study. Bramlett et al. similarly demonstrated that higher doses of liposomal bupivacaine significantly enhanced analgesic outcomes following TKA [32]. However, the LB group received a total dose of 399 mg (133 mg via adductor canal block and 266 mg via periarticular infiltration), exceeding the maximum recommended dose according to the product labelling. A study by Springer et al. reported that, in patients undergoing bilateral TKA, the use of a total dose of 532 mg of liposomal bupivacaine did not result in dose-related cardiovascular or central nervous system toxicities [33]. It is noteworthy that the postoperative telephone follow-up in this study revealed no persistent neurotoxic symptoms, further supporting the safety of moderately exceeding the recommended dose of liposomal bupivacaine in clinical practice. These findings suggest that, in clinical settings, the dose of liposomal bupivacaine can be appropriately adjusted to optimize pain management while ensuring safety.

Traditional postoperative analgesic protocols have primarily relied on opioids, but concerns regarding opioid-related adverse effects (e.g., respiratory depression, nausea, vomiting, urinary retention, and dependence) have significantly limited their routine use [34]. Prior studies have demonstrated that liposomal bupivacaine can reduce postoperative opioid consumption [23, 35]. However, our study did not find significant differences among groups in opioid requirements (oral morphine equivalents). This result may be attributable to the effectiveness of our MMA protocol, which reduces overall opioid demand. Additionally, only intravenous PCA opioid consumption was documented; analgesics such as flurbiprofen axetil, ketorolac, or dezocine, given at patient request, were not captured, so total analgesic use may have been underestimated.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study with a moderate sample size, which may limit external generalizability. The sample size was planned based on effect-size assumptions derived from an internal pilot dataset; however, effect-size estimates from small pilot samples can be imprecise and potentially optimistic, and therefore the study may have been underpowered to detect smaller effects, particularly for interaction terms. Second, the follow-up period was limited to 72 h and did not capture the theoretical maximum duration of action of liposomal bupivacaine (up to 96 h). Third, postoperative activity intensity and mechanical load were not objectively quantified. Activity-related pain was assessed using standardized, low-load tasks (ankle pumps and a supine unassisted active heel-slide to 60° knee flexion); thus, our findings may not be generalizable to weight-bearing ambulation or higher-intensity activities, and no mechanistic inference regarding “high mechanical stress” conditions can be made. Fourth, pharmacokinetic monitoring was not performed, and long-term functional outcomes after knee arthroplasty were not assessed. Finally, although we performed a supplementary linear mixed-effects model analysis and obtained results consistent with the repeated-measures ANOVA, residual confounding related to perioperative care and rehabilitation pathways cannot be fully excluded. Future studies should include larger multicenter randomized controlled trials with longer follow-up and objective quantification of postoperative activity to further evaluate the durability of analgesia, safety, and longer-term functional recovery.

Conclusions

A multimodal analgesic regimen incorporating adductor canal blockade and periarticular infiltration is an effective approache for postoperative pain control following total knee arthroplasty. The prolonged analgesic effect of liposomal bupivacaine was associated with lower activity-related pain during joint mobilization, which may facilitate early functional recovery. that this approach may support early postoperative recovery.
