Nursing informatics competence and its associated factors among nurses in Tanzania: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mariam A. Kidayi, Elihuruma Eliufoo Stephano, Ziada Juma Sellah, Mtoro J. Mtoro, Yang Min
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNursing informatics competence has emerged as a critical factor in ensuring high-quality patient care. Developing nations lag in equipping nurses with the informatics competencies to navigate the technologically driven healthcare landscape. Insufficient infrastructure, limited access to technology, and inadequate training programs hinder the development of this competence. This study assesses the nursing informatics competence and its associated factors among nurses in Tanzania.MethodsA facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted in Regional Referral Hospitals, and 428 participants were selected through a simple random sampling technique. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire that included the Self-Assessment of Nursing Informatics Competencies Scale (SANICS) to assess competence in computer literacy, informatics literacy, and informatics management skills. Multiple linear regression analysis was employed to determine the relationships between individual and institutional factors that affect informatics competence, using Stata version 18. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsThe mean score of nursing informatics competencies was 79.58 ± 31.44, while other scores were computer literacy (26.83 ± 9.99), informatics literacy (34.35 ± 13.89), and informatics management (18.39 ± 8.17). The Spearman rank-order correlation revealed significant associations with individual factors, including Information computer technology (ICT) knowledge score (rs=0.7397), ICT attitude score (rs=0.7735), ICT practices score (rs=0.7409), and self-efficacy score (rs=0.7456). On the institutional side, the organization of training (rs=0.7085), management support (rs=0.6877), availability of policies on informatics use (rs=0.6918), ICT infrastructure (rs=0.7181), and human resources (rs=0.6950), a moderate association was observed for workload score (rs=0.4735). Regression analysis identifies several factors, including individual factors such as ICT knowledge score (coefficient = 0.6026, p = 0.023), ICT attitude score (coefficient = 0.7929, p = 0.018), and self-efficacy score (coefficient = 2.2891, p < 0.001). For institutional factors, the training organization (coefficient = 1.2157, p = 0.005) and policies on informatics use (coefficient = 0.8847, p = 0.001) also significantly influence nursing informatics competence.ConclusionThis study highlights a critical understanding of nursing informatics competence and its associated factors. The findings underscore the pressing need for targeted educational reforms, notably in ICT, to elevate nurses’ informatics competencies and align them with global health standards.

Background

Nursing informatics (NI) competence has emerged as a critical factor in ensuring high-quality patient care and the effective use of health information systems in the rapidly evolving healthcare landscape [1]. Nursing informatics has become increasingly significant globally as health systems adopt digital technologies to enhance patient care and outcomes [2]. This field integrates nursing science with information management and analytical sciences to develop, implement, and evaluate information and communication technology (ICT) solutions that enhance patient care delivery [3]. However, the level of informatics competence among nursing professionals varies widely across different countries and regions, which can significantly impact the effectiveness of healthcare delivery [3, 4]. In developed nations, nursing informatics competency is often integrated into nursing education, and continuous training supports professionals in utilizing health information technology systems proficiently [5]. In contrast, many developing nations, particularly those in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), face challenges that hinder the effective integration of informatics into nursing practice [6].

Nursing informatics has become an essential component of modern nursing practice worldwide, with several key initiatives and frameworks established to guide the development of nursing competencies. The Technology Informatics Guiding Education Reform (TIGER) initiative provides a comprehensive international framework that outlines core informatics competencies tailored to various nursing roles, emphasizing the integration of informatics skills into clinical practice and management [7]. Complementing this, the Canadian Association of Schools of Nursing (CASN) has developed Entry-to-Practice Competencies that focus on the fundamental informatics skills registered nurses require upon graduation, including information and knowledge management, professional accountability, and the use of information and communication technologies [8]. Additionally, the Australian Digital Health Agency (ADHA) has introduced the National Nursing and Midwifery Digital Health Capability Framework, which outlines five domains of digital capability, ranging from digital professionalism to technology use, intended to support nurses in advancing their digital health skills throughout their careers [9, 10]. Within the African context, specifically in Tanzania, understanding and enhancing nursing informatics competence is critical, as these global frameworks provide valuable benchmarks and guidance for developing tailored informatics education and practice standards that respond to local healthcare needs and technological capacities [11].

Despite progressing toward digital healthcare transformation, SSA lags in equipping nurses with the informatics competencies necessary to navigate the technologically driven healthcare landscape [6, 12]. Insufficient infrastructure, limited access to technology, and inadequate training programs hinder the development of critical informatics skills among healthcare professionals [6, 13]. As frontline healthcare providers, nurses in SSA have a direct impact on patient outcomes [14]. Thus, their ability to utilize digital technologies for data management and clinical decision-making is vital [6, 15]. Unfortunately, a significant knowledge gap exists in understanding the specific factors affecting nursing informatics competence in the SSA region.

Tanzania exemplifies the challenges many rural and urban health facilities face regarding informatics competence [16, 17]. Although efforts have been made to promote digital health initiatives, implementing electronic health record (EHR) systems remains sporadic, and many healthcare facilities still rely on traditional paper-based methods [18]. The Government of Tanzania’s Health Sector Strategic Plan (HSSP V) 2021–2026 stated the need for primary healthcare facilities to improve the utilization of nursing informatics [19]. One of the programs introduced was the “Afya-Tek Program,” which helped integrate with the government’s Unified Community System (UCS) [20] and other electronic logistics management systems [21]. Several electronic medical records (EMRs) are also used in Tanzania, including the government of Tanzania hospital management information system (GoTHOMIS), Jeeva, Afya Care, and the CTC2 database [22, 23]. More than 1,500 healthcare facilities currently utilise the GoTHOMIS [23]. Previous research indicates that Tanzanian nurses generally report low confidence and competence in using ICT tools, particularly EHRs, EMRs, and other digital health technologies [23]. Barriers such as unreliable internet connectivity, insufficient training, and a lack of uniformity in the nursing curriculum contribute to this competence gap [22–24].

​Given this context, the necessity for a focused study on nursing informatics competence in Tanzania becomes apparent.​ This competency variation can be attributed to several underlying factors, including educational resources, infrastructure, ongoing training opportunities, and individual attitudes toward technology [22–24]. The current body of research inadequately addresses the educational and infrastructural challenges nurses face in utilising digital tools effectively. Understanding the informatics competencies of nurses in Tanzania and identifying the factors that influence these competencies will provide crucial insights into designing targeted educational programs and policies that enhance nursing practice. This study assessed nursing informatics competence and its associated factors among nurses in Tanzania. This survey aims to fill the existing research gap and lay the groundwork for developing tailored training and resource allocation strategies that align with the unique needs of healthcare professionals in Tanzania. This study will help to advance nursing informatics initiatives and foster improved healthcare delivery in the context of rapidly advancing digital healthcare environments.

Methods

Study design and setting

A facility-based cross-sectional study using a quantitative approach was conducted to assess the knowledge and attitudes toward nursing informatics and its associated factors among nurses working in regional referral hospitals (RRH) on the Tanzanian mainland. The study was conducted in Tanzania, located in East Africa, with a total land area of 947,303 km² (365,756 sq. mi), and a population of 61,741,120 [25]. The mainland is divided into 28 administrative regions, organized into four zones, with each zone having one Regional Road Headquarters (RRH). This study was conducted in four regions of mainland Tanzania, specifically in the central, southern, and southwestern highlands.

Study participants

The study population consisted of all nurses providing nursing care in the selected RRH on the Tanzanian mainland. The Inclusion criteria focused on active, licensed nurses with at least one year of working experience, specifically those working in healthcare facilities, to ensure that the findings accurately reflect the practices of those currently working in the field and are relevant to the existing healthcare environment. We excluded participants who were not presently employed in formal healthcare settings, such as volunteers or auxiliary staff, as their experiences may not comprehensively represent the professional competencies required in nursing informatics practices.

Sample size and sampling procedures

The sample size for this study was calculated using a single proportion formula, considering the following assumptions: the proportion of favourable knowledge and attitudes toward nursing informatics was assumed to be 50%, based on a recent study conducted in Ethiopia [26]. With a 95% confidence interval and a 5% margin of error, an additional 10% was added to account for the non-response rate, increasing the study’s power. Therefore, the total sample size was calculated to be 384, plus an adjustment for the 10% non-response rate, resulting in a final sample size of 428.

Our sampling involved a multistage sampling technique. The selection of geographical zones for this study was conducted using purposive sampling, targeting the major zones of Tanzania’s mainland: Western, Northern, Central, Southern, Southwest Highlands, Lake, and Eastern [27]. Then, seven RRHs were purposively selected from the 26 regions of mainland Tanzania, which represent the main zones of the country, based on their exemplary provision of nursing informatics services. However, we included four hospitals due to unforeseen challenges, including timely ethical approval from the targeted regions and budget constraints. The four hospitals were selected as they had established informatics systems and were relevant to our study objectives.

This study was conducted in four public RRHs located in different areas of Tanzania, such as Dodoma, Katavi, Rukwa, and Mtwara. We conducted a facility-based study at four RRHs, namely Dodoma RRH, Sumbawanga RRH (Katavi), Rukwa RRH, and Mtwara RRH. To ensure representation, we used a proportional sampling method based on the number of eligible participants in each hospital. The number of eligible participants was 290 in Dodoma RRH, 255 in Rukwa RRH, 250 in Sumbawanga RRH, and 275 in Mtwara RRH, making a total of 1,070 potential participants. We then distributed the calculated sample size proportionally across the four RRHs, resulting in 116 participants for Dodoma, 102 for Sumbawanga RRH, 101 for Rukwa RRH, and 109 for Mtwara RRH. Following the selection of hospitals, simple random sampling was used to select study participants, ensuring that every nurse had an equal chance of being included in the study.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected in June and November 2024, following receipt of ethical clearance. Trained research assistants collected data. During the data collection period, the research assistants communicated with the department heads of each selected hospital and health centre unit, presenting the permission letter to obtain approval for the commencement of the study in their units. The structured questionnaire was then electronically distributed to nurses after obtaining informed consent. Respondents were asked to complete the questionnaire in their free time, without interfering with their regular activities, and submit it electronically within five days. The completed electronic questionnaires were checked for completeness and consistency of responses. Data were electronically collected using the Open Data Kit (ODK) Collect app, version 2023.2 [28].

Measurement tools

This study adopted the SANICS scale tool to assess levels of competence and other associated factors, which have been previously published elsewhere [29]. This scale is in the form of a Likert scale, which is the most widely used psychometric scale in survey research. The questionnaire is divided into four parts:

PART ONE: Demographic characteristics, which include sex (male or female); age; marital status (single or married); professional title (registered nurse or enrolled nurse); education level (certificate, diploma, or degree); working experience (less than 10 years, more than 10 years); and department of employment (Intensive Care Unit, Maternity Ward, Medical Ward, Mental Health Ward, Surgical Ward, and Other). These demographic characteristics enable the researcher to understand the study population’s socio-demographic, workplace, and workload factors.

PART TWO: This measures the competence of nursing informatics among the participants. The SANICS scale assesses competence in nursing informatics using 30 items.

PART THREE: This measures individual factors, including knowledge, attitude, usage/practices and self-efficacy, using 29 items on a Likert scale.

PART FOUR: This measures institutional factors, including training, management support, availability of ICT policies, ICT infrastructure, workload, and human resources. It consists of 27 items, all of which use a Likert scale.

Dependent variable (Nursing informatics competency)

This study was guided by the operational definition of nursing informatics as previously defined in existing studies. Nursing informatics is defined as a specialized field that integrates nursing science with computer science and information science to manage and communicate data, information, knowledge, and wisdom in nursing practice, ultimately enhancing patient care and outcomes [11]. The scale consists of 30 items and covers three domains: computer literacy (10 items), informatics literacy (13 items), and informatics management skills literacy (7 items). Computer literacy refers to an individual’s familiarity with nursing informatics concepts, principles, and applications. It includes performing basic tasks, using computer resources, managing software installations and updates, and troubleshooting common technical issues. Nursing informatics literacy encompasses the competencies and proficiencies necessary to effectively utilize nursing informatics tools, systems, and software applications within daily clinical workflows. Nursing informatics management skills encompass the knowledge and skills required to oversee, coordinate, and optimize nursing informatics in healthcare settings. All items are scored on a 5-point Likert scale from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”. Total scores on the scale range from 32 to 150, with higher scores indicating higher levels of NI competency. In our study, Cronbach’s Alpha was 0.954, indicating high internal consistency.

Independent variables

The independent variables in this study were grouped into two domains: individual factors (i.e., knowledge, attitude, usage/practices, and self-efficacy) and institutional factors (i.e., training, management support, availability of ICT policies, ICT infrastructure, workload, and human resources). All were measured using a four-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = agree, 4 = strongly agree). For individual factors, 29 items were used across the four domains: knowledge (10 items), attitude (8 items), usage/practices (7 items), and self-efficacy (4 items). For each domain, items were summed and the mean was calculated: knowledge (range = 10–20, mean = 19), attitude (range = 8–16, mean = 15), usage/practices (range = 7–14, mean = 12), and self-efficacy (range = 4–8, mean = 7). However, for institutional factors, 27 items were used across six domains: training (5 items), management support (5 items), availability of ICT policies (5 items), ICT infrastructure (5 items), workload (3 items), and human resources (4 items). For each domain, items were summed and the mean was calculated: training (range = 5–10, mean = 8.15), management support (range = 5–10, mean = 8.61), availability of ICT policies (range = 5–10, mean = 7.25), ICT infrastructure (range = 5–10, mean = 8.71), workload (range = 3–6, mean = 4.77), and human resources (range = 4–8, mean = 7). Raw scores were used during the analysis.

Data management and statistical analysis

Data management and analyses were performed using STATA version 18.5 (STATA Corp, College Station, TX). Categorical data were described by calculating frequencies and percentages, while continuous data were presented as the mean and standard deviation (SD). The normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, which revealed that the variables in this study followed a normal distribution. Consequently, we employed the two-sample t-test and One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), both parametric tests, to analyse influencing factors. The Correlation between NI competency and individual and institutional factors was analysed using Pearson’s two-sided test, a parametric measure of rank correlation (statistical dependence between the rankings of two variables). Multiple linear regression was performed to explore the factors associated with NI competency. The result was considered statistically significant when the two-tailed p-value was less than 0.05.

Results

Demographic characteristics of the nurses

This study surveyed 428 nurses, primarily located in Dodoma (27.1%), Katavi (23.8%), Rukwa (23.6%), and Mtwara (25.5%), with a median age of 31 years (Interquartile range (IQR), 27–35). Most respondents are relatively young, with 75.2% being 35 years old or younger. The gender distribution is nearly balanced, with 47.0% of the population being male and 53.0% female. Educational qualifications vary, with 11.2% holding a certificate, 69.9% having a diploma, and 18.9% possessing a degree or higher. The median work experience is 4 years (IQR 2–10), with 77.1% having 10 years or less. Regarding documentation practices, 43.7% of nurses document electronically, while 56.3% initially use paper before transitioning. On average, nurses spend 3 h daily using the Health Information System (HIS), with 62.2% utilizing it for 4 h or less. Experience levels with HIS vary, with 22.0% at low, 47.9% at moderate, and 30.1% at high proficiency levels. Furthermore, 87.9% are regular users of digital devices, reflecting a strong engagement with technology in their work environment (Table 1).

Nursing informatics competencies and distribution of individual and institutional factors assessment

The distribution of nursing informatics competencies among nurses shows significant variability, with an overall mean score of 79.58 and a standard deviation of 31.436. Computer literacy has a mean score of 26.83 (SD = 9.999), indicating moderate variation. Informatics literacy exhibits a higher mean score of 34.35 (SD = 13.897), suggesting greater variability in this area. Conversely, informatics management skills have a lower mean score of 18.39 (SD = 8.169), reflecting a more consistent competency level among nurses.

On the individual level, the mean score for ICT knowledge is 28.97 (SD = 8.007), indicating a moderate level of understanding, while attitudes towards ICT usage average 24.52 (SD = 5.100), reflecting generally positive perspectives, albeit with variability. Practical application and usage of ICT scored lower, with a mean of 19.57 (SD = 5.832) and a self-efficacy average of 11.49 (SD = 2.864), suggesting moderate confidence levels among the nurses. On the institutional side, the organization of training scored 13.92 (SD = 3.229), whereas management support averaged 14.38 (SD = 2.225). There is variability in the availability of policies on informatics use, which scored 12.08 (SD = 4.062), while ICT infrastructure availability is relatively strong, with a mean of 14.62 (SD = 2.602). However, workload pressures are significant, with a mean score of 7.94 (SD = 1.542), and the availability of human resources scored 11.35 (SD = 1.825), suggesting inconsistencies in their adequacy (Table 2).

Univariate analysis of demographic characteristics on nurse informatics competence

Overall, the univariate analysis of demographic characteristics on nurse informatics competence highlights significant variations based on specific factors. Nurses from Rukwa had the highest mean competence score of 111.28 (SD = 30.939), while those from Mtwara had the lowest at 53.07 (SD = 9.101), showing significant regional differences (F-value = 165.72, p < 0.0001). Documentation practices showed substantial differences, with nurses documenting electronically directly having a mean competence score of 95.25 (SD = 28.547) compared to those using paper-based methods first, who scored 67.41 (SD = 28.047) (T-value = 10.1053, p < 0.0001). Time spent using health information systems (HIS) also influenced competence, with those spending 4 h or less having a higher mean score of 85.33 (SD = 34.306) compared to those spending more than 4 h, who scored 70.13 (SD = 23.223) (T-value = 4.9858, p < 0.0001). Experience in using HIS was highly significant, with nurses having high experience scoring the highest at 114.21 (SD = 25.414), followed by those with moderate experience at 70.39 (SD = 20.757) and low experience at 52.10 (SD = 9.484) (F value = 288.63, p < 0.0001). Regular users of digital devices had a higher mean competence score of 81.66 (SD = 31.839) compared to occasional users, who scored 64.54 (SD = 23.602) (T-value = 3.7360, p = 0.0002), indicating the importance of frequent digital interaction in developing informatics competence (Table 3).

Correlational analysis of the association of individual and institutional factors on nursing informatics competencies

The Spearman rank-order correlation analysis demonstrates significant positive associations (p < 0.05) between individual and institutional factors and nursing informatics competence. At the individual level, strong positive correlations were observed for ICT knowledge (rs = 0.7397), ICT attitude (rs = 0.7735), ICT practices (rs = 0.7409), and ICT self-efficacy (rs = 0.7456). This suggests that nurses with greater ICT knowledge, more positive attitudes towards ICT, more frequent ICT usage, and higher confidence in their ICT abilities exhibit significantly higher levels of nursing informatics competence.

Similarly, at the institutional level, strong positive correlations were found between nursing informatics competence and the organization of training (rs = 0.7085), management support (rs = 0.6877), the availability of policies on informatics use (rs = 0.6918), the quality of ICT infrastructure (rs = 0.7181), and the adequacy of human resources for ICT (rs = 0.6950). These findings highlight the crucial role of a supportive institutional environment in fostering nursing informatics competence. Specifically, well-organised training programs, strong management support, policies on informatics use, ICT infrastructure, and Human resources dedicated to ICT are all strongly linked to higher nurses’ competence levels. Notably, workload (rs = 0.4735) also showed a moderate positive association with nursing informatics competence, suggesting that higher workload might be associated with increased competence. However, the relationship is not as strong as the other factors examined (Table 4).

Discussion

This study assessed nursing informatics competence and its associated factors among nurses in Tanzania, revealing significant variability in health informatics competencies similar to trends observed across SSA. This lack of uniformity mirrors findings from South Africa, where nursing informatics competencies, particularly in clinical information management, were also suboptimal, indicating systemic challenges across the region. South African researchers have consistently highlighted deficiencies in integrating informatics effectively within nursing curricula and clinical practice, emphasising the need for curriculum reform to address competency gaps [11]. Our findings in Tanzania align with these regional observations, underscoring a critical need for targeted educational reforms with a focus on clinical information management skills, which are essential for delivering high-quality, technology-driven nursing care [11]. Compared to Kenya, where studies report relatively higher informatics literacy scores among nurses reflecting progressive integration of technology in nursing education and practice, our study reveals a significant competence gap [30]. The findings highlight the critical need for targeted educational reforms in Tanzania, particularly in informatics management skills, to enhance overall competency levels and align with global health standards [31]. Emulating Kenya’s example by emphasising technology integration in nursing curricula could reduce these disparities and improve care quality in resource-limited settings. Moreover, global nursing informatics initiatives such as the TIGER project [7], emphasise the importance of standardising core competencies, which could provide a valuable framework for Tanzanian nursing education reforms [30].

The correlational findings of this study demonstrate the substantial influence of both individual and institutional factors on nursing informatics competence, consistent with existing literature emphasising a multifaceted approach to competency development. Individual factors, such as ICT knowledge, attitudes, practices, and self-efficacy, showed significant positive correlations with nursing informatics competence, affirming the literature that recognises ICT literacy as foundational for successful informatics adoption in clinical workflows [32, 33]. These findings are consistent with studies indicating that self-efficacy directly influences competency and fosters a supportive environment for integrating informatics into nursing practice [34]. Institutional factors, including the organisation of training programs, managerial support, and the presence of clear informatics policies, also demonstrated positive associations, corroborating findings from Iranian and Canadian contexts, where institutional backing and structured training significantly improved informatics competence [8, 35]. These results align with the TIGER initiative recommendations promoting organisational investment in informatics education and leadership support to facilitate digital health integration [7, 36, 37]. The moderate correlation of workload with informatics competence reveals a nuanced relationship consistent with literature indicating that while moderate workload may stimulate nurses to adopt informatics solutions to enhance efficiency, excessive workload can conversely impede effective technology utilisation due to fatigue and stress [38, 39]. Overall, the strong correlations observed in this study affirm the necessity for tailored training programs and supportive health information technology policies to foster a more competent nursing workforce in the digital age.

Regression analysis in the current study further highlights ICT knowledge as a fundamental prerequisite for informatics competence in nursing, supporting extensive prior research that identifies comprehensive ICT literacy as a cornerstone for effective use and integration of health information technologies. Nurses with strong ICT foundations are better equipped to navigate complex clinical information systems, contributing to improved patient outcomes and care quality [40]. Building self-efficacy through tailored competencies training and experiential learning can therefore enhance informatics integration in daily nursing practice [41]. These insights resonate with the Nursing Informatics Competencies framework, which advocates for cultivating both technical skills and attitudes that enable nurses to meet the demands of modern digital healthcare environments [9].

The positive association between structured training programs and nursing informatics competence observed in this study aligns with extensive global evidence advocating education and continuous professional development as critical levers for enhancing nursing informatics skills [4, 11, 35]. Prior studies in Iran and South Africa emphasise that well-designed informatics education programs not only enhance technical capabilities but also foster a culture of lifelong learning and technological adaptability essential for evolving healthcare demands [11, 35]. Robust training systems, coupled with supportive policies, form the backbone of successful informatics competency enhancement, as recognised by initiatives like the American Nurses Association and TIGER, which call for curricula integrating informatics competencies throughout nursing education and clinical practice [4, 7, 42]. They advocate for robust training systems and highlight the need for supportive policies acknowledging technological competency, ultimately advancing nursing practice and improving patient care outcomes.

The finding that higher workload correlates with increased informatics competence presents an intriguing and complex narrative. Similar observations have been reported where nurses facing greater demands tend to develop enhanced informatics skills as a strategic response to improve workflow efficiency and manage time constraints [35, 39]. However, the duality of this relationship is recognised in the literature, which also warns that excessive workload may precipitate burnout, reduce time for adequate technology training, and negatively impact care quality [43]. This paradox suggests that the interplay between workload and informatics competence is mediated by organisational factors such as leadership support, policy implementation, and availability of resources [2, 4]. Effective management and leadership are therefore critical to create work environments where informatics can serve as a facilitative tool rather than an additional burden, emphasising the strategic role of nursing management in balancing clinical workload and technological adoption. This nuanced understanding calls for further research exploring how different workload thresholds influence nursing informatics competence and the development of tailored interventions to optimise both nurse well-being and digital proficiency.

Strengths and limitations

We used SANICS, a validated and reliable tool specifically designed to measure informatics competence among nurses. This study collected data from multiple regions across the country, introducing diversity that enhances the generalizability of the findings to similar settings. However, our study has some limitations. The cross-sectional study design allows for association rather than establishing temporal relationships. The assessment is based on self-reported responses, which may be prone to socio-desirability biases. Additionally, the items used to measure NI provide a limited range of knowledge and skills required by nurses working in digitalised healthcare settings, whilst many factors could have affected digital competence.

Implications for healthcare practice and policy in Tanzania

The implications of these findings for healthcare practice and policy in Tanzania are profound and multifaceted. First, there is an urgent need to develop and implement comprehensive training programs that integrate ICT training as a core component of nursing education among nurses. This approach would enhance nurses’ confidence and competence in effectively utilising informatics. Additionally, healthcare policies must prioritise institutional support systems that mitigate the adverse effects of excessive workload, fostering an environment conducive to learning and technological adoption. Policymakers should also consider creating frameworks that facilitate ongoing professional development in nursing informatics, aligning training with the strategic goals of improving health service delivery in Tanzania. By establishing robust structures that support the education of nursing professionals and the integration of digital health solutions into healthcare systems, Tanzania can enhance the overall quality of care and strengthen the capabilities of its nursing workforce to address contemporary health challenges.

Conclusion

This study highlights a critical understanding of nursing informatics competence and its associated factors among nurses in Tanzania, revealing significant variability comparable to patterns observed in other SSA countries. The findings highlight the pressing need for targeted educational reforms, particularly in ICT, to enhance the competencies of nurses and align them with global health standards. Notably, the strong positive correlations identified between individual factors, such as ICT knowledge, self-efficacy, and institutional support, highlight the indispensable role of comprehensive training programs and supportive healthcare policies in enhancing informatics practices. Furthermore, the complex relationship between workload and informatics competence necessitates a balanced approach in healthcare settings, ensuring that as demands on nurses increase, they receive the necessary resources and support to optimise their use of technology. These findings highlight the need for proactive measures from stakeholders across the healthcare spectrum to cultivate a skilled nursing workforce capable of leveraging informatics to enhance patient care outcomes in Tanzania’s healthcare system.
