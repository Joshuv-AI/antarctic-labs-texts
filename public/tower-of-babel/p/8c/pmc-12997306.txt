Pilot trial of delayed versus immediate financial incentives for smoking cessation
Authors: Sneha Sathya, Catherine Zaw, Jalima Quintero, Lisa Hue, Fei Tang, Michael Campos, Elizabeth Balbin, Mehdi Mirsaeidi, Gregory E. Holt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study compared delayed- versus immediate-reward financial incentives using a weekly reward frequency.MethodsSmokers were randomized in a single blinded study of 2 groups: Reward Group used an immediate-reward strategy and Banked-Money Group used a delayed-reward strategy. Subjects received personalized smoking cessation plans, presented weekly for smoking tests measuring exhaled CO (COex) and received $40 for passing. The Reward Group immediately received $40 whereas the Banked-Money Group's $40 was placed in an account they could access only upon successful study completion. During the first 3 months, subjects could fail 2 tests before a 3rd disqualified them. During the last month, any positive smoking test disqualified them. If disqualified, the Reward Group members kept all previously received rewards however the Banked-Money Group members lost all accrued money. Follow up visits occurred at 3 and 6 months after trial completion.ResultsTwelve (33%) participants quit smoking in intention to treat analysis with 9 (75%) remaining abstinent 6 months after trial completion. The Reward Group had 5 of 15 (33%) quit versus 7 of 16 (43.8%) in the Banked-Money Group. Conclusions: This pilot trial shows the financial incentive protocol is feasible with favorable quit rates compared to previously published data.

Introduction

The Center for Disease Control and Prevention [1] considers smoking to be the leading preventable cause of death [1]. Smoking is directly responsible for cardiovascular disease, chronic respiratory diseases and cancer and quitting smoking reduces the risk and/or progression of all three diseases [3]. Smoking related illnesses cost the United States $600 billion dollars with $240 billion spent on medical care and $185 billion in lost productivity [[1], [2]]. Currently, the rate of smoking cessation is only 7.5% per year using standard of care [4]. Improving smoking quit rates is a critical need to both improve people's health and save healthcare expenditures on smoking related diseases.

Financial incentives influence healthcare behaviors by using monetary rewards to offset negative feelings associated with the desired behavior and are proven cost-effective [5]. Since smokers who experience nicotine withdrawal symptoms are less likely to quit smoking [6], use of monetary rewards to counteract negative nicotine withdrawal symptoms can improve quit rates [7,8]. Using an immediate-reward strategy, a randomized, controlled trial that paid smokers $200 at 2 weeks, 4 weeks and 6 months for smoking abstinence increased quit rates from 6% to 15.7% at 6 months [7].

Immediate-reward financial incentive strategies provide the reward promptly upon completion of the desired healthcare behavior and work well for immediate and irreversible healthcare interventions like cancer screenings [9] and single dose vaccinations [10]. However, modification of lifestyle behaviors, such as smoking cessation or weight loss, carry the risk of relapse after the financial incentive has been received. For lifestyle behaviors, a delayed-reward strategy may work better because the person earns the reward only after changing the behavior for an extended time that creates a durable change. A smoking cessation study comparing delayed versus immediate rewards awarded at 1-, 3- and 6-month intervals and found quit rates of 12.7% versus 9.5%, respectively [11].

Behavioral Economic Theory suggests applying financial incentives to treat addictive behaviors requires the frequency of the reward to approximate the frequency of the withdrawal symptoms [[12], [13], [14]]. The frequency of nicotine cravings experienced in smoking cessation, especially within the first month of quitting [15], suggests the long intervals between rewards in previous trials may have reduced the effectiveness of the financial incentives.

To improve the intensity of a delayed-rewards strategy's effectiveness, this study used weekly smoking tests that provided a reward frequency that aligns better with the persistence of nicotine withdrawal symptoms [16,17]. This also breaks down the longer term goal of months of smoking cessation into smaller, easily attainable goals of quitting for a week. This technique of setting smaller more manageable goals increases the success in accomplishing the longer-term goal [18].

Hypothetically, the delayed reward strategy using weekly rewards will improve quit rates. This manuscript describes a pilot study using a randomized, single blinded trial to compare an immediate versus delayed reward financial incentive strategy on smoking quit rates using weekly rewards.

Methods and materials

Trial population

Veterans were recruited from the pulmonary clinic at the Miami VA Medical Center if they were over 18 years of age, smoked at least a half a pack of cigarettes a day and had a ten-pack year history, were interested in quitting smoking, agreed to undergo smoking tests and had reliable means to visit the clinic weekly.

Trial design

This is a single blinded, randomized clinical trial of two financial incentives for smoking cessation. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Miami VA Medical Center (IRB#1160736), is registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT03608852) and complies with the Declaration of Helsinki principles of ethics. After informed consent, subjects received $50 to meet the smoking cessation counsellor to choose a quit date and possibly obtain pharmacologic interventions (e.g. nicotine replacement therapy, varenicline or bupropion). Starting on their quit date, subjects presented weekly and were tested for smoking through measurement of exhaled carbon monoxide (COex) where levels ≤6 ppm were considered negative. Subjects were randomized into one of two groups called either the Reward Group or Banked-Money Group based on the timing of receiving financial incentives. Reward Group subjects used an immediate reward strategy and received $40 when they passed the smoking test. Banked-Money Group subjects used a delayed reward strategy where their $40 reward was held in an account. Banked-Money Group members could only access the money in their account after quitting smoking for the entire trial. During the trial, all subjects who fail the smoking test received a strike instead of the financial incentive. For the first three months (Grace Period), subjects were allowed two “strikes” (a positive COex test) until the third strike disqualified them. For the last month (Tobacco Free Period), any strike disqualified the subject. Upon disqualification, Reward Group subjects kept any money they earned during the trial whereas Banked-Money Group subjects lost all the money held for them in their study account. Subjects followed up at 3 and 6 months after completing the trial or disqualification to analyze smoking status. Trial schematic is presented online in Supplemental Table 1.

Questionnaires

Upon enrollment, a questionnaire collected detailed information on smoking history including quit attempts, associated alcohol and drug use, household income and current cost of preferred cigarettes in addition to medical history and medications. Subject's nicotine dependence was determined by the Fagerström Test of Nicotine Dependence (FTND) and nicotine withdrawal symptoms was determined by the Minnesota Nicotine Withdrawal Scale (MnNWS). Becks Anxiety Inventory (BAI) and Beck's Depression Inventory (BDI) recorded baseline values for anxiety and depression[19]. Completion of Barrat's Impulsivity Scale tested subject's impulsivity. The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) evaluated sleep and the Primary Care PTSD Screen for DSM-5 (PC-PTSD-5) screened for PTSD. A subject's stage of quitting was also assessed and individuals in the active and maintenance phase were not enrolled. Subjects repeated the PSQI, MnNWS, BAI and BDI monthly during the trial and at both follow up visits. At the end of the trial, subjects completed an exit questionnaire. All questionnaires are available in the appendix of this manuscript.

Smoking tests

Exhaled Carbon Monoxide: Using a Micro+™pro Smokelyzer® (Covita, UK) exhaled breath analyzer, subjects were instructed to exhale through their mouth for at least 6 s into the mouthpiece. COex levels were recorded for each visit.

Venous Carboxyhemoglobin levels: Subjects disputing the COex results underwent venous blood sampling that were analyzed using the Stat Profile Prime Critical Care Analyzer (Nova Biomedical, US).

Outcomes

This study was performed to determine if the study design is feasible. All other outcomes are considered exploratory to influence the follow up trial. These outcomes include smoking cessation based on COex measurements between financial incentive groups at the end of the four-month trial and 6 month follow up. Additional outcomes are descriptive statistics to characterize the study population as it pertains to financial incentive models.

Results

Participants

Thirty-six veterans with an average age of 65.6 (34 – 79) were enrolled in this study after signing informed consent. All participants were male (subjects recruited from the veterans administration hospital) and 44.4% identified as black, 22% identified as Hispanic, 11.1% identified as Native American and 11.1% identified as White (Fig. 1A). Almost half of the participants were divorced. Disabled veterans made up 37.1% while the rest were retired (30.6%), wage earners (19.4%) or self employed (8.3%). Most participants had attended some college/technical school (41.7%) or completed high school (36.1%). Most participants were in the preparation phase (63%) of quitting and had significant smoking histories with 47% and 33% having greater than a 30 and 50 pack-year history, respectively (Fig. 1B). Using the Fagerström Test of Nicotine Dependence (FTND), 67% scored as high or very high nicotine dependence. The Minnesota Nicotine Withdrawal Scale (MnNWS) found an average score of 15.3 (scored 0 to 60 with zero indicating no withdrawal symptoms).

This population had significant behavioral health and sleep issues (Fig. 1B). Using the Beck's Anxiety and Depression indices, 50% and 31% of participants had significant anxiety and depression. The Primary Care PTSD Screen for DSM-5 (PC-PTSD-5) revealed 28% of participants had a positive PTSD screen. Initial sleep disturbance evaluation using the PSQI found 80.5% of participants had significant sleep disturbances.

Several questionnaires (PSQI, MnNWS, BAI and BDI) were repeated during the study to determine if sleep disturbance, nicotine withdrawal symptoms and anxiety/depression changed during quitting smoking. However, small the small sample size prevents meaningful representation of this data.

Of the 36 participants who signed informed consent, 5 participants were disqualified for failing to see the smoking cessation counsellor and 31 (86.1%) were randomized after meeting the smoking cessation counsellor (Fig. 2). Six randomized participants met the smoking cessation counsellor but never presented to clinic on their quit date. Smoking cessation

Twelve participants (33%) successfully completed the trial by quitting smoking based on COex measurements in intention to treat analysis (Fig. 3). Thirteen (36.1%) participants started the study but were disqualified due to an inability to quit smoking. One member of the Reward Group was disqualified with a positive COex measurement on his last scheduled study visit. One member of the Banked-Money Group was diagnosed with metastatic lung cancer halfway through the trial and died in hospice three days prior to his last scheduled study visit. Analysis of the 25 participants who made the first clinic visit showed an average initial COex of 11.7 PPM. Those who failed to quit smoking had a higher beginning COex PPM that decreased during the study visits but rarely tested under the required 6PPM threshold (Fig. 4B). Of the 13 participants who made clinic visits but ultimately failed, only 6 (46.2%) ever recorded a COex of 6PPM or less and ten participants collectively passed three tests.

Follow up period

Twelve participants completed the trial and all made it to their first visit in the Follow Up Period. Three (25%) participants had a positive COex test at the three month visit and none had a positive COex test at 6 months. At six months after trial end, 9 (25%) of the original participants remained abstinent and passed the final COex smoking test. Five (71.4%) of the 7 participants in the Banked-Money Group and four (80%) of the 5 participants in the Reward Group who completed the study remained abstinent by COex testing at 6 months.

Financial compensation

In total, $8390 was spent on study participants. On an intention to treat analysis, $232.22 was spent per participant. In total, the Banked-Money Group spent $4840 for 7 participants who quit smoking or $691 per quitter. The Reward Group spent $3550 for 5 participants who quit smoking or $710 per quitter. $1550 was spent on participants meeting with the smoking cessation counsellor and $6840 was spent on financial incentives for smoking cessation. $640 in financial incentives were paid to seven members of the Reward Group who ultimately failed to quit smoking. No financial incentives were given to the six members of the Banked-Money Group who failed to quit smoking since they lost all incentives when they were disqualified.

Participant trial evaluation

Upon trial completion or disqualification, participants were asked to complete an exit questionnaire containing 13 questions evaluating the study design. Sixteen participants (44.4%) completed the questionnaire, 7 (19.4%) from the Reward Group, 9 (25%) from the Banked-Money Group, 10 (27.8%) who passed and 6 (16.7%) who failed. The survey used a scale of 1 to 5 where 5 was associated with the highest, longest or highest frequency of the asked questions. Participants were generally happy with their group assignment (3.6) and thought the financial incentive provided for each visit (3.1) and in total (3.0) was sufficient (1 being too low, 5 being too high). Participants scored 2.3 when asked if the frequency of visits “needed to be more frequent” (1) or were “too frequent” (5). When asked if the Tobacco Free period was “too short” (1) or “too long” (5), they recorded an average score of 2.1. Most were satisfied with the total length of the study (2.9) and Grace Period (2.6) but would have appreciated more strikes (2.1). Finally, participants were confident they would remain tobacco free (3.6) and were likely to recommend the study as a mechanism to quit smoking to their friends (4.6).

Discussion

This pilot trial using the clinical trial design to compare two methods of financial incentives for smoking cessation was feasible but needs improvement. We observed that of 36 participants, 31 (86.1%) signed informed consent, met with the smoking cessation counsellor and were randomized while 25 (69.4%) made the initial clinic visit and completed the smoking test and questionnaires. Removal of the $50 compensation to meet the smoking cessation counsellor could improve adherence since 6 (16.7%) took this money and never returned.

As a smoking cessation strategy, 12 (33%) quit smoking on an intention to treat analysis; 5 of 15 (33%) in the Reward Group and 7 of 16 (43.8%) in the Banked-Money Group. Although the small number of participants prevents statistical analysis between groups, the quit rate compares favorably to other smoking cessation trials using financial incentives. Finding 25% of participants remained abstinent at ten months is also encouraging.

Of the 13 participants that started the trial but failed to quit smoking, they rarely earned the financial incentive meant to change their behavior. A financial incentive is most effective if the smoker receives it. Ten participants were disqualified within the first five visits and collectively only received the financial incentive three times. This suggests the 6PPM COex threshold is too stringent at trial initiation where the average COex was 11.7 PPM. Relaxing the COex threshold for the first month may ensure participants receive the financial incentives meant to counteract nicotine withdrawal symptoms when they are peaking [16,17]. These COex cut-offs could slowly be lowered to the level indicative of not smoking (eg 6 PPM) over the next several visits. Providing a lenient run-in period has been beneficial in other studies of incentives to combat substance abuse addictions [20]. Additionally, these relaxed cut-offs would provide a measure of success to the participants that has also been shown to improve performance as described by the “success breeds success” phenomenon [21].

Weekly clinic visits for COex testing could be considered burdensome especially amongst active workers. Exit questionnaires from the participants did not find the frequency of visits to be overly burdensome but many were retired. However, the intensity of weekly visits may be critical to overcome the difficulty of smoking cessation. Data show increased frequency of interactions with a smoker improved smoking cessation rates [22]. Individuals are more successful in achieving a long term goal when it is broken down into more frequent, easily attainable goals [23]. Meeting achievable interval goals provides more frequent positive stimuli shown to improve success in reaching the ultimate goal [18]. To facilitate weekly visits for the active worker would necessitate the use of current employee/student health services some of which already use financial incentives [24]. Ultimately, nationwide pharmacy chains/drug stores could also be contracted to perform the COex measurements.

The delayed reward strategy is advantageous from a health economics standpoint since it only provides financial incentives to smokers who quit for the desired duration of time. $640 in financial incentives were provide to members of the Reward Group who failed to quit smoking and could be considered wasted money. Whereas by trial design, no member of the Banked-Money Group who failed to quit received a financial incentive. The Banked-Money strategy ensures precious healthcare dollars are focused on achieving the desired goal of smoking cessation.

This study recorded data on comorbidities associated with smoking including nicotine addiction [25], depression [26], anxiety [27], PTSD [28] impulsiveness [[29], [30], [31]], and sleep quality [[32], [33], [34], [35]]. Due to the small sample size of a pilot trial, no inferences could be made beyond confirming that data were obtainable. Understanding the effect these comorbidities exert on the use of financial incentives to improve quit rates is important for personalizing future strategies to the individual smoker [14]. Future studies will determine whether strategies tailored to mitigate the impact of these comorbidities on smoking cessation improve quit rates.

Theoretically, the “banked-money” delayed reward financial incentive strategy for smoking cessation has additional advantages over an immediate reward strategy. First, the delayed reward strategy produces a constant threat of losing the money if participants fail to quit smoking. The constancy of this threat produces a more durable stimulus to abstain from smoking than an immediate reward whose effect dissipates once it is spent. Second, research shows that humans experience a stronger negative reaction when they lose money compared to the positive reaction when they gain the same amount of money [36]. This fear of losing money creates a more powerful stimulus for change than simply obtaining an instant reward. Finally, the amount of money in each participant's account increases the longer they remain abstinent. This produces an ever increasing financial reward that strengthens the incentive to remain abstinent.

In conclusion, a delayed reward financial incentive strategy for smoking cessation is a feasible endeavor that may prove both effective and fiscally sound. Adjustments to this trial's design to remove payment for meeting smoking cessation counsellors and incorporate a lenient run-in period to ensure smokers receive the financial incentive could further improve this strategy.
