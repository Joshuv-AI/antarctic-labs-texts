Effect of Antiresorptive Therapy on Development of Posttraumatic Osteoarthritis after Intra-articular Distal Radius Fracture in Female Patients
Authors: Ji Sup Hwang, Chung Yeop Shin, Wan Kee Hong, Hyun Sik Gong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroudSubchondral bone is a potential target for osteoarthritis (OA) prevention. Antiresorptive therapy has been demonstrated to be chondroprotective in surgically induced OA animal models, but results were mixed in clinical studies. We evaluated radiographic and symptomatic OA development at 1 year postoperatively. The odds ratio (OR) for the OA development was analyzed using logistic regression for factors including age, lowest T score, bone mineral density, presence of osteoporosis, residual intra-articular step-off, and antiresorptive therapy. To delineate the effect of the osteoporosis status, subgroup analyses were performed.ResultsDevelopment of radiographic OA was identified in 80 patients (27%), and the mean pain score on a visual analog scale was 2.2 ± 1.1. In the regression analysis, radiographic OA was associated with the presence of osteoporosis (OR, 3.97; 95% CI, 1.89–8.32), residual intra-articular step-off (OR, 3.68; 95% CI, 1.45–9.39), and not starting antiresorptive therapy (OR, 0.29; 95% CI, 0.14–0.61). In subgroup analysis, radiographic OA was associated with not starting antiresorptive therapy (OR, 0.24; 95% CI, 0.07–0.93) for patients without osteoporosis. However, for patients with osteoporosis, no association was found between radiographic OA and the evaluated factors.ConclusionsInitiation of antiresorptive therapy was associated with a lower incidence of radiographic OA after an intra-articular DRF, especially in those without osteoporosis. Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most common musculoskeletal disease worldwide. It is characterized by cartilage destruction within the joint and manifests as pain and/or limited range of motion. Despite recent advances in biologics, drugs that can regenerate the cartilage are currently unavailable.1) Therefore, preventing early-stage OA from further progression is important. Subchondral bone is a potential novel target for OA prevention. Lying beneath the articular cartilage, it consists of subchondral plate and trabecular bone.2) Sclerotic subchondral bone is widely considered as a hallmark of OA.3) However, recent studies have revealed that in the early stage of OA, the bone turnover rate is increased and the subchondral bone density is smaller than in the advanced stage of OA.4,5) Since this phenomenon is also observed in the pathophysiology of osteoporosis, downregulation of the bone turnover rate through antiresorptive medication has been attempted to treat patients with OA.6,7,8)

Successful antiresorptive therapy has been demonstrated in OA animal models.9,10) In these models, OA is induced by surgically removing their meniscus or ligamentous structures. Therefore, they represent posttraumatic OA rather than degenerative OA.11) However, the role of antiresorptive osteoporosis medication in the prevention of posttraumatic OA has not been well evaluated. We sought to determine whether patients with traumatic joint injury might benefit from antiresorptive therapy and whether bone health status might affect the chondroprotective effect, if any, of the therapy.

We therefore asked as follows: Does antiresorptive therapy play a preventive role in the development of Posttraumatic OA after intra-articular distal radius fracture (DRF)? Is there any difference in the effect according to osteoporosis status?

METHODS

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (IRB No. B-2202-737-101). The requirement for informed consent was waived because all data were fully anonymized for analysis.

Study Design and Setting

This was a single-center, retrospective study of patients from an urban tertiary care hospital who underwent open reduction and internal fixation for the diagnosis of DRFs from March 2001 to December 2020.

Study Sample

Between March 2001 and December 2020, we treated 438 patients for acute intra-articular DRFs through open reduction and internal fixation. We considered patients who were female, had acute DRFs caused by minor trauma, such as a fall from standing height, and had a follow-up of at least 1 year as potentially eligible. Thus, 80.1% of the treated patients (351 patients) were eligible; however, 11.8% (52 patients) were excluded because of associated carpal bone fracture or history of any anti-osteoporosis therapy or any bone metabolic disease before the surgery, leaving 68.2% (299 women) for analysis. Only women were included in this study, as the effects of antiresorptive agents on bone metabolism would differ between sexes. The mean age of the patients included in this study was 64.7 ± 8.6 years. The mean lowest T score was –2.2 ± 0.9. Bone mineral density (BMD) was measured in grams per square centimeter at the lumbar spine (L1–L3) and total hip using a dual-energy x-ray absorptiometer (Hologic Horizon W, Hologic Inc.). The in vivo coefficient of variation was 1.7% for the lumbar spine and 1.0% for the total hip. The measurements were 0.869 ± 0.134 g/cm2 at the lumbar spine and 0.783 ± 0.107 g/cm2 at the hip. According to the World Health Organization diagnostic criteria for osteoporosis, 37.5% (112 patients) were osteoporotic and 57.5% (172 patients) had osteopenia. All the patients underwent open reduction and internal fixation using locking plates through a volar approach within 3 days after injury. According to AO/Orthopaedic Trauma Association (AO/OTA) classification, 55 patients (18%) were included in type B and 244 patients (82%) in type C. None of the patients suffered ligament injuries such as scapholunate dissociation that required additional surgical procedures. Residual intra-articular step-off of 2 mm12) or greater was found in 6.7% (20 patients) according to either simple radiographs or computed tomography scans. Ulnar variance was restored in all cases and none had secondary ulnar impaction syndrome. Approximately 49.8% (149 patients) indicated for secondary prevention of osteoporosis started antiresorptive therapy immediately after surgery. The determination of antiresorptive therapy was based on patient's age, menopausal state, and results of an osteoporosis study by a single clinician. Raloxifene 60 mg once daily (a selective estrogen receptor modulator [SERM]) and risedronate 35 mg once weekly (bisphosphonate) were prescribed, and denosumab 60 mg was injected once every 6 months. As a result, SERM was prescribed for 68 patients, bisphosphonate for 42 patients, and denosumab for 32 patients. Compliance with the medication was confirmed through routine visits to our clinic until 1 year after surgery. During this treatment period, no adjustment was made in the regimen initially decided upon for each patient at the first visit.

Posttraumatic OA Assessment

We collected plain wrist radiographs taken immediately after surgery and 1 year later. Two orthopedic surgeons (CYS and WKH) reviewed the plain radiographs to determine posttraumatic OA. Following Knirk and Jupiter criteria,13,14) we determined the degree of change in narrowing of the radiocarpal joint space and formation of osteophytes, sclerosis, and cysts (Fig. 1). All images were anonymized and reviewed in a randomized and blinded fashion. The radiographic measurements were repeated twice by 2 orthopedic surgeons (CYS and WKH) with an interval of 3 weeks to evaluate the intra- and interobserver reliability. We also collected pain scores measured on a visual analog scale (VAS) at 1 year after surgery.

Intraobserver and Interobserver Measurement Reliability

Before taking the radiographic measurements, we held consensus building and reliability sessions using intraclass correlation coefficients. Interobserver reliability was determined by the 2 orthopedic surgeons who determined posttraumatic OA. Each surgeon was unaware of the other observer's findings. The interobserver reliability of each radiographic measurement was calculated as an intraclass correlation value between the 2 observers. The measurement order for each observer was randomly assigned. In addition, one of the observers repeated the above radiographic measurements 3 weeks later, and intraobserver reliability was confirmed. The interobserver reliability was 0.909 and the intraobserver reliability was 0.932. Because a good result of ≥ 0.90 was obtained, the measurements made by the author who performed the intraobserver reliability testing (CYS) were used for the analysis.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp.), and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. We generated descriptive statistics for all variables. Comparison between patients who developed OA and those who did not was made using paired t-test or chi-square test. We then calculated the adjusted odds ratio (OR) for OA development using multivariable logistic regression analysis with age, lowest T score, BMD levels, the presence of osteoporosis, and the presence of residual articular step-off used as independent variables. In detail, age, lowest T score, and BMD levels were evaluated as continuous variables and the presence of osteoporosis and residual articular step-off as categorical variables. To assess multicollinearity among the independent variables, the variance inflation factor (VIF) was calculated. All VIF values were below the commonly accepted threshold of 5, indicating an absence of multicollinearity. To delineate the effect of the presence of osteoporosis, subgroup analyses were performed. To calculate the inter- and intraobserver reliability, intraclass correlation analysis was used.

RESULTS

Factors Associated with OA Development

Development of radiographic OA was identified in a total of 80 patients (27%). Patients who developed OA and those who did not showed no significant difference in demographic factors, time to operation after trauma, fracture type, type of antiresorptive therapy, and pain VAS score (Table 1). Multivariable logistic regression analysis showed that the presence of osteoporosis (OR, 3.97; 95% CI, 1.89–8.32), residual articular step-off (OR, 3.68; 95% CI, 1.45–9.39), and not starting antiresorptive therapy (OR, 0.29; 95% CI, 0.14–0.61) were associated with radiographic OA development regardless of the patient's age.

DISCUSSION

OA development after intra-articular fracture is initiated by subchondral bone remodeling.15) Given that antiresorptive therapy can decrease the bone remodeling process16) and preserve periarticular bone architecture,6,17) we thought that it might positively affect the risk of posttraumatic OA development. In this study, we evaluated the prevalence of OA at 1 year after DRF surgery in 299 patients. Results showed that the incidence of radiographic OA was associated with the presence of osteoporosis, residual step-off, and not starting an antiresorptive therapy regardless of age. Furthermore, in the subgroup of patients without osteoporosis, not starting an antiresorptive therapy was the only associated factor, whereas none of the variables reached statistical significance in the subgroup of patients with osteoporosis. Our study shows that antiresorptive therapy may have a chondroprotective effect in patients with intra-articular fracture and early bone health deterioration. The current National Osteoporosis Foundation guideline recommends initiating antiresorptive therapy in elderly patients with DRF not only in the osteoporotic range of BMD but also in the osteopenic range.18) Therefore, our study supports this recommendation as it may have an additional benefit of preventing posttraumatic OA.

Our study has some limitations. First, extra-articular DRFs were not evaluated in this study. This was to choose the fracture type that might have affected the subchondral bone and the development of posttraumatic OA.13) Second, due to the retrospective nature of this study, OA changes were evaluated only through plain radiographs 1 year after surgery. Several published studies that evaluate the development of posttraumatic OA after DRF followed patients for at least 3 years. However, the prevalence of posttraumatic OA in this study was not different from that in a previous 2-year follow-up study.19) Third, considering the study subjects' age and sex, it might be difficult to generalize the effectiveness of antiresorptive therapy for DRF. Fourth, other factors such as body mass indices, job, or smoking status were not included in the regression analysis. They were not collected due to the retrospective nature of the study; however, we believe that posttraumatic OA is more affected by the evaluated factors.

Several factors are known to determine the development of OA following an intra-articular fracture. A large articular step-off is a well-known major risk factor for OA development,20) and accurate reduction of the fracture is emphasized during a fixation surgery. In addition, antiresorptive therapy might have reversed the decreased subchondral bone density after an intra-articular fracture.21,22) Despite the role of antiresorptive therapy in suppressing BMD loss in both preclinical9,10,23) and clinical6,8,24) studies, a meta-analysis on human trials reported that they had no advantage over placebo in OA prevention.8) However, participants of those human trials were heterogeneous in their OA severity. On the other hand, when knee joints with no or early-stage OA were compared with those with advanced OA, the former benefited from an antiresorptive therapy while the latter did not.7,25) Furthermore, nonarthritic hip joints of 45 patients undergoing antiresorptive therapy were protected from OA development, whereas those of 353 patients without antiresorptive therapy were not during a similar period of follow-up.26) We looked into OA development after intra-articular DRFs, which might reflect early stages of OA development. We found that antiresorptive therapy's effect outweighed the effects of patient's age, BMD level, or residual step-off. Once antiresorptive therapy was initiated, a decrease in bone turnover might have influenced the subchondral bone remodeling, regardless of the drug type.

The effect of the antiresorptive therapy differed according to osteoporosis status. In a previous animal study, rabbits were induced with osteoporosis, and administration of bone forming agents could prevent their cartilage damage progression after traumatic joint injury.27) On the other hand, well-designed human trials did not consider the osteoporosis status.6,7,8) In osteoporosis, subchondral bone density is decreased,28) which is a similar finding observed in early OA.4,29) According to our results, the chondroprotective effect of antiresorptive therapy might be more expected in patients with relatively preserved subchondral bone (normal or osteopenic ranges) than in those with osteoporotic subchondral bone. Possible mechanisms of antiresorptive therapy in preserving the subchondral bone microarchitecture include decreased recruitment of osteoclasts.30) Whether a patients' bone health and subchondral bone status are important in determining the effect of antiresorptive therapy requires further studies.

In conclusion, initiation of antiresorptive therapy was associated with a lower incidence of radiographic OA after intra-articular DRF, especially in those without osteoporosis. This study suggests that attempts to decrease bone remodeling might be effective for OA prevention in a joint at risk.
