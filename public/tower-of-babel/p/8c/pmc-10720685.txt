An education‐based telehealth group improves the management and clinical outcomes of patients with inflammatory bowel disease in China (THEIM study)
Authors: Jian Kang, Juan Su, Jing Wang, Haixia Ren, Anning Yin, Jiao Li, Qian Zhou, Yang Wang, Peng Zhu, Liping Zou, Wei Wang, Zhishun Tang, Weiguo Dong, Yijuan Ding, Ping An
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough poor medication adherence has a negative impact on disease prognosis in patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), finding proven solutions remains a challenge. In this study, we developed a telehealth management model based on education and patient‐centered medical care (PCEB) using the social media platform WeChat.ObjectiveTo investigate the effect of PCEB on adherence and clinical outcomes.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort, 543 IBD patients (274 in the PCEB group and 269 in the routine group) at the IBD center of Renmin Hospital (Wuhan University, Wuhan, China) were enrolled between January 2020 and September 2022. The routine group received routine follow‐up and management, while for PCEB patients, a comprehensive IBD education program and PCEB were conducted. Medication adherence and clinical outcomes were also evaluated.ResultsThere were no differences between the PCEB and routine groups in terms of patient demographics and clinical characteristics, including disease classification, duration, biological treatment, and educational background at baseline. Compared with routine treatment, PCEB greatly improved patient medication adherence, as assessed by compliance with oral medication, enteral nutrition, biological infusion, and scheduled endoscopic assessment. Clinical and endoscopic remission in patients with PCEB increased during short‐term (month 4) and long‐term (month 12) follow‐ups, along with a decrease in relapse rates for CD (13.3% vs. 31.8%) and UC (19.8% vs. 37.2%).ConclusionThe telehealth model applied to the PCEB group improved medication adherence and clinical outcomes in patients with IBD. This is a new and powerful solution for the long‐term management of this chronic and progressive disease.Keywords: group management, inflammatory bowel disease, medication adherence, patient education, patient‐centered and education‐based, PCEB, tele health

INTRODUCTION

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), due to its chronicity and progressive and remitting nature, necessitates lifelong therapy and medical care to relieve and/or prevent inflammation relapse and maintain remission and optimal mucosal healing. 1 Although medication adherence is the cornerstone of controlling IBD symptoms and achieving remission, it remains a complex issue in the long‐term management of IBD. 2

Poor medication adherence in IBD is currently considered to be associated with various negative clinical outcomes, including increased risk of disease activity, 3 relapse or flare, 3 , 4 loss of response to biologics, 5 higher risk of surgery, and 6 poor quality of life and higher disability. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 However, previous studies have shown that adherence in patients with IBD varies widely, with non‐adherence rates ranging from 7% to 72% and most negative outcome rates ranging from 30% to 45%. 11 , 12 Although patient characteristics such as age, sex, and employment have been previously established as potential predictors of non‐adherence, the method of adherence measurement and actual patient management are also important factors influencing patient adherence. 13 , 14 , 15

In recent years, telemedicine has contributed to improving the quality and efficiency of care, particularly during the COVID‐19 pandemic. 16 , 17 , 18 In China, WeChat, one of the most popular mainstream social media platforms, has more than three billion active users. 19 It offers a variety of communication services and provides group participation to certain populations. Due to its broad population reach and powerful peripheral features, WeChat has been integrated into the daily lives of most Chinese people and offers unprecedented opportunities for the education and management of patients with IBD. In this study, we developed a telehealth group management model based on patient education (patient‐centered and education‐based group management, PCEB) via a free social media platform via smartphones. We evaluated the impact of PCEB group management on short‐ and long‐term medication adherence and clinical outcomes in patients with IBD.

PATIENT AND METHODS

Study design and patients

We conducted a retrospective observational cohort study at the IBD Center of the Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University, Wuhan, China. Between January 2020 and September 2022, we enrolled 543 IBD patients [275 with Crohn's disease (CD) and 268 with ulcerative colitis (UC]) managed by two IBD medical teams. Patients were managed using WeChat via either PCEB group management (PCEB group) or routine group management (routine group). Each team consisted of IBD specialists (leaders), physicians, nurses, and volunteers. Both teams attended academic discussions and communicated regularly as medical teams at the same IBD center. The therapeutic strategy for patients in both the PCEB and routine groups compliantly followed the IBD guidelines in China. 20

All included patients had a confirmed diagnosis of IBD based on history, physical and laboratory examinations, esophagogastroduodenoscopy, and ileocolonoscopy with histology and radiological imaging of the small bowel. Patients with comorbid conditions that could act as confounders in data gathering and interpretation, and those with difficulty in understanding the content were excluded (Figure 1). The study protocol was approved by the Ethical Committee of the Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University (research project number: WDRY2020‐Y046).

Patient‐centered medical care group management intervention

In the PCEB group, the medical team was organized by IBD specialists, who were team leaders and responsible for the design of the PCEB model and the assignment of all team members, including physicians, nurses, and volunteers (Figure 2).

Overview of the components and function of PCEB group management. CD, Crohn's disease; PCEB, patient‐centered and education‐based; UC, ulcerative colitis.

Through WeChat, the medical team conducted a patient education program offering two regular monthly 30‐min online PowerPoint sessions on the importance of IBD self‐management, etiology, pathology, anatomy, medical and surgical treatment, disease course, compliance, nutrition, the COVID‐19 pandemic, physical activities, and complications of IBD, including mortality risk and colorectal cancer monitoring, pregnancy, and breastfeeding, followed by a 20–30 min question and answer session. The importance of weight monitoring, timely biological treatment, EN, and healthy eating was constantly emphasized and repeated in monthly presentations and daily communications. Based on education, PCEB aimed to educate patients about the concept and stages of the disease, possible development, complications, correct treatment options and their received therapy strategy, further evaluation plans, and recognition of disease relapse. Patients were also instructed to contact the medical team via WeChat in case of the following symptoms: Increased stools/day by more than three stools over normal, rectal bleeding, fever >37.5°C, abdominal pain and/or tenderness, abdominal mass, marked bloating, perianal pain, or new‐onset of the above symptoms. The PCEB group leaders were responsible for sending weekly summaries or tips regarding the above information.

To support patients during IBD management, the medical team provided reminders and appointments for biological infusions, endoscopy, biochemical tests, and inpatient appointments when needed. The PCEB team responded to the patients' questions about their medication, physical activity, and other issues, including health insurance reimbursement, as soon as possible. To promote community sharing and education among patients, the PCEB team encouraged them to post comments and share their experiences.

Routine care group management

Patients in the routine group were also managed through WeChat according to the consensus and guidelines for IBD treatment in China and the treatment process of the IBD Center of the Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University. Unlike in the PCEB group, the medical team in the routine group did not conduct regular patient education through WeChat. Five 30‐min online PowerPoint sessions were conducted during this study. The team rarely initiated reminders to patients in the WeChat group regarding on‐time treatment, routine follow‐up, and assessment. For patient inquiries, team members were not required to respond in a timely manner. However, regarding treatment strategies and procedures, consistency was observed between the routine and PCEB groups.

Data collection

Patient and disease demographics, educational background, drug history, and clinical, biochemical, endoscopic, radiological, and histopathological characteristics were reviewed using the patients' medical records and included age, sex, history of medical and surgical treatment of IBD, date of diagnosis, and disease behavior according to the Montreal classification. Clinical activity was assessed using the Harvey Bradshaw Index (HBI) in patients with CD and the partial Mayo Clinic score in patients with UC. Endoscopic activity was assessed using the simple endoscopic score (SES‐CD) for CD and the Mayo endoscopic subscore for UC. Questionnaires were sent to patients by Questionnaire Star (a WeChat mini program for questionnaire collection) every 4 months to assess patient conditions, evaluate medical adherence, and populate the database.

Assessment of adherence

Adherence to oral medication

The Medication Adherence Report Scale (MARS‐5) was used to evaluate patient compliance with oral medication. (The MARS‐5 is a measurement tool for eliciting patients' reports of non‐adherence.) With this self‐report tool, five items concerning medication intake were assessed (I alter the dose/I forget to use it/I stop taking it for a while/I decide to miss out on a dose/I take less than instructed). Answers were graded on a 5‐point Likert scale (range, 5–25 points) with a score ≥23 indicating high adherence to treatment. In addition, missing dose times in the 2 weeks before the questionnaires were recorded.

Adherence to enteral nutrition for CD patients

All patients with moderate‐to‐severe CD were asked to receive 3 months of exclusive EN (EEN), followed by another 3 months of partial EN (PEN) treatment. A full volume of EEN feed was achieved and tolerated 48–72 h after initiation. Volumes could be increased if the patient was hungry, and the rate could be decreased if there were side effects such as discomfort or distension. The duration of patient adherence to EEN and PEN was investigated using a Questionnaire Star.

Adherence to biologics treatment

To assess adherence to biologics (including infliximab, adalimumab, vedolizumab, and ustekinumab), the delayed time was calculated based on the difference between the scheduled date of the next dose, as determined by physicians, and the actual date of dosing. 21 For the regimens of induction and maintenance (short‐term: <6 months, moderate‐term:6–12 months and long‐term: >12 months), the delayed time for biological treatment was also assessed. For dose intensification, the next scheduled dose date was calculated based on the prescription provided by the physicians. Regarding the early or intentional delay, suspension, or discontinuation of dosing due to unavailability on public holidays, acute illness, or other clinician‐led reasons, the next dosing date was considered to be the scheduled date.

Adherence to endoscopic assessment

Patients with active CD or UC were scheduled to visit the center for clinical and endoscopic assessments 4 and 12 months after induction treatment, and patients in disease remission required an endoscopic assessment every 12 months. Adherence was considered to be within 1 month earlier or later than the scheduled endoscopic evaluation date, and non‐adherence was defined as a delay in endoscopic evaluation of more than 1 month, except in cases of intentional delay or withholding due to the biological agent treatment described above.

Clinical outcomes

Disease activity and safety were assessed at various time points: At the first administration of biologics, at inclusion, every 6 months until 24 months after the discontinuation of biologics, and at the end of follow‐up. Clinical outcomes were clinical remission (CDAI <150) for patients with CD and a partial Mayo Clinic score of <3 for patients with UC with a combined stool frequency and rectal bleeding sub‐score of 1 or less. Endoscopic remission was defined as an SES‐CD score <4 for CD and a Mayo endoscopic subscore <1 for UC. Clinical relapse was defined as active disease according to an HBI ≥4 and the introduction of any specific systemic IBD treatment and/or surgery.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are expressed as the means and standard deviation (SD) or as medians and interquartile ranges, whereas qualitative data are expressed as a number (%). The chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test was used to test categorical data. Student's t‐test, Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, or one‐way analysis of variance were used for noncategorical data. p < 0.05. Relapse‐free survival was studied using the Kaplan‐Meier method. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 20 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA).

RESULTS

Patient characteristics of the study population

A total of 543 patients with IBD (143 with CD and 131 with UC in the PCEB group, and 132 with CD and 137 with UC in the routine group) were retrospectively included, and 90 patients, including 48 in the PCEB group and 42 in the routine group, were excluded. The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients with CD and UC are shown in Table 1. There were no significant differences in relevant demographic and clinical characteristics at baseline. In brief, 45 (31.5%) patients with CD and 32 (24.4%) patients with UC in the PCEB group, 44 (33.3%) in the CD group and 41 (29.9%) in the routine group were in remission at inclusion. Disease duration did not differ between the two groups. A total of 89 (62.2%) patients with CD and 42 (32.1%) patients with UC in the PCEB group, and 85 (64.4%) in the CD group and 42 (30.7%) in the routine group were treated with biologics. There was no significant difference in educational background between the two groups.

Medication adherence

Biological adherence is critical to clinical outcomes. We recorded the actual and scheduled dates for each dose and calculated the delay time (days). As shown in Figure 3, in the routine group, the dosing delay was progressively longer as the duration of biological treatment increased in both CD patients (from 0.95 days for dose 1–9.15 days for dose 7) and UC patients (from 0.62 days for dose 1–13.07 days for dose 7). Delays were more pronounced in the maintenance regimen than in the induction regimen. Delayed treatment with biologics significantly improved under PCEB administration, and a reduction in delay time was observed (from 0.27 days for dose 1 to 0.7 days for dose 7 and from 0.44 days for dose 1–1.53 days for dose seven in CD patients). Patient‐centered medical care significantly improved delayed treatment with biologics in both induction and maintenance regimens.

According to the MARS‐5 response, at 12 months, we found more adherent individuals in the PCEB group (68.5% CD and 69.5% UC) than in the routine group (26.5% CD and 27.0% UC, p < 0.001) (Figure 4). More than 60% of the patients in the PCEB group adhered to their oral medication and rarely missed oral medication. In contrast, among patients in the routine group, more nonadherent individuals were identified, with 67.9% of patients missing more than five doses per week.

For patients with moderately to severely active CD, EEN for 3 months followed by PEN for another 3 months is recommended. Encouragingly, PCEB markedly improved patient compliance with both EEN (78.3% vs. 21.5%) and PEN (62.7% vs. 5.1%) compared with that in the routine group (p < 0.001).

Patient adherence for endoscopic assessment

We further evaluated patient adherence to endoscopic assessment. For UC and CD, PCEB greatly improved patient compliance with endoscopic assessment, with 69.2% of CD and 77.1% of UC patients completing the scheduled endoscopic evaluation (Figure 4d).

Clinical outcomes

The clinical remission rates of CD patients in the PCEB group were higher (81.1% and 75.0% at months 4 and 12, respectively) than those in the routine group (48.8% and 44.8%, respectively) (Figure 5). More importantly, PCEB greatly improved the endoscopic remission rate of CD patients (74.0% at 4 months and 71.7% at 12 months) compared with that of the routine group (p < 0.001).

Similarly, more UC patients managed with PCEB achieved clinical remission (77.1% at month 4% and 72.6% at month 12) compared with 43.4% at month 4% and 53.6% at month 12 in the routine group. Patient adherence for biological tests is shown Supplementary Figure 2. Higher endoscopic remission rates were also observed after PCEB intervention (76.9% vs. 47% at month 4% and 77.1% vs. 44.9% compared with the routine group).

Finally, clinical relapse rates of CD and UC were investigated. The results indicated that PCEB markedly reduced the relapse rates of CD and UC (13.3% for CD and 19.8% for UC in the PCEB group compared with 31.8% for CD and 37.2% for UC in the routine group) (Figure 5, Supplementary Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

Inflammatory bowel disease presents a high‐risk situation for non‐adherence owing to its chronicity and relapsing nature. Previous studies have revealed that educational and behavioral interventions have a positive impact on medication adherence. 22 , 23 , 24 Although reminder systems via traditional methods and telemedicine (including mobile application programs and innovative telemedicine devices or the sending of texts) have been suggested to be useful for patient adherence issues, 22 , 25 , 26 good patient‐doctor relationships and optimized patient education are critical for both accidentally and volitionally nonadherent IBD patients. 22 , 25 Multicomponent interventions combining educational and behavioral approaches suggest a more positive effect on adherence than either approach alone. 27

However, patient education is time‐consuming and inefficient because of limited patient attendance at lectures and lack of sufficient communication. In China, with the increasing incidence of IBD, physicians and medical teams are relatively short staffed and overburdened. To solve the above contradictions, Chinese IBD medical centers try to manage patients using the telemedicine method with WeChat groups, which is a daily mode of communication for most Chinese people.

Based on the WeChat group concept, we propose a novel group management model for IBD patients. An education program via PCEB with lessons presenting IBD information, regular summaries, and daily responses to patients' questions, especially focusing on repeated education on self‐management, timely biological treatment, EN, and healthy eating, was the central part of management. The combined functions of education, consultation, appointments, and community sharing greatly simplified patient access to healthcare, improved patient‐doctor relationships, increased patient participation (weekly messages from patients), and satisfaction (Supplementary Figure 1).

The PCEB group management improved patient medication adherence and empowered self‐initiated treatment. More adherent individuals in the PCEB group were identified by the MARS‐5 evaluation, and over 60% maintained adherence to oral drug use and rarely missed oral medications (Figure 4). For patients with moderately to severely active CD, adherence to EEN for 3 months and PEN for 3 months increased significantly (Figure 4c).

By responding to and addressing patient inquiries in a timely manner, all the patients in the PCEB group were educated about the resolution of relevant issues. By providing medical care through appointments and consultations, medical procedures were streamlined and patients' willingness to follow up increased. Empowering and engaging patients are critical for improving medication adherence. Our results showed that the PCEB telemedicine model effectively improved biological treatment delays in patients with CD and UC and significantly improved their compliance with endoscopic evaluation during follow‐up (69.2% for CD and 77.1% for UC) (Figure 4). More importantly, patients in the PCEB group achieved better clinical outcomes, including higher clinical and endoscopic remission rates, and lower clinical relapse rates (Figure 5). The management of the PCEB group is a practical patient‐centered, education‐based management model that should provide useful information for the further management of patients with IBD.

Our research strengths include assessing patient adherence to oral medications, biological therapy, and follow‐up assessments, especially endoscopic assessments. In addition, we used WeChat, a free platform for patient population management, to integrate education, appointment booking, consultation, community sharing, and database populating, which greatly improved the relationship between the patients and the healthcare team. In addition, to our knowledge, our study is the first to report the impact of PCEB intervention on medication adherence and clinical outcomes in Chinese patients with IBD.

The limitations of this study must be addressed. First, this study had a retrospective observational design. However, data were prospectively collected. Second, the study was conducted at a single medical center for IBD. Despite this limitation, with widespread WeChat participation in China, PCEB management has the potential to become a viable intervention for IBD patients. Third, MARS‐5 was used to assess adherence to oral therapy. Although validated tools exist, there is a lack of tools and a consensus definition of inadequate adherence to biological therapies. To date, there is no gold standard set of medication adherence measures. Finally, medication adherence varied at different points in a particular disease timeline. To better evaluate patient adherence to biologics, we calculated the delayed dosing time not only according to each dose but also based on disease regimen activity.

In conclusion, we confirmed the strong positive impact of the PCEB telemedicine model on improving medication adherence and favoring long‐term treatment response in patients with IBD.
