In vitro evaluation of the antibacterial, antioxidant, and phytochemical investigation of Clematis simensis Fresen and Warburgia ugandensis Sprague in Ochollo, South Ethiopia
Authors: Muluken Gebi, Nebiyu Chali, Fitsum Dejene, Dawit Albenea, Daniel Woldeyes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) represents a critical global health crisis, threatening the efficacy of conventional antibiotics and challenging modern healthcare systems. In light of declining synthetic drug discovery, medicinal plants are gaining renewed attention as sustainable sources of novel bioactive compounds with antimicrobial and antioxidant potential. This study evaluated the phytochemical profile, antibacterial, and antioxidant activities of Clematis simensis (C. simensis) Fresen and Warburgia ugandensis (W. ugandensis) Sprague, two ethnomedicinal plants traditionally used in southern Ethiopia.MethodsA laboratory-based experimental study was conducted at Arba Minch University, Microbiology and Parasitology Laboratory, between March and August 2020. Fresh leaves of both plants were collected from Ochollo, Arba Minch Zuria Woreda. Qualitative and quantitative phytochemical analyses were performed, followed by antibacterial and antioxidant assays. The bioactive compounds in the methanolic extract of the most active plant, W. ugandensis, were further characterized using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS).ResultsMethanol extracts yielded higher crude extract percentages (18.4% for C. simensis) than petroleum ether extracts. Phytochemical screening revealed the presence of phenols, flavonoids, saponins, tannins, steroids, and terpenoids in both species, with methanol as the optimal solvent. The methanol extract of W. ugandensis exhibited the highest antibacterial activity, producing a 26.33 ± 0.44 mm inhibition zone against Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), followed by C. simensis (21.83 ± 0.44 mm against Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa). The petroleum ether extract of W. ugandensis showed the lowest activity (11.0 ± 0.58 mm against Listeria monocytogenes (L. monocytogenes). Methanol extracts showed strong antioxidant activity in the DPPH assay, with the lowest IC₅₀ for W. ugandensis (118.22 µg/mL), whereas petroleum ether extracts exhibited weaker activity (highest IC₅₀: 476.93 µg/mL). FTIR analysis identified functional groups such as aliphatic C–H, carbonyl (C = O), alkoxy (C–O), and C = C bonds. GC–MS analysis of W. ugandensis revealed key metabolites including squalene, hexadecanoic acid methyl ester, methyl 9, 10-octadecadienoate, methyl 12, 13-tetradecadienoate, and 1-isothiocyanato-3-methyladamantane.ConclusionsThe findings confirm that C. simensis and W. ugandensis possess a measurable antimicrobial and antioxidant activities, attributed to their diverse phytochemicals. These species may serve as potential sources of bioactive compounds and warrant further investigation through detailed mechanistic and in vivo studies.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Antibacterial activity, Antioxidant activity, Clematis Simensis, Warburgia Ugandensis

Introduction

Traditional medicine continues to play a vital role in healthcare around the world, especially in low- and middle-income countries where access to modern drugs and hospitals can be limited. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that Majority of people in developing regions rely on traditional herbal remedies as their main source of healthcare [1]. This widespread use of medicinal plants stems not only from cultural traditions and local knowledge, but also from how accessible these remedies are and the trust people place in them to treat both infectious and chronic illnesses [2]. Medicinal plants are widely acknowledged as valuable sources of bioactive compounds that form the foundation of many modern medicines. It is estimated that nearly 25–30% of all clinically prescribed drugs worldwide have their origins in plant-derived substances, underscoring the crucial role of natural products in pharmaceutical discovery and development [3, 4].

Across Africa, medicinal plants remain an essential part of primary healthcare systems. Some ethnobotanical research shows that many African communities have developed indigenous knowledge for treating a wide range of health conditions, including infections, inflammatory diseases, and disorders linked to oxidative stress [5, 6]. Ethiopia, renowned for its exceptional biodiversity and long-standing tradition of herbal medicine, is home to more than 6,500 species of vascular plants, over 12% of which are found nowhere else in the world [7]. Despite this remarkable heritage, only a small portion of Ethiopia’s medicinal flora has been scientifically explored for its pharmacological potential. Among the plants deeply rooted in traditional healing practices are Clematis simensis Fresen (Ranunculaceae) and Warburgia ugandensis Sprague (Canellaceae), both widely recognized for their use in managing microbial infections and related ailments [8], wounds, and inflammatory conditions [9].

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has become one of the most pressing global health challenges of the 21st century, threatening the effectiveness of existing treatments and reversing decades of medical progress. Recent global surveillance reports and burden estimates reveal a sharp increase in resistant pathogens, accompanied by a significant rise in morbidity and mortality worldwide [10]. Low and middle-income countries (LMICs) bear a disproportionate share of the antimicrobial resistance burden. This is largely due to the high prevalence of infectious diseases, under-resourced healthcare systems, limited diagnostic and laboratory capacity, and restricted access to second-line drugs all of which contribute to poorer clinical outcomes and hinder effective containment of resistant pathogens [11]. Worsening the crisis, the development of new antibiotics has failed to keep pace with the rapid emergence of resistant strains. This stagnation in the clinical pipeline has led to repeated global calls for renewed efforts in antimicrobial discovery, alongside increased investment in research, innovation, and stewardship programs to ensure sustainable use of existing drugs [12]. In this context, natural products particularly those derived from medicinal plants continue to represent a vital and historically rich source of novel antimicrobial compounds. Recent comprehensive reviews emphasize the enduring importance of plant-based secondary metabolites and soil-derived natural products as promising scaffolds for the development of next-generation antibacterial agents [3].

Beyond their antimicrobial potential, phytochemicals also play vital roles in antioxidant defense by scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) and protecting cells from oxidative stress induced damage. Such activity is particularly important in mitigating the onset and progression of chronic diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular disorders, and neurodegenerative conditions [13, 14]. Plant extracts rich in antioxidants often demonstrate synergistic therapeutic effects protecting host tissues from oxidative damage while simultaneously combating infections. This dual action enhances the overall pharmacological value of medicinal plants and underscores their potential as multifaceted therapeutic agents [15]. Consequently, the combined antibacterial and antioxidant properties of medicinal plants position them as promising candidates for modern drug development and for the scientific validation of traditional remedies through in vitro pharmacological studies.

Despite the long history of ethnomedicinal use of C. simensis and W. ugandensis in Ethiopia and across East Africa, systematic scientific validation of most medicinal plant bioactivities and chemical composition remains limited [16–18]. Most available studies are restricted to ethnobotanical surveys or preliminary screenings that lack detailed characterization of bioactive phytochemicals and their underlying antibacterial and antioxidant mechanisms [19]. This research gap is particularly evident in southern Ethiopia, especially in Ochollo, a biodiversity-rich region where traditional medicine continues to serve as a cornerstone of primary healthcare [18, 20]. Comprehensive phytochemical profiling, supported by spectroscopic and chromatographic techniques such as FTIR and GC–MS, is widely recognized as essential for identifying functional groups and volatile constituents associated with biological activity [21]. When integrated with in vitro antibacterial and antioxidant assays, these analytical approaches provide a systematic framework for investigating the pharmacological potential of medicinal plants and advancing natural product based drug discovery. Accordingly, this study aims to evaluate the antibacterial, antioxidant, and phytochemical properties of methanolic and petroleum ether extracts of C. simensis Fresen and W. ugandensis Sprague collected from Ochollo, southern Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Study area, period and design

Laboratory based experimental study was conducted at Arba Minch University in Microbiology and Parasitology Laboratory, from April 1, 2020-January 30, 2021.All experiments were performed in triplicate. The sampling area was located in Dega Ochollo, a rural community situated within the Arba Minch Zuria District of the Gamo Zone, Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples’ Region (SNNPR), Ethiopia. Dega Ochollo lies in the central highlands of the Rift Valley on the western escarpment of Lake Abaya. Geographically, the study area extends between approximately 37°35′ E and 37°37′ E longitude and 6°8′ N and 6°10′ N latitude, as shown in Fig. 1. The elevation ranges from 1,322 m to 2,189 m above sea level, covering an estimated area of about 9 km². The population consists of approximately 5000 people, living on top of hills and across steep slopes. The hills give rise to caves and crevices and the landscape is overall rocky and relatively densely vegetated [22, 23]. Like other Ethiopians, people of Ochollo have used several medicinal plants to treat various infectious diseases such as, diabetes, cutaneous leishmaniasis and other skin related disease. Instrumentation and reagent

In this study, analytical precision was prioritized by using high-quality instruments and reagent-grade chemicals for the accurate characterization of bioactive constituents from Warburgia ugandensis and Clematis simensis. The main instruments employed included a UV–Visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800) for absorbance readings during antioxidant and phytochemical assays, a Fourier Transform Infrared Spectrometer (Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50ABX) for detecting functional groups, and a Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometer (Agilent 8890 A GC linked with 5977B MSD) for analyzing volatile compounds. Extraction and quantitative tests were carried out using analytical-grade solvents and reagents such as methanol, petroleum ether, n-hexane, ethyl acetate, and Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Phytochemical and antioxidant analyses utilized ferric chloride, aluminum chloride, Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, sodium carbonate, 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), gallic acid, and quercetin. Microbiological evaluations were performed on Mueller–Hinton agar and broth, with gentamicin serving as the positive control and DMSO as the negative control. All reagents were procured from reputable suppliers (Sigma–Aldrich, USA, and Merck, Germany), ensuring consistency and reproducibility throughout the experiments.

Plant material collection and identification

As shown in Fig. 2, fresh and disease-free leaves of Clematis simensis Fresen and Warburgia ugandensis Sprague were collected from Ochollo, in the Arba Minch Zuria district of southern Ethiopia, during February and April 2020. Collection of those plant materials were conducted after the necessary permission was granted from Arba Minch University Research Committee.

A: C. simensis Fresen and B: W. ugandensis Sprague (photographs taken at the study site by the principal investigator)

The specimens were promptly transported to the Department of Biology at Arba Minch University for laboratory processing. The leaves were air-dried under shade at ambient temperature (25–30 °C) for approximately one month, placed on clean newspaper sheets to avoid direct sunlight and loss of volatile compounds. Once dried, the samples were separately ground into a fine powder using an electric grinder, labeled appropriately in polyethylene bags, and stored until extraction. The plant species were identified by a botanist, Mulugeta Kibebew Robi, an assistant professor at Arba Minch University, Abaya Campus, using the taxonomic keys and descriptions provided in the Flora of Ethiopia and Eritrea series [24, 25]. The taxonomic identification of both plant species was further validated at the National Herbarium, Department of Biology, College of Natural and Computational Sciences, Addis Ababa University. Voucher specimens were prepared and deposited in the Botanical Laboratory of Arba Minch University, Abaya Campus, for future reference. Clematis simensis Fresen (Family: Ranunculaceae), a climbing plant locally known as Soggo in the Ochollo area, was represented in this study by its leaf samples and assigned the voucher number CS-2026. Likewise, Warburgia ugandensis Sprague (Family: Canellaceae), a shrubby medicinal plant locally referred to as Gesso, was collected from the same locality, and its specimen was recorded under voucher number WU-2045.

Plant sample description

W. ugandensis Sprague (family Canellaceae), commonly known as the “East African greenheart” or “Uganda greenheart,” is a well-documented medicinal tree native to eastern and southern Africa. It has been traditionally used across Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, and Ethiopia to treat a range of ailments, including bacterial and fungal infections, neglected tropical diseases such as leishmaniasis and malaria, as well as fever, toothache, and respiratory disorders like coughs [26–28].

C. simensis Fresen (family Ranunculaceae) is an endemic climbing plant predominantly found in the Ethiopian highlands, especially across the southern and southwestern regions. In traditional Ethiopian medicine, different parts of the plant—particularly the leaves and roots—are widely used to treat wounds, respiratory infections, rheumatism, skin ailments, and inflammatory conditions [29].

Plant crude extraction

Approximately 1.5 kg of plant material from each species was air-dried under shade for 20–30 days at ambient environmental temperatures ranging between 27 and 37 °C. The dried samples were then pulverized into fine powder using an electric grinder. A 25 g portion of each powdered sample was successively extracted with petroleum ether and methanol using a Soxhlet apparatus for 1.5 h [30, 31]. The resulting extracts were concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator (RSO 400, Germany). Residual solvent traces were removed by allowing the concentrated extracts to stand in evaporating dishes at room temperature for two days. Phytochemical screening and characterization

Preliminary qualitative phytochemical screening was carried out on the crude leaf extracts of Clematis simensis Fresen and Warburgia ugandensis Sprague to identify the presence of major classes of bioactive secondary metabolites. The analysis focused on detecting compounds such as phenols, flavonoids, saponins, alkaloids, steroids, glycosides, tannins, terpenoids, anthraquinones, diterpenes, reducing sugars, proteins, and amino acids using a 100 mg/mL stock solution. All assays were conducted following standard and widely accepted phytochemical screening protocols described in established literature [33–39] .

Quantitative analysis of crude extracts

Determination of total phenol

The total phenolic content of each extract was determined using the Folin Ciocalteu colorimetric method with slight modifications from the standard protocol. In brief, 0.5 mg of each crude extract was dissolved in 2 mL of methanol. A 10% Folin Ciocalteu reagent was prepared by diluting 10 mL of the stock solution with 90 mL of distilled water, while a 5% sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃) solution was obtained by dissolving 3 g of Na₂CO₃ in 50 mL of distilled water. For the assay, 0.5 mL of each extract was mixed with 1.5 mL of the diluted Folin Ciocalteu reagent and kept in the dark for 5 min. Subsequently, 1.5 mL of the 5% Na₂CO₃ solution was added, and the mixture was incubated again in the dark for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 725 nm using a UV–Visible spectrophotometer to quantify the total phenolic content [40–42]. A calibration curve was constructed using gallic acid as the reference standard at concentrations ranging from 10 to 100 µg/mL. A reagent blank containing methanol in place of the extract was prepared under identical conditions and used for baseline correction. Absorbance values were plotted against gallic acid concentrations, and linear regression analysis yielded the equation y = 0.0014x + 0.5423 (R² = 0.9955). Total phenolic content (TPC) of the extracts was calculated from this regression equation and expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents per gram of dry extract (mg GAE/g). All measurements were performed in triplicate, and results were reported as mean ± standard deviation.

Determination of total flavonoid content

The total flavonoid content of each extract was determined using the aluminum chloride colorimetric assay, following standard procedures with minor modifications. Briefly, 0.5 mL of each extract was mixed with 1.5 mL of 80% methanol, followed by the addition of 0.1 mL of 10% aluminum chloride, 0.1 mL of 0.1 M sodium acetate, and 2.8 mL of distilled water. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min to allow for color development. Absorbance was then measured at 415 nm using a UV–Visible spectrophotometer against a reagent blank. A quercetin stock solution (500 ppm) was used to prepare the calibration curve through serial dilutions of 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, and 300 ppm. The total flavonoid content (TFC) of each extract was expressed as milligrams of quercetin equivalents per gram of extract (mg QE/g). Antioxidant activity assay

The antioxidant potential of the leaf extracts of Clematis simensis Fresen and Warburgia ugandensis Sprague was evaluated using the 2, 2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) free radical scavenging assay. The procedure followed the method described by Dehshahri et al. [46] with slight modifications. In this assay, 500 µL of a 0.2 mM DPPH solution prepared in methanol was mixed with 500 µL of each extract at different concentrations ranging from 40 to 200 ppm. A mixture containing methanol and DPPH alone served as the control. The reaction mixtures were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min to prevent photo-degradation of DPPH radicals. Absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a UV–Visible spectrophotometer. Gallic acid was used as the reference antioxidant (positive control) and was tested under the same experimental conditions at concentrations ranging from 40 to 200 ppm. Antibacterial activity assay

Test microorganisms

The bacterial strains employed in this study were obtained from the Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI). Four standard reference organisms were selected based on their clinical significance and frequent association with secondary infections: Escherichia coli (E. coli) (ATCC 25922), Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) (ATCC 29213), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) (ATCC 27853), and Listeria monocytogenes (L. monocytogenes) (ATCC 19115). These strains are recommended quality control reference organisms for antimicrobial susceptibility testing according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines [48].

Preparations of plant extract solutions

Each plant extract was prepared for bioassay by dissolving 1 g of the crude material in 1 mL of 0.5% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma Aldrich, USA) inside sterile, pre-labelled universal bottles. The mixtures were homogenized using a vortex mixer until complete dissolution was achieved, yielding a stock concentration of 1 g/mL. The same procedure was followed for all plant extract fractions prior to antibacterial testing [49].

Agar well diffusion method

Antibacterial screening of methanolic and petroleum-ether extracts of C. simensis and W. ugandensis was carried out using the agar well diffusion technique as described by Balouiri et al. [50]., Mostafa et al. [51]., and Yemata et al. [52]. With minor modifications. Mueller Hinton agar (MHA) was prepared according to the manufacturer’s specifications by dissolving the required amount of medium in distilled water, heating with stirring until completely liquefied, and autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 min. The sterilized medium was then cooled to 45–50 °C in a water bath, poured aseptically (25 mL per plate) into sterile 90 mm Petri dishes, and allowed to solidify.

Bacterial inocula were standardized to 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL, corresponding to 0.5 McFarland turbidity standards [53]. Using sterile cotton swabs, each test organism was evenly spread over the agar surface under aseptic conditions and the plates were left to dry for approximately 30 min. Wells of 6 mm diameter were then bored into the agar using a sterile cork borer, ensuring uniform spacing between wells. Four extract concentrations (1000, 750, 500, and 250 mg/mL) were prepared in DMSO, and 100 µL of each solution was dispensed into the designated wells using a micropipette. DMSO served as the negative control, whereas gentamicin (10 mg/mL) was used as the positive reference antibiotic. The plates were kept at ambient temperature for 2 h to facilitate diffusion of the test substances and subsequently incubated at 37 °C for 24 h.

Following incubation, the plates were examined for clear inhibition zones surrounding the wells, indicating antibacterial activity. The diameters of these zones were measured in millimetres using a Vernier caliper. All experiments were conducted in triplicate for each bacterial strain, and the mean inhibition zone diameters were calculated for analysis in accordance with CLSI M100 [54] guidelines.

Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

Extracts that exhibited measurable antibacterial activity in the agar well diffusion assay were further analyzed to determine their minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) using the macro-broth dilution method, following the standard protocol [55] and Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines [56]. A stock solution of each active extract was prepared by dissolving 250 mg of the sample in 1 mL of DMSO. Two-fold serial dilutions were then performed in sterile Mueller-Hinton broth (MHB) to obtain a range of concentrations: 125, 62.5, 31.25, 15.62, 7.81, 3.9, and 1.9 mg/mL.

The bacterial inoculum was standardized according to CLSI (2015) by adjusting a fresh culture to 0.5 McFarland turbidity, equivalent to approximately 1.5 × 10⁶–10⁸ CFU/mL, and then diluted at a 1:4 ratio in MHB to achieve the final working concentration. Within 15 min of standardization, 1 mL of the prepared bacterial suspension was added to each test tube containing 1 mL of extract dilution, except for the negative control. The negative control contained only MHB, while the positive control included MHB inoculated with bacteria but no extract. The final bacterial density in each tube was approximately 1.5 × 10⁶ CFU/mL.

All tubes were incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h. Following incubation, bacterial growth was evaluated visually by the presence of turbidity, and the absence of turbidity indicated inhibition. Absorbance at 600 nm was also recorded using a spectrophotometer for confirmation. The MIC was defined as the lowest extract concentration that completely inhibited visible bacterial growth. Each test was conducted in triplicate for all bacterial species, and mean MIC values were calculated.

Determination of minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

After the MIC determination, the minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) was evaluated to confirm bactericidal activity. Samples from tubes showing no visible growth in the MIC assay were sub cultured on freshly prepared Mueller Hinton agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Plates were then examined for the presence or absence of bacterial colonies. The lowest concentration that produced no visible growth was recorded as the MBC (Mathur, 2013). All MBC tests were performed in triplicate for each microorganism to ensure reproducibility [55, 57].

Isolation and purification of bioactive compounds

Based on the results of phytochemical and biological analyses, the methanolic leaf extract of Warburgia ugandensis—which demonstrated the highest phenolic and flavonoid contents as well as the strongest antioxidant and antibacterial effects—was selected for further fractionation. The extract was successively separated using a combination of hexane and ethyl acetate as the eluting solvents. Column chromatography was employed to fractionate the extract, followed by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) for purification and profile determination.

Antibacterial activity of semi-fractionated compounds

In addition to the crude extracts, selected semi-fractionated compounds (F14, F20, and F25) obtained from column chromatography of the methanolic extract of Warburgia ugandensis were evaluated for antibacterial activity using the agar well diffusion protocol described above [50, 51, 58]. Briefly, each fraction was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) at concentrations of 3.0 mg/mL and 1.5 mg/mL. A volume of 100 µL of each solution was dispensed into 6 mm wells bored into Mueller–Hinton agar plates previously inoculated with standardized bacterial suspensions (1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL; 0.5 McFarland standard). DMSO at equivalent volumes served as the negative control to exclude solvent effects, while gentamicin (10 mg/mL) was included as a reference antibacterial agent under identical experimental conditions. Plates were allowed to stand at room temperature for 2 h to permit diffusion before incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. Following incubation, inhibition zone diameters were measured in millimetres using a Vernier caliper. All assays were performed in triplicate. The diffusion assay was used as a preliminary qualitative screening tool to assess relative antibacterial activity of the fractions and was not intended for direct potency equivalence comparison with the reference antibiotic.

Column chromatography (CC)

Fractionation of the crude methanolic extract of W. ugandensis was carried out using a silica-gel column (60–120 mesh size) as the stationary phase. The column was first packed by preparing slurry of silica gel in n-hexane. A mixture containing 12 g of the dried extract and 12 g of silica gel was blended thoroughly with n-hexane and gently loaded on top of the packed column. Elution was performed using gradient mixtures of n-hexane and ethyl acetate of increasing polarity. The mobile phase was allowed to pass through the column under gravity, assisted by gentle air pressure when necessary.

Compounds within the extract interacted differently with the stationary and mobile phases depending on their polarity and affinity, resulting in differential migration and separation. Each fraction was collected sequentially for further chromatographic analysis [59]. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) profiling

Thin-layer chromatography was employed to analyze and purify the fractions obtained from the column separation. TLC plates (5 × 20 cm, 0.5 mm thickness) coated with silica gel were used, following the method described by Hemavathy [60] and Yessuf [61]. Each fraction was carefully spotted on the baseline using a fine capillary tube. Plates were developed in various ratios of n-hexane and ethyl acetate with progressively increasing polarity.

After development, the plates were air-dried and visualized under ultraviolet light at 254 nm and 365 nm. To visualize non-UV-active compounds, the developed TLC plates were sprayed with vanillin–sulfuric acid reagent and gently heated to enhance color development. Ferric chloride reagent was applied for the detection of phenolic compounds, and aluminum chloride reagent was used to identify flavonoids. These visualization methods allowed detection of additional phytochemical constituents not visible under UV light. Solvent combinations that produced distinct and well-resolved spots such as n-hexane: ethyl acetate ratios of 9.5:0.5, 7:3, 4:1, and 1:1 were identified as optimal for further purification. Characterization of fractionated compounds from W. ugandensis

The identification and structural characterization of bioactive components obtained from the fractionated methanolic extract of W. ugandensis were conducted at the laboratories of Addis Ababa Science and Technology University (AASTU) and JIJE Lab-Galss Pvt. Ltd., Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Both Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT–IR) and Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS) were utilized to determine the functional groups and molecular constituents present in the active fractions.

Fourier transform infrared (FT–IR) spectroscopic analysis

Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was employed to identify the major functional groups present in the fractionated plant extract. A small portion of the semi-solid sample was directly applied to the sample holder of an FT-IR spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50ABX, USA) without any prior chemical treatment. Spectral measurements were obtained within the mid-infrared range of 400–4000 cm⁻¹ at a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹, following established protocols for plant extract analysis. The resulting spectra, recorded in transmittance mode, were analyzed to identify characteristic absorption bands corresponding to specific functional groups using standard IR correlation charts and reference spectral libraries. These bands were then interpreted to determine the major classes of phytochemicals such as alcohols, carboxylic acids, alkanes, and esters present in the extract. This analytical approach aligns with widely accepted FT-IR profiling techniques commonly applied in the study of medicinal plants [62, 63].

Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis

The methanolic leaf extract of W. ugandensis was subjected to GC–MS profiling to identify its principal phytoconstituents. An Agilent 8890 A gas chromatograph coupled with a 5977B mass selective detector (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was employed. A HP-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 μm film thickness) served as the stationary phase. Helium (99.995% purity) was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL min⁻¹. The injector was operated in splitless mode, with 1 µL of sample (1% w/v in suitable solvent) introduced. The column oven temperature programme was set as follows: initial temperature of 50 °C (hold 1 min), ramp at 3 °C min⁻¹ to 150 °C (hold 10 min), then increase at 10 °C min⁻¹ to 300 °C (hold 5 min). The mass spectrometer operated in electron-impact (EI) ionisation mode at 70 eV, with full scan acquisition range m/z 50–550.Chromatographic peaks were identified by comparing their retention times and mass spectra with entries in the NIST/EPA/NIH mass spectral library. Quantitative proportions of each component were calculated as the percentage of total ion current (TIC) peak area. The procedure employed in this investigation was adapted from standard protocols with minor modifications [64–66]. Generally, the overall workflow of this research project was summarized in Fig. 3.

Data analysis

All quantitative data generated from phytochemical, antioxidant, and antibacterial assays were statistically analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), OriginPro 2025 (version 9.5; OriginLab, USA), and Microsoft Excel 2016. Experimental values were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Each measurement was performed in triplicate, and the average value was considered for statistical interpretation. Differences among means were regarded as statistically significant at a 95% confidence level (p < 0.05).

Percentage of crude extract

The percentage yield of leaf extracts from C. simensis and W. ugandensis was determined using the standard equation described in Sect. 3.2.2. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the petroleum ether extract of W. ugandensis exhibited the lowest yield (3.13%) compared to both extracts of C. simensis and the methanol extract of W. ugandensis. The highest yield was recorded for the methanol extract of C. simensis (18.4%). Overall, methanol extraction produced a greater quantity of crude extract than petroleum ether, indicating that the bioactive components of both plant species are predominantly polar in nature.

Qualitative phytochemical analysis

In the present study, as illustrated in the heatmap (Fig. 5), the methanol extract of C. simensis revealed the presence of several phytochemical classes, including phenols, saponins, flavonoids, steroids, tannins, terpenoids, and diterpenoids. Phenols, saponins, flavonoids, steroids, tannins, and terpenoids were commonly detected in both plant species when extracted with methanol. However, alkaloids and anthraquinones were not detected in either plant under both solvent extraction methods. Determination of minimum inhibitory and bactericidal concentrations

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of the plant extracts was determined using the broth dilution method, while the minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) was assessed by subculturing on agar plates. As presented in Tables 2 and 3, MIC and MBC values of the crude extracts of C. simensis and W. ugandensis varied across the tested bacterial isolates.

Among the C. simensis leaf extracts, the methanolic extract (Cm) exhibited the lowest MIC against P. aeruginosa (1.95 mg/mL), with a corresponding MBC of 3.95 mg/mL. Higher MIC values were observed against S. aureus (15.6 mg/mL) and E. coli (32.25 mg/mL), while both MIC and MBC for L. monocytogenes were 7.9 mg/mL. For most extract–bacteria combinations, the MBC/MIC ratios were ≤ 4, indicating bactericidal activity under the tested in vitro conditions. These MIC and MBC values provide a quantitative assessment of antibacterial activity, whereas diffusion assays were considered preliminary screening only.

FTIR analysis

Based on the characteristic absorption peaks observed in the infrared (IR) region, the FTIR spectrum was employed to identify the functional groups of the active constituents present in the methanolic leaf extract of Warburgia ugandensis. When the extract was analyzed using FTIR, distinct absorption bands corresponding to various functional groups were detected, reflecting the chemical diversity of the bioactive compounds. The FTIR findings were consistent with the results of the qualitative phytochemical screening, confirming the presence of phenols, flavonoids, terpenoids, tannins, and saponins.

As illustrated in Fig. 10, the FTIR spectrum of the Warburgia ugandensis fraction revealed prominent absorption bands at 2958.32, 2923.60, 2859.96, 1749.1, 1465.66, 1238, 1049.1, 887.1, and 724.44 cm⁻¹, indicating the presence of characteristic functional groups. The strong peaks at 2958.32, 2923.60, and 2859.96 cm⁻¹ correspond to aliphatic C–H stretching vibrations typical of long-chain hydrocarbons. The absorption band at 1749.1 cm⁻¹ is attributed to ester carbonyl (C = O) stretching, while the peaks at 1238 and 1049.1 cm⁻¹ correspond to C–O stretching vibrations. These spectral features are consistent with the presence of ester-containing fatty acid derivatives and hydrocarbon compounds identified by GC–MS analysis, including squalene, hexadecanoic acid methyl ester, methyl 9,10-octadecadienoate, and methyl 12,13-tetradecadienoate.

GC-MS analysis of identification compounds

Gas Chromatography Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis was performed on the purified fractions of Warburgia ugandensis methanol extract to tentatively characterize the major constituents. The chromatographic peaks obtained were integrated and compared against reference spectra in the GC–MS library database for preliminary compound assignment. Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) profiles of the methanol extract were correlated with the GC–MS results to support the presence of separated constituents; however, definitive structural confirmation was not performed.

Analysis of fraction twenty (F20) of W. ugandensis revealed several compounds tentatively identified based on peak area percentage and spectral similarity indices. As shown in Fig. 11, the most abundant putatively identified compounds included squalene, hexadecanoic acid methyl ester, methyl 9, 10-octadecadienoate, methyl 12, 13-tetradecadienoate, and 1-isothiocyanato-3-methyladamantane. Although these compounds have been previously reported in the literature to possess antibacterial and antioxidant properties, their identification in the present study is based solely on GC–MS library matching. No co-injection with authentic standards, NMR confirmation, or bioactivity testing of isolated compounds was performed. Therefore, the detected constituents should be interpreted as tentatively assigned compounds, and the observed biological activities cannot be attributed to individual components without further validation.

Discussion

This study investigated the pharmacological applications and analytical profiles of the medicinal plants C. simensis and W. ugandensis, with particular focus on their antibacterial and antioxidant properties. In the first phase of our research, we examined the percentage yield of crude extracts obtained with two solvents: methanol and petroleum ether. According to our findings, the petroleum ether extract of W. ugandensis exhibited the lowest yield (3.12%) compared to both extracts of C. simensis and the methanol extract of W. ugandensis. The highest yield was recorded for the methanol extract of C. simensis (18.4%). Overall, methanol extraction produced measurable amount of crude extract than petroleum ether extract. This is attributed to the fact that methanol is a polar solvent, allowing it to dissolve a wider range of polar and semi-polar compounds compared to non-polar solvents like petroleum ether. A study conducted at Ton Duc Thang University, Vietnam, supports our results, reporting that methanol extracts have a higher affinity for phytochemical components, yielding greater extract quantities from Severinia buxifolia compared to other solvents such as ethanol, acetone, and water [67]. Similarly, previous studies have reported that methanol-based extraction often yields higher quantities of phytochemicals than less polar solvents. This is attributed to the higher polarity of methanol, which facilitates the dissolution of a broader spectrum of bioactive compounds compared with non-polar solvents such as petroleum ether [68].

A qualitative phytochemical analysis of C. simensis and W. ugandensis revealed that phenols, saponins, flavonoids, steroids, tannins, and terpenoids were commonly present in both species when extracted with methanol. In contrast, alkaloids and anthraquinones were absent in both plants across all solvent extraction methods. Steroids were detected exclusively in C. simensis under both extraction conditions. A related study conducted at Addis Ababa University reported comparable findings, showing that the methanolic extract of C. simensis contained flavonoids, terpenoids, saponins, tannins, and phenolic compounds [9]. Likewise, research by Denis Okello and Youngmin Kang demonstrated that extracts of W. ugandensis possess strong antimicrobial properties, largely attributed to their high content of terpenoids particularly drimane- and coloratane-type sesquiterpenoids as well as tannins, flavonoids, saponins, and steroids [69].

The quantitative analysis of both plant extracts also revealed that the methanol extract of C. simensis contained 1.17 mg GAE/g of total phenolics and 1.12 mg QE/g of total flavonoids, whereas the methanol extract of W. ugandensis exhibited comparatively higher values of 3.23 mg GAE/g and 2.55 mg QE/g, respectively. These results indicate that W. ugandensis possesses a greater concentration of phenolic and flavonoid compounds under similar extraction conditions. A study conducted by Teshome et al. [9] supports this general trend, reporting higher phenolic and flavonoid contents in extracts obtained using more polar solvents such as methanol consistent with our current findings [9]. A study conducted in eastern Uganda similarly reported higher phenolic and flavonoid content in W. ugandensis extracts compared with other medicinal plants processed under comparable extraction conditions [70]. In conclusion, methanol seems a better solvent for extracting phenolic and flavonoid compounds from medicinal plants, and that W. ugandensis may possess much more concentration of pharmacologically active metabolites.

The antioxidant activity of our plant extracts followed a similar trend to that reported in other studies, showing clear differences depending on the extraction solvent used. As expected, the methanolic extracts of both medicinal plants exhibited markedly higher antioxidant activity compared to the petroleum ether extracts, as present in Fig. 6. Specifically, the methanol extract of W. ugandensis demonstrated the relatively higher free radical scavenging capacity, ranging from 6.77% to 87.79%, followed by the methanol extract of C. simensis (8.22–56%). In contrast, the petroleum ether extracts of W. ugandensis and C. simensis displayed relatively lower scavenging activities, ranging from 14.77 to 27.80% and 11.38–27.28%, respectively. Comparable results have been reported elsewhere, where more polar solvents particularly aqueous or methanolic extracts were shown to yield higher total phenolic content and stronger antioxidant potential than less polar solvents [71]. Likewise, a study published in BMC Research Notes confirmed that methanolic extracts contained higher levels of phenolic compounds and exhibited stronger antioxidant activity in DPPH assays compared to extracts obtained with other solvents [72]. Overall, these findings indicate that methanol extracts are rich in phytochemicals such as phenols and flavonoids, which play a crucial role in scavenging DPPH radicals and enhancing antioxidant capacity.

A study regarding the antibacterial activity of C. simensis has also shown a similar trend of superiority, where the methanolic extract exhibited larger inhibition zones compared to the petroleum ether extract. Consistent with our findings, the crude extracts demonstrated activity against both Gram-positive (S. aureus and L. monocytogenes) and Gram-negative (E. coli and P. aeruginosa) bacteria using the agar well diffusion method (Fig. 7).The methanol extract of W. ugandensis exhibited the strongest antibacterial activity, producing an inhibition zone of 26.33 ± 0.44 mm against S. aureus, followed by the methanol extract of C. simensis, which showed 25 ± 0.44 mm inhibitions against P. aeruginosa. Conversely, the petroleum ether extract of W. ugandensis displayed the least activity, with an inhibition zone of 11.0 ± 0.58 mm against L. monocytogenes. Similar investigations have shown that more polar extracts (e.g., ethanol, methanol) of medicinal plants frequently exhibit stronger antibacterial activity than non-polar solvent extracts (e.g., petroleum ether) [73]. In other study, plant-species like W. ugandensis and C. simensis display significant antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus, C. albicans and other bacterial species with inhibition zones ranging from 6 to 13 mm in laboratory tests [17, 74]. Similar to its antioxidant potential, the methanol extract rich in diverse phytochemical compounds has significantly contributed to this remarkable biological activity.

Our study has also showed the MIC and MBC values of the selected plant extracts. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of the two plant extracts was determined using the broth dilution method, while the MBC was assessed on agar plates to evaluate the bactericidal efficacy of the active extracts from C. simensis and W. ugandensis. The MIC and MBC values obtained from the crude extracts of C. simensis Fresen and W. ugandensis Sprague indicate that among the C. simensis leaf extracts the methanolic extract exhibited the lowest MIC value of 1.95 mg/mL against P. aeruginosa, suggesting effective growth inhibition at comparatively low concentration. In contrast, against S. aureus the MIC and MBC were higher (15.60 mg/mL and 32.25 mg/mL), respectively, illustrating the well-recognized phenomenon of differential susceptibility between Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. These findings align with previous research on these species. For example, a recent systematic review reported that W. ugandensis extracts display broad‐spectrum antimicrobial activities, which have been attributed to the presence of abundant terpenoids, flavonoids and sesquiterpenoids in the plant [69]. Moreover, several investigations of C. simensis have shown measurable antibacterial activity of methanolic leaf extracts (though often at higher MIC values than what we observed) [75]. The relatively lower MIC value recorded for the methanolic extract of C. simensis against P. aeruginosa underscores the importance of solvent polarity in the extraction of bioactive constituents. A similar phenomenon has been reported in studies on sister species, C. hirsuta, where methanolic extracts exhibited higher antibacterial activity against pathogenic bacteria, achieving inhibition at lower concentrations compared to extracts obtained with less polar solvents [76].

The chromatographic fractionation of the methanolic extract of W. ugandensis successfully separated bioactive compounds with distinct physicochemical properties. In the initial elution phases (fractions F1–F12), no visible compounds were detected, suggesting the predominance of solvent residues and non-pigmented components. However, from fraction F13 onward, the emergence of distinct colored bands indicated the elution of phytochemicals active constituents. Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) served as a rapid and reliable analytical tool to monitor separation efficiency and purity, consistent with its established role in phytochemical analysis [77]. The solvent systems composed of n-hexane and ethyl acetate at varying ratios provided optimal resolution of the target compounds. Among the 25 fractions collected, only F14, F20, and F25 yielded single, well-defined spots under UV detection at 254 nm and 365 nm, confirming their relative purity. The Rf values of 0.62, 0.76, and 0.81, respectively, further indicated chemical diversity among these fractions, likely to represent different classes of terpenoids and phenolic derivatives.

Following chromatographic purification, the methanolic extract of W. ugandensis was analysed by FTIR to identify its functional groups (Fig. 10). The spectrum showed major absorption bands at 2958.32, 2923.60, and 2859.96 cm⁻¹, corresponding to aliphatic C–H stretching vibrations, indicating the presence of long-chain hydrocarbons such as squalene. A prominent peak at 1749 cm⁻¹ was assigned to C = O stretching of ester carbonyl groups, while the band at 1238 cm⁻¹ represented C–O stretching vibrations, confirming the presence of ester linkages typically found in compounds such as hexadecanoic acid methyl ester and methyl linoleate. In addition to these major peaks, absorption bands at 1465.66, 1049.1, 887.1, and 724.44 cm⁻¹ were attributed to C = C bending and sp² C–H out-of-plane vibrations, consistent with the occurrence of unsaturated esters and alkene groups. Similar findings have been reported in previous research, where FTIR spectroscopy successfully identified comparable functional groups in methanolic plant extracts rich in phenolic, flavonoid, and terpenoid compounds [78, 79]. As emphasized by Sumantri et al. [78], FTIR spectral fingerprints provide reliable evidence of phytochemical diversity and complement conventional qualitative analyses. Therefore, the FTIR results obtained in this study reinforce our phytochemical screening data, confirming the coexistence of phenolic, flavonoid, terpenoid, tannin, and saponin compounds that collectively contribute to the biological activities of W. ugandensis.

Our GC-MS analysis of the methanolic extract of W. ugandensis revealed several prominent bioactive compounds, including squalene, hexadecanoic acid methyl ester, methyl 9, 10-octadecadienoate, and methyl 12, 13-tetradecadienoate, each identified through comparison of chromatographic peaks with library reference spectra (Fig. 11). These bioactive compounds are widely recognized for their antimicrobial and antioxidant potential. For instance, hexadecanoic acid methyl ester has been shown to possess notable antibacterial, antioxidant, and immunostimulant properties [80]. Similarly, detailed GC-MS profiling of W. ugandensis by Abuto et al. [81] reported that fatty-acid derivatives (9–23%) and sesquiterpenoids constitute key chemical components of the plant, aligning closely with our observations. The detection of squalene, a well-known antioxidant hydrocarbon, further reinforces the functional diversity and therapeutic relevance of the extract. The presence of these metabolites explains the antibacterial and antioxidant effects of W. ugandensis, validating that its medicinal properties stem from a rich profile of fatty acid esters, hydrocarbons, and terpenoid-derived molecules. Conclusion

This study demonstrated that C. simensis Fresen and W. ugandensis Sprague, both traditionally used in Ethiopian herbal medicine, possess significant antibacterial and antioxidant properties. Phytochemical screening confirmed the presence of key bioactive compounds—phenols, flavonoids, tannins, saponins, terpenoids, and steroids—most probably responsible for the observed biological activities. Both plants exhibited comparable antibactrial effects, with S. aureus and P. aeruginosa showing relatively higher susceptibility, while E. coli showed resistance. Notably, the methanolic extract of W. ugandensis displayed relatively stronger antibacterial potency and lowest MIC values, outperforming the petroleum ether extract. The pronounced antioxidant activity and the presence of squalene and other potent metabolites suggest that methanolic extract of W. ugandensis could be a promising natural source for developing plant-based therapeutic agents. Overall, these findings support the potential of these species as complementary alternatives to conventional antibiotics and as valuable leads for future drug formulation targeting resistant pathogens and oxidative stress–related disorders.
