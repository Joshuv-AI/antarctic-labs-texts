Acute exacerbation of interstitial lung disease in a patient with chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy: A case report
Authors: Koharu Harada, Takunori Ogawa, Ryosuke Nagaoka, Akihiko Kawana, Yoshifumi Kimizuka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP) is a rare autoimmune neuropathy, and its association with interstitial lung disease (ILD) is extremely uncommon. Herein, we report the case of a 65-year-old woman with a long-standing history of CIDP who presented with simultaneous relapse of CIDP and acute exacerbation of ILD. The ILD exacerbation was resistant to high-dose corticosteroids, with worsening respiratory symptoms and progressive ground-glass opacities on imaging. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), administered 10 days after steroid initiation, resulted in significant clinical and radiological improvement in both ILD and neuropathy. This is the first reported case of acute exacerbation of CIDP-related ILD successfully treated with IVIG, which suggests a potential relationship between CIDP and ILD and suggests that IVIG may be a viable therapeutic option for steroid-resistant ILD exacerbations in patients with CIDP.

Introduction

Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP) is an autoimmune-mediated disorder characterized by progressive or relapsing-remitting motor and sensory dysfunction due to peripheral nerve demyelination [1]. Interstitial lung disease (ILD) is more commonly linked to autoimmune diseases, such as connective tissue diseases (CTDs) [2], but only a few reports have described the coexistence of ILD with CIDP, and none have documented acute exacerbation [3,4]. Given that acute exacerbations of ILD are associated with rapid respiratory deterioration and high mortality [5], the identification of new clinical contexts wherein they arise has important clinical implications.

Here, we present the first reported case of a patient with CIDP who developed an acute exacerbation of ILD coinciding with a CIDP relapse. Notably, the exacerbation of ILD was resistant to high-dose steroids but responded dramatically to intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), a first-line treatment for CIDP. This case highlights a potential relationship between CIDP and ILD and suggests IVIG as a possible therapeutic option for steroid-resistant CIDP-related ILD exacerbations.

Case presentation

A 65-year-old woman, a never-smoker, was admitted to our hospital due to progressive bilateral limb weakness and sensory disturbances. Nerve conduction studies (NSC) showed prolonged motor distal latency with conduction block and slowed sensory conduction in the median and tibial nerves, which were consistent with demyelinating polyneuropathy. Magnetic resonance imaging findings were unremarkable, while cerebrospinal fluid analysis revealed elevated protein levels. Based on a strong clinical suspicion of CIDP or Guillain-Barré syndrome, the patient was treated with IVIG (0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days), resulting in significant improvement in muscle weakness and sensory deficits. Maintenance therapy with oral prednisolone was initiated at a dose of 30 mg/day, but the patient experienced several relapses. These recurrent episodes culminated in a definitive diagnosis of CIDP. Over time, the frequency of exacerbations decreased, and the prednisolone was gradually tapered to 10 mg/day, effectively controlling her condition.

Seven years after the initial diagnosis of CIDP, a chest computed tomography (CT) image for health checkups revealed basal predominant reticulation with traction bronchiectasis (Fig. 1A). Eight years later, the patient presented with progressive dyspnea, exertional desaturation, and weakness of the bilateral upper and lower limbs. Chest CT demonstrated diffuse ground-glass opacities superimposed on preexisting ILD, indicating an acute exacerbation of ILD (Fig. 1B). There were no recent medication changes and no significant history of inhalation exposure. Serum autoimmune test results were unremarkable (Table 1). Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) analysis revealed a lymphocytic cellular pattern. No pathogens were detected in either the BALF or blood cultures. NCS were performed to evaluate the progressive weakness in the upper and lower limbs that developed simultaneously with respiratory symptoms. The results revealed prolonged motor and sensory conduction velocities, suggesting a relapse of CIDP. For the treatment of the acute exacerbation of ILD and the relapse of CIDP, high-dose methylprednisolone therapy (1000 mg/day for 3 days) followed by prednisolone (1 mg/kg/day) was initiated. IVIG is the first-line treatment for CIDP; it induces an earlier therapeutic effect compared to steroids [1]. In the present case, the acute exacerbation of ILD developed concurrently with the relapse of CIDP, suggesting a potential relationship between ILD and CIDP. Therefore, for the treatment of the steroid-resistant acute exacerbation of ILD related to CIDP, IVIG (0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days) was administered 10 days after the initiation of high-dose corticosteroid therapy, instead of adding immunosuppressants. One week after IVIG treatment, the patient's respiratory condition and ground-glass opacities on CT imaging significantly improved (Fig. 1D). Muscle weakness began to improve 2 weeks after IVIG treatment. Prednisolone was gradually tapered with the continued improvement of ILD (Fig. 1E), and the patient is maintained on the same dose of oral prednisolone (10 mg/day) as before the relapse of CIDP, with no recurrence of acute exacerbation of ILD.

Discussion

We present a case of a patient with CIDP who developed acute exacerbation of ILD coinciding with a CIDP relapse. ILD is occasionally associated with various autoimmune diseases, most commonly CTDs, such as polymyositis (PM), dermatomyositis (DM), systemic sclerosis, and rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [2]. CIDP is also classified as an autoimmune disease, causing an immune-mediated demyelinating peripheral neuropathy. It is characterised by progressive or relapsing-remitting muscle weakness and sensory disturbances lasting more than 8 weeks [6]. However, ILD associated with autoimmune neuropathies, including CIDP, is extremely rare. There have only been two reported cases of ILD coexisting with CIDP in the literature (Table 2) [3,4]. The two cases previously reported both involved stable ILD, with no acute exacerbation occurring. In our case, ILD coexisting with CIDP experienced an acute exacerbation, which, to our knowledge, is the first reported instance. The two previously reported cases of ILD showed predominantly ground-glass opacities with a distribution in the peripheral and basal areas of both lungs on CT images, without honeycombing. This pattern was similar to the CT imaging pattern of ILD observed in our case prior to the acute exacerbation. There was no history of medication changes, exposure to any obvious irritants, or environmental changes, making an acute exacerbation of ILD due to external factors unlikely. Although the ILD before acute exacerbation is predominantly in the lower lobes, the patient has no significant smoking history, and the CT images do not show a usual interstitial pneumonia pattern, making the possibility of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis unlikely. The patient lacked clinical manifestations that are typically associated with CTD, such as arthralgia, cutaneous eruptions, Raynaud's phenomenon, and sicca symptoms, and tested negative for a comprehensive autoantibody panel (Table 1). Accordingly, CTD-ILD was not considered to be likely. BALF in ILD associated with autoimmune diseases tends to exhibit a lymphocytic cellular pattern [7]. Our case also exhibited a lymphocytic BALF pattern, which supports the possibility that the ILD is associated with CIDP, an autoimmune disease. In addition, infiltrating T lymphocytes in the BALF showed a predominance of CD4-positive cells (Table 1). Similarly, T cell infiltrates observed in nerve biopsy specimens from patients with CIDP are predominantly CD4-positive [8]. Patients with CIDP exhibit a marked elevation of CD4-positive-IFN-γ-producing Th1 cells and CD4-positive-IL-17-producing Th17 cells in both peripheral blood and cerebrospinal fluid, suggesting that these proinflammatory cytokines contribute to peripheral nerve inflammation [9]. A similar inflammatory cascade activation has been reported in autoimmune disease-associated ILD. In RA-ILD lungs, increased expression of the IL-17A receptor has been observed and is linked to fibrosis progression [10]. Additionally, in DM-associated ILD, IFN-γ–mediated immune responses contribute to the rapid progression of ILD [11]. Although reports of the simultaneous onset of CIDP and ILD are extremely rare, some immunological mechanisms contributing to each disease are partially shared. Therefore, we hypothesize that the immune cascade that is activated during CIDP relapse may have played a role in triggering ILD exacerbation. However, this hypothesis remains speculative, because no tissue-level or functional evidence was available to support a direct causal relationship. In our case, the temporal association between ILD exacerbation and CIDP relapse raises the possibility of such a link, but further investigation is needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

The treatment for acute exacerbation of ILD associated with autoimmune diseases, including CTDs, has not been well established. High-dose steroids are usually considered the first-line treatment for patients with acute exacerbations of CTD-ILD; however, robust evidence is limited [12]. In the treatment of acute exacerbation of CTD-ILD, immunosuppressive agents, such as cyclophosphamide, cyclosporine A, and tacrolimus, are often considered in combination with steroids [12]. Immunoglobulin G, the major component of IVIG, plays a central role in immune-mediated inflammation by recognizing self-antigens, activating immune cells, and inducing inflammatory responses that lead to cellular damage, receptor dysfunction, and altered signaling pathways [13]. While IVIG supports immune function in severe infections, high-dose IVIG paradoxically exerts immunosuppressive and anti-inflammatory effects by neutralizing autoantibodies, suppressing aberrant immune cells, and reducing inflammation. IVIG is not typically considered a treatment option for acute exacerbation of ILD; however, there have been a few reports of its effectiveness in cases of acute exacerbation of PM- and DM-related ILD [14]. In the treatment of CIDP, IVIG is widely recognized as a first-line therapy [6], exerting its effects through diverse immunomodulatory mechanisms. These effects include suppressing co-stimulatory molecules to inhibit T-cell activation, regulating antibody production, inhibiting antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity via macrophages, regulating complement activation, and modulating chemokine and cytokine production [1,15]. A similar pathophysiological mechanism underlies the development and exacerbation of ILD, wherein a complex cascade of interactions among T cells, alveolar macrophages, neutrophils, chemokines, and cytokines contributes to inflammatory processes [16]. As with CIDP, IVIG may influence key immune processes involved in ILD pathogenesis, which could theoretically reduce lung tissue damage. In the two previously reported cases of stable CIDP-associated ILD, one patient was treated with corticosteroids alone, while the other received a combination of corticosteroids and IVIG; in both cases, the ILD went into remission (Table 2) [3,4]. In our case, IVIG appeared to be effective during the initial neurogenic symptoms of CIDP, leading us to consider that IVIG might be beneficial for the acute exacerbation of ILD associated with CIDP relapse. Indeed, IVIG seemed to have a notable effect on the high-dose steroid-resistant acute exacerbation of ILD.

This case report has several limitations. First, although a temporal association was observed, a delayed effect of the high-dose corticosteroids administered prior to IVIG could not be excluded; thus, the therapeutic efficacy of IVIG remains uncertain. Furthermore, interactions between corticosteroids and IVIG have been reported [17], making it difficult to attribute the clinical improvement solely to IVIG in this case. Second, this is a single case report without additional supporting cases or retrospective data, and the observed response to IVIG in CIDP-related ILD should be interpreted as a hypothesis rather than a definitive conclusion. Third, although autoimmune serologies were negative, the attribution of CIDP to the ILD exacerbation remains uncertain. Other potential causes, such as subclinical CTD or the idiopathic acute exacerbation of ILD, cannot be fully ruled out. Therefore, a causal relationship between CIDP relapse and ILD exacerbation cannot be definitively established.

Conclusions

Our findings highlight the presence of CIDP-related ILD and suggest that IVIG has potential efficacy not only for neuropathy but also for steroid-resistant acute exacerbations of CIDP-related ILD. However, because this was a single case report, further studies are needed to confirm these therapeutic effects.
