Nationwide Analysis of Sociodemographic Disparities in Health‐Related Quality of Life of Colon Cancer Survivors
Authors: Kaelyn C. Cummins, Mohamad El Moheb, Susan J. Kim, Chengli Shen, Victor Zaydfudim, Samantha M. Ruff, Allan Tsung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHealth‐related quality of life (HRQOL) measures patients' overall well‐being and can help guide survivorship care. Evidence comparing individual patients' HRQOL pre‐ and post‐diagnosis is limited due to the unpredictable nature of cancer onset. Our study aims to evaluate trends in HRQOL in elderly colon cancer patients and identify predictors of HRQOL change across two key transitions: (1) pre‐ to post‐diagnosis, and (2) at diagnosis versus 2 years later, with the goal of identifying at‐risk patients for a significant HRQOL decrease.MethodsUtilizing the SEER‐Medicare Health Outcomes Survey (SEER‐MHOS), patients diagnosed with colon cancer between 1998 and 2019, ≥ 65 years old, and at least two HRQOL surveys were identified and grouped. Group 1 comprised patients with an initial survey pre‐diagnosis and a follow‐up survey within 1 year from diagnosis, while Group 2 included patients with an initial survey within 1 year from diagnosis and a follow‐up survey 2 years later. Bayesian linear regression identified predictors of physical (PCS) and mental (MCS) score changes, incorporating demographic, socioeconomic, comorbidity, and cancer‐related variables.ResultsThe analysis included 1139 patients (Group 1: 571; Group 2: 568). PCS and MCS scores decreased substantially at diagnosis (4.4 and 2.2 points, respectively) compared to baseline but changed minimally from baseline (−0.3 and +0.6 points, respectively) 2 years post‐diagnosis. Advanced disease stage and baseline ADL limitations were the strongest predictors of HRQOL decline, both from pre‐ to post‐diagnosis and from diagnosis to 2 years later. Conversely, positive baseline health perception was protective (probability of direction > 90% for all estimates).ConclusionsAmong colon cancer patients, patient‐reported baseline health perception and functional limitations emerged as critical determinants of HRQOL trajectories, outweighing socioeconomic and treatment‐related factors. These factors remain consistent upon diagnosis and during survivorship. Assessment of these factors could estimate HRQOL outcomes and facilitate early identification of at‐risk patients.

Introduction

Colon cancer is the third most common cancer in the US in both men and women, with an estimated 1.4 million Americans living with a previous colorectal cancer diagnosis in 2022 [1]. As oncologic treatment evolves with safer surgical techniques and novel chemotherapeutic and targeted agents, patient‐reported outcomes (PROs), particularly health‐related quality of life (HRQOL) measures, have become increasingly crucial in addition to assessing therapeutic outcomes. HRQOL encompasses the physical, mental, emotional, and social aspects of a person's well‐being as they relate to their health conditions and treatments. It serves as an indicator of how well patients have reintegrated into their normal life and how they manage long‐term consequences of their disease and treatment [2, 3], and has been shown to be independently associated with survival in colon cancer patients [4, 5, 6, 7]. As a result, HRQOL has become a prevalent endpoint in recent clinical trials, informing both clinical decision‐making and patient‐centered care [8]. Given the importance of HRQOL in cancer care, understanding the factors that influence HRQOL trajectories is crucial for early identification of patients that would most benefit from supportive care strategies. This is especially important for elderly patients, who often have lower baseline HRQOL [9], making them more vulnerable to declines associated with a cancer diagnosis.

Notably, there is a significant gap in our understanding of how HRQOL changes from pre‐ to post‐diagnosis. This gap largely stems from the unpredictable nature of cancer, which makes it challenging to administer HRQOL assessments prior to a diagnosis. The Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results‐Medicare Health Outcomes Survey linked data resource (SEER‐MHOS) offers a unique solution to these challenges. By combining clinical cancer registry data with self‐reported survey data from Medicare‐managed enrollees, SEER‐MHOS provides longitudinal HRQOL information for cancer patients at various stages of their disease course (pre‐, post‐, and long‐term post‐diagnosis) [10]. The SEER‐MHOS linked data resource, which encompasses patients from up to 13 participating US states, therefore allows researchers to investigate previously unanswerable questions, potentially improving our understanding of cancer patient outcomes [10].

The objective of this study was to leverage the SEER‐MHOS linked data resource to evaluate nationwide trends in HRQOL among survivors of colon cancer by focusing on two key transitions: (1) from pre‐diagnosis to within one‐year post‐diagnosis, and (2) from diagnosis to 2 years post‐diagnosis. Furthermore, we sought to identify factors predictive of HRQOL deterioration and develop a model to predict HRQOL trajectories. We hypothesized that disparities in HRQOL among colon cancer survivors in the US are significantly influenced by socioeconomic and racial factors.

Methods

Data Source and Study Population

This study is a retrospective analysis of the SEER‐MHOS linked data resource, which links data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) cancer with the Medicare Health Outcomes Survey (MHOS) data. The MHOS is a questionnaire that is administered to a randomly selected cohort of Medicare Advantage enrollees. Each cohort includes at least 500 enrollees from each Medicare Advantage Organization, with surveyed enrollees receiving a follow‐up survey 2 years after the initial survey. Patients are selected independent of specific diagnoses, meaning that some subset of patients selected will be diagnosed with cancer within a short window after survey completion. The linked data resource therefore provides comprehensive information on cancer incidence, treatment, and outcomes, alongside patient‐reported HRQOL measures [10]. Detailed information on the SEER‐MHOS linked data resource has been previously published [10]. We included patients 65 years of age or older at diagnosis of colon cancer of all stages between 1998 and 2019, no previous history of cancer, and at least two completed HRQOL surveys. Patients were excluded if they only completed one survey. Race, sex, and ethnicity were administrative variables, while the remainder of the variables were self‐reported.

Clinical variables including cancer‐related variables and comorbidities were determined from SEER data. Cancer stage was categorized as in situ, localized, direct extension, regional lymph node involvement, both direct extension and regional lymph node involvement, and distant metastasis, based on the SEER Summary Stage variable and prior studies [11, 12, 13]. While these categorizations are not directly interchangeable with AJCC stages, they approximately correspond with Stage 0 (in situ), Stage I (localized), Stage II (localized or direct extension), Stage III (regional lymph node involvement with or without direct extension), and Stage IV (distant metastasis) [14]. Chemotherapy and radiation therapy are reported as binary variables. Surgery was categorized into none/local excision, partial/segmental resection, and total colectomy/proctocolectomy.

The interpretation and reporting of these data are the sole responsibility of the authors. The authors acknowledge the efforts of the National Cancer Institute; the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services; Information Management Services (IMS) Inc.; and the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program tumor registries in the creation of the SEER‐MHOS linked data resource.

Health Related Quality of Life

HRQOL was assessed using the 36‐Item Short Form Health Survey (SF‐36) validated questionnaire from 1998 to 2005 [15], and the Veterans RAND 12‐item health survey (VR‐12) thereafter [16]. Both of these instruments measure eight key health domains: physical functioning, role limitations due to physical health, role limitations due to emotional problems, bodily pain, social functioning, mental health, vitality, and general health perceptions. The Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS) scores are derived from these domains, with values ranging from 0 to 100 [17, 18, 19]. These scores are standardized T scores based on the U.S. population, with a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10, and scoring on the two surveys is closely comparable [20]. PCS and MCS scores from the VR‐12 and SF‐36 were made consistent using the Modified Regression Estimate algorithm from Boston University [16].

Study Groups and Outcomes

Eligible patients were divided into two groups based on the timing of survey completion in relation to when they were diagnosed (Figure 1). The first group included patients who completed a survey before diagnosis, measuring their baseline pre‐diagnosis HRQOL, and a follow‐up survey within 1 year of diagnosis. This analysis aimed to assess how cancer diagnosis and initial treatment affect PCS and MCS scores, and to identify independent predictors of HRQOL change, characterizing patients most at risk of deterioration after diagnosis compared to baseline. The second group of patients completed both surveys after diagnosis. The first survey was completed within 1 year of diagnosis, and the follow‐up survey approximately 2 years later. All follow‐up surveys were completed within 2 years of the initial survey for both groups. HRQOL change was measured by calculating the difference between the MCS and PCS scores of the follow‐up survey compared to the first survey completed by patients. A positive difference indicated an improvement in HRQOL, while a negative difference indicated a worsening in HRQOL. To prevent data re‐identification, variables with categories where the number of patients was less than 11 were combined when comparing the characteristics of the patient populations.

Schematic representation of study design. The study utilized data from the SEER‐MHOS linked data resource, which links SEER cancer registry data with MHOS survey data. Patients over 65 years old with newly diagnosed colon cancer between 1998 and 2019, no previous history of cancer, and at least two completed health‐related quality of life (HRQOL) surveys were selected and stratified into two groups: one for pre‐ and post‐diagnosis comparisons and another for long‐term post‐diagnosis analysis. Bayesian linear regression was used to compare changes in the physical and mental components of HRQOL in each group and to identify predictors of HRQOL changes. Model covariates included demographic, socioeconomic, comorbidities, and cancer‐related factors.

Descriptive Analysis

Changes in PCS and MCS scores were analyzed and reported as means with standard deviations. These measurements were used to describe trends in HRQOL changes across two key transitions: from before diagnosis to after diagnosis and from within 1 year of diagnosis to 2 years later. Additionally, changes in each of the eight subdomains of HRQOL were compared across these same two transition points.

Bayesian Model Building

Bayesian linear regression models were developed to identify predictors of change in PCS and MCS scores. We first identified general predictors of HRQOL change, then specifically focused on predictors associated with HRQOL changes greater than the Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID), which refers to the smallest change in a treatment outcome that a patient would identify as important, either beneficial or harmful. It represents a threshold that distinguishes between a meaningful improvement (or deterioration) in a clinical outcome and a change that is too small to have practical significance for the patient. MCID was taken to equal 2 based on previous literature [21]. A Bayesian machine learning approach was chosen due to (1) the relatively small sample size compared to the large number of predictors, which increased the risk of overfitting in a standard (frequentist) linear regression approach, (2) its ability to better handle multicollinearity among predictors, and (3) improved quantification of uncertainty. Unlike frequentist models, which rely on point estimates and confidence intervals to assess statistical significance, Bayesian models estimate the full distribution of possible values for each coefficient, generating a 95% credible interval (CI), and allow for direct probability statements. For example, instead of stating that an association between functional limitations and HRQOL decline is “statistically significant at p < 0.05,” the Bayesian approach allows us to say that there is a 95% probability that the effect of functional limitations on HRQOL is negative, thus providing a more intuitive interpretation of uncertainty.

A key component of Bayesian analysis is the use of priors, which represent existing knowledge or beliefs about the expected relationship between a predictor and the outcome. When strong evidence from previous studies was available, we used informative priors based on that literature. In the absence of prior evidence, we used weakly informative priors (mean = 0, SD = 10), which help stabilize model estimates without heavily influencing results (Table 1). This comprehensive set of covariates allowed us to examine the independent effects of sociodemographic factors, including race, sex, concurrent Medicaid status, and self‐reported income and education data, on the outcomes of interest. Models were adjusted for the interval between diagnosis and survey completion, the interval between surveys, and whether patients were receiving treatment at the time the survey was completed. Each model was run with 4 independent simulation chains, each with 2000 iterations. The first 1000 iterations in each chain were used as a warm‐up period to allow the model to stabilize. These iterations are part of the Bayesian sampling process, which repeatedly estimates model parameters to determine their most likely values. The warm‐up helps ensure that early, less stable estimates do not bias the final results. Model convergence was assessed using the R‐hat statistic, with values < 1.01 indicating good convergence. Multiple imputation was performed to handle missing data using the Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) method, generating 20 imputed datasets. The results from these datasets were pooled to obtain final estimates. Probability of direction (PD) was used to measure the certainty of the association between predictors and outcomes. PD quantifies the probability that a coefficient is different from zero or exceeds a specified threshold (such as the MCID of 2 or −2). The PD ranges from 50% to 100%, with values closer to 100% indicating higher certainty in the direction and magnitude of the association. This approach provides a more nuanced interpretation of the results compared to traditional p‐values, focusing on both the direction and clinical significance of the effects. Parameters with PD values ≥ 90% were considered strong predictors of HRQOL change in this study. All statistical analyses were conducted using R, and the brms package was used for Bayesian inference (version 4.3.1).

Results

A total of 1139 colon cancer patients (571 in Group 1, and 568 in Group 2) were included in the analysis. The population characteristics of the patients analyzed are presented in Table 1. For patients in Group 1, 85% of patients underwent segmental resection and 23% received chemotherapy. The average time from diagnosis to completion of the follow‐up survey was 6.1 months (SD: 3.4). For patients in Group 2, 85% underwent segmental resection and 19% received chemotherapy. The average time from diagnosis to completion of the first survey was 5.8 months (SD: 3.5).

Change in HRQOL Pre‐ Vs. Post‐ Diagnosis for Colon Cancer Patients

The average pre‐diagnosis PCS and MCS scores for patients in Group 1 were 38.9 (SD: 11.9) and 48.8 (SD: 11.8), respectively. Within 1 year of cancer diagnosis, these scores decreased by 4.4 and 2.2 points, respectively (Figure 2). The HRQOL subdomain with the largest change was social functioning (−5.2 points) and role‐physical (−4.7 points), and the smallest was in bodily pain (−1.7 points) (Figure 3).

Distribution of physical and mental health scores before and after cancer diagnosis in Group 1 and Group 2. Dotted lines indicate mean scores. PCS, physical component score; MCS, mental component score.

Comparison of health‐related quality of life subdomains in cancer patients pre‐diagnosis, at diagnosis, and 2 years post‐diagnosis.

Among sociodemographic factors, Hispanic ethnicity was linked to greater deterioration in both PCS and MCS. Higher education levels were protective against decline in MCS, but not PCS. Advanced cancer stage was the strongest predictor of PCS decline, with a stepwise decrease observed as the stage progressed. However, this pattern was not seen for MCS. The number of baseline ADL limitations had a strong dose‐dependent relationship with HRQOL decline, particularly for physical components. Estimated PCS changes were −2.69 (credible interval [CI] −5.12, −0.27), −2.89 (CI −5.51, −0.12), and −4.59 (CI −7.39, −1.83) points in patients with limitations in 1, 2, and ≥ 3 ADLs, respectively. In contrast, patient‐rated excellent baseline general health perception was protective against HRQOL deterioration, with PCS and MCS change estimates of 3.81 (CI −1.52, 9.17) and 3.73 (CI −1.84, 9.24), respectively, compared to patients with “poor” general health perception at baseline. When reanalyzed using a two‐point threshold for MCID, advanced disease stage and baseline ADL limitations remained strong predictors of HRQOL decline, while higher education and excellent health perception showed moderate to strong protective effects. Notably, no surgical factors were strong predictors of MCS change; however, total colectomy/proctocolectomy was protective against PCS decline when compared to patients who had no surgery or local excision only, with estimated MCS change of 5.01 points (CI −1.9, 11.78). Finally, a later year of diagnosis was correlated with better protection against PCS, but not MCS, decline (Table 2).

Long‐Term Change in HRQOL Post‐Treatment for Colon Cancer Patients

For patients in Group 2, the average baseline PCS and MCS scores at diagnosis were 37.1 (SD: 11.9) and 49.6 (SD: 11.4), respectively. Two years post‐diagnosis, PCS decreased by just 0.3 points, while MCS increased by 0.6 points (Figure 2). Changes across the HRQOL subdomains were minimal, with all shifts being less than one point (Figure 3).

Among sociodemographic factors, higher income was associated with a significant improvement in MCS, exceeding the MCID threshold (CI −1.22, 6.23). Although Medicaid and higher education were linked to declines in MCS and PCS, respectively, these changes did not reach the MCID threshold. Similarly, partial/segmental resection was associated with decreased MCS (CI −7.05, 0.01). No other surgical factors were predictive. The only other variables predictive of HRQOL changes greater than the MCID 2 years post‐diagnosis were ADL limitations and general health perception. Patients with more ADL limitations experienced greater deterioration in physical HRQOL, while better baseline general health perception offered protection against further decline (Table 3).

Discussion

This nationwide analysis of HRQOL among patients with colon cancer assessed the predictors of HRQOL changes across two key transition points: before and at diagnosis, and at diagnosis versus 2 years post‐diagnosis. Our analysis of sociodemographic factors revealed several key predictors of HRQOL changes in colon cancer patients across transition points. Higher socioeconomic status, as measured by education and income, generally protected against HRQOL decline. This trend aligns with observations in clinical endpoints such as disease‐free and overall survival, reinforcing the close relationship between HRQOL, PROs, and clinical outcomes in cancer patients [6, 22, 23]. Importantly, our findings highlight that socioeconomically disadvantaged patients are most vulnerable to HRQOL decline. This underscores the need for targeted interventions and support for this population. Interestingly, contrary to expectations, race was not predictive of meaningful HRQOL changes in our study. This apparent divergence from previous research stems from our focus on HRQOL trajectories rather than absolute levels. While prior studies have associated race with lower overall HRQOL [23, 24], our findings suggest it may not significantly influence recovery patterns. This nuanced approach reveals that although racial disparities exist in HRQOL baselines, they do not necessarily dictate the course of improvement or decline over time in cancer patients.

Importantly, our findings identified ADL limitations and general health perception, as reported in the first survey, as two of the most significant predictors of future HRQOL change. These initial measurements, taken before observing any HRQOL changes, could allow for reliable prediction of future outcomes. This has important practical implications for identifying patients at risk for HRQOL deterioration. By simply assessing patients' perceived health and functional status at diagnosis, clinicians can predict long‐term HRQOL outcomes. This approach offers a more accessible and efficient method for identifying patients who may require additional support than complex risk calculators or nomograms that are cumbersome and therefore rarely used in clinical settings [25].

Our findings show a significant improvement in HRQOL over time. Colon cancer care has advanced drastically in recent decades, leading to better clinical outcomes for patients [26, 27, 28]. Our study demonstrates that these advances have not only improved survival rates but also enhanced HRQOL. This finding is novel and may be related to the increasing focus on survivorship care in recent years. As survival rates post‐cancer care have improved, the need to prioritize cancer survivorship and HRQOL has become more pressing [29, 30, 31, 32]. Cancer survivors often experience significant anxiety and distress during and after treatment, including fear of recurrence [33]. Clinical guidelines have thus emphasized the importance of comprehensive follow‐up care, early rehabilitation referrals, and long‐term survivorship support [34].

A unique strength of this project is the use of Bayesian analysis to identify predictors of HRQOL. This approach offers several advantages over standard regression methods. First, Bayesian analysis allows us to incorporate existing knowledge and established evidence from the literature, using our data to update this knowledge and provide comprehensive, robust estimates. We used informative priors only when strong supporting evidence existed. However, given the limited data on this topic, we often employed non‐informative priors to prevent bias from influencing our results. These non‐informative priors act as regularization parameters in the model, helping to prevent overfitting and mitigate multicollinearity issues, which are concerns particularly relevant to our study. Moreover, Bayesian analysis offers a more nuanced approach to handling and quantifying uncertainty. Rather than categorizing predictors as simply statistically significant or not, potentially dismissing factors that fall short of significance due to small sample size, our approach quantifies the likelihood of a factor being predictive of HRQOL change. This results in a graded assessment based on the degree of evidence, providing a more natural and interpretable way of understanding the relationship between predictors and outcomes. Consequently, our results offer a realistic reflection of the complex relationships at play in HRQOL changes among cancer patients, allowing for a rich interpretation of the data and potentially uncovering subtle but important predictors that might be overlooked in traditional analyses.

Our study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, our analysis focused exclusively on Medicare patients, which encompassed only a fraction of the total US population who develop colon cancer each year and may also limit the generalizability of our findings to the broader elderly US population. The same results may not hold true for younger colon cancer patients. Additionally, there is potential for both selection bias and survivorship bias among patients who completed both surveys. Specifically, the population completing the second survey certainly includes only patients who lived at least 2 years after their first survey and may also represent a healthier population than those who were lost to follow‐up. This may result in overrepresentation of patients with better prognoses and therefore an overestimation of HRQOL stability. Finally, the SEER‐MHOS linked data resource does not capture recurrence data and has limited granularity regarding cancer treatment, so analysis is limited regarding the impact of these factors on patient HRQOL. For instance, details on chemotherapy timing and regimen are lacking, which is an important limitation given that different chemotherapy regimens are known to impact HRQOL differently [35]. Future studies would ideally be conducted using more detailed treatment data. Despite these limitations, this study utilizes the most comprehensive resource available for evaluating long‐term HRQOL changes at the population level.

Conclusions

This nationwide population‐level analysis examined predictors of HRQOL change across two critical transition points in elderly patients with colon cancer: from pre‐ to post‐diagnosis, and from diagnosis to long‐term follow‐up. Baseline health perception and functional limitations, particularly ADL impairments, emerged as critical determinants of HRQOL trajectories across both transition points, surpassing the influence of socioeconomic factors and specific cancer treatments. A simplified assessment of these factors could model and predict patients' long‐term HRQOL trajectories. These results highlight the importance of early identification of at‐risk patients and strategic implementation of targeted interventions. Future research should focus on developing and evaluating personalized supportive care strategies based on these key predictors to optimize HRQOL outcomes in elderly cancer patients. Additionally, replicating these findings in a clinical trial setting, with detailed patient information and treatment specifics, could provide stronger evidence to guide interventions for improving long‐term HRQOL.
