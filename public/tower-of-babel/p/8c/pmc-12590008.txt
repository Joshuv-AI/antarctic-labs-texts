Mesh erosion into the cecum following laparoscopic intraperitoneal onlay mesh (IPOM) repair
Authors: Tahmaseb Jouzdani, Mohammad Javanbakht, Mahdi Mohebbi, Helia Ghorbani, Seyed Mohammad Hossein Tabatabaei Nodushan, Hassan Mohammadpour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIncisional hernia is a common complication of abdominal surgery, characterized by protrusion of abdominal contents through a weakened incision site. Although laparoscopic IPOM repair reduces recurrence rates compared to open repair, it carries a risk of rare complications, such as erosion of prosthetic mesh.Case presentationWe describe a 50-year-old woman with a history of scleroderma who presented with chronic abdominal pain, intermittent diarrhea, and fullness in the right lower quadrant. Imaging evaluation, including CT scan and colonoscopy revealed erosion of prosthetic mesh into the cecum, which was confirmed during exploratory laparotomy. The patient underwent a segmental colectomy with ileocolic anastomosis and recovered without postoperative complications.DiscussionMesh erosion is an uncommon but serious complication of laparoscopic IPOM repair, potentially influenced by impaired wound healing in connective tissue disorders such as scleroderma. This case highlights the importance of careful postoperative monitoring and timely recognition of mesh-related complications.ConclusionThis case emphasizes the need for increased awareness of the risk of prosthetic mesh erosion following intraperitoneal mesh repair and underscores the urgent need for further research into its long-term outcomes and complications.Keywords: Incisional hernia, Prosthetic mesh erosion, Chronic abdominal pain, Scleroderma, Segmental colectomy

Introduction

Incisional hernia, defined as the protrusion of intra-abdominal contents through a weakened abdominal wall at the site of a previous surgical incision, is a common postoperative complication, with an incidence of up to 20 % in the general population and as high as (35–50 %) in high-risk patients such as those with obesity, wound infection, or connective tissue disorders [1]. Various surgical approaches exist for repair, including onlay, inlay, sublay, and laparoscopic intraperitoneal onlay mesh (IPOM) techniques [1]. The IPOM technique, first described by LeBlanc and Booth in 1993 [2], offers several advantages, including minimal dissection, a lower incidence of surgical-site infections, and faster recovery [3]. However, complications such as seroma formation, recurrence, and rarely mesh displacement or erosion have been reported [4,5]. Mesh erosion into the cecum is exceptionally rare and is infrequently reported in patients with underlying autoimmune disease. The existing literature on this serious but uncommon complication is limited [6]. This case aims to highlight this unusual presentation and discuss potential contributing factors.

Case presentation

A 50-year-old woman presented with a chief complaint of chronic abdominal pain, intermittent diarrhea, and a sensation of fullness in the right lower quadrant (RLQ) of her abdomen. She denied any nausea, vomiting, or anorexia and was referred to our center as an outpatient for further evaluation.

Her medical history included a Cesarean section via a Pfannenstiel incision, which led to an incisional hernia at the incision site. Eight years after IPOM repair, she began experiencing her current symptoms. At the time of presentation, her body mass index (BMI) was 26.5 kg/m2. Additionally, she had a diagnosed history of scleroderma from three years ago for which she was receiving routine follow-up and treatment with CellCept (mycophenolate mofetil).

During the physical examination, her vital signs were stable (respiratory rate: 16/min, blood pressure: 120/80 mmHg, pulse rate: 80/min, temperature: 37.5 °C). Abdominal examination revealed a soft, non-tender abdomen without rebound tenderness or any signs of an acute abdomen. However, a palpable fullness in the RLQ was observed, and there were no indications of hernia recurrence at the site of her previous incision.

The patient's radiological workup included an upright abdominal X-ray and a kidney-ureters-bladder (KUB) study. Imaging revealed numerous small “spring-like” artifacts overlying the RLQ abdominal wall, suggesting the presence of hernia repair mesh (Fig. 1).

(A) Upright abdominal radiograph showing spring-like radiopaque artifacts in the right lower quadrant, consistent with mesh. (B, C) Colonoscopy demonstrating mesh eroding through and protruding into the cecal lumen.

Subsequently, a colonoscopy revealed normal rectum, sigmoid, descending, and transverse colonic mucosa. However, a suspicious lesion was identified in the cecum, which was reported as a foreign body. Attempts to remove the foreign body during colonoscopy were unsuccessful.

Based on the gastroenterologist's findings, the patient was deemed a candidate for exploratory laparotomy. The implanted mesh was a composite type, commonly used in intraperitoneal onlay mesh (IPOM) repairs, and fixation was performed with a combination of tacks and sutures, following standard surgical practice. During surgery, the cecum was adherent to the anterior abdominal wall and required sharp dissection for release. Due to dense adhesions and technical difficulty, a midline incision was preferred over a laparoscopic approach. Segmental colectomy was performed, followed by ileocolic anastomosis. (B) Further exploration delineating the extent of mesh involvement. (C) Complete exposure of mesh adherent to the cecal wall. (D) Displaying the excised cecum with attached mesh. (E) Explanted specimen demonstrating the relationship between the mesh and cecal tissue.

The patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery and was discharged on the 5th postoperative day. During follow-up 10 days later, she remained asymptomatic and free from further complications. The patient expressed satisfaction with the surgical outcome and reported no any other abdominal symptoms at follow-up.

Discussion

Pathophysiology

Systemic sclerosis (SSc) is characterized by immune-mediated microvascular injury and fibroblast activation, leading to excessive extracellular matrix deposition and progressive fibrosis. Chronic hypoxia resulting from microvascular dysfunction impairs tissue remodeling and neovascularization, which are critical processes for mesh incorporation. Dysregulation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs) further disrupts normal extracellular matrix turnover, creating a profibrotic environment that compromises mesh integration and predisposes to mesh migration and erosion [[8], [9], [10]]. The physical characteristics of mesh, such as weight, pore size, and surface properties, are known to influence erosion risk and host-tissue response [[11], [12], [13], [14]].

Literature review

Mesh repair remains the standard of care for abdominal and incisional hernia repairs, but mesh-related complications, though uncommon, can be severe. Mesh erosion and migration have been reported with both open and laparoscopic approaches. Mesh-related erosion may present as chronic abdominal pain, gastrointestinal bleeding, fistula formation, or bowel perforation [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. Erosion results from multiple factors, including improper fixation, foreign body reaction, infection, and patient-related issues like immunosuppression, malnutrition, and connective tissue disorders [[11], [12], [13], [14]].

Comparative cases

Several case reports have documented mesh-related complications following abdominal and inguinal hernia repairs. Jangjoo et al. reported a case of mesh migration after abdominal hernia repair with delayed presentation [11]. Asano et al. reported mesh erosion into the cecum and bladder after inguinal hernia surgery [12]. Likewise, Goswami et al. documented mesh erosion into the cecum following laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair, which necessitated a right hemicolectomy [13]. Narang et al. described delayed mesh migration into the cecum and ascending colon three years after laparoscopic hernia repair [14]. Aziz and Zaeem [15] and Gandhi et al. [16] reported cases of chronic abdominal pain due to mesh erosion into the cecum after open incisional hernia repairs. Our patient similarly experienced delayed mesh erosion into the cecum following an open incisional hernia repair using the IPOM technique. However, in our case, dense adhesions between the cecum and anterior abdominal wall precluded safe laparoscopic removal, necessitating segmental colectomy.

These findings highlight that mesh erosion can occur regardless of the surgical method, with reports following open and laparoscopic hernia repairs. Although laparoscopic repair offers potential benefits such as shorter recovery time and reduced wound complications, it does not eliminate the risk of mesh migration or erosion. The choice of open approach in our case was justified by the patient's previous complex surgical history and anticipated dense intra-abdominal adhesions.

Additionally, autoimmune complications associated with mesh placement have been recognized. Dias et al. presented a case of ASIA syndrome following inguinal mesh repair with resolution after mesh removal [17]. Jisova et al. conducted a comprehensive review highlighting reports of autoimmune and allergic-like responses linked to mesh implantation [18]. Recent studies have highlighted autoimmune responses and adjuvant-induced syndromes (ASIA) related to hernia mesh implants [17,18]. Although our patient did not exhibit systemic autoimmune manifestations, the background of systemic sclerosis might have contributed to altered wound healing and fibrosis, increasing the risk of mesh erosion. Management challenges

Management of mesh erosion remains a clinical challenge and must be individualized based on patient comorbidities, symptom severity, and intraoperative findings. For cases with mild symptoms, partial, or asymptomatic erosion, conservative treatment coupled with close observation has been reported [15,16]. However, most cases involving erosion into hollow viscera, particularly the cecum, require surgical intervention due to the risks of perforation, peritonitis, and sepsis [13,14]. Differential diagnosis in such cases includes internal hernia, fistula formation, or adhesive bowel obstruction [[11], [12], [13], [14]].

Several reports have described successful laparoscopic removal of migrated mesh [13,14]. However, in our case, the presence of extensive intra-abdominal fibrosis, likely exacerbated by the patient's systemic sclerosis, and dense adhesions between the cecum and anterior abdominal wall rendered laparoscopic excision unsafe. We therefore performed an open segmental colectomy with ileocolic anastomosis, which allowed safe complete removal of the mesh with minimal risk of further bowel injury.

The literature emphasizes that the surgical strategy should be tailored to individual patient factors, including prior surgical history and anticipated adhesions [11,12]. This decision-making highlights the critical role of comprehensive preoperative planning and careful intraoperative judgment. Early diagnosis of mesh erosion and timely surgical intervention are crucial for ensuring favorable patient outcomes [13,14].

Clinical implications

Although rare, mesh erosion into the cecum is a serious clinical concern. Surgeons should maintain a high index of suspicion in patients with unexplained chronic abdominal pain, gastrointestinal bleeding, or nonspecific gastrointestinal symptoms after hernia repair, particularly when the mesh was placed intraperitoneally [11,12,15,16]. Preoperative patient selection and risk stratification are essential, especially for individuals with underlying connective tissue diseases such as systemic sclerosis, which may predispose to abnormal fibrosis and impaired mesh integration [17,18]. In such patients, the choice of mesh type, fixation method, and consideration of alternative surgical techniques, such as extraperitoneal mesh placement, should be tailored on a case-by-case basis [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. Early diagnosis and timely surgical intervention are paramount to prevent life-threatening complications such as perforation, peritonitis, or sepsis. Additionally, long-term follow-up after mesh implantation is recommended to monitor delayed complications, given that cases of mesh migration and erosion have been reported several years postoperatively [13,14]. Further research is needed to better understand the long-term outcomes, risk factors, and preventive strategies for mesh-related complications in high-risk populations, including those with autoimmune diseases [17,18].

Conclusion

Mesh erosion, though rare, must be considered in the differential diagnosis for patients who develop chronic gastrointestinal symptoms following IPOM, particularly in those with systemic connective tissue disorders. Early detection and surgical management are essential.
