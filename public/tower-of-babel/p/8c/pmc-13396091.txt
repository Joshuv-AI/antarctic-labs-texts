Beyond B Cell Depletion: Adhesion and Activation Network Remodeling During Repeated Intravenous Anti-CD20 Therapy in Multiple Sclerosis
Authors: Julia Feige, Tobias Moser, Katja Akgün, Kerstin Schwenker, Wolfgang Hitzl, Tjalf Ziemssen, Johann Sellner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionImmune cell depletion with anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) is an effective disease-modifying therapy in multiple sclerosis (MS). CD20 is mainly specific to the B cell lineage and is lost upon differentiation into plasma cells. Here, we aimed to elucidate the consequences of intravenous CD20 depletion every 6 months on the expression of adhesion molecules and activation networks of peripheral immune cell subsets.MethodsIn this exploratory study, we investigated 15 people with MS (pwMS) treated with either rituximab (RTX, n=7) or ocrelizumab (OCR, n=8), with examinations scheduled before treatment initiation and every 12 weeks for up to 15 months. Phenotyping of immune cell surface molecules for adhesion and activation in peripheral blood was performed by multi-parametric fluorescence cytometry.ResultsAnti-CD20 therapy was associated with changes in adhesion and activation markers on B and T lymphocytes, indicating effects beyond simple B cell depletion. Both residual and repopulating CD19+ B cells were enriched for an adhesion-competent phenotype. In contrast, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells showed reduced activation and decreased expression of effector markers, consistent with secondary T cell deactivation. Most changes became apparent after at least two treatment cycles. Taking the limited sample size into consideration, we observed no significant differences in the expression kinetics of these surface markers between the RTX- and OCR-treated groups.ConclusionProfiling of surrogate markers of cell adhesion and activation in peripheral immune cell subsets in pwMS indicates that anti-CD20 treatment leads to a disruption of B cell-dependent immune networks. Although OCR and RTX bind to distinct sites and use distinct mechanisms, their effects were indistinguishable. Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic autoimmune disorder of the central nervous system (CNS) characterized by inflammatory demyelination and neurodegeneration [1]. A key step in MS pathogenesis is the migration of autoreactive lymphocytes across the blood–brain barrier (BBB) into the CNS via interactions between cell adhesion molecules (CAMs) on lymphocytes and their ligands on BBB endothelial cells [2, 3]. Relevant CAM families include (1) integrin (leucocyte function-associated molecule-1, LFA-1), (2) very-late antigen-4 (VLA-4 = CD49d + CD29), (3) immunoglobulin (intercellular adhesion molecule, ICAM; activated leukocyte cell adhesion molecule, ALCAM; vascular cell adhesion molecule, VCAM), and (4) sialomucin (P-selectin glycoprotein ligand-1, PSGL-1) [2]. CAMs also stabilize the immunological synapse (IS), which represents the interface between antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and immune cells, including T and B cell subtypes [4, 5]. Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) further contributes by infecting and reprogramming naïve B cells, promoting memory B cell survival and potentially disturbing T cell control of autoreactive responses [6].

These insights led to the evaluation of anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) in MS [7, 8]. CD20 is primarily expressed on B cell subsets, including pre-, naïve, and memory B cells, as well as on a small subset of CD3+ T cells [9–11]. In contrast, stem cells, pro-B cells, and plasma cells do not express the CD20 surface marker [12, 13]. Ocrelizumab (OCR) is approved for both relapsing–remitting (RR-MS) and primary progressive MS (PP-MS). Rituximab (RTX) is widely used off-label in RR-MS and secondary progressive MS (SP-MS) [8, 11]. The chimeric mAb RTX and the humanized mAb OCR bind to distinct, yet overlapping, CD20 epitopes [8, 13, 14]. Both achieve near-complete depletion of circulating B cells via cytotoxic and phagocytic pathways [15, 16]. B cell reconstitution is skewed toward naïve phenotypes with lower adhesion and chemokine receptor expression, potentially limiting CNS homing [17, 18]. In addition, chemotactic responsiveness to CNS-homing signals is reduced in repopulating B cells during or after anti-CD20 therapy [19]. By depleting CD3+CD20+ T cells producing pro-inflammatory cytokines, peripheral inflammation is reduced as well [9]. This broader immunomodulation likely contributes to clinical efficacy.

This study aimed to assess the effects of repeated intravenous administration of anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies (OCR or RTX) on immune cell surface molecules involved in adhesion and activation over 15 months in people with MS (pwMS).

Methods

Study Cohort

In this exploratory study, we enrolled 15 patients with MS at the Christian Doppler Medical Center in Salzburg, Austria, who were scheduled to initiate CD20-depleting therapy with either ocrelizumab or rituximab. We collected demographic data, medical history, and details of the previous MS course, and assessed the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) at baseline and every 12 weeks during anti-CD20 treatment. Neurological examination was performed, and blood samples were collected at baseline before the start of anti-CD20 treatment and subsequently every 12 weeks (±4 weeks) for up to 15 months. Anti-CD20 therapy with either ocrelizumab or rituximab was started as soon as an absolute lymphocyte count of ≥ 1000 cells/µL was attained. As the immunological effects of OCR and RTX did not differ in a previous study, the decision was made to pool these populations [20].

The use and dosage of OCR were based on the label provided by the European Medicines Agency. OCR was administered intravenously twice within the first 2 weeks of treatment at a dose of 300 mg each. Subsequent doses were 600 mg, administered every 6 months. RTX was used off-label and administered intravenously every 6 months at a dose of 1000 mg each. All patients received 500 mg acetaminophen tablets, 30 mg diphenhydramine, and 250 mg methylprednisolone infusions approximately 30 min before the start of anti-CD20 therapy. Inclusion in our study did not interfere with further therapeutic approaches, such as treatment with steroids or plasma exchange in the case of a relapse, or the need for symptomatic treatment.

A relapse was defined as the appearance of new or the reappearance of previous neurological deficits for at least 24 h in the absence of fever or an infection. In contrast, disease progression was confirmed by a persistent increase in the EDSS (> 0.5 points) for at least 3 months.

Routine Blood Testing and Immune Cell Phenotyping by Flow Cytometry

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were isolated and processed according to standard operating procedures as described in a previous publication [21]. All samples were thawed simultaneously, as multiple freeze–thaw cycles could compromise the integrity of our specimens. The samples were then analyzed in a single batch to avoid bias in data acquisition and enable low inter-assay variability. PBMCs were isolated from heparinized or citrated blood and cryopreserved at −130 °C. After thawing, cells were incubated with the viability marker Zombie Green–Alexa Fluor 488 (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) and washed with fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer (phosphate-buffered saline, 0.2% fetal calf serum, and 0.02% sodium azide). Subsequently, subpopulations were identified and quantified by surface staining with fluorescently labeled antibodies (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA: CD8 PerCP-Cy5.5, CD4 PE-Cy7, CD3 APC-H7, CD19 BV510, ICAM-1-APC, ICAM-2-PE, ICAM-3-BV421, ALCAM-APC, VCAM-BV786, LFA-1-A700, PD-1-BV605, PSGL-1-PE-CF-594, CD49d-BV711, CD29-BV786, CD28-PE-Cy5, CD44-BV421, HLA-DR-PE). Corresponding isotype antibodies were used as negative controls. Upon harvesting, cells were incubated with viability dye staining (Zombie Green–Alexa Fluor 488) for 20 min. Afterwards, surface staining with fluorescently labeled antibodies was performed and cells were incubated for a further 20 min. All samples were rinsed with FACS buffer and subsequently measured on an LSRFortessa (BD Biosciences). FACS data were then evaluated via FACSDiva software (BD Biosciences). Immune cell populations were gated as Th (CD3+CD4+), cytotoxic T (cT, CD3+CD8+), and B cells (CD3−CD19+). Fluorescence-minus-one (FMO) controls were used to define positive gates. Compensation was performed by using single-stained compensation beads. In addition to changes in proportions, i.e., the frequencies of B and T cells expressing specific surface markers, we assessed alterations in signal intensity, measured as mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) for each marker on the surface of the respective lymphocyte subset. MFI is a quantitative measure of the average brightness of the cell population. Surface molecules of interest included ICAM-1, ICAM-2, ICAM-3, ALCAM, VCAM, LFA-1, PD-1, PSGL-1, CD49d, CD29, CD28, CD44, HLA-DR, and VLA-4.

Statistical Analysis

Data were checked for consistency and normality. Fisher’s exact or Pearson’s chi-square tests were used to compare the categorical variables. Data were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. In the case of non-normality, generalized linear models with log-normal distribution were used for continuous variables. The Mann–Whitney U-test for independent samples was used to detect differences between the baseline values and control samples. We used generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) with Bonferroni’s correction for pairwise multiple comparisons to evaluate changes over time. Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, data acquisition was sometimes irregular; with mixed models, all available data points were used without needing to exclude subjects with missing observations. All reported tests were two-sided; p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses in this report were performed using STATISTICA 13 (Hill, T. and Lewicki, P.; Statistics: Methods and Applications. StatSoft, Tulsa, OK). Graphs were created with GraphPad PRISM 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Ethical Approval

The procedures performed in our study were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee, the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments, or comparable ethical standards. All patients provided written informed consent for participation and subsequent publication of data after approval by the Ethics Committee of Bundesland Salzburg (415-E/161 2111-2018). The study was carried out according to the Helsinki protocol in its current version.

Results

A total of 15 patients were enrolled. Twelve had previously received other immunomodulatory or immunosuppressive therapies with ongoing disease activity, whereas the remaining three were treatment-naïve.

Eight patients (53%) received OCR. Among these, three patients (38%) had been diagnosed with RR-MS and five patients (63%) with PP-MS. The other seven patients (47%) had already progressed to a secondary progressive disease course and were treated with RTX.

Demographic information for the cohort is shown in Table 1. Eight patients (53%) were women. The median age was 46 years, whereas the median age at diagnosis was 33 years. At treatment initiation, patients with RR-/PP-MS were younger than those with SP-MS. At the time of diagnosis, however, patients with RR-MS and PP-MS were approximately the same age as patients with SP-MS. As could be expected, disease duration was considerably longer in patients with SP-MS than in patients with RR-MS or PP-MS. On average, each patient received 3 ± 1 treatment cycles of either OCR or RTX. Discussion

This study provides further insight into how repeated infusion cycles of CD20-targeting monoclonal antibodies such as OCR and RTX impact peripheral immune cell phenotypes in MS. Surface markers were grouped into adhesion/trafficking (CD49d, CD29, LFA-1, ICAM-1/2/3, VCAM, PSGL-1, and ALCAM) and activation/costimulation clusters (HLA-DR, PD-1, and CD44). In our cohort, anti-CD20 therapy induced profound and coordinated alterations in adhesion, activation, and trafficking profiles of both B and T lymphocytes. Specifically, we detected a coordinated upregulation of adhesion-associated molecules in residual CD19+ B cells, whereas CD4+ and CD8+ T cells predominantly showed downregulation of activation-associated markers, indicating global T cell deactivation rather than a uniform inhibition of migratory capacity. B cell depletion appears to reorganize the adaptive immune network, leading to secondary effects on T cell activation and migratory behavior. The emergence of an adhesion-competent B cell phenotype during repopulation raises questions about the qualitative nature of immune reconstitution and its relationship with disease reactivation, which should be addressed in longitudinal studies. At least two treatment cycles were required to induce and maintain these changes. Although OCR and RTX bind to distinct sites and use distinct depletion mechanisms, their effects were indistinguishable.

Firstly, these changes seem to go well beyond simple B cell depletion and provide important insight into the immunological reprogramming underlying the clinical efficacy of anti-CD20 therapies in MS. The residual and repopulating B cell compartment showed enrichment of adhesion-related molecules, including ICAM-1, ICAM-2, ICAM-3, VCAM, LFA-1, PSGL-1, CD49d, and CD29. Qualitative shifts in the reconstituting B cell compartment have been described previously [15]. This may reflect the selection of antigen-experienced or compartmentalized B cell subsets with enhanced migratory capacity. While the absolute number of B cells remains reduced during repeated intravenous administration of anti-CD20 antibodies, the qualitative shift toward an adhesion-competent phenotype may have implications for disease control upon B cell reconstitution and the rationale for continuous treatment strategies. Although anti-CD20 therapy profoundly depletes circulating B cells, repopulation is heterogeneous; early reconstituting cells are often transitional or naïve, but exhibit increased activation and costimulatory marker expression rather than simply representing newly generated resting B cells [22]. Experimental models support the notion that antigen-experienced B cells may be preferentially resistant to depletion and contribute to the recovery pool [23]. Thus, anti-CD20 therapy might reshape both B cell subset composition and activation state rather than simply de novo generating naïve B cells [24]. However, as anti-CD20 therapy significantly alters B cell numbers and immune cell composition, some of the observed phenotypic changes could also reflect shifts in cellular composition rather than true modulation of marker expression within stable populations.

Secondly, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells exhibited reduced expression of activation- and effector-associated markers, consistent with indirect T cell modulation following B cell depletion [25]. The observed downregulation of HLA-DR, PD-1, and CD44, along with reduced expression of other adhesion molecules such as ALCAM and ICAM-2, could indicate a global dampening of T cell activation and tissue-invasive potential. Similar effects of ocrelizumab on migration markers in CD8+ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells have already been described elsewhere; however, some findings, such as increased MFI of CD49d in CD8+ T cells, are in direct contrast to the reduced MFI of CD49d as seen in a previous study [26]. This discrepancy most likely reflects the dynamic and context-dependent regulation of adhesion molecules during anti-CD20 therapy. CD49d expression, for example, is influenced not only by activation status but also by shifts in T cell subset composition. Enrichment of effector or memory CD8+ T cells, which typically express higher levels of CD49d, may result in increased MFI, whereas expansion of naïve or less activated subsets may lead to reduced expression [27]. Furthermore, immune modulation evolves over time and may differ between early and later phases of therapy [9]. Correspondingly, in our study, the increase was observed between baseline and month 6; in the study mentioned above, a decrease in MFI of CD49d was noted within 12 months. Similarly, the apparent discrepancy between our finding of increased CD8+CD28+ T cell frequencies and reports of elevated CD8+CD28− T cells under anti-CD20 therapy may indicate that CD28 expression on CD8+ T cells reflects dynamic immune remodeling rather than a uniform directional change and should be interpreted in the context of subset composition and functional phenotype [28]. Differences in disease activity, prior treatments, and methodological factors, including flow cytometry settings and gating strategies, may further contribute to variability across studies.

As already mentioned, these changes might not represent a direct pharmacological effect of anti-CD20 treatment on T cells but rather a secondary consequence of B cell removal. When considering the roles of B cells in antigen presentation, costimulation, and cytokine production, B cells could act as key organizers of pathogenic T cell responses in pwMS. Their depletion may lead to a functional de-escalation of the T cell compartment, characterized by reduced chronic stimulation and diminished capacity for CNS tissue invasion. However, as with B cells, the effects of anti-CD20 mAbs on T cell phenotypes should not be interpreted as the emergence of newly generated naïve T cells. CD20+ T cells with enhanced activation and migratory potential are effectively depleted by anti-CD20 therapy, indicating that treatment impacts a defined subset of existing T cells rather than promoting new T cell generation [29]. Naïve T cells are conventionally distinguished from memory/effector cells by a combination of surface markers that reflect both their antigen-inexperienced state and their homing behavior; the observed phenotypic changes might therefore be more consistent with modulation of existing T cells rather than the generation of new thymic emigrants, particularly given age-related declines in thymic output [30].

Thirdly, the effects observed in our cohort differ from those induced by classic anti-trafficking therapies such as natalizumab (NTZ), a monoclonal antibody targeting the CD49d subunit of VLA-4. We did not observe uniform or dominant downregulation of VLA-4 or other core trafficking molecules, implicating a primary blockade of BBB transmigration. Instead, our data indicate that anti-CD20 therapy might act primarily by altering the immunological activation architecture that drives pathogenic immune cell trafficking. Immune cells may retain the physical capacity to enter the CNS, but they appear less activated, less organized, and less able to sustain compartmentalized inflammation. NTZ not only impedes adhesion of white blood cells to their receptors but also reduces VLA-4 expression on several immune cell subtypes, including B cells [31]. The number of T cells adhering to VCAM is reduced during NTZ treatment; however, there are no changes in T cell binding to ICAM-1 [32]. Dimethyl fumarate (DMF) and fingolimod interfere with the T cell-mediated immune surveillance of the brain, albeit via different mechanisms [32]. PwMS treated with DMF showed a decrease in ICAM-1-adhering T cells, while the expression of cytokine-induced E-selectin, VCAM, and ICAM-1 on human endothelial cells and LFA-1-mediated binding of T cells to ICAM-1 were inhibited [31, 32]. Fingolimod impairs interactions between the CNS, certain integrins, and their ligands, thereby decreasing T cell adhesion and reducing T cell binding to VCAM and ICAM-1 [32]. Other treatment options, such as teriflunomide, also inhibit adhesion molecules, leading to the disruption of T cell/APC interaction and integrin signaling during T cell activation [31]. In a cohort of MS patients treated with cladribine, the frequencies of B cells expressing CD44, CD29, and CD49d were reduced 24 months after the first treatment course; furthermore, the densities of ICAM-1, CD44, and HLA-DR dropped compared to baseline [33]. On the other hand, no relevant longitudinal alterations in the expression of adhesion molecules and costimulatory signals on immune cells were found in healthy controls analyzed in the same study [33]. Prior data from patients treated with OCR suggest that anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies alter the expression of regulatory surface molecules on B cells, potentially influencing their capacity to interact with the BBB [34]. As for RTX, repopulating B cells appear to be more activated, with a higher costimulatory phenotype; on the other hand, RTX treatment has been shown to reduce the expression of ICAM-1 and VCAM in the cerebrospinal fluid of pwMS [35]. In summary, the available data on anti-CD20 therapies are not entirely consistent, as they point toward both inhibitory and stimulatory effects [35].

Of note, most changes observed in our study, especially in B lymphocytes, became apparent after two infusion cycles; in T cells, some changes were detected as early as 3 months after the first infusion cycle. Consequently, these effects seemed to consolidate after a third treatment cycle. Our findings are similar to those in peripheral immune cell subsets (particularly memory B cells and TH17.1 central memory cells), with most pronounced changes occurring only after the second treatment cycle [20]. It has already been postulated that anti-CD20 therapies could also act as immune reconstitution therapies, such as cladribine or alemtuzumab, although this should be viewed with caution [36].

Differences in the mechanistic, pharmacological, and clinical characteristics of RTX and OCR are otherwise well established [37, 38]. The depleting effects of both drugs on CD20-expressing immune cells are, however, near-complete and do not differ [20]. Both antibodies bind to the large extracellular loop of CD20—RTX to the amino acid residues 168–175, and OCR to 165–180 [37]. Despite distinct depletion mechanisms and differences in terminal half-life, we did not find differences in immune marker modulation at months 3 and 6. Given that our cohort was small, with differences in disease course, age, and disease duration, these findings need to be corroborated by independent studies.

To elicit memory lymphocytes, the pairing of costimulatory molecules within the IS is required [4]. Several CAMs are expressed on memory B cell subsets and are critical for their adhesion to CNS endothelium [39]. Furthermore, some functions are crucial for immune cell development, localization, and pathogen clearance [40]. In short, these molecules are upregulated during inflammation and CNS injury and facilitate the binding of peripheral blood leukocytes to endothelial cells, enabling them to cross the BBB [41, 42]. Concisely, our data might indicate that anti-CD20 therapy could exert its therapeutic effects in pwMS primarily by disrupting B cell-driven immune organization and T cell pathogenicity rather than by directly blocking CNS immune cell entry. Our observations may provide a further mechanistic explanation for the efficacy of anti-CD20 antibodies across disease stages and highlight the central role of B cells as orchestrators of chronic neuroinflammation in MS.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these data. Firstly, our analyses are limited by the small patient cohort and the decline in patient numbers over time. Secondly, our data are based on peripheral blood immune phenotyping and may therefore not fully reflect immune processes occurring within the CNS, the meningeal compartment, secondary lymphoid organs, and the bone marrow. We relied on phenotypic marker expression without direct assessment of immune cell function. Although adhesion and activation markers are commonly used as surrogate indicators of migratory capacity and effector function, they do not provide direct evidence of these processes. Consequently, the functional implications of our findings—particularly with regard to cell migration and cytokine production—remain to be established. Future studies incorporating dedicated functional assays will be required to validate these observations. Additionally, changes in adhesion molecule expression in blood do not necessarily translate into proportional changes in actual CNS trafficking. Furthermore, specific immune cell populations may be more susceptible to cell death after freezing and thawing. Both treatment- and cohort-related confounders, such as prior therapies, disease stage, age, and treatment duration, may also influence immune phenotypes and cannot be fully excluded, especially in real-world cohorts.

Conclusions

Profiling of surrogate markers of cell adhesion and activation in peripheral immune cell subsets in pwMS suggests that anti-CD20 treatment could lead to a disruption of B cell-dependent immune networks. Although OCR and RTX bind to distinct sites and use distinct mechanisms, their effects were indistinguishable. Moreover, our results indicate that at least two treatment cycles are required to achieve pronounced and sustained changes in the surface expression patterns of molecules involved in cell adhesion and costimulation.

To validate and further elucidate the mechanisms suggested by these findings, future studies on immune reconstitution following B cell depletion and on altered expression patterns of adhesion molecules on immune cell surfaces will be required, ideally in larger and more homogeneous cohorts, including functional assays and healthy control comparisons.
