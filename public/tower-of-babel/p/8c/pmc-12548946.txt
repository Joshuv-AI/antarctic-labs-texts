Three‐Step One‐Pot Photocatalytic Oxidation of Feedstocks and Organocatalytic Sequential Cascade to Access Enantioenriched α‐Aminonitriles
Authors: Stefania Perulli, Sandra Ardevines, Sara Malagón, Gaukhar Khassenova, Timo Hülße, Eugenia Marqués‐López, Raquel P. Herrera, Olga García Mancheño
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A three‐step, one‐pot, sequential cascade starting from simple feedstocks to increase complexity toward value‐added chiral synthetic building blocks is reported. This is achieved by precisely integrating organic photocatalysis and noncovalent organocatalysis, often operating at dissimilar conditions and reaction media. In particular, this strategy is used to enable the direct transformation of readily available benzylic substrates, such as methylbenzenes, benzyl alcohols, or amines, into enantioenriched α‐aminonitriles by benzylic C—H photooxidation to their corresponding aldehydes, followed by in situ imine formation and final asymmetric organocatalytic Strecker reaction. The enantioenriched α‐aminonitrile products are built in moderate to good yields and moderate to high enantiomeric ratios (up to 96:4 e.r.), highlighting the effectiveness of the overall cascade process. This methodology simplifies complex synthetic pathways, enhances atom economy, and broadens the applicability of metal‐free photocatalytic methodologies, offering a robust platform for constructing chiral building blocks from abundant starting materials and feedstocks.

Introduction

Triple cascade reactions represent an appealing strategy in synthetic chemistry and catalysis, as they enable complex transformations by integrating multiple reaction steps into a single process toward the construction of decorated, often chiral, molecules.[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 ] This elegant orchestration allows the direct transformation of initial simple reactants to final highly elaborated products, minimizing the need for additional reagents, solvents, and energy inputs, thus fostering the development of greener and more efficient procedures. Such efficiency offers significant environmental and economic benefits, highlighting the versatility and importance of this strategy in modern chemistry.[ 6 , 7 ] These processes mimic natural cascades, like metabolic pathways, where enzyme‐catalyzed reactions take place in a perfect and precise sequence.[ 8 , 9 , 10 ] Inspired by nature, researchers have developed more efficient and selective syntheses for pharmaceuticals, fine chemicals, and materials. Each method involves a tailored sequence of transformations to achieve both efficiency and complexity.[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 ] Despite substantial advancements over the past decades,[ 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 ] one‐pot multistep reactions remain limited in their general applicability, mainly due to challenges associated with the compatibility of each step's reaction conditions, mutual deactivation of catalysts, or the potential instability of intermediates generated in individual elementary reactions.[ 16 , 17 ]

Over the past decade, visible light has garnered significant attention as a safe and clean energy source for driving chemical reactions.[ 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 ] As a consequence, visible light photocatalysis (PC) has emerged as a powerful technology in synthetic organic chemistry.[ 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 ] Several asymmetric strategies have been developed by employing chiral photocatalysts,[ 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 ] as well as the combination with enantioselective metal,[ 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 ] enzymatic,[ 37 , 38 , 39 ] or organocatalysis (OC),[ 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 ] the later including enamine, iminium‐ion, Brønsted acid/base, N‐heterocyclic carbene, or more recently chiral peptide‐thiol hydrogen atom transfer catalysis.[ 46 ] However, the integration of PC and enantioselective OC into one‐pot cascade reactions remains a major challenge.[ 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 ] In this regard, remarkable efforts have been done toward the development of multistep‐processes. As a result, the Rovis's group reported a pioneer one‐pot sequence involving a chiral carbene‐based (NHC) covalent catalysis for the reaction between aldehydes and in situ photo‐generated tetrahydroisoquinolinium ions (Scheme 1A, left).[ 47 ] However, most approaches still require a work‐up, a change of reaction vessel, or the switch of the solvent between the catalytic processes, as shown by the early pivotal approach of Jacobsen and Stephenson relying on noncovalent OC. In this case, the photocatalyzed oxidation of tetrahydroisoquinolines was performed in acetonitrile as solvent, whereas the subsequent thiourea‐based anion‐binding catalysis[ 48 ] had to be run in nonpolar solvents, such as ethers, at low temperature to achieve high enantioselectivities (Scheme 1A, right).[ 49 ]

A) Pivotal examples of cascade PC and enantioselective OC reactions by oxidative activation of a prochiral sp3 C—H bond, and B) our approach for the three‐step, one‐pot sequential metal‐free photocatalytic C—H oxidation, imine formation, and asymmetric organocatalyzed Strecker reaction cascade.

Aiming at addressing some of the current limitations and compatibility obstacles in the merge of tandem photo‐ and asymmetric noncovalent OC, we envisioned the development of one‐pot cascade reactions based on our recently developed benzylic photocatalytic aerobic[ 50 ] oxygenation.[ 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 ] This method proved compatible with a broad variety of solvent systems and allow the C—H bond oxidation of readily available feedstock chemicals, such as methylbenzenes[ 50 ] and benzyl alcohols,[ 50 , 56 , 57 , 58 , 59 , 60 , 61 ] with molecular oxygen to produce valuable and versatile carbonyl building blocks.[ 62 , 63 , 64 , 65 , 66 ] Thus, we herein present the first one‐pot, three‐step, sequential cascade process by integrating photo‐ and enantioselective noncovalent OC for the synthesis of enantioenriched α‐aminonitrile compounds[ 67 , 68 , 69 ] (Scheme 1B). Our approach involves a metal‐free photocatalytic C—H oxidation to build aldehydes (Int‐1), which undergo in situ imination (Int‐2), followed by an asymmetric thiourea‐based organocatalytic Strecker reaction.[ 70 , 71 , 72 , 73 ] This new approach simplifies the synthesis of enantioenriched valuable compounds by combining multiple reaction steps from feedstock molecules into a single and efficient sequence, thereby demonstrating its potential for broader application in fine chemical processes.

Results and Discussion

We started our study by shortly analyzing separately the photo‐ and organocatalytic steps with 4‐bromo benzyl alcohol 1a and benzaldehyde Int‐1a, respectively (Scheme 2 ). Initially, we investigated the possibility to switch the solvent in the oxidation of alcohol 1a to the corresponding aldehyde using the imide‐acridinium photocatalyst PC‐I [E*red: + 2.40 V vs. saturated calomel electrode (SCE)],[ 50 , 74 , 75 ] under blue LED irradiation and air as oxidant (Scheme 2A). We were delighted to find out that the reaction could be carried out not only in acetonitrile, as previously reported, but also in dichloromethane (DCM) as solvent, allowing for better integration with the planned final asymmetric organocatalytic step (see Supporting Information, Table S2 and Scheme S1 for other solvent screening). We then proceeded to examine the generation of the imine Int‐2a from the carbonyl intermediate Int‐1a, both using pyrrolidine as catalyst (20 mol%)[ 76 ] and in its absence (Scheme 2B). In this case, the imine formation was efficient in both acetonitrile and dichloromethane, the latter providing high conversions after only 2 h without the need of pyrrolidine. Based on these results, we decided to proceed by conducting the oxidation of alcohol 1a in dichloromethane within 2 h at room temperature. Moreover, the process allowed the subsequent in situ formation of the imine Int‐2a directly from the intermediate carbonyl compound Int‐1a in a remarkable high yield. This transformation was achieved under the same reaction conditions, eliminating the need for additives or external reagents, as well as change of solvent.

Screening of the reaction conditions for A) the photocatalytic oxidation and B) imine formation steps. a) Yield determined by 1H NMR using CH2Br2 as internal standard. b) Overoxidation to benzoic acid was observed.

Next, we investigated the feasibility of incorporating a one‐pot asymmetric organocatalytic Strecker reaction[ 70 , 71 , 72 , 73 ] into a complete cascade process. Therefore, following the photooxidation of alcohol 1a and the in situ formation of the imine Int‐2, the optimization of the conditions and identification of possible incompatibilities for the final organocatalytic Strecker step were carefully explored (Table 1 ). An extensive investigation of the reaction parameters was carried out, including temperature and reaction time of the final organocatalytic step, for which we used a known efficient thiourea‐shift base organocatalyst (OC‐I)[ 73 ] (see, Table S3, Supporting Information for full screening and Figure S4, Supporting Information, for other OC).

Interestingly, we observed that it was not necessary to run the reaction under argon atmosphere, as previously reported in the literature.[ 73 ] Indeed, a slightly higher enantioselectivity [80:20 e.r. (entry 2) vs. 71:29 e.r. (entry 1)] was obtained under air atmosphere. The effect of moisture was also evaluated. Thus, the presence of molecular sieves proved beneficial when using nondried DCM from the beginning to the end of the reaction (entries 3 and 4), while the reaction in dry‐DCM led to both lower yield and enantioselectivity (entry 5). Interestingly, similar yield but inferior enantioselectivity (73%, 85:15 e.r.) was obtained when running the reaction from the aldehyde (entry 7). Moreover, no improvement was observed when using the isolated imine[ 77 ] (entry 8) or by replacing the solvent between the imine formation and final Strecker reaction (entry 6), being the one‐pot process more efficient. These observations remark again the difficulties of achieving efficient cascade reactions, as some traces of remaining unreacted reagents or formed byproducts in each step of the cascade can alter the outcome of the next step. Thus, the best results in terms of reactivity (75% yield) and enantioselectivity (93:7 e.r.) were achieved using 3 Å MS at −40 ºC over 48 h under ambient atmosphere (entry 4).

Having established the optimal conditions for the complete cascade process, we next explored the scope and limitations of this methodology (Scheme 3 ). Besides employing a variety of benzyl alcohols 1, the more challenging and difficult to oxidize methylbenzenes 3, as well as benzylamines 4, which would lead to products with the same aromatic residues at both ends upon oxidation to the aldehyde and condensation with a second equivalent of the amine, were investigated.

Scope of the three‐step, one‐pot, sequential cascade reaction. a) Method A: DCM and PC‐I (5 mol%) were used in the oxidation of 1 (for 2 h) and the Strecker reaction was run at −40 °C (for 48 h). b) Method B: DCE and PC‐I (10 mol%) were used in the oxidation of 3 (for 18 h) and the Strecker reaction was run at −30 °C (for 72 h). c) Method C: DCM and PC‐I (5 mol%) were used in the oxidation of 4 (for 8 h) and the Strecker reaction was run at −40 °C (for 48 h). d) Use of PC‐II (10‐(3,5‐dimethoxyphenyl)‐9‐mesityl‐1,3,6,8‐tetramethoxyacridin‐10‐ium tetrafluoroborate) photocatalyst (E*red: +1.65 V vs. SCE)[ 81 , 82 ] in presence of 2,6‐lutidine (see Supporting Information for details).

In general, we observed that the oxidation step proceeds more efficiently with substrate bearing electron‐donating groups or in the absence of any substituent in the aromatic ring. In contrast, in the last asymmetric step, the highest enantiomeric excesses are achieved with electron‐withdrawing groups in the aromatic ring of the in situ formed imine from the corresponding benzylamine. Therefore, we first examined the reaction between partial deactivated halogenated benzyl alcohols 1 for the first step with a variety of benzylamines presenting different stereoelectronic properties (Scheme 3, top). On the one hand, para‐bromo benzyl alcohol generally showed higher yields and enantioselectivities (2a–2f), although an ortho and meta substitution was also compatible. In particular, the same enantioselectivity of 93:7 e.r. (2a) was obtained for the 2‐Br‐substituted product 2g, whereas for 2h an almost racemic mixture was formed. Moreover, aliphatic alcohols could not be enrolled as the first oxidation step was not efficient. On the other hand, the best results were obtained with the 4‐chlorobenzylamine, providing the desired α‐aminonitrile 2e in a 74% yield and 96:4 e.r. The absolute configuration of the final products 2 was assumed to be (S) by comparison with the previously reported in the literature.[ 73 ]

Having gained important insights into the reaction with alcohols 1, we next focused on the more demanding methylbenzenes 3 (Scheme 3, middle). Because of the difficulty on the oxidization of this class of substrates, we had to reoptimize the first step on this cascade procedure to ensure suitable conversion. Consequently, a change of the solvent from DCM to dichloroethane (DCE) and a prolonged reaction time (18 h vs. 2 h) were required to enhance the reactivity in the oxidation step (see, Table S4, Supporting Information, for more details). Moreover, for the last enantioselective step, –30 °C was the chosen temperature and 72 h as reaction time to reach a compromise between yield and enantioselectivity (see, Table S5, Supporting Information). In these examples (2j and 2l–s), the inherent challenge first oxidation step led to moderate overall reaction yields, while the enantiomeric ratios were of the same order, or slightly lower, than those obtained when starting from alcohols.

The robustness of the cascade method was next validated by scaling the reaction tenfold (to 1 mmol) from alcohol 1a. Under unoptimized reaction conditions, identical to those used on the 0.1 mmol scale (Table 1, entry 4), the corresponding product 2a was obtained in 40% yield and enantioselectivity of 73:27 e.r. (Scheme 4 ).

Finally, the potential for photocatalyzing the oxidation of benzylamines 4 to generate imine (Int‐2) in situ[ 78 , 79 , 80 ] as part of the same oxidative process was investigated with our photocatalytic system. In a preliminary attempt, we used twice the molar amount of benzylamine (4a). Interestingly, analysis of the reaction crude by nuclear magnetic resonance 1 H (NMR) revealed clear evidence supporting the viability of this strategy and confirmed the successful formation of the desired imine Int‐2, for which 8 h reaction time appeared to be optimal (see, Table S6, Supporting Information). Subsequently, we investigated the integration of this process into the same one‐pot cascade approach for obtaining the final Strecker products, 2j and 2t–w (Scheme 3, bottom). Although yields and e.r. ranged from low to moderate (up to 88:12 e.r.), to the best of our knowledge this represents the first example in which this procedure has been successfully integrated into a cascade process for the synthesis of enantioselective target compounds. This is particularly significant as previous studies have focused exclusively on achieving imine formation without extending the process to allow enantioselective synthesis of more complex products. Therefore, our approach highlights the potential of combining photocatalytic oxidative coupling with asymmetric OC, paving the way for more versatile and efficient synthetic methodologies in this field.

Conclusion

A three‐step cascade process was developed by successfully integrating PC, imine formation, and an asymmetric Strecker reaction in a one‐pot approach. This methodology demonstrated remarkable versatility and efficiency, providing a simplified route for the enantioselective synthesis of enantioenriched α‐aminonitriles from simple, readily available feedstocks. The photocatalytic oxidation step efficiently converted benzyl alcohols to their corresponding aldehydes under mild and sustainable conditions, showing the advantages of using metal‐free PC and air oxygen as oxidant. The in situ imine formation step proceeded smoothly without requiring additional reagents. The final Strecker reaction achieved the enantioenriched α‐aminonitriles in moderate to high yields and enantiomeric ratios (up to 96:4 e.r.), underscoring the effectiveness of the cascade process in producing valuable chiral compounds.

Furthermore, the adaptability of this methodology was highlighted through its application to the more challenging methylbenzene substrates, due to their higher oxidation potentials. In addition, the successful inclusion of benzylamines in the cascade, facilitated by photocatalytic oxidation to the aldehyde and in situ coupling with a second equivalent of the amine to directly generate the imine intermediates, expands the scope of this approach.

This novel integration not only provides a complementary pathway for enantioselective synthesis but also demonstrates the potential of the cascade process to accommodate diverse substrates. Moreover, a 10‐times up‐scaling (to 1 mmol) of the reaction was possible, thereby demonstrating the operational validity of the transformation and potential applicability in larger‐scale syntheses. Together, these results emphasize the robustness, selectivity, and sustainability of the developed methodology, which offers a powerful tool for the efficient and green synthesis of enantioenriched compounds from simple and readily available feedstock chemicals, with significant potential implications for pharmaceutical and fine chemistry applications.
