Reirradiation: Evaluation of the occurrence and type in cancer treatment for lung – 2024☆

Published: 2025

Introduction

Since its introduction into clinical practice, radiotherapy has been a field of constant evolution. Advancements in practice in recent years, including the introduction of modulated treatments and the use of image guidance, have enabled radiotherapy to be delivered with a greater precision than previously possible [1,2]. This has enabled dose escalation to target volumes while maintaining acceptable treatment related toxicities [1,3,4]. As a result of these advancements, and improvements in systemic therapies, patients are living longer and are now presenting for additional courses of radiotherapy including reirradiation [3].

European Society for Radiotherapy and Oncology (ESTRO) and European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) developed a consensus definition of reirradiation as a new course of radiotherapy either to a previously irradiated volume (irrespective of concerns of toxicity) or where the cumulative dose raises concerns of toxicity [3]. The group also defined two other types of retreatment, repeat organ irradiation and repeat irradiation. Recommendations on reirradiation reporting and clinical decision making were also made.

While reirradiation is becoming more common, there is a lack of high-quality data to guide the practice [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. When planning reirradiation, particularly in the thoracic region, there are several key considerations to ensure both effective treatment and minimisation of toxicity. These considerations include previous dose, time since previous treatment, tumour location and size, normal tissue tolerances, radiotherapy technique and total dose [3,5]. Various methods are used to assess toxicity and organ dose accumulation, including 3D rigid dose summation represented by a plan summation, deformable image registration (DIR) and point dose summation. No gold standard has been defined and therefore this may have contributed to inconsistencies in the way that this information is represented and assessed.

As reirradiation is becoming more common there is a need to reflect on current practice. Both small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) are associated with significant rates of recurrence and though the specifics vary between the two types, a subsequent course of radiotherapy can provide disease control, particularly in those deemed inoperable [5,10]. This retrospective audit aims to identify the number of patients with primary lung cancer undergoing radical thoracic retreatment in our department during 2024 and to classify the type using the ESTRO/EORTC definitions. We will explore the assessment methods used for dose overlap and organ dose accumulation, identify any gaps and inconsistencies and make recommendations to improve our current clinical practice.

Materials and method

An ethically approved (Project 25/32R, approved on 14 March 2025) audit that complied with relevant laws and institutional guidelines was undertaken across our institute’s multi-campus radiotherapy patient database to identify patients with a primary lung cancer diagnosis (Primary Site IDC10 code C34) that presented for a return course of radiotherapy in 2024. This was limited to radical treatment and an initial course with a target site for chest from a lung primary. All retreatments to the thorax were included in this audit, regardless of being recurrence, metastases or new primary lung lesions. All courses outside of the thorax were excluded for the purpose of this research. This is a retrospective study. Patient consent has been waived by the Ethics committee.

Radiotherapy treatment information was then manually searched and extracted from MOSAIQ (Elekta, Stockholm, SE) and EPIC Electronic Medical Record (Epic Systems Corporation, Verona, WI, USA) including treatment technique, modality, dose fractionation and radiotherapy start dates. During this step any outliers discovered, such as those whose first course of radiotherapy was to a different primary, those whose treatment intent changed from radical to palliative during the planning process, those who were planned to a palliative dose per RO discretion, and those who proceeded to a different treatment modality were removed. Notes made by the radiation oncologist into either of the two systems were manually interrogated for details relating to toxicity concerns or altered prescriptions due to dose overlap. Classification as palliative or radical retreatment was determined by the intent recorded in the radiation prescription in MOSAIQ, this was cross checked against the prescription dose for verification. Patients were considered to have received radical treatment if they were given a total dose ≥ 40 Gy or an equivalent dose in 2 Gy fractions (EQD2) of ≥ 40 Gy.

The remaining data was then categorised as per ESTRO/EORTC definitions below [3]:

•Reirradiation type i: a new course of radiotherapy that has geometrical overlap with the irradiated volume of previous courses.

Reirradiation type i: a new course of radiotherapy that has geometrical overlap with the irradiated volume of previous courses.

•Reirradiation type ii: a new course of radiotherapy with concerns of toxicity from the cumulative doses without overlap of irradiated volumes.

Reirradiation type ii: a new course of radiotherapy with concerns of toxicity from the cumulative doses without overlap of irradiated volumes.

•Repeat organ irradiation: a new course of radiotherapy to a previously irradiated organ but without overlap of the irradiated volumes or concerns for toxicity from cumulative doses.

Repeat organ irradiation: a new course of radiotherapy to a previously irradiated organ but without overlap of the irradiated volumes or concerns for toxicity from cumulative doses.

The ESTRO/EORTC definition includes repeat irradiation (a new course of radiotherapy to an organ that has not been irradiated, without overlap and without concerns for toxicity from cumulative doses) [3]. Patients in this category were excluded from this audit, as the focus was the clinical management of patients with multiple radical lung courses.

Assessment methods of dose overlap, and organ dose accumulation were noted. This included summation via rigid or deformable registration to map the dose distribution from the previous course to the most recent, or DVH metric summation. Note that plans with different daily dose fractionations were not converted to biologically equivalent doses for overlap assessment. The treatment technique, either 3-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3DCRT), conventionally fractionated volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) or stereotactic ablative radiation therapy (SABR), was also recorded. The time between the primary treatment course and 2024 course was also recorded, and the median calculated. Any cases where the Radiation Oncologist was aware of both lesions from the beginning and the lesions were planned at the same time were excluded from this median, as were patients who had a known second primary or previous reirradiation prior to 2024 so as not to skew the results. Even though these patients were excluded from the median calculation, they were still included in the audit.

Results

The number of patients returning for additional treatment in our department has been increasing over time. Fig. 1 demonstrates the gradual increase over an 8-year period at our institution (2016–2024). Over this time, returning cases went from accounting for 31 % to 45 % of all lung cases.

The number of primary lung patients attending for a return course of radiotherapy from 2016 to 2024.

Of the 1132 lung patients that attended across the 5 campuses of our institute in 2024, 511 were returning cases. Of these, 42 were identified as having had previous radical radiotherapy treatment for a lung primary followed by a radical course of retreatment to the thorax. Eleven (26 %) were classed as Type i, 20 (48 %) were classed as Type ii, and 11 (26 %) as repeat organ irradiation. A flowchart summarising the data collection process is presented in Fig. 2.

Flow chart showing the process of establishing the study cohort, and categorisation by reirradiation type, dose assessment method and treatment technique.

Nine (82 %) of the Type i reirradiation cases had dose accumulation assessed by a global rigid 3D dose summation, while the remaining 2 (18 %), who both were > 12 years between courses, were assessed via point dose summation. All (100 %) the Type ii reirradiation cases were assessed via a global rigid 3D dose summation, as were 10 (91 %) of the repeat organ irradiation cases. The remaining repeat organ irradiation case (9 %) did not have any overlap evaluation recorded. All cases had previous 3D radiation dose data available in the Eclipse treatment planning system (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, USA). All 42 patients in the audit had their 2024 courses planned in Eclipse, and so all 3D dose summations were able to be reviewed in the plan evaluation workspace at the time of planning.

Of the 42 cases, 13 (31 %) were treated with the same daily dose/fraction as their first course, while 29 (69 %) were treated with a different daily dose/fraction. Two of the cases treated with different daily dose/fraction had documentation indicating that EQD2 calculations were performed, one case had an EQD2 3D dose plan created and the other had EQD2 manual point dose calculations documented. Fig. 3 displays the techniques used in both the initial and subsequent course and the variety of dose/fractionation regimes that were used. Thirty-one (74 %) of the retreatment courses were delivered with a SABR technique, while the remaining 11 (26 %) were delivered with a conventionally fractionated VMAT technique. The median (range) time between the initial course of radiotherapy and 2024 course was 25 months (9 – 150 months).

Discussion

Our audit demonstrated that lung patients returning for additional courses of treatment has been on an upward trend since 2016, now accounting for 45 % of all lung cases. Our results show that dose accumulation assessment is common practice with all previous dose distributions having been made available in electronic format which is in line with ESTRO/EORTC recommendations. Preferred radiotherapy techniques for thoracic reirradiation depends on several factors, including the previous treatment and the location and size of recurrence [5]. In our department, SABR is the leading treatment technique used for lung reirradiation, particularly in cases where the volume is non-central and therefore further away from dose-limiting mediastinal structures. In our audit conventionally fractionated VMAT was found to be more common for cases where target overlap was present between the initial course and reirradiation course (i.e. Type i reirradiation), whereas where no target overlap was present (i.e. Type ii reirradiation or repeat organ irradiation) or when the target volumes had minimal overlap with previously treated organs at risk, SABR presented as the most common choice. This is consistent with findings in the literature [5,8].

Using advanced methods of image registration has the potential to provide greater confidence in the reirradiation process [11]. Deformable image registration (DIR) is an emerging technique not currently routinely used for clinical lung reirradiation assessment at our institution. Dose mapping based on deformable image registration is more efficient and has been shown to be more consistent when compared to other techniques of image alignment, such as rigid registration [12]. Using a standardised workflow including spatially mapped doses improves consistency in determination of accumulated dose in reirradiation scenarios [12]. DIR in the setting of reirradiation has a positive risk/benefit ratio especially when there is a long period of time between courses and high uncertainties in tissue changes [13]. The median time between the primary course and 2024 course seen in our study was 25 months, therefore making anatomical variations and uncertainties between courses likely in this patient cohort. Therefore, we recommend further investigation into the clinical benefits of DIR and its implementation into routine reirradiation practice.

Information on how to manage multiple situations of reirradiation is limited, in some literature it accounted for < 1 % of departmental workload, the majority of which were for metastatic bone lesions [14]. Due to the low occurrence rate, there is a lack of meaningful analysis. Literature calls for cross institution collaboration, to set up large databases from which to draw evidence-based treatment regimens [14]. Even in large institutions like ours, cases are uncommon. Fig. 4 illustrates the timeline of radiotherapy treatments for seven patients who underwent multiple radical reirradiation courses at our institution, with an average of 4 courses per patient (range 3–5). It highlights the complexity these patients would have involved, as with each additional course prescribed, not only the first course but each subsequent course needed to be considered during treatment planning [14]. This results in a combination of both type i and type ii reirradiation. The creation of a prospective registry within our department would assist radiation oncologists and therapists to consider patient outcomes when prescribing and planning future courses, particularly in complex cases. Over time this would also allow us to collect evidence to inform clinical guidelines and establish standard practice to support clinical decision making.

Our study showed inconsistencies in the assessment of dose overlap and organ dose accumulation in reirradiation cases at our institution. We have made some recommendations, summarised in Table 1, to improve our clinical processes. Our results show that the majority (82 %) of repeat organ irradiation classified patients had a dose accumulation plan created despite a lack of concerns for toxicity. Our institute currently has no formal departmental protocols for the classification and evaluation of dose in retreatment cases and therefore processes and documentation are largely dependent on the prescribing radiation oncologist, medical physics, and radiation therapy teams. Accurate classification of retreatment type would ensure appropriate workflow management and allocation of staff resources. We recommend formalising this process by making it a requirement to classify retreatment cases as per ESTRO/EORTC definitions, and to create a plan sum using either rigid or deformable 3D dose summation or point dose summation for Type i and Type ii reirradiation cases, this would not be a requirement for repeat organ irradiation.

Key recommendations from the audit.

Evaluating dose accumulation can be challenging, particularly if different dose per fraction regimes have been used. Total dose plans that have been converted to biologically effective dose (BED) may provide a more accurate picture of doses to organs at risk [15,16]. ESTRO/EORTC recommendations state that equieffective doses (e.g., EQD2) should be calculated when summating doses of treatment plans [3]. Fig. 3 outlines the variation in prescribed doses across both the initial and subsequent courses of radiotherapy. Across both the primary and the subsequent courses 10 different dose fractionation combinations were used. Of the 42 patients who underwent radical retreatment to the thorax region in 2024, 2 had radiobiological considerations documented in the form of EQD2 notes, while an EQD2 plan was only created for 1 of these patients in the treatment planning system. Dose fractionations and/or constraints to organs at risk were adjusted in some cases to be stricter per RO discretion where significant overlap and toxicity was a concern. Therefore, we recommend in future that an effective plan be documented in the planning system to demonstrate radiobiological significance of each reirradiation course classed as Type i or Type ii. A clinical note should also accompany this where dosimetric or toxicity concerns exist. Acceptable organ at risk doses for reirradiation patients is an area requiring further investigation, ideally, as more recommendations become available, they would be included in departmental policy.

Using a consistent, standardised workflow can assist in ensuring there is clear documentation of the clinical decisions made [17]. All our institution’s radical and complex palliative lung patients are presented at a cross campus departmental chart round for review prior to the start of radiotherapy. These meetings are attended by radiation therapists, radiation oncologists and medical physicists and present the opportunity for discussion and peer review of RO volumes and dosimetry. While we found in our study cohort that these discussions were documented for most patients, it would be beneficial to have this process standardised so that all chart round discussions are available in case of patients returning for further treatment. The creation of a chart round electronic template in the EMR could be considered in future to aid the efficiency of this process.

There is a large amount of research to be done in the reirradiation space. While this study focused on radical retreatment it would be beneficial to also consider dose assessment in the palliative setting as these cases make up a far greater proportion of retreatments at our institution. This can be seen in Fig. 2, where cases with a palliative primary course or palliative reirradiation, as well as those with repeat irradiation delivered to a different body site were filtered out (in total, this made up 466 cases). Though this study focused solely on the thorax region, exploring the assessment of dose overlap and organ dose accumulation in the reirradiation of other body sites would be beneficial to establish a standardised process for all tumour streams.

Conclusion

Of the 42 patients returning for radical lung retreatment in 2024, 11 (26 %) were type i reirradiation, 20 (48 %) were type ii reirradiation, and 11 (26 %) were repeat organ irradiation. Forty-one (98 %) had some form of dose accumulation assessment completed but only 2 (4.7 %) had a record of biological effective dose calculations. After comparing our current practice to the published recommendations and considerations from ESTRO/EORTC we have identified areas for practice improvement at our institution. These include classification as per ESTRO/EORTC guidelines and the calculation of biologically effective 3D dose summation plans. Documentation recommendations for our institute regarding chart round discussions have also been made to start bridging the identified gaps in our reirradiation processes.
