Increased neuroplastic activity in the pathogenesis of Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Corey J. Bolton, Panpan Zhang, Sydney R. Wilhoite, Elizabeth E. Moore, Michelle L. Houston, Kimberly R. Pechman, Logan Dumitrescu, Amalia Peterson, Omair A. Khan, Dandan Liu, Katherine A. Gifford, Kaj Blennow, Timothy J. Hohman, Henrik Zetterberg, Angela L. Jefferson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWe test the hypothesis that high levels of neuroplasticity in the context of Alzheimer's disease (AD) risk factors are involved in AD pathogenesis by investigating interactions between cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) levels of growth‐associated protein‐43 (GAP‐43) and AD risk factors (female sex, cerebrovascular risk, mild cognitive impairment, apolipoprotein E [APOE] ε4 genotype, amyloid positivity) on CSF biomarkers of AD pathology (amyloid beta 42/40[Aβ42/40], phosphorylated tau (p‐tau)) and neurodegeneration (tau).METHODSBaseline GAP‐43 levels in 161 non‐demented older adults were related to cross‐sectional and longitudinal (mean follow‐up = 4 years) CSF biomarkers of AD, adjusting for covariates, with GAP‐43 x AD risk factor interaction terms.RESULTSHigher GAP‐43 was cross‐sectionally related to all AD biomarkers (p‐values < 0.0001) and predicted longitudinal reductions in Aβ42 (p < 0.0001). Associations were stronger in AD risk groups.DISCUSSIONWe found strong support linking increased levels of neuroplasticity in the context of AD risk factors to the pathological cascade of AD over a 4‐year mean follow‐up period.Highlights Cerebrospinal fluid growth‐associated protein‐43 (GAP‐43) is associated with Alzheimer's disease (AD) biomarkers cross‐sectionally and longitudinally.GAP‐43 interacts with AD risk factors to predict AD biomarkers.Increased neuroplastic activity may play a role in AD pathogenesis.

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a complex and etiologically heterogeneous neurological disease. Although core AD pathology consists of abnormal accumulation of amyloid beta (Aβ) and tau proteins, decades of research targeting these proteins have yielded modest clinical advances. Thus there is an urgent need for innovative hypotheses regarding how core AD pathology and concomitant pathways interact to produce and accelerate dementia. One such hypothesis is that a failure in neuroplasticity, the ability of the brain to adapt and reorganize neuronal connections in response to learning, experience, and injury, may be a causative factor in AD. 1 This theory posits that known risk factors for AD, such as increasing age, apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 genotype, and low estrogen levels, all contribute to an increased cellular burden associated with neuroplastic activity, making the previously adaptive process of neuroplasticity now harmful. Increased neuroplastic activity is associated with numerous physiologic changes at the cellular level including increased tau phosphorylation 2 and increased expression of amyloid precursor protein (APP), 3 which, when dysregulated, could potentially trigger the neuropathological cascade of AD.

Growth‐associated protein‐43 (GAP‐43) is a presynaptic protein involved in synaptic plasticity through axonal outgrowth, 4 which facilitates learning and long‐term potentiation. 5 Higher GAP‐43 levels represent increased neuroplastic activity, making it an ideal candidate for testing the hypothesis that increased neuroplastic burden is associated with AD pathology. 1 Indeed, GAP‐43 has been implicated in AD, with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) levels of GAP‐43 correlating with Aβ plaque and neurofibrillary tangle burden and levels being higher in patients with AD compared to other neurodegenerative diseases. 6 CSF GAP‐43 levels have also been linked with CSF levels of tau and soluble APP, 7 cortical thinning in AD‐related brain regions, and altered functional connectivity in the default mode network. 8 , 9 Although this burgeoning body of literature suggests a key role for increased neuroplastic activity in AD, many questions remain. Existing work has focused on cross‐sectional methods in patients at later disease stages, thus limiting our knowledge of this biomarker to its expression following widespread pathology. The role of increased neuroplastic activity as a driving force in the pathogenesis of AD remains underexplored.

This study seeks to investigate the hypothesis that AD pathology emerges due to increased neuroplastic activity in the context of AD risk factors that exacerbate the cellular burden of neuroplasticity. To do so, we investigate cross‐sectional and longitudinal interactions between baseline CSF GAP‐43 levels (representing increased neuroplastic activity) and common AD risk factors, including sex, cerebrovascular risk profile, cognitive status, APOE ε4 carrier status, and amyloid positivity on CSF biomarkers of core AD pathology (Aβ and phosphorylated tau [p‐tau]) and neurodegeneration (total tau [t‐tau]). We hypothesize that higher baseline levels of GAP‐43 will be associated with all outcomes both cross‐sectionally and longitudinally, with stratified analyses showing more prominent findings in participants with additional risk factors for increased cellular burden of neuroplasticity (i.e., AD risk factors).

METHODS

Cohort

The Vanderbilt Memory and Aging Project is a longitudinal, observational study investigating vascular health and brain health among aging adults. 10 Inclusion criteria required participants to be age 60 or older, speak English, have adequate visual and auditory acuity, and have a reliable study partner. Participants underwent a medical history review, clinical interview, and neuropsychological assessment to determine study eligibility. Cognitive status was determined by consensus, including cognitively unimpaired (CU), early mild cognitive impairment (eMCI) based on the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) criteria, 11 or mild cognitive impairment (MCI) based on the National Institute on Aging/Alzheimer's Association Workgroup clinical criteria. 12 Participants were excluded for dementia diagnosis, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) contraindication, history of neurological disease (e.g., epilepsy, stroke), heart failure, major psychiatric illness, severe head injury (loss of consciousness >5 min), and systemic or terminal illness affecting follow‐up participation. Participants completed a comprehensive evaluation at study enrollment (2012–2014), including, but not limited to, lumbar puncture. All methods were repeated at 18 months (2014–2016), 3 years (2015–2018), and 5 years (2017–2020). Baseline data were defined as first time point with available CSF data, which for most participants was at study entry. Participants were excluded from the current analyses for missing CSF data, baseline covariate data, or for lacking at least one outcome at baseline.

The protocol was approved by the Vanderbilt University Medical Center Institutional Review Board. Written informed consent was obtained from participants prior to data collection. Data, analytic methods, and study materials can be obtained by contacting the corresponding author.

Lumbar puncture and biochemical analyses

Participants completed an optional fasting lumbar puncture at study enrollment and at each follow‐up visit. CSF was collected with polypropylene syringes using a Sprotte 25‐gauge spinal needle (Teleflex Medical, Wayne, PA) in an intervertebral lumbar space. Samples were immediately mixed and centrifuged, and supernatants were aliquoted in 0.5 mL polypropylene tubes and stored at −80°C. Samples were analyzed in batches using commercially available enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA; Fujirebio, Ghent, Belgium) to determine the levels of Aβ42 (Lumipulse G β‐Amyloid1‐42), Aβ40 (Lumipulse G β‐Amyloid1‐40), t‐tau (Lumipulse G Total Tau), and tau phosphorylated at threonine 181 (p‐tau; Lumipulse G pTau 181). Aβ40 was calculated only for use in the Aβ42/40 ratio to determine amyloid positivity and was not included in primary analyses due to lack of specificity for AD. 13 GAP‐43 levels were assessed using an in‐house ELISA combining the mouse monoclonal GAP‐43 antibody NM4 (Fujirebio, Ghent, Belgium) and a polyclonal GAP‐43 antibody (ABB‐135, Nordic Biosite, Täby, Sweden) with a C‐terminal epitope, as described previously. 6 Board‐certified laboratory technicians processed data blinded to clinical information. Intra‐assay coefficients of variation were <10%.

Covariates

As described previously, 14 covariates were collected at baseline and were selected a priori for their potential confounding effects. Covariates included age, sex, education, race/ethnicity, APOE ε4 carrier status, Framingham Stroke Risk Profile (FSRP) (excluding points for age), and cognitive status. APOE genotyping was performed using a TaqMan assay on DNA extracted from whole‐blood samples as described previously, 15 and APOE ε4 carrier status was defined as positive (ε2/ε4, ε3/ε4, ε4/ε4) or negative (ε2/ε2, ε2/ε3, ε3/ε3). FSRP calculations have been described previously. 14

Analytic plan

Linear regression models with ordinary least square estimates, adjusting for covariates delineated previously, cross‐sectionally related CSF GAP‐43 to CSF biomarkers of AD (Aβ42, p‐tau) and neurodegeneration (t‐tau). For longitudinal analyses, linear mixed‐effects models with subject‐level random effects and follow‐up time interactions related baseline GAP‐43 to trajectories of CSF biomarkers of AD (Aβ, p‐tau) and neurodegeneration (tau) over the follow‐up period, adjusting for baseline age, sex, education, race/ethnicity, APOE ε4 status, modified FSRP, and cognitive status. We calculated 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using the Wald method based on model standard errors. Missing data were handled with complete‐case analysis.

Follow‐up models assessed GAP‐43 x sex, GAP‐43 x FSRP, GAP‐43 x cognitive status, GAP‐43 x APOE ε4 status, and GAP‐43 x amyloid status interactions using Wald tests on the baseline and longitudinal outcomes, with subsequent models following any significant interaction models stratified by sex (male, female), FSRP (dichotomized by median split), cognitive status (CU, MCI), APOE ε4 status (positive or negative for an ε4 allele), and amyloid status (positive, negative). Amyloid status was based on the Aβ42/40 ratio, 16 with ≤0.072 as positive and >0.072 as negative. 17

Sensitivity analyses excluded predictor outcome values >4 standard deviations (SDs) from the group mean to determine if outliers influenced results. Multiple comparison correction was performed across outcomes using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure for controlling the false discovery rate (FDR). 18 FDR‐adjusted p‐values are reported for all significant models throughout the results section. Significance was set a priori at p < 0.05, and all analyses were performed using R 4.2.1 (www.r‐project.org) with random seeds saved to ensure reproducibility.

DISCUSSION

Among community‐dwelling older adults free of clinical dementia at baseline, we found strong support linking increased neuroplastic activity (as measured by CSF GAP‐43) to the pathological cascade of AD over an average 4‐year follow‐up period. Higher levels of CSF GAP‐43 at baseline were cross‐sectionally associated with higher CSF Aβ40, a finding driven largely by individuals at increased cerebrovascular risk, potentially reflecting an initial role of increased neuroplasticity in generating an overproduction of amyloid. Over time, high baseline levels of GAP‐43 were associated with reduced Aβ40, suggesting that the initial overproduction of soluble Aβ40 has transitioned to amyloid plaque formation, a hallmark feature of AD. The other hallmark proteinopathy of AD, p‐tau, and a marker of neurodegeneration, tau, were also strongly associated with GAP‐43, with these associations being significantly more pronounced in individuals at increased risk of AD dementia, including female participants, participants with MCI, APOE ε4 carriers, and amyloid‐positive individuals. Taken together, these results suggest that increased neuroplastic activity, especially when in the context of known AD risk factors, is a key driver of the pathogenesis of AD pathology, including both amyloid and tau proteinopathies.

Aberrant Aβ40 is a defining feature of AD pathology, marked initially by increased expression and then by accumulation into plaques. We found that higher levels of GAP‐43 were associated with higher levels of CSF Aβ40 (i.e., likely increased expression 19 ) at baseline, with findings driven by individuals less likely to be amyloid positive (CU and APOE ε4 noncarriers) at baseline. We also found higher GAP‐43 to be associated with higher Aβ40, independent of cognitive status or APOE ε4 carrier status. It is likely that individuals with MCI and APOE ε4 carriers have already begun to accumulate amyloid plaques, thereby not showing the effect of increased neuroplastic activity on CSF levels of Aβ40 as these proteins are accumulating in the brain. On the other hand, amyloid overexpression would cause increases in CSF Aβ40 regardless of diagnostic group, as this species is less likely to accumulate in plaques. Our findings of increased amyloid expression likely reflect the physiological functions of Aβ, which play a key role in neuroplasticity (e.g., regulating synaptic activity, guarding against excitotoxicity). 20 Expression of APP is increased during neuroplasticity 21 and has been found to co‐localize with GAP‐43. 22 We found this neuroplasticity‐related increased amyloid expression to be intensified in those individuals with greater cerebrovascular risk, especially for Aβ40. Cerebrovascular dysfunction and ischemia increase compensatory neuroplasticity. For example, in mouse models, ischemia induces more than a 40x expression of GAP‐43. 23 Simultaneously, cerebral ischemia increases the expression and activity of β‐ and γ‐secretases, culminating in an overproduction of Aβ40. 24 Cerebrovascular dysfunction thus creates a need for increased neuroplastic activity (and thereby increasing APP expression) as well as an environment in which neuroplastic activity selectively promotes Aβ40 production.

We did not find cross‐sectional associations between CSF GAP‐43 and Aβ40 in individuals with MCI, consistent with past work in a more clinically impaired cohort (i.e., MCI and dementia). 25 It would be expected that at symptomatic disease stages, CSF levels of Aβ40 would decrease as plaques begin to form, especially in individuals with increased Aβ40 production. This hypothesis is confirmed by past work associating GAP‐43 with amyloid positron emission tomography, reflecting increased Aβ plaques in the brains of individuals with more advanced disease. 26 When examined longitudinally, higher GAP‐43 levels at baseline were associated with decreases in CSF Aβ40, suggesting that neuroplasticity‐related overproduction of Aβ42 (cross‐sectional findings) leads to an aggregation of Aβ42 into plaques (longitudinal findings). Taken together, these findings suggest a key role for excessive neuroplastic activity early in the disease process that, especially in the context of cerebrovascular dysfunction, promotes Aβ42 production and eventually leads to the formation of Aβ plaques.

We also found GAP‐43 levels to be closely linked with CSF levels of p‐tau and tau proteins, consistent with past work. 25 , 26 Phosphorylation of the tau protein supports neurite outgrowth, a key neuroplastic activity. 27 Increased neuroplastic demand would thereby increase both GAP‐43 and p‐tau levels in the CSF. Thus, under normal physiological circumstances, a link between GAP‐43 and p‐tau would be expected, as observed here. Of interest, we found significant interactions between GAP‐43 and various AD dementia risk factors (i.e., female sex, MCI diagnosis, APOE ε4 positivity, amyloid positivity) such that those individuals at increased risk had much stronger associations between GAP‐43 and p‐tau and tau proteins. This finding suggests that in the context of AD risk factors and amyloid pathology, which both place an excessive burden on neuroplasticity, 1 increased neuroplastic activity leads to an overabundance of tau protein expression and phosphorylation. Of note, these associations were only observed cross‐sectionally and were not present longitudinally. The lack of longitudinal associations may reflect plateauing levels of tau pathology, as individuals with MCI or amyloid positivity showed little annual change in tau levels across the follow‐up period.

This study has several strengths. Using a very well‐characterized cohort facilitated a comprehensive consideration of potential confounding variables. The longitudinal nature of the study is a strength, particularly given the average 4‐year follow‐up time. CSF biomarker quantification was performed in research‐grade labs with technicians blinded to clinical data. There are limitations to this study as well. The cohort is predominantly non‐Hispanic White, well‐educated, and with fewer medical comorbidities than the general public, which limits generalizability to other populations. Finally, with longitudinal follow‐up, there exists a risk of more cognitively impaired or medically unhealthy individuals dropping out prematurely. However, baseline levels of GAP‐43 and all other CSF biomarkers did not differ between those 7 participants (4%) who did not complete follow‐up visits and participants who continued (data not shown).

In summary, this study identifies associations between CSF levels of GAP‐43, a marker of synaptic plasticity, and AD biomarker changes. The temporal course of these associations, with higher GAP‐43 predicting increased Aβ42 at baseline and decreased Aβ42 longitudinally, as well as strong associations with p‐tau and tau proteins, suggests that increased neuroplastic activity plays a key role in the pathogenesis of AD. Our findings that associations are far stronger in individuals with common AD risk factors further suggest that increased neuroplastic activity driving the pathogenesis of AD is a potentially unifying theory.
