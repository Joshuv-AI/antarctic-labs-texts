The fossil vertebrate primary type specimens in the collection of the University of Otago Department of Geology
Authors: Jeffrey H Robinson, Daphne E Lee, Marcus D Richards, Sophie E M White, R Ewan Fordyce
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe 47 vertebrate type specimens held in the University of Otago Geology Department are catalogued in detail. A short history of the collection is followed by lists of the type specimens under the Classes Actinopterygii, Reptilia, Aves and Mammalia. A fish trace-fossil is included at the end of the Actinopterygii. Where appropriate, the name changes of the genus or species are given in chronological order. The specimens are briefly described, locality and geological age information is provided.KEYWORDS: Geology Department, University of Otago, type catalogue, primary types, vertebrates, Actinopterygii, Reptilia, Aves, Mammalia

Introduction

The fossil collections of the University of Otago Geology Department (UOGD) comprise some 60,000 specimens. The only complete record of the collection is contained in a series of eight hard-bound paper catalogue books. Digitisation of the collection has begun, with the intention of making the collection accessible online to both paleontological researchers and to the general public. Until now, no lists of the primary types held in the UOGD collections have been published. This paper lists the vertebrate primary type specimens in the collection and subsequent publications of the primary types of the invertebrate collection and plant collection will follow.

History of the collection

The University of Otago has included ‘Geology’ as part of its BSc degree since 1885, originally in the Otago School of Mines and then in the separate Department of Geology. Early staff members Patrick Marshall (1901–1916) and Noel Benson (1916–1949) both published paleontological papers (on molluscs and graptolites respectively). The UOGD fossil catalogue book series was started circa 1940, with each specimen being given a unique ‘OU’ number. However, fossil specimens were certainly collected before then. For example, Benson et al. (1936) mentions a collection of graptolites in the UOGD made by Marshall in 1907.

The first publications featuring UOGD vertebrate type specimens were Chapman (1935) and Oliver (1936), both describing Cenozoic fossil fish, but neither paper included collection numbers. These specimens were given OU collection numbers sometime after 1975. The vertebrate collection of the UOGD comprises circa 1200 specimens, including 46 primary types, and an ichnospecies type, listed below. Twenty-five specimens are the type for both the genus and species and one specimen is the type for the family, genus and species.

A forty-year research programme by Ewan Fordyce, collecting large Late Cretaceous and Cenozoic vertebrate fossils in blocks of matrix and transporting them back to the Geology Department for expert preparation, has produced a hugely valuable series of specimens (ranging from partial skeletons, with or without the skull, to single bones), including the type specimens of whales and dolphins (Fordyce and Marx 2016), penguins (Ksepka et al. 2012), fish (Gottfried et al. 2006; 2012), a turtle (Köhler 1995) and a plesiosaur (Cruickshank and Fordyce 2002). Many of the ‘undescribed specimens’ mentioned in the review by Fordyce (1991) have been carefully analysed and described by a succession of postgraduate students, several of whom have gone on to run their own research programmes in universities and museums around the world.

A series of research projects since 2009 on the Late Oligocene (Duntroonian) Chatton Formation in eastern Southland, including discovery of an extremely rich and diverse rocky-shoreline fauna at Cosy Dell farm in Southland (Lee et al. 2014), produced a large and diverse fauna of fish otoliths, including ten new species (Schwarzhans et al. 2017; Schwarzhans 2019) many of which have descendants still living in New Zealand waters. King et al. (2009, p. 461) noted that the ‘otolith-based teleost fauna from the Tertiary of New Zealand now counts as one of the best known in the world’.

The discovery of a series of Early to Middle Miocene maars near Dunedin, including Foulden Maar (Lee et al. 2016) and Hindon Maar (Kaulfuss et al. 2018), has produced a world-class collection of invertebrates and plants and a few vertebrates, including the type specimen of a new fish species (Lee et al. 2007) and an eel (undescribed).

Primary type material in the UOGD collection

The vertebrate Primary Types listed below include members of four Classes: Actinopterygii (ray-finned fishes), Reptilia (turtles and plesiosaurs), Aves (penguins) and Mammalia (whales and dolphins). Within each Class the type specimens are listed under their Order, Family and date of publication. The fish type material is divided into skeletal specimens first and otolith specimens second, followed by a fish ichnofossil. All changes to species and genus names are listed chronologically, beginning with the original description. Each type specimen represents a single fossil animal: all type specimens are dry.

The type specimens are listed with their OU collection numbers, the type status, a short description of its preservation state and the common name of the animal. Several types include more than one OU number, but are considered to be separately numbered parts of a single type specimen. The type locality information includes: the rock formation from which it was collected, the locality name, the local area, the district, the country, the latitude and longitude, the geological epoch, as precise an age as can be given in Ma if one is published (including dates provided by Marx et al. 2024, in this volume), the New Zealand stage name, the stage boundaries in Ma if no more specific age is provided and the Fossil Record File number (preceded by ‘FRF’). The stage boundaries are from Raine et al. (2015). Useful remarks on specimens may be included.

The primary type material listed herein is held in the Geology Museum, University of Otago, or is on loan to the Otago Museum, Dunedin, as indicated below. The New Zealand Fossil Record Electronic Database (FRED) (https://www.fred.org.nz/), administered by the Geoscience Society of New Zealand and GNS Science, catalogues fossil localities by giving each a number prefixed with the NZMS260 map sheet number, followed by a running locality number.
