Reduction of Metal Artefacts in Pediatric Imaging - New Possibilities Through Photon Counting Computed Tomography
Authors: GRETA THATER, STEFAN O. SCHOENBERG, MEIKE WEIS
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background/AimMetal artefacts from medical implants significantly impair computed tomography (CT) image quality, necessitating their reduction. Photon counting technology helps to reduce artefacts through spectral information and monoenergetic reconstructions, but its use in children is limited due to radiation dose concerns. The aim of this study was to evaluate photon-counting-based metal artefact reduction (MAR) techniques for their applicability and diagnostic utility under dose-adapted conditions in pediatric patients.Materials and MethodsA 10-year-old phantom with femoral nail osteosynthesis was studied on a photon-counting CT (NAEOTOM Alpha, Siemens Healthineers) using a pediatric reference protocol (70 kVp) without iterative metal artefact reduction (MAR), a 100Sn protocol with iterative MAR, Quantum-Plus (120 kVp) after dose adjustment (Computed Tomography Dose Index volume (CTDIvol) average: 1.30 mGy), and virtual monoenergetic reconstructions (VMI) from 60 to 190 keV. Image datasets were compared based on noise and artefact index (AIx) in different regions of interest (ROI).ResultsMAR techniques reduced noise. Near osteosynthesis (ROI 1), noise reduction was 49.40% for the 100Sn protocol and 46.76% for the Quantum-Plus protocol. The artefact index was highest for the 70 kVp protocol at 58.73 and nearly halved for dose-adapted protocols with iterative MAR (100Sn: 32.90; Quantum-Plus: 29.83). Image noise decreased by 40.46% from 60 to 110 keV, with the greatest reduction near osteosynthesis (59.12). Artefact index was highest at 60 keV (18.54), decreased to 5.88 at 100 keV, and then increased to 7.58 at 190 keV.ConclusionAll MAR techniques improved CT number accuracy and significantly reduced image noise, especially near the prothesis. Using the full spectral information protocol in pediatric imaging is reasonable after dose adjustment to pediatric reference values.

Introduction

Metal artefacts, such as those caused by medical implants, e.g., metallic dental prostheses, vascular stents but also orthopedic osteosynthesis material, can significantly impair the image quality adjacent to the implant and still represent an important limitation of computed tomography (CT) diagnostics today (1). In principle, various physical phenomena underlie the formation of metal artefacts, including radiation hardening, photon starvation, and the edge effect (2). What they all have in common is the high absorption of X-rays when passing through an object with a high atomic number, such as metals (3-5).

Typically, metal artefacts appear in the form of thick, band-like hardening artefacts (dark bands) emanating from the implants themselves, surrounded by thin lines that appear alternately dark and light (stripe artefacts) around the metal implants. Metal artefacts can therefore take different forms caused by combinations of physical phenomena (2). This makes it difficult to assess not only the immediately adjacent bone-metal interface, but also the surrounding soft tissue and organ structures.

CT imaging, as used for example in postoperative follow-up or other indications such as the identification of aseptic material loosening or search of the infection focus on septic patients, is thus considerably limited, especially in its diagnostic and therapeutic validity due to artefacts (3-5).

The reduction of metal artefacts is therefore crucial to improve image quality and ensure accurate diagnosis. In recent years, various MAR algorithms for metal artefact reduction have been investigated for dual-source systems and adult patients, among others (6,7).

Photon-counting computed tomography (PCCT) has established itself as a new CT technology in recent years. In contrast to conventional CT scanners that use analogue detectors, photon-counting CT detects each photon individually, counts it and immediately converts it into an electrical signal, allowing the full spectral information to be imaged. From this spectral image data, virtual monoenergetic images can be generated using iterative reconstruction algorithms. By using a threshold, the electrical noise can be reduced and the applied dose reduced. This enables higher spatial resolution with lower radiation dose compared to previous CT techniques (1,8). PCCT offers significant clinical advantages through higher spatial resolution, improved contrast-to-noise ratio, and substantial radiation dose reduction. This is particularly relevant for dose-sensitive populations such as pediatric patients, where minimizing radiation exposure is critical due to increased radiosensitivity and lifelong cancer risk. Furthermore, the spectral capabilities of PCCT -including the generation of virtual monoenergetic images and material decomposition- allow for more precise lesion characterization and quantification. This enhances tissue differentiation and reduces metal artefacts, thereby markedly improving diagnostic confidence in complex cases such as oncology, cardiology, and musculoskeletal disorders, with direct positive impact on patient management and treatment planning.

Recent studies have already investigated the reduction of metal artefacts in PCCT of adult patients (9). However, studies in pediatric patients are scarce and non-existent. This is clinically relevant, as children are more sensitive to ionizing radiation due to ongoing tissue development, and their smaller body size increases the relative impact of metal artefacts on proportionally larger anatomical regions. Moreover, implants are often placed near growth plates, where artefact-related image degradation can hinder the evaluation of skeletal maturation and postoperative outcomes. These considerations underscore the importance of optimized, dose-efficient artefact reduction techniques specifically adapted to the needs of pediatric imaging (10).

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate photon-counting-based techniques for metal artefact reduction in terms of their applicability and diagnostic utility under dose-adapted conditions in routine pediatric clinical imaging, using a 10-year-old pediatric phantom study with unilateral femoral nail osteosynthesis. It enables a standardized, anatomically realistic assessment of photon-counting CT efficacy in reducing metal artefacts in pediatric imaging.

As a further objective, the comparison with the already available and established methods in pediatric imaging for the examination of the pelvis without special metal artefact reduction algorithms was aimed at regarding the effects on the interpretability and the diagnostic value of the resulting image data.

Materials and Methods

Ethical approval of the study was not required as it was a phantom study.

Phantom. A 10-year-old pediatric phantom (10 years, CIRS ATOM® Dosimetry phantom, Norfolk, VA, USA) was fitted with a unilateral titanium femoral nail with a diameter of 9 mm and a shaft length of 32 cm. The femoral nail was fixed to the lateral hip using fixation plaster (Leukosilk 2.5×5.0 m, BSN Medical, Hamburg, Germany) (Figure 1).

Pediatric phantom setup with femoral nail osteosynthesis. Image of the 10-year-old anthropomorphic phantom placed on the computed tomography (CT) table of the NAEOTOM Alpha (Photon-Counting CT scanner, Siemens Healthineers), fitted with a unilateral titanium femoral nail osteosynthesis. The phantom was scanned using standardized pediatric protocols for evaluating metal artefact reduction techniques.

Image generation and scan-protocols. CT imaging was performed with the Photon-Counting CT (NAEOTOM Alpha; Siemens Healthcare GmbH, Forchheim, Germany).

The phantom with femoral nail osteosynthesis was placed with its body center in the central axis of the gantry. The scan field was defined from the crista iliaca to the proximal femur. A CT scout view was created with the following scan parameters, which were used for all further protocols: 100Sn; tube voltage of 25 mAs; CTDIvol: 0.01; Pitch: 1.5.

Images were generated using the following parameters: 1) Pediatric protocol: tube voltage was 70 kVp for the pediatric reference protocol; 2) Routine dose-adapted protocol: 100 kVp with spectral tin filter (100Sn); 3) Quantum-Plus dose-adapted protocol: 120 kVp.

Automatic tube current modulation was applied for artefact reduction and for radiation dose balancing. The new manufacturer-specific image quality parameter (BQ level) was automatically selected by the system, BQ 170 for the pediatric reference protocol (70kVp). BQ level was adjusted to achieve similar CTDIvol (1.30 mGy) for the other protocols (BQ 104 for the 100Sn protocol, BQ 66 for the Quantum-Plus protocol). The gantry rotation time was 0.25 s.

Raw data were reconstructed using iterative reconstruction techniques or virtual monoenergetic reconstruction with a pixel matrix of 512×512 pixels and a slice thickness and increment of 1.5 mm in axial planes. Br60 was used for the bone kernel and Br40 for the soft tissue kernel.

The pediatric reference protocol (70 kVp) was scanned as a standard reference without iterative metal artefact reduction (MAR) (hereafter referred to as the "original image dataset").

The 100Sn protocol (100kVp) and the Quantum-Plus protocol (120 kVp) as scanner-specific adult protocols with internal iterative MAR method were scanned after dose adaptation to pediatric CTDIvol standard values of mean 1.30 mGy (range=1.29-1.31 mGy).

The spectral image data from the Quantum-Plus protocol was stored via a PCCT-specific data format, the spectral post processing (SSP) format, and virtual monoenergetic reconstructions were made using post-processing software (syngo.via, Siemens Healthineers) with a slice thickness of 1.5 mm in 10 keV-steps from 60 to 190keV.

To quantify the radiation hardening artefacts, CT numbers were measured in different regions of interest (ROI). The ROI area here is uniform at 2.915 cm2.

The four ROI positions were saved as a file and could thus always be placed at identical positions in the images of all measurements. The positions of the ROIs were determined analogously to the clinical significance: Artefacts near osteosynthesis can obscure the diagnosis of material loosening or inflammatory changes, artefacts in the small pelvis can distort the density of the bladder or, in the worst case, lead to the inability to adequately assess pelvic structures. Artefacts in the adjacent musculature may obscure a hematoma or a possible source of bleeding (7).

ROI 1 was in the immediate vicinity of the femoral osteosynthesis (ROI 1-metal), ROI 2 between the two femoral heads in the small pelvis (ROI 2-small pelvis) and ROI 3 in the gluteus muscle close to the osteosynthesis (ROI 3-muscle) (Figure 2A).

Positioning of regions of interest (ROIs) for artefact assessment. A) ROIs 1–3 were defined for quantitative assessment of artefacts in clinically relevant areas: ROI 1–adjacent to the femoral osteosynthesis; ROI 2–in the small pelvis between the femoral heads; ROI 3–in the gluteus muscle near the implant. Identical ROI positions were used across all datasets. B) ROI 4 was placed in tissue remote from the implant to serve as a reference region (artefact-free control). Artefact index (AIx) and image noise were calculated in each ROI based on standard deviation (SD) of Hounsfield Units (HU).

Another ROI was placed as a reference ROI (ROI 4-reference tissue) at the maximum possible distance from the metal implant in tissue that was visually unaffected by metal artefacts, as shown in Figure 2B.

Artefact-Index (A-Index). For general comparability and quantification of the collected ROI data of all scan protocols and keV levels with each other and with the results of previous studies (11-14), the artefact index was calculated using a previously applied formula (Figure 3). The higher the artefact index (AIx), the higher the extent of artefacts compared to the reference tissue, which is visually unaffected by artefacts. The image datasets of the PCCT-specific protocols and the VMI reconstructions were then compared with the original image dataset and with each other based on the image noise and artefact index.

Formula for calculating the artefact index. The artefact index represents the influence of the metallic foreign material on the adjacent tissue. Aix: Artefact index; SD location: standard deviation of the corresponding ROI 1, 2 or 3; SD fat: standard deviation ROI 4 (reference tissue).

In pediatric CT imaging, no internationally standardized threshold values currently exist for image noise or artefact indices. Consequently, evaluation is based on reference values intended to ensure diagnostically sufficient image quality while minimizing radiation exposure. Image noise was assessed as the standard deviation (SD) of Hounsfield unit (HU)-values in homogeneous tissue regions (e.g., soft tissues), with the literature reporting typical values of approximately 8-12 HU for pediatric thoracic and abdominal CT scans (15-17).

The artefact index is a parameter primarily used in scientific studies, for which no standardized threshold values exist; artefact evaluation is predominantly qualitative, considering the diagnostic interpretability of the images.

Results

Comparison of the mean artefact indices and image noise of the three examination protocols. The pediatric reference protocol (70 kVp) was compared with the two protocols with internal iterative MAR method (100Sn and Quantum-Plus after dose adaptation to pediatric CTDIvol standard values) in terms of image noise (represented by the SD of the mean HU values) and by means of the artefact index (Table Ι).

In near osteosynthesis (ROI 1) and in gluteal soft tissue (ROI 3), the best noise reduction of 49.40% (ROI 1) and 28.56% (ROI 3) was achieved by applying the dose-adapted 100Sn protocol. Using the dose-adapted Quantum-Plus protocol, the image noise in the vicinity of the osteosynthesis (ROI 1) was reduced by 46.76% compared to the original image data set, whereas for the gluteal soft tissue (ROI 3) it was only slightly reduced by 12.15% compared to the original image data set.

For the pelvic region, only the dose-adapted 100Sn protocol was able to slightly reduce image noise (15.56%). The dose-adapted Quantum-Plus protocol even slightly increased the noise compared to the pediatric protocol (9.34%).

Image noise around the reference tissue, which did not appear to be visually affected by metal artefacts, could also be slightly reduced by using the dose-adapted 100Sn protocol compared to the original image data set (13.71%). In contrast, the use of the dose-adapted Quantum-Plus protocol resulted in a moderate increase in image noise (26.55%).

The average artefact index across all ROIs of an examination protocol was highest for the pediatric reference protocol (70 kVp) at 58.73 and could be almost halved for the dose-adapted protocols with internal iterative MAR (100Sn protocol: 32.90; Quantum-Plus protocol: 29.83).

Comparison of the (mean) artefact indices and the image noise of the keV levels. The comparison of the standard deviations of the CT number mean values as parameters of the image noise showed that the image noise in the low-keV range (60-110 keV) decreased continuously with increasing keV level, averaging overall ROIs by approximately 40.46%. The average noise reduction was lowest for ROI 4 (17.72%) and highest for ROI 1 (59.12%). From 120 keV, the image noise remained constant from increasing keV values with an average noise increase of 5.56% averaged over all ROIs (maximum noise reduction with an average of 2.54% for ROI 3 and maximum noise increase with an average of 14.21% for ROI 1).

In summary, the application of MAR-optimized protocols (100Sn and Quantum-Plus) led to a significant reduction in both image noise and artefact burden compared to the pediatric reference protocol (70 kVp). Noise levels decreased from ±33-105 HU (reference) to ±28.57-55.98 HU, with elevated values near the metallic osteosynthesis explained by the complex anatomical conditions and metal-induced artefacts. Except for ROI 1, noise values remained within clinically acceptable ranges.

The artefact index (AIx) was reduced from 58.73 [pediatric reference protocol (70kVp)] to 32.90 (100Sn), 29.83 (Quantum-Plus), and reached its lowest value at 5.88 with 100 keV-VMI, representing a maximum artefact reduction of 89.99%. This is consistent with findings by Dong et al., who reported optimal artefact suppression at 110 keV (12).

The most pronounced relative noise reduction (81.21%) was achieved at 110 keV-VMI, further underscoring the value of high-keV reconstructions in artefact-prone pediatric CT imaging.

Discussion

Artefacts caused by orthopedic metal implants influence the image quality and diagnostic image interpretation directly at the bone-to-metal interface but also at the surrounding periprosthetic structures. To meet these technical challenges of CT and to efficiently reduce image noise, various methods for (metal) artefact reduction, e.g., for dual-source systems, have been developed in recent years, including conventional filtered back projection (FBP), iterative reconstructions (IR) with the further development of model-based iterative reconstruction (MBIR) and virtual monoenergetic reconstructions (6,18). Some studies have already addressed the impact on the interpretability and diagnostic value of the resulting image data of adult patients (2,6,7,19).

Appropriate studies for pediatric patients have so far primarily focused on dose reduction in thoracoabdominal imaging (20-22). Toso et al. compared the conventionally available advanced modelled iterative reconstruction (ADMIRE) with advanced iterative metal artefact noise reduction (iMAR) algorithms in pediatric patients with orthopedic prostheses and can be already seen from their results that the iMAR algorithm is suitable for artefact reduction, improves CT numbers close to the implant and reduces image noise (23).

Photon-counting technology makes it possible to reduce artefacts through intrinsic full spectral information and monoenergetic reconstructions. However, its application in children has been limited so far, especially regarding radiation exposure. Recent studies have investigated the reduction of metal artefacts in PCCT in adult patients (11,24).

The present phantom study investigates the applicability and diagnostic utility of scanner-specific protocols with internal iterative metal artefact reduction under dose-adapted conditions in pediatric imaging. Furthermore, it compares these to the virtual monoenergetic image data generated from the full spectral information of the Quantum-Plus data set, about their applicability for the reduction of metal artefacts in pediatric patients with inserted osteosynthesis material, but also regarding the effects on the interpretability and diagnostic value of the resulting image data.

According to the results, the most effective relative artefact and noise reduction is achieved by virtually monoenergetic generated image data at the medium keV level (110; 110 keV). The 100keV-VMI enabled the highest relative artefact reduction, reducing artefacts by 89.99% compared to the original image data set [pediatric reference protocol (70 kVp)]. The most effective relative noise reduction by 81.21%, was achieved using virtual monoenergetic at 110keV compared to the original image data set, whereby the noise could be significantly reduced especially in the vicinity of the osteosynthesis.

This level of noise and artefact reduction can be considered clinically sufficient for typical pediatric follow-up examinations, such as the assessment of bone healing or the detection of infectious complications. The image quality achieved through MAR-optimized and monoenergetic reconstructions enables reliable diagnostic evaluation while maintaining a low radiation dose. This assessment is supported by comparison with other studies whose findings are largely consistent with our findings.

Previous phantom studies (19) have already investigated the reduction of metal artefacts in photon counting CT. Analogous to our results, a relevant artefact reduction using the tin filter could also be demonstrated here. Popp et al., who report effective artefact reduction (70%) at 110 keV, as well as Rau et al. and a recent study by Patzer et al., who also compare keV levels in their PCCT studies, come to similar conclusions (11,25,26). The latter were able to demonstrate more effective artefact reduction at medium keV levels of VMI reconstructions (130 keV) compared to low keV (65 keV).

Older studies by Mangold et al. using a single source device and Lewis et al. using a dual source device also confirm efficient metal artefact reduction by VMI (27,28). However, Dong et al., Große Hokamp et al. and Laukamp et al. using a spectral detector CT conclude that VMI is only efficient in reducing metal artefacts at high energy levels -Dong et al.: 110 keV, Große Hokamp et al. and Laukamp et al.: 200 keV (12,29,30).

In a direct comparison of iMAR with VMI for examinations of metallic medical implants, current PCCT studies by von Layer et al. and Patzer et al. recommend a combination of iMAR and VMI at high energy levels (100 to 110 keV) for particularly dense artefacts for optimal artefact reduction and image interpretation (24,31). A recent study by Marcus et al. confirmed this approach and showed that combining VMI and iMAR further reduces artefacts, especially at higher energy levels (150 keV) (32). Marcus et al. cite the use of a higher tube potential of 140 kV as a possible explanation, which consistently resulted in lower artifact volumes compared to the 120 kV used in previous studies (24,31,32). The same assessment has already been reached by Neuhaus et al. in their studies on dual-energy CT scanners, which demonstrate a higher efficacy of VMI in combination with iMAR compared to VMI without iMAR and compared to iMAR in terms of artefact reduction, but also using high keV levels (140-200 keV) (33). The study by Ishikawa et al., which describes analogous observations, justifies this with the fact that the contrast is lower for images reconstructed at higher keV levels (120-150 keV) (2). Virtual monoenergetic reconstructions are also possible by dual energy CT scans, as stated before (33). The comparison between dual energy based and photon counting CT based monoenergetic reconstruction is beyond the scope of the present study, which evaluates the usability of full spectral information in dose reduced pediatric examinations.

Study limitations. The present study is a single device study. The CT device as well as the reconstruction algorithms came from a single manufacturer. This limitation arises primarily because a cross-vendor comparison of this newly implemented technology is currently not feasible, due to the limited availability of photon-counting technology across manufacturers and the small number of institutions equipped with such systems. Future studies should aim to compare different manufacturers, reconstruction algorithms, and applicability to the pediatric sector. Manufacturer-specific implementations of metal artefact reduction techniques may have variable effects on pediatric imaging and warrant further evaluation.

Moreover, the evaluation was conducted using a phantom model with unilateral femoral nail osteosynthesis. A standardized, age-appropriate pediatric phantom provided by the German Federal Office for Radiation Protection (BfS) was used to evaluate the effectiveness of different metal artefact reduction techniques in a clinically realistic simulation scenario. As the phantom must not be modified invasively, the femoral nail could not be positioned intramedullarily within the bone as would be typical in clinical practice but was instead attached externally to the lateral thigh. This positioning does not correspond to the physiological implant location and may limit the direct transferability of the results to deep in vivo implant situations. However, the aim of the study was to approximate a realistic clinical scenario under ethically acceptable conditions. Since comparable examinations in pediatric patients are not feasible due to radiation exposure, the chosen setup represents a methodologically sound and clinically relevant compromise.

The findings from this phantom study should, wherever ethically and radiologically justifiable, be translated into clinical routine and guide further investigations across different anatomical regions and implant types (e.g., axial skeleton). Additionally, future follow-up studies should focus on protocol optimization and further dose reduction in pediatric imaging.

In this context, novel approaches such as the integration of artificial intelligence-based algorithms to reduce metal artefacts in photon-counting CT hold great potential. These hybrid techniques could enable a further improvement in image quality while maintaining or even reducing the radiation dose, which is particularly important for the sensitive pediatric population.

Conclusion

The image comparison between the standard protocol without metal artefact reduction with the MAR techniques and the virtual monoenergetic reconstruction shows that all MAR techniques investigated significantly reduce image noise, especially near the prosthesis, despite dose adjustment. The most effective technique to improve image quality and diagnostic accuracy by reducing metal artefacts in children with metallic implants is virtual monoenergetic reconstruction based on the protocol with full spectral information (120kVp). It particularly facilitates the assessment of peri-implant bone structures, the detection of peri-implant inflammation or loosening, and the follow-up after orthopedic procedures. This supports precise diagnosis, therapy planning, and monitoring in pediatric patients with metallic implants, thereby contributing to improved clinical care.
