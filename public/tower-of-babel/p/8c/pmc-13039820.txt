Investigating PTSD, mental disorders, and suicide through self-comparison: a self-controlled case series study over two decades
Authors: Yi-Lung Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) significantly impacts mental health and society, yet gaps exist in understanding its temporal relationships with comorbid mental disorders. This study used self-comparison approach to explore the longitudinal relationships between PTSD and comorbid mental disorders, suicide deaths, and all cause of death, identifying risk periods and temporal sequences. A cohort study using the Taiwan National Health Insurance Research Database included adults with incident PTSD diagnosis from 2000 to 2012, followed until 2018 or death. The study employed a self-controlled case series design, and risk period encompasses the present year and five years both preceding and following the incident PTSD diagnosis, with time outside the risk period functioned as a self-controlled period for comparison. Analysis showed reciprocal relationships between PTSD and various mental disorders, especially within the year of or preceding PTSD diagnosis. However, major depressive disorders primarily emerged as a predictor of PTSD. Furthermore, an increased risk of suicide deaths was noted within three to five years post-PTSD, while no association with all cause of death was observed within five years post-PTSD. This study reveals complex, bidirectional relationships between PTSD and several mental disorders, emphasizing the need for integrated treatment and targeted prevention. Immediate attention to PTSD and comorbid mental disorders is crucial, particularly within the first year. The findings provide deeper insights for psychiatric nosology, psychiatric disorder diagnosis, treatment, and prevention.

Introduction

Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a prevalent mental health condition resulting from exposure to traumatic events. Characterized by symptoms such as re-experiencing the trauma, avoiding reminders of the trauma, negative alterations in cognitions and moods, and hyperarousal [1]. PTSD can significantly impact an individual’s quality of life, affecting their relationships, work, and overall well-being. Research estimates that approximately 70% of individuals have experienced at least one traumatic event [2] and 15% of individuals in the general population receive a diagnosis of PTSD [3]. High costs of PTSD to both individuals and society have been well-documented [4]. For instance, in the United States, the total excess economic burden of PTSD was estimated to be $232.2 billion in 2018 [5]. The high prevalence of traumatic experiences and the substantial burden of PTSD underscore the urgent need for ongoing research and intervention efforts to address this pervasive and costly mental health condition.

Individuals with PTSD commonly experience various comorbid mental disorders, with studies indicating that 88.3% of those affected by PTSD suffer from at least one additional mental health issue [6]. These mental health problems include substance use disorders [6], major depressive disorders [6], bipolar disorders [6–9], anxiety disorders [6, 7, 10–13], obsessive-compulsive disorder [6], schizophrenia [12], eating disorders [14], and suicide death [15]. Theoretical studies suggest that these comorbidities may be direct negative consequences of trauma or PTSD itself [16, 17]. However, while the prevailing body of research, predominantly cross-sectional, offers limited insight into the temporal relationships [6–8, 12, 14, 18–20], there is evidence from longitudinal studies that challenges this sequence. Empirical evidence supports three potential longitudinal relationships: (1) PTSD as a predictor of subsequent mental disorders [21–23], (2) mental disorders predicting subsequent PTSD [10, 19, 24, 25], and (3) reciprocal relationships between mental disorders and PTSD [13, 26, 27]. However, two significant research gaps persist in these studies. Firstly, these studies have examined only the longitudinal association between PTSD and one or two mental disorders. Secondly, the follow-up periods in these longitudinal studies varied significantly, ranging from a few months to two years [22, 26, 27] to as long as twenty years [21], potentially contributing to inconsistent findings.

To address the research gaps in the current literature, I conducted a retrospective cohort study, defining various risk periods for mental disorders, suicide deaths, and all cause of death before and after the incidence of PTSD to explore their potential sequences. Significantly distinct risk periods for mental disorders, either prior to or following PTSD, suggest mental disorders as potential predictors or outcomes of PTSD. Conversely, significant risk periods both before and after PTSD imply reciprocal relationships. While suicide deaths and all cause of death are definitive outcomes of PTSD, identifying sensitive risk periods is crucial for prevention efforts. Understanding the comorbidity and sequencing of PTSD with other mental disorders is crucial because the presence of comorbid disorders can complicate treatment and worsen the prognosis for both PTSD and other conditions. Additionally, elucidating their sequence and comorbidity facilitates more effective healthcare management, mental health improvement, and the implementation of appropriate prevention strategies and treatments.

Method

Data

Data used in this study was the Taiwan National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD) from Health and Welfare Data Science Center, Ministry of Health and Welfare, encompassing almost the entire population (99%). It offers a detailed repository of data encompassing medical treatments, prescriptions, and diagnostic records across outpatient, inpatient, and emergency services. To maintain confidentiality, patient identities within the NHIRD are obscured. The NHIRD utilizes the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) for disease diagnosis and recording up until 2015, switching to the Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) thereafter. This study was approved by the Central Regional Research Ethics Committee of the China Medical University (IRB number: CRREC-112-008). Informed consent is not required because this study used existing data of the NHIRD in Taiwan, which are not individually identifiable. All study procedures were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Cohort selection

A cohort of adult patients who were newly diagnosed with PTSD and with any associated mental disorders, suicide, or other causes of death. Age was determined as of January 1, 2000, with individuals under the age of 18 at that time being excluded. PTSD was identified using ICD-9-CM code 309.81 and ICD-10-CM code F43.1, as recorded in the National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD) between January 1, 2000, and December 31, 2012. To exclude cases of PTSD or any mental disorders that pre-dated the study period, individuals who were diagnosed or deceased during 1998 to 1999, were not included. The follow-up period for participants from January 1, 2000, to December 31, 2018, or until the participant’s death, whichever occurred first. Because this study utilized data from the NHIRD, the sample size was not predetermined by a priori power calculation. Instead, the sample size was fixed by the total number of eligible patients who met the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria during the study period. Including all available eligible cases in the cohort maximizes the statistical power and ensures the external validity of the findings.

Outcome

This study’s primary outcomes are incident diagnosis of mental disorders (regardless of whether outpatient or inpatient visit), suicide death, and all cause of death. For mental disorders, it comprises bipolar disorders, obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), eating disorders, major depressive disorder (MDD), substance use disorders, schizophrenia, which extracted from the National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD. The study utilized ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM codes to identify mental disorders: 296.0–296.9 and 301.13, F31 for bipolar disorders; 300.3, F42 for OCD; 300.02, F41.1 for GAD; 307.1, 307.5, F50 for eating disorders; 296.2, 296.3, 300.4, 311, F32–F34 for MDD; 304, 305.2–305.9, F10–F19 for substance use disorders; and 295, F20 for schizophrenia. For suicide deaths and related all cause of death, it was extracted from Cause of Death Data.

Statistical analysis

All data management and statistical analyses were conducted using SAS Version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). The study initially presents demographics (sex and age), age at PTSD diagnosis, and the risk of comorbid mental disorders, suicide, and all cause of death. Means with standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies with proportions for categorical variables were reported.

This study employed a self-controlled case series (SCCS) design to establish distinct risk periods for mental disorders, suicide, and all cause of death (Fig. 1). In the SCCS, time was bifurcated into a risk period and a self-controlled period. For mental disorders (Fig. 1a), the risk period encompasses the present year and five years both preceding and following the incident PTSD diagnosis. Time outside the risk period functioned as a self-controlled period for comparison. For suicide death and all cause of death (Fig. 1b), given their impossibility before PTSD, only post-PTSD risk periods were considered. Analysis of the SCCS was using the stratified Cox model, with each individual as a separate stratum (i.e., the individual served as his or her own control). The SCCS analysis necessitates that individuals with PTSD have at least one documented outcome; consequently, individuals lacking any outcomes were excluded from the analysis. The SCCS model automatically adjusts for all time-invariant factors (e.g., sex, race/ethnicity, and genetic predisposition) across an individual’s lifespan. The results of the SCCS analysis are presented as hazard ratio (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Although we used Cox regression to conduct the SCCS analysis, this approach does not require the usual proportional hazards assumption of the Cox model because SCCS analyses can also be formulated using a conditional Poisson model. Instead, the SCCS method relies on other assumptions, such as (1) events arise independently within individuals or, if nonrecurrent, are uncommon and (2) exposures do not influence event ascertainment [28]. This study satisfies these assumptions because I specifically examined incident (first-time) diagnoses of mental disorders, death by suicide, and all-cause mortality rather than recurrent events. By restricting the analyses to the first occurrence of each outcome, dependence within individual events was avoided. Results

From 2000 to 2012, the Taiwan National Health Insurance Research Database identified 16,775 adults with incident PTSD diagnosis. Of these individuals, 11,155 (66.5%, 11,155/16,775) reported experiencing mental disorders, death by suicide, or all cause of death from 2000 to 2018 (Table 1). Predominantly, the PTSD-affected participants were female (77.9%), with an average age of 35.8 years and an average age at PTSD diagnosis of 43.0 years. Discussion

In this 20-year cohort study, it reveals reciprocal relationships between PTSD and several mental disorders, including schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, eating disorders, and substance use disorders, evidenced by significant risk periods both before and after the PTSD diagnosis, especially with pronounced effects during the same year of PTSD occurrence or the preceding year of risk period. However, major depressive disorder primarily appears as a predictor of PTSD, not a consequence, suggesting a complex causality between these conditions. The study also finds an increased risk of suicide deaths within three to five years following PTSD diagnosis, but not all cause of death. These findings indicated the need for integrated treatment approaches for PTSD and its comorbidities and emphasizing the importance of targeted prevention efforts and follow-up for PTSD, mental disorder, and suicide risk.

Several factors may elucidate the bidirectional links between PTSD and other mental disorders. Primarily, commonalities in pathologies, such as genetic and biological factors, have been observed. Genetic studies reveal notable correlations between PTSD and other conditions. For instance, a moderate to strong genetic correlation exists between PTSD and depression with 0.71 to 0.80 [29], generalized anxiety disorders with 0.64 to 1.08 [30], eating disorders with 0.30 to 0.53 [31], substance use disorder with 0.35 [32]. Furthermore, dysregulation in the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, a key biological factor, has been documented in PTSD [33], as well as in schizophrenia [34], and bipolar disorder [35]. Notably, dopamine dysfunction presents similar patterns in PTSD and other common mental disorders [36]. In addition, it has been suggested that traumatic events may induce diatheses that lead to the development of either PTSD or other mental disorders, or both [37].

In addition to common etiologies between PTSD and mental disorders, PTSD and mental disorders also mutually influence each other. First, PTSD impacts mental disorders in several ways. For example, PTSD symptoms, such as avoidance and numbing, may lead to social isolation, subsequently increasing the risk of schizophrenia, depression, and suicide [16]. Furthermore, individuals with PTSD experiencing significant trauma may adopt negative coping strategies like substance use disorder, eating disorders, or suicide [38]. Conversely, mental disorders can affect PTSD; a prevalent theory suggests that preexisting psychiatric disorders heighten the likelihood of trauma exposure, which can lead to PTSD development [37]. Based on findings of this study, the risk periods for mental disorders are most pronounced during or just before the onset of PTSD suggests that the increased vulnerability to trauma due to other mental disorders occurs immediately following the onset of these disorders.

Unlike other mental disorders that exhibit reciprocal relationships with PTSD, this study observed that MDD primarily acts as a predictor for PTSD. This observation aligns with previous studies [13, 19, 24], but contrasts with reports of a bidirectional relationship between MDD and PTSD [26, 27] or cases where PTSD is solely a predictor for MDD [21, 23]. The reasons for these inconsistent findings remain unclear, though methodological differences likely contribute to the disparities. Our study utilized an adult sample (aged 18 to 64) and based diagnoses on physician assessments over five years before and after the incident PTSD diagnosis. In contrast, other studies with differing results focused on children or adolescents [23, 27], employed psychological questionnaires to define PTSD and MDD [21, 23, 26], and varied in their time frames from two [27] to twenty years [21]. A key methodological distinction of our study is the use of the self-controlled case series (SCCS) design for within-individual comparison, in contrast to the between-individual comparisons used in all other studies. The self-controlled case series design’s advantage lies in its automatic adjustment for all time-invariant variables, such as sex, race/ethnicity, and genetic predisposition, thereby reducing bias in our study.

Implications

Based on the findings regarding risk periods of mental disorders and suicide death relative to the incident diagnosis of PTSD, this study has significant clinical and theoretical implications. Clinically, it highlights the necessity of timely preventions for PTSD and other mental disorders after the occurrence of either disorder, emphasizing the importance of immediate attention following the onset of PTSD or other mental disorders. In this critical period, clinicians should be vigilant about the potential comorbidity with other mental disorders, particularly within the same or following year. Likewise, there appears to be an increased likelihood of suicide deaths occurring within three to five years post-PTSD diagnosis. Theoretically, these findings suggest a need for further research on shared etiologies between PTSD and other mental disorders. A notable example is the recent hypothesis proposing PTSD as a potential subtype of major depressive disorder [29]. These insights are crucial for refining psychiatric nosology and enhancing the diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of psychiatric disorders.

Limitations

There were several limitations in this study. Firstly, while the Taiwan National Health Insurance Research Database enabled the identification of the timing of incident diagnosis of PTSD, other mental disorders, suicide deaths, and all-cause deaths, it cannot provide information on the timing of traumatic events. Without the timing of trauma, it prevents this study from providing a deeper understanding about the complicated relationship between PTSD, other mental disorders and suicide death. Similarly, our study only determined the incident diagnosis of PTSD and other mental disorders, rather than the onset of the diseases themselves. Consequently, there could be a substantial delay between the actual onset of the disease and its diagnosis. This delay might occur, for example, when individuals seek clinical consultation long after experiencing symptoms. Secondly, although the self-controlled case series method inherently adjusts for time-invariant confounders, it remains susceptible to time-varying confounders. Future studies are warranted to adopt the within-individual comparison to replicate or explore the longitudinal relationships between PTSD, other mental disorders, and suicide deaths.
