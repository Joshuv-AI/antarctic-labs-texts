Investigation of how protein structure affects the adsorption of furan flavor compounds based on flash-evaporation treatment-induced soy protein isolate
Authors: Zhongjiang Wang, Jiankun Shi, Genna Ba, Caihua Liu, Yuan Meng, Yapeng Liu, Zengwang Guo, Haotian Liu, Hongbo Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the binding capacity and mechanism of flash-evaporation-treated SPI with 2-acetylfuran at 75–130 °C. SPME-GC/MS, spectroscopic methodologies, FRET, and ITC are used to estimate binding ability. The highest binding percentage (89.17 %) is observed at 100 °C. The conformational changes of flash-evaporation-treated SPI reveal that 100SPI exhibits the highest flexibility and solubility, as well as lower surface tension. Correlation analysis demonstrates that the structural changes of 100SPI enhance the effective binding between SPI and 2-acetylfuran, thereby improving the adsorption capacity of SPI. This work offers a theoretical foundation for flavor selection in SPI-based food production by shedding light on the mechanism through which conformational changes in flash-evaporation-treated SPI impact its capacity for flavor adsorption.Keywords: Soy protein isolate, 2-acetylfuran, Flash-evaporation treatment, Adsorption capacity, Conformational changes

Introduction

In recent years, the contemporary diet has been progressively transitioning from animal-based to plant-based foods to meet consumer demands for products that are better for the environment, food sustainability, human health, and animal welfare. Soybean stands out as a premium protein source. Due to its favorable processing qualities, soy protein isolate (SPI) has been widely used in the food industry (Zhang et al., 2022). However, SPI-based products have flavor defects and often struggle to achieve the desired flavor. As a result, flavoring SPI-based products is essential for increasing consumer acceptance. Many studies show that plant proteins can adsorb and/or bind the flavor compounds, thereby affecting the flavor perception of plant-based foods during consumption, such as SPI (Guo et al., 2020), pea protein isolate (PPI) (Guo et al., 2024), peanut protein (PP) (Chen et al., 2025), chickpea protein isolate (CPPI), and fava bean protein isolate (FBPI) (Snel et al., 2023). SPI's complex chemical sites for flavor compound adsorption make it a good flavor carrier and an essential component of SPI-based products. Therefore, SPI can adsorb flavor compounds to optimize the flavor profile of SPI-based products. SPI adsorbs flavors through interactions with flavor compounds. Developing plant products based on SPI with highly palatable flavor attributes can be made easier by gaining a thorough understanding of the relationship between SPI and flavor compounds (Kong et al., 2024).

The retention of flavor compounds in SPI-based products can be affected by changes in how SPI and flavor compounds relate to each other. These interactions include van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interactions depending on the flavor compounds and the structure of the SPI (Li et al., 2024; Wongprasert et al., 2024). Any factor that alters the structure of SPI will affect its adsorption affinity for flavor compounds. On the one hand, the increased α-helix content and decreased sulfhydryl content indicate that SPI aggregates and gets closer together (Duppeti et al., 2023). The hydrophobic binding sites and flavor binding ability are diminished as the exposed specific surface area decreases. Additionally, the intermolecular forces between SPI disrupt, leading to the unfolding of SPI. This alteration exposes internal hydrophobic cores and sulfhydryl groups, consequently increasing the number of binding sites and affinity for flavor compounds. In addition, studies report that SPI unfolding increased molecular flexibility and enhanced SPI adsorption capacity (Wang et al., 2020). Therefore, it is essential to fully comprehend how structural modifications to SPI impact its adsorption capacity, as this is crucial to comprehending its mechanism.

However, the industrial production environment of SPI is extremely complex. Current research on conformational changes and adsorption capacity of SPI mainly focuses on laboratory aspects. There are still significant differences between the laboratory and industrial results. There is almost no research on the changes in SPI adsorption capacity during industrial production processes. To better align with industrial production, flash-evaporation treatment of SPI could provide guidance for enterprises. Flash-evaporation is widely used in the food industry for processing products such as soy milk, soy flour, milk and plant-based meat It plays a crucial role in food processing. Flash-evaporation not only removes unpleasant odors from food but also serves as a powerful method to improve protein extraction rates. In recent years, flash-evaporation treatment has made considerable progress in improving protein structure. Flash-evaporation treatment is now a necessary step in the actual industrial production of protein products. Studies show that after flash-evaporation treatment, SPI aggregates dissociate, and protein molecules unfold. This increases the exposed specific surface area, affecting the flexibility and hydrophobicity of SPI molecules. (Zhang et al., 2017) Therefore, flash-evaporation treatment can also affect SPI flavor adsorption. No studies have investigated the flavor adsorption of flash-evaporation-treated SPI. The changes in the adsorption capacity of SPI treated with flash-evaporation should be thoroughly understood. In addition, the mechanism by which the conformational changes of flash-evaporation-treated SPI affect flavor adsorption capacity has not been elucidated. This restricts the development of high-flavor SPI-based products through flash-evaporation treatment.

An essential flavoring ingredient in smoked or barbecued meat is furan, an aromatic heterocyclic compound. The strength of the adsorption capacity of furan compounds for proteins significantly affects the release of meat aroma (Yu et al., 2024). Among these, 2-acetylfuran is a common furan flavor compound in plant protein-based products. It primarily exhibits smoky and roasted aromas and is found in roasted sesame seeds, fried beef, and sweet corn products. It is currently widely used in the food industry as a smoky flavor ingredient. Therefore, to understand the mechanism of changes in proteins' adsorption of 2-acetylfuran is crucial for maintaining the smoky flavor of plant protein-based products. No published studies have explained the mechanism by which changes in SPI structure influence its adsorption capacity for furan flavor compounds. This study investigates the effect of conformational changes in flash-evaporation-treated SPI on the adsorption of 2-acetylfuran. Solid-phase microextraction combined with headspace gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC/MS), spectroscopic methodologies, Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET), and isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) are used to estimate binding ability, quenching behavior, binding energies, and binding sites. The secondary structures, flexibility, and surface properties of flash-evaporation-treated SPI are measured to assess structural changes. Furthermore, we conduct a correlation analysis between the conformational changes of flash-evaporation-treated SPI and its adsorption capacity for 2-acetylfuran. The relationship between furan flavor compounds and SPI is thoroughly examined in this study. This study compares the structural differences of flash-evaporation-treated SPI at various temperatures, providing essential information on how SPI conformation influences adsorption capacity to 2-acetylfuran. It may direct the creation and modification of SPI-based product processes, improving the overall flavor of the product.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Industrial flash-evaporation-treated SPI was prepared in Shandong Wonderful Industrial Group Co., Ltd. (Dezhou, China). 2-acetylfuran was procured from Aladdin (China, Shanghai). All water was deionized, and all chemicals were analytical grade.

Industrial preparation of SPI

The industrial preparation of SPI is shown in Fig. 1. Defatted soybean meal (DSM) was dispersed in deionized water at a ratio of 1:9 (w/v), and the pH of the DSM solution was adjusted to 8.4 by the addition of NaOH, followed by hydrated. After centrifuging the mixture for 20 min at 4 °C and 8000g, HCl was used to bring the supernatant's pH down to 4.5. Following centrifugation at 10,000 g for 20 min, the precipitate was collected and subsequently rinsed three times with distilled water. Lastly, the NaOH solution was used to adjust to pH 7.0. The steam sterilization was performed at 140 °C. As the control, one portion of the protein was dried right away. The remaining portion was divided into five aliquots, which were subjected to flash-evaporation treatment at 75 °C, 85 °C, 95 °C, 100 °C, or 130 °C. These samples were labeled as 75SPI, 85SPI, 95SPI, 100SPI, and 130SPI, respectively, followed by spray drying.

Industrial preparation process of flash-evaporation-treated SPI.

Binding percentage

According to the method described by Sun et al. (2023), a 1 % SPI solution was created using deionized water. A 4 mM stock solution of 2-acetylfuran was prepared in 10 % ethanol and subsequently diluted to 2.4 mM for further experiments. The composition of the test sample included 0.4 mL of 2-acetylfuran, 6.8 mL of deionized water, and 0.8 mL of 1 % SPI. The control sample was prepared using 0.4 mL of 2-acetylfuran and 7.6 mL of deionized water. The final concentration of protein was 0.1 %. All the samples were maintained in vials at 37 °C for a duration of 12 h.

A Thermo Scientific TSQ 9000 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Shanghai, China) in conjunction with a Thermo Scientific TSQ 1310 gas chromatograph was used to determine the binding percentage. At a rate of 15 °C per minute, the temperature was raised from its starting point of 40 °C to 250 °C. The quadrupole, auxiliary heater, and ion source had respective temperatures of 150 °C, 250 °C, and 230 °C.

Time-resolved fluorescence spectroscopy

The samples were prepared following the procedure outlined in section 2.5. These time-resolved fluorescence measurements were conducted at 25 °C using a spectrophotometer (Fluorolog-3-2 Ultrafast, HORIBA Scientific, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). The wavelengths for excitation and emission were set at 290 and 330 nm, respectively.

ITC

Using the ITC200 microcalorimeter (MicroCal Inc., Northampton, UK), the thermodynamic parameters of the interactions between SPI and 2-acetylfuran were measured at 298 K (Li et al., 2024). Prior to measurement, the samples were degassed and filtered using 0.45 μm membrane filters after being prepared in accordance with the instructions in the method of 2.5. A 1 % solution of SPI made with deionized water was used to gather the flavor compounds (4 mM) into 40 μL syringes. The 1 % SPI solution was then added to the 200 μL reactive cells. By using the ‘one-binding-site’ model to fit binding isotherms, thermodynamic parameters were estimated with the help of Microcal ORIGIN software.

Particle size and turbidity

SPI, 75SPI, 85SPI, 95SPI, 100SPI, 130SPI were diluted to 0.1 % (w/v). A Nano-ZS90 zetasizer (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Great Malvern, UK) was used to measure the particle size of the supernatant samples at 25 °C (Avelar et al., 2024). Measurements of particle size distributions were conducted in triplicate, and the results were presented using mean values.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy analysis (FTIR)

Using the tablet press, the proper quantity of dried potassium bromide and lyophilized samples were compressed into thin slices. The Nicolet IS50 FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Nicolet Ltd., Waltham, MA, USA) was used to perform a full-band (4000–500 cm−1) scan with a resolution of 4 cm−1. Secondary structure fitting was performed using the Peakfit 4.12 software (Systat Software Inc., Palo Alto, CA, US).

Endogenous fluorescence

SPI samples were subjected to fluorescence spectra analysis using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (RF-6000, Hitachi Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). In short, 0.1 % was the concentration of SPI that was prepared. The scanning parameters were as follows: 280 nm for the excitation wavelength, 300–500 nm for the emission wavelength range, and 120 nm/min for the scanning speed. The emission and excitation slits were set to 5 nm.

Sulfhydryl (SH)

After thoroughly mixing 1 mL of SPI (0.1 %) with 4 mL of urea reagent (8 M) and 0.02 mL of prepared Ellman's reagent, the mixture was left to stand for 15 min in the dark. Ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectrophotometry (Genesys 10s, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) was used to measure the absorbance at 412 nm (Liu et al., 2024). Transmission electron microscope (TEM)

DI (pH 2.0) was used to dilute the samples 400 times. Using a transmission electron microscope (HT7800, Hitachi High-Technologies Corp., Tokyo, Japan) and an accelerating voltage of 100 kV, the microscopic morphology was examined.

Flexibility

The SPI was dissolved in 0.05 mol/L Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) to prepare a 1 % protein solution; 1 mg/mL trypsin enzyme solution (2500 U/mg, 16,1, v/v) was added, centrifuged at 5000g for 30 min, and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min (Cui et al., 2020). The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 280 nm and its value represents the absorbance of the sample, which indicates the degree of molecular flexibility.

Solubility

The SPI solution was prepared in PBS and centrifuged at 8000g for 20 min at 4 °C (Zhang et al., 2023). The SPI content in the supernatant was determined by the BCA method using bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard protein to draw the standard curve (y=0.7765x+0.1384;R2=0.9975); the protein solubility (%) could be obtained by calculating the ratio of SPI concentration before and after centrifugation.

Surface tension

The 1 % SPI solutions at different flash-evaporation temperatures were prepared; the time variations of surface pressure and surface dilatancy parameters were recorded at 25 °C using an optical contact angle meter to monitor the dynamic surface properties of SPI at the gas-water interface at different flash-evaporation temperatures (Ma et al., 2023). A flat needle with a diameter of 1.8 mm was used, and the injection rate and volume were 1.6 mL/min and 25 μL. The surface tension was calculated by the Young-Laplace equation in SCA20 software.

Statistical analysis

The mean ± standard deviation of the three triplicate experiments is used to present the experimental results. Duncan's test (p < 0.05) was used after a one-way analysis of variance to compare means. The analysis of the data was carried out using Origin 2021 software by OriginLab, based in Northampton, MA, USA.

Results and discussion

Binding percentages of SPI with 2-acetylfuran

HS-SPME-GC–MS is employed to analyze changes in the adsorption capacity of flash-evaporation-treated SPI for 2-acetylfuran. The adsorption percentage of SPI under different temperatures is shown in Fig. 2A. Notably, 75SPI exhibits a lower adsorption percentage for 2-acetylfuran compared to unheated SPI. This is likely due to conformational changes in SPI molecules induced by flash-evaporation at 75 °C. It is speculated that the aggregation of 75SPI reduces the available binding sites for 2-acetylfuran. This weakens the affinity between 75SPI and 2-acetylfuran, leading to a weakening of the adsorption capacity of 75SPI for 2-acetylfuran (Tavel et al., 2010). Additionally, the adsorption percentage of SPI for 2-acetylfuran initially increases and then decreases with rising flash-evaporation temperature. The maximum adsorption percentage of SPI for 2-acetylfuran is 89.17 % at 100 °C. The results indicate that the adsorption capacity of SPI for 2-acetylfuran varies significantly with temperature. Previous studies suggest that flash-evaporation treatment modifies SPI's structural features (Zhang et al., 2017). This may be due to the flash-evaporation treatment affecting the structure of SPI and the interactions with 2-acetylfuran, which in turn influences the adsorption capacity of SPI for 2-acetylfuran. To further investigate the interaction mechanism between SPI and 2-acetylfuran and its impact on adsorption capacity, FRET analysis is conducted.

Binding percentage and FRET of SPI at different heat treatment temperatures with 2-acetylfuran.

FRET of SPI with 2-acetylfuran

FRET serves as a technique for determining the separation between SPI fluorescent donors and 2-acetylfuran receptors. FRET occurs when the furan's absorption spectrum overlaps with the SPI's emission spectrum (Siddiqui et al., 2018). In Fig. 2B, the extent of overlap between the UV absorption spectrum of 2-acetylfuran and the fluorescence spectrum of SPI is displayed. This indicates that FRET may occur between flash-evaporation-treated SPI and 2-acetylfuran. Based on energy transfer theory, the energy transfer parameters in the binding process of flash-evaporation-treated SPI with 2-acetylfuran are calculated. The energy transfer parameters of the SPI-2-acetylfuran system at different temperatures are shown in Table 1. The findings indicate that the binding distance (r) between 2-acetylfuran and SPI subjected to flash-evaporation at different temperatures is below 7 nm. For varying temperatures, it is evident that the chromophore in SPI can transfer energy to 2-acetylfuran through non-radiative processes. Consequently, the non-radiative energy transfer mechanism may partly explain the reduction of SPI's intrinsic fluorescence across different temperatures induced by 2-acetylfuran. Additionally, it is found that 0.5 R0 < r < 1.5 R0. This result suggests that, in comparison to static quenching, non-radiative energy transfer contributes less to the quenching of SPI's intrinsic fluorescence. Furthermore, it demonstrates that static quenching is the main factor responsible for the decrease in the intrinsic fluorescence intensity of SPI at various temperatures (Mehranfar et al., 2013). In addition, as the flash-evaporation temperature increases, the binding distance (r) between SPI and 2-acetylfuran decreases, indicating tighter binding. This suggests that flash-evaporation treatment shortened the distance between SPI and 2-acetylfuran, enhancing SPI's adsorption capacity for furan. It is speculated that some amino acid residues of SPI are re-oriented because of flash-evaporation treatment. This change provides an enabling spatial layout for the binding of 2-acetylfuran. On the other hand, flash-evaporation-treated SPI may expose more binding sites. These binding sites may make 2-acetylfuran more accessible, thus enabling tighter binding to SPI. To further investigate the interaction mechanism between SPI and 2-acetylfuran, fluorescence quenching is performed.

Fluorescence quenching of SPI with 2-acetylfuran

Because of interactions with furan or conformational changes in SPI, the microenvironment of SPI experiences minor alterations. Meanwhile, SPI is sensitive to its local environment and can alter its intrinsic fluorescence. Fluorescence quenching, which measures the intrinsic fluorescence, can therefore be used to characterize the binding capacity of furan to SPI. The fluorescence spectra of flash-evaporation-treated SPI for 2-acetylfuran are shown in Fig. 3A. As shown in Fig. 3A, the increase in 2-acetylfuran concentration leads to a decrease in the fluorescence intensity of the SPI-2-acetylfuran complex (P < 0.05). This indicates that 2-acetylfuran has strong quenching effects on the intrinsic fluorescence of SPI. There are interactions between SPI and 2-acetylfuran. The maximum emission wavelength of SPI-2-acetylfuran shows a red shift. This indicates that Trp and Tyr residues undergo rearrangement in the SPI microenvironment (Fu et al., 2023). This may be due to the binding of SPI to 2-acetylfuran, which alters the tertiary structure.

(A)Fluorescence spectra and (B) Stern Volmer correction diagram of SPI-2-acetylfuran at different flash-evaporation temperatures at 298 K, 304 K, 310 K.

Some investigations demonstrate that a single quenching mechanism, which may be static or dynamic, can be identified using the linear range in the Stern-Volmer plot (He et al., 2021). To more closely simulate the temperature of food flavors in a real setting, the association constants for fluorescence quenching of SPI-2-acetylfuran complexes are determined at 298 K, 304 K, and 310 K. The association constants related to SPI-2-acetylfuran at three temperatures are presented in Table 2, with the Stern-Volmer plots depicted in Fig. 3B. From Fig. 3B, the Stern-Volmer curves for SPI-2-acetylfuran deviate from linearity and tend to approach the y-axis at three temperatures. It suggests that the fluorescence quenching of SPI to 2-acetylfuran is primarily accomplished by a mixture of static and dynamic quenching. For the dynamic quenching process, the frequency of diffusion and collision is higher at higher flash-evaporation temperatures, so quenching will continue to increase with increasing temperature. However, the formation of weakly bound complexes results in static quenching. This static quenching gradually decreases with increasing temperature (Zhang et al., 2018). Table 2 shows that the KSV, KD, and KS values of treated SPI decrease as the temperature increases. It indicates that the quenching mechanism between SPI and 2-acetylfuran is dominated by static quenching. At the same quenching temperature, the quenching constants of 100SPI-2-acetylfuran, including KSV, KD, and KS, show greater values than those of other SPI-2-acetylfuran complexes. Further accelerating the fluorescence quenching process is 100SPI's enhanced static quenching capacity on 2-acetylfuran. Moreover, 100SPI-2-acetylfuran shows the largest Ka and n values, indicating that 100SPI has stronger binding affinity and more binding sites with 2-acetylfuran. This means that 100SPI has a stronger flavor adsorption capacity with 2-acetylfuran, which is consistent with the previous results. This also verifies the conjecture about the binding site in FRET. In summary, flash-evaporation treatment at different temperatures does not change the quenching type of SPI with 2-acetylfuran, but it influences the degree of quenching. The observed effects are likely due to structural alterations in SPI induced by flash-evaporation treatment. To confirm the quenching mechanism and validate the FRET analysis, time-resolved fluorescence spectroscopy is performed.

Time-resolved fluorescence spectroscopy of SPI with 2-acetylfuran

Time-resolved fluorescence is yet another crucial spectroscopic technique for researching interactions between SPI and 2-acetylfuran. One crucial parameter that can be obtained from time-resolved fluorescence is the lifetime of a fluorophore. We investigated the extent of interactions by examining the variation of lifetime in the presence of ligands. The fluorescence lifetime also provides an understanding of the variation in the microenvironment of a fluorophore (Bhat & Patel, 2024). Fig. 4A shows the fluorescence decay curves of SPI-2-acetylfuran at different flash-evaporation temperatures. It exhibits dual exponential decay behavior. The results of fluorescence lifetime are displayed in Table 3. The initial fluorescence lifetime (τ0) of SPI is 3.3708 ns. As the concentration of 2-acetylfuran increases, the τ of SPI gradually decreases. This points to a dynamic quenching phenomenon taking place between SPI and 2-acetylfuran. At different temperatures, the τ0/τ value for SPI-2-acetylfuran is approximately 1, which means the fluorescence lifetime is steady. This suggests that the predominant mechanism of fluorescence quenching of SPI-2-acetylfuran is static quenching (Li et al., 2024). This confirms the results of the previous fluorescence quenching.

At various flash-evaporation temperatures, we examined the rate at which the fluorescence lifetime of SPI-2-acetylfuran changed. ITC of SPI with 2-acetylfuran

ITC is an effective method for describing the mechanism of interaction between SPI-2-acetylfuran (Chen et al., 2020). This technique evaluates the thermodynamic information of 2-acetylfuran with flash-evaporation-treated SPI at different temperatures. The results are shown in Fig. 5. The heat generated by injecting 2-acetylfuran into the SPI is represented by each peak in the isotherm, and this heat is directly proportional to the mass of 2-acetylfuran that is adsorbed or bound. Negative values are observed for all peaks in the binding isotherms during the titration process. It suggests that the adsorption process of SPI with 2-acetylfuran is exothermic. As the titration progresses, SPI binds to more 2-acetylfuran, resulting in a decrease in the available binding sites on SPI. When all binding sites on SPI are saturated, the binding isotherm tends to flatten.

It suggests that 100SPI can bind more 2-acetylfuran, indicating that 100SPI has more binding sites to 2-acetylfuran, compared to lower temperatures. According to He et al. (2021), the type of molecular bonds is determined by the sign and size of the thermodynamic constant: hydrophobic interactions are characterized by ΔH > 0 and ΔS > 0; hydrogen bonds and/or van der Waals forces are indicated by ΔH < 0 and ΔS < 0; electrostatic interactions are characterized by ΔH ≈ 0 or smaller and ΔS > 0. It suggests that the binding between SPI with 2-acetylfuran is thermodynamically favorable, spontaneous, and exothermic. Additionally, the molecular bonds are van der Waals forces and hydrogen bonds. The binding reaction between SPI and 2-acetylfuran depends critically on hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces. The primary kind of molecular bonds that bind 2-acetylfuran to SPI are reversible noncovalent linkages. These results are similar to those described previously.

Particle size and turbidity of SPI

The size and dispersion degree of SPI are detected by particle size and turbidity, which reflect its specific surface area and diffusion behavior. These factors directly affect the binding ability of SPI to 2-acetylfuran(Li et al., 2022). The average particle size and turbidity results of flash-evaporation-treated SPI at different temperatures are shown in Fig. 6A. With increasing flash-evaporation temperature, the average particle size and turbidity of SPI first increase and then decrease, with the largest particle size and turbidity occurring at 85 °C. When heated between 75 °C and 85 °C, the particle size and turbidity of SPI gradually increase. This is because flash-evaporation treatment at lower temperatures induces the aggregation of SPI particles. However, low temperatures are insufficient to disrupt intermolecular interactions and chemical bonds, resulting in larger particles. The larger particle size increases the kinetic barrier of SPI and reduces its dispersibility in solutions (Chutinara et al., 2024). Previous experiments show that 75SPI and 85SPI have lower flavor adsorption capacity than unheated SPI. The non-uniform distribution of SPI reduces the probability of colliding with 2-acetylfuran, lowering the likelihood of adsorption. In addition, the aggregation of SPI molecules will form a more compact structure. This reduces the specific surface area of 75SPI and 85SPI, masking the binding sites between SPI and 2-acetylfuran. Therefore, this structure limits the effective binding of SPI with 2-acetylfuran. When the temperature rises to 95–130 °C, the particle size and turbidity of SPI decrease. The particle size and turbidity of 100SPI and 130SPI are lower than those of unheated SPI. This is because the higher flash-evaporation temperature provides sufficient energy for the collision and dissociation of SPI. It causes SPI molecules to dissociate into smaller ones. On the one hand, the dissociation of SPI into smaller molecules exposes more hydrophobic regions, increasing its affinity for furan molecules. On the other hand, this loose SPI network provides more adsorption sites and has higher permeability. This increases the opportunities for SPI to interact with 2-acetylfuran, allowing more 2-acetylfuran to bind to SPI and enhancing the flavor adsorption effect. Among all the samples, 100SPI shows the strongest adsorption capacity for 2-acetylfuran. This result does not align with the smallest particle size and turbidity observed in 130SPI. This suggests that while particle size and turbidity significantly affect SPI's flavor adsorption capacity, they are not the only influencing factors. To further investigate the impact of SPI's structure on its adsorption capacity, we analyze the secondary structural changes of SPI after flash-evaporation treatment.

FTIR of SPI

FTIR is used to elucidate the secondary structure transition of SPI when heating, to explore the influence of secondary structure on adsorption capacity (Yan et al., 2022). The spatial structure of SPI is changed by the flash-evaporation treatment, which modifies the chemical bonds and forces between molecules. According to Fig. 6B, there is a significant change in the peak intensity of amide I among the SPI, which implies changes in the secondary structure of SPI. The fitted curve of the amide I band is obtained by fitting the second-order derivative of the amide I band in the SPI spectra at different temperatures using the Gaussian area method.

The content of secondary structure is calculated, and the results are shown in Fig. 6C. The secondary structure of flash-evaporation-treated SPI displays significant (P < 0.05) differences among the groups. The ordered structure of the protein is represented by the α-helix and β-sheet, whereas the disordered structure is represented by the random coil. With increasing temperature, the α-helix and random coil contents of SPI first increase and then decrease, while β-sheet gradually decrease. At 100 °C, the α-helix and random coil contents of SPI reached their maximum value.

In 100SPI, the random coils content increases while β-sheet content decreases. This observation indicates that flash-evaporation at 100 °C induces structural unfolding of SPI. This phenomenon not only reflects the transition of SPI from an ordered to a disordered structure but also provides compelling evidence for its enhanced flavor adsorption capacity. According to the study by Wang, Li, et al. (2022), when the β-sheet content is low, and the random coil content is high, the secondary structure of the protein typically shows greater flexibility and looseness. Specifically, this disorder in the structure makes the 100SPI molecule more prone to swelling and loosening, allowing its backbone structure to unfold more easily. This structural flexibility is crucial for the flavor compound capture capacity of 100SPI, as it facilitates the interaction between the protein and flavor compounds, thereby improving its flavor adsorption capacity. In the structure of 100SPI, the lower β-sheet content is closely related to the reduced degree of protein aggregation. Studies show that β-sheet structures are usually located in the interior of protein molecules and are not easily exposed to the external environment (Lian et al., 2023). Therefore, the reduction in β-sheet content indicates that the hydrophobic sites of SPI are more exposed, providing more surface area for interaction with 2-acetylfuran. This phenomenon is consistent with the results of fluorescence quenching experiments, which show that the interaction between 100SPI and 2-acetylfuran is most significant, further confirming the role of reduced β-sheet content in promoting flavor adsorption. However, when the temperature increases to 130 °C, the random coil content of SPI begins to decrease. The disordered structure of 130SPI is reduced, making the overall structure more orderly. As a result, the flavor adsorption capacity of 130SPI has decreased. This indicates that excessively high temperatures may lead to a more compact and regular structure of SPI, thus reducing its interaction with flavor compounds.

Endogenous fluorescence of SPI

The changes in the fluorescence spectrum of SPI are primarily reflected through the characteristics of endogenous fluorophores such as tryptophan. The alterations in the microenvironment of these fluorophores can reveal the conformational changes of SPI, thereby indirectly indicating its adsorption capacity for flavor compounds (Yan et al., 2022).

When temperature increases to 100 °C, SPI exhibits a red shift from 332 nm to 337 nm, accompanied by a decrease in fluorescence intensity (Fig. 6D). We find a correlation between the unfolding behavior of the protein structure and the red shift. During the heating process, SPI gradually unfolds, leading to the exposure of buried tryptophan residues. This increases the surface hydrophobicity of protein. As a result, hydrophobic flavor compounds such as 2-acetylfuran are more likely to bind to the protein through hydrophobic interactions, thereby enhancing the flavor adsorption capacity of SPI. The fluorescence intensity of SPI gradually decreases at higher temperatures, which is related to fluorescence quenching (Na et al., 2023). This suggests that SPI may undergo collisions under heating conditions, leading to conformational rearrangements and changes in the fluorophores. Elevated temperatures facilitate interactions between tryptophan residues and other molecules, resulting in fluorescence quenching, which indirectly reflects the enhanced adsorption behavior of SPI.

SH of SPI

The change in free SH content corresponds to the formation and breakage of disulfide bonds (S—S). This can further affect the binding and adsorption of SPI with flavor compounds like 2-acetylfuran. From Fig. 6E, the free SH content of SPI first decreases and then increases with increasing temperature, reaching the lowest value at 95 °C. The S—S content does not change significantly between 75 and 95 °C. However, when temperature increases to 100 °C, the S—S content decreases noticeably. The initial decrease in free SH groups is likely attributed to their oxidation into S—S during flash-evaporation treatment. At this stage, the flavor adsorption capacity of SPI is low because the formation of S—S occurs along with the folding of SPI. The interactions between SPI molecules increase, forming soluble aggregates (Tang et al., 2024). The exposed SH groups are re-embedded, reducing the number of reactive free groups, which weakens the interaction between SPI and 2-acetylfuran. Furthermore, between 75 and 95 °C, the S—S content is higher, indicating that SPI is more stable and less likely to interact with 2-acetylfuran. When the temperature increases to 100 °C, the elevated temperature causes partial unfolding of SPI and the breaking of S—S. The buried SH groups inside SPI are moved to the surface, providing numerous binding sites for flavor ligands and increasing the likelihood of interaction with flavor compounds. The increase in free SH content may also indicate the exposure of sulfur-containing hydrophobic amino acids, enhancing the hydrophobicity of the SPI surface and increasing the hydrophobic areas available for flavor adsorption. Therefore, flash-evaporation treatment at 100 °C increases the degree of SPI unfolding, raises the free SH content, and promotes its ability to bind with flavor compounds.

TEM of SPI

TEM can investigate the morphological characteristics and internal structure of SPI. The TEM results of flash-evaporation-treated SPI are shown in Fig. 7A. A homogeneous dispersion of natural SPI is observed, consisting of smaller particles. SPI begins to aggregate at 75 °C. With increasing temperature, SPI aggregated to a greater extent, forming larger aggregates with some degree of cross-linking. This aggregation may be due to the denaturation of some SPI during flash-evaporation treatment. The unfolding of SPI and the exposure of hydrophobic groups can result from heating. This facilitates the formation of aggregates through hydrophobic interactions among SPI molecules. 100SPI aggregation decreases while cross-linking increases. This may be attributed to the increased denaturation of 100SPI. The denatured SPI structure unfolds, providing bridges for polymerization (Xiang et al., 2021). This cross-linking can form more hydrophobic pockets, exposing more hydrophobic groups, thereby enhancing flavor adsorption capacity. Therefore, the cross-linking structure of 100SPI is more favorable for the adsorption of 2-acetylfuran. At 130 °C, partial disruption of cross-linked regions is observed. The SPI structure transforms into numerous aggregates, exhibiting a sheet-like structure. At this stage, the number of binding sites decreases. As a result, this hinders the adsorption of SPI to 2-acetylfuran.

Flexibility of SPI

The ability of SPI to adjust to changes in the external environment is referred to as flexibility. It is closely related to its secondary structure, the degree of exposure of hydrophobic groups, and intramolecular interactions. Flexibility also affects the ability of flavor compounds to bind to SPI. (Liu et al., 2012).

As shown in Fig. 7B, compared to unheated SPI, the flexibility of 75SPI and 85SPI decreases (P < 0.05); however, as the temperature increases, the flexibility of SPI increases significantly. The flexibility of SPI is the lowest at 85 °C. According to the results of FTIR, SPIs at lower temperatures have a higher β-sheet content and a more ordered structure. This reduces molecular flexibility. In addition, flash-evaporation treatment at lower temperatures causes the aggregation of SPI. In this case, the hydrophobic region could be hidden inside the SPI molecule. SPI forms a more rigid structure and loses some binding sites. Therefore, SPIs at lower temperatures have a lower flavor adsorption percentage. However, there is a discernible increase in SPI's flexibility as the temperature rose. 100SPI shows the highest flexibility. This is consistent with the results of the adsorption percentage. This could be due to elevated temperatures attacking amino acid side chains, causing the dissociation of SPI molecules. This modification has the potential to disturb various intramolecular bonds within proteins, facilitating their transformation from stable to disordered structure (Yuan et al., 2024). This is consistent with the results of FTIR. SPI with high flexibility has strong conformational variability and could adapt to the morphology and properties of flavor compounds by adjusting its structure. This enhances the flavor affinity of SPI. In addition, SPI with high flexibility exposes more hydrophobic regions, providing additional binding sites. This characteristic increases the binding rate of SPI to flavor compounds. Therefore, SPI with high flexibility has a stronger adsorption capacity for flavor compounds. As the temperature increases to 130 °C, the flexibility of 130SPI begins to decrease. Excessively high temperatures induce aggregation of 130SPI, forming dense aggregates. The disordered structure of 130SPI is reduced, resulting in a more ordered overall structure. In addition, the aggregated 130SPI has fewer flavor binding sites. As a result, the flavor adsorption capacity of 130SPI decreases. This indicates that excessively high temperatures may cause SPI to become denser and more regular, thereby weakening its flavor adsorption capacity.

Solubility of SPI

The solubility of SPI is closely related to its flavor adsorption capacity. Fig. 7C shows the solubility of flash-evaporation-treated SPI. As the temperature rises, the solubility of SPI first increases and subsequently decreases. Flash-evaporation treatment significantly enhances the solubility of SPI compared to the control. This is explained by the fact that heating breaks down the structure of protein aggregates and encourages hydration between water molecules and the side chains of amino acids or protein peptide chains, which increases solubility (Kong et al., 2023). The solubility of 100SPI reaches its peak. SPI with high solubility has uniformly dispersed in solutions, forming a more stable state. This uniform dispersion state increases the contact area and binding sites, helping flavor compounds to fully contact protein molecules in the solution, thereby improving the adsorption efficiency of flavor compounds. Secondly, SPI with high solubility could increase the stability of the solution. This stable system helps reduce the volatilization or loss of flavor compounds and enhances the effective adsorption of SPI and flavor compounds (Wang et al., 2018).

Nevertheless, the solubility of SPI130 decreases significantly. This is attributed to the fact that elevated temperatures greatly enhance the hydrophobicity of 130SPI. After hydrophobic binding exceeds a critical level, the solubility decreases. At this point, the contact area between 130SPI and flavor molecules decreases, and the flavor adsorption capacity weakens. This indicates that better solubility may provide more SPI for adsorbing flavor compounds. Therefore, flash-evaporation treatment at 100 °C improves the solubility of SPI, thereby enhancing its flavor adsorption capacity.

Surface tension of SPI

Through adsorption, SPI molecules alter the surface activity of the liquid interface. The adsorption behavior of SPI is not only related to the intrinsic properties of the molecules but also influenced by their surface activity. Consequently, the adsorption capacity of SPI for flavor compounds is also affected by its surface tension. Generally, lower surface tension indicates stronger adsorption capacity. Fig. 7D illustrates the changes in surface tension of SPI over 10 min under different temperatures. As the flash-evaporation temperature increases from 75 °C to 130 °C, the surface tension of SPI initially increases and then decreases. Lower temperatures (75–85 °C) promote further aggregation of SPI, leading to reduced surface activity and increased surface tension. SPI molecules form more compact structures, which reduces the opportunities for flavor compounds to bind to SPI. Additionally, an increase in surface tension indicates a weakening of the adsorption capacity of SPI molecules at the gas-liquid interface, thereby reducing the flavor adsorption capacity (Guldiken et al., 2023). However, when temperature rises (95–130 °C), the surface tension decreases, indicating that the SPI aggregates unfold and stretch. The exposed hydrophobic groups increase the surface hydrophobicity of the protein, significantly enhancing SPI's adsorption capacity for flavor compounds. The unfolded SPI molecules exhibit stronger amphiphilicity, enabling more effective adsorption at the interface and enhancing surface activity. This increases the contact opportunities between SPI and flavor compounds at the interface, thereby improving the adsorption capacity (Wang, Wang, et al., 2022). Finally, the unfolded SPI molecules have more flexible conformations, potentially forming more active sites, providing additional binding sites for flavor compounds. However, the surface tension of 100SPI is not the lowest, which is inconsistent with the adsorption percentage results. This implies that surface tension is one of the main elements affecting SPI's ability to adsorb flavors.

Correlation analysis

Pearson correlation analysis is used to examine the relationships between the flavor and structural characteristics of flash-evaporation-treated SPI.

As presented in Fig. 7E, flexibility, solubility, random coil content, and the adsorption capacity of SPI exhibit significant positive correlations. The correlation coefficients of flexibility and solubility with adsorption capacity are 0.8 and 0.78, respectively. This indicates that the flexibility and solubility of flash-evaporation-treated SPI are the main factors contributing to their adsorption capacity. On the other hand, the particle size, turbidity, surface tension, and β-turn content show highly significant negative correlations with the adsorption capacity of 2-acetylfuran. These results indicate that flash-evaporation treatment at 100 °C may disrupt the internal interactions of SPI, leading to its unfolding. This enhances the disorder and flexibility of SPI and decreases the surface tension. These conformational changes increases the chances of SPI interacting with 2-acetylfuran, thereby enhances its flavor adsorption capacity. However, flash-evaporation treatment at other temperatures promotes protein aggregation. This ordered, compact structure buries binding sites and reduces solubility and flexibility. As a result, SPI struggles to adsorb 2-acetylfuran, leading to a decrease in flavor adsorption percentage.

In summary, the combined effects of high flexibility, high solubility, and disordered structure are key to enhancing flavor adsorption capacity. Rigid structures and molecular aggregation significantly inhibit flavor adsorption capacity. Flash-evaporation treatment at 100 °C precisely regulates this balance, markedly improving the adsorption capacity of SPI for 2-acetylfuran.

Conclusion

The flash-evaporation-treated SPI at different temperatures exhibit varying adsorption capacities for 2-acetylfuran. The SPI treated at 100 °C shows the highest adsorption capacity (89.17 %) for 2-acetylfuran. 2-acetylfuran quenches the fluorescence of SPI through a combined static and dynamic quenching mechanism, where the static quenching is predominant. The interaction between flash-evaporation-treated SPI and 2-acetylfuran is primarily driven by hydrogen bonding or van der Waals forces. The study compares the structures of flash-evaporation-treated SPI at different temperatures and analyzed the conformational changes induced by flash-evaporation. In comparison, flash-evaporation at 100 °C induces the maximum unfolding of SPI. This treatment significantly reduces particle size and turbidity. The 100SPI becomes more hydrophobic, flexible and soluble. At the same time, its surface tension drops significantly. These structural changes enhance the effective binding between SPI and 2-acetylfuran, thereby improving the adsorption capacity of SPI. This study investigates the interaction mechanisms between 2-acetylfuran and flash-evaporation-treated SPI and explores the effect of SPI conformational changes on flavor adsorption. The study provides a theoretical framework for optimizing flavor in foods produced with SPI. In the future, we can explore more typical flavor compounds to verify the adsorption patterns of flavor compounds with different structures.
