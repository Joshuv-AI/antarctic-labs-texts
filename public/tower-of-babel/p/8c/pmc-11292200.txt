Exposure–Response and Subgroup Analyses to Support Body Weight–Based Dosing of Brentuximab Vedotin in Children and Young Adults with Newly Diagnosed High-risk Classical Hodgkin Lymphoma
Authors: Zufei Zhang, Daping Zhang, Wenchuan Guo, Keenan Fenton, Sujata Narayanan, Shweta Jain, Joy Jiang, Sharon M. Castellino, Kara M. Kelly, Peter D. Cole, Frank G. Keller, Amit Garg, Yen Lin Chia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose:The purpose of the study was to evaluate the relationships between brentuximab vedotin (BV) pharmacokinetics, age, and body weight (BW) with efficacy and safety in pediatric and young adult patients with previously untreated, high-risk classical Hodgkin lymphoma in the phase III AHOD1331 study.Experimental Design:Overall, 296 patients (age 2–21 years) in the overall population were randomized to and received BV + chemotherapy; the pharmacokinetic subpopulation comprised 24 patients (age <13 years). Age- and/or BW-based (pharmacokinetic surrogates) subgroup analyses of efficacy and safety were conducted for the overall population. Exposure–response analyses were limited to the pharmacokinetic subpopulation.Results:There were no visible trends in disease characteristics across pediatric age subgroups, whereas BW increased with age. Observed antibody–drug conjugate exposures in patients ages <12 years were lower than those in adults administered BV 1.8 mg/kg every 3 weeks, as exposure increased with BW. Nevertheless, no detrimental impact on event-free survival was seen in younger subgroups: 3-year event-free survival rates were 96.2% (2–<12 years) and 92.0% (12–<18 years), with no events observed in those ages <6 years. Neither early response nor lack of need for radiation therapy was associated with high pharmacokinetic exposure. No evidence of exposure-driven grade ≥2 or ≥3 peripheral neuropathy or grade ≥3 neutropenia was seen in exposure-safety and BW-based subgroup analyses; the incidence of these safety events was comparable across pediatric age subgroups, despite lower exposure in younger children.Conclusions:No further adjustments based on age or BW are required for the BV dosage (1.8 mg/kg every 3 weeks) approved in children.

Translational Relevance

The CD30-directed antibody–drug conjugate brentuximab vedotin (BV) is currently approved as first-line treatment for adult patients with advanced Hodgkin lymphoma in combination with doxorubicin/vinblastine/dacarbazine and for pediatric patients ages ≥2 years with previously untreated high-risk classical Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL) in combination with doxorubicin/vincristine/etoposide/prednisone/cyclophosphamide at 1.8 mg/kg every 3 weeks. In the current study, relationships between BV pharmacokinetics, age, and body weight were assessed using data from patients ages 2 to 21 years with high-risk cHL in the open-label, multicenter, randomized phase III trial (AHOD1331). Although BV exposures in patients ages <12 years were lower than those in adult patients, no detrimental impact on event-free survival was seen in younger subgroups, with safety profiles comparable across age groups. Thus, the BV dosing regimen (1.8 mg/kg every 3 weeks) approved for use in pediatric patients with previously untreated high-risk cHL does not require further adjustment.

Introduction

Hodgkin lymphoma (HL), a B-cell lymphoproliferative disorder, represents 3% of all cancers diagnosed in children ages ≤14 years and 11% of all cancers diagnosed in adolescents/young adults ages 15 to 19 years (1). In 2020, an estimated 800 adolescents/young adults ages 15 to 19 years were diagnosed with Hodgkin lymphoma in the United States (2). Historically, the treatment of pediatric Hodgkin lymphoma has involved chemotherapy, including anthracyclines and alkylating agents administered alone or in combination with radiation therapy, with more intensive regimens reserved for high-risk patients. Although the overall efficacy of conventional therapy is such that 5-year event-free survival (EFS) rates range from 85% to 92% (3) and 5-year disease-free survival rates approach 95% (4), the risk of all-cause mortality in pediatric patients with Hodgkin lymphoma remains higher than that in the age-matched population (3, 4); this increased risk can be attributed to high cumulative doses of chemotherapy and extended-field radiation therapy. Therefore, continued refinement of the first-line treatment of pediatric HL is needed.

The use of tumor biology to guide the development of novel treatments for pediatric Hodgkin lymphoma began with CD30-directed therapy. Brentuximab vedotin (BV) is an antibody–drug conjugate (ADC) comprising a CD30 mAb covalently attached to monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE), a microtubule-disrupting agent, via a protease-cleavable linker (5). After binding to CD30, BV is internalized, and the dipeptide bond is efficiently cleaved by lysosomal proteases to release MMAE (6). In the United States, BV is approved for the treatment of adult patients with select CD30-expressing hematologic malignancies, including classical Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL), systemic anaplastic large-cell lymphoma (ALCL), peripheral T-cell lymphoma, and primary cutaneous ALCL, at an intravenously infused dosage of 1.8 mg/kg every 3 weeks (Q3W) or 1.2 mg/kg every 2 weeks (Q2W; ref. 7). In November 2022, BV 1.8 mg/kg Q3W was approved by the FDA for use in combination with chemotherapy in pediatric patients ages 2 years and older with previously untreated high-risk cHL (8).

Approval of BV in pediatric patients was based on the results of the phase III AHOD1331 study (9). In this study, pediatric and young adult patients (2–21 years) with previously untreated high-risk cHL were randomized to receive open-label treatment with BV + chemotherapy (doxorubicin, vincristine, etoposide, prednisone, and cyclophosphamide) for a maximum of five cycles or standard care (doxorubicin, bleomycin, vincristine, etoposide, prednisone, and cyclophosphamide). A bleomycin-free regimen was sought because of the adverse effects of this drug on the pulmonary system (10–12). The primary endpoint of AHOD1331 was met, with a 3-year EFS rate of 92.1% in the BV + chemotherapy group and 82.5% in the standard-care group (P < 0.001). Importantly, replacement of bleomycin with BV did not lead to an increase in toxicity (9).

The pharmacokinetics (PK) of BV has been extensively studied in clinical trial populations consisting primarily of adult patients (13–15). Notably, exposure following intravenous administration of BV has been shown to be consistent across tumor types (13–15) when used as a single agent or as part of combination therapy (14, 15). Body weight is the only known covariate with a clinically significant effect on the PK of BV, with heavier patients exhibiting faster clearance (13–15). Limited data exist on the PK of BV in pediatric patients, but lower PK exposures for both ADC and MMAE were observed in younger patients with lower body weight receiving BV under body weight–based dosing in two open-label phase I to II studies (16, 17), and BV ADC exposures are known to increase with body weight (18).

Here, we report observed PK data from the PK subpopulation of the phase III AHOD1331 study, composed of 24 patients ages <13 years, to confirm whether exposures are lower in younger patients receiving body weight–based dosing of BV. Exposure–response analyses were also performed to evaluate whether there were exposure-driven effects on either efficacy or safety. The size of the PK subpopulation in AHOD1331 was limited by design. As such, subgroups based on age and/or body weight, which served as surrogates of exposure, were created from the overall population of patients treated with BV 1.8 mg/kg Q3W in AHOD1331 (N = 296). Efficacy and safety outcomes were examined in these subgroups to assess further the adequacy of BV 1.8 mg/kg Q3W in pediatric patients across a range of ages/body weights.

Materials and Methods

Participants

As emerging clinical data from pediatric patients with advanced Hodgkin lymphoma demonstrated that the clearance of BV trended with body weight, with younger pediatric patients having lower PK exposures than adult patients (17), the protocol of the AHOD1331 study was amended to explore the PK of BV in children ages <13 years. Twenty-four patients treated with BV + chemotherapy in AHOD1331 opted in to the PK substudy and had ≥1 evaluable PK sample (herein referred to as the PK subpopulation). As the size of the PK subpopulation was small by design, age-based and/or body weight–based subgroup analyses were performed on all patients who were randomized to and received ≥1 intravenous dosage of BV (1.8 mg/kg Q3W) + chemotherapy (N = 296; herein referred to as the overall population) to further characterize the impact of PK on clinical outcomes. In these subgroup analyses, age and body weight served as surrogates for exposure.

Bioanalytical assays

Serum ADC concentrations were measured via a validated ELISA with a mAb against MMAE used for capture and a monoclonal anti–idiotypic-cAC10 used for detection (lower limit of quantitation, 12.5 ng/mL; ref. 19). Plasma MMAE concentrations were measured by LC/MS-MS following solid-phase extraction (limit of quantitation, 25 pg/mL; ref. 19).

PK analysis

Blood samples for the PK analysis were obtained at steady-state Cycle 4 [predose; end of infusion (day 1 of cycle); and 24-, 48-, and 96-hour postdose] and Cycle 5 [predose (day 1 of cycle)]. A population PK model previously developed for the analysis of BV exposures in adult patients (13) was refined to fit PK data collected from AHOD1331 (9) and two other pediatric studies of BV (16, 17). Individual empirical Bayesian estimates of PK parameters [e.g., AUC, maximum concentration (Cmax), and average concentration (Cave)] from the validated pediatric population PK model (18) were used for quantifying Cycle 1 and Cycle 4 exposures for ADC and MMAE.

Exposure–response assessments in the PK subpopulation

The primary efficacy endpoint in AHOD1331, EFS (defined as the time until disease progression, relapse, development of a second malignancy, or death), was only analyzed in subgroup analyses performed on the overall population because none of the patients in the PK subpopulation experienced a qualifying event for ≥3 years. However, the relationships between ADC exposure and the secondary efficacy endpoints of early response and response-directed radiation therapy rates were evaluable in the PK subpopulation. Early responses were centrally evaluated per Deauville criteria (20) after two cycles of BV + chemotherapy, with rapid-responding lesions defined as Deauville score ≤3. Patients needing response-directed radiation therapy (regardless of whether it was actually received) were defined as those with any slow-responding lesion at a site other than large mediastinal adenopathy after two cycles of BV + chemotherapy. Exposure–efficacy analyses were performed only for ADC, the primary analyte associated with efficacy (15), because MMAE exposures decline over time (13, 15) and are likely confounded by disease remission. Larger declines in MMAE exposure are associated with improved disease status (18). Cycle 1 Cave (Cycle 1 AUC0–21d) was used as the exposure metric in the exposure–safety analysis, as BV dose modification was minimal (<10%) and to avoid the bias introduced by the decline in MMAE exposure over time. A similar approach has been proposed for immuno-oncology agents (21).

Age- and body weight–based subgroup analyses in the overall population

EFS, early response rate, and response-directed radiation therapy rate were evaluated in age-based subgroups from the overall population (2 to <12 years vs. 12 to <18 years vs. 18–21 years). The adverse events (AE) most frequently associated with BV are peripheral neuropathy (PN) and neutropenia; these safety outcomes were evaluated to explore whether any relationship to exposure existed in pediatric patients, as correlative relationships have been observed in BV-treated adult patients (14). In accordance with the study protocol, information was not collected on most grades 1 to 2 AEs; thus, the present analysis focused on grade ≥3 AEs, except for PN. PN events of grade 2 or higher were recorded in AHOD1331 and graded per the modified Balis Pediatric Scale of Peripheral Neuropathies (22). Because body weight was found to be a reasonable surrogate for ADC exposure under body weight–based dosing (18) and lower BV exposures in younger children are primarily driven by body weight, additional body weight–based subgroup analyses were undertaken in the overall population (<50 kg vs. 50 to <60 kg vs. 60 to <70 kg vs. ≥70 kg) to confirm whether there was any relationship between exposure and grade ≥2 PN and grade ≥3 neutropenic events.

Results

Characteristics of the overall population

Among the 296 patients in the overall population, the median (range) age at baseline was 15 (3–21) years, with 1.7% (n = 5) of patients ages 2 to <6 years, 15.8% (n = 47) ages 6 to <12 years, 74.3% (n = 220) ages 12 to <18 years, and 8.1% (n = 24) ages ≥18 years. The median (range) body weight at baseline was 57.4 (15.4–178.4) kg. Body weight distribution and baseline disease characteristics by age group are illustrated in Fig. 1. As expected, body weight increased with age, such that the proportion of pediatric patients in the BV + chemotherapy arm weighing <50 kg decreased from 80.8% among those ages <12 years to 21.4% among those ages 12 to <18 years (Fig. 1A). The median body weight was 33.5 kg in those ages 2 to 12 years and 59.9 kg in those ages 12 to <18 years. Except for body weight, no obvious visual trends were noted for baseline disease characteristics [i.e., disease stage (Fig. 1B), B symptoms (Fig. 1C), and bone marrow involvement (Fig. 1D)] when patients were stratified by age.

Distribution of (A) body weight, (B) disease stage, (C) B symptoms, and (D) bone marrow involvement at baseline by age subgroup (overall population). Chemotherapy comprised doxorubicin, vincristine, etoposide, prednisone, and cyclophosphamide. Standard care comprised doxorubicin, bleomycin, vincristine, etoposide, prednisone, and cyclophosphamide. B symptoms were defined as weight loss, night sweats, and fever. Bulk was defined as large mediastinal adenopathy (transverse tumor diameter more than one third the thoracic diameter at the dome of the diaphragm on a 1.83-m posterior–anterior upright chest radiograph) or extramediastinal bulk (a continuous aggregate of nodal tissue outside the mediastinum that measured >6 cm in the transverse dimension on axial CT or the longest dimension on coronal or sagittal reformatted CT).

Patient characteristics and exposure metrics in the PK subpopulation

Of the 24 patients in the PK subpopulation, the median (range) age and body weight at baseline were 11 (3–12) years and 38.4 (15.4–87.2) kg, respectively. A total of 12.5% (n = 3) of patients in the PK subpopulation were aged 2 to <6 years. The PK subpopulation represented 38.7% (24/62) of patients ages <13 years in the overall population. Body weight distribution was similar in the 24 patients in the PK subpopulation and the other 41 patients ages <13 years in the overall population who were not enrolled in the PK substudy (Supplementary Fig. S1). As expected, the median age (11 vs. 15 years) and body weight (38.4 vs. 57.4 kg) at baseline were lower in the PK subpopulation than in all patients enrolled in the overall population (i.e., irrespective of age). When stratified by body weight, 70.8% (n = 17) of patients weighed <50 kg, 8.3% (n = 2) weighed 50 to <60 kg, 16.7% (n = 4) weighed 60 to <70 kg, and 4.2% (n = 1) weighed ≥70 kg.

In the PK subpopulation, the mean (SD) trough concentrations of ADC before infusion were comparable at 0.795 (0.357) and 0.887 (0.322) µg/mL for Cycle 4 and Cycle 5, respectively, indicating achievement of steady state. In Cycle 4, the geometric means [percentage coefficient of variation (%CV)] of AUC0–21d (in which d = days) and the corresponding average concentration over 21 days (Cave) were 75.9 (28.8) μg × day/mL and 3.61 (28.8) μg/mL, respectively, for ADC and 8.44 (52.9) ng × day/mL and 0.40 (52.9) ng/mL, respectively, for MMAE (Supplementary Table S1; time course in Supplementary Fig. S2). In Cycle 1, estimated BV ADC exposures generally overlapped with those observed at steady state (Cycle 4), which is consistent with the reported minimal accumulation of ADC. However, MMAE exposures were higher in Cycle 1 versus Cycle 4, suggesting a decline in MMAE over time, as previously reported (13, 23). For both analytes, lower exposures were noted in younger patients (Supplementary Table S2); exposures in patients ages ≤12 years were lower than those previously reported in pediatric patients ages 12 to 17 years (16, 17).

Exposure–response assessments in the PK subpopulation

No exposure–response assessment was conducted because the 3-year EFS in the PK subpopulation was 100%. In addition, only a limited number of patients in the PK subpopulation did not attain an early response (n = 3) or require response-directed radiation therapy (n = 2). However, the Cycle 1 ADC Cave of these patients was within the range of those who experienced an early response and those who did not require response-directed radiation therapy, respectively (Fig. 2). The early response rate (83.3% vs. 91.7%, respectively) and rate of response-directed radiation therapy (8.3% vs. 8.3%, respectively) in patients with Cycle 1 ADC Cave below (<) the median value were comparable with those whose Cycle 1 ADC Cave was equal to or above (≥) the median value.

Cycle 1 ADC Cave in (A) patients with and without an early response and (B) patients needing and not needing response-directed radiation therapy (PK subpopulation). Medians are indicated by the red dashed lines. For n = 2, the median was calculated as the mean; a scatter plot is provided due to the low number of datapoints.

With regard to safety, Cycle 1 ADC and MMAE exposures were similar between patients in the PK subpopulation who experienced grade ≥2 or grade ≥3 PN (median time to onset, 31 and 19.5 days, respectively) and those who did not (Supplementary Fig. S3). Cycle 1 ADC and MMAE exposures were also comparable in patients who did and did not develop grade ≥3 neutropenic events (median time to onset, 11 and 11.5 days, respectively; Supplementary Fig. S4). Furthermore, the incidence rates for these safety events were consistently comparable when patients were stratified by Cycle 1 ADC and MMAE exposures. No ADC exposure–driven trends were noted for safety in patients whose Cycle 1 ADC Cave was < versus ≥ the median value. The observed incidence rates of grade ≥2 PN were 25.0% and 16.7%, respectively. The incidence rates of grade ≥3 PN, grade ≥3 febrile neutropenia, and grade ≥3 neutrophil count decrease were identical (8.3% vs. 8.3%, 50.0% vs. 50.0%, and 58.3% vs. 58.3%, respectively). Similarly, the observed incidence of these safety endpoints did not increase with Cycle 1 MMAE exposure. When patients were grouped by Cycle 1 MMAE Cave < versus ≥ the median value, the observed incidence rates of grade ≥2 PN were 25.0% and 16.7%, respectively; of grade ≥3 PN were 8.3% and 8.3%, respectively; of grade ≥3 febrile neutropenia were 58.3% and 41.7%, respectively; and of grade ≥3 neutrophil count decrease were 66.7% and 50.0%, respectively.

Subgroup analysis of efficacy in the overall population

As previously reported (9), the 3-year EFS rate [95% confidence interval (CI)] in the BV + chemotherapy arm was 92.1% (88.4%–94.7%). In the age-stratified subgroup analysis of the overall population, the median (95% CI) 3-year EFS rates were comparable between younger children and adolescent patients, with a rate of 96.2% (91.1%–100%) in those ages 2 to <12 years and 92.0% (88.5%–95.7%) in those ages 12 to <18 years (Fig. 3) despite lower exposures in younger children.

With regard to the secondary efficacy endpoints, the early response rate (95% CI) and response-directed radiation therapy rate (95% CI) in the overall population of BV-treated patients were 79.3% (74.3%–83.8%) and 13.3% (9.7%–17.7%), respectively. Consistent with the lack of an observable relationship between age and EFS, there was no evidence that younger children had lower early response rates or higher rates of response-directed radiation therapy. The early response rates for patients ages 2 to <12 years, 12 to <18 years, and ≥18 years were 80.8% (42/52), 80.5% (177/220), and 75.0% (18/24), respectively (Fig. 4A), and the rates of response-directed radiation therapy were 11.5% (6/52), 11.8% (26/220), and 20.8% (5/24), respectively (Fig. 4B).

Subgroup analysis of PN in the overall population

In the AHOD1331 study, the overall incidence rates of grade ≥2 and grade ≥3 PN were similar in the BV + chemotherapy and standard-care arms (Fig. 5). In the age-stratified subgroup analysis of the overall population, the incidence rates for grade ≥2 and grade ≥3 PN in the BV + chemotherapy arm were comparable between younger children (<12 years) and adolescents (12 to <18 years); however, it was slightly higher in young adults (18 to <22 years; Fig. 5A). The median time to onset of grade ≥2 PN was 40.5 days in the BV + chemotherapy arm and 47.5 days in the standard-care arm. In the BV + chemotherapy arm, despite lower ADC and MMAE exposures, a more rapid median time to onset of grade ≥2 PN was observed among those ages <12 years (19.5 days; n = 10) relative to those in the older subgroups (41–43 days; n = 244); however, this may have been attributable to the limited number of patients ages <12 years (n = 10). Thus, despite higher ADC and MMAE exposures, there was no evidence of an increased rate of either grade ≥2 or grade ≥3 PN among adolescents (12 to <18 years) versus young children ages <12 years. Similarly, patients with higher body weight tended to have higher exposures. Although patients ≥70 kg had a numerically higher incidence of PN, rates of this AE were comparable across the lower body weight subgroups, supporting the lack of a relationship between exposure and PN in pediatric patients (Fig. 5B).

Subgroup analysis of neutropenia in the overall population

In AHOD1331, the overall incidence rate of grade ≥3 neutropenia was similar for the BV + chemotherapy and standard-care arms (Fig. 6); the median time to onset of grade ≥3 neutropenia was 12 days and was comparable across age subgroups. No apparent relationship between BV exposure and the incidence of grade ≥3 febrile neutropenia was observed when patients were stratified by age (Fig. 6A) or body weight (Fig. 6B). The reduction in BV exposure in younger patients of lower body weight did not associate with a numerically lower incidence of grade ≥3 febrile neutropenia; in fact, febrile neutropenia was slightly increased among those ages <12 years, with four of five patients ages <6 years experiencing grade ≥3 febrile neutropenia. A slightly higher occurrence of grade ≥3 neutrophil count decrease was observed in the BV + chemotherapy arm versus standard-care arm in AHOD1331 (Fig. 6). Consistent with febrile neutropenia, the incidence of grade ≥3 neutrophil count decrease trended higher in the younger subgroups (Fig. 6C) but was comparable across body weight subgroups (Fig. 6D). The lack of exposure–response relationships for both grade ≥3 febrile neutropenia and grade ≥3 neutrophil count decrease was confirmed in the PK subpopulation, in which Cycle 1 Cave for ADC and unconjugated MMAE was similar in patients who did and did not experience grade ≥3 neutropenic events (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Discussion

The concentration–time profile of BV in younger patients is consistent with that reported for adult patients: serum ADC concentrations peak at the end of infusion and decline multiexponentially, whereas MMAE plasma concentration–time curves follow formation-limited kinetics, with a delayed time to maximum concentration of approximately 2.2 days (Supplementary Fig. S2). As in adults, BV ADC concentration–time profiles in pediatric patients generally overlap between Cycle 1 and Cycle 4, and the time-varying PK of MMAE is reflected by declining concentrations over time; the decline in MMAE over time may be due to changes in disease burden or inflammatory conditions (18). In relation to adult patients (13, 23), both ADC (1.56 vs. 0.88 L/day) and MMAE (55.7 vs. 35.1 L/day) clearance values were lower in the pediatric PK subpopulation. This was driven by the lower body weight of pediatric patients. After correcting for body weight, BV clearance in pediatric patients is comparable with that seen in adult patients (23).

Consistent with the literature, PK exposures under body weight–based dosing are numerically lower in younger pediatric patients than in older children and adults (17). In the AHOD1331 PK subpopulation, the exponents for the effect of body weight on ADC clearance and volume of distribution were 0.63 and 0.73, respectively, following allometry scaling (data on file). Therefore, a body weight–adjusted dose would lead to lower exposure in younger children (due to lower body weight) because the dose reduction is greater than the decrease in the body’s capacity to clear BV along the age continuum. Indeed, at steady state (Cycle 4), Cmax for BV ADC was 28.5 μg/mL, which represents a 15% reduction from the 34.4 μg/mL value reported in adult patients administered the same dose (24). Of the 24 patients in the PK subpopulation, only one, a patient of age 3 years, had the dose of BV reduced to 1.2 mg/kg at Cycle 4; this reduction was per protocol guidelines and was accounted for. In adult patients with relapsed CD30-positive lymphoma (n = 12), observed Cycle 1 geometric mean (%CV) for AUC0–21d and Cmax were 76.65 (31) μg × day/mL and 31.98 (29) μg/mL, respectively, for the pharmacologically active analyte ADC and 0.036 (47) μg × day/mL and 0.005 (43) μg/mL, respectively, for MMAE (25). In comparison with simulated Cycle 1 exposures (Supplementary Table S2), Cycle 1 AUC0–21d for ADC and MMAE were reduced by approximately 13% and 40%, respectively, in the AHOD1331 PK subpopulation versus adult patients. The magnitude of the reductions in BV PK exposures was similar to that observed in children ages 6 to <12 years. In an open-label, phase I/II dose-escalation study of pediatric patients with relapsed/refractory Hodgkin lymphoma or systemic ALCL, the observed median Cycle 1 ADC and MMAE AUC0–∞ values in younger pediatric patients ages 6 to <12 years (median body weight, 29.1 kg; n = 12) were lower by 12% and 59.0%, respectively, than those seen in pediatric patients ages 12 to 17 years (median body weight, 56.5 kg; n = 24) whose body weight was closer to that of adults (16). Although the patient number is small (n = 3), moderate reductions in observed ADC and MMAE exposures were noted in patients ages <6 years in relation to those aged 6 to <12 years (Supplementary Table S3), consistent with the lower body weight of younger patients (Fig. 2A). Given the principle of exposure matching, body surface area (BSA)–based dosing was proposed to reduce the PK variability observed between young children and adolescents/adults. Exposures following intravenous infusion of BV 48 mg/m2 Q2W in pediatric patients matched those in adult patients administered BV 1.2 mg/kg Q2W (26); consequently, this BSA-based dosage of BV was approved in Japan for the treatment of advanced cHL in combination with doxorubicin, vinblastine, and dacarbazine.

Despite lower exposures in younger patients, 3-year EFS rates in the overall population of patients treated with BV + chemotherapy in AHOD1331 were generally similar across age subgroups (2 to <12 years, 96.2%; 12 to <18 years, 92.0%), suggesting that younger age and the associated reduction in BV exposure do not result in declines in treatment efficacy. The secondary efficacy endpoints of early response rate and response-directed radiation therapy rate were also similar across age subgroups, and neither outcome was associated with higher PK exposures. In addition, there were no obvious trends in patient disease characteristics across pediatric age subgroups. BV exerts its targeted antitumor activity by binding specifically to CD30; following internalization, its cytotoxic payload, MMAE, a microtubule-disrupting agent that induces cell cycle arrest at the G2–M phase, is released (27). Because cell division occurs more frequently in younger patients than in adult patients, BV may be more efficacious in pediatric patients, thereby compensating for the reductions in drug exposure. Lower exposures in pediatric patients may also mitigate any potential increased sensitivity to cytotoxicity. Indeed, in pediatric patients with previously untreated high-risk cHL, administration of up to five cycles of BV (cumulative dose of 9 mg/kg) + chemotherapy demonstrated a favorable risk–benefit profile (9). The study protocol for AHOD1331 included dose-modification guidelines that involved reducing the day 8 dose of vincristine to maintain the BV dose in patients who developed grade ≥2 PN (7). This strategy was successful, leading to lower rates of BV dose reduction and grade ≥3 PN in AHOD1331 (8% and 6.7%, respectively) than those observed in the ECHELON-1 study of adults (29% and 11%, respectively; refs. 9, 28). In AHOD1331, rates of grade ≥3 neutropenic events trended higher in those ages <12 years despite lower exposures to ADC and unconjugated MMAE. Baseline neutrophil counts were not available in AHOD1331. A similar, numerically higher incidence of grade ≥3 neutropenia was observed in patients ages <12 years versus 12–18 years from the C25002 study (data on file), although the subgroup of patients ages <12 years had lower neutrophil counts at baseline. Therefore, there seems to be no exposure-driven grade ≥3 febrile neutropenia or grade ≥3 neutrophil count decrease in pediatric patients despite lower exposures.

Although these PK and exposure–response exploratory analyses were limited by a small sample size and lacked statistical power, the age-based and body weight–based subgroup analyses performed on the overall population indicated consistent efficacy and safety across age subgroups; age and body weight in these subgroup analyses served as surrogates for PK exposure in pediatric patients. Among adult patients treated with BV 1.8 mg/kg, efficacy and safety (PN and neutropenia) trend higher with increased body weight, as patients of greater body weight tend to have higher PK exposure (18). Additionally, the reductions in exposure observed in younger patients have been shown to be driven primarily by body weight rather than age (data on file). Taken together, these results add to the available evidence demonstrating that the approved dosing regimen of BV (1.8 mg/kg Q3W) for pediatric patients with previously untreated high-risk cHL does not require adjustment based on age or body weight.
