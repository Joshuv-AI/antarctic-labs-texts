Allowable robot-related material cost as a contribution margin threshold for financial sustainability of robot-assisted urologic surgery
Authors: Atsunari Kawashima, Takuji Hayashi, Masataka Kawamura, Yoshiyuki Yamamoto, Yu Ishizuya, Norichika Ueda, Shigeaki Nakazawa, Kentaro Takezawa, Taigo Kato, Koji Hatano, Yoichi Kakuta, Norio Nonomura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRobot-assisted surgery offers well-recognized clinical advantages; however, its financial sustainability at the institutional level varies substantially across procedures and healthcare systems. We evaluated the institutional financial implications of robot-assisted urological surgery using an allowable cost framework designed to clarify how robot-related expenditures restrict practical robotic resource utilization. The allowable robot-related material cost (ARMC) was defined as the maximum robot-related variable material cost at which robot-assisted surgery achieves contribution margin equivalence with conventional laparoscopic surgery under existing reimbursement conditions. ARMC was calculated for representative urological procedures, including adrenalectomy, radical nephrectomy, nephroureterectomy, and radical cystectomy. For adrenalectomy, ARMC adjusted for operative time was additionally calculated to assess time-based economic efficiency and the impact of operative duration on contribution margin. ARMC values were 24,903 yen for adrenalectomy, 46,472 yen for radical nephrectomy, − 7,139 yen for nephroureterectomy, and 92,591 yen for radical cystectomy. The negative ARMC for nephroureterectomy indicates that contribution margin equivalence was not observed under current reimbursement and cost structures. In adrenalectomy, ARMC increased to 70,415 yen after adjustment for operative time, suggesting improved procedural efficiency and highlighting the economic importance of operative duration. Based on this allowable threshold, routine use of a three-arm configuration, including one camera arm, was necessary to maintain contribution margin equivalence. The financial impact of robot-assisted urological surgery is highly procedure dependent. The ARMC framework quantitatively defines procedure-specific tolerance for robot-related variable costs and clarifies practical constraints on robotic resource allocation, providing a pragmatic institutional framework for maintaining financial sustainability while preserving clinical benefit.Keywords: Financial sustainability, Robot-assisted surgery, Urologic surgery, Health economics, Reimbursement

Introduction

Robot-assisted surgery has rapidly gained widespread adoption in urology, and many procedures now covered by national health insurance systems [1, 2]. Robot-assisted surgery offers technical advantages, such as improved maneuverability and visualization [3]. Compared with laparoscopic surgery, clinical benefits have been demonstrated for procedures such as radical prostatectomy [4] and partial nephrectomy [5, 6], leading to their recognition in medical fee evaluations.

However, the equipment costs for robot-assisted surgery remain high, constituting a significant financial burden for healthcare institutions [7, 8]. For robot-assisted procedures beyond radical prostatectomy and partial nephrectomy, the balance between reimbursement rates and equipment costs remains unclear [9–11]. Furthermore, there has been insufficient examination of how the introduction and continued use of robot-assisted surgery affects the profitability of healthcare institutions [10, 12].

Most previous economic evaluation studies on robot-assisted surgery focused on total costs comparisons, operative time, or cost-effectiveness analyses from payer or social prospectives [13–15] However, hospital-level financial decision-making often depend on contribution margin, defined as reimbursement revenue minus variable procedural costs. From this institutional perspective, understanding the acceptable level of robot related material expenditure required to maintain contribution margin equivalence with conventional surgery is critical for the sustainable implementation of robotic programs [16].

Therefore, this study defines the allowable robot-related material cost (ARMC) as the maximum robot-related variable material cost that allows robot-assisted surgery to achieve contribution margin equivalence with laparoscopic surgery under identical reimbursement conditions. Unlike conventional economic evaluations that assess social cost-effectiveness, the ARMC framework focuses on institutional financial sustainability by quantifying procedure-specific tolerance for robot-related material expenditures. This approach is conceptually related to contribution margin analysis in hospital financial management but adapts the framework specifically to robot-related surgical resource allocation. This study aimed to clarify procedure-specific financial constraints associated with robotic surgery and to provide practical insights for optimizing robotic resource utilization while maintaining institutional financial sustainability by quantifying ARMC for each procedure.

Materials and methods

Study population

This was a single-center, retrospective, observational study. It included urological surgeries performed at our hospital between April 2016 and February 2025, specifically adrenalectomies, nephrectomies, radical nephroureterectomies, and radical cystectomies, with identical insurance reimbursement for the robot-assisted and laparoscopic approaches.

For adrenalectomies, 70 patients underwent laparoscopic adrenalectomy (LA) and 38 underwent robot-assisted adrenalectomy (RA). Cases involving pheochromocytomas and malignant tumors were excluded due to potential differences in surgical difficulty and perioperative management.

For nephrectomy, 121 patients underwent laparoscopic radical nephrectomy (LRN) and 41 underwent robot-assisted radical nephrectomy (RARN). For nephroureterectomy, 84 patients underwent laparoscopic nephroureterectomy (LNU) and 47 underwent robot-assisted nephroureterectomy (RANU). For radical cystectomy, 15 patients underwent laparoscopic radical cystectomy (LRC) and 70 underwent robot-assisted radical cystectomy (RARC).

Data collection

Data were collected retrospectively for each patient from medical records and the hospital database. The collected variables included patient background, operative time, total revenue per procedure, equipment costs, and contribution margin. Operative time was defined as the time from skin incision to closure.

Total revenue was calculated based on the medical fee points billed for each procedure. Material costs were categorized as robot-related and other material costs. Robot-related material costs included equipment specific to robot-assisted surgery, such as robotic arms and specialized forceps. Other material costs included consumables shared with laparoscopic surgery.

Contribution margin per procedure was calculated as total reimbursement minus total material costs. Operating room staffing costs, capital acquisition costs, depreciation, and maintenance contract were treated as fixed institutional costs and were therefore not included in the base contribution margin calculation.

Definition of ARMC

In this study, the ARMC is defined as the maximum robot-related material cost permissible in robot-assisted surgery to achieve contribution margin equivalence with laparoscopic surgery.

ARMC was calculated as follows:

ARMC (JPY) = Total revenue from robot-assisted surgery − Other (non-robot-related) material costs in robot-assisted surgery – Contribution margin from laparoscopic surgery.

This metric represents the allowable level of robot-related material expenditure while maintaining institutional contribution margin equivalence with laparoscopic surgery.

Adjustment for operative time

A significant reduction in operative time was observed only for adrenalectomy when comparing laparoscopic and robotic surgery. Therefore, time-adjusted ARMC was additionally calculated for this procedure. Specifically, the contribution margin of laparoscopic surgery was adjusted for operative time and compared with the operative time for robot-assisted surgery using the following formula:

ARMC incorporating contribution margin (JPY) = Total revenue from robot-assisted surgery − Other equipment costs for robot-assisted surgery – Contribution margin from laparoscopic surgery × (Robot-assisted surgery time / Laparoscopic surgery time).

Scenario-based sensitivity analysis incorporating amortized fixed costs

To evaluate the potential impact of capital investment and maintenance costs on institutional financial sustainability, a scenario-based sensitivity analysis was performed. Scenario ranges were basically selected to represent plausible variations in robotic plat form acquisition costs, maintenance contracts, system lifetime and institutional surgical volume reported in previous literature on robotic surgery economics [7, 17]. Fixed costs (capital acquisition and maintenance costs) were annualized and divided by the assumed annual robotic case volume to estimate fixed cost per case. The system lifetime was set at 6 years to reflect the standard accounting depreciation period for medical equipment in Japan, while a 10-year lifetime was included as an extended operational scenario reflecting the typical maintenance support period offered by manufacturers. These assumptions are consistent with lifetime sensitivity analyses commonly used in health technology assessments.

Annual robotic case volumes were defined based on realistic operating schedules. Assuming one robotic procedure per working day, excluding weekends and national holidays in Japan corresponds to approximately 250 procedures per year. A lower utilization of 200 cases per year (approximately 80% utilization) and a higher utilization scenario of 400 cases per year (approximately 1.6 procedures per working day) were additionally evaluated.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Comparisons between laparoscopic and robot-assisted surgery groups were performed using t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, depending on data distribution. All statistical analyses were performed using the JMP Pro software (version 17.0; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2024) and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Osaka University Hospital (#23530(T13)). The requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective study design using de-identified data. Patient confidentiality was strictly maintained throughout data collection, analysis, and reporting.

Financial outcomes of each procedure

Table 2 summarizes the financial outcomes of each procedure. In all three procedures (adrenalectomy, nephrectomy, and nephroureterectomy), the robot-assisted approach incurred higher material costs and yielded lower contribution margin than the laparoscopic approach. On average, robot-assisted cases included additional robot-related material costs, such as robotic instruments and disposables, which substantially increased the total costs compared with laparoscopy. For example, RA had a mean total cost of approximately 290,000 yen compared with 190,000 yen for LA, resulting in reduced contribution margin (mean 237,000 yen for RA vs. 369,000 yen for LA). A similar pattern was observed in nephrectomy and nephroureterectomy, where robot-assisted surgery was associated with higher costs and, consequently, lower contribution margins than laparoscopic surgery.

The mean total revenue for RARC was approximately 1.54 million yen, compared with 1.28 million yen for LRC, and the contribution margin was correspondingly higher for RARC (approximately 957,000 yen vs. 814,000 yen). Time-adjusted ARMC in adrenalectomy and robotic arm utilization

Because RA demonstrated significantly shorter operative time compared with LA, an additional time-adjusted ARMC was calculated for adrenalectomy (Fig. 2a). When the contribution margin was normalized per operative hour, the ARMC for adrenalectomy increased to 70,415 yen. This higher time-adjusted ARMC indicates that after factoring in RA’s time savings, RA can justify substantially greater robot-related material cost while maintaining profitability comparable to LA.

Discussion

This study evaluated the financial implications of multiple robot-assisted urological procedures using the ARMC framework from an institutional contribution margin perspective. Our findings demonstrate that the financial impact of robot-assisted surgery varies substantially across procedures, indicating that a uniform economic assessment of robotic surgery is inappropriate. Instead, procedure-specific characteristics, including operative complexity, efficiency gains, and reimbursement structure, play critical roles in determining institutional financial sustainability.

Among the procedures analyzed, RANU exhibited the lowest ARMC, yielding a negative value. This suggests that, under current conditions, the robotic approach cannot achieve contribution margin equivalence with LNU. RANU includes both a nephrectomy component and distal ureter and bladder cuff excisions. The latter step requires intracorporeal suturing to close the bladder, adding operative complexity and material costs. Although RANU and RARN receive the same level of insurance reimbursement, the additional technical demands of RANU likely contribute to the higher overall cost and unfavorable financial outcomes observed in our analysis [18]. These findings suggest that procedural complexity can significantly influence contribution margin tolerance for robotic surgery.

In contrast, RA demonstrated a more favorable financial profile when operative time was considered. RA was associated with a significantly shorter operative time than LA, and adjustment for operative time substantially increased ARMC [19]. This finding suggest that improvements in surgical efficiency may partially offset robot-related material cost [20]. At our institution, RA is routinely performed using a three-arm configuration. Although this strategy was initially adopted to maintain surgical feasibility and efficiency, it also effectively reduces robot-related material costs. The present analysis suggests that optimizing robotic resource utilization can improve institutional financial sustainability without compromising clinical outcomes.

RARC showed a distinct pattern. It exhibited higher ARMC and greater total revenue than LRC. However, this apparent economic advantage should be interpreted with caution. The revenue gap largely reflects the revision of the reimbursement system during the transition from LRC to RARC, which added reimbursement for urinary diversion using bowel segments. Despite this increase, no proportional increase in contribution margin was observed, and the normalized contribution margin per operative time did not clearly improve.

The relatively high ARMC observed for RARC in Fig. 1 may overestimate its true economic flexibility. In practice, RARC involves prolonged operative time and substantial robot-related material costs. To address this, our institution implemented cost-reduction strategies, including routine selection of tip-up forceps for the left-hand robotic arm during intracorporeal urinary diversion instead of using more expensive ProGrasp forceps. These measures reduce robot-related material costs and are considered essential for maintaining financial sustainability in RARC. Continued refinement of these strategies is necessary as robotic cystectomy programs expand.

It is important to contextualize these findings with previously reported clinical benefits at our institution [21]. RA achieved an approximately 30-minute reduction in operative time, and RARC was associated with reduced blood loss, lower transfusion rates, and shorter postoperative hospital stays compared with laparoscopic approaches [22, 23]. These results indicate that robot-assisted surgery provides tangible perioperative benefits for patients [24, 25]. Nevertheless, despite these patient-centered advantages, contribution margin per unit operative time remained unchanged or decreased in several robot-assisted procedures compared with laparoscopic surgery [26, 27].

This discrepancy highlights an important challenge in the current healthcare environment: improvements in surgical quality and patient outcomes, such as shorter operative time, reduced perioperative morbidity, and earlier discharge, are not necessarily reflected in time- or procedure-based reimbursement metrics. Recent revisions to Japanese national fee schedule have introduced additional reimbursement for robotic surgery in selected institutions. Such policy adjustments may partially mitigate institutional financial pressure associated with robotic programs. However, this additional reimbursement is likely to cover only the amortized capital and maintenance costs, as our fixed-cost adjusted ARMC data showed. Therefore, from the perspective ARMC, surgeons are still required to continue exploring strategies to reduce the costs associated with robotic surgical arms and instruments.

This study has some limitations. First, it is a single-center retrospective analysis, which may limit generalizability. Second, laparoscopic cystectomy cases were predominantly performed earlier in the study period, which may introduce temporal bias related to learning-curve effects and evolving perioperative management. Third, the base ARMC calculation focused primarily on variable robot-related material costs and did not incorporate capital depreciation, maintenance fees, or personnel costs, though scenario-based sensitivity analyses were performed to illustrate the potential influence of fixed-cost allocation. Accordingly, ARMC should not be interpreted as a comprehensive economic evaluation but rather as an institutional contribution margin-based analytical framework.

Despite these limitations, the ARMC framework offers a practical and transferable approach for institutional decision-making regarding the financial sustainability of the robotic surgery program. By defining a procedure-specific threshold for acceptable robot-related costs, ARMC allows institutions to assess whether robotic surgery can be implemented while maintaining financial sustainability. Rather than negating the value of robotic surgery, this framework clarifies the conditions under which robotic technology can be responsibly integrated into clinical practice. Lastly, the present analysis is based on data collected over several years, and future fluctuations in prices or exchange rates could further affect contribution margins.
