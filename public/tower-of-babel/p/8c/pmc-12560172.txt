Upregulation of ALDH1 as an adaptive epigenetic response to anthracyclines in acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Francesco Leonetti, Sladjana Kosanovic, Rocio Rebollidos‐Rios, Iris Manosalva, Céline Baier, Muhube Yazir, Andrada Constantinescu, Charbel Souaid, Raquel Pequerul, Miroslava Kari Adamcova, Mehak Shaikh, Magdalena Havlová, Regis Costello, Jaume Farrés, Guillaume Martin, Meritxell Alberich‐Jorda, Salvatore Spicuglia, Mileidys Perez‐Alea
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a genetically heterogeneous malignancy characterized by the clonal proliferation of undifferentiated myeloid precursors in the bone marrow. Although standard induction regimens based on anthracyclines often achieve initial remission, up to 25% of patients exhibit primary refractory disease and nearly 50% relapse, underscoring the urgent need to overcome therapy resistance. Aldehyde dehydrogenase 1 (ALDH1) contributes to leukemic cell survival by maintaining stemness, proliferation, and chemoresistance through aldehyde detoxification and retinoic acid synthesis. Here, we identify two enhancer elements, ALDH1A1‐E3 and ALDH1A2‐E1‐A, that mediate transcriptional activation of ALDH1A1 and ALDH1A2 in response to the anthracycline daunorubicin. These enhancers are regulated by STAT3 and FOS/JUN transcription factors, which cooperatively link drug response to ALDH1 induction. Functional validation in AML cell lines, primary samples, and xenograft models shows that ALDH1 upregulation is part of an adaptive stress response and may contribute to reduced anthracycline sensitivity. Co‐treatment with the ALDH1A1/1A2 inhibitor DIMATE synergistically enhances daunorubicin efficacy across in vitro and in vivo resistant models. Consistently, high ALDH1 expression is associated with adverse genetic risk, prior anthracycline exposure, and inferior OS, particularly in relapsed/refractory AML. These findings uncover a novel enhancer‐mediated mechanism of ALDH1 induction in the context of anthracycline exposure and support the rationale for future clinical trials combining standard treatments with ALDH1‐targeted approaches, including the clinical‐stage inhibitor DIMATE.

INTRODUCTION

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is an aggressive, genetically heterogeneous malignancy marked by the clonal expansion of undifferentiated myeloid precursors in the bone marrow. 1 Despite initial responses to standard induction regimes based on anthracycline–cytarabine combinations, a significant proportion of patients develop refractory disease or relapse, resulting in poor long‐term outcomes and limited curative options. 2 , 3 In leukemic cells, multiple stress‐response pathways are frequently activated to support survival under conditions of rapid proliferation, heightened metabolic demands, and chemotherapy‐induced stress. 4 , 5 , 6 Targeting vulnerabilities arising from these adaptive responses represents a promising therapeutic approach, particularly in treatment‐resistant AML.

Aldehyde dehydrogenase 1 (ALDH1) activity is a recognized functional marker of cancer stem cells in multiple malignancies, including hematologic cancers, where it contributes to both tumor maintenance and resistance to therapy. 7 , 8 , 9 The ALDH1 enzymes are a subclass within the larger ALDH superfamily and include at least three distinct isoforms: ALDH1A1, ALDH1A2, and ALDH1A3. These isoforms share common catalytic functions, oxidizing endogenous and exogenous aldehydes—reactive by‐products of intermediary metabolism (e.g., glucose, amino acids, and lipids)—and xenobiotic metabolism, including chemotherapeutic agents. 10 , 11 , 12 Despite their catalytic similarities, these isoforms differ markedly in their tissue distribution, regulatory mechanisms, and specific substrate affinities, influencing their distinct biological roles. 10 , 13

In AML, genetic alterations often lead to mitochondrial dysfunction, dysregulated metabolism, chronic inflammation, and elevated lipid peroxidation, all processes that exacerbate the generation of reactive aldehydes. 4 , 5 While some aldehydes promote oncogenic signaling via covalent modification of proteins and nucleic acids, their elevated accumulation is cytotoxic, causing oxidative damage and cell death. 12 , 14 ALDH1 enzymes attenuate this stress by detoxifying reactive aldehydes, maintaining redox homeostasis, and supporting cancer cell survival under conditions of metabolic and therapeutic stress.

There is strong evidence that ALDH1 contributes to AML progression by promoting chemotherapy resistance, treatment failure, and disease relapse. 8 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 Early insights emerged from studies employing the ALDEFLUOR enzymatic assay, widely used to assess ALDH1 activity in intact cells. 18 , 21 Although the ALDEFLUOR substrate can be metabolized by multiple ALDHs, high ALDH activity detected specifically in AML blasts correlated strongly with leukemic stem cell properties and resistance to multiple chemotherapeutic agents known to generate reactive aldehydes. 17 , 18 , 21 When used as a selection biomarker, ALDH activity identified cell populations with enhanced tumorigenic potential in preclinical models. 21 These early findings suggested that ALDH1 could serve as a functional marker of drug‐resistant, leukemia‐initiating cells. Our own work further supported this role, revealing a therapeutic window for ALDH1 inhibition that selectively targets primary AML stem cells while sparing normal hematopoietic progenitors. 19 Recent transcriptomic analyses of AML patient cohorts reinforced these observations, linking elevated ALDH1A1 expression to adverse‐risk classification, higher relapse rates, and, in some cohorts, reduced overall survival (OS). 22

Although various studies have associated high ALDH1 expression with leukemic stemness and drug resistance, the regulatory mechanisms controlling its expression in AML remain largely undefined. 23 Here, we demonstrate that anthracyclines transcriptionally induce ALDH1A1 and ALDH1A2 via two drug‐responsive enhancer elements that are epigenetically regulated and bound by STAT3 and FOS/JUN transcription factors (TFs)—critical mediators of inflammatory signaling and cellular stress responses. We show that ALDH1A1/1A2 inhibition disrupts cellular adaptive stress responses and synergistically enhances the antileukemic efficacy of daunorubicin (DNR) in resistant AML models both in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, our clinical analyses support the prognostic relevance of ALDH1A1 in relapsed/refractory AML. Altogether, these findings uncover a novel stress‐responsive regulatory axis underlying ALDH1‐mediated resistance and provide a mechanistic rationale for therapeutic strategies targeting ALDH1 in treatment‐refractory AML.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Human data analysis

An R‐based data processing and analysis pipeline was established to evaluate the prognostic role of ALDH1A isoforms in AML, using transcriptomic and clinical data from the Beat AML 2.0 dataset (Waves 1–4). 24 Normalized gene expression data were imported and merged with clinical annotations from the public repository https://biodev.github.io/BeatAML2/. Samples with missing or undefined vital status were excluded, and OS time was converted from days to years. Vital status was encoded as a binary variable (status: 1 = deceased, 0 = alive). Patients with duplicate subject identifiers were removed to retain only unique individuals, resulting in a curated dataset comprising 591 patient samples. Samples were stratified based on expression levels of each ALDH1A subfamily isoform (ALDH1A1, ALDH1A2, and ALDH1A3) using a quartile‐based thresholding approach. Specifically, individuals with expression values above the 75th percentile (upper quartile) were assigned to the “High” expression group, while those at or below this threshold were categorized as “Low.” Clinical and molecular covariates were merged with the stratified expression groups. Group comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test as implemented in the rstatix package. 25 Survival outcomes were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier estimates, log‐rank tests, and Cox proportional hazards models, using functions from the survival 26 package. Competing risk analysis was conducted using subdistribution hazard models, and time‐dependent risk profiles were assessed via conditional survival analysis. Visualization of survival curves and summary statistics were performed using the survminer 27 and ggsurvfit 28 packages. Detailed results of ALDH1A expression‐based stratification and corresponding clinical annotations are provided in Supporting Information S1: Table 1 and Supporting Information S2: Table 2.

Cell lines and culture

The AML cell lines used in this study were K562 (DSMZ, Cat# ACC 10; Braunschweig, Germany), Kasumi‐1 (DSMZ Cat# ACC 220), MONO‐MAC‐6 (DSMZ, Cat# ACC 124), OCI‐AML3 (DSMZ, Cat# ACC 582), Kasumi‐3 (DSMZ, Cat# ACC714), THP‐1 (DSMZ, Cat# ACC 16), MV‐4‐11 (DSMZ, Cat# ACC102), MOLM‐14 (DSMZ, Cat# ACC 777), HL‐60 (DSMZ, Cat# ACC ACC3), and KG‐1a (DSMZ, Cat# ACC 421). All cells were cultured in the media and supplements recommended by the suppliers at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

Patient samples

Peripheral blood samples were collected from 15 adult patients with newly diagnosed AML and one patient with T‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T‐ALL) treated in the Department of Hematology at La Conception University Hospital (AP‐HM), Marseille, France (Tables 1 and 2). Mobilized peripheral blood CD34+/CD8− hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs) (herein referred to as HSPC‐enriched cells) were obtained by apheresis from patients with nonmyeloid malignancies undergoing autologous transplantation (n = 4), as described previously. 19 The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and French regulatory requirements. Samples were collected after obtaining informed consent and based on documented non‐opposition from all participants, in accordance with French RIPH3 regulations. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board (Comité de Protection des Personnes Méditerranée V, Ref. 15.013) and the Agence Nationale de Sécurité du Médicament (Ref. 150054B–11).

Inhibitors and therapeutic compounds

All chemotherapeutic agents used in this study, including anthracyclines and various alkylating agents, were purchased from MedChem Tronica (Sollentuna, Sweden) and are listed in Supporting Information S3: Table 3. The JNK inhibitor JNK‐IN‐8 was obtained from MedChem Tronica (Cat# HY‐13319; New Jersey, France), the STAT3 inhibitor TTI‐101 was purchased from MedChem Tronica (Cat# HY‐112288), and the ALDH1A1/1A2 inhibitor DIMATE was synthesized according to the method previously described. 29

Drug response assay using eGFP reporter

The K562‐ALDH1A1‐eGFP cell line was generated by CRISPR‐mediated insertion of a P2A_eGFP_P2Adeg_Neo cassette into the terminal exon of the ALDH1A1‐201 transcript (ENST00000297785.8), replacing its stop codon. Neomycin resistance, flanked by LoxP sites, was used for selection and isolation of recombinant clones. This cell line reporter was employed to evaluate the effect of chemotherapeutic agents on ALDH1A1 expression (Supporting Information S3: Table 3). Cells were seeded at 0.5 × 106 cells/mL 24 h before treatment and subsequently exposed to either vehicle or 1 µM of each compound for 24 h. ALDH1A1 induction was assessed by measuring eGFP fluorescence using a BD Fortessa X20 flow cytometer.

Cell treatments for transcript and protein expression analysis

AML cell lines. Cells were seeded at 0.5 × 106 cells/mL in their respective culture media, 24 h before treatment. Cultures were then exposed for 18 or 24 h (37°C, 5% CO₂) to 1 µM DNR, epirubicin, idarubicin, or doxorubicin. In separate experiments, cells received 10–20 μM of TTI‐101, 5–15 μM of JNK‐IN‐8, or the corresponding vehicle controls. After incubation, cells were washed with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), pelleted, snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C for subsequent RNA and protein extraction.

Primary AML blasts. Cells were seeded at 1 × 106 cells/mL in RPMI‐1640 GlutaMAX, supplemented with 10% heat‐inactivated fetal bovine serum and immediately treated with 0.5 µM of DNR or vehicle (0.002% dimethyl sulfoxide [DMSO]) for 24 h. Cells were then processed as above.

Non‐leukemic HSPC‐enriched cells. CD34+/CD8− cells were seeded at 1.5–5.5 × 105 cells/mL in StemSpan™ SFEM supplemented with 100 ng/mL SCF, 100 ng/mL TPO, 100 ng/mL FLT3L, 20 ng/mL IL‐3, and 20 ng/mL IL‐6, and treated with DNR (200 nM and 1 μM DNR) for 16 h. Cells were collected and stored at −80°C for gene expression analysis.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR) assays

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat# 74136; Courtaboeuf, France), RNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen, Cat# 74004; Germantown, Maryland, USA), or RNeasy Plus Micro Kit (Qiagen, Cat# 74034; Germantown, Maryland, USA). One microgram of total RNA was reverse‐transcribed into cDNA using the SuperScript™ VILO™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 1755250; Abingdon, UK), following the manufacturer's protocol. For non‐leukemic HSPCs, cDNA was synthesized from all isolated RNA using Maxima H Minus Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# EP0752) following the manufacturer's instructions. Quantitative PCR was performed using the SYBRTM green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 4367660). Reactions were run on a QuantStudioTM 6 Flex Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) or a LightCycler® 480 Instrument II (Roche Molecular Systems, Pleasanton, CA, USA). Primers used are listed in Supporting Information S4: Table 4. Gene expression was normalized to the mRNA levels of the housekeeping genes GAPDH, RPLP0, RPL13A1, and/or B2M.

Capillary electrophoresis immunoassays

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with phosphatase and protease inhibitor cocktails (Calbiochem, Cat# 524625 and 539134, respectively; San Diego, CA, USA). Total protein concentration was determined with the PierceTM BCA Protein Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 10678484). Equal amounts of lysate were analyzed on a WES Simple Western platform (Bio‐Techne, Minneapolis, MN, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Immunodetection, chemiluminescent signal acquisition, and band quantification were performed using standard WES protocols and Compass software (Bio‐Techne). The following primary antibodies were used: ALDH1A1 (Proteintech, Cat# 15910‐1‐AP; Manchester, UK), ALDH1A2 (Proteintech, Cat# 13951‐1‐AP), ALDH1A3 (Bio‐Techne, Cat# NBP2‐15339), JNK (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 9252; Saint‐Cyr‐L'École, France), c‐Jun (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 9165), Phospho‐c‐Jun Ser73 (S73) (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 3270), STAT3 (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 9139), Phospho‐STAT3 (Y705) (Cell Signaling technology, Cat# 9145) and Protein Simple loading control (Bio‐Techne, Cat# 042‐196).

Enzymatic activity assays

Fresh cell extracts were prepared using MPER cell lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 11885095), sonicated twice for 10 min (Branson B200), and collected by centrifugation. ALDH1 activity in the supernatants was measured at 37°C by monitoring the NAD⁺‐dependent oxidation of the fluorogenic substrate MONAL‐62 (5 µM) (CymitQuimica, Cat# 3B‐M1090; Barcelona, Spain) to MONOIC‐62 in a reaction buffer containing 50 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 0.5 mM EDTA (pH 8.1), and 150 µM NAD⁺, adapted from a previously described method. 30 Product fluorescence was measured using a Cary Eclipse fluorometer (λ_ex = 288 nm, 5 nm band‐pass; λ_em = 360 nm, 10 nm band‐pass). MONOIC‐62 (1 μM) was included as an internal standard for determining the absolute reaction rate, calculated as v=dFdt×CstFst, where C st is the concentration of MONOIC‐62, F st its fluorescence, and dFdt the time‐dependent fluorescence slope. The absolute rate (U/mg) was calculated using the formula: μmol MONOIC‐62L·s×1L103mL×1mL reaction0.01mL enzyme×1mLmg enzyme×60s1min×enzyme dilution factor. Enzyme kinetic parameters were calculated by fitting data to the standard Michaelis–Menten equation using GraFit 5.0 software (Erithacus Software, West Sussex, UK).

Cytotoxicity assays

Dose–response curves were generated to determine the half‐maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) of each compound. Cells (1.5 × 10⁴ per well) were seeded in 96‐well plates and treated for 48 h with serial dilutions of DNR (0.1–10 μM) or DIMATE (1–50 μM). Viability was measured with a resazurin‐based assay (Sigma‐Aldrich, Cat# TOX8; Darmstadt, Germany) and expressed as a percentage of vehicle‐treated controls. IC₅₀ values were obtained by nonlinear regression analysis of log‐transformed dose–response curves in GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.1; GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).

Drug–drug interaction assays

Drug–drug interaction studies were conducted using the same 48‐h treatment protocol as the single‐agent cytotoxicity assays. Cells were treated with various pairwise compound combinations, centered around the IC₅₀ values of each agent. Viability was assessed using a resazurin‐based assay, and growth inhibition was normalized to vehicle‐treated controls. Drug combination synergy scores were computed using the R package synergyfinder 31 (version 3.12). Dose–response matrices were analyzed using the Bliss independence model, with no baseline correction applied (correct_baseline = “non”). Each drug combination was tested in three biological replicates. To account for variability, synergy scores were estimated by averaging results across 10 bootstrap iterations (iteration = 10). Synergy scores along with associated statistics including means, standard deviations, and 95% confidence intervals are detailed in Supporting Information S5: Table 5.

Chromatin extraction and chromatin‐immunoprecipitation‐sequencing

K562 cells, either untreated or treated with 1 µM DNR for 24 h (as described above), were harvested by centrifugation and washed twice with PBS. For each replicate, 10 × 106 cells were fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, quenched with glycine at 250 mM, washed with cold PBS, and stored at −80°C. Chromatin was sonicated using Bioruptor® Pico from 10 cycles (30 s ON, 30 s OFF) at 4 °C to obtain DNA fragments between 200 and 500 bp. Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was carried out using 1 µg of H3K27ac antibody (Diagenode, Cat# C15410196; Seraing, Belgium) following a previously described protocol. 32 Immunoprecipitated DNA was purified using QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, Cat# 28104; Courtaboeuf, France). ChIP‐sequencing (ChIP‐seq) libraries were prepared using the MicroPlex Library Preparation Kit (Diagenode, Cat# C05010001) and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq® 500/550 platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, USA) with a 50/30 bp paired‐end mode. Sequencing data were processed using the established BLUEPRINT pipeline, 33 as previously described. 32 Peaks were called using MACS2 v2.2.7.1 34 with the ‐‐broad argument, using their respective input DNA as controls. Signal was quantile‐normalized with DANPOS v2.2.2 and used for visualization in the IGV genome browser, 35 to produce heatmaps with the R package genomation v1.30.0 242 and to compute the fold‐change between the DNR and the untreated conditions.

Motif enrichment

Motif enrichment analysis was conducted with the SEA tool (https://meme.i‐med.ac.at/tools/sea) from the MEME suite 36 using the peaks with fold‐change > 3 as query and the peaks with |log2 fold‐change| < 0.1 as control. These thresholds were picked to extract similar quantities of DNR‐induced and non‐induced regions. The list of enriched motifs was compared with the transcription factor binding sites (TFBS) present on the ALDH1A2‐E3 and ALDH1A1‐E1 enhancers based on Jaspar core Database 2022 37 (Supporting Information S6: Table 6).

ChIP‐qPCR assays

ChIP‐enriched DNA (1 μL) was used to assess H3K27ac enrichment at candidate enhancer regions. Primer pairs (Supporting Information S7: Table 7) were designed with the Ensembl BLAST tool. Reactions were set up in PowerSYBR® Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat# 4367659) and run on a QuantStudio™ 6 Flex Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Quantification used the relative‐standard‐curve method, and values were assessed as a percentage of the input. Signals were normalized to the ACTB promoter and expressed as the percentage of input relative to pACTB.

Luciferase reporter assay

Putative enhancer regions of ALDH1 gene family members (Supporting Information S8: Table 8) were amplified by PCR from human genomic DNA and cloned upstream of the SV40 promoter into the pGL4.13[luc2/SV40] plasmid (Promega, Cat# E6681; Madison, USA) at the BamHI restriction site. For transfection, 0.5 × 106 K562 cells were electroporated with 1 μg of the reporter construct and 300 ng of a Renilla luciferase control vector using the Neon® Transfection System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with settings of 1450 V, 10 ms pulse width, and 3 pulses. Transfected cells were transferred to six‐well plates containing K562‐specific supplemented growth media. After 24 h, half of the cells were treated with 1 µM DNR for an additional 18 h. Cells were then harvested, and firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured using the Dual‐Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Promega, Cat# E1910). Luminescence was quantified using a VICTOR Nivo™ Multimode Plate Reader (PerkinElmer). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity to account for transfection efficiency. Results are presented as fold‐change in relative light units (RLU) compared to untreated cells transfected with the empty pGL4.13 vector.

Mutagenesis experiments

Site‐specific mutagenesis was conducted directly on the sequence of the ALDH1A1‐E3 and ALDH1A2‐E1‐A regions cloned into the pGL4.13 plasmid using the Q5 site‐directed Mutagenesis Kit (NEB, Cat# E0554S). Mutated sequences are listed in Supporting Information S9: Table 9.

Clonogenic assays

K562 (3000 cells), Kasumi‐1 (1500 cells), MOLM‐14 (1000 cells), MV‐4‐11 (1000 cells), and OCI‐AML3 (1500 cells) cell lines were cultured in MethoCult™ H4535 medium (STEMCELL Technologies, Cat# 04545; Vancouver, Canada). K562 and Kasumi‐1 cells were treated with suboptimal concentrations of DIMATE (200 nM), DNR (1 µM), or their combination at the same doses. MOLM‐14 and MV‐4‐11 cells received 500 nM DIMATE and 10 nM DNR; OCI‐AML3 cells received 200 nM DIMATE and 5 nM DNR. Cultures were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂ and scored after 7 days (K562, Kasumi‐1, and MOLM‐14) or 10 days (MOML‐14 and MV‐4‐11). A cluster of more than 30 cells was defined as a colony. Images were acquired using a Zeiss AxioZoom Apotome Microscope with a 16× objective.

Animal models

NOD‐SCID‐gammaIL2Rnull (NSG) mice (8–12‐week‐old) were employed in this study. Mice were housed in the specific‐pathogen‐free (SPF) animal facility at the Institute of Molecular Genetics of the CAS. Animals were maintained on a standard diet and 12 h light–dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. All procedures were approved by the Ethical Committee of the Institute of Molecular Genetics of the CAS under license number 6416 SOV II (Prague, Czech Republic). After 2 Gy total body irradiation, each mouse was injected i.v. with 2 × 106 KG1a cells and, 7 days later, randomized to: (i) PBS; (ii) DIMATE 10 mg kg⁻¹ i.p. three times weekly (Mon/Wed/Fri) for 4 weeks; (iii) DNR 0.6 mg/kg i.v. on Days 1–3 of Weeks 1 and 3; or (iv) the combination of drugs as in Groups ii–iii. At Week 5, bone marrow was harvested, stained with FITC‐anti‐human CD45 (clone HI30, Cat# 982316) and PE‐Cy7‐anti‐mouse CD45 (clone A20, Cat# 110730), and analyzed on a BD Symphony cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, California, USA) to quantify human CD45⁺ (KG1a) cells. Murine cells were depleted by MACS (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) using biotin‐anti‐mouse CD45.1 (A20) and anti‐biotin microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec); the negative (human) fraction was stored at −80°C. All antibodies were from BioLegend (San Diego, CA, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.1), except for analyses involving human data from the Beat AML 2.0 dataset and drug–drug interaction studies, which are described above. Differences between two independent groups were assessed using the two‐sided Mann–Whitney U test or unpaired Student's t‐test, as appropriate. Paired data comparisons utilized the paired t‐test. Multiple‐group comparisons were analyzed by one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with appropriate post hoc corrections or by the Kruskal–Wallis test, depending on data distribution. P‐values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

High expression of ALDH1A1 correlates with prior chemotherapy exposure, intrinsic chemotherapy resistance, and poor AML survival

We preprocessed and reanalyzed Class 1 ALDHs gene expression and clinical data from the public Beat AML 2.0 dataset 38 to investigate potential associations between ALDH1 isoforms expression levels and key clinical characteristics, including prognosis, patient survival, response to chemotherapy, and distribution across different AML clinical subtypes (Figure 1A). An initial comparison using conventional genetic risk stratification (ELN‐2022) confirmed a significant enrichment of ALDH1A1 expression in patients classified as adverse risk compared to those in the intermediate (adjusted P = 0.002) and favorable risk subgroups (adjusted P < 0.0001) (Figure 1B; Supporting Information S1: Table 1). These findings align with prior analyses of AML datasets from both adult and pediatric patients, comprising independent clinical studies and earlier Beat AML data. 17 , 22 , 39 , 40 In line with the strong association between high ALDH1A1 expression and adverse genetic risk, we observed significantly elevated ALDH1A1 levels at diagnosis in refractory AML cases compared to samples from patients who achieved a complete response following induction chemotherapy (adjusted P < 0.0001) (Figure 1C; Supporting Information S1: Table 1). This observation independently validates our recent findings in a smaller cohort of 59 adult AML patients 40 and parallels prior reports in pediatric AML, where ALDH1A1 was linked to relapsed disease. 22 Notably, we found that these associations include a significantly higher expression of ALDH1A1 in AML with myelodysplastic syndromes (MDSs) or myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasms (MDS/MPNs) (adjusted P < 0.0001) and an increased expression of both ALDH1A1 and ALDH1A2 in therapy‐related AML (adjusted P = 0.002 and P < 0.001, respectively) compared to de novo AML cases lacking these diagnostic qualifiers (Figure 1D; Supporting Information S1: Table 1). No significant associations were observed for ALDH1A3 across any of the analyzed parameters. This suggests that the ALDH1A1 isoform may have potential as a biomarker for secondary AML subtypes, typically characterized by distinct clinical features and poorer outcomes. Additionally, both ALDH1A1 and ALDH1A2 may contribute to therapy resistance or clonal evolution in the context of chemotherapy exposure.

Supporting this, Kaplan–Meier analyses revealed a significant association between high ALDH1A1 expression and reduced OS across multiple AML subgroups (Figure 1E). In the overall cohort, patients with high ALDH1A1 expression had significantly shorter survival, with a median OS of 10.8 versus 19.2 months (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.54, P < 0.001) (Figure 1E, upper panel; Supporting Information S2: Table 2). This association persisted among chemotherapy‐exposed patients, who comprised 54% of the cohort (HR = 1.55, P = 0.004) (Figure 1E, middle panel), and was even more pronounced in the relapsed/refractory subgroup (20% of the cohort) where the HR increased to 1.98 (95% CI 1.29–3.05, P = 0.002) (Figure 1E, bottom panel). Notably, in this subgroup, survival curves diverged sharply beyond 18 months: at 24 months, the estimated survival probability was approximately 45% in the low‐ALDH1A1 group versus ~5% in the high‐ALDH1A1 group, highlighting a late‐stage adverse impact of elevated ALDH1A1 expression in the relapsed/refractory setting.

ALDH1A2 expression showed a trend toward reduced OS but did not reach statistical significance in the overall AML cohort (P = 0.08), nor in the chemotherapy‐treated (P = 0.09) or relapsed/refractory subgroups (P = 0.2) (Supporting Information S10: Figure 1; Supporting Information S2: Table 2). ALDH1A3 expression was not associated with OS in any of the groups analyzed.

To further investigate whether the association between ALDH1A1 expression and prognosis is linked to chemotherapy, we performed an additional analysis in the subgroup of AML patients with no documented history of anthracycline exposure (n = 186). This group included treatment‐naive patients as well as those who had received targeted therapies, palliative/supportive care, or underwent hematopoietic stem cell transplantation following non‐anthracycline regimens. In this cohort, the association between ALDH1A1 expression and OS was no longer observed (P = 0.58; Supporting Information S10: Figure 1A). A similar lack of association is apparent in a cytogenetically normal, chemotherapy‐naive AML population from a previously published meta‐analysis, 22 further supporting the notion that the prognostic value of ALDH1A1 may be specifically linked to anthracycline‐based chemotherapy, rather than representing a general survival marker in AML.

Anthracyclines induce ALDH1 transcriptional activation in AML cells, with enhanced enzymatic activity observed in response to DNR in drug‐resistant models

To investigate ALDH1A1 as a potential component of the adaptive cellular response to chemotherapy, we developed a fluorescent reporter assay in the multidrug‐resistant human leukemic K562 cell line (Figure 2A). Stable clonal K562 cells with a monoallelic ALDH1A1‐eGFP knock‐in were generated, allowing the direct assessment of chemotherapy‐induced ALDH1A1 activation. Among the 12 DNA‐targeting chemotherapeutic agents screened, including alkylating agents (nitrogen mustards, nitrosoureas, triazines, and an alkyl sulfonate), platinum‐containing drugs, antimetabolites, and anthracyclines, only the anthracycline class (DNR, doxorubicin, and epirubicin) elicited a robust and significant increase in fluorescence intensity, exceeding a threefold induction compared to the vehicle control (P < 0.0001; Figure 2B).

We validated the transcriptional activation of ALDH1A1 by RT‐qPCR in wild‐type K562 cells and in Kasumi‐1, KG‐1a, and Mono‐Mac‐6 AML cell lines treated with four clinically relevant anthracyclines (Figure 2C; Supporting Information S10: Figure 2A). These AML cell lines, along with K562, were selected from a panel of nine AML cell lines based on their relative low sensitivity to DNR, with IC50 values in the high nanomolar range (162.6 ± 29.7, 996.8 ± 147, 112.9 ± 22.1, and 122.7 ± 17.7 nM, respectively) (Supporting Information S10: Figure 2B). We observed sustained ALDH1A1 transcriptional induction up to 24 h in response to DNR, doxorubicin, and epirubicin across all four AML cell lines (Figure 2C; Supporting Information S10: Figure 2A). Idarubicin also triggered ALDH1A1 transcription in K562, Kasumi‐1, and Mono‐Mac‐6, but the response was transient, with expression returning to baseline by 24 h. In KG1a cells, no transcriptional induction by idarubicin was detected at 18 or 24 h.

At the protein level, anthracycline treatment generally increased ALDH1A1 expression across the tested cell lines. In KG1a, however, only modest increases were observed with DNR and epirubicin, while doxorubicin led to a ~50% reduction in ALDH1A1 protein. Notably, despite the absence of detectable mRNA upregulation with idarubicin in KG1a at the tested time points, ALDH1A1 protein levels increased at 24 h, suggesting either a transient transcriptional response at an earlier time point or regulation at the posttranscriptional level. The limited ALDH1A1 induction in KG1a could be attributable to its well‐documented high expression of ABC transporters, particularly P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp), which actively efflux DNR and doxorubicin. 41 , 42 In contrast, the greater lipophilicity of idarubicin may render it less susceptible to P‐gp–mediated efflux, potentially explaining the more pronounced increase in ALDH1A1 protein levels observed with this anthracycline in KG1a cells.

Most anthracyclines also induced transcription of ALDH1A2 and ALDH1A3, though translation to protein‐level responses varied across cell lines (Figure 2C,D). The increase in ALDH1A2 protein levels was observed after DNR treatment in K562, Kasumi‐1, and Mono‐Mac‐6; after epirubicin treatment, in KG1a and Mono‐Mac‐6; after idarubicin treatment, in KG1a and K562; and after doxorubicin treatment, only in Mono‐Mac‐6, suggesting either a context‐dependent response or differences in induction kinetics. Although ALDH1A3 showed transcriptional upregulation in some settings, it did not result in increased protein levels under any of the tested conditions (Figure 2C,D), suggesting a limited functional role in the anthracycline response.

Consistent with the expression patterns described above, ALDH1‐specific enzymatic activity increased significantly in response to DNR in K562, Kasumi‐1, and Mono‐Mac‐6, while only a nonsignificant trend was observed in KG1a (Figure 2E).

ALDH1A1 and ALDH1A2 isoform‐specific regulation was further validated in primary leukemic samples from patients diagnosed with AML of adverse (n = 11) or intermediate (n = 3) genetic risk, as well as in two patients with chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML) and B‐acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B‐ALL), both classified as adverse risk (Figure 2F; Tables 1 and 2). Upon ex vivo treatment with DNR, both ALDH1A1 and ALDH1A2 were upregulated in most samples, except for a few cases, including two patients from the intermediate‐risk group. Notably, DNR also elicited a dose‐dependent increase in ALDH1A1 and ALDH1A2 transcripts in non‐leukemic HSPCs from four donors (Supporting Information S10: Figure 3), suggesting that this induction may not be restricted to malignant cells. Altogether, these results suggest that anthracyclines, particularly DNR, promote ALDH1 transcription activation and enhance ALDH1 enzymatic activity in AML cells.

DNR induces epigenetic enhancers that activate ALDH1A1 and ALDH1A2 loci

We hypothesized that anthracyclines might epigenetically modulate cis‐regulatory elements leading to ALDH1 induction. To test this hypothesis, we profiled ChIP‐seq from the H3K27ac epigenomic mark; a histone posttranslational modification associated with activated promoters and enhancers, in DNR‐treated versus untreated K562 cells (Figure 3A). ChIP‐seq analysis within the topologically associating domain (TAD) of each ALDH1 locus revealed DNR‐dependent increase of H3K27ac at six potential enhancer regions: ALDH1A1‐E1, ALDH1A1‐E2, and ALDH1A1‐E3 for ALDH1A1; ALDH1A2‐E1 and ALDH1A2‐E2 for ALDH1A2; and ALDH1A3‐E1 for ALDH1A3. Five of these putative enhancers—excluding ALDH1A2‐E2—showed a reproducible increase by ChIP‐qPCR following DNR treatment of K562 cells at both 18 and 24 h (Supporting Information S10: Figure 4). The corresponding regions of interest carrying these fragments were cloned into luciferase reporter constructs driven by the SV40 minimal promoter. The enhancer activities for the five candidates were further evaluated by transient transfection and luciferase assays in K562 cells. Among these, only ALDH1A1‐E3 and ALDH1A2‐E1 showed significantly increased luciferase activity in response to DNR (Figure 3B).

To further refine these drug‐responsive elements and identify potential TFs influencing the response to DNR within the ALDH1A1‐E3 and ALDH1A2‐E1 regions, we isolated different contiguous fragments and reassessed their drug responsiveness. First, we integrated H3K27ac ChIP‐seq data, DNase‐seq data, and TF binding data from the ReMap database 43 for K562 cells. This analysis revealed a high density of TF binding sites and DNase I peaks in both enhancer regions. Based on these findings, we segmented ALDH1A1‐E3 into Fragments A and B, and ALDH1A2‐E1 into Fragments A–C (Figure 3C) and performed luciferase assays using the shortened forms of these enhancers (Figure 3D). For ALDH1A1‐E3, neither Fragment A nor B retained DNR‐responsiveness, suggesting that the TFs responsible for enhancer activity could bind across the entire enhancer region. In contrast, for ALDH1A2‐E1, Fragment A retained full enhancer activity and DNR responsiveness, pinpointing a 222 bp segment as critical for ALDH1A2 induction following DNR treatment (Figure 3D).

We next focused on ALDH1A1‐E3 and ALDH1A2‐E1 Fragment A (ALDH1A2‐E1‐A) for further analysis. To uncover potential TFs mediating the response to DNR within these enhancers, we first compared the genome‐wide relative enrichment of H3K27ac signals between DNR‐treated and untreated cells using our ChIP‐seq data. This analysis identified a set of 1743 DNR‐induced enhancers, including the 6 ALDH1 enhancers (Figure 3E; Supporting Information S6: Table 6). Motif enrichment analysis of these enhancers revealed 247 enriched TF binding sites, including 21 motifs found in ALDH1A2‐E1‐A, 22 in ALDH1A1‐E3, and 10 shared between them (Figure 3F; Supporting Information S6: Table 6). Among the shared TF families, we identified TF binding sites for STATs, FOS/JUNs, TEADs, and ETVs (Figure 3F,G), suggesting that these TFs may be recruited at ALDH1A1‐E3 and ALDH1A2‐E1‐A enhancer elements to regulate ALDH1A1 and/or ALDH1A2 expression in response to DNR.

Mutations in the ETV, FOS/JUN, or STAT binding sites of the ALDH1A1‐E3 enhancer completely abolished its enhancer activity in DNR‐treated conditions (Figure 4B), suggesting that the intact enhancer sequence might be required for its drug‐responsive function. In contrast, mutation of the STAT or FOS/JUN binding sites within the ALDH1A2‐E1‐A enhancer led to a partial reduction in enhancer activity in DNR‐treated conditions, whereas mutations in the TEAD binding site had no measurable effect (Figure 4C). Notably, simultaneous disruption of both STAT and FOS/JUN binding sites fully abrogated ALDH1A2‐E1‐A activity, suggesting a cooperative role for these TFs in mediating the DNR response.

To further validate the role of STAT and JUN family members, we focused on STAT3 and c‐JUN, given their known involvement in stress‐responsive gene regulation and chemoresistance in leukemia. 44 , 45 , 46 Capillary‐based immunoassays revealed a time‐dependent increase in STAT3 and c‐JUN phosphorylation following DNR treatment (Figure 4D), which correlated with the upregulation of ALDH1A1 and ALDH1A2 in K562 cells (Figure 4E). Furthermore, pharmacological inhibition of STAT3 (TTI‐101) or JUN signaling (JNK‐IN‐8) significantly reduced DNR‐induced ALDH1A1 and ALDH1A2 expression (Figure 4E), reinforcing their role as key upstream regulators of these genes in response to anthracyclines.

Subsequently, we conducted drug combination studies to ascertain the contribution of STAT3 and c‐JUN in anthracycline resistance. Consistent with the previous results, co‐treatment with DNR and either TTI‐101 or JNK‐IN‐8 resulted in enhanced cytotoxic effects (Figure 4F). Altogether, our results demonstrate that c‐JUN and STAT3 bind ALDH1A1 and ALDH1A2 enhancer elements and promote their transcriptional activity upon DNR treatment, ultimately supporting the notion that targeting these signaling pathways may potentiate anthracycline efficacy and help overcome drug resistance.

Pharmacological inhibition of ALDH1 activity with DIMATE synergizes with DNR and reduces clonogenic potential in AML cells

Recognizing that TF inhibition may pose toxicological challenges, particularly in older and frail AML patients, we next evaluated whether direct ALDH1 inhibition could represent a more suitable therapeutic strategy. For this, we tested DIMATE, an irreversible inhibitor of ALDH1A1 and ALDH1A2 29 currently under clinical investigation, 47 in combination with DNR in AML cell lines spanning a range of DNR sensitivities and representing diverse genetic and differentiation phenotypes 48 (Figure 5A; Supporting Information S5: Table 5). All cell lines exhibited dose‐dependent sensitivity to DIMATE, with THP‐1, OCI‐AML3, and Kasumi‐1 being the least responsive (Supporting Information S10: Figure 5A). Bliss synergy analysis revealed synergistic interactions between DIMATE and DNR in most models tested (Figure 5A; Supporting Information S10: Figure 5B). The highest Bliss scores were observed in OCI‐AML3 (57.96) and MV‐4‐11 (30.79), followed by MOLM‐14 (22.26), Mono‐MAC‐6 (18.52), Kasumi‐1 (16.99), and KG1a (16.84), indicating robust synergy at select concentration pairs (Supporting Information S5: Table 5). K562 cells exhibited a more moderate interaction (peak Bliss score = 9.99), whereas THP‐1 remained within the additive to antagonistic range. THP‐1 is the most differentiated monocytic model in the panel and exhibits a high redox buffering capacity (e.g., elevated thioredoxin and glutathione levels). 49 , 50 This combination of advanced differentiation and strong intrinsic stress‐response mechanisms may limit the oxidative and aldehyde stress induced by the combination, thereby contributing to the antagonistic interaction observed.

To assess the impact on leukemic growth, we performed clonogenic assays in K562, MOLM‐14, MV‐4‐11, OCI‐AML3, and Kasumi‐1. Notably, suboptimal concentration of DIMATE combined with suboptimal concentration of DNR significantly reduced colony formation compared to either agent alone (Figure 5B). Altogether, these data suggest a cooperative interaction between ALDH1 inhibition and DNR, improving anthracycline sensitivity and limiting the clonogenic potential of leukemic cells in vitro.

Sequential administration of DIMATE and DNR resensitizes an anthracycline‐refractory AML murine model and improves in vivo efficacy

To further investigate the therapeutic potential of the DIMATE and DNR combination, we conducted in vivo studies using NSG mice orthotopically engrafted with human KG1a cells. This AML cell line is known to exhibit intrinsic resistance to DNR and harbors multiple chromosomal abnormalities characteristic of complex karyotype AML, 51 , 52 a high‐risk subtype associated with poor prognosis and refractoriness to standard anthracycline‐based therapy.

Upon confirmed engraftment, mice were randomized into four treatment groups: vehicle control (PBS), DNR, DIMATE, or the combination of DNR with DIMATE. Treatment was initiated 7 days after transplantation. DNR was administered at a low dose of 0.6 mg/kg daily for three consecutive days, followed by a 1‐week rest period, in two cycles. DIMATE (10 mg/kg) was given three times per week for four consecutive cycles (Figure 6A). The combination schedule was designed to limit anthracycline‐related toxicity in immunocompromised mice while maintaining a continuous inhibition of ALDH1.

Before evaluating therapeutic efficacy, we assessed ALDH1 enzymatic activity in human CD45+ leukemic cells isolated from the bone marrow after treatment. DNR monotherapy significantly increased ALDH1‐specific enzymatic activity compared to controls (Figure 6B), recapitulating our in vitro findings and supporting the hypothesis that ALDH1 upregulation represents an adaptive response to anthracyclines. This increase was detected one week posttreatment, suggesting a sustained enzymatic effect consistent with the elevated ALDH1 levels observed in anthracycline‐resistant patient samples.

Overall, all treatment regimens were well tolerated, with no significant body weight loss or overt signs of toxicity observed in the majority of animals (Supporting Information S10: Figure 6 and data not shown). As expected, KG‐1a leukemic cells exhibited high resistance to DNR in vivo, with no significant differences in leukemic burden compared to the vehicle control group (Figure 6C,D). In contrast, DIMATE monotherapy resulted in approximately a 50% reduction in leukemic burden (P = 0.0016), and the combination of DIMATE and DNR further potentiated this antileukemic effect, decreasing the leukemic burden to ~5% (P < 0.0001) (Figure 6C,D). Altogether, these results suggest that the combination of DIMATE and DNR enhances therapeutic efficacy and may represent a promising treatment strategy for DNR‐resistant AML.

DISCUSSION

AML remains a highly challenging malignancy to treat due to its genetic and epigenetic heterogeneity, high relapse rate, and frequent emergence of drug resistance. With many AML‐associated oncogenic drivers clinically undruggable, targeting stress‐response pathways activated during leukemic transformation and treatment pressure represents a promising therapeutic strategy, particularly in refractory or relapsed disease. The rationale behind this approach is that both malignant proliferation and cytotoxic treatment generate high levels of oxidative stress in leukemic cells. Identifying and targeting the specific mechanisms these cells rely on to mitigate oxidative damage may reveal convergent, targetable stress‐adaptive programs across genetically diverse AML subtypes.

ALDH1 enzymes catalyze the oxidation of reactive aliphatic aldehydes into nontoxic carboxylic acids, maintaining intracellular redox homeostasis and preventing aldehyde‐induced proteotoxicity and genotoxicity. 10 Reactive aldehydes also function as electrophilic signaling mediators, forming adducts with nucleophilic residues on regulatory proteins, modulating pathways involved in inflammation, oxidative stress responses, and cell survival. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 23 In AML, accumulating evidence implicates the ALDH1A1 in disease progression, chemotherapy resistance, and poor prognosis. 8 , 15 , 17 , 18 , 20 , 22 , 40 While the underlying regulatory and molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood, they likely involve its detoxifying activity as well as its role in retinoic acid biosynthesis, through which various genes involved in cell differentiation, proliferation, and stem cell maintenance are modulated. 10 , 13 , 23 Together, these functions position ALDH1A1 as a central metabolic node linking redox balance, signaling, and therapeutic resistance in AML.

Leveraging the updated Beat AML 2.0 dataset, we found a significant association between high ALDH1A1 expression and intermediate‐, adverse‐risk genetic profiles, prior history of anthracycline exposure, either in the context of a myeloid or nonmyeloid malignancy, and shorter OS, particularly in relapsed/refractory cases. These results align with previous observations from pediatric TARGET and adult TCGA cohorts as well as chemotherapy‐exposed patients from the MLCG cohort, but differ from findings in cytogenetically normal, chemotherapy‐naïve AML populations, 53 or in survival analyses that excluded recurrent AML 22 or with prior anthracycline treatment. Together, these findings suggest that the prognostic value of ALDH1A1 is strongest in anthracycline‐exposed, high‐risk AML, where oxidative stress and aldehyde burden can be elevated.

Here, we demonstrate that ALDH1A1 and ALDH1A2 are transcriptionally induced by anthracyclines in AML, both in vitro and in vivo. This upregulation translates into increased protein levels and enzymatic activity and appears sustained beyond drug exposure in our murine AML model 1 week following DNR treatment. These findings correspond to our previous clinical observations of progressive ALDH1 activity increases in persistent AML blasts during chemotherapy, 40 and are consistent with elevated ALDH1A1 and ALDH1A2 expression in non‐responders or therapy‐related AML within the Beat AML cohort. Moreover, this notion of ALDH1A1 involvement in adaptive resistance is strongly supported by recent longitudinal single‐cell RNA sequencing analyses in refractory or early‐relapsed AML patients, identifying ALDH1A1 as prominently upregulated within a subpopulation of quiescent stem‐like cells exhibiting high chemotherapy‐induced plasticity and strong association with poor patient outcomes. 54 Notably, these leukemic stem cells exhibited significant activation of chemoresistance‐associated transcriptional signatures including STAT3 and metabolic reprogramming.

Mechanistically, we identified two anthracycline‐responsive enhancer elements, ALDH1A1‐E3 and ALDH1A2‐E1‐A, mediating transcriptional induction of ALDH1A1 and ALDH1A2 via cooperative binding of STAT3 and FOS/JUN TF families. Co‐treatment with STAT3 or JNK inhibitors in vitro, or the dual ALDH1A1/1A2 inhibitor DIMATE, both in vitro and in vivo, enhanced DNR efficacy, functionally validating these TFs and ALDH1 as relevant contributors to drug resistance. These results align with the well‐established role of STAT3 and FOS/JUN in progression and stress‐induced gene regulation in AML, 45 , 46 and are consistent with the robust upregulation of FOS and JUN observed in early responses to DNR. 44

STAT3 signaling and the JNK/FOS‐JUN axis are frequently dysregulated in AML, often downstream of oncogenic mutations linked to poor prognosis. Compared to healthy controls, significantly higher JUN expression has been reported in AML cases with 11q23 abnormalities (KMT2A rearrangements) and in cases with complex karyotypes. 46 JUN upregulation has also been observed in AML characterized by core‐binding‐factor rearrangements, including t(8;21) (RUNX1::RUNX1T1) and inv(16)(p13.1q22) (CBFB::MYH11). 55 , 56 Although these subtypes typically respond favorably to standard chemotherapy, elevated ALDH1A1 at diagnosis, notably in t(8;21) AML, has been identified as predictive of relapse. 20 Mechanistically, JUN is proposed to contribute to prosurvival corrective functions of the unfolded protein response (UPR) in AML, with its expression notably increased following chemically induced endoplasmic reticulum stress. 46 Accordingly, elevated JUN levels have been linked to AML recurrence, 57 and therapy‐related myeloid malignancies (t‐MDS/t‐AML). 58

Constitutive activation of STAT3 occurs in approximately 25%–50% of AML cases, particularly those harboring FLT3‐ITD, c‐KIT, SRC or PTPN11 mutations. 59 , 60 , 61 Notably, PTPN11 mutations are frequently enriched in secondary or therapy‐related AML compared to de novo cases. 62 STAT3 activity can also be indirectly modulated via epigenetic, splicing, or transcriptional regulators frequently altered in high‐risk AML, such as EZH2, SF3B1, and TP53, 63 , 64 each strongly associated with chemoresistance. 2 Similarly, enhanced JAK/STAT signaling is a hallmark of inflammatory and proliferative MDS subtypes and MPNs characterized by mutations in JAK V617F , CALR, MPL, or TP53, or by complex karyotypes commonly observed during AML progression. 63 Collectively, the frequent constitutive activation of STAT3 and JUN signaling pathways in secondary AML may underlie the elevated ALDH1A1 and ALDH1A2 expression levels observed in patients with prior MDS, MDS/MPN, or t‐AML in the Beat AML dataset.

While other mechanisms undoubtedly contribute to anthracycline resistance, our data highlight ALDH1 upregulation as a targetable adaptive vulnerability shared across AML genotypes activating FOS/JUN and STAT3 pathways. Compared to alternative detoxification routes, such as glutathione conjugation or aldehyde reduction by aldo‐ketoreductases, ALDH1 uniquely converts aldehydes to excretable carboxylic acids without generating reactive oxigene species (ROS) unlike xanthine oxidase; simultaneously supporting redox homeostasis and energy metabolism through NADH production. 10 These features make ALDH1 a central metabolic defense system against aldehyde and oxidative stress, and a compelling therapeutic target in AML.

It is worth noting that, although exploratory (n = 4), non‐leukemic HSPCs exhibited a dose‐responsive increase in ALDH1A1 and ALDH1A2 transcripts following DNR exposure, suggesting that anthracycline‐induced ALDH1A upregulation may not be confined to malignant cells despite the typical higher basal ALDH1A1 expression in HSPCs compared with AML cells. 65 , 66 Whether induction in HSPCs also involves JNK and STAT3 acting at ALDH1A enhancer regions remains to be determined. Future studies should elucidate the magnitude and mechanism of DNR‐induced ALDH1A1 regulation in normal HSPCs. In conclusion, our study identifies ALDH1A1 and ALDH1A2 as transcriptional targets of stress‐activated STAT3 and FOS/JUN signaling in anthracycline‐treated AML, uncovering a novel epigenetic adaptive mechanism mediated by specific enhancers that act as integration hubs for these pathways. The demonstrated therapeutic synergy of ALDH1 inhibition with DNR provides a strong mechanistic rationale and preclinical framework to support clinical trials combining ALDH1 inhibitors, such as DIMATE, with conventional therapies.
