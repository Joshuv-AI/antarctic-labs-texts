Capillary-based optical fiber sensor for turbidity measurement
Authors: Evelyn Vanegas-Tenezaca, Angel I Correa Serrano, Marko Galarza, Raphael Jamier, Philippe Roy, Adolfo Cobo, Rosa A Perez-Herrera, Manuel Lopez-Amo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis work introduces an innovative capillary (hollow core) optical fiber-based sensor designed to measure turbidity using light reflection along its cladding. The structure consists of two capillary sections surrounding a single-mode fiber (SMF) section. The final capillary has been optimized to maximize light interaction with the external liquid. Experimentation includes data collection from different turbidity levels using the reflected light spectrum. Machine learning has been implemented to improve the measuring by exploring the effectiveness of various algorithms and neural network architectures in achieving a good root mean square error. We have obtained a maximum testing error of 5.88% NTU.Keywords: Capillary, Machine learning, optical fiber sensor, turbiditySubject terms: Engineering, Optics and photonics

Introduction

Monitoring water quality is essential in order to gain a deeper understanding and ensure the long-term preservation of aquatic ecosystems. In this context, fiber optics represent an innovative and ecologically friendly solution for measuring crucial parameters such as water flow, level, and turbidity. Turbidity is a key factor in assessing water quality. It is used to quantify the cloudiness or haziness caused by suspended particles. The measurement is expressed in nephelometric turbidity units (NTU), which provide a standardized scale for assessing water clarity.

There are a number of commercially available turbidity meters on the market1, with varying degrees of accuracy. Some have been known to experience measurement issues, particularly when there are rapid changes in turbidity. Furthermore, these devices frequently have limited functionality within a specific NTU range, which restricts their suitability for comprehensive water quality monitoring. In light of these considerations, there is a clear need to develop new and improved sensors of this kind.

For example, a recent optical sensor for turbidity measurement employs two near infrared (NIR) cameras2 to capture scattered and transmitted images as light passes through turbid solutions. The study established a functional relationship between color components and water turbidity, and the precision of the method was validated against a commercial turbidimeter over a wide linear range. However, there may be challenges when considering deploying this sensor in environments with constant motion.

As an alternative, optical fiber sensors are considered as a suitable and novel technology for these measurements. There are several notable references in this field, including an infrared turbidity sensor for the aluminum sulfate coagulant process3. This pioneering approach demonstrated the potential of optical fiber sensors to provide users with continuous turbidity information, offering a cost-effective solution for process monitoring with a maximum testing error of ± 11.6% NTU.

The sensor in4 relies on collecting scattered light through a fiber probe4. This sensor uses a fiber optic probe and a mirror to estimate turbidity, which is calculated using the total interaction coefficient. This method provides a straightforward effective solution for the detection of suspended impurities in liquids.

In the case of reference5, a plastic optical fiber is used to assess the turbidity level. The sensor incorporates a pair of multimode fibers (MMF) arranged in parallel with vertically mounted beveled ends. It has been shown to be an effective and reliable means of measuring turbidity, achieving an R2 value of 0.9947 in the best-case scenario. In the case of6, a tilted fiber Bragg grating (TFBG) is used. The sensor is in direct contact with the turbid liquid and is encapsulated within a microfluidic capillary. This allows the TFBG surface to be exposed to the turbid liquid. This enables the use of polarized evanescent waves to interact with particles suspended in the liquid, providing a highly sensitive and accurate measurement of surface turbidity. This work introduces a new optical fiber sensor based on a multi-capillary structure. To the best of our knowledge, no previous turbidity sensor combines a coaxial dual-capillary structure, operating in reflection along the cladding, with a machine-learning pipeline that maps the full reflected spectrum directly to turbidity in NTU. We have previously employed capillary structures for the measurement of strain, temperature and water level7,8. Our sensor system has been designed to guide light along its cladding, facilitating interaction with the environment and making it ideal for use as a turbidity sensor. The measurement is performed by capturing the reflected spectrum of the capillary and comparing it with those from a commercial sensor in order to validate the NTU values.

Conventional demodulation methods based on tracking a single spectral feature (e.g., peak/valley tracking or wavelength-shift calibration) assume a stable spectral shape and a monotonic relation between the selected feature and the measurand. In our multi-capillary structure, turbidity simultaneously modifies the phase and the attenuation of the interfering cladding modes through changes in the effective refractive index and particle-induced scattering, which alters both fringe position and fringe visibility/envelope. As a result, a single-feature demodulation becomes ambiguous and may lead to non-monotonic or non-unique mappings over a wide NTU range. Similar limitations of conventional peak/correlation/transform-based processing under spectral deformations have been reported in fiber-optic sensing, and machine-learning approaches have been shown to provide more robust spectrum-to-measurand mappings in such cases. For example, Dey et al. [9] demonstrated improved decoupling and accuracy in simultaneous strain/temperature estimation using ML with FBG sensors, and Djurhuus et al. [10] applied Gaussian process regression to directly map FBG reflection spectra to temperature, outperforming conventional processing under challenging conditions; a broader overview of ML-based demodulation in optical fiber sensors is given in Zhou et al. [11]

Given the non-monotonic and multi-parameter nature of the spectral response across the investigated turbidity range, a regression approach was adopted utilizing machine learning. To this end, we have conducted an exhaustive study to determine the optimal neural network configuration using MATLAB and its Regression Learner application. The aim is to enhance the network’s capacity for accurately predicting turbidity from reflection spectra associated with different NTU levels, achieving an R² of 0.999 and a maximum error of ± 5.88% NTU. This represents an improvement on previous studies in the field. This comprehensive approach aims to validate the capillary sensor and advance the application of innovative technologies for effective water quality monitoring.

This paper is an extended version of a work paper presented at the OFS29 conference [12]. It now provides a detailed modal study of the multifiber structure and a complete detailed description of the fabrication process and sensor measurement set-up. It also presents a full set of results and a new analysis of the improvement in measurement when machine learning is applied.

Sensor design

The turbidity of a liquid is defined as its extinction coefficient due to light scattering and it can only be measured on the basis of this definition, i.e. optically [13]. In the majority of cases, light scattering from a turbid liquid is asymmetrical, with greater intensity observed in the forward direction than in the backward direction. The extent of this asymmetry is primarily dependent on two variables: particle size and particle shape.

We have developed a fiber optic structure suitable for detecting variations of turbidity in water within the usual range of measurement in water quality applications. Our sensing transducer operates on the principle of light interaction with the surrounding medium - turbid water - as it propagates through the cladding of a capillary or hollow core fiber (HCF). To efficiently transfer light from a standard single-mode fiber (SMF) to the outer layer of the capillary fiber and to excite as many higher-order modes as possible, we propose and demonstrate the SMF-HCF structure shown in Fig. 1. The initial 55 μm diameter HCF fiber expands the incoming beam from the input SMF fiber to excite as many cladding modes as possible in the subsequent SMF section. These modes undergo multimodal interference, and with appropriate length, their field distribution allows effective coupling to the cladding of the final sensing HCF. The ring-shaped silica structure, which is surrounded by air on the inside and turbid water on the outside, provides fully guided propagation of light without any leakage loss.

Schematic of the double SMF-HCF structure. The length of the elements is properly designed to provide controlled power transfer between the input SMF fiber mode and the highest possible order modes in the final capillary.

A commercial fully vectorial finite difference mode solver [14] was used to design the structure. Due to its symmetry, only radially symmetric modes were considered. The lengths of the intermediate capillary fiber (LCAP) and the SMF (LSMF) fibers (see Fig. 1) were carefully optimized to balance between transmitted power and the excitation of the highest possible order modes.

In brief, the first capillary section acts as a modal coupler, transferring power from the SMF fundamental mode to higher-order cladding modes. The intermediate SMF section then recombines and sets the modal distribution injected into the final sensing capillary, maximizing the fraction of the optical field that interacts with the external liquid. Turbidity modifies the real part of the effective refractive index, producing phase changes that shift the interference fringes, and introduces concentration-dependent attenuation associated with particle-induced scattering, which changes fringe visibility and the spectral envelope. As a result, single-feature demodulation can become non-monotonic over a wide NTU range, motivating the use of full-spectrum regression.

Figure 2 (a) shows the modal power distribution in the intermediate SMF fiber resulting from the modal interference in the first capillary as a function of its length (LCAP). The guided mode and the first nine symmetric cladding modes show significant coupling. To determine the length of the first capillary, we considered the power minima of the guided mode LP01 and the power maxima of the higher-order cladding modes. At lengths around 0.9, 2.5, and 4.5 mm, there is a pronounced power minimum in the guided mode, and up to seven cladding modes are significantly excited. The power loss at these lengths is as low as 0.3 dB. Since longer fibers are easier to process, a length of 4.5 mm was chosen for the first capillary LCAP.

Fabrication

Following the theoretical design shown in Sect. 2, the first capillary section was cut to 4.5 mm and the intermediate SMF section to 7.5 mm, matching the optimized lengths LCAP and LSMF.

The proposed structure was fabricated using a Fujikura FSM-100P fusion splicer. It should be noted that fusion between a capillary and a single-mode fiber is not as direct as a fusion between fibers with the same mechanical characteristics. A weak fusion may result in a vulnerable connection that is susceptible to breaking. Conversely, a strong fusion may pose a risk of core collapse in the capillary. Furthermore, this process can cause both fibers to deform.

To obtain mechanically robust splices without collapsing the capillary, the standard fusion program of the FSM-100P for SMF–SMF splices was slightly tuned rather than designing an entirely new profile. In practice, only the power and duration of the cleaning and main arcs were modified, while the other parameters (gap, alignment, prefusion, etc.) were left at the factory defaults. The cleaning step was set to a Cleaning Arc Power of STD-62 bit with a Cleaning Arc Time of 150 ms, which provided sufficient removal of surface contamination while limiting lateral deformation of the capillary. The main arc was then applied with a power of STD-97 bit and a duration of 1500 ms. These values are expressed in the internal STD units referenced to the Arc Calibration Method Standard of the splicer, so that the same relative conditions are recovered after each automatic arc calibration. This tuning should be regarded as a practical guideline for reproducible assembly of the SMF–HCF–SMF structure, rather than as a general-purpose new fusion profile.

The initial section of the structure is constructed using a standard single-mode fiber cable terminated with an Angled Physical Contact (APC) fiber connector. The end was prepared for fusion with a capillary featuring a hollow core with a diameter of 55 μm.

Compared with the default SMF–SMF program recommended by Fujikura for the FSM-100P, the main differences are therefore limited to the choice of the cleaning arc power and the main arc duration, which were empirically adjusted to avoid collapse of the hollow core while ensuring a reliable mechanical joint between the SMF and the capillary. No additional modifications to the internal fusion algorithms were required. These settings were used consistently for all the splices in the proposed sensor, and microscopic inspection confirmed the preservation of the inner hole and the absence of significant outer-cladding deformation, as illustrated in Fig. 4.

Composite micrograph of the full multi-capillary structure. The figure is shown in sections because the fusion splicer cannot capture the entire device in a single frame. From left to right, the image displays: the first fusion splice between the input SMF patch cord and the initial capillary segment of length Lcap; the subsequent splice to the intermediate SMF section of length LSMF; and the final splice to the long capillary segment (75 mm). Each region preserves the inner hole of the capillaries and exhibits minimal outer-cladding deformation, as designed in the sensor layout shown in Fig. 1.

The process requires clean cuts to be made in both the SMF and the capillary. This clean cut was made at each fiber end in preparation for fusion. Subsequently, a new incision was made, leaving a 4.5 mm capillary fragment fused to the SMF cable with connector. A new 7.5 mm SMF section was then fused. Once the initial preparation of the sensing head had been completed, a further fusion was carried out with a fully stripped capillary fragment measuring 7.5 cm-length. Figure 1 illustrates the schematic configuration of the final multi-capillary structure.

The cuts were made using the fiber cutter (FITEL S326) which has millimeter markings. This facilitates the precise cutting of fibers to the required length. The final capillary section was sealed using a last discharge of the Splicer to prevent any liquid from entering the hole in the last capillary. Once the structure has been fabricated, a high-quality optical reflection signal is obtained using a fiber Bragg grating (FBG) optical interrogator (Micron Optics SM130).

Figure 5 shows the reflected signal obtained from the structure, which exhibits the Fabry-Pérot interference pattern predicted by our theoretical calculations (see Fig. 3). This pattern is characterized by evenly spaced interference fringes, resulting from the constructive and destructive interference of light reflected between the interfaces of the structure. The maximum value of the envelope of reflected power is observed between 1520 and 1532 nm wavelengths, with a period of interference in this range of 0.24 nanometers.

Normalized reflected spectrum from the multi-capillary structure.

After validating the interferometric response of the assembled structure in reflection, the sensor was integrated into a setup for turbidity measurements, as described in Sect. 4.

Set up

The experimental setup employed to evaluate the multicapillary sensor as a turbidity transducer is depicted in Fig. 6, with all optical, mechanical, and fluidic components explicitly labeled. A rectangular Polylactic Acid (PLA) vessel (internal dimensions: 160 × 10 × 12 mm) was developed to ensure a narrow optical path and to maintain the fiber in a fixed, reproducible position. The sensing head was positioned horizontally and fully submerged at a depth of around 9 mm, maintaining a minimum clearance of 5 mm from the vessel walls to prevent spurious reflections. The capillary structure was secured at both ends to ensure a straight geometry devoid of curvature.

Experimental setup used for turbidity measurements. The figure shows the PLA vessel, the position of the multi-capillary sensing head, the interrogation scheme based on the SM130 optical interrogator.

A standard SMF patch cord with an APC termination connected the capillary sensor to a Micron Optics SM130 interrogator via one of the 4 channels. This interrogator, designed specifically for FBG sensors, is also suitable for real time measurements of interferometric sensors, as demonstrated in [15]. The interrogator was configured with the following parameters: a gain of 18 dB, a spectral range of 1510–1590 nm, 32,768 spectral points, an integration time of 200 ms, and continuous data acquisition.

All measurements were carried out in a temperature-controlled laboratory at 25 °C. Formazin solutions with turbidity levels of 0 to 280 NTU were prepared using a certified reference standard in accordance with ISO 7027. For each turbidity level, the vessel was emptied, rinsed three times with ultrapure water, and filled with 15 mL of the corresponding solution. An ISO 7027-compliant commercial turbidimeter [16], with a precision of 0.01–0.1 NTU, was employed to verify the turbidity of each prepared solution prior to measurement. The solution was allowed to stabilize for 60 s to ensure particle homogeneity prior to data acquisition. Two hundred reflection spectra were recorded for each turbidity level, yielding a total dataset of 1800 spectra.

The resulting dataset of 1800 reflection spectra, covering turbidity values from 0 to 280 NTU, was then analyzed using both conventional wavelength-shift demodulation and machine-learning-based regression, as detailed in Section V.

Data analysis

The initial analysis, which was based on tracking individual spectral features, was inconclusive because turbidity affects multiple parameters simultaneously through changes in the real and imaginary parts of the refractive index of the suspension, including fringe visibility, depth and phase. The first step in the analysis consisted in applying a conventional demodulation strategy based on the wavelength shift Δλ of a selected interference minimum as a function of turbidity. In many interferometric fiber sensors, this approach provides a simple single-parameter calibration curve. In our case, however, the superposition of several cladding-mode interference patterns and the attenuation induced by the scattering particles lead to spectra in which both the position and the contrast of the fringes change with NTU (see inset of Fig. 7). As a result, different NTU values can produce very similar apparent Δλ, and the resulting Δλ-versus-NTU curve is not strictly monotonic over the whole 0–280 NTU range, preventing the definition of a single-valued analytical calibration.

Reflected spectra obtained by immersing the multi-capillary sensing head in Formazin solutions with turbidity levels between 0 and 280 NTU.

As shown in Fig. 7, an increase in turbidity is accompanied by a shift in the transfer function and a significant modification of the fringe visibility and overall envelope. When the analysis is restricted to a single spectral feature, using only one parameter becomes unreliable at high turbidity levels, so wavelength tracking alone cannot provide a robust calibration.

The behavior observed in Fig. 7 is consistent with the fact that the effective refractive index of a turbid particle suspension in a liquid depends on particle size, scattering angles, refractive indices and particle concentration [17]. Therefore, the wavelength shift of the interference pattern arises from changes in the real part of the effective refractive index of the surrounding medium, as predicted by the model in Fig. 3. b, which modifies the boundary conditions of the guided modes and hence their phase.

In addition, the proposed design enhances the interaction between the evanescent fields of the higher-order modes propagating in the capillary and the surrounding suspension. A fraction of the light scattered by the particles is coupled back into these modes, while the imaginary part of their complex effective refractive index accounts for a concentration-dependent attenuation of the fringes. As a result, both the spectral position and the visibility of the interference fringes evolve with turbidity.

Therefore, instead of compressing the spectrum into a single variable (Δλ), we adopted a data-driven regression approach that exploits the full reflected spectrum as input.

For the machine-learning analysis, each reflection spectrum acquired by the SM130 interrogator was treated as one sample. As described in Section IV, we recorded 200 spectra for each of the 9 turbidity levels between 0 and 280 NTU, resulting in a dataset of 1800 spectra. Each spectrum spans the 1510–1590 nm range and is sampled at 32,768 wavelength points, so that the reflected intensity values form a 32,768-dimensional feature vector x ∈ ℝ³²⁷⁶⁸. The corresponding turbidity value in NTU is used as the scalar regression target y for that sample (with y ∈ [0, 280.0]). The wavelength axis is identical for all spectra and is therefore not included as an input feature.

The regression model was trained and evaluated using ISO 7027 Formazin standards for reproducible calibration; transferability to real environmental samples was not assessed in this study and may require additional training with representative datasets.

Among the different algorithms tested, a trilayer neural network provided the best compromise between complexity and performance, yielding nearly ideal linearity between predicted and true NTU (slope 0.999 in Fig. 8) and a maximum testing error of 5.9% NTU. In other words, the machine-learning-based demodulation does not change the underlying physics of the sensor, but it enables an accurate and unambiguous retrieval of turbidity from complex spectral signatures in a regime where a wavelength-shift analysis is insufficient.

Best value of RMSE obtained, using a trilayered neural network.

To assess the model’s predictive capability, a five-fold cross-validation was conducted using MATLAB’s regression learner tool [18]. This approach involves dividing the data set into five equal parts, with the model trained on four parts, while the remaining part is used for validation. This process was repeated five times, with the validation partition changing in each iteration. Prior to performing this split, the data was randomly shuffled to ensure that each partition is representative of the entire data set, avoiding bias and ensuring that the partitions contain a similar distribution of features and results.

To reduce the risk of overfitting, the neural network was trained using validation-based early stopping, which stops the training process when the validation loss no longer improves. This method was used in addition to the five-fold cross-validation described above.

Finally, the average validation error across the five iterations was calculated to obtain a robust estimate of the model’s accuracy. This approach not only provides a more reliable evaluation of the performance of the model, but also makes the most of the available data by ensuring that each data point is used for both training and validation. The root mean square error (RMSE), calculated as the difference between the model predictions and the actual values of the target variable (in this case, NTU levels), allows for the selection of the model that best fits the data.

It is important to emphasize that having several spectra per turbidity level is essential to train and validate the regression models in a statistically meaningful way. In our case, the combination of 9 turbidity levels and 200 spectra per level, as described above, yielded a total of 1800 samples, which proved sufficient to obtain stable cross-validation errors without evidence of overfitting.

The sets of trained models were drawn from the following groups: support vector machines (SVM), Kernel Regression, Ensemble and Regression Neural Networks. In order to ascertain which model is the most appropriate for our data set, we have analyzed the RMSE value. In our preliminary study, neural networks yielded significantly superior results. Subsequently, we determined which of the five neural networks is the most effective. These neural networks, include two-layer and three-layer models. The selection of an appropriate model is based on the results of multiple iterations and a thorough evaluation of the outcomes.

Figure 9 illustrates the RMSE values obtained in each iteration, offering valuable insights into the performance of neural network models across different iterations. The results demonstrate that the Bilayered and Trilayered Neural Network models have superior performance, as evidence by their lower RMSE values.

Figure 8 depicts a “Predicted Response vs. True Response” graph (obtained with the minimum RMSE, iteration 13, in the Trilayered Neural Network), a standard tool in machine learning utilized to assess predictive model performance. Each data point on the graph represents an observation, with the y-axis indicating the model’s predicted values and the x-axis representing the true values. In an ideal scenario, all blue points would align on a perfect diagonal line with a slope of 1, indicating flawless predictions.

In this instance, the slope of the line is 0.999, which demonstrates that the Trilayered Neural Network model’s predictions are in close alignment with the actual values. This near-perfect alignment indicates that the model is highly accurate in its predictions. The maximum testing error is 5.88% NTU, and the average error is 3.88% NTU. It is therefore evident that the application of machine learning for regression, specifically with the Trilayered Neural Network model, proves to be an effective method for predicting turbidity levels. This proves the resilience of the model and dependability in real-world scenarios, making it a valuable asset for analyzing and predicting continuous data.

MATLAB enables the export of trained models from the Regression Learner application, facilitating their deployment in practical applications. Once the model has been trained and exported, it can be integrated into a real-time workflow. Our Micron Optics SM130 interrogator has been used to obtain spectral data over a TCP connection. Simultaneously, MATLAB enables the real-time reception of this data and its processing with the trained model. This eases the real-time measurement of the turbidity level based on the received spectrum. This real-time demonstration is implemented in a PC-based using windows 11 environment; an optimized embedded implementation is beyond the scope of this work.

In addition, a long-duration monitoring test was performed to evaluate the temporal stability of the system during continuous operation with liquid renewal in the container and periodic ultrasonic cleaning cycles. Data were acquired in intervals of approximately 60 min between cleaning cycles, with a cleaning duration of ~ 10 min; to visualize the trend, a 20-point moving average was also plotted (MATLAB movmean). In Fig. 10 (a), two different series of measurements were carried out for tap water versus turbid water extracted from a river. The estimated signal remains stable within each measurement interval (the peak-to-peak variation is indicated in the figure) and, after each cleaning cycle, the level returns to a consistent behavior, with no evidence of cumulative drift over the evaluated period. Likewise, the multi-day time series in Fig. 10 (b) shows stability between cycles and no appreciable drift, even when considering the temperature variations recorded before and after re-immersing the sensor between cleaning cycles, supporting the robustness of the system during prolonged operation.

Conclusion

This study presents a multi-capillary sensor structure optimized for turbidity measurement, using light reflection along the cladding of the last capillary. The sensor design leverages the interaction between the high-order cladding modes of a HCF and the surrounding medium, which is, in this case, turbid water. The length of the elements has been optimized to facilitate controlled power transfer between the SMF input fiber mode and the cladding modes in the final capillary.

The experimentation phase involves the collection of data from a range of NTU levels using the reflected spectrum. Due to the multiple physical variables involved in the measurements, it was decided that the received reflected spectrum should be analyzed using machine learning for data regression. In this instance, the MATLAB Regression Learner tool was employed.

In terms of neural networks, the application incorporates models inspired by the structure of the human brain, such as Bilayer and Trilayer neural networks. These models are used to learn complex patterns in data and make predictions. The selection of the most appropriate model is based on a process of iteration and evaluation of results over multiple repetitions.

Following an examination of the results obtained over several iterations, it has been determined that the three-layer neural network consistently delivers the lowest RMSE values, indicating overall superior performance. With this improvement, a maximum testing error of 5.9% NTU is achieved.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the viability of utilizing a multi-capillary sensor and machine learning methodology to accurately and effectively measure water turbidity, which has the potential to enhance water quality monitoring and management in diverse settings.

As a next step, the proposed sensor and machine-learning demodulation will be validated using real environmental water samples, and the transferability of the trained model will be assessed, including potential re-calibration or re-training to ensure robustness across different particle compositions and size distributions.
