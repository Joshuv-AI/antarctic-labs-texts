Prevalence of stress its sources and coping strategies among final year medical students at the University of Dongola Sudan 2025
Authors: Kamal A A Mohammed, Rowaa G A Ahmed, Anfal A O Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedical education is globally recognized as a high-stress environment, yet localized data regarding final-year students in Northern Sudan remain scarce. This study investigated the prevalence, independent predictors, and coping mechanisms of stress among final-year medical students at the University of Dongola.MethodsA descriptive, cross-sectional study was conducted in (2025) at the University of Dongola, Sudan. The study population comprised 120 final-year medical students. A total of 100 students completed the questionnaire, representing a response rate of 83.3%. Perceived stress was quantified using the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10), while a structured 22-item checklist was utilized to identify specific academic/non-academic stressors and coping behaviors. The questionnaire was administered in English to align with the medium of instruction. Data were analyzed using Chi-square tests (X2) and Binary Logistic Regression to identify independent predictors of stress (aOR; 95% CI).ResultsA total of 100 students participated (83.3% response rate). The findings revealed that 77% of respondents experienced moderate-to-high stress levels (67% moderate; 10% high), with a mean PSS score of 18.17 ± 6.56. Exam pressure (73%) and academic workload (61%) were the dominant stressors. Multivariable analysis confirmed that exam pressure (aOR = 4.82, 95% CI:} 1.95–11.84, p < 0.001) and academic workload (aOR = 2.54, 95% CI:} 1.12–5.76, p = 0.026) were the primary independent predictors of stress. Regarding coping, prayer (67%) and social interaction (46%) were the most utilized strategies. Notably, physical exercise (aOR = 0.28, p = 0.008) and social support (aOR = 0.35, p = 0.024) were independently associated with a significantly reduced likelihood of high stress.ConclusionA significant majority of final-year students at the University of Dongola operate under high levels of psychological distress, primarily driven by structural curriculum demands. While religious and social buffers are utilized, the high prevalence of stress suggests that students have surpassed the optimal "eustress" threshold. Institutional interventions, including examination reform and the establishment of formal counseling services, are urgently required to safeguard student well-being and clinical competence.Keywords: Medical students, Perceived stress, Coping strategies, Sudan, Exam pressure, Multivariable analysis

Introduction

Background

The concept of stress is frequently defined as a state of psychological trauma or physiological tension that arises when an individual perceives that the demands of their environment exceed their available coping resources. These coping mechanisms—the cognitive and behavioral efforts used to manage internal and external pressures—are not universal; they vary significantly from one individual to another and are shaped by a complex interplay of personality traits, past experiences, and environmental support systems [16].

In the pedagogical landscape, academic stress is specifically characterized as a product of the interaction between rigorous academic demands and the adaptive materials or psychological resilience available to the student [18]. Within medical education, several “stressors” have been consistently identified as primary triggers. These include the pervasive fear of academic failure, feelings of personal inadequacy within a highly competitive cohort, and dissatisfaction with teaching–learning activities. Furthermore, the quality of the teacher–pupil relationship and the availability of adequate learning resources at home significantly dictate a student’s stress levels [25].

The relationship between stress and achievement is nuanced. Historically, it has been observed that a moderate degree of stress—often referred to as “eustress”—can act as a catalyst for performance. Research indicates that students experiencing high or moderate stress may perform better than those with negligible stress, as the pressure fosters alertness and ensures the timely completion of complex tasks [15, 28]. However, when stress becomes chronic or overwhelming, it crosses into “distress.” Excessive stress is a precursor to debilitating psycho-social and emotional consequences, including generalized anxiety, clinical depression, social withdrawal, and, in extreme cases, suicidal ideation [6, 23]. Globally, the mental health of medical students is a growing concern,a comprehensive meta-analysis revealed that the prevalence of depressive symptoms among this group is approximately 27.2%, with 11.1% reporting suicidal ideation—rates significantly higher than their non-medical peers [27].

Final-year medical students represent a particularly vulnerable demographic within the university ecosystem. As they stand at the threshold of professional practice, they face a “triple burden”: an immense academic workload, increasing clinical responsibilities involving real patient care, and the looming pressure of high-stakes final examinations. This accumulation of pressure can severely impair mental health, diminish academic performance, and—most critically—compromise the quality of patient care they provide [9].

While various coping strategies—ranging from healthy lifestyle choices to maladaptive behaviors—exist, there is a notable lack of localized data. Specifically, at the University of Dongola in Sudan, little is known about the current prevalence of stress or the specific mechanisms these students employ to survive their final year. This knowledge gap prevents the institution from identifying students at risk and implementing effective support systems.

This study is essential for several reasons. First, by identifying the root causes of stress at the University of Dongola, the faculty can better support academic performance and ensure that graduates possess the “professional readiness” required for their internship year [13].

Second, the study aims to promote the holistic well-being of students, recognizing that mental health is a prerequisite for physical health and long-term career sustainability [26]. Furthermore, the findings will serve as a foundational guide for creating context-specific interventions, such as tailored wellness programs and counseling services that respect the cultural and economic realities of Northern Sudan. Finally, this research will contribute valuable data to the regional literature on medical student stress, filling a gap in the Sudanese context and providing a benchmark for future studies in similar environments [7].

Over the past decade, there has been increasing global attention on psychological stress among medical students. Evidence consistently demonstrates that academic and social pressures significantly affect both mental well-being and academic performance.

A study conducted in Saudi Arabia reported that 82% of respondents experienced academic stress, 38.7% financial stress, and 56% social stress [4]. Female students exhibited higher perceived stress than males but adopted more coping strategies. Notably, 30% of participants used alcohol or illicit drugs, while 94.2% relied on prayer and 82% engaged in social interactions with friends as coping mechanisms. Similarly, a cross-sectional study in Pakistan found that 23.3% of respondents had psychological morbidity, and 52.3% demonstrated distress [14].

In India, Anuradha et al. [3] reported that heavy academic workloads, fear of failure, and personal issues such as loneliness were the main sources of stress. The mean perceived stress score was 25.64 ± 5.44. At Rabigh Medical College, Gazzaz et al. [11] identified a stress prevalence of 59.2%, with academic stressors being significantly associated with academic performance (p < 0.05).

In Nigeria, Fasoro et al. [10] found that 67% of medical students perceived their studies as stressful, with female students more affected than males. In South Africa, Mahlangu, Mokoena and Ntuli (2024) reported that 25% of students experienced symptoms of perceived stress after the COVID-19 pandemic, identifying age, gender, and marital status as significant predictors.

A multicenter study across six Sudanese medical schools found that time pressure, heavy workload, and fear of failure were the main stressors [22]. At the University of Gezira, 72.9% of medical students experienced moderate stress and 16.1% severe stress, with the most frequent coping mechanism being sleeping [8]. Currently, no published data are available regarding stress and coping strategies among medical students at the University of Dongola.

Objective

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the prevalence and severity of perceived stress among final-year medical students at the University of Dongola. Specifically, the study aimed to identify the predominant academic and non-academic stressors and to determine the independent predictors of moderate-to-high stress levels through multivariable modeling. Furthermore, the research sought to evaluate the utilization and perceived effectiveness of various coping strategies employed by students to mitigate these pressures within the local sociocultural context.

Methods

Study design and setting

A descriptive, cross-sectional institutional-based study was conducted among final-year medical students at the Faculty of Medicine, University of Dongola, Sudan, during the 2025 academic session.

Population and sampling

The target population for this study comprised the entire cohort of final-year medical students (MBBS, Year 6) registered at the University of Dongola during the 2025 academic session (N = 120). Using a convenience sampling approach, all eligible students were invited to participate, resulting in a final sample of 100 respondents. This 83.3% response rate ensures strong internal representativeness and minimizes potential selection bias for this institutional cohort.

Data collection tools

Data were collected from October to December 2025 using a multi-component, self-administered structured questionnaire. The instrument was designed and organized based on the Transactional Model of Stress, ensuring that all variables—ranging from environmental triggers to internal appraisals—were theoretically grounded. The questionnaire consisted of three parts:

Socio-demographics: This section recorded participant age, gender, and residency status.

Theoretical framework

The study was grounded in two complementary psychological frameworks to ensure theoretical alignment across the research design, variable selection, and data interpretation:

The Transactional Model of Stress [16]: This model served as the primary foundation for the questionnaire design. It defines stress as a dynamic interaction between environmental demands and an individual’s psychological resources. By applying this theory, the study categorized variables into:Primary Appraisal (Stressors): Specifically selected to identify “evaluative threats” (e.g., exam pressure) and environmental demands (e.g., workload).Secondary Appraisal (Coping): Selected to identify how students utilize available resources (e.g., social support, religious practices) to mitigate perceived threats. This theoretical mapping ensured that the checklist items were not merely descriptive but were theoretically motivated constructs.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Students officially registered in the final year (6th year) of the MBBS program at the University of Dongola.

Students present on campus during the data collection period.

Students who voluntarily provided informed consent to participate.

Exclusion criteria

Students in Years 1–5 were excluded to ensure a homogeneous study population and focus on the unique “Triple Burden” of the final year: high-stakes exit examinations, professional transition anxiety, and increased clinical responsibilities. Theoretically grounded in the Yerkes-Dodson Law, this focus targets the “tipping point” where academic pressure reaches its maximum intensity.

Students on official leave or those who were absent due to illness during the study period.

Incomplete questionnaires (missing 20% of the PSS-10 responses).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0. Initially, descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, and frequencies) were calculated to characterize the sample and the prevalence of stress.

Bivariate Analysis: Chi-square tests (X2) were used to explore the associations between perceived stress levels and various academic and non-academic stressors. The strength of these associations was assessed using the Phi (Φ) coefficient.

Multivariate Analysis: To control for potential confounding variables such as age and gender, a Binary Logistic Regression model was constructed. The dependent variable was binary perceived stress (Low Stress vs. Moderate/High Stress). All independent variables that reached statistical significance in the bivariate analysis (p < 0.05) were entered into the model simultaneously. Adjusted Odds Ratios (aOR) and 95% Confidence Intervals (CI) were reported to determine independent predictors. Statistical significance for all tests was set at p < 0.05.

Perceived stress, sources, and coping strategies

The overall PSS-10 scores followed a near-normal distribution with a mean (SD) of 18.17 ± 6.56. Stress stratification (Table 1) revealed that 77.0% of students experienced moderate-to-high stress levels. Item-level analysis showed that while 47.0% of students felt that difficulties were "piling up too high," 57.0% reported confidence in handling personal problems, indicating a degree of functional resilience.

Academic stressors and coping mechanisms are detailed in Table 2. Exam pressure (73.0%) and academic workload (61.0%) were the most prevalent stressors. To mitigate these pressures, students primarily relied on meditation/prayer (67.0%), sleeping (51.0%), and social support (46.0%). Notably, 86.0% of students acknowledged that stress negatively impacted their academic performance.

Discussion

This study investigated the prevalence, etiology, and mitigation strategies of psychological distress among final-year medical students at the University of Dongola. Our findings indicate that 77.0% of respondents experience moderate-to-high stress levels. This high prevalence confirms that medical education in Northern Sudan remains an exceptionally high-pressure environment, mirroring the intense psychological demands documented throughout global medical literature. This suggests that the 'Triple Burden' of academic workload, clinical transition, and high-stakes exit examinations reaches a critical threshold during the final year of MBBS training.

Perceived stress and comparative analys

The mean PSS-10 score (18.17 ± 6.56) aligns with findings from other Sudanese institutions, such as the University of Gezira, where 72.9% of students reported moderate stress [8], and broader national cohorts facing intense academic workloads [22]. Globally, these trends are consistent with reported stress rates in Pakistan [14], India [3], and Bangladesh [2]. Within the Middle Eastern context, where rates often fluctuate between 59 and 82% [11, 24], our data reinforces the premise that medical students experience significant psychological strain regardless of curriculum structure or socioeconomic environment.

While the Yerkes-Dodson Law suggests that moderate pressure can enhance alertness, the 77.0% prevalence observed in this cohort suggests students have been pushed beyond their optimal threshold into the 'distress' phase. Interestingly, a duality emerged: while students felt overwhelmed, a majority reported confidence in handling personal problems. This high stress coupled with functional resilience mirrors observations from Brazil and Syria [1, 19], where trainees maintain academic progress and personal efficacy despite persistent psychological strain.

Drivers of academic stress

Exam pressure (73.0%) and academic workload (61.0%) emerged as the dominant stressors. Bivariate analysis confirmed a robust association between exam pressure and high stress levels, yielding a significant Chi-square value (χ2 = 18.42, p < 0.001) and a Phi coefficient of Φ = 0.43, indicating a moderately strong positive correlation. Furthermore, the multivariable logistic regression model identified exam pressure as the most potent independent predictor of distress (aOR = 4.82, 95% CI: 1.95–11.84, p < 0.001). This suggests that the psychological burden is fundamentally anchored in structural curriculum demands and high-stakes assessments [20]. These findings support the existence of an Eclipse Effect, as described by Murakami et al. [19], where the intensity of academic concerns effectively marginalizes other life domains such as financial or social issues rendering them non-significant within the multivariable model.

Coping mechanisms and buffers

Consistent with Islamic cultural contexts, students primarily utilized prayer/meditation (67.0%) and social interaction (46.0%) as core coping mechanisms. These findings align with previous studies in Saudi Arabia [4, 24] and Nepal [21], which highlight the central role of spiritual and communal activities in mitigating academic pressure. Crucially, our multivariable model further validated these choices, demonstrating that social support acted as a significant independent buffer against high stress (χ2 = 9.88, p = 0.012). Similarly, our statistical model confirmed that physical exercise was significantly associated with lower stress levels (χ2 = 12.15, Φ = −0.35), serving as a strong independent protective factor (aOR = 0.28, p = 0.008).

While 82.0% of students perceived their coping as effective, 67.0% expressed a desire for formal psychological support, revealing a critical gap in institutional mental health services. Furthermore, the low prevalence of maladaptive strategies (< 15\%) suggests a socially moderated coping culture, contrasting with higher rates of passive coping reported in some Western contexts [12]

Gender neutrality in stress response

Despite a female predominance in the sample (77.0%), gender was not a significant independent predictor of stress levels (p = 0.812). This aligns with recent gender-based analyses by Mahlangu et al. [17], which suggest that in high-intensity clinical environments, the structural pressure of the curriculum exerts a psychological impact that transcends demographic variables, affecting the student body relatively uniformly.

Limitations

Sampling and Generalizability: The use of convenience sampling may limit external validity. However, the 83.3% response rate provides strong internal representativeness for this institutional cohort.

Study Design: The cross-sectional nature precludes causal claims. The relationship between exercise and lower stress is reported strictly as an association; as noted in similar regional studies [20], it is possible that students with lower baseline stress are more capable of engaging in healthy behaviors.

Self-Report Bias: Reliance on self-reported data may introduce social desirability bias. Furthermore, the stressors checklist was an ad hoc tool without established formal reliability metrics, unlike the validated PSS-10.

Objective Metrics: Because academic performance (GPA) was not objectively measured, the application of the Yerkes-Dodson Law remains a theoretical inference. Future longitudinal research incorporating objective performance data is recommended.

Conclusion

Final-year medical students at the University of Dongola experience a high prevalence of psychological pressure, with 77.0% of the cohort reporting moderate-to-high stress levels. While bivariate analysis identified multiple stressors, the subsequent application of a multivariable logistic regression model confirmed that exam pressure (aOR = 4.82, p < 0.001) is the primary independent predictor of distress. The high Chi-square (χ2 = 18.42) and Phi (Φ = 0.43) values associated with academic factors demonstrate that student distress is fundamentally linked to structural curriculum demands and high-stakes assessments, remaining significant even when controlling for demographic variables such as age and gender.

Regarding resilience, students demonstrate a cultural reliance on religious coping, social support, and physical activity. The stability of physical exercise (aOR = 0.28) and social interaction (aOR = 0.35) as significant independent buffers against stress suggests that these are not merely "spurious" correlations but critical elements of the students' coping architecture. Furthermore, the non-significance of non-academic factors supports the existence of an "Eclipse Effect," where academic demands dominate the psychological landscape of medical trainees.

While the Yerkes-Dodson Law provides a theoretical framework for these results, the 77.0% prevalence indicates that many students have surpassed their peak performance threshold and moved into the "distress" phase. Without targeted institutional intervention, this level of chronic stress risks compromising student well-being and, ultimately, the quality of patient care. These findings underscore an urgent need for medical faculties to implement structured psychological support, examination reform, and curriculum-based wellness programs to support students during this critical professional transition.

Recommendations

Based on the independent predictors and protective factors identified in this study, it is recommended that the Faculty of Medicine at the University of Dongola distribute examinations more evenly to reduce the high stress associated with clustered assessments. Establishing a confidential counseling service would address the substantial proportion of students who expressed a need for professional psychological support. Incorporating brief stress-management training into the curriculum may strengthen students’ coping abilities, while improving access to recreational facilities could enhance the use of physical activity as an effective stress-buffering strategy. Implementing structured mentorship programs may help final-year students navigate academic and clinical pressures, and encouraging student-led wellness initiatives can reinforce social support networks. Providing clearer clinical expectations may reduce uncertainty-related stress, and collaborating with other Sudanese medical schools could help standardize wellness support across the region.
