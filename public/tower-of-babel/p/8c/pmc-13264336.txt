Dual-tunable liquid crystal-forming system enables sustained ropivacaine delivery for prolonged local analgesia
Authors: Chuansheng Fu, Donghong Chen, Sihan Yu, Jiao Zhang, Qi Xie, Bingzheng Shen, Conglian Yang, Li Kong, Zhiping Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Postoperative pain management remains a challenge in clinical practice. Traditional local anesthetics often require repeated administration due to their short duration of action, which increases the risk of systemic toxicity and nerve damage. Herein, we developed an injectable, dual-tunable, low-ethanol lyotropic liquid crystal-forming system (LCFS) for sustained local ropivacaine (Rop) release, achieving long-lasting analgesia. Upon injection, the precursor solution transformed into a gel in situ, forming a drug reservoir for sustained release of Rop. The system was optimized by adjusting the ratio of phosphatidylcholine to glyceryl monooleate and the content of corn oil, enabling dual modulation of drug release kinetics. The optimized Rop@LCFS formulation demonstrated sustained release for up to 7 days, with an initial burst followed by controlled release. Compared with commercial bupivacaine liposome injection, Rop@LCFS exhibited 8-fold prolongation of effective sensory blockade duration in the rat sciatic nerve block model, while maintaining over 12 h of sustained analgesia in the subcutaneous infiltration anesthesia model. The formulation also displayed favorable biodegradability and biocompatibility both in vitro and in vivo. The Rop@LCFS features easy preparation, sustained release, and biocompatibility, offering potential for clinical translation and long-acting local anesthetics development.

Introduction

Postoperative pain (POP) refers to discomfort or pain initiated by surgical trauma, with over 300 million patients worldwide undergo operations each year [1,2]. Among them, 75% of affected patients reported moderate to severe pain, yet fewer than half of cases receive effective analgesia [3,4]. Despite the use of opioids, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and local anesthetics, the management of POP remains suboptimal. The short duration of action of traditional local anesthetics limits their effectiveness in providing prolonged pain relief, typically lasting only 8 to 9 h [5]. Current formulations often require repeated administrations, leading to increased risks of systemic toxicity, nerve damage, and poor patient compliance [6]. Therefore, the development of a sustained-release delivery system for local anesthetics, which provides continuous and effective analgesia while minimizing side effects and operational complexity, is of significant clinical importance.

Currently, four long-acting formulations have been approved by the FDA for clinical use: Exparel® (liposomal bupivacaine), Xaracoll® (bupivacaine-impregnated collagen sponge), Posimir® (bupivacaine solution), and Zynrelef® (bupivacaine/meloxicam extended-release solution) [[7], [8], [9]]. However, these formulations still suffer from limitations such as insufficient analgesia duration, high costs, and safety concerns [10]. Numerous preclinical studies on sustained-release delivery systems for local anesthetics have been reported, including liposomes [11], microneedles [12], nanoparticles [13], microspheres [14], and hydrogels [15]. For instance, Zhang et al. [16] developed a pH-responsive microneedle patch in which the local anesthetic ropivacaine was encapsulated and released in response to the acidic environment at the wound site, providing analgesia for over 72 h. Despite these advancements, these systems still face challenges related to complex preparation, limited drug loading capacity, and inconsistent release profiles. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop more long-acting and safe local anesthetic delivery strategies through streamlined fabrication approaches.

The liquid crystal-forming system (LCFS), formed by the self-assembly of amphiphilic molecules (such as phospholipids or surfactants) in water, has emerged as a promising candidate for controlled drug release. LCFS can form highly ordered structures, such as lamellar, hexagonal, or cubic phases, and possesses high drug loading capacity and sustained-release properties. Our earlier studies confirmed the favorable sustained-release performance of the LCFS system [17,18]. However, the high ethanol content (EtOH, 20% w/w) in this formulation may cause undesirable irritation at the injection site. Moreover, the ability to tailor drug release profiles is constrained when using a single component. Hence, optimizing the formulation through ethanol content reduction and multiple components incorporation would be a favorable approach for drug delivery.

To address these issues, we developed a dual-tunable LCFS system, wherein the release profile can be controlled by adjusting two key parameters: the ratio of phosphatidylcholine (PC) to glyceryl monooleate (GMO) and the content of corn oil (CO) (Scheme 1). Ropivacaine (Rop) was chosen as a model local anesthetic for its lower cardiotoxicity profile than bupivacaine. This dual modulation enables fine-tuning of both the structural phase and the rheological properties, resulting in sustained and predictable drug release. Result and discussion

LCFS preparation and characterization

LCFS is composed of four pharmaceutical excipients: PC, GMO, CO, and ethanol. The amphiphilic materials PC and GMO, characterized by hydrophilic heads and hydrophobic tails in their molecular structures, serve as the primary matrix for liquid crystal formation (Fig. 1A). Ethanol is added as a solvent to dissolve the lipids and to inhibit bacterial growth during preparation and storage. Additionally, the addition of CO in this system serves multiple functions. First, CO acts as a partial solvent to disperse the lipid matrix, thereby reducing injection-site irritation by decreasing ethanol usage. Second, CO contributes to the formation of the liquid crystal structure and regulates drug release.Fig. 1Optimization and characterization of LCFS formulations. PC and GMO were dissolved in a small amount of ethanol and CO. After simple mixing and stirring, the LCFS precursor solution was successfully prepared, manifesting as a viscous, transparent yellow liquid. After the LCFS precursor solution was injected into the PBS solution, due to the water absorption of the prescription, the amphiphilic lipids self-assembled in the presence of excess water, eventually converting the LCFS precursor solution into LCFS gel (Fig. 1B). To visually elucidate the hydration-driven phase transition, the microscopic morphology of LCFS was imaged in real-time using CLSM after exposure to water containing dissolved FITC. As shown in Fig. 1C, the LCFS precursor solution exhibited a dense and smooth liquid layer on the slide before hydration. When the LCFS precursor solution came into contact with water, water at the interface rapidly diffused into the interior of the LCFS within 30 s, causing an immediate change in the internal structure of the precursor solution. With continued hydration for 300 s, increased water penetration facilitated the gelation of the LCFS. Additionally, to further verify whether the LCFS precursor solution undergoes an in situ phase transition upon exposure to physiological fluids, 100 μL of the precursor solution was injected into KM mice. The results showed that the LCFS precursor rapidly formed a gel within 30 min in vivo and remained in situ after 7 day (Fig. S1).

Optimization of prescriptions

A series of optimization experiments were conducted to screen LCFS formulations with optimal sustained-release capability and biocompatibility. The LCFS formulation was optimized by adjusting the PC-to-GMO mass ratio and the CO content. Firstly, precursor solutions of different LCFS formulations were prepared by simple one-pot mixing method, and their gelation properties were investigated. As shown in Fig. 1D, when the mass ratios of PC to GMO were 40:60, 50:50, and 60:40, with CO contents of 7% and 14%, all the six formulations were successfully transformed into gel states upon contact with water. At elevated CO concentration (28%), formulations with PC to GMO mass ratios of 40:60 and 50:50 successfully formed gels, while the 60:40 ratio formulation remained in a solution state. The result indicated that PC to GMO mass ratios ranging from 40:60 to 60:40 exhibited good in vitro gel-forming properties.

The above prescriptions were labeled as F1-F9. Next, their hemocompatibility were examined according to previously reported methods [18]. 0.9% NaCl solution and deionized water (H2O) served as a negative and positive control, respectively. As shown in Fig. 1E, the hemolysis rate of all nine prescriptions were less than 3%, indicating good hemocompatibility. This was further confirmed by direct observation of the colourless and transparent supernatant in the LCFS-treated RBC suspension.

The crystalline types of LCFS gel were characterized by polarizing microscope (PLM) to examine their macroscopic texture. PLM is among the most commonly employed techniques for characterizing lyotropic liquid crystals and enables the rapid distinction of isotropic and anisotropic crystals [19]. The lamellar phase (La) and the hexagonal phase (HII) are two common lyotropic liquid crystals with anisotropic properties. Under the PLM, the birefringence pattern of HII crystals is fan-shaped or striped [20], while La crystals exhibit a Maltese cross pattern [21]. In contrast, the cubic phase is isotropic, appearing as a dark gray field under PLM [22]. As shown in Fig. 1F, when the CO content was 7%, the PLM images of different formulations (F1-F3) exhibited a fan-shaped and striped texture, indicating the presence of hexagonal phase. When the CO content was 14%, the PLM images of F4 displayed speckled oil stains, and the irregular morphology suggested that polycrystalline forms might have been formed [23]. F5 and F6 exhibited a striped texture, indicating that they were also in the hexagonal phase. When the CO content was 28%, the PLM field of F7 and F8 appeared as a dark field, indicating that the two formulations were in a cubic phase and behaved as isotropic crystals. In contrast, the PLM images of F9 displayed a Maltese cross texture, indicating that the formulation was in a lamellar phase.

The lamellar phase consists of planar lipid layers separated by water, stacked in a one-dimensional direction, and capable of sliding over each other when a shear force is applied [24]. This further explains the observation that F9 behaves as a low-viscosity solution in tube inverting experiments. It has been reported that non-lamellar phases (i.e., hexagonal and cubic phases) lyotropic liquid play a crucial role in sustained-release capacity, and the hexagonal phase exhibits a stronger sustained-release ability than the cubic phase due to its more complex channel structure [18]. Therefore, F1-F6 were selected for subsequent rheological behavior testing.

Rheological behavior of LCFS precursors and gels

We then investigated the rheological properties of the above-mentioned LCFS precursors and gels (F1-F6). The viscosity test results indicated that as the phospholipid content in the formulation increased, the viscosity of the precursor solution gradually increased (Fig. 2A). The rheological results showed that the viscosity of the precursor solution decreased gradually with elevated shear rate (Fig. 2B), which indicated that they were pseudoplastic fluids with shear-thinning properties. Upon fully hydration and swelling, the precursor solution developed into LCFS gel with different viscoelastic properties. The viscoelasticity is quantified by the viscous modulus (G″) and elastic modulus (G′), which represent the liquid and solid properties, respectively. In frequency-sweep experiments, all LCFS gel formulations showed G’ > G” within the frequency range of 0.1–10 Hz, indicating predominant elastic properties (Fig. 2C). This was consistent with its macroscopic behavior as a semi-solid gel state. The strong elasticity is attributed to the long-range ordered nanostructures of liquid crystals, which restrict Rop diffusion within the LCFS, leading to sustained drug release [19].

An oscillatory shear study was conducted to demonstrate the self-healing property of the LCFS gels. When a 100% strain was applied, the structural integrity of the all LCFS gels was disrupted, and both G′ and G″ decreased significantly, with G’ < G″, indicating a solution state. However, when subjected to a low strain of 1% again, both G′ and G″ increased, with G’ > G”, indicating that LCFS transitioned from the solution state back to the gel state (Fig. 2D). During the alternating strain sweep, the recovered G′ values are generally slightly lower than the initial values, indicating that the network structure does not achieve 100% reconstruction. This is likely due to the irreversible rearrangement of some physical crosslinking points (e.g., hydrophobic interactions, chain entanglements) under intense shear, preventing complete restoration of the initial configuration. The result demonstrated that the LCFS gel exhibited good self-healing ability. The self-healing ability prolongs the in situ retention of the LCFS gel at surgical wound sites, functioning as a depot for sustained drug release [26].

These results indicated that the LCFS precursor solution based on PC and GMO was a pseudoplastic fluid with good injection properties. Upon contact with physiological fluids, precursor solutions could be gelled in situ (Fig. 2E). LCFS gels with different formulations exhibited varying viscoelasticity, all displaying predominant elastic properties. Additionally, the LCFS gels also displayed good self-healing ability. The viscoelastic differences and excellent in situ retention of LCFS gels provided a theoretical foundation and reference for studying their drug release behaviors.

Drug release behavior of LCFS

Rop was encapsulated within different LCFS formulations, and the in vitro release behavior was evaluated under physiological-like conditions. Upon contact with an aqueous environment, the LCFS precursor solution underwent quick solvent exchange, forming a hexagonal phase structure. This hexagonal phase consists of infinite rod-like water channels separated by a lipid bilayer, with the water channels connected to the external environment. Hydrophilic molecules dissolve in the water channels, hydrophobic molecules are encapsulated in the lipid bilayers, and amphiphilic molecules reside at the interface [19]. During LCFS gel formation, a portion of the drug is released rapidly via solvent exchange. Following complete hexagonal phase stabilization, the hydrophilic drug ropivacaine hydrochloride is released in a sustained and controlled manner through diffusion within aqueous channels and progressive gel erosion [27]. In vitro release study demonstrated that Rop was sustainably released from different LCFS gels over 7 day. The release profile exhibited a biphasic pattern, with an initial rapid release within the first 12 h, followed by prolonged sustained release (Fig. 3A). This biphasic release is potentially advantageous in clinical applications. The initial rapid release at the surgical site ensures rapid pain relief, while the subsequent sustained release meets long-term post-surgery analgesic needs. The results showed that increasing the CO content from 7% to 14% led to a minor increase in both the drug release rate and the 7-day cumulative release across all six formulations with different PC-to-GMO ratios. This suggested that the presence of CO could influence drug release, though the effect remained relatively limited. At a PC-to-GMO ratio of 40:60, both the 7% and 14% CO formulations exhibited a comparable 7-day cumulative release (79.03 ± 4.33% vs 83.66 ± 7.76%). However, as shown in the amplified figure in Fig. 3A, the 14% CO group showed a higher initial burst release within the first 12 h (68.49 ± 5.70%) compared to the 7% group (53.19 ± 2.66%). Moreover, the 14% group reached near-complete drug release by 84 h, while the 7% group maintained a sustained release profile throughout the 7-day period. To further confirm that the selected LCFS formulation exhibited a hexagonal phase structure, Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) measurement was performed. The SAXS results showed that the Bragg peak ratio of the gel was 1: √3: √7 (Fig. 3B), indicating the presence of a hexagonal phase [28]. To investigate the degradation behavior of the LCFS gel in vitro, it was placed in the PBS solution with varying ethanol contents, and the morphology and weight changes of the gel were recorded (Fig. 3C). The primary components of LCFS, PC and GMO, are soluble in ethanol. Therefore, adding ethanol to the PBS solution can partially simulate the in vivo degradation of the LCFS gel by certain lipases [29]. Morphological changes in LCFS gels over time could be observed in Fig. S2. As shown in Fig. 3D, the remaining relative mass of the LCFS gel exhibited an initial increase followed by gradual decline. The reason for this pattern was that after the LCFS precursor solution was injected into the degradation medium, it first absorbed water and swelled to form the LCFS gel, and then gradually degraded over time. Additionally, this degradation behavior depended on the concentration of ethanol in the culture medium. With an increase in ethanol concentration, the degradation rate of the LCFS gel gradually accelerated. At ethanol concentrations of 0% and 5%, the remaining mass of the LCFS gel after 28 d was 78.80 ± 7.84% and 70.99 ± 29.75%, respectively. Comparatively, when the ethanol content increased to 10% and 15%, the remaining mass was approximately 59.92 ± 21.14% and 39.47 ± 8.78%, respectively. The result demonstrated that the LCFS exhibited prolonged in situ retention after gel formation, supporting its potential for sustained in situ drug release.

To evaluate the cytotoxic potential of the LCFS gel, CCK8 assay were conducted on L929 cells. As shown in Fig. 3E, after incubation with various concentrations of LCFS extracted liquid for 24 h, the cell viability of L929 cells in all groups remained above 90%. This result confirmed that the LCFS gel exhibited good cytocompatibility. Furthermore, Live/dead staining results were shown in Fig. 3F. The cells in all groups were stained green during metabolism, indicating live cells, and exhibited normal cell morphology. No significant differences were observed among the groups, which was consistent with the cytotoxicity results from the CCK8 assay.

The degradability and cytocompatibility of LCFS

To further validate the in vivo release behavior of the LCFS gel, indocyanine green (ICG), a water-soluble fluorescent chromogen, was used as a substitute for Rop and incorporated into the LCFS (ICG@LCFS). The release of ICG was dynamically monitored by changes in its fluorescence signal. The real-time local retention of ICG in rats was monitored using the IVIS Lumina XR system (Fig. 4A). After injection around the sciatic nerve of rat, the free ICG group demonstrated rapid signal diminution with near-complete clearance within 24 h. In contrast, the ICG@LCFS group exhibited significant sustained release characteristics, and the fluorescence signal of ICG remained detectable even after 7 day. The quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensities was shown in Fig. 4B. The fluorescence intensity of the ICG@LCFS group was stronger than that of free ICG. The degradation behavior in vivo was further investigated (Fig. 4C). The results indicated that, after 1 d of subcutaneous injection of the LCFS precursor solution into KM mice, a yellow translucent gel formed in situ (Fig. 4D). The LCFS gel remained morphologically intact within the subcutaneous tissue of the mice for one week after administration. On day 14 and 28, the gel in mice gradually transitioned from its initial yellow semi-solid state to a white viscous liquid state. The remaining undegraded gel was encapsulated within this liquid phase and appeared light yellow upon exposure. This gradual degradation behavior indicated that the LCFS gel may follow a surface erosion mechanism in vivo, favoring sustained drug release kinetics. The degradation primarily results from the main components of LCFS, PC and GMO, which are degraded into small molecules with good biocompatibility, such as free fatty acids and glycerol, under the action of lipase in vivo [29,30].

Local inflammation at the injection site is a common side effect associated with in situ gel formulations. H&E staining of skin sections (Fig. 4E) revealed that mild inflammatory infiltration in the subcutaneous tissue on day 1. Subsequent examination on day 7 revealed slightly augmentation of inflammatory cells, including lymphocytes and granulocytes. By day 28, their numbers had declined, suggesting that the LCFS gel induced a mild and transient inflammatory response that gradually subsided over time. These results indicated that the LCFS gel could be degraded both in vivo and in vitro. It also exhibited good biocompatibility, making it a safe and promising delivery vehicle for local anesthetics.

Formulations with good storage stability are more conducive to clinical applications. The short-term storage stability of Rop@LCFS was evaluated. The result showed that, after one month of storage, no significant changes were observed in the appearance of Rop@LCFS. Additionally, the relative content of Rop in Rop@LCFS remained stable, indicating that this formulation exhibits good short-term storage stability (Fig. 4F and G).

Anesthetic effects of LCFS in sciatic nerve blockade model

In this study, we employed a rat sciatic nerve block model to verify anesthetic effect of Rop@LCFS (Fig. 5A). 0.9% NaCl solution (Saline) and commercial bupivacaine liposome injection (CBL) served as a negative and positive control, respectively. Firstly, to optimize the Rop dosage, the analgesic duration and neurotoxicity profiles of various formulations at different doses were evaluated in rats, including high dose (-H), medium dose (-M) and low dose (-L). A hot plate test was used to assess sensory blockade in each group. The formulations were injected into the right hind limb of rats surrounding the sciatic nerve via a 23G syringe. Within 1 h post-injection, the free Rop-H group induced systemic toxicity in rats due to high-dose administration of Rop, with signs of limb twitching, respiratory depression, and eventual death (Supplementary Video 1). These findings were consistent with the results in a previous study [31], free Rop concentration above the threshold can cause local or even systemic toxicity. In contrast, the Rop@LCFS-H group, despite containing equivalent Rop payload, showed normal nerve blockade without observable abnormal symptoms. This differential outcome indicated that the initial burst release of Rop from Rop@LCFS-H was sufficient to achieve effective nerve blockade. Subsequently, the entire course of sensory blockade was systematically monitored (Supplementary Video 2). All treatment groups, except for the Saline and Blank LCFS groups, exhibited the expected sensory blockade within the 1 h after injection. The PWL values of rats in these treatment groups gradually decreased over time (Fig. 5B). Effective sensory blockade was defined as a PWL exceeding 10 s. As shown in Fig. 5C, rats in the Saline and Blank LCFS groups did not exhibit any sensory blockade. The duration of effective sensory blockade in the Free Rop-L (2.00 ± 1.10 h) and Free Rop-M (2.50 ± 1.23 h) groups was slightly shorter than that in the CBL group (2.83 ± 1.84 h), with no statistically significant difference. Compared to the Free Rop-L group, the Rop@LCFS-L (5.00 ± 1.67 h) group exhibited a moderately prolonged blockade duration, although this increase was also not statistically significant. This trend may be attributed to the extended release of Rop, despite an insufficient initial loading [32]. Importantly, the Rop@LCFS-M (24.33 ± 8.43 h) and Rop@LCFS-H (24.00 ± 6.69 h) groups exhibited significantly longer sensory blockade durations compared to Rop@LCFS-L. However, no statistically significant difference was observed between them. This phenomenon may be related to the dose-response relationship of local anesthetics [33]. Within a certain dosage range, an increase in drug concentration enhances the anesthetic effect. However, once a threshold is reached, the effect tends to plateau, and further dose escalation does not significantly prolong the duration of anesthesia. Overall, the Rop@LCFS formulations exhibited longer durations of sensory blockade compared to the Free Rop groups, demonstrating the sustained-release capability of the LCFS system.

The motor blockade in each group was also assessed (Fig. 5D). At predetermined time points, the tails of rat were raised, and the foot status on the administered side was observed to evaluate motor function using a 4-point scale (Supplementary Video 3). Consistent with the sensory blockade results, the Saline and Blank LCFS groups did not exhibit any motor blockade (Fig. 5E). Effective motor blockade time was defined as the duration during which the motor blockade score was reduced to 2 points. As shown in Fig. 5F, the effective motor blockade duration was slightly longer in the Free Rop-L (4.33 ± 0.82 h) and Free Rop-M (4.00 ± 1.27 h) groups compared to the CBL (3.67 ± 1.51 h) group, with no statistically significant difference. The effective motor blockade duration was significantly longer in the Rop@LCFS-M (17.33 ± 8.91 h) and Rop@LCFS-H (21.67 ± 8.80 h) groups compared to the Rop@LCFS-L group (5.33 ± 1.63 h). This finding was also consistent with the sensory blockade results. These results demonstrated that both the Rop@LCFS-M and Rop@LCFS-H groups exhibited prolonged nerve blockade, with their effective sensory blockade duration being slightly longer than their effective motor blockade duration. This was largely attributed to the pharmacological properties of Rop, which enabled the separation of sensory and motor blockade [34]. This characteristic may provide potential clinical advantages, as it allows patients to regain movement without pain during the early postoperative period. In fact, both mobility and effective pain relief are essential for postoperative recovery.

Histological analysis of local tissues

To systematically assess the degradation kinetics of each formulation in rats and their impact on sciatic nerve and surrounding muscle tissues, we performed macroscopic examinations of the injection sites through dissection on the day7 and day21 after administration. Macroscopic examination of the local anatomical structure revealed that (Fig. 6A) the sciatic nerves (indicated by blue arrows) surrounding the injection sites retained their morphological integrity in all treatment groups. Given the limited sensitivity of H&E staining in detecting neurodegenerative changes, TBO staining was employed in this study to specifically observe myelin structures [35]. On the 7th and 21st day after administration, sciatic nerve tissue samples from rats were collected, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for cross-sectional analysis. As shown in Fig. 6C, TBO staining results revealed that nerve fibers in all groups were arranged in regular bundles and encased by circular or oval myelin sheaths. Importantly, no signs of nerve damage were observed in any treatment group or in untreated rats at any time point, and no significant changes were noted in the density of axonal elements or the structure of myelin. These results suggested that LCFS gel administration around the sciatic nerve did not cause structural damage to nerve fibers.

Anesthetic effects of LCFS in subcutaneous infiltration anesthesia model

Having verified the good nerve blockade effect of Rop@LCFS-M in the sciatic nerve block model, we subsequently explored the anesthetic effect of this formulation in a rat subcutaneous infiltration anesthesia model (Fig. 7A). Quantitative analysis revealed that all treatment groups except for the saline group exhibited the effective pain inhibition within the initial 1 h post-administration. Over time, the pain inhibition rate gradually decreased in these drug-treated groups (Fig. 7B). Effective infiltration blockade was defined as a pain inhibition rate exceeding 50%. As shown in Fig. 7C, the effective infiltration blockade durations for CBL, Free Rop-M, and Rop@LCFS-M were 3.0 ± 2.37 h, 4.67 ± 1.03 h, and 12.33 ± 5.85 h, respectively. Notably, the infiltration blockade duration in the Rop@LCFS-M group was 4.11 times and 2.64 times longer than that in the CBL and Free Rop-M groups, respectively. These experimental findings validated the long-lasting anesthetic effects of Rop@LCFS-M in both the rat sciatic nerve blockade and subcutaneous infiltration anesthesia models. Mechanistically, the prolonged analgesic effect is attributed to the LCFS gel's ability to provide in situ sustained release of Rop, thereby maintaining extended nerve blockade. It is noteworthy that the sensory blockade duration of CBL in this study was slightly shorter than that of the free ropivacaine group. This observation can be primarily attributed to the differences in dose and mechanism of action. Based on clinical equivalent dose conversion, the CBL group received 300 μL (containing 4 mg of bupivacaine), with its effect relying on sustained release from the formulation. In contrast, the free ropivacaine group received 400 μL (containing 10 mg of ropivacaine), and the higher dose itself contributed to a certain degree of prolonged effect. In addition, factors such as the local clearance rate of liposomal formulations in rat tissues, injection site differences, and species specificity may also affect the actual blockade effect of CBL. Nevertheless, the Rop@LCFS-M formulation developed in this study achieved a significantly longer duration of analgesia than both of the aforementioned groups, demonstrating the effectiveness of its sustained-release strategy.

Despite the mismatch between vehicle retention and effect duration, the optimized Rop@LCFS already achieves an approximately 8-fold and 4-fold prolongation of sensory blockade compared with Exparel® in sciatic nerve and subcutaneous infiltration models, respectively, adequately covering the critical 24-72 h postoperative analgesic window. Such dissociation between carrier residence and pharmacological action is not uncommon among long-acting local anesthetic depots. As the primary function of the carrier is to modulate release kinetics and ensure complete bioresorption rather than to extend drug action indefinitely.

Conclusion

In clinical practice, the therapeutic efficacy of local anesthetics for POP management is often limited by their short duration of analgesia. Encapsulating them in sustained-release delivery systems enables prolonged drug release, thereby enhancing the duration of analgesic effects after a single injection. Based on this, this study developed a sustained-release delivery system using an injectable LCFS for ropivacaine, providing extended postoperative pain relief. By adjusting the formulation parameters, we optimized the drug release profile to achieve prolonged sensory blockade while maintaining good biocompatibility. Benefiting from its in situ gelation capability, LCFS could be directly injected around the sciatic nerve or into subcutaneous tissues, providing prolonged local anesthesia in both rat sciatic nerve block and subcutaneous infiltration anesthesia models. Importantly, since the formulation was composed entirely of pharmaceutical excipients and partially replaced the irritant solvent ethanol with CO (achieving a substantially lower ethanol content than earlier formulations without CO), it exhibited good biocompatibility. Furthermore, the phospholipid-based gel matrix gradually degraded in vivo, enhancing biocompatibility and safety. In summary, this study innovatively employed LCFS as the delivery carrier for Rop, achieving stable and sustained drug release in vivo through its in situ gelation property, which significantly prolonged the duration of nerve block. Additionally, CO, a biocompatible pharmaceutical excipient that serves as a structural modulator capable of inserting into the lipid bilayer, was introduced into the LCFS system. It participates in liquid crystalline structure formation while reducing the ethanol content, thereby minimizing injection pain and tissue irritation and improving formulation safety. Moreover, the drug release behavior could be dual-modulated by adjusting the PC-to-GMO ratio and the CO content in the formulation. Compared to existing long-acting local anesthetic formulations, Rop@LCFS offered an extended anesthetic duration and better biocompatibility, making it a promising candidate for clinical translation in POP management.

Materials and methods

Materials

Ropivacaine hydrochloride and corn oil were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai China). Phosphatidyl choline (PC, LIPOID S-100) was obtained from Xi'an Ruixi Biological Technology Co. Ltd. (Xi'an China). Glycerol monooleate (GMO) was purchased from Jiaxing Sicheng Chemical Co. Ltd. (Zhejiang, China). Commercial bupivacaine liposome injection (CBL; Hengrui Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd.) was kindly provided by the Department of Anesthesiology, Union Hospital, Wuhan, China.

Cell culture

Mouse fibroblast cell line (L929) was obtained from Chinese Academy of Science and Technology Cell Bank. L929 were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin (100 IU/mL) and streptomycin (100 μg/mL). Cells were cultured in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Animals

Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (male, 8 weeks old) were obtained from the Hubei Provincial Center for Disease Prevention and Control (Wuhan, China). All SPF animals were housed in the Animal Facility Center at Huazhong University of Science and Technology (HUST) following the guidelines of the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of HUST. All animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Tongji Medical College, HUST, under IACUC number [2024] (4330).

Preparation of LCFS precursor solutions

PC and GMO, in mass ratios ranging from 3:7 to 7:3, were weighed and dissolved in a mixture of a definite amount of ethanol (7%) and CO with different mass fractions (7%, 14%, 28%). The above mixture was stirred on a rolling mixer at room temperature for 3 h until the lipid materials were completely dissolved. Air bubbles were subsequently removed by ultrasonication, forming a translucent yellow liquid identified as the LCFS precursor solution.

To prepare ropivacaine-loaded formulations (Rop@LCFS), a measured amount of ropivacaine hydrochloride powder was added to the precursor solution, stirred for 0.5 h, and then subjected to ultrasonication.

In situ solution-gel transition of precursor solution

The solution-gel transition behavior of precursor solution was investigated using the tube inverting method [36]. A total of 400 μL of precursor solution was injected into a test tube pre-filled with 200 μL of PBS solution. After 12 h of equilibration, the tube was inverted. The solution-gel transition behavior was confirmed by observing whether the sample flowed. Confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) was employed to further observe the microscopic solution-gel transition behavior of the precursor solution [37]. Briefly, one drop of precursor solution was placed on a glass slide, followed by surrounding it with the FITC probe aqueous solution. Upon contact with water, the microscopic morphology of the precursor solution was observed in real time by CLSM and captured at preset time points of 0 s, 30 s, and 300 s. To further observe the solution-gel transition of the precursor solution upon injection, the precursor solution was drawn into a 1 mL syringe and then injected into PBS solution to examine the gel morphology.

Polarized light microscopy characterization

The phase structure of the LCFS gel was analyzed using polarized light microscopy (PLM; Nikon, Japan). 10 μL of precursor solution was evenly spread onto a clean glass slide. An appropriate amount of PBS solution was then added to fully cover the precursor solution and left for 10 min, allowing the gradual formation of a thin gel layer. After this, excess water was gently removed using filter paper, and a coverslip was carefully placed. Finally, the birefringence textures of the LCFS gel were observed by PLM at 25 °C.

Hemocompatibility assay

A hemolysis assay was performed to evaluate the hemolytic capacity of different LCFS formulations. Fresh blood was collected from the abdominal aorta of healthy rats. Red blood cells (RBCs) were isolated by centrifuging the collected blood at 2000 rpm for 10 min, which were then washed five times with PBS. The purified RBCs were subsequently diluted with PBS to prepare a 5% (v/v) RBC stock suspension.

1 mL of 0.9% NaCl solution was used as the negative control group, while 1 mL of deionized water (H2O) was used as the positive control group. In the experimental group, 50 μL of precursor solutions with varying formulations were added to 1 mL of 0.9% NaCl solution. Then, 300 μL of the diluted RBC stock suspension was introduced into each group. The mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h, followed by centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 10 min. The hemolytic properties of various LCFS formulations were recorded using a digital camera. The supernatant was aspirated carefully, and its absorbance was determined at 540 nm with a microplate reader (Multiskan MK3, Thermo, MA, USA). The hemolysis percentage was calculated using the formula below:Hemolysis ratio (%) = [(Asample - ANaCl)/(AH2O-ANaCl)] × 100%

Rheological behavior of precursor solutions and LCFS gels

The rheological study was employed to thoroughly investigate the viscosity, viscoelasticity and self-healing property of LCFS precursor solutions and gels by a Kinexus Rotational Rheometer (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK).

The viscosity of precursor solutions was determined using a Kinexus Rotational Rheometer with a 15 mm diameter parallel plate at 25 °C, over a shear rate range of 0.1 to 100 s−1. To study the viscoelasticity of LCFS gels, 800 μL of precursor solution was injected slowly into PBS and allowed to equilibrate for 12 h to form the gel. The gel was transferred to the center of the parallel plate with a 1 mm sample gap and equilibrated at 25 °C for 5 min prior to each measurement. Frequency-sweep measurements were performed to record the elastic (G’) and viscous (G”) moduli of LCFS gels over an angular frequency range of 0.1 to 10 Hz at a constant strain of 0.5%. The self-healing properties of LCFS gels were evaluated by applying a sweep frequency of 1 Hz, alternating strain ranges from 1% to 100%, with 120 s intervals between changes. Alternating strains were applied to LCFS gels, and strain-induced damage and recovery were recorded.

In vitro drug release study

To evaluate the drug release behavior of Rop@LCFS, 100 μL of Rop@LCFS precursor solution with varying LCFS formulations was injected into 8 mL of PBS release medium at pH 7.4. The samples were placed in a horizontal shaker at 37 °C and 100 rpm for drug release experiments. The release medium was then removed completely at preset time points (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48, 84, 120, and 168 h) and replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS. For analysis, 2 mL of release medium was collected at each time point and lyophilized. Rop concentration was determined using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (UltiMate 3000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile-phosphate buffer (50:50), with the column temperature set to 30 °C, and UV detection was performed at 240 nm.

In vivo drug release study

To further verify the sustained-release capability of the LCFS gel with the optimized formulation, Rop was replaced by ICG to evaluate in vivo drug release from LCFS gel. ICG@LCFS was prepared by adding a specific amount of ICG solution to the precursor solution. Healthy SD rats were randomly divided into two groups, each consisting of three rats. One group received free ICG, while the other group was administered with ICG@LCFS. Specifically, all rats were shaved, anesthetized with oxygen containing 2% isoflurane, and positioned laterally to ensure that the femur was perpendicular to the trunk. A 23-gauge needle attached to a syringe was inserted into the posteromedial aspect of the greater trochanter and advanced in an anteromedial direction, parallel to the spine. Upon contacting the surface of the sciatic bone, the needle was retracted by 1 mm, and 400 μL of the formulation was injected near the sciatic nerve [38].

At time points of 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 96, and 168 h, in vivo images were captured under both white light and fluorescence using the IVIS Lumina XR system (Pearl Trilogy, LI-COR, USA). The in vivo release behavior of ICG in rats was evaluated by analyzing fluorescence signal intensity, with quantitative analysis performed using the IVIS onboard software.

LCFS degradation behavior in vitro

The degradation medium was prepared by mixing ethanol with PBS (pH 7.4) at different ratios. 100 μL of the LCFS precursor solution was carefully injected into the degradation medium. Subsequently, all samples were placed in a horizontal shaker at 37 °C and shaken at 100 rpm. At the predetermined time intervals, the remaining LCFS gel was weighed and photographed.

In vitro cytocompatibility of the LCFS

The cytotoxicity of LCFS toward L929 cells was evaluated via the CCK-8 assay. Following a previously reported method [30], a 100% LCFS extracted fluid was prepared by adding 10 mg of LCFS precursor solution to 1 mL of DMEM, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. The resulting extracted liquid was serially diluted with blank DMEM to achieve concentrations of 50%, 25%, and 10%. L929 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1×104 cells per well and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, 100 μL of extracted liquid at varying concentrations was added, followed by incubation for another 24 h. The viability of L929 cells was assessed via the CCK-8 assay.

Next, a live/dead cell staining kit (Beyotime, China) was further used to visually assess the effect of LCFS on L929 cell viability. Briefly, L929 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The medium was replaced with LCFS extracted liquid at varying concentrations, followed by incubation for another 24 h. Finally, the cells were stained with a mixed dye solution of Calcein-AM and PI, and then imaged by CLSM.

LCFS degradation behavior in vivo

To investigate the in vivo degradation behavior of LCFS gel, 100 μL of the LCFS precursor solution was subcutaneously injected into each healthy KM mouse. At predetermined time points, three mice were randomly selected for dissection to observe morphological changes of LCFS gel in the mice and to capture photographs. Following dissection, the skin at the injection site was collected and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution. The samples were then paraffin-embedded and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The stained slides were examined under a light microscope to assess the irritation of the formulation on the skin.

Sciatic nerve block model

Based on the clinically used dose and body surface area conversion, the single dose of CBL for adults (266 mg/70 kg) was converted to an equivalent rat dose of approximately 1.5-2.0 mg/100 g body weight. Accordingly, for rats weighing 250-300 g, the positive control group received CBL at a dose of 4 mg per rat (equivalent to 1.3-1.6 mg/100 g).

Healthy SD rats (n = 6 per group) were randomly assigned to nine experimental groups: (1) Saline (0.9% saline), (2) Blank LCFS, (3) CBL (commercial bupivacaine liposome injection; each rat received 4 mg bupivacaine), (4) Free Rop-L (each rat received 5 mg Rop), (5) Free Rop-M (10 mg Rop), (6) Free Rop-H (20 mg Rop), (7) Rop@LCFS-L (5 mg Rop), (8) Rop@LCFS-M (10 mg Rop), and (9) Rop@LCFS-H (20 mg Rop). 400 μL of formulation was administered around the sciatic nerve in all groups, except for the CBL group, which received a 300 μL of CBL solution [39]. The method for drug administration around the sciatic nerve is detailed in Section 2.9. Sensory and motor functions were assessed at predetermined time points using the hot plate test and a scoring scale.

A custom-made hot plate preheated to 56 °C was used to evaluate thermal nociception during the sciatic nerve block [40]. Specifically, the hind paw of each rat was placed on the preheated hot plate, and the time until the rat lifted its paw was recorded as paw withdrawal latency (PWL). The baseline PWL of the rats was measured 3 d before administration, with the value set at 5 s. Rats with baseline value higher or lower than 5 s were excluded. The maximum PWL was capped at 16 s to ensure animal safety.

Motor blockade was assessed using a 4-point scale [38], with the scoring system defined as follows: normal appearance (1 point); intact dorsiflexion of the foot with impaired splaying of the toes when the animal was lifted by the tail (2 points); plantarflexion of the foot with no ability to splay the toes (3 points); and complete loss of dorsiflexion and plantarflexion, along with an impairment in gait (4 points).

Subcutaneous infiltration anesthesia model

SD rats were randomly divided into four groups, with 6 rats in each group (n = 6): (1) Saline, (2) Free Rop-M, (3) CBL and (4) Rop@LCFS-M. Following previously reported method [40], the hair in a 6 cm × 8 cm area along the midline of the rat's back was shaved. A pharmacodynamic detection area with a 2 cm radius was marked using a non-toxic pen, centered on the injection site. Except for the CBL group, which received a 300 μL subcutaneous injection, all other groups received 400 μL injection of formulations.

At the pre-determined time points, a sterile injection needle was used to operate within the test area, puncturing the skin once every 3 - 5 s for a total of 6 times. The number of skin contraction responses in this area was negatively correlated with the local anesthetic effect. The number of times the rats showed no response was recorded, and the pain inhibition percentage was calculated based on the formula:(No contraction response times / 6) × 100%

Biosafety evaluation

SD rats were euthanized on d 7 and 21 after administration. The injection site was dissected and photographed to observe gel degradation in the rats. Sciatic nerves and surrounding muscle tissues were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. These tissues were then subjected to H&E staining to evaluate the tissue compatibility of different formulations.

To further assess the neurotoxicity of the formulation, a Toluidine Blue (TBO) staining assay was performed. Sciatic nerves surrounding the gel were excised and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde-PBS solution for 24 h, then further fixed overnight in 1% (w/v) osmium tetroxide. The nerve tissues were dehydrated sequentially using ethanol solutions of varying concentrations. The dehydrated tissues were embedded in epoxy resin and stained with TBO. Finally, the stained slides were examined under a light microscope.

Storage stability

We prepared Rop@LCFS-M and evaluated its storage stability at low temperature [41]. The formulation was placed in small vials and stored in the dark at 4 °C. On d 0, 3, 7, 14, 21, and 30, three vials were randomly selected. The appearance of Rop@LCFS-M was observed, and 50 μL of the sample was taken for drug content determination by HPLC.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and graphical representations were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5. Quantitative data from at least three independent experiments are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD). For comparative analysis of multiple experimental groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's post hoc test was conducted using the software's analytical modules. The following thresholds for statistical significance were applied: ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, ns (not significant).
