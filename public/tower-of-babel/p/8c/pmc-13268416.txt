Anxiety, depression, and quality of life in metastatic cancer patients receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors: A 2-center cross-sectional study
Authors: Betul Aktepe, Tugce Ulasli, Pinar Ezgi Dama, Ilkay Tugba Unek, Aziz Karaoglu, Rezan Berkay Izgor, Mehmet Hamid Boztas, Suayib Yalcin, Oktay Halit Aktepe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed cancer treatment, but their psychological impact remains underexplored. This study evaluated the relationship between emotional distress and quality of life (QoL) among patients undergoing ICI therapy. In this 2-center cross-sectional study, 200 adults with metastatic solid tumors who had received ≥3 ICI cycles were assessed using the Turkish version of the Short Form-36 (SF-36) and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS). Patients were categorized into high and low QoL groups according to the median global SF-36 score, with a cutoff value of 60.5. Correlation analyses and multivariate logistic regression were used to identify factors associated with poor QoL. The median age was 61 years; 73% were male, and 60.5% had Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group 1 to 2. Fifty-two percent of patients had low QoL. HADS-anxiety subscale (HADS-A) and HADS-depression subscale (HADS-D) scores were higher in the low QoL group (11 vs 8; 8 vs 6; both P < .001). Both subscales showed significant negative correlations with all SF-36 domains. In multivariate analysis, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group ≥ 1 (odds ratio [OR]: 5.92, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.61–13.40, P < .001), age ≥ 60 (OR: 2.98, 95% CI: 1.35–6.57, P = .007), ≥2 metastatic sites (OR: 3.62, 95% CI: 1.64–7.98, P = .001), second-line or later therapy (OR: 3.11, 95% CI: 1.23–7.82, P = .016), HADS-A (OR: 3.74, 95% CI: 1.70–8.18, P < .001), and HADS-D (OR: 5.03, 95% CI: 2.32–11.34, P < .001) independently predicted poor QoL. Anxiety and depression are common among ICI-treated patients and independently predict poorer QoL, regardless of clinical factors. Routine psychosocial assessment and early supportive interventions should be integrated into comprehensive immunotherapy care.

1. Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have markedly changed cancer therapy by achieving durable responses and improving long-term survival outcomes across diverse tumor types. Agents targeting programmed cell death protein 1, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), or cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4) restore immune surveillance and are now cornerstone treatments for various tumors.[1,2] Despite their remarkable efficacy, they are associated with immune-related adverse events (irAEs) and distinct symptom profiles that may considerably impair patients’ physical health and psychosocial well-being.[3]

Cancer-related distress, described as the “sixth vital sign” in oncology, underscores the importance of emotional well-being in comprehensive cancer care.[4] However, psychological screening remains insufficiently integrated into routine immunotherapy care. Moreover, prior research addressing quality of life (QoL) and emotional well-being has mainly investigated cancer patients treated with chemotherapy or targeted therapies,[5,6] whereas the psychosocial consequences of ICIs have not been comprehensively characterized. ICIs may also indirectly affect psychological health via inflammatory, endocrine, and immune pathways.[7] Conversely, improved tumor control and restored immune homeostasis might alleviate cancer-related psychological distress, suggesting a bidirectional relationship between immunotherapy and emotional outcomes.[8]

Validated patient-reported outcome tools, including the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) and the Turkish version of the Short Form-36 Health Survey (SF-36), provide multidimensional assessments of emotional and functional domains in cancer populations.[9,10] However, few studies have specifically evaluated QoL, depression, and anxiety in patients undergoing long-term ICI therapy.[11,12] Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the relationship between QoL, depression, and anxiety among patients who had received at least 3 cycles of ICI.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Patients’ characteristics and study design

This 2-center, cross-sectional study was conducted at Dokuz Eylül University Faculty of Medicine, Department of Medical Oncology (Izmir, Turkey), and Bolu Abant İzzet Baysal University Training and Research Hospital, Department of Medical Oncology (Bolu, Turkey). Psychometric assessments using the SF-36 and HADS were performed at a single time point, whereas demographic and clinical variables were retrospectively extracted from medical records. The study included adult patients with histologically confirmed metastatic solid tumors who had received at least 3 cycles of ICI therapy between October 2022 and October 2025. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed because of the exploratory, real-world design of the study. The sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients treated at the 2 participating centers during the prespecified study period who met the inclusion criteria and provided complete SF-36 and HADS data. Demographic and clinical parameters – age, sex, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, primary tumor site, number of metastatic sites, comorbidities, treatment line, and number of ICI cycles – were extracted from the medical records of both centers. Patients were required to have completed a minimum of 3 treatment cycles and to have sufficient cognitive ability to complete self-administered questionnaires. Patients with incomplete questionnaire data were excluded from the final analysis, and no prorating or imputation was performed for missing SF-36 or HADS items. Exclusion criteria were active central nervous system metastases, concurrent psychotropic medication use, and incomplete clinical or questionnaire data.

2.2. Assessment of QoL

QoL was assessed using the Turkish version of the SF-36, a validated multidimensional instrument widely applied in oncology research.[10] The questionnaire includes 8 functional domains: physical functioning, role-physical, bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, role-emotional, and mental health. Each subscale was linearly transformed to a 0 to 100 scale, with higher scores indicating better perceived QoL. A modified global QoL score was calculated as the mean of all 8 SF-36 subscales to represent the patient’s overall QoL, as applied in previous studies.[13]

2.3. Assessment of HADS-anxiety (HADS-A) subscale

Anxiety levels were measured using the HADS-A, which consists of 7 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0–3). Total scores range from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating greater anxiety severity.[9] A threshold of ≥10 was used to define clinically relevant anxiety based on the validated Turkish version of the HADS.[14]

2.4. Assessment of HADS-depression (HADS-D) subscale

Depressive symptoms were evaluated using the HADS-D, which also comprises 7 items rated from 0 to 3, yielding a total score between 0 and 21.[9] A score of ≥7 was considered indicative of clinically relevant depressive symptoms, according to the validated Turkish version of the instrument.[14]

2.5. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons between low and high QoL categories were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous data and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical data, as appropriate. Correlations between QoL domains and emotional distress (ED) scores (HADS-A and HADS-D) were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ). Because correlations were examined between 2 HADS subscales and 9 QoL measures, a total of 18 correlation tests were performed. These analyses were interpreted as exploratory, with attention to the consistency, direction, and magnitude of associations rather than isolated P-values alone. To determine independent predictors of poor QoL (≤60.5), univariate logistic regression analyses were first performed for selected clinical and psychological variables. Those with P ≤ .20 in univariate analyses were subsequently entered into a multivariate logistic regression model to avoid premature exclusion of potential confounders. Multicollinearity among variables included in the final multivariate logistic regression model was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF) values. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk), with 2-sided testing and statistical significance set at P < .05.

3. Results

3.1. Patient characteristics

A total of 200 patients receiving ICIs were included in the analysis. Baseline demographic and clinical features of all patients and of subgroups categorized by QoL are shown in Table 1. The median age was 61 years, and 73% were male. Most patients had an ECOG performance status of 1 to 2 (60.5%). Regarding primary tumor type, non-small cell lung cancer constituted the largest group (58.5%), followed by renal cell carcinoma (14%), melanoma (9.5%), and others (18%). Metastatic involvement was most frequently observed in the bone (43.0%), followed by the lung (26.0%) and liver (22.5%), while adrenal metastasis was present in 13.5% of patients. Approximately 53.5% of patients had 2 or more metastatic sites. Overall, anti-programmed cell death protein 1 therapies accounted for 84.0% of treatments, anti-PD-L1 for 13.0%, and anti-CTLA-4–based regimens for 3%. Regarding ICI type, nivolumab was the most frequently administered agent (n = 151, 75.5%), followed by pembrolizumab (n = 17, 8.5%), atezolizumab (n = 16, 8.0%), anti-CTLA-4–based combination therapy (nivolumab plus ipilimumab; n = 6, 3.0%), avelumab (n = 5, 2.5%), and durvalumab (n = 5, 2.5%).

Patients were categorized into 2 subgroups according to the median global QoL score, with a cutoff value of 60.5 as the low QoL group (≤median, n = 104, 52%) and the high QoL group (>median, n = 96, 48%). At the time of psychometric assessments, patients had completed a median of 6 (IQR 4–8) ICI cycles. Compared with the high QoL group, patients with low QoL were more frequently aged ≥ 60 years (56.7% vs 31.2%, P < .001), female (33.7% vs 19.8%, P = .027), and had an ECOG performance status ≥1 (76.9% vs 42.7%, P < .001). A higher proportion of patients in the low QoL group had ≥2 metastatic sites (68.3% vs 37.5%, P < .001) and were receiving second-line or later ICI therapy (79.8% vs 62.5%, P = .007). No significant differences were observed between the groups with respect to primary tumor type or comorbidities. However, liver metastasis was more frequent in the high QoL group than in the low QoL group (29.2% vs 16.3%, P = .030). In the overall cohort, clinically relevant anxiety symptoms, defined as HADS-A ≥10, were observed in 85 patients (42.5%), whereas clinically relevant depressive symptoms, defined as HADS-D ≥7, were observed in 98 patients (49.0%). The median HADS-A and HADS-D scores were also significantly higher among patients with low QoL (11 [IQR 8–13] vs 8 [IQR 6–9], P < .001; and 8 [IQR 6–11] vs 6 [IQR 4–7], P < .001, respectively), indicating a close association between ED and impaired QoL.

3.2. Emotional correlates and clinical predictors of QoL

As shown in Table 2, Spearman’s correlation analysis demonstrated a significant inverse relationship between ED and all domains of QoL. Both HADS-A and HADS-D scores showed negative correlations with all SF-36 domains (P < .001), with numerically stronger associations HADS-A in most domains. The highest correlation was found between HADS-A and bodily pain (r = −0.55, P < .001), followed by social functioning (r = −0.45, P < .001) and mental health (r = −0.44, P < .001). Depression scores (HADS-D) also correlated negatively with these domains, although with slightly lower coefficients. Global QoL exhibited a moderate negative correlation with both anxiety (r = −0.42, P < .001) and depression (r = −0.26, P < .001).

As an exploratory sensitivity analysis, the final multivariate model was further adjusted for major tumor type (NSCLC vs non-NSCLC) and ICI regimen class (anti-PD-L1 vs other regimens), while retaining the original clinical and psychometric covariates. In this model, HADS-A (OR: 4.04, 95% CI: 1.81–9.02, P < .001) and HADS-D (OR: 5.24, 95% CI: 2.28–12.01, P < .001) remained independently associated with poor QoL, supporting the robustness of the main findings. Major tumor type was not significantly associated with poor QoL (NSCLC vs non-NSCLC: OR: 1.52, 95% CI: 0.61–3.81, P = .373), whereas anti-PD-L1 therapy showed a borderline but nonsignificant association (OR: 3.13, 95% CI: 0.97–10.10, P = .057).

4. Discussion

This cross-sectional study explored the association between ED and QoL in patients receiving ICI therapy. The findings highlight that both anxiety and depression, assessed using the HADS, were significantly correlated with lower QoL scores across all 8 functional domains of the Turkish version of the SF-36. Notably, anxiety symptoms showed the strongest inverse correlation with bodily pain, social functioning, and mental health subdomains. These results underscore the burden of psychological symptoms in patients receiving ICIs and suggest that ED is an important correlate of poorer QoL, independent of measured clinical factors.

In the present study, patients were categorized into lower and higher QoL groups using the cohort-specific median global SF-36 score because no universally accepted SF-36 global score threshold has been established for defining low QoL in ICI-treated cancer populations. In oncology, clinically meaningful health-related quality of life thresholds are generally context-dependent and are commonly used to interpret longitudinal changes in questionnaire scores rather than to define a single cross-sectional low QoL category.[15-17] Therefore, the cutoff value of 60.5 should be interpreted as an exploratory, distribution-based threshold rather than as a universally validated clinical value. Nevertheless, this stratification allowed us to examine the clinical and psychological characteristics associated with relatively poorer QoL within the present cohort.

Recent studies have increasingly emphasized that ED represents a major yet often underrecognized component of the cancer experience in patients receiving ICIs.[11,18] Our data show a substantial burden of clinically relevant anxiety and depressive symptoms in this cohort, consistent with the growing body of evidence showing substantial psychological burden among patients receiving ICIs. Zeng et al demonstrated that nearly half of patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer reported significant ED before ICI initiation, which was associated with shorter progression-free survival, a lower objective response rate, and a reduced 2-year overall survival rate, suggesting a bidirectional interaction between psychological and immunologic factors.[8] However, in the prospective real-world study by Grote et al, which evaluated advanced melanoma patients treated with the ipilimumab plus nivolumab combination, QoL remained largely stable during therapy and follow-up. In the same study, the authors also noted that moderate irAEs did not significantly affect QoL, though severe or persistent toxicities may still impair well-being.[19] Moreover, evidence from malignant melanoma survivors indicates that unmet supportive care needs are associated with higher anxiety and depression scores, poorer health status, and lower QoL, suggesting that psychosocial and supportive care issues may persist during survivorship.[20] Collectively, these findings reinforce that QoL is not merely a reflection of physical well-being but is deeply shaped by emotional and psychological dimensions that persist throughout and beyond ICI treatment.

The association between ED and QoL is well recognized in cancer patients.[21] However, the domain-level SF-36 results provide additional insight into how QoL impairment was reflected in this ICI-treated cohort. Both HADS-A and HADS-D were negatively correlated with all SF-36 domains, with the strongest associations observed for anxiety in bodily pain, social functioning, and mental health. This pattern suggests that ED was not limited to psychological domains alone but was also associated with physical discomfort and social functioning. Because the present study did not include a chemotherapy-treated comparator group, we could not directly determine whether specific SF-36 domains are differentially affected by ICI therapy compared with chemotherapy.

Pivotal ICI trials and patient-reported outcome analyses have generally reported preserved or improved QoL compared with chemotherapy.[22-24] However, the relatively high proportion of patients classified as having lower QoL in our cohort should be interpreted within the context of a real-world, cross-sectional population. Unlike randomized clinical trials, which often include selected patients and evaluate health-related quality of life longitudinally, our cohort included patients with a substantial disease burden. More than half of the patients had 2 or more metastatic sites, most had ECOG performance status 1 to 2, and the majority were receiving ICI therapy in the second-line or later setting. Therefore, lower QoL in this cohort may reflect the combined burden of advanced cancer, impaired functional status, prior treatment exposure, metastatic disease, and ED rather than poor tolerability of ICI therapy alone. In addition, because the lower QoL group was defined using a cohort-specific median SF-36 score rather than an externally validated clinical threshold, this classification should be interpreted as relative QoL impairment within the present cohort.

In multivariate logistic regression, poor QoL was independently associated with poorer ECOG performance status (ECOG ≥ 1), older age, the presence of ≥2 metastatic sites, and receipt of ICI therapy in the second-line or later setting. HADS-A and HADS-D scores also remained strong independent correlates of impaired QoL. The association between ECOG status and QoL has been consistently reported across cancer populations.[25-28] Even a modest decline in physical function, such as ECOG 1, can lead to limitations in daily activities, loss of autonomy, and increased vulnerability to psychological distress.[29,30] Our results reaffirm the need to preserve physical functioning and mobility to support broader aspects of well-being in cancer care. Patients receiving ICIs in the first-line setting demonstrated better QoL outcomes, likely reflecting lower symptom burden, fewer prior toxicities, and preserved functional status. Across pivotal trials, first-line ICI therapy has been consistently associated with better preservation or improvement of QoL compared with both chemotherapy and later-line ICI use.[22,31,32]

The observed psychological burden in ICI-treated patients may be partly explained by immunologic and endocrine mechanisms. ICIs enhance T-cell activation and cytokine release, processes that can extend beyond tumor control to affect systemic homeostasis..[33] ICIs induce T-cell activation and loss of self-tolerance, resulting in direct lymphocytic infiltration and destruction of endocrine organs such as the pituitary, thyroid, and adrenal glands.[34] This autoimmune process may lead to hormonal imbalances and secondary systemic inflammation, which can manifest as fatigue, mood changes, and reduced cognitive function.[34] Emerging evidence indicates that inflammatory cytokines can modulate microglial activation and impair neuroplasticity, supporting the existence of an immune–brain interface that may be relevant to the development of mood and cognitive symptoms during immunotherapy.[35,36] Conversely, chronic stress and depressive symptoms have been shown to impair immune surveillance and alter cytokine profiles, potentially affecting treatment efficacy. Taken together, these findings support a bidirectional relationship between immune modulation and mental health: excessive or dysregulated immune activation may precipitate mood alterations, whereas successful immune normalization and tumor response may promote psychological recovery. This dynamic underscores the need for integrative psycho-neuro-immunologic research to fully elucidate the biological underpinnings of emotional well-being in the immunotherapy era.

A major strength of this study lies in its focus on a clinically homogeneous and psychotropically untreated cohort of patients receiving prolonged immunotherapy. By excluding patients receiving psychotropic medication and including only those who had completed at least 3 ICI cycles, the study reduced some potential sources of heterogeneity. However, the cross-sectional design does not allow the attribution of psychological outcomes directly to immunotherapy. Furthermore, the use of 2 validated, complementary tools – HADS for ED and SF-36 for multidimensional QoL evaluation – adds methodological robustness. To our knowledge, this is among the few real-world studies to examine associations among treatment-related clinical characteristics, ED, and QoL in ICI-treated patients.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; therefore, the observed associations between ED and QoL should not be interpreted as causal relationships. Second, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed; thus, the sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients available during the study period, which may have limited the statistical power for subgroup analyses. Third, irAEs could not be comprehensively analyzed because detailed information on grading, timing, duration, and biomarker correlations was unavailable. As irAEs may influence mood and fatigue through inflammatory, endocrine, and neuroimmune mechanisms such as cytokine imbalance, thyroiditis, or adrenal dysfunction, the absence of concurrent inflammatory, endocrine, and immune biomarker data limited our ability to directly explore the biological mechanisms that may link ICI therapy to psychological symptoms. Therefore, future multicenter prospective studies integrating toxicity grading, biomarker assessment, and psychometric data are warranted. Fourth, low QoL was defined using the cohort-specific median global SF-36 score rather than an externally validated clinical threshold. Although this approach allowed exploratory stratification within the study population, the median-based cutoff should not be interpreted as a universally applicable clinical value and requires validation in independent ICI-treated cohorts. Fifth, the study was conducted in a Turkish patient population, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings, cultural contexts, and patient populations. Sixth, patients receiving psychotropic medication were excluded; therefore, the results may not be generalizable to ICI-treated patients with preexisting or medically managed psychiatric comorbidities. Seventh, several potential determinants of QoL and ED, including socioeconomic status, education level, marital status, social support, detailed symptom burden, pain severity, and disease duration, were not systematically collected or available. In addition, the number of variables entered into the multivariate model was also intentionally limited to reduce the risk of overfitting given the number of outcome events. Finally, the study relied on self-reported questionnaires, which are subject to response bias but remain the most standardized approach for evaluating ED and QoL in oncology.

5. Conclusion

This cross-sectional study highlights the high prevalence and clinical relevance of psychological distress among cancer patients undergoing ICI therapy. Anxiety and depression were consistently associated with poorer QoL across all domains and remained independent correlates of poor QoL after adjustment for clinical variables. Routine psychosocial screening and early supportive interventions should be considered essential components of comprehensive care in the era of ICIs.
