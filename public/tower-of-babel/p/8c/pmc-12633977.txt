A rare case report of Sturge–Weber syndrome type 2 variant on Roach scale
Authors: Akhil Pratap Singh, Prabhat Agrawal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA facial birthmark known as a port-wine stain, neurological symptoms like seizures, hemiparesis, intellectual disability, and learning difficulties, and ocular abnormalities like glaucoma, choroidal haemangiomas, or heterochromia of irides are all associated with Sturge–Weber syndrome (SWS), a rare vascular disorder. We report a novel case of SWS in a 19-year-old woman who had an unusual presentation of the type 2 Roach variation of SWS, characterized by gingival hypertrophy and typical port-wine staining of the face, bilaterally, without any other symptoms.

Introduction

Sturge–Weber syndrome (SWS) is a rare vascular disorder, with an estimated prevalence of 1: 20–50,000 births with no sexual predilection, characterized by the association of a facial birthmark called a port-wine stain, so named for their coloration, which is similar in colour to port wine, a fortified red wine from Portugal, involving trigeminal nerve distribution, neurological manifestations including seizures, hemiparesis, intellectual disability, and learning difficulties; and ocular abnormalities such as glaucoma, choroidal haemangiomas, or heterochromia of irides.[1] William Allen Sturge reported the case of a six-and-a-half-year-old girl who had experienced seizures since she was six months old in 1879. He hypothesized that a lesion on the surface of the same side of the brain was the cause of the patient’s neurological deficiency.[2]

Case Report

A 19-year-old unmarried woman arrived at our hospital’s outpatient department complaining of gingival hypertrophy on her left lower alveolus and reddish-purple staining on both cheeks [Figures 1 and 2]. Seizures, anomalies of vision, headaches, localized neurological deficits, were not present in the past. Examining the patient’s past medical records, it was discovered that she had surgery to remove gingival hypertrophy around five years prior, but the hypertrophy returned shortly after. There was no noteworthy medical, menstrual, or familial history. A large reddish-purple lesion covering the skin on the right side of the face in the mandibular (V2) division of the trigeminal nerve, as well as the maxillary (V2) and mandibular (V3) divisions on the left side of face, which is present since birth, was observed during the general examination. The area of skin affected has grown in proportion to overall growth since birth and has turned more purplish with age. These birthmarks are indicative of port-wine stain. The patient’s vital signs were stable. Hypertrophied tongue mucosa on the ventral surface [Figure 3] with discoloration of left anterior gingivo-buccal mucosa was also noted. Upon ocular assessment, nothing stood out. Brain computed tomography scan showed no abnormalities. The patient was therefore diagnosed with SWS after taking into account the history, clinical symptoms, and testing. The condition, its prognosis, consequences, and the significance of routine medical follow-up were all explained to the patient. She was recommended to have her face treated with laser photocoagulation.

Discussion

SWS is a member of the group of diseases known as phakomatoses. It consists of congenital hamartomatous anomalies involving skin, central nervous system (CNS), and eyes, in variable combinations. Angiomas of the meninges typically affect the parietal and occipital region on one side. Underneath the angioma, they alter vascular flow and result in calcium deposition in the cerebral cortex. Depending on the extent of the lesion, this can lead to epilepsy, mental retardation, hemiplegia, or hemiparesis. The ocular division is pathognomonic for port-wine stains (PWS), though it can be seen on any dermatome of the trigeminal nerve. Rarely, PWS might appear bilaterally, completely absent, or spread to the neck, limbs, and other body areas. Some of the recognized ocular symptoms of the illness include hemianopia, glaucoma, buphthalmos, and choroidal haemangioma.[3]

When SWS affects both the CNS and the face, it is categorized as complete SWS; when it only affects one, it is categorized as incomplete SWS. The ‘encephalotrigeminal angiomatosis’ spectrum was first classified by Dr. E. Steve Roach in 1992 [Table 1], and it is currently understood to be a spectrum illness with different combinations of involvement in the brain, skin, and eyes.[4]

Certain symptoms might not appear till later in life. Somatic mosaic mutation was first proposed by Rudolf Happle in 1987 to better understand this complex pathophysiology.[56]

No specific therapy exists for SWS. Medical therapies offered to individuals with SWS include anticonvulsants for seizure management, glaucoma therapy to lower intraocular pressure, dye laser photocoagulation for port-wine stain, and headache prevention and treatment. Surgery, including corpus callosotomy, hemispherectomy, or targeted cortical resection, is the treatment of choice for patients with glaucoma and refractory seizures.[7] Pulsed dye laser is the widely used therapy for cutaneous manifestations of PWS. It makes use of the selective photo-thermolysis concept, which targets the damaged cutaneous blood vessels while sparing the surrounding skin. There is no standard age at which treatment should begin; however, it may take several therapy sessions. Since children’s skin is thinner and their veins are more superficial, the likelihood of a better outcome increases as the patient’s age decreases.[89] According to a 1998 research, early intervention is necessary based on the percentage decline in PWS size that occurs with age. After five treatment sessions, there was a 63% improvement in the age group under one year, compared to 48% and 54% improvement in the age groups of one to six years.[10] Our patient was emphasized about the significance of periodic follow-up. The patient was referred to the dermatology department for laser photocoagulation for skin lesions. Since our patient had no other symptoms, no other medication was advised.

This patient is a newly diagnosed case of SWS with PWS on bilateral cheeks with gingival hypertrophy with no other manifestations, making this a rare presentation. Active search for various features of this syndrome must be done by primary care physicians, paediatricians, and dermatologists to pick it up early and offer treatment at the earliest for optimal outcomes.
