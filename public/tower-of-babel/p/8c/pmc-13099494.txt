Pulsed ultrasonic-assisted nanoliposomes co-encapsulating maize peptide and β-carotene: Structural alterations for enhanced activity and stability
Authors: Ying Liu, Wenjuan Qu, Liting Zhu, Xinyue Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Currently, the efficient co-encapsulation of different bioactives remains a challenge. To enhance the activity and stability of encapsulation systems and reduce energy consumption, this study employed pulsed ultrasonic treatment (PUT) to prepare a nanoliposome system and evaluated its effects by combining molecular simulation and experiments. Results showed that treatment with a 15/5 s pulse ratio for 9 min significantly improved the activity (38.25%) and co-encapsulation efficiency of β-carotene (βC) and maize peptide (MP) (66.75% and 60.38%), while reducing energy consumption by 25%. Molecular insights revealed that PUT induced structural relaxation of MP, thereby enhancing its binding with βC and liposomes (by 11.73%) and consequently improving co-encapsulation. This method also significantly enhanced the stability under high temperature, oxidation, and light. This research elucidated the molecular mechanism by which PUT promoted encapsulation, offering a promising approach for the efficient and low-energy co-encapsulation of bioactives, while also contributing to enhanced activity and stability.

Introduction

In recent years, numerous food-derived functional components have been incorporated into food systems to meet human nutritional needs (Park et al., 2023). Among these components, β-carotene (βC) has attracted considerable attention due to its remarkable antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties (Hamadou et al., 2023). However, its strong hydrophobicity (with a logP value of approximately 15 > 0.5), limited bioaccessibility, and instability under light and heat conditions severely limit its application in food matrices (Gupta et al., 2025; Liu, Qu, et al., 2025). To improve its delivery, this study selects amphiphilic maize peptide (MP) as both a functional co-delivery component and a structural stabilizer. As a bioactive compound derived from the hydrolysis of natural corn protein, MP exhibits superior antioxidant potential, anti-allergenic properties, amphiphilicity, and compatibility with food matrices compared to other peptides sourced from fish, legumes, nuts, and vegetables (Fan et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2022; Zu et al., 2023). More importantly, owing to its unique amphiphilic structure, MP can interact with and stabilize hydrophobic compounds like βC. Despite this, MP faces its own stability challenges, which constrain its application. To overcome these challenges, developing efficient and stable delivery systems for bioactive substances is essential.

Compared to other delivery carriers, liposome (Lipo) encapsulation is often considered superior due to its unique advantages in addressing a variety of biomedical and engineering challenges (Castro-Muñoz & Cabezas, 2026). Liposomes serve as a biocompatible, biodegradable, and versatile delivery system capable of encapsulating a wide range of bioactive compounds, including amphiphilic MP and hydrophobic βC (Zhang et al., 2024). In liposomes, non-polar, lipid-soluble and unstable βC can be incorporated into the hydrophobic tail region of the phospholipid bilayer, while polar, water-soluble and unstable MP can be encapsulated within the internal aqueous phase or anchored at the hydrophilic interface (Liu et al., 2020). This “each-in-its-place” distribution pattern is expected to maximize encapsulation efficiency and stability while optimizing the utilization of liposomal space, thereby addressing the low solubility and instability challenges associated with βC. However, the practical application of liposomes encounters challenges related to preparation methods that balance efficiency, stability, and cost. Common techniques such as extrusion and high-pressure homogenization often require complex equipment and may not consistently produce uniformly small particles with high encapsulation efficiency (Yugatama et al., 2026). Ultrasonication has emerged as a promising alternative due to its ability to generate smaller and more uniform liposomes with improved encapsulation performance, operational simplicity, and compatibility with food-grade systems (Qu, Deng, et al., 2025). Nevertheless, conventional continuous ultrasonic processing suffers from significant limitations, including localized overheating, excessive cavitation, and high energy consumption (Wu et al., 2022). These issues can compromise liposome integrity, lead to leakage of encapsulated actives, and reduce process sustainability. Therefore, it highlights the need for a more controlled and efficient ultrasonic strategy. In this context, pulsed ultrasound offers a targeted innovation. By alternating active and resting cycles, it offers better control of cavitation, pressure, and thermal effects, thereby minimizing energy input and preventing structural damage to both the liposomes and the encapsulated bioactive compounds (Malinowska et al., 2026). Moreover, current encapsulating research has expanded to valorize diverse by-products and wastes apart from phenolics, such as prebiotic arabinoxylans from cereal bran (Hernández-Pinto et al., 2024), antioxidant mangiferin from mango kernels (Castro-Muñoz et al., 2024), and sustainably produced carminic acid (Ferreyra-Suarez et al., 2024). Emerging technologies like ultrasound are pivotal in enabling these sustainable and efficient processes, facilitating the extraction, separation, and purification of target molecules. More importantly, this approach not only enhances the formation of stable, small-sized nanoliposomes but also improves encapsulation efficiency and functional performance (Moosavi-Nasab et al., 2023). Chen et al. (2023) found that smaller liposome sizes exhibit higher encapsulation efficiencies and enhanced small intestinal transmembrane absorption capabilities. Therefore, it is hypothesized that combining pulsed ultrasonication with liposome encapsulation technology reduces damage to thermostable active substances, reduces liposome size, while improving particle dispersion uniformity. This approach could achieve efficient, stable, low-energy encapsulation of active substances with varying polarities, addressing key gaps in current delivery system design.

Ultrasound treatment is known to alter the conformation of protein-based raw materials, which can potentially affect their interactions with other components. For instance, Wang et al. (2025) reported that ultrasonic pretreatment (400 W, 20 min) has been shown to partially unfold wheat gliadin, enhancing its binding with quercetin and improving encapsulation efficiency and bioavailability. While such conformational changes are commonly studied through spectroscopic methods, these techniques often fail to provide quantitative, dynamic insights at the molecular level, such as detailed geometry, atomic fluctuations, or hydrogen-bonding evolution. Computational simulations, like Molecular dynamics (MD), can overcome these limitations by precisely quantifying protein behavior and structural changes under simulated ultrasound conditions. Crucially, MD simulations can effectively mimic the physical effect of ultrasound, namely the periodic and intense fluctuations in hydrostatic pressure. This is achieved by applying simulated pressure waves, such as through the application of simulated pressure waves, such as positive and negative square-wave variations, to model the ultrasonic environment. For example, Han et al. (2022) employed MD with a segmented simulation approach to investigate the mechanism by which ultrasound influences the adsorption of phenol onto single-walled carbon nanotubes. However, there have been no published reports integrating MD simulations and molecular docking with experimental spectroscopy to investigate how ultrasonic parameters (e.g., power and frequency) mechanistically modify the structure of bioactive peptides and regulate their interactions with co-encapsulated compounds and delivery carriers. Addressing this integrated multiscale gap forms a key innovation of the present work.

This study aims to develop a pulsed ultrasound (PUT)-assisted nanoliposome system for the co-encapsulation of βC and MP. By optimizing ultrasonic parameters, this research seeks to reduce particle size and energy consumption while improving co-encapsulation, bioactivity, and stability. The innovative aspect of this study lies in combining MD simulations, molecular docking, and multi-spectral analysis to reveal, at the molecular level, how ultrasound modifies MP conformation and enhances its interactions with βC and the Lipo. Furthermore, the stability of the formulated system will be evaluated under various temperature, oxidation, and light conditions. This research is expected to provide an efficient, stable, and low-energy delivery strategy for active ingredients with different polarities, thereby offering valuable insights for future applications in functional foods.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Maize peptide (MP) (Asp-Val-Phe-Phe-Gln-Ala-Val-Lys-Ala-Leu-Pro-Cys-Pro-Lys-Leu, with a relative molecular mass of 1726 Da and 95% purity) was solid-phase synthesized by Shanghai Apeptide Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). β-carotene (βC, purity>98%) was purchased from Adamas Reagents Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Lecithin and cholesterol were procured from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). 1,1-Diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH, purity ≥90%), 8-anilino-1-naphthalenesulfonic acid (ANS, purity ≥97%), and 1,1,3,3-tetraethoxypropane (purity ≥96%) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All other reagents used were of analytical grade and purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of liposomes assisted by pulsed ultrasound

The reported method (Qu et al., 2025a) was adapted with appropriate modifications. Lecithin and cholesterol (mass ratio 8:1) were thoroughly dissolved in 10 mL of anhydrous ethanol, along with Tween-80 (2%, v/v). Subsequently, 5 mL of a 4 mg/mL βC solution in chloroform was added, and the mixture was vacuum distilled at 35 °C to form a βC-Lipo film. An MP solution (9 mg/mL) was added to the βC-Lipo film at a mass ratio of MP: Lipo = 3:8. The MP-βC-Lipo@PUT complex was prepared using a pulsed ultrasonication (PUT) device (Jiangsu Jiangda Wukesong Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China) at 30 °C. Key operational parameters, namely the pulse ratio, applied power, and frequency, needed to be set at this device. Unlike continuous ultrasonication, the pulsed ultrasound mode was characterized by alternating periods of active ultrasonication (Working time) and rest intervals (Intermittent time), as described by Fig. S1. Studies were conducted at different ultrasonic frequencies of 20, 28, 33, 40, and 60 kHz (power 300 W, pulse ratio 10/5 s/s, duration 6 min), varying ultrasonic power levels of untreated (0 W), 100, 200, 300, and 400 W (28 kHz, pulse ratio 10/5 s/s, duration 6 min), different pulse ratios of continuous, 5/5, 10/5, 15/5, and 20/5 s/s (Fig. S1 of the Supplementary Material, 28 kHz, power 300 W, duration 6 min), and different treatment durations of 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15 min (28 kHz, power 300 W, pulse ratio 15/5 s/s) on the preparation of the MP-βC-Lipo@PUT complex. Samples prepared via the same procedure but without ultrasonication (0 W) served as the untreated control group.

Determination of liposome encapsulation efficiency

The βC encapsulation rate (Hamadou et al., 2023), MP encapsulation rate (Puchalska et al., 2014), the yield (Qu et al., 2025a), and the DPPH radical scavenging rate (Liu, Qu, et al., 2025) of the liposome system were determined. Additionally, the cooperative coefficient of MP and βC was evaluated. Detailed methods are provided in Sections 2.3.1–2.3.4 of the Supplementary Material.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation of MP structural changes under different ultrasonic power and frequency

Following the reported methodology (Han et al., 2022), ultrasonic powers and frequencies were simulated using pressure and period variations from positive and negative square waves. The 2D structure of MP was first mapped, then converted into a 3D model and subjected to energy minimization. Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted using GROMACS software (version 2022.5). First, the MP structure was centered within a 10 × 10 × 10 nm3 cubic box. Sufficient ions were added to replace solvent molecules, ensuring the system was electrically neutral. Energy minimization was performed. Subsequently, an isothermal-isovolumetric (NVT) equilibration was performed, maintaining the system temperature at 303 K. This was followed by an isobaric-isothermal (NPT) equilibration, controlling the system pressure. The ultrasonic powers of 0 (Untreated), 100, 200, and 300 W were employed to explore the impact of ultrasonic power on the MP structure. The ultrasonic frequency was varied by altering the period of the square wave. Simulating ultrasonic frequencies in the kHz range necessitates molecular dynamics simulation times on the microsecond scale, which demands substantial computational resources and processing time. Therefore, to simulate the influence of ultrasonic frequency within a feasible computational and temporal scope, low, medium, and high frequency conditions at the MHz level were selected, following the reported methodology (Han et al., 2022). Consequently, the square wave period was set to T = 2.5, 4, and 5 ns, corresponding to ultrasonic frequencies of 400, 250, and 200 MHz. During the simulation, the cubic box was deformed via the deform command at a rate of ±0.0005 nm/ps in the xx direction to simulate ultrasonic shear effects. The total simulation duration was 20 ns, with data output at 1 ps intervals for subsequent analysis. Schematic diagrams of the periodic square waves for different ultrasonic pressures and frequencies are provided in Supplementary Material Figs. S2 and S3.

Molecular interaction characterization under ultrasonic fields

Following completion of MD simulations under varying ultrasonic powers and frequencies (Section 2.4), blind-box molecular docking of MP and βC was performed using AutoDock 4 software (version 4.2.6). The βC structure (CID: 5280489) was retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Docking results were visualized using Discovery Studio 2019 software (version 19.1).

MD simulations of the MP-βC complex were conducted using GROMACS software, following the procedure described in Section 2.4. The complex was placed at the center of a cubic simulation box, followed by system neutralization and energy minimization. The ultrasonic power, square wave, and temperature were set to 300 W, 4 ns, and 303 K, respectively. Binding energy components of the complex were calculated, including contributions from van der Waals forces (ΔEvdw), electrostatic solvation free energy (ΔEelec), polar solvation free energy (ΔGpolar), and nonpolar solvation free energy (ΔGnonpolar).

Determination of liposome structural changes

The fluorescence spectra, fluorescence quenching (Özbağcı & Erdağı, 2026), Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra (Liu, Yang, et al., 2025), particle size, zeta potential (Hamadou et al., 2023), and results from optical microscope, confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) (Liu, Liang, Liu, Rashid, et al., 2024), scanning electron microscope (SEM) (Liu, Lin, Wei, Hu, & Wang, 2024), as well as thermogravimetric (TG) analysis (Zhang et al., 2024) of the MP-βC-Lipo@PUT (with a frequency of 28 kHz, a power of 300 W, a pulse ratio of 15/5 s/s, and a duration of 9 min) and MP-βC-Lipo without ultrasonication were determined. Detailed methods are provided in Sections 2.6.1–2.6.4 of the Supplementary Material.

Liposome stability assessment

Temperature test

The liposome stability was assessed as described previously (Zhang et al., 2024) with some modifications. Liposome samples were incubated at different temperatures (25, 35, 45, 55, and 65 °C) for 30 min, after which they were immediately water-cooled to room temperature (25 °C). The concentrations of βC and MP in each sample were subsequently determined according to Sections 2.3.1 and 2.3.2 of the Supplementary Material, allowing for the calculation of retention rates. Additionally, the particle size and zeta potential of the samples were measured, as outlined in Section 2.6.3 of the Supplementary Material.(1)Retention rate%=ReRs×100%

Where Re is the residual concentration of βC or MP in the treated sample, mg/mL, and Rs is the initial concentration of encapsulated βC or MP in the untreated sample, mg/mL.

Accelerated oxidation assay

Liposome samples were subjected to accelerated oxidation at 37 °C. The concentrations of βC and MP were measured at various time points (0, 3, 6, 9, 10, 12, 14, 16, and 17 days), and their retention rates, particle size, and zeta potential were subsequently calculated. The malondialdehyde (MDA) content was determined (Liu, Liang, Liu, Rashid, et al., 2024). A thiobarbituric acid (TBA) test solution was prepared by dissolving trichloroacetic acid (15%, w/v) and TBA (0.375%, w/v) in 100 mL of 0.25 mol/L HCl. Next, 200 μL of the liposome sample was mixed with 4 mL of the TBA test solution, heated in boiling water for 15 min, then cooled to ambient temperature and centrifuged at 4000 ×g for 15 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 532 nm. The MDA content was calculated using a 1,1,3,3-tetraethoxypropane standard curve (Y = 4.7217× + 0.6295, where X represents absorbance and Y represents the standard concentration in mmol/L).

Light-exposed storage test

Liposome samples were stored at 25 °C under both light-exposed and dark conditions for 20 days. The concentrations of βC and MP in the samples were measured on days 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 14, 16, 18, and 20. Retention rates, particle size, and zeta potential were then calculated.

Statistical analysis of data

All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Significant differences were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) via Tukey's test. Data were considered significantly different when p < 0.05. Graphs were plotted using Origin 2019b software (OriginLab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA).

Results and discussion

Effect of pulsed ultrasonic treatment on liposome preparation

Ultrasonic frequency

Fig. 1A demonstrates that variations in ultrasonic frequency significantly impacted βC and MP encapsulation rates, yield, and DPPH radical scavenging rate (p < 0.05). As the frequency increased from 20 kHz (low) to 60 kHz (high), all indices initially increased significantly before decreasing. The optimal values were achieved at 28 kHz, with encapsulation rates of 79.39% for βC, 65.26% for MP, a yield of 38.36%, and a radical scavenging rate of 48.73%. At low frequencies (20 kHz), ultrasonic-induced cavitation bubbles grow large, generating stronger shockwave energy upon rupture. This excessive energy caused damage to liposomes, leading to structural instability and aggregation. Consequently, the encapsulation of βC and MP, as well as liposome yield and activity, was impaired. Conversely, at higher frequencies (33–60 kHz), numerous smaller cavitation bubbles form. Although these microbubbles produce lower localized thermal and pressure intensities, their collapse frequency is higher. This high-frequency cavitation led to excessive dispersion of lipid particles, reducing effective interaction with the active substances and, consequently, decreasing both βC and MP encapsulation, yield, and activity. Extreme frequencies, whether high or low, might cause partial damage to chemical bonds or undesirable conformational changes in molecules (Liu, Liang, Liu, Rashid, et al., 2024). The 28 kHz frequency represented the optimal condition, as it generated cavitation bubbles of moderate size and energy, preventing excessive disruption of liposomal structure. Additionally, at this frequency, the arrangement of the lipid bilayer's internal hydrophobic and external hydrophilic regions was optimized. This facilitated the incorporation of hydrophobic βC and amphiphilic MP into the lipid interior, improving both the encapsulation of active substances and the yield. Since βC and MP possess antioxidant properties, they effectively scavenge DPPH radicals. βC, with its conjugated alkene structures, scavenges radicals by donating electrons (Liu, Qu, et al., 2025), while MP, with its aromatic amino acids, dissociates hydrogen atoms (Wei et al., 2022), thus contributing to antioxidant activity. At 28 kHz, the molecular structures of βC and MP remained relatively stable, with functional groups such as conjugated double bonds and hydroxyl groups retaining high activity. These functional groups acted as key sites for radical scavenging, allowing the liposomes to achieve optimal radical scavenging activity. In conclusion, the 28 kHz ultrasonic frequency was the most suitable for liposome preparation in this study. The researcher also reported that, compared to other ultrasonic frequencies (38, 45, and 100 kHz), 28 kHz generated more stable cavitation effects during the propagation of acoustic waves in liquids, resulting in improved outcomes in the preparation of zinc nanoparticles (Iino et al., 2021). Similarly, an earlier study reported that ultrasonic treatment at 28 kHz enhanced the radical scavenging activity of Inonotus obliquus polysaccharides (IOPs) against DPPH radicals, superoxide anion radicals, and hydroxyl radicals (Zhang, Li, et al., 2025).Fig. 1The effects of ultrasonic frequency (A), power (B), pulse ratio (C), and time (D) on β-carotene (βC) encapsulation rate, maize peptide (MP) encapsulation rate, DPPH scavenging rate, and liposome yield. Ultrasonic power

Fig. 1B demonstrates that ultrasonic treatments at varying power levels significantly enhanced the βC encapsulation, MP encapsulation, yield, and DPPH radical scavenging rate (p < 0.05), compared to the untreated control (0 W). This suggested that ultrasonic processing facilitated the encapsulation of active substances and complex liposome formation. As power increased from 100 W to 400 W, all indices initially rose significantly before decreasing. The optimal values were achieved at 300 W, with βC encapsulation, MP encapsulation, yield, and activity reaching 80.77%, 65.24%, 38.29%, and 48.92%, respectively. Compared to the control, βC encapsulation increased by 55.30%, MP encapsulation by 47.40%, yield by 24.58%, and activity by 31.87%. Lipophilic βC primarily binds to the hydrophobic regions of MP, which are usually buried within the peptide chain, reducing the likelihood of MP-βC interactions. Ultrasonication induces shear and cavitation effects that cause the peptide molecular structure to unfold and expose internal hydrophobic groups. As power increases, more hydrophobic groups were exposed (as confirmed by fluorescence spectroscopy in Section 3.4.1). This promoted interactions between MP and βC, thereby enhancing encapsulation. Additionally, the increased ultrasonic power generates microjet effects, which boost intermolecular collisions during liposome preparation. This increased the likelihood of contact between MP, βC, and Lipo, improving yield. However, excessively high ultrasonic power intensified cavitation, which might disrupt the bilayer structure of liposomes under extreme temperature and pressure conditions. Moreover, high-temperature treatment could also induce structural changes in the compounds and promote degradation. Therefore, this disruption led to oxidation or degradation, resulting in the leakage of encapsulated active substances (Sun, Wang, et al., 2024) and a decrease in βC and MP co-encapsulation, as well as liposome yield. Similarly, an earlier study reported that ultrasonication enhanced curcumin loading efficiency during the preparation of curcumin-loaded oil bodies. As power increased, encapsulation efficiency rose initially but then declined. This decrease at excessive power levels was likely due to severe ultrasonic disruption of the oil body structure (Zhu et al., 2023). Furthermore, excessively high ultrasonic power decomposed water to produce significant amounts of ·H and ·OH radicals (Shishkina et al., 2023), which diminished the radical scavenging capacity. Therefore, this study recommended an optimal ultrasonic power of 300 W for liposome preparation.

Ultrasonic pulse ratio

Fig. 1C demonstrates that, compared to continuous ultrasound, ultrasonic treatment at various pulse ratios significantly enhanced the βC encapsulation, MP encapsulation, yield, and activity (p < 0.05). This suggested that pulsed-mode ultrasound was more effective than continuous mode for promoting liposome formation and encapsulating active ingredients. However, as the pulse ratio increased from 5/5 s/s to 20/5 s/s, all indices initially increased significantly and then decreased (p < 0.05). At a pulse ratio of 15/5 s/s, the maximum values for these indexes were achieved (90.41%, 77.51%, 40.86%, and 53.05%, respectively). Compared to continuous mode, the βC encapsulation increased by 45.28%, the MP encapsulation by 30.18%, the yield by 22.08%, and the activity by 26.41%, while also saving 25% of ultrasonic energy consumption. An earlier study also reported that pulsed ultrasound (1–8 s) significantly improved the encapsulation efficiency of stigmasterol in zein-based phytosterol nano-dispersions compared to continuous ultrasound (Feng et al., 2019). This phenomenon was attributed to the fact that an increase in pulse ratio extended the ultrasonic operating time. This extension enhanced cavitation effects, exposing internal hydrophobic sites within MP and promoting interactions between MP, βC, and Lipo. Consequently, this promoted βC and MP encapsulation and complex liposome formation. An excessively low pulse ratio leads to frequent interruptions during ultrasonic processing, resulting in intermittent energy delivery. This hinders the tripartite interactions, preventing optimal encapsulation. Conversely, an excessively high pulse ratio represented longer ultrasonic working times, which intensified the cavitation effect and caused MP molecular denaturation and aggregation, reducing their binding affinity with βC and Lipo. Additionally, it increased the permeability of the formed liposome membrane (Wu et al., 2022), causing leakage of the encapsulated βC and MP, which reduced the encapsulation efficiency and liposome formation. These factors also decreased the antioxidant capacity of the liposomes. Therefore, a pulse ratio of 15/5 s/s was determined to be optimal.

Ultrasonication duration

Fig. 1D demonstrates that as the ultrasonication duration increases, the encapsulation rates of βC and MP, yield, and activity all initially increased significantly, reaching a peak, and then decreased sharply (p < 0.05). These indices reached their optimal values at 9 min. This phenomenon was attributed to the fact that a suitable ultrasonication duration, on one hand, enhanced the exposure of hydrophobic sites on MP, facilitating its binding with βC and Lipo. On the other hand, a prolonged ultrasonication time optimized the phospholipid bilayer structure of the liposomes, thereby improving the encapsulation efficiency of both hydrophobic βC and hydrophilic MP. As a result, higher encapsulation rates of βC and MP were achieved, alongside increased yield and activity. However, this might be caused by the phase change expanding effect of the liposomal lipid bilayer when the time of ultrasound was too long, increasing the permeability of the liposomes encapsulated compounds, thereby reducing the encapsulation rate, yield, and activity (Sun, Wang, et al., 2024). Similarly, an earlier study reported that during ultrasound-assisted quercetin delivery using soy protein isolate, encapsulation efficiency initially increased, but declined as the sonication time extended from 10 to 20 min (Lin et al., 2023).

Through the optimization of ultrasonic parameters, the ideal conditions for preparing pulse-ultrasonic nanoliposomes (MP-βC-Lipo@PUT) were established: a frequency of 28 kHz, a power of 300 W, a pulse ratio of 15/5 s/s, and a duration of 9 min. Liposomes prepared under optimized conditions exhibited superior performance: βC encapsulation of 92.22%, MP encapsulation of 80.22%, activity of 55.30%, and yield of 42.63%. Compared to untreated samples, βC encapsulation increased by 66.75%, MP encapsulation by 60.38%, activity by 38.25%, and yield by 28.91%. Furthermore, compared to continuous ultrasonication, the pulse process reduced energy consumption by 25%. Moreover, the cooperative coefficient for MP-βC-Lipo was observed to be less than 1 (0.90), indicating an antagonistic interaction between MP and βC. However, a significantly synergistic antioxidant effect was observed at MP-βC-Lipo@PUT (p < 0.05), with a cooperative coefficient of 1.20 (>1). A cooperative coefficient greater than 1 signifies synergy, while a coefficient equal to 1 suggests additivity, and a coefficient below 1 indicates antagonism. The synergistic effects were observed at MP-βC-Lipo@PUT, indicating the total antioxidant effect of the combination exceeded the sum of the individual antioxidant effects from MP and βC. This enhancement likely resulted from PUT fostering a tighter and more effective spatial organization and molecular interaction network between MP and βC within the liposomes, enabling them to exert a “1 + 1 > 2” effect in DPPH radical scavenging. This integrated enhancement was mechanistically attributed to the modulation of molecular assembly and nanostructure by PUT. First, under the optimized pulse ratio of 15/5 s/s, the sample underwent periodic cycles. This prevented the cumulative damage and over-processing associated with continuous sonication. Secondly, the optimized frequency (28 kHz) and power (300 W) generated cavitation bubbles of moderate size and energy. These mechanical forces provided were sufficient to induce favorable structural relaxation of MP (as indicated by MD in Section 3.2), exposing more hydrophobic sites and thereby significantly strengthening its interactions with both βC and phospholipid molecules. Finally, this combination of energy input and duration ultimately optimized the nanoscale architecture of the liposomes, resulting in vesicles with a smaller particle size and higher zeta potential (as shown in Section 3.4.4). These synergistic alterations at the molecular and structural levels collectively explained the comprehensive improvements in encapsulation, yield, and activity. In conclusion, pulsed ultrasonication offered significant benefits for enhancing the encapsulation efficiency, activity, and synergistic antioxidant effects of active substances in liposomes, while saving energy. Its ease of operation, low equipment/energy costs, and high compatibility with food matrices also make it economically viable for industrial applications.

Mechanisms of ultrasonic power and frequency effects on MP structure

Root mean square deviation (RMSD) and root mean square fluctuation (RMSF)

RMSD is an indicator used to quantify the structural alteration of a protein's final conformation relative to its initial structure. As shown in Fig. 2A and Table 1, RMSD values increased progressively with rising ultrasonic power, peaking at 300 W, which represented a 31.31% increase compared to the untreated control. Fig. 2B and Table 1 further demonstrate that RMSD values for MP also rose progressively with increasing ultrasonic frequency, with the largest increase (53.12%) observed at 400 MHz. These results indicated that exposure to ultrasonic power and frequency significantly accelerated the molecular vibrations of MP, leading to greater alterations in its final molecular structure compared to its initial configuration. RMSF measures the root mean square displacement of each α‑carbon atom relative to its native conformation, with higher values indicating greater conformational fluctuations and a looser structure. As shown in Fig. 2C and Table 1, RMSF values increased significantly with rising ultrasonic power, exhibiting a trend consistent with the RMSD results. For example, the RMSF values of amino acid residues (such as Ala6, Lys8, Pro11, and Lys14) increased by 14.02% after 300 W treatment, compared to the untreated control. Fig. 2D and Table 1 further show that RMSF values also increased with rising ultrasonic frequency, with a 24.02% increase at 400 MHz. This indicated that elevated ultrasonic power and frequency induced greater displacement of specific amino acid residues, resulting in a looser MP structure.

Solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), radius of gyration (Rg), and number of hydrogen bonds

SASA is a parameter used to quantify the contact area between a protein and the solvent, reflecting the compactness of the protein structure (Liu, Qu, Liu, Tuly, & Ma, 2024). A higher SASA value suggests a more extended structure. As shown in Fig. 2E and Table 1, SASA increased progressively with increasing ultrasonic power. At 300 W, SASA was elevated by 6.54% compared to the untreated control. Similarly, Fig. 2F and Table 1 show that SASA values increased significantly with rising ultrasonic frequency, with the greatest increase (14.43%) observed at 400 MHz. This suggested that both increased ultrasonic power and frequency contributed to the loosening of the MP structure. Liu, Qu, Liu, Tuly, and Ma (2024) also found that increasing SASA values represented the expansion of the molecular structure of the protein.

The Rg measures the distribution of atoms relative to its center of mass, providing insight into the protein's average size and the compactness of its molecular conformation. A larger Rg value indicates a more loosely packed molecular structure. As demonstrated in Fig. 2G and Table 1, the Rg gradually increased with rising ultrasonic power, reaching a 0.64% increase at 300 W compared to the control. Fig. 2H and Table 1 also show a progressive increase in the Rg with increasing ultrasonic frequency, with a 0.98% elevation at 400 MHz. These findings indicated that as both ultrasonic power and frequency increase, the distance between atoms and the molecular center increased, leading to a more dispersed atomic distribution and a loosening of the MP structure. These observations aligned with conclusions drawn from RMSD, RMSF, and SASA analyses. A study on the effects of ultrasound on myofibrillar protein structure through molecular dynamics simulations found that Rg increased following ultrasonic treatment (Liu, Liang, Liu, Rashid, et al., 2024), consistent with our results.

Intramolecular hydrogen bonds play a key role in protein structural formation and stability. The integrity of protein secondary and tertiary structures largely depends on hydrogen bonds. A reduction in hydrogen bonds often leads to protein structural instability, causing the overall structure to become looser. As shown in Fig. 2I and Table 1, the number of hydrogen bonds gradually decreased with increasing ultrasonic power, with a 3.72% reduction at 300 W. Fig. 2J and Table 1 show that the number of hydrogen bonds also decreased with rising ultrasonic frequency, showing a 6.22% reduction at 400 MHz. These results suggested that ultrasonic treatments caused the breakage of hydrogen bonds and an increase in intermolecular distances, which was a key factor contributing to the loosening of the MP structure, as supported by RMSD, RMSF, SASA, and Rg analyses. Similar studies had shown that simulated ultrasonic treatment at 500 MHz increased the number of hydrogen bonds in walnut protein isolate (WPI) (Zhang, Zhu, et al., 2025).

Mechanism of ultrasonic power and frequency effects on MP-βC molecular interactions

Molecular docking effectively predicts the binding patterns and affinities of molecules. Docking results with binding energies below −1.2 kcal/mol are considered reliable (Vidal-Limon et al., 2022). A greater number of intermolecular interactions and a higher absolute binding energy indicate stronger molecular affinity and a more stable structure. Fig. 2K and Table 2 present all docking binding energies after treatments with varying ultrasonic powers and frequencies, being below −1.2 kcal/mol, suggesting a strong affinity between MP and βC, capable of forming stable complexes. Table 2 shows that MP and βC primarily interacted through hydrophobic forces and hydrogen bonds. Specifically, βC engaged in hydrophobic interactions with MP residues Val2, Pro11, Pro13, or Leu15 and formed hydrogen bonds with Cys12. Compared with the untreated control, ultrasonic power and frequency treatments stabilized the number of hydrogen bonds between MP and βC molecules, enhanced hydrophobic interactions, and increased the absolute value of binding energy. At 300 W and 400 MHz, these increases were 11.73% and 18.56%, respectively. The combined enhancement of hydrophobic interactions and binding energy suggested that ultrasonic treatments improved MP-βC binding and complex stability. This effect was attributed to ultrasonic processing, which loosened the MP molecular structure (Section 3.2), exposing additional hydrophobic groups and facilitating MP interactions with hydrophobic βC and Lipo. This likely explained the increased encapsulation efficiency of βC and MP following ultrasonic treatment (Section 3.1). Liu, Qu, Liu, Feng, et al. Molecular dynamics and molecular docking analyses (Sections 3.2–3.3) revealed the mechanism by which ultrasound modified the MP structure and enhanced MP-βC interactions. Figs. 3B-1 and B-2 illustrate the effects of varying βC concentrations on the intrinsic fluorescence intensity of MP during the preparation of MP-βC-Lipo and MP-βC-Lipo@PUT complexes. As the βC concentration increased, a marked decrease in MP fluorescence intensity was observed, indicating that βC interacted with MP to form non-fluorescent complexes. This interaction likely involved charge or energy transfer between the quencher (βC) and the fluorophore (MP), resulting in fluorescence quenching. To elucidate the quenching mechanism, the Stern–Volmer and Van't Hoff equations were applied. As shown in Table 4, under non-ultrasonic conditions, the binding constant (Ka) of MP-βC-Lipo was relatively low (1.09 × 105–1.33 × 106 L/mol), the Stern-Volmer quenching constant (Ksv) was also low (4.39 × 10−3 - 5.80 × 10−3 L/mol), and the number of binding sites (n) was small (1.33–1.55). This suggested that, under non-ultrasonic treatment conditions, the dynamic fluorescence quenching process between βC and MP was accompanied by weak transient binding, indicating relatively weak intermolecular interactions. In contrast, ultrasonic treatment led to significant changes. The Ka value for MP-βC-Lipo@PUT increased dramatically to 3.69 × 108–6.76 × 1010 L/mol, an increase of 103–104 times, and the n rose to 2.09–2.65. These changes indicated that ultrasonic treatment induced a fundamental shift from weak dynamic interactions between βC and MP to the formation of complexes with higher frequency and stability. Thermodynamic analysis further supported this conclusion. Following ultrasonic treatment, the ΔG for the binding process between βC and MP decreased significantly (from −29.22 kJ/mol to −63.49 kJ/mol), indicating a marked increase in the spontaneity of their binding. The positive ΔH and ΔS values further suggested that the binding of the complex was primarily driven by hydrophobic interactions. This was likely due to the cavitation effect and microstreaming induced by ultrasound, which unfolded the MP structure (as confirmed by Section 3.4.3 on secondary structure results), thereby providing more binding sites and significantly enhancing the hydrophobic interactions between MP and βC, leading to the formation of more complexes. The peak at 1420 cm−1 is attributed to the stretching vibration of the acyl-COO− groups, and the peak at 1035 cm−1 corresponds to the C—O stretching vibration of the amide carbonyl groups (Chen et al., 2020). In βC monomers, the peak at 3028 cm−1 is assigned to the stretching vibration of alkene CH bonds, and the peak at 2940 cm−1 represents the asymmetric stretching vibration of methyl C—H bonds. The peak at 1580 cm−1 corresponds to the characteristic vibrations of the B-ring skeleton, while the peak at 1460 cm−1 represents the symmetric C—H bending vibration of alkenyl methylene groups. The peak at 965 cm−1 denotes the out-of-plane bending vibration of trans-conjugated alkenes (-CH=CH-) (Heriyanto et al., 2021). In the Lipo monomer, the peak at 3272 cm−1 corresponds to O—H stretching vibrations, and the peaks at 2920 cm−1 and 2855 cm−1 correspond to C—H stretching vibrations associated with hydrophobic interactions, characteristic of the bilayer membrane structure of liposomes. The peak at 1730 cm−1 corresponds to the phospholipid ester carbonyl stretching vibration of phospholipid headgroups, while the peak at 1472 cm−1 indicates C—H bending vibrations. The peak at 1060 cm−1 corresponds to the symmetric stretching vibration of the phospholipid characteristic PO₂− groups, and the peak at 818 cm−1 relates to the asymmetric stretching vibration of -N+(CH₃)₃ groups (Zhang et al., 2024).

In the FTIR spectra of MP-βC-Lipo, the stretching vibration peaks of O—H, N—H, and = C-H shifted to 3300 cm−1 compared to the monomers MP, βC, and Lipo (3265, 3028, and 3272 cm−1, respectively), indicating the formation of numerous hydrogen bonds between the three components to form the complex (Qu et al., 2025b). Compared to the MP monomer, MP-βC-Lipo exhibited an amide carbonyl CO stretching peak at 1670 cm−1, confirming the presence of MP within the complex. This peak showed a blue shift from 1658 cm−1 to 1670 cm−1, indicating enhanced binding strength between MP and the other components. The characteristic βC peaks at 1580, 1380, and 965 cm−1 disappeared in MP-βC-Lipo, suggesting βC encapsulation within the liposome. Compared to the Lipo monomer, MP-βC-Lipo exhibited lipid C—H deformation vibration peaks at 2920 and 2850 cm−1, phospholipid ester carbonyl CO stretching at 1730 cm−1, C—H bending at 1470 cm−1, symmetric PO₂ stretching at 1062 cm−1, and asymmetric stretching of the choline group at 822 cm−1. These characteristic peaks collectively confirmed the presence of a lipid bilayer structure within the complex. Notably, the C—H deformation vibration associated with hydrophobic interactions in MP-βC-Lipo red-shifted from 2855 cm−1 in the Lipo monomer to 2850 cm−1, while the C—H bending peak shifted from 1472 cm−1 to 1470 cm−1. This indicated that the binding of MP and βC to Lipo weakened the vibrational and bending frequencies of the liposome's C—H bonds, suggesting that ultrasonic treatment enhanced hydrophobic interactions among the three components. This was supported by studies showing that strengthened hydrophobic interactions can stabilize the electron cloud distribution of C—H bonds, reducing their vibrational frequency and causing a red shift in the wavenumber (Liu et al., 2023). Furthermore, the vibrational peak of the polar regions of phosphatidylcholine in MP-βC-Lipo shifted from 818 cm−1 in the Lipo monomer to 822 cm−1, indicating possible electrostatic interactions between MP and βC with the choline moiety of the phospholipid (Sarabandi et al., 2019). Following ultrasonic treatment, the peak corresponding to O—H or N—H stretching vibrations in MP-βC-Lipo@PUT shifted from 3300 cm−1 to 3310 cm−1, while the peak for phospholipid ester carbonyl CO stretching shifted from 1730 cm−1 to 1735 cm−1. Additionally, the peak for the symmetric PO₂− stretching vibration of phospholipids shifted from 1062 cm−1 to 1066 cm−1, suggesting that ultrasonication enhanced hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions between MP, βC, and Lipo. This result was consistent with another study (Ma et al., 2025), which demonstrated a blue shift in the O—H stretching vibration peak in ultrasonically treated soybean peptide aggregates, indicating enhanced hydrogen bond formation. Moreover, the C—H bending peak shifted from 1470 cm−1 to 1463 cm−1, indicating that ultrasonication strengthened the hydrophobic interactions among MP, βC, and Lipo. This was likely due to ultrasonication causing MP structural unfolding, which exposed more hydrophobic binding sites, facilitating interactions with βC and Lipo. An earlier study similarly observed enhanced hydrophobic interactions between β-lactoglobulin and chlorogenic acid following ultrasonication (Liu et al., 2022). To further illustrate the MP-βC-Lipo interactions, a molecular interaction schematic based on FTIR results was presented in Fig. 3D. This diagram revealed that the interactions consist primarily of hydrogen bonds (blue), electrostatic forces (yellow), and hydrophobic interactions (red). In summary, FTIR results indicated that ultrasonic treatment altered MP's structure, enhancing hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and hydrophobic forces among MP, βC, and Lipo, promoting a stable complex formation. This conclusion was further supported by the fluorescence quenching results of βC and MP (Section 3.4.1), which confirmed the presence of intermolecular interactions leading to the formation of non-fluorescent complexes. Molecular docking simulations between MP and βC also corroborated this finding (Fig. 3E). Compared with the non-ultrasonicated MP-βC, ultrasonic treatment increased the van der Waals energy (ΔEvdw), polar solvation energy (ΔGpolar), and nonpolar solvation energy (ΔGnonpolar) of the MP-βC@PUT by 12.19%, 6.38%, and 17.73%, respectively. A greater absolute value of these parameters indicates stronger intermolecular interactions and enhanced structural stability. These results suggested that various binding energies, including van der Waals interactions, hydrogen bonding, electrostatic forces, and hydrophobic interactions, play critical roles in stabilizing the MP-βC complex.

Secondary structure

A higher proportion of α-helix and β-sheet content within a protein's secondary structure suggests a more compact conformation, whereas a higher proportion of disordered coils indicates a looser structure. Table S1 shows that, compared to MP monomers, MP-βC-Lipo exhibited significantly reduced α-helix and β-sheet content (p < 0.05), with decreases of 2.24% and 1.04%, respectively. β-turn content remained unchanged (p > 0.05), while disordered coils increased significantly by 3.65% (p < 0.05). These findings suggested that interactions with βC and Lipo altered MP's secondary structure, leading to a more disordered conformation and exposing additional binding sites for interactions. An earlier study similarly observed changes in the secondary structure of soybean protein isolate hydrolysates upon encapsulation in liposomes, suggesting that such structural transitions are partly attributable to liposomal encapsulation (Chen et al., 2022). In comparison with MP-βC-Lipo, the secondary structure of MP in MP-βC-Lipo@PUT was significantly altered after ultrasonic treatment (p < 0.05). The α-helix and β-sheet contents further decreased by 1.60% and 1.96%, respectively, while disordered coils increased by 4.13%, indicating that ultrasonication resulted in a more disordered MP structure. These results supported the conclusion that ultrasonication increased MP's SASA (Section 3.2.2). This suggested that ultrasonic treatment induced changes in MP's secondary structure, leading to a more loosely packed conformation that facilitated stronger binding with βC and Lipo.

Particle size, zeta potential, and microstructure

Fig. 3F illustrates that, compared to the untreated control, ultrasonic treatment significantly reduced the particle size of MP-βC-Lipo@PUT to 230 nm, reflecting a decrease of 70.63%. Additionally, the polydispersity index (PDI) was markedly reduced, increasing by 14.3% (p < 0.05). These results indicated that ultrasonication effectively reduced the particle size, narrowed the particle size distribution, and enhanced the dispersion stability of the system. This was due to the cavitation effect of ultrasound, which facilitated particle size reduction and demonstrated effective control over particle size (Sun, Roos, & Miao, 2024), as evidenced by the improved dispersion and stability of the liposomal system. Compared to MP-βC-Lipo, ultrasonic treatment also significantly lowered the Zeta potential of MP-βC-Lipo@PUT to −15.39 mV (p < 0.05). This was attributed to ultrasonic cavitation, which altered the conformation of MP (Section 3.4.1), exposing more negatively charged groups, thereby decreasing the potential. This resulted in enhanced interparticle repulsion, preventing aggregation (Zhang et al., 2024) and promoting the stability of the system.

Figs. 3G-Fig. 4, Fig. 3G-5 demonstrate that, in contrast to MP-βC-Lipo (Figs. 3G-Fig. 1, Fig. 3G-2), ultrasonic treatment significantly reduced the size of MP-βC-Lipo@PUT, leading to a more uniform and dispersed distribution of liposomal particles. This observation was consistent with the particle size data presented in Fig. 3F. The reduction in size and the increased particle count from ultrasonication contributed to an increased surface area of the system, which could improve the transmembrane absorption and transport capacity of the liposomes in the small intestine (Chen et al., 2023). Furthermore, Fig. 3G-2 reveals that in MP-βC-Lipo, the majority of MP particles remained in the aqueous phase rather than being encapsulated in liposomes. In contrast, the decreased red fluorescence intensity in the aqueous phase of MP-βC-Lipo@PUT (Fig. 3G-5) suggested that most MP particles had been successfully encapsulated within liposomes, confirming that ultrasonication improved MP encapsulation efficiency. These findings showed that the cavitation effects induced by ultrasonication effectively reduced the size by 70.63%, resulting in a larger number of more dispersed and stable liposomal particles, which enhanced their capacity to encapsulate active substances. To further validate these findings at the morphological level, SEM was employed. As clearly shown in Fig. 3G-6, ultrasonic treatment made the liposomal structure into a lot of smaller, well-defined, and discrete particles. Compared to the morphologically heterogeneous and larger aggregates of MP-βC-Lipo (Fig. 3G-3), this visually apparent reduction in particle size and improvement in uniformity were consistent with the findings in Fig. 3F, G-Fig. 4, Fig. 3G-5.

Thermal characteristics

Fig. 3H shows that the weight loss for MP primarily occurred between 70 °C and 400 °C, while for βC, it spanned from 70 °C to 450 °C. In contrast, the weight loss zones for both MP-βC-Lipo and MP-βC-Lipo@PUT were observed between 250 °C and 350 °C. Fig. 3I reveals a minor mass loss peak for MP at 94.5 °C, which was attributed to water volatilization. The obvious melting point of MP was observed at 298.5 °C. βC exhibited maximum thermal loss at 305.1 °C, which was primarily due to significant desorption of coking products at elevated temperatures, resulting in a pronounced thermogravimetric loss. For Lipo, thermal decomposition of lecithin occurred at 320.5 °C (More & Arya, 2024). Compared to the monomers MP, βC, and Lipo, the thermal degradation temperature of MP-βC-Lipo increased to 326.7 °C, likely due to strong interactions between MP, βC, and Lipo, as confirmed in Section 3.4.2. This enhanced the thermal stability of the liposomal complex. Compared to MP-βC-Lipo, MP-βC-Lipo@PUT exhibited a higher maximum thermal weight loss temperature (331 °C) and a reduced weight loss rate (0.26%) following sonication. This improvement might be attributed to ultrasound-enhanced binding forces among MP, βC, and Lipo, as confirmed by FTIR results in Section 3.4.2, which further enhanced thermal stability.

These structural analyses elucidated the mechanism by which ultrasound modulated MP structure and the intermolecular interactions within the complex. Ultrasonic treatment altered the conformation of MP, enhanced fluorescence quenching, and unfolded secondary structure remarkably. This looser structure strengthened hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and hydrophobic forces between MP, βC, and Lipo, further facilitating the formation of the liposomal complex. These findings were consistent with molecular dynamics and molecular docking simulations (Sections 3.2–3.3), which explained the increased encapsulation efficiency of βC and MP after ultrasonic treatment (Section 3.1). Additionally, ultrasonic treatment significantly reduced the size of the liposomal complex by 70.63%, resulting in a more dispersed, uniform, and stable system. This enhanced thermal stability of the system's potential for encapsulating and delivering bioactive substances.

Effects of pulsed ultrasonic treatment on liposome stability

Temperature stability

As shown in Fig. 4A and B, when the temperature increased to 65 °C, the retention of free MP and βC decreased significantly (p < 0.05) by 56.58% and 77.63%, respectively, compared to the control group (25 °C). In contrast, the retention rates of MP and βC in MP-βC-Lipo were significantly higher by 17.43% and 39.97%, respectively (p < 0.05), compared to those of free MP and βC. This phenomenon might be attributed to the fact that high temperatures promoted peptide degradation, while the multiple conjugated double bonds in βC molecules rendered it temperature-sensitive. After liposome encapsulation, the bilayer membrane of MP-βC-Lipo formed a protective barrier for the bioactive substances (Wang et al., 2024), thus improving the retention of MP and βC. Ultrasonic treatment further increased the retention of MP and βC in MP-βC-Lipo@PUT, with increases of 24.88% and 10.22%, respectively, compared to MP-βC-Lipo (p < 0.05). This enhancement was because ultrasonic treatment further optimized the structure and thermal stability of the system (Sections 3.4.1–3.4.5), resulting in the highest retention rates of MP and βC.

The particle size results in Fig. 4C showed particle aggregation induced by high temperature (65 °C). Compared to 25 °C, the sizes of free MP, free βC, and non-ultrasonicated MP-βC-Lipo increased by 267.61 nm, 425.29 nm, and 155.00 nm, respectively (p < 0.05). This phenomenon occurred because high temperatures caused intense molecular motion, increasing the likelihood of particle collisions and subsequent aggregation. In comparison to free MP and βC, the liposome encapsulation provided a protective effect that alleviated the aggregation tendency of MP-βC-Lipo particles. Furthermore, MP-βC-Lipo@PUT, prepared by ultrasonic treatment, exhibited smaller sizes than free MP, free βC, and MP-βC-Lipo. This might be attributed to the fact that MP-βC-Lipo@PUT showed the largest absolute Zeta potential (p < 0.05), as presented in Fig. 4D, which maintained strong mutual repulsion between particles, thereby preventing aggregation (Zhang et al., 2024). As a result, the highest retention rates of MP and βC and the smallest particle size were maintained at high temperatures.

Oxidative stability

Fig. 4E and F demonstrate that MP monomers exhibited flocculation by day 9 of storage, resulting in a significant 43.46% reduction in the MP retention compared to day 0 (p < 0.05). Similarly, Fig. 4E and G show the precipitation of βC monomers by day 10 of storage, with a significant 74.49% decrease in the βC retention compared to day 0 (p < 0.05). The flocculation of MP was likely attributed to prolonged storage at 37 °C, which altered the peptide structure, reduced its stability, and induced flocculation. βC, which contains conjugated double bonds that readily delocalize electrons, is particularly susceptible to oxidation by dissolved oxygen during extended storage at 37 °C. This led to electron abstraction from the double bond structure, disrupting its stability. Following liposome encapsulation, the stability of MP-βC-Lipo was significantly extended to 12 days, with flocculation occurring thereafter. At day 9, MP retention in MP-βC-Lipo increased by 17.70% compared to MP monomers at the same time point. By day 10, βC retention in MP-βC-Lipo significantly improved by 50.32% (p < 0.05) compared to βC monomers at the same time. These results suggested that liposomal encapsulation enhanced the oxidative stability of MP and βC. This might be attributed to the confinement of MP and βC within the lipid bilayer, limiting their exposure to oxygen (Liu, Lin, Wei, Hu, & Wang, 2024) and thus improving stability and retention rates. Ultrasonication further extended the stability of MP-βC-Lipo@PUT to 17 days, during which the retention rates of MP and βC were significantly increased by 33.59% and 17.61%, respectively (p < 0.05) compared to non-ultrasonicated MP-βC-Lipo at day 12. This was due to the relatively large particle size of non-ultrasonicated MP-βC-Lipo (Fig. 4H), which tended to induce aggregation and facilitated the leakage of MP and βC from the liposomes at elevated temperatures, thereby reducing retention rates. In contrast, ultrasonication significantly reduced the particle size of MP-βC-Lipo@PUT (Fig. 4H), resulting in a 48.12% decrease in particle size at day 17 compared to MP-βC-Lipo at day 12 (p < 0.05). A smaller particle size helps mitigate aggregation, thus prolonging the stability of MP-βC-Lipo@PUT during oxidative storage. Moreover, Fig. 4I illustrates that on day 12, the absolute zeta potential of MP-βC-Lipo@PUT was 41.17% higher than that of non-ultrasonicated MP-βC-Lipo (p < 0.05), suggesting that ultrasonication enhanced the repulsive interactions between particles. This increased repulsion effectively prevented liposome aggregation during storage (Zhang et al., 2024), thus enhancing the stability of MP-βC-Lipo@PUT. This finding was consistent with the smaller particle size of MP-βC-Lipo@PUT observed in Fig. 4H.

MDA is a hallmark of lipid peroxidation, and its content serves as an indicator of lipid oxidation (Liu, Liang, Liu, Rashid, et al., 2024). As shown in Fig. 4J, during the first 6 days of storage, MDA content in MP-βC-Lipo@PUT was higher than in non-ultrasonicated MP-βC-Lipo. This was because ultrasound generated small quantities of hydroxyl and hydrogen radicals (Shishkina et al., 2023), which could slightly oxidize phospholipids, leading to an increase in MDA content. A previous study also found that ultrasonic treatment can lead to higher MDA content in emulsions during the early storage phase (Liu, Liang, Liu, Rashid, et al., 2024). However, after the 6th day, the MDA content of MP-βC-Lipo@PUT became significantly lower than that of non-ultrasonicated MP-βC-Lipo (p < 0.05). By day 12, MDA content in MP-βC-Lipo@PUT was 15.47% lower than in non-ultrasonicated MP-βC-Lipo. This might be attributed to the larger particle size (Fig. 4H) and lower zeta potential (Fig. 4I) of non-ultrasonicated MP-βC-Lipo, which led to liposome aggregation and accelerated lipid oxidation upon exposure to external factors, such as oxygen. Conversely, MP-βC-Lipo@PUT optimized by ultrasound demonstrated higher βC and MP retention rates. Additionally, the encapsulated βC and MP scavenged free radicals, such as alkoxyl radicals generated during lipid oxidation (Iino et al., 2021), which slowed down lipid oxidation and reduced MDA content. In summary, during accelerated oxidative storage at 37 °C, compared with the free monomers, liposome-encapsulated MP-βC-Lipo exhibited a longer stable storage period and higher retention rates of bioactive substances. Furthermore, MP-βC-Lipo@PUT, treated with ultrasound, exhibited a further extended oxidative stability period (with no flocculation observed within 17 days) compared with non-ultrasonicated MP-βC-Lipo. At day 17, MP-βC-Lipo@PUT achieved the highest retention rates for MP (85.57%) and βC (88.60%) and exhibited lower MDA content. These results suggested that ultrasonic treatment effectively reduced the exposure of bioactive substances to oxygen, thereby protecting them from oxidative degradation. Consequently, MP-βC-Lipo@PUT demonstrated superior oxidative storage stability and higher retention rates of bioactive substances.

Light stability

Fig. 4K and L demonstrate that MP exhibited flocculation after storage for 9 days. Compared to the control (0 day), the retention rate of the MP-lighted sample decreased significantly by 40.99% (p < 0.05). In contrast, the MP-dark sample showed a significant improvement of 3.30% in retention rate relative to the MP-lighted sample (p < 0.05). Fig. 4K and M indicate that βC exhibited flocculation after storage for 12 days. Compared with the control, the retention rate of the βC-lighted sample decreased significantly by 73.99%, while the retention rate of the βC-dark sample represented a significant improvement of 9.67% compared to the βC-lighted sample (p < 0.05). The light exposure studies on MP and βC monomers indicated that both underwent photodegradation. Certain chemical bonds within MP molecules (e.g., peptide bonds) and amino acids (e.g., cysteine and tryptophan) readily transition to excited states upon exposure to ultraviolet and blue light. These excited-state molecules are more prone to chemical reactions, such as oxidation or photochemical decomposition, leading to photodegradation of MP and reduced retention rates (Qu, Guo, et al., 2025). The light-induced degradation of βC is primarily associated with its conjugated double-bond structure. In the ground state, the electron cloud between the double bonds of the βC molecule is conjugated. Upon light exposure, the βC molecule absorbs photon energy, causing electrons to transition from the ground state to an excited state. The excited-state molecules possess higher energy, with an altered electron cloud distribution on the double bond, rendering the bond angle and length between carbon atoms unstable. This instability facilitates the rotation of the double bond, triggering a transition from the all-trans to the cis isomer of βC. Research indicates that the Z-isomers of β-carotene are more thermodynamically unstable and more susceptible to degradation (Honda, 2022), thus reducing the retention rate of βC under light exposure. After encapsulation within liposomes, the stability of the MP-βC-Lipo-lighted sample was significantly extended to 14 days (flocculation was observed beyond this point). Compared to the MP-dark sample at day 9, MP retention was significantly increased by 12.80% (p < 0.05); compared to the βC-dark sample at day 12, βC retention was significantly increased by 34.96% (p < 0.05). This improvement was attributed to the phospholipid bilayer of liposomes, which blocked some light, thereby reducing the chance of MP and βC absorbing light energy and entering a highly reactive excited state. Additionally, liposome encapsulation reduced the contact between MP, βC, and oxygen, decreasing oxidative reactions and delaying oxidative degradation. Consequently, the retention rate of active substances was higher compared to monomers. On day 14, compared to the MP-βC-Lipo-lighted sample, the retention rates of MP and βC in the MP-βC-Lipo-dark sample increased by 3.55% and 7.33%, respectively (p < 0.05). This suggested that while liposomes provided protection, the stability of MP-βC-Lipo remains affected by light exposure, likely due to its larger particle size and lower zeta potential (Fig. 4N and O). Compared to the non-ultrasonicated MP-βC-Lipo, the ultrasonicated MP-βC-Lipo@PUT remained stable throughout the 20-day observation period. Compared to the MP-βC-Lipo-dark sample at day 14, the retention rates of MP and βC in the MP-βC-Lipo@PUT-dark sample were significantly increased by 10.90% and 15.78%, respectively (p < 0.05) on day 20. This indicated that ultrasonication significantly improved the photostability of the liposomes. As shown in Fig. 4N and O, this improvement was attributed to the fact that ultrasonic treatment reduced the particle size to 720.89 nm and increased the zeta potential to −3.82 mV. According to the study by Liu, Liang, Liu, Rashid, et al. (2024), the smaller droplet size was proven to be beneficial for improving emulsion stability. In conclusion, liposome-encapsulated MP-βC-Lipo samples exhibited a longer stable storage period and higher retention rates compared to the free monomer groups under both light and dark conditions at 25 °C. Furthermore, ultrasonically treated MP-βC-Lipo@PUT samples demonstrated an extended storage period. Specifically, compared to the non-ultrasonicated MP-βC-Lipo-dark sample at day 14, the MP-βC-Lipo@PUT-dark sample at day 20 exhibited the highest MP retention rate (89.56%) and βC retention rate (93.76%). These results suggested that liposome encapsulation partially delayed photodegradation, while ultrasonication further enhanced the light storage stability of the liposomes.

Through the stability analyses, the following key findings were obtained: within the temperature range of 35–65 °C, the ultrasonically treated MP-βC-Lipo@PUT exhibited the highest retention rates of MP (85.74%) and βC (72.56%) at 65 °C. This indicated that ultrasonic treatment effectively enhanced the thermal stability of active substances. During accelerated oxidation at 37 °C, ultrasonic treatment further extended the oxidative stability period, resulting in the highest retention rates of MP (85.57%) and βC (88.60%) and lower MDA content. This result demonstrated that liposome encapsulation and ultrasonic treatment reduced the exposure of active substances to oxygen, effectively protecting them from oxidative degradation. Under storage at 25 °C, both in light and in the dark, ultrasonication extended the stability period and improved the retention rates of active components. On day 20, the MP-βC-Lipo@PUT-dark sample exhibited the highest MP retention rate (89.56%) and βC retention rate (93.76%), indicating that liposomal encapsulation and ultrasonic treatment retarded the photodegradation of active substances and enhanced the light stability of the liposomes. Overall, it demonstrated enhanced environmental stability of the pulsed ultrasonic-assisted nano-liposome encapsulation system, showing resistance to high temperatures, oxidation, and light exposure, compared to both MP and βC monomers and non-ultrasonicated liposomal encapsulation. This was attributed to the optimized MP structure (Section 3.2), the enhanced interactions between MP, βC, and Lipo molecules (Sections 3.3–3.4), the reduced particle size, and the improved dispersion stability (Section 3.4.4) within the pulsed ultrasonic-assisted nano-liposome encapsulation system.

Conclusion

This study developed a novel nano-liposome encapsulation system based on pulsed ultrasonication. Under optimized ultrasonic parameters (frequency: 28 kHz, power: 300 W, pulse ratio: 15/5 s/s, duration: 9 min), the system achieved high co-encapsulation efficiencies of 80.22% for MP and 92.22% for βC, along with an antioxidant activity of 55.30% and a production yield of 42.63%. The encapsulated formulations exhibited significantly improved environmental stability, including resistance to high temperature, oxidation, and light exposure, compared to MP and βC monomers and non-ultrasonicated liposomes. Moreover, pulsed sonication significantly reduced energy consumption by 25% relative to continuous sonication, underscoring its potential for cost-effective industrial applications. These enhancements were attributed to the synergistic effects between encapsulation and ultrasonication, which optimized the MP structure, strengthened the interactions among MP, βC, and liposomes, and reduced particle size while improving dispersion stability. These factors collectively contributed to the higher encapsulation efficiency and superior activity and stability of the system. The underlying ultrasonic promotion mechanism was further corroborated through molecular simulation and spectroscopic analyses. In conclusion, the proposed pulsed ultrasonication-assisted liposome offers a viable and efficient method for co-encapsulating active ingredients of different polarities, with improved stability and reduced energy input, indicating strong potential for applications in functional foods and beverages.

The present study effectively demonstrated the efficiency and energy-saving benefits of pulsed ultrasonic treatment in the fabrication of co-encapsulated nanoliposomes. Several strengths and limitations could be identified. Notably, compared with the current literature, key advantages included a significant improvement in encapsulation efficiency and bioactivity, reduced energy consumption, enhanced stability under environmental stresses, and valuable molecular-level insights into the underlying mechanisms. However, certain limitations persisted, such as potential variability in effectiveness across different bioactive compounds, inadequate assessment of long-term stability, pH stability, and water presence stability, as well as a need for more detailed characterization of release profiles and scalability for industrial applications. Looking forward, future work could explore integrating the current nano-encapsulation approach with emerging strategies, such as deep eutectic solvent (DES)-based tuning of biopolymer matrices. This combination may facilitate the design of advanced hybrid systems (such as nanoliposome-loaded, DES-structured chitosan hydrogels) that not only address the existing limitations regarding controlled release and long-term, pH, and water presence stability but also introduce scaffold-like functions for sophisticated biomedical applications. Such research directions would further enhance the functionality, applicability, and translational potential of bioactive co-encapsulation platforms.
