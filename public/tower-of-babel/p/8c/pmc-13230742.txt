Mechanical performance and microstructural behavior of ultrasonically welded PVC-coated polyester fabric seams at subzero temperature
Authors: Ghazaleh Eskandarnia, Sayyed Mahdi Hejazi, Parham Soltani, Mohammad Mashayekhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUltrasonic welding is increasingly used to join PVC-coated polyester fabrics in membrane structures where seam reliability is critical, particularly under cold-service conditions. This study systematically investigates the effects of welding pressure (0.5,1.5 and 2.5 bar), coating thickness (0.6 and 0.8 mm), seam margin (6 and 12 mm), and temperature (20 °C and − 20 °C) on the mechanical performance and failure behavior of continuously ultrasonically welded seams. A full-factorial experimental design was employed, and seam strength (N/5 cm), extension at failure, work of rupture, and seam efficiency were evaluated using load–extension analysis. Increasing welding pressure from 0.5 to 2.5 bar produced a 2.5–5 fold increase in seam strength and substantially enhanced energy absorption. Increasing thickness from 0.6 to 0.8 mm improved seam strength. Furthermore, at a pressure of 2.5 bar, increasing the seam margin from 6 to 12 mm raised the seam strength from 546.0 to 1190.4. At − 20 °C, seams exhibited higher strength but reduced ductility, reflecting a stiffer and more brittle response. Fractographic analysis revealed a transition from predominantly adhesive failure at low pressure to cohesive failure at 2.5 bar, consistent with improved interfacial fusion. FTIR and EDS analyses confirmed that ultrasonic welding primarily induced morphological rather than major chemical changes. Regression-based predictive models were developed and validated, demonstrating reliable strength prediction under independent processing conditions. The results provide quantitative guidance for optimizing ultrasonic welding parameters to ensure durable seam performance in PVC-coated textile structures operating under both ambient and subzero environments.Keywords: Ultrasonic welding, PVC-coated polyester fabric, seam strength, low-temperature performance, failure mechanism, SEM fractographySubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Membrane structures have attracted increasing attention in recent years due to their lightweight nature, architectural formability, high tensile strength, and environmental durability. Among the materials used, polyester fabrics coated with polyvinyl chloride (PVC) are widely employed in tensile membranes, awnings, and temporary coverings because of their favorable mechanical performance, weather resistance, and cost-effectiveness. These technical textiles exhibit anisotropic and nonlinear mechanical behavior, which depends strongly on the weave pattern of the fabric substrate, the microstructure of the polyester yarns, and the properties of the polymeric coating, as well as on ambient conditions such as temperature and humidity1,2.

Due to the limited width of manufactured membrane rolls, joining of fabric panels is unavoidable in practical applications. The performance and durability of the entire structure thus depend heavily on the quality of these joints. Among the available joining methods, ultrasonic welding (USW) has emerged over the past two decades as an advanced alternative to traditional sewing and adhesive bonding for thermoplastic-coated fabrics. In this technique, high-frequency mechanical vibrations (typically around 20 kHz) are applied under controlled pressure, generating localized heating that softens or melts the PVC coating at the interface, thereby producing a solid-state bond upon cooling. Ultrasonic welding offers several advantages, including high processing speed, energy efficiency, and compatibility with automation, making it particularly suitable for the industrial fabrication of large membrane structures3.

To optimize seam performance in coated technical textiles, it is essential to understand how ultrasonic welding parameters influence heat generation, material flow, and joint integrity. Numerous studies have investigated these effects across various thermoplastic and composite systems. Shi et al.4 examined the joining of nylon, cotton, and polyurethane composites and found that no chemical bond was formed within the typical ultrasonic welding range, highlighting the primarily mechanical nature of the bonding mechanism. Foundational studies in ultrasonic welding of thermoplastics have shown that vibration amplitude, welding time, and applied force can influence welding quality and joint strength5–10. In particular, the applied welding force and vibration amplitude determine the amount of heat generated at the joint interface during welding5,6,9. Furthermore, Tao et al.11 demonstrated that welding time is one of the key parameters in the ultrasonic welding process. By varying welding time while keeping other parameters constant, they found that a duration of 0.9 s produced the highest weld strength in carbon-fiber-reinforced PEEK composites. This aligns with other observations that insufficient welding time leaves voids near the fusion zone, reducing effective contact area and causing premature failure, whereas longer weld times promote uniform melt flow, minimize interfacial voids, and yield higher fracture loads (up to 3.10 kN)8. Ghosh and Reddy12 further emphasized that welding speed and time are interdependent parameters that together influence seam strength. Rani et al.13 noted that amplitude and frequency cannot easily be studied independently because of equipment limitations.

Beyond vibration amplitude and welding time, welding pressure also plays a critical role in determining joint quality. Although some studies have regarded pressure as a less influential parameter compared with amplitude or time14 its interaction with other factors has been shown to be highly significant14. Welding pressure serves a dual function: it stabilizes the contact surfaces during vibration to prevent slippage and simultaneously regulates the amount of frictional and viscoelastic heat generated at the interface. Extensive parametric studies indicate that pressure below the optimal range results in weak bonding and low joint strength, whereas excessive pressure can cause over-melting, polymer degradation, and the formation of locally hardened regions15–18. Wang et al.19 demonstrated in the ultrasonic welding of carbon-fiber-reinforced PEEK composites that precise control of pressure reduces interfacial defects and shifts the failure mode from interfacial to cohesive failure within the base material. Similarly, Zhao et al.20 observed through fractographic analysis that CF/PEEK single-lap joints typically fail by adhesive debonding and interlayer separation, with these interfacial defects directly linked to decreased joint strength.

It is important to note, however, that the type of polymer coating and its thermal characteristics strongly influence both the weld formation and the dominant failure mechanisms. For example, in joints of PTFE-coated fabrics, failures are frequently governed by adhesive separation and coating debonding from the textile substrate. In contrast, studies on PVC-coated polyester fabrics have more commonly reported fiber pull-out, fiber breakage, and coating rupture as the main failure modes21. This contrast suggests that adhesive and interfacial failures are less prevalent in PVC-based systems, likely due to the better thermal compatibility and melt bonding of the PVC coating with the polyester substrate.

PVC-coated membrane structures are widely deployed in outdoor environments and are therefore exposed to substantial seasonal temperature variations. The mechanical response of these materials, particularly in welded joint regions, is strongly temperature-dependent. At elevated temperatures, softening of the thermoplastic coating may reduce load-bearing capacity and promote coating–substrate separation, whereas at lower temperatures reduced molecular mobility can increase stiffness and alter failure mechanisms. Previous studies have reported significant changes in tensile strength and fracture strain of PVC-coated fabrics under non-ambient conditions, including coating detachment and mode transitions during tensile loading22,23. These findings demonstrate that temperature plays a critical role in governing the structural integrity of coated textile systems.

Despite the widespread use of PVC-coated polyester fabrics in outdoor membrane structures, the mechanical performance of continuously ultrasonically welded seams under subzero environmental conditions remains insufficiently documented. Existing studies predominantly focus on room-temperature behavior or on thermal aspects of the welding process itself, while systematic evaluation of seam performance in controlled cold environments is limited. More importantly, previous investigations rarely integrate welding process parameters, seam geometry, structural thickness effects, and microstructural failure characterization within a unified low-temperature mechanical framework. Consequently, there remains an incomplete understanding of how cold exposure influences load transfer, structural stiffness, ductility reduction, and failure mechanisms in ultrasonically welded PVC-coated textile seams. This gap is particularly significant for membrane and tensile structures deployed in continental and cold-climate regions, where subzero temperatures are frequently encountered during winter service conditions.

In response to this need, the present study systematically investigates the mechanical and microstructural behavior of continuously ultrasonically welded PVC-coated polyester fabric seams under both ambient (20 °C) and subzero (− 20 °C) conditions. A full-factorial design of experiments is employed to quantify the individual and interactive effects of welding pressure, coating thickness, seam margin, and temperature on seam strength, structural stiffness, and work to rupture. The mechanical findings are corroborated through fractographic (FE-SEM), elemental (EDS), and chemical (FTIR) analyses, enabling direct correlation between welding parameters, microstructural evolution, and failure mechanisms. Furthermore, regression-based predictive models are developed and independently validated to provide quantitative guidance for seam design and process optimization in cold-environment textile applications.

Materials and methods

Preparation of PVC-coated fabric samples

Samples were prepared from a plain-weave polyester (PET) monofilament fabric. The fabric was coated with a polyvinyl chloride (PVC) paste supplied by Satex Co. at two nominal coating thicknesses. The coating was done under controlled laboratory conditions by a knife-over-roll (doctor blade) technique. In this technique, the blade gap was adjusted precisely to give uniform thickness in both machine and cross-machine directions of the fabric. After coating, the samples were cured in a hot-air oven at 170 °C for 5 min to stabilize the PVC layer on the base fabric. The coated samples were allowed to cool and equilibrate to ambient laboratory temperature. Thickness was measured in accordance with ISO 2286-3:201624 by means of a circular presser foot having a diameter of (contact area 1000 mm2 under 1.0 kPa with dwell time 30 s). Test pieces were conditioned before testing in accordance with ISO 13925. Details of the base material and the two coated materials are listed in Table 1.

Continuous ultrasonic seam welding

The seam configuration followed ISO 491626 specifications for superimposed seams (class S). The overlap between the upper and lower PVC-coated plies positioned the weld at a defined distance from the fabric edge. Two seam margin (SM) levels were examined: 6 mm and 12 mm, where SM represents the distance between the specimen’s free edge and the centerline of the weld (Fig. 1). Seams were produced in a single step using continuous ultrasonic welding. During welding, high-frequency mechanical vibrations were transmitted to the overlap region, where interfacial and molecular friction at the PVC–PVC interface generated heat, softening the thermoplastic coating. Under the applied normal force, the softened PVC deformed and fused, creating a strong interlayer bond. Proper control of ultrasonic power and vibration amplitude was maintained to prevent thermal degradation of the PVC coating and mechanical damage to the polyester yarns.

Schematic of (a) welded specimen positioned between the grips of the tensile testing machine, and (b) superimposed -overlap seam: definition of the seam margin.

The welding system comprised a 20 kHz digital ultrasonic generator operating in constant-amplitude control mode, a titanium sonotrode, and a plain anvil wheel with a diameter of 65 mm. The vibration amplitude was fixed at 30 μm (peak-to-peak) for all experiments, and a constant weld width of 6 mm was maintained for all specimens. The process-controlled parameters included welding pressure and welding speed. The reported pressure values (0.5, 1.5, and 2.5 bar) correspond to the pneumatic line pressure applied to the welding head. This line pressure governs the normal loading at the sonotrode–anvil; however, it does not represent the true interfacial contact stress at the PVC–PVC interface, as the effective contact area depends on wheel geometry and material compliance during welding. Throughout all trials, the welding speed (2 m/min) and vibration amplitude were held constant, while welding pressure was systematically varied according to the experimental design. Welding power and energy were not directly controlled and were allowed to vary automatically in response to material behavior under constant-amplitude conditions.

Measurements

Mechanical testing

Seam strength was evaluated following the guidelines established by in a manner consistent with ISO 13935-1:201427 using the strip method. Welded textile panels, measuring 250 × 300 mm were fabricated. From each panel, strips measuring 50 mm in width were extracted to ensure that the seam line was aligned at the center along the specimen’s length. Tensile tests were conducted utilizing a Santam universal testing machine (STM-50, Iran) under standard conditions (20 ± 2 °C, 65 ± 2% RH) (see Fig. 2). The gauge length was configured to 200 mm, with the crosshead speed set at 100 mm/min.

To evaluate seam performance under cold-service conditions, additional tensile tests were conducted at − 20 °C, a temperature selected to represent a realistic lower-bound winter exposure condition relevant to PVC-coated membrane structures used in continental and cold-climate environments. This temperature lies within a mechanically critical subzero regime for plasticized PVC, where increased stiffness and reduced ductility are expected without inducing an unrealistically brittle response.

Prior to testing, specimens were immersed in a liquid-nitrogen-assisted cooling bath for approximately 10 min until the surface temperature stabilized at − 20 ± 1 °C. Immediately upon reaching the target temperature, the specimens were rapidly mounted in the tensile grips and tested. The total handling and loading time was kept below one minute to minimize temperature rise due to ambient exposure and grip contact. Surface temperature was monitored using a calibrated infrared thermometer immediately before loading and immediately after fracture. These measurements confirmed that the specimen temperature remained within − 20 ± 1 °C during the effective testing period. Tests in which the temperature deviated from this range were discarded and repeated using a new specimen.

Except for the controlled specimen temperature, all other testing parameters, including loading rate and gripping configuration, were identical to those used under standard laboratory conditions (20 ± 2 °C, 65 ± 2% RH).

The work of rupture was determined as the total mechanical energy absorbed by the specimen up to the failure point. This parameter was calculated as the area under the load–extention curve obtained from the tensile test. The numerical integration of the experimental data was performed using the integration method implemented in the data analysis software OriginLab. The integration limits were defined from the initial load application to the point corresponding to complete seam failure. Prior to integration, raw load-extension data were examined to ensure accurate identification of the rupture point in the measurements. To allow comparison between different seam configurations, the calculated work of rupture values were reported as the average of three of test repetitions. The work of rupture parameter was used as an indicator of seam energy absorption capability and overall mechanical reliability of the welded joints.

Fractography

Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE-SEM) fractography was conducted to enhance the understanding of tensile failure mechanisms. The fracture surfaces within the welded areas were analyzed using FE-SEM (FEI Quanta FEG 450, Hillsborough, USA) under high vacuum conditions.

X-ray spectroscopy

To complement the fractographic observations and to assess potential chemical variations induced by different welding pressures, Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) was performed on the welded regions. The elemental analysis was conducted using the EDS detector integrated into the FE-SEM system (FEI Quanta FEG 450, Hillsborough, USA). This elemental assessment provided a complementary basis for interpreting the influence of welding pressure on the material behavior and supported the mechanical and fractographic findings presented in subsequent sections.

FTIR

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, BOMEM FTIR MB-series, MB-100, Hartmann & Braun, Canada) was performed to investigate chemical changes in the polymer at the ultrasonic welded regions compared to the non-welded fabric. The analysis identified functional groups and chemical compositions of the fibers and polymers, revealing chemical structure changes induced by ultrasonic welding.

Design of experiments (DoE)

To assess the effects on seam strength and seam efficiency, a randomized full-factorial experimental design (3 × 2 × 2 × 2) was employed, incorporating four factors: welding pressure P (three levels within the safe operating range: 0.5, 1.5, and 2.5 bar), fabric thickness t (two levels corresponding to PVC-coated fabrics of 0.6 and 0.8 mm), seam margin SM (two levels: 6 and 12 mm, as defined above), and temperature T (two levels: 20 °C and − 20 °C). This design yielded a total of 24 treatment combinations, and the run order was fully randomized. For each treatment, three independent specimens (n = 3) were prepared and tested. The experimental results for each condition are reported as mean ± standard deviation. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was then performed, including all main effects and all two-way and three-way interaction terms. Normality and homoscedasticity of residuals model assumptions were tested. DoE analyses were performed using Minitab 21.4.2, and all plots were generated using OriginPro 2024.

Results and discussion

Fractography

To examine the influence of ultrasonic welding pressure on the microstructure of the joint region FE-SEM was performed on specimens welded at 0.5, 1.5, and 2.5 bar prior to tensile testing. Figure 3 illustrates the corresponding structural variations observed under different pressure conditions. Morphological analysis of these micrographs provides valuable insight into the surface and interfacial transformations that occur during ultrasonic welding.

FE-SEM micrographs of the joint region before tensile testing at 0.8 mm thickness and three welding pressures: (a) 0.5, (b) 1.5, and (c) 2.5 bar at two fifferent magnifications: (left) and (right).

During the welding process, energy is transmitted to the bond interface, causing the polymer coating to heat rapidly and then cool abruptly. These thermal cycles generate transient thermal stresses within the coating, which may lead to the formation of a non-uniform surface morphology in the welded region. Figure 3a shows the effect of 0.5 bar pressure on the quality of the bond and the surface structure. At 1,000X magnification, pores with a circular pattern centered around a common center are visible. These voids indicate incomplete bonding between the surfaces due to the low applied pressure. In situations where the ultrasonic energy is insufficient to fully melt the contact surfaces, this lack of energy results in localized air entrapment, ultimately leading to the formation of surface voids. Compared with the results at 0.5 bar, the FE-SEM images at 1.5 bar (Fig. 3b) show a significant improvement in the surface morphology. The number and size of surface voids are significantly reduced, and the existing voids are discrete. The observation of these discrete microvoids may be attributed to the non-uniform energy distribution at a pressure of 1.5 bar. By applying appropriate pressure (2.5 bar), ultrasonic energy was efficiently transmitted to the bonding area, and as a result, sufficient heat was generated to melt the coating polymer in the bonding region. As can be seen in Fig. 3b, unlike the sample bonded with a welding pressure of 0.5 bar, fewer surface defects were observed, indicating that high pressure increased the adhesion of the bonding layers.

Although the microstructural investigations at different pressures provided valuable insights into weld quality, a more accurate assessment of fracture behavior requires examination of the FE-SEM images obtained after the tensile test. Figure 4 shows the FE-SEM image of the bonded sample under 0.5 bar pressure after failure. In this image, the presence of large voids and discontinuities in the coating indicates that the welding pressure applied was insufficient, resulting in poor interlayer adhesion.

FE-SEM micrographs of the specimen ultrasonically welded at 0.5 bar (Thickness = 0.8 mm) after failure: (a) 1000X, (b) 4000X, and (c) 4000X magnification.

Continuing the fracture microstructure investigation, Fig. 5 shows the fracture surface morphology of the bonded specimen under 1.5 bar pressure. As expected, increasing the welding pressure compared to the 0.5 bar condition resulted in a reduction of interfacial voids and a relative improvement in interlayer adhesion. At this pressure, the residual PVC matrix particles on the monofilaments were significantly higher than in the 0.5 bar specimen, indicating an increased contribution of cohesive failure and more effective structures engagement in the bond region29,30.

FE-SEM micrographs of the specimen ultrasonically welded at 1.5 bar (Thickness = 0.8 mm) after failure: (a) 1000X and (b) 4000X magnification.

Figure 6 illustrates that increasing the welding pressure to 2.5 bar results in a pronounced reduction in the size of interfacial voids at the 1000X magnification. Moreover, the presence of residual PVC particles between the monofilaments indicates a strengthened interfacial bonding, enabling a more uniform transfer of stress across the joint region. The remaining voids are small, separated, and lack any continuous pathways for crack propagation.

FE-SEM micrographs of the specimen ultrasonically welded at 2.5 bar (Thickness = 0.8 mm) after failure: (a) 1000X and (b) 4000X magnification.

At the 4000X magnification, distinct indentations are observed that correspond to the surface topography of the monofilaments. This feature reflects deeper penetration of the PVC coating around the monofilaments and more effective deformation of the matrix during the bonding process. Such shallow fiber imprints confirm that the dominant failure mechanism at this pressure is cohesive failure20,21,29,31–34. Overall, comparison of these observations with those obtained at 0.5 and 1.5 bar demonstrates that increasing welding pressure progressively restricts interfacial crack-growth pathways and significantly enhances interlayer adhesion. At 2.5 bar, this improvement reaches a point where fracture occurs predominantly within the matrix, highlighting the formation of a strong and stable interfacial joint.

To directly compare the influence of coating thickness, samples with 0.6 mm and 0.8 mm thickness were tested under a constant welding pressure of 1.5 bar. FE-SEM observations clearly demonstrate the influence of PVC coating thickness on the fracture morphology at both welding pressures. In specimens with a 0.6 mm thickness under 1.5 bar (Fig. 7), a significant decrease in the residual coating particles on the monofilaments and a notable increase in interfacial voids were observed compared to the 0.8 mm thickness (Fig. 5) previously reported. This reduction in coating particles on the monofilaments reflects weaker interlayer adhesion and a tendency toward interfacial separation (adhesive failure). Increasing the pressure to 2.5 bar in the 0.6 mm specimen (Fig. 8) resulted in a considerable increase in the residual particle of PVC on the monofilament surfaces compared to the 1.5 bar condition, indicating stronger bonding and a higher contribution of cohesive failure. However, even with the increased welding pressure, adhesive failure remained the predominant mechanism in the 0.6 mm specimens31.

FE-SEM micrographs of the specimen ultrasonically welded at 2.5 bar (Thickness = 0.6 mm) after failure: (a) 1000X and (b) 4000X magnification.

To corroborate these fractographic interpretations, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis was performed. At 0.5 bar, the spectrum was dominated by carbon (), accompanied by high oxygen () and moderate chlorine () contents. In contrast, at 2.5 bar, the carbon and oxygen contents decreased to 39.31 and 20.68%, respectively, while the chlorine content increased significantly to . This compositional shift indicates enhanced penetration and redistribution of the PVC coating matrix under higher pressure, resulting in improved interfacial adhesion and reduced surface discontinuities. The elevated chlorine concentration, characteristic of PVC, further confirms stronger bonding, consistent with the FE-SEM observations and mechanical test results.

FTIR analysis of ultrasonically welded samples

FTIR spectroscopy was performed to evaluate possible chemical or physical modifications in the PVC-coated fabrics induced by ultrasonic welding. The results are depicted in Fig. 9. Overall, the shape and positions of the major peaks in both spectra (blue: unwelded, and red: ultrasonically welded) are nearly identical, indicating that the primary chemical structure of the polyester and PVC coating remains largely intact, with no significant signs of degradation15. Nevertheless, several notable differences in peak intensities were observed and are discussed below. A pronounced absorption band at approximately 2927 , assigned to the aliphatic C–H stretching vibrations, exhibits increased absorbance in the welded sample (). This enhancement may reflect redistribution of plasticizers, alternatively changes in crystallinity or molecular orientation caused by localized thermal–mechanical effects during welding. The 600–1200 region contains several overlapping bands originating from multiple components of the formulation. The distinct peak near , corresponding to the C–Cl stretching vibration, serves as a reliable marker for PVC in plasticized formulations35,36. A relative decrease in the intensity of this band in the welded sample may suggest a localized reduction of C–Cl functionality, potentially due to minor dehydrochlorination, or alternatively a local change in the surface abundance of PVC within the probed volume. The most prominent differences between the welded and unwelded samples occur in the 1200–1730 cm−1 region. Key absorptions in this interval include the ester C=O stretching band near , the C–O and C–O–C stretching around , and the CH2 bending vibration at ~1450–1465 cm−1. The consistent reduction in absorbance (increase in transmission) observed for these bands in the welded sample most likely reflects morphological or physical alterations such as local thinning, chain re-orientation, redistribution of the PVC coating, or migration of additives (e.g., plasticizers). Overall, the FTIR results indicate that ultrasonic welding primarily causes physical and morphological changes in the polyester/PVC interface but does not introduce major chemical decomposition or formation of new functional groups.

At a welding pressure of 0.5 bar (Fig. 10a), the load–extension curves for (for both thicknesses and temperatures) exhibit an almost linear behavior up to the moment of failure. Failure occurs at relatively small extension, and the maximum load sustained by the joint is low. This behavior is consistent with adhesive failure between the layers, which is also confirmed by the FE-SEM images: the joint behaves weakly at this pressure and, as a result, the joint fails before it is able to sustain any considerable deformation31. With increasing welding pressure to 1.5 bar (Fig. 10b), both the maximum load and the extension at failure increase relative to 0.5 bar. The load–extension curves nonlinear behavior is observed in the intermediate region before final fracture. This means that before failure, the joint is able to accommodate more deformation. Although at − 20 °C fracture occurs earlier than at 20 °C, even at this lower temperature, the load and the extension before fracture are higher than those of the corresponding specimen at 0.5 bar. These results indicate that at 1.5 bar, the interlayer cohesion has become stronger and the failure mechanism is no longer purely interfacial debonding, but rather a combination of adhesive and cohesive failure. This change in failure mechanism, as also observed in the fracture surface morphology, explains the simultaneous increase in ultimate load and extension at failure. At welding pressure of 2.5 bar (Fig. 10c), a pronounced increase is observed in both the load and the energy absorbed prior to failure. The load–extension curves, particularly for the 0.8 mm thickness tested at 20 °C, reach significantly higher load levels than those obtained at lower pressures, and failure occurs at larger extensions. At − 20 °C, although the extension at failure decreases, the maximum load remains relatively high compared to lower welding pressures, indicating that the seam retains substantial strength despite reduced ductility at low temperature. This behavior shows that at 2.5 bar the welded surface mechanically acts essentially as a continuous region, and its failure is a gradual, high-energy process rather than a sudden interfacial separation. The post-tensile fracture morphology at 2.5 bar confirms these results. In addition to pressure, the effect of coating thickness on joint performance is evident across all tested conditions. The specimens with a thickness of 0.8 mm consistently exhibit higher maximum loads and larger extension at failure compared to the specimens with a thickness of 0.6 mm. FE-SEM images of the fracture surfaces after testing show that in the thicker specimen (0.8 mm thick), remnants of the coating remain on part of the monofilaments, whereas in the thinner specimen (0.6 mm thick), the monofilaments have separated over wider regions, almost bare or with little coating. This microstructural difference accounts for the enhanced load-bearing capacity and deformation capability of the thicker joints. Temperature also has a pronounced effect on the mechanical response. At − 20 °C, for a given extension, higher loads are recorded, reflecting an increased joint stiffness; however, this is accompanied by a significant reduction in the extension at failure. This response indicates a stiffer but more brittle joint behavior at subzero temperature30. In contrast, at 20 °C the joints exhibit a more compliant response, allowing larger extension and greater energy absorption before final failure.

At each pressure level, increasing the seam margin from 6 to 12 mm increases the seam strength. A higher seam margin actually lengthens the stress propagation distance before the seam line and reduces the stress concentration near the seam. In fact, the effect of the seam margin parameter increases with higher welding pressure. The largest difference in seam strength between the 6 mm and 12 mm seam margins, occurs at a pressure of 2.5 bar and a thickness of 0.8 mm. In previous studies, it has been established that at a seam margin of less than 5 mm, the edge of the fabric also enters the fused area, causing shrinkage due to the tensile strength of the structure, and ultimately reducing the strength of the connection. At seams with sema margin of more than 5 mm, the seam is more stable. It should be further stated that the coating layer is an interface to facilitate the transfer of load from the stitching line to the base fabric. A thicker coating increases its capacity to transfer the applied load along its length17,38. It is observed that, tensile testing at -20 °C shows higher seam strength compared to 20 °C for all pressure conditions. The maximum increase in seam strength, is achieved at a pressure of 0.5 bar. Previous studies have also shown that both the modulus and ultimate tensile strength of PVC polymers increase with decreasing temperature39.

From a mechanical standpoint, the work of rupture corresponds to the cumulative energy dissipated through progressive damage mechanisms occurring within the welded joint during loading. These mechanisms may include interfacial debonding between welded layers, plastic deformation of the polymer coating, progressive fibre reorientation and stretching, and final rupture of the load-bearing structure. Consequently, higher work of rupture values indicate an enhanced ability of the seam to sustain deformation and resist crack initiation and propagation prior to failure. The results presented in Fig. 13 demonstrate that the work of rupture of the welded seams is strongly influenced by welding pressure, seam margin, structural thickness, and testing temperature. For specimens with a thickness of 0.6 mm, increasing welding pressure from 0.5 to 2.5 bar leads to a substantial rise in work of rupture under all testing conditions. For example, at a seam margin of 6 mm and a temperature of 20 °C, the absorbed energy increases from approximately 1.03 J to 3.55 J, while at − 20 °C the increase is more pronounced, rising from 1.09 J to 5.02 J. A similar but more significant trend is observed for the 12 mm seam margin, where the work of rupture increases from 1.76 J to 6.57 J at 20 °C and from 1.63 J to 11.19 J at − 20 °C. This improvement in work of rupture is attributed to enhanced seam-bond quality at higher welding pressure. Increased pressure increases the actual contact area between surfaces, decreases microvoids, and promotes greater interdiffusion of polymer chains across the interface; consequently, seam strength increases and the failure mode shifts from interfacial to cohesive failure. This transition enables the material to absorb greater amounts of energy before ultimate fracture. Seam margin also plays a critical role in determining the energy absorption capacity of the welded joints. Increasing the seam margin from 6 mm to 12 mm markedly enhances the work of rupture under all testing conditions. In specimens with a narrow seam margin, stress is localized near the interface, facilitating rapid crack initiation and propagation. Conversely, a larger seam margin distributes the applied load more uniformly across the joint, extends the crack propagation path, and increases the energy required for complete failure. This effect is particularly evident at higher welding pressures, where increasing the seam margin from 6 mm to 12 mm at − 20 °C and 2.5 bar results in more than a twofold increase in absorbed energy. Furthermore, increasing the structural thickness from 0.6 mm to 0.8 mm enhances the work of rupture. At a welding pressure of 2.5 bar and a seam margin of 12 mm, the absorbed energy increases significantly with thickness under both temperature conditions. Increasing coating thickness introduces a larger volume of deformable material within the welded region, thereby increasing the extent of viscoelastic and plastic deformation in the PVC coating and extending the crack propagation path. Consequently, greater mechanical energy is required for fracture. In addition to structural parameters, environmental conditions also significantly influence seam fracture behavior. Temperature affects both the deformation characteristics of the polymer coating and the efficiency of stress transfer across the welded interface. At lower temperatures, reduced polymer chain mobility limits molecular sliding at the bonded interface, increasing resistance to interfacial deformation and improving load transfer efficiency. This behavior contributes to the increased work of rupture observed under sub-zero testing conditions, highlighting the strong influence of environmental factors on the fracture resistance and energy absorption capacity of ultrasonically welded coated textile seams. These findings emphasize the importance of considering service temperature conditions during the design and optimization of ultrasonically welded textile structures.

The data presented in Table 2 indicate that seam efficiency is directly influenced by welding pressure, coating thickness, and seam margin, exhibiting similar trends at both 20 °C and − 20 °C, albeit with different magnitudes. Increasing the welding pressure consistently improves seam efficiency at both temperatures, with this effect becoming more pronounced in samples with greater thickness and larger seam margins. A comparison of the two test temperatures shows that, depending on the configuration, seams may demonstrate higher or lower efficiency at − 20 °C relative to ambient conditions. In certain combinations of thickness and margin, enhanced cohesion at low temperature results in increased efficiency, whereas in others, the reduction in ductility leads to a relative decrease. Overall, the results demonstrate that the mechanical response of the seam is governed by the simultaneous effects of processing conditions and temperature, and that evaluating seam performance requires considering the combined influence of geometric and process parameters rather than any single variable.

Model development and statistical analysis

Regression modeling and sensitivity analysis

A multi-factor linear regression was developed to predict seam strength as a function of welding pressure (P), seam margin (SM), thickness (t), and temperature (T). The final predictive equation for seam strength is provided in Eqs. 2,3. All regression equations represent the actual (uncoded) models, as the variables were mean-centered only to reduce multicollinearity and to improve numerical stability during computation. Temperature was included as a categorical factor (20 °C and − 20 °C). Because its interaction terms were significant for only a subset of the effects, the final model was constructed hierarchically, including all main effects and only the statistically significant interactions. Interaction terms were included following hierarchical modeling principles, retaining all main effects and only statistically significant higher-order interactions.

Future perspectives and limitations

This study provides important insight into how welding pressure, coating thickness, seam margin, and temperature collectively influence the mechanical and microstructural behavior of ultrasonically welded PVC-coated polyester fabrics. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The experiments were conducted using a single welding setup with fixed vibration amplitude and welding speed, which limits the generalization of the findings to other ultrasonic systems. Future work should include additional processing variables, such as sonotrode geometry, energy mode, welding time, and frequency, to establish broader processing guidelines.

Mechanical characterization in this work was restricted to uniaxial tensile loading. Since technical textiles in real applications may experience multiaxial stresses, shear, fatigue, and creep, future studies should evaluate welded seams under more service-representative loading and long-term conditions. Likewise, while FE-SEM, FTIR, and EDS provided meaningful microstructural evidence, advanced three-dimensional and in-situ characterization techniques (e.g., micro-CT, nanoindentation mapping, or in-situ thermal imaging during welding) could offer deeper insight into interfacial bonding mechanisms.

Environmental exposure is another limitation. Only two temperatures (20 °C and − 20 °C) were investigated, whereas real outdoor systems experience wider thermal fluctuations, freeze–thaw cycles, humidity variations, and UV degradation. More extensive environmental conditioning is therefore needed to evaluate long-term durability.

Implications for real-world structures

The results of this study have direct relevance for the design and fabrication of membrane structures, temporary shelters, and protective covers used in cold climates. The combined mechanical and microstructural evidence indicates that a welding pressure near 2.5 bar and a seam margin of at least 12 mm provide a more reliable load-transfer region. However, at subzero temperatures, seams become stiffer and less ductile, meaning they are more susceptible to brittle fracture under dynamic loading or stress concentration. Therefore, engineers should incorporate appropriate safety factors, avoid sharp geometric transitions near seam lines, and account for temperature-dependent reductions in fracture strain when designing welded PVC-coated textile structures. These guidelines bridge the experimental findings with practical design considerations for improving seam reliability in cold-environment applications.

In general, to achieve the highest strength and durability in industrial applications of PVC-coated fabrics, it is recommended to set the welding pressure to around 2.5 bar and the seam margin to around 12 mm. These settings have been shown in tests to provide the best balance between strength, stiffness, and energy absorption. It should be noted that 2.5 bar represents the highest welding pressure evaluated in the present study and should therefore be interpreted as the upper bound of the investigated range rather than a confirmed global optimum. While increased pressure enhances interfacial contact and polymer flow up to a critical level, excessive pressure may lead to coating thinning, localized overheating, polymer degradation, or potential damage to the reinforcing yarns. Consequently, further increases in welding pressure may not necessarily yield additional strength improvements and should be examined cautiously in future investigations to establish a true optimum operating window.
