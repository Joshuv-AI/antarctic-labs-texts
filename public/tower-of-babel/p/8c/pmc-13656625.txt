Diagnostic utility of endoscopic ultrasound features in pancreatic tumors: a retrospective observational study focusing on tumor size and imaging characteristics
Authors: Ming-Sheng Chien, Ting-Chung Wo, Jian-Han Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEndoscopic ultrasound (EUS) is a highly sensitive imaging modality for detecting pancreatic tumors, particularly small lesions. However, its ability to distinguish malignant from benign lesions remains a diagnostic challenge. This study aimed to evaluate the imaging characteristics observed via EUS in differentiating malignancy and to assess the influence of tumor size on diagnostic accuracy. Methods: This retrospective study included 315 patients who underwent EUS evaluation for pancreatic tumors between January 2019 and July 2024. The final diagnosis was established based on surgical pathology, EUS-FNB results, or clinical follow-up ≥ 6 months. Key imaging features assessed included echogenicity, lesion margins, pancreatic duct dilatation, and distal pancreatic atrophy. Tumors were categorized by size (< 2 cm or > 2 cm), and statistical analyses were conducted to identify predictive factors of malignancy.ResultsOf the 315 tumors, 205 (65.1%) were malignant. Five EUS imaging features—hypoechoic pattern, heterogeneous echotexture, irregular margins, pancreatic duct dilatation, and distal pancreatic atrophy—were identified as predictive of malignancy. The ROC-derived cutoff of 3.5 corresponds clinically to ≥ 4 features. AUC was 0.807 (95% CI: 0.759–0.849). Using a ≥ 4-feature threshold, sensitivity was 87.8%, specificity 54.5%, PPV 78.6%, and NPV 69.4%. Subgroup multivariable analysis revealed that heterogeneous echotexture and pancreatic duct dilatation were significant predictors of malignancy in tumors < 2 cm. However, in tumors > 2 cm, no individual feature significantly distinguished malignancy.ConclusionAmong pancreatic tumors, those exhibiting four or more of the five EUS features should be prioritized for malignancy evaluation. In small tumors, heterogeneous echotexture and pancreatic duct dilatation offer valuable diagnostic insight. Conversely, larger tumors present greater diagnostic challenges due to overlapping features on B-mode imaging alone.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05085-w.

Introduction

Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) has emerged as a pivotal imaging modality in the evaluation of pancreatic lesions, transforming the landscape of diagnosis and management. Its high sensitivity enables the precise detection of even small pancreatic tumors, offering a significant advantage over other imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which may fail to detect subtle lesions [1–3]. The ability of EUS to provide high-resolution images of the pancreas, combined with its capacity to perform fine-needle biopsy (FNB) for tissue sampling, makes it an indispensable tool for the early detection and characterization of pancreatic tumors. Early detection of pancreatic cancer is of paramount importance, as it directly influences treatment options and patient prognosis [4]. Detecting tumors at an early stage—when they are potentially resectable—can significantly improve survival rates [4, 5].

However, despite its strength in tumor detection, EUS faces limitations in reliably distinguishing between benign and malignant pancreatic lesions. This differentiation is a critical clinical need, as it guides therapeutic decisions and shapes patient management strategies. Accurate identification of malignancy is essential to avoid unnecessary surgical interventions for benign conditions while ensuring timely and appropriate treatment for malignant tumors [4, 6, 7].

Several EUS imaging characteristics have been identified as potential indicators of malignancy. These include hypoechoic appearance, heterogeneous echotexture, and irregular tumor margins. Secondary features, such as upstream pancreatic duct dilatation and distal pancreatic atrophy, are also frequently associated with malignant tumors [8, 9].

Despite the recognition of these imaging features, differentiating between benign and malignant pancreatic lesions remains complex. Overlapping imaging characteristics can obscure accurate diagnosis, particularly in cases involving small tumors or lesions with atypical presentations [10]. While pathological diagnosis obtained through tissue sampling remains the gold standard for confirming malignancy, the interpretation of EUS images plays a crucial role in the initial assessment and guides the endoscopist's decision-making process [11]. The endoscopist’s impression of the tumor based on EUS imaging is pivotal in determining the subsequent course of action—such as whether to employ advanced imaging techniques like contrast enhancement, proceed with fine-needle biopsy for tissue confirmation, or adopt a watchful waiting approach with imaging follow-up.

Tumor size is an important factor that may influence the accuracy of EUS in distinguishing between benign and malignant pancreatic lesions. Small tumors often present greater diagnostic challenges due to subtle imaging features and difficulty in obtaining adequate tissue samples. Larger tumors may also be difficult to assess, as both benign and malignant lesions can exhibit similar imaging characteristics as they enlarge [12, 13].

This study aims to address the clinical challenge of differentiating benign from malignant pancreatic tumors using EUS imaging. Specifically, it evaluates which EUS imaging features best support this differentiation and investigates how tumor size modifies their diagnostic accuracy. Through detailed analysis of a five-feature EUS model in a size-stratified cohort, this study seeks to refine diagnostic criteria, enhance the precision of EUS-based assessment, and ultimately contribute to improved clinical outcomes for patients with pancreatic tumors.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective study received approval from the Mackay Memorial Hospital Institutional Review Board (IRB No: 25MMHIS003e). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study and use of anonymized data. This study was designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines. It involved a review of electronic medical records for 315 patients who underwent EUS examinations for pancreatic evaluations between January 2019 and July 2024. To ensure data accuracy and consistency, specific inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied (Fig. 1). The final diagnosis was established based on one of the following: (1) surgical pathology, (2) EUS-FNB, or (3) clinical and imaging follow-up for at least 6 months confirming stability of benign lesions. Patients with uncertain final diagnoses or benign lesions without follow-up exceeding six months were excluded. The analysis focused solely on solid tumors; cystic lesions were excluded. In cases where patients had multiple solid lesions, only the largest lesion was included to maintain consistency. With regard to malignancy, metastatic lesions and neuroendocrine tumors were excluded, as the focus was restricted to adenocarcinoma. Collected data included patient demographics, history of chronic pancreatitis, tumor characteristics observed via EUS imaging, FNB tissue confirmation rate, and cancer staging.Fig. 1STROBE flow diagram of patient selection and study cohort

Study endpoints

This study had one primary aim and several secondary objectives. The primary aim was to evaluate EUS imaging characteristics for distinguishing between benign and malignant pancreatic lesions. Secondary objectives included evaluating differences in tumor presentation by size (< 2 cm vs. ≥ 2 cm, corresponding to cancer T staging) and identifying independent predictors of malignancy using univariable and multivariable analyses.

Procedure details

All EUS procedures were conducted by four experienced endoscopists with expertise in image acquisition and interpretation. The procedures were performed using a GF-UCT260 curvilinear echoendoscope (Olympus Optical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The ultrasound systems used included the EU-ME2 and the EU-ME2 Premier Plus, allowing for standard EUS imaging. However, elastography was performed only in a subset of patients due to equipment limitations. To ensure patient comfort and minimize procedural discomfort, conscious sedation was administered via pre-medication with fentanyl and midazolam.

In cases where contrast enhancement was employed, patients who provided consent received Sonazoid (GE Healthcare, USA). The contrast agent was administered at a standard dose of 0.015 mg/kg to enhance tumor visualization.

FNB was performed when tissue sampling was required for diagnostic purposes. A 22-gauge FNB needle was used, with devices including Acquire™ (Boston Scientific, Natick, MA, USA) and TopGain® (Medi-Globe, Achenmühle, Germany). The FNB technique incorporated the fanning method combined with either a stylet slow-pull or low-negative suction technique to optimize tissue acquisition. To facilitate rapid assessment of collected samples, macroscopic on-site evaluation was performed by the endoscopists without on-site cytological review. It is important to note that tissue sampling was omitted in specific clinical scenarios. For patients with resectable cancers, surgical resection was performed without prior tissue acquisition. Additionally, tissue sampling was not conducted in patients with benign tumors confirmed through clinical and imaging assessments.

The management of patients following EUS procedures varied based on the diagnosis. Patients with resectable cancers underwent surgical resection as part of their treatment plans. Those with benign tumors were managed through imaging follow-up, which included modalities such as CT, MRI, or EUS, with the specific choice and frequency determined by clinical judgment and the patient's condition.

In cases of interpretative disagreement, a second experienced endoscopist was consulted, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when approximately normally distributed, and as median with interquartile range (IQR) when non-normally distributed. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages.

Comparisons between groups were conducted using appropriate statistical tests. For continuous variables, the independent-samples t test was used for approximately normally distributed data, whereas the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate based on expected cell counts.

To identify factors associated with malignancy, univariable logistic regression analyses were first performed to assess the association between each variable and malignancy. Variables with p < 0.05 in univariable analysis or deemed clinically relevant were included in multivariable logistic regression models. Collinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), and no significant collinearity was observed among the included variables (all VIFs < 5). Model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the discriminatory performance of EUS features in differentiating benign from malignant tumors. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated as a measure of diagnostic accuracy, and optimal cutoff values were determined based on ROC analysis.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sample size calculation

The primary endpoint of this study was the discriminatory performance of EUS features for malignancy, assessed using ROC curve analysis. Assuming an expected AUC of approximately 0.80 and a malignancy prevalence of around 65%, a total sample size of 315 patients was estimated to provide a sufficiently narrow confidence interval for the AUC estimate, thereby ensuring adequate precision and robustness of the primary analysis.

The tumors included in this study exhibited wide variability in their characteristics. The mean tumor size was 2.87 ± 1.50 cm. Tumors were predominantly located in the pancreatic head (51.4%), which is the most common site for pancreatic adenocarcinoma. The remaining tumors were located in the body (23.2%), tail (17.1%), and uncinate process (8.2%) of the pancreas. In terms of imaging features, 84.8% of tumors appeared hypoechoic. Heterogeneous echotexture was observed in 78.7% of cases, and irregular tumor margins were present in 87.3% of tumors. Secondary findings on EUS imaging included upstream pancreatic duct dilatation in 42.9% of patients and distal pancreatic atrophy in 18.1% of cases. Vessel invasion—a critical indicator of advanced disease—was observed in 18.7% of tumors.

Among the 315 tumors, 205 (65.1%) were confirmed as malignant. EUS-guided fine-needle biopsy (EUS-FNB) was performed in 221 patients, achieving a high diagnostic success rate of 90.5%.

The benign lesions (n = 110) comprised inflammatory masses associated with chronic pancreatitis (n = 89), autoimmune pancreatitis (n = 19), and lesions of unknown etiology (n = 2).

Comparison of tumor characteristics by tumor size

There were no statistically significant differences between the two tumor size groups in terms of age, sex, or history of chronic pancreatitis, indicating that these factors did not significantly influence the likelihood of a tumor being benign or malignant within each size category. However, significant differences were observed in tumor characteristics between the two groups. Heterogeneous echotexture was more frequently observed in larger tumors (91.6%) than in smaller tumors (51%, p < 0.001), suggesting that larger tumors are more likely to exhibit a mixed echotexture pattern. Irregular margins were also more common in larger tumors (91.2% vs. 79%, p = 0.003). Upstream pancreatic duct dilatation and distal pancreatic atrophy—both secondary signs of pancreatic tumors—were significantly more frequent in larger tumors (p = 0.001 and p = 0.004, respectively). These findings suggest that larger tumors are more likely to cause ductal obstruction and parenchymal changes in the surrounding pancreatic tissue.

Comparison with CT imaging

In addition to EUS findings, 27 of the 29 patients with small pancreatic cancer also underwent CT imaging. Among these 27 cases, the lesion was not detected on CT in 9, yielding a CT sensitivity of 66.7% for small tumors. This finding underscores the superior sensitivity of EUS in detecting small pancreatic tumors compared to CT.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that a simple combination of routinely assessed EUS imaging features can provide clinically meaningful discrimination between benign and malignant pancreatic tumors. Rather than relying on individual imaging characteristics, our findings highlight that the cumulative presence of multiple features significantly improves diagnostic performance, achieving an AUC of 0.807. Importantly, the ROC-derived cutoff of 3.5 features translates into a clinically applicable threshold of ≥ 4 features, offering a straightforward and practical approach that can be readily implemented during real-time EUS examination. This model is not intended to replace EUS-FNB, but rather to complement existing diagnostic strategies by providing an initial risk stratification tool to guide further clinical decision-making.

These findings are consistent with prior studies demonstrating the limited specificity of individual EUS features in differentiating pancreatic lesions. While characteristics such as hypoechogenicity or irregular margins have been associated with malignancy, their standalone diagnostic value remains insufficient. Our results extend this concept by showing that a feature-based cumulative approach provides improved discrimination, supporting the integration of multiple imaging parameters in clinical assessment.

To ensure analytical homogeneity, this study deliberately restricted its scope to pancreatic adenocarcinoma and excluded neuroendocrine and metastatic tumors. This decision reflects fundamental differences in tumor origin and biological behavior. Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma arises from the ductal epithelium and is characterized by marked desmoplasia and early ductal involvement, whereas neuroendocrine tumors originate from islet cells of the pancreatic parenchyma and exhibit distinct imaging signatures and clinical trajectories [14, 15]. Metastatic lesions to the pancreas further complicate the diagnostic landscape, as their imaging appearance varies considerably depending on the primary tumor site [16, 17]. Including these heterogeneous tumor types would have confounded feature-based analyses; therefore, maintaining a focused adenocarcinoma cohort was essential for the validity of our findings.

Our findings build upon and extend prior literature on EUS-based malignancy prediction. Previous studies involving patients with chronic pancreatitis identified three key EUS features—hypoechoic pattern, pancreatic duct dilatation, and distal pancreatic atrophy—as predictors of malignancy when more than two were present [8]. Our study, conducted in a predominantly non-chronic pancreatitis population (80.6%), expanded this framework to five features by additionally incorporating heterogeneous echotexture and irregular margins. The broader feature set likely reflects the distinct pathological milieu of a general pancreatic tumor population unconfounded by background parenchymal inflammation. This expanded model achieved an AUC of 0.807, with a sensitivity of 87.8% and specificity of 54.5% at the ≥ 4-feature threshold, indicating that it performs well as a screening tool to flag high-risk lesions warranting further evaluation [18].

A central finding of this study is that tumor size profoundly influences the diagnostic utility of individual EUS features. In tumors ≤ 2 cm, heterogeneous echotexture and pancreatic duct dilatation emerged as robust independent predictors of malignancy on multivariable analysis, likely reflecting the early biological hallmarks of ductal adenocarcinoma—namely, ductal obstruction and heterogeneous cellular proliferation—before extensive secondary changes obscure the primary lesion. Conversely, in tumors > 2 cm, no single EUS feature retained independent discriminative power. This is because, as both benign and malignant lesions enlarge, they increasingly acquire overlapping imaging characteristics—such as heterogeneity from inflammation, fibrosis, or cystic degeneration—that erode feature specificity [19]. These findings underscore a critical clinical implication: the standard EUS feature set, while reliable for overall risk stratification, must be interpreted with caution in larger tumors where B-mode imaging alone is insufficient for definitive characterization.

Pancreatic duct dilatation deserves particular attention as a size-dependent diagnostic marker. In our subgroup analysis, 71% of tumors ≤ 2 cm were ultimately benign; however, among the small malignant tumors, 65.5% exhibited pancreatic duct dilatation—a significantly higher proportion than in benign counterparts. This observation is biologically plausible: pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma arises from the ductal epithelium and can cause early luminal obstruction even when the tumor itself is small, a phenomenon consistent with CT findings showing that an abrupt duct cut-off favors malignancy, whereas a penetrating duct sign is more indicative of a benign process [20]. In contrast, among tumors > 2 cm, pancreatic duct dilatation was also present in 33.3% of benign lesions, likely due to mass effect causing secondary compression of the duct rather than intrinsic ductal involvement. Consequently, pancreatic duct dilatation is most informative as a malignancy predictor in small tumors, where it can alert the clinician to early adenocarcinoma, but loses specificity in larger lesions where extrinsic ductal compression becomes an equally plausible explanation.

The heterogeneous echotexture pattern exhibited analogous size-dependent behavior. In tumors ≤ 2 cm, heterogeneity was a strong independent predictor of malignancy, reflecting the rapid and disorganized growth that characterizes early adenocarcinoma—including uneven cell proliferation, angiogenesis, focal necrosis, hemorrhage, and nascent cystic changes. Benign small lesions, in contrast, tend to maintain a relatively uniform internal architecture due to slower, more organized growth. However, as tumors enlarge beyond 2 cm, both benign and malignant lesions progressively acquire heterogeneous features through secondary processes such as fibrosis, chronic inflammation, and cystic degeneration, rendering echotexture less discriminative in this size range [21]. Together, these data reinforce the principle that the diagnostic weight assigned to any individual EUS feature must account for tumor size.

Given the limitations of B-mode EUS in larger tumors, advanced modalities such as contrast-enhanced EUS (CE-EUS) and elastography represent important adjuncts. CE-EUS enhances tumor characterization by enabling real-time assessment of vascularity: malignant tumors typically exhibit hypoenhancement due to their disorganized and deficient blood supply, whereas benign lesions more often display homogeneous or isoenhancement patterns. EUS elastography complements this by evaluating tissue stiffness—malignant tumors generally appear stiffer than benign ones due to increased cellular density and associated desmoplastic fibrosis [10, 22–24]. These functional imaging properties can help resolve ambiguous cases that B-mode imaging alone cannot reliably classify.

In our study, CE-EUS was not routinely employed because Sonazoid—the contrast agent available at our institution—is offered only on a self-pay basis, limiting its routine use. Nevertheless, when feasible, CE-EUS provides supplementary information that extends beyond the capabilities of conventional B-mode imaging by enabling assessment of tumor perfusion and vascularity [24, 25]. Its broader adoption, however, remains constrained by cost, agent availability, patient contraindications, and operator dependency. Similarly, EUS elastography was not incorporated into our analysis due to equipment limitations, although it represents a promising non-invasive technique that can quantify tissue stiffness and add diagnostic value in indeterminate cases [26, 27]. Future studies incorporating these modalities alongside B-mode feature scoring may further refine risk stratification, particularly for tumors exceeding 2 cm where conventional EUS features are less reliable.

EUS does not operate in diagnostic isolation, and integration with cross-sectional imaging remains essential. CT and MRI provide complementary information that enriches the overall assessment of pancreatic lesions [3, 6, 28–30]. Multiphasic contrast-enhanced CT excels at delineating vascular involvement, assessing resectability, and characterizing tumor margins, but is limited in its sensitivity for small lesions and is prone to false positives in the setting of chronic pancreatitis. Notably, among the 27 small pancreatic cancers in our cohort who also underwent CT, 9 (33.3%) were missed on CT, yielding a sensitivity of only 66.7%—reinforcing the superior sensitivity of EUS for small tumor detection. MRI with magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography offers superior soft tissue contrast and diffusion-weighted imaging, improving characterization of indeterminate lesions. A multimodal strategy that combines EUS-based feature scoring with CT or MRI staging and, where feasible, CE-EUS or elastography, is likely to yield the most accurate and comprehensive diagnostic assessment [6, 28, 29].

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. First, its retrospective, single-center design introduces potential selection bias and limits the generalizability of results to other clinical settings. Second, the absence of an internal or external validation cohort raises concerns about model overfitting, and the feature-counting threshold of ≥ 4 should be interpreted as preliminary until prospectively validated. Third, interobserver variability in EUS image interpretation was not formally assessed, which may affect the reproducibility of feature classification in routine practice. Fourth, although several clinically relevant variables such as age, sex, tumor location, and chronic pancreatitis were considered, these variables were not included in the final multivariable model due to sample size considerations and the risk of model overfitting. Therefore, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Fifth, due to the retrospective nature of this study, it was not always possible to reliably distinguish between arterial and venous invasion or to assess vessel abutment based on the available medical records. Finally, the relatively small number of malignant cases in the ≤ 2 cm subgroup may have limited statistical power and the precision of subgroup estimates. Future prospective, multicenter studies with larger sample sizes and dedicated validation cohorts are warranted to confirm and refine these findings.

Conclusions

Pancreatic cancer remains a disease of devastating prognosis, and early, accurate diagnosis is central to improving patient outcomes. This study highlights the diagnostic value of EUS in characterizing solid pancreatic tumors through a practical, feature-based approach. The presence of ≥ 4 of five defined EUS features provides a readily applicable threshold for flagging high-risk lesions and guiding subsequent management decisions, complementing established strategies such as EUS-FNB. In tumors ≤ 2 cm, heterogeneous echotexture and pancreatic duct dilatation are reliable independent predictors of malignancy, offering critical support for early detection. However, these features lose discriminative power in larger tumors (> 2 cm), where overlapping imaging characteristics necessitate adjunct modalities such as CE-EUS and elastography. Taken together, our findings advocate for a size-stratified, individualized approach to EUS interpretation—one that integrates feature counting with tumor size to optimize early diagnosis and guide timely treatment planning.
