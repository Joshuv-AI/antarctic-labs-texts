Methodological concerns in the association between gut microbiota and sarcopenia: from cross‑sectional associations to statistical fragility
Authors: Sharipova Gulnihol, Nurullaev Abdukhamid, Turakulov Rustam, Ulmasov Firdavs, Arman Abroumand Gholami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis commentary critically appraises the cross‑sectional study by Nasrollahizadeh et al. on gut microbiota and sarcopenia in Iranian older adults. Key limitations include; after FDR correction for twelve bacterial genera, no significant differences remained between groups; Akkermansia lost significance in sensitivity analyses; Lactobacillus showed a confidence interval including 1.00; four primer pairs lacked validation with no MIQE‑compliant efficiency data; the cross‑sectional design precludes causal inference; and no sample size justification was reported. The study offers valuable hypothesis‑generating data, but evidence remains preliminary. Future longitudinal studies with metagenomic approaches are essential.Keywords: Gut microbiota, Sarcopenia, Cross-sectional study, FDR, MIQE guidelines

Dear Editor

We read with great interest the article by Nasrollahizadeh and colleagues, recently published in Aging Clinical and Experimental Research, which investigates the association between gut microbiota composition and sarcopenia in an Iranian older adult cohort from the Birjand Longitudinal Aging Study (BLAS) [1]. The authors are to be commended for addressing an important and understudied question in a Middle Eastern population, thereby contributing valuable hypothesis‑generating data to the growing field of the gut‑muscle axis. While the study offers valuable insights, several methodological limitations warrant explicit clarification to help readers interpret the findings appropriately.

The unresolved discrepancy between unadjusted and adjusted analyses

The authors tested twelve bacterial genera using Wilcoxon rank‑sum tests and applied False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons. After FDR correction, none of the twelve genera showed a statistically significant difference between sarcopenic and non‑sarcopenic groups (Table 2 of the original article by Nasrollahizadeh et al.). The authors themselves acknowledge this finding in the Results section. However, this critical limitation is entirely absent from the Discussion and Conclusion, where the uncorrected associations are interpreted as if they were definitive. Testing twelve hypotheses without correction would yield a ~ 46% chance of at least one false positive, a risk that FDR correction is designed to eliminate [2]. The omission is particularly concerning because the Discussion and Conclusion repeatedly refer to ‘significant associations’ without noting that these associations did not survive correction for multiple comparisons. This risks misleading readers about the strength of the evidence. We recommend that future studies present both unadjusted and adjusted effect estimates in their interpretive sections, and that formal correction for multiple comparisons be applied consistently across all analyses.

Fragility of effect estimates and definition‑dependent findings

Two observations indicate that the reported associations are statistically fragile. First, in the primary logistic regression model (Table 3), Lactobacillus showed an odds ratio of 1.08 with a 95% confidence interval of 1.00–1.17. The lower confidence limit of exactly 1.00 means that the true effect could be zero,i.e., no association at all. Second, and more critically, when the authors broadened the sarcopenia definition to include “possible sarcopenia” in a sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table 3 of the original article by Nasrollahizadeh et al.), Akkermansia lost statistical significance (OR 1.04, p = 0.056), whereas Roseburia remained robust. Consequently, the study’s conclusion that Akkermansia and Lactobacillus are robustly associated with sarcopenia is overstated. The evidence for these associations is fragile and definition‑dependent, and only Roseburia emerges as a stable finding across sensitivity analyses. STROBE guidelines recommend that sensitivity analyses with alternative outcome definitions be reported to assess the robustness of findings [3]. In this study, such analyses revealed that Akkermansia lost statistical significance when the definition was broadened.

Methodological concerns in qPCR validation

The authors quantified twelve bacterial genera using quantitative real‑time PCR. However, several essential quality controls mandated by the MIQE guidelines are missing [4]. First, four primer pairs (Alistipes, Oscillospira, Ruminococcus, Dialister) were labeled “This Study” (Supplementary Table 1 of the original article by Nasrollahizadeh et al.), meaning they lack independent validation. Second, no data on amplification efficiency, linear dynamic range, or limit of detection were reported for any of the twelve primer sets. Without these parameters, the quantitative abundance values (log CFU/g) presented in Table 2 (of the original article by Nasrollahizadeh et al.) are methodologically unsubstantiated. Consequently, the validity of the abundance measurements for these four genera cannot be assessed, and the study’s findings regarding their association with sarcopenia remain unverifiable. The lack of MIQE‑compliant reporting also precludes replication by independent groups.

Cross‑sectional design cannot establish causal direction

The study employs a cross‑sectional design, which can identify associations but cannot determine temporal sequence or causality. Nevertheless, the authors state in the Discussion that Akkermansia may “affect the onset of sarcopenia”, a directional claim that the data do not support. As Dorn noted more than six decades ago, observational studies alone cannot establish causality [5]. Reverse causation is equally plausible: sarcopenia, and its associated changes in diet, physical activity, systemic inflammation, and medication use, could drive alterations in gut microbiota composition. Notably, the authors acknowledge this limitation in the text (“we cannot determine whether the observed microbiota differences are a cause or a consequence of sarcopenia”) but this caveat is absent from the Discussion and Conclusion, where the findings are interpreted as if directionality were established. Consequently, the reported associations should be interpreted as correlational only. Future longitudinal studies are needed to establish temporal precedence.

Absence of a priori power analysis

The authors did not report how the sample size of 293 participants was determined. According to best practices for observational studies outlined by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ), investigators should “provide a rationale for study size during the planning stages” [6]. An a priori justification would have specified the anticipated effect size, desired statistical power (80%), and acceptable type I error rate (0.05). Without this information, it is unclear whether the study was sufficiently powered to detect the modest effect sizes reported (e.g., OR = 1.07 for Akkermansia with 95% CI 1.02–1.12). Consequently, the risk of type II errors (false negatives) cannot be assessed, and readers cannot determine whether non‑significant findings reflect true absence of association or insufficient statistical power.

In conclusion, the study by Nasrollahizadeh and colleagues provides an important hypothesis‑generating contribution to the understanding of gut microbiota in sarcopenia from a Middle Eastern population. The authors are to be commended for addressing an understudied population and using standardized sarcopenia criteria. However, the evidence presented remains preliminary. The findings are constrained by the discrepancy between unadjusted and adjusted analyses (FDR), the absence of MIQE‑compliant qPCR validation, the cross‑sectional design that precludes causal inference, and the lack of a priori sample size justification. The potential issues we raise are not intended to diminish the study’s value but rather to provide a more rigorous and nuanced interpretive framework for its findings and to guide future research. Future longitudinal studies employing unbiased metagenomic approaches, independent validation cohorts, and rigorous methodological standards are essential to determine whether these microbial signatures represent robust biomarkers or statistical artefacts.
