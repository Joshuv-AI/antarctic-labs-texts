Analysis of the empowerment of disabled older people in rural areas in Henan Province based on the random forest algorithm: a survey
Authors: Xizheng Li, Yuwen Yan, Huizhong Zhang, Shuaiyang Dong, Yan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to understand the health empowerment status of rural disabled older people and analyse the influencing factors to provide a reference for targeted intervention measures.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted using cluster sampling in natural administrative villages (the cluster units) involving 208 disabled older individuals from a rural community in Henan Province. The empowerment level was measured using a chronic disease empowerment scale containing five dimensions and 23 items (score 23–115). Variables were screened by the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) method (λ = 0.0529); their relative importance was ranked with the random forest algorithm. Multiple regression analysis was conducted to determine the significant predictors.ResultsThe overall empowerment score for rural disabled older people was moderate (mean 2.42 ± 0.53 on a 5-point Likert scale, 48th percentile). The LASSO analysis identified education, age, main economic source, monthly income, monthly medical expenses, degree of disability, number of chronic diseases, number of children and marital status as the top nine influential factors. Multiple regression analysis revealed that the number of children, type of chronic diseases, education level and degree of disability affected the empowerment level of rural disabled older individuals (p < 0.05).ConclusionsThe current situation of empowerment of disabled older people in rural areas is at the middle level, especially in the aspects of self-decision, health records and smart pensions. Empowerment can be implemented through the following: (1) village doctor health education programmes; (2) caregiver skills training; (3) county-level disability/chronic disease joint follow-up systems.Keywords: Rural areas, disabled older people, active health, empowerment

Background

With the ageing population, the number of disabled older people in China has surged synchronously, and the problem of disability in older adults is becoming increasingly serious. It is predicted that the number of disabled older people in China will increase from 136.24 million to 136.74 million by 2030 [1]. Rural disabled older people, in particular, represent a significant proportion of this population and often face greater health disparities than their urban counterparts [2].

With a large rural population, Henan is representative of many regions in China where access to healthcare and social services is limited. The province’s rural areas are characterised by lower levels of economic development, education and healthcare infrastructure than urban centres [2]. This setting presents unique challenges for the health empowerment of disabled older individuals, making it an ideal location to study the factors influencing their health outcomes and to develop targeted interventions [3].

The China Geriatric Health Research Report (2020–2021) [3] points out that empowerment is considered to be the key to improving the internal ability of older people, advocating for older adults to explore their resources, encouraging self-care and promoting health empowerment. The World Health Organization defines empowerment as a positive collaborative relationship aimed at improving health status and quality of life, which is essential for achieving better health outcomes [4, 5]. Empowerment encompasses three key dimensions: self-efficacy (confidence in one’s ability to manage health), access to information (the ability to obtain and understand health resources) and participation in decision-making (active involvement in health choices). These dimensions work synergistically to enhance an individual’s control over their health and life circumstances.

Recent studies have highlighted the urban–rural disparity in health empowerment among older populations. Li et al. (2023) found that older adults in rural Central China had significantly lower awareness of and need for home modifications than their urban counterparts, with only 30% expressing such needs despite living in potentially unsafe environments [6]. Similarly, research on technology adoption barriers (Bertolazzi et al., 2024) demonstrated that rural older people with disabilities face unique barriers, including limited transportation, fewer healthcare providers and reduced social support networks [7].

However, China’s traditional care methods mainly focus on daily living and medical care and ignore the willingness and ability of disabled older people to actively pursue health, leading to the continuous decline of internal ability and an inability to maintain their function [8].

Given these challenges, this study aims to investigate the health empowerment status of disabled older individuals in rural Henan Province and to identify the factors influencing their empowerment. We hypothesise that factors such as education level, economic status and the presence of chronic diseases significantly impact the empowerment levels of this population.

Methods

Study design

This study is a cross-sectional survey conducted in rural areas of Henan Province. The natural administrative villages served as cluster units in the cluster sampling design. A design effect of 1.5 was applied to account for clustering, ensuring the sample size of 208 remained adequate (138 × 1.5 = 207 < 208).

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee. Ethical approval was obtained from the Zhengzhou University Ethics Review Committee for Life Sciences at Zhengzhou University (Approval Number: ZZUIRB2024-198). All participants provided informed consent prior to their involvement in the study.

Selection of survey respondents

Disabled older people in the natural administrative villages of Henan Province were selected as the respondents. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) rural disabled older person; (2) aged ≥ 60 years; (3) Barthel Index (BI) rated at ‘slightly dependent’ or above; (4) their caregivers were non-medical professionals; (5) voluntarily participated in the study.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with severe mental disorders; (2) participating in other clinical trials; (3) without caregivers; (4) with chronic infectious diseases, such as AIDS, hepatitis or tuberculosis.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was estimated using 5–10 times the maximum number of items in the questionnaire [9]. There were 23 items in the chronic disease empowerment scale used in this study, and the calculated sample size was 115–230. Considering the design effect of 1.5 for cluster sampling, at least 138 participants were needed. In this study, 214 questionnaires were issued, and 208 questionnaires were recovered, with an effective recovery rate of 97.2% (208/214).

Study tools

The questionnaire includes general data on older adults, the chronic disease empowerment scale and the BI.

(1) General data questionnaire.

(1) General data questionnaire.

The general data questionnaire for disabled older people in rural areas includes age, gender, education level, marital status, monthly medical expenses, income, number of children, main sources of income, types of chronic diseases and disability degree.

(2) Barthel Index evaluation scale.

(2) Barthel Index evaluation scale.

This scale contains 10 items [10]: eating (5–10 points), bathing (0–5 points), grooming (0–5 points), dressing (5–10 points), bowel control (5–10 points), bladder control (5–10 points), toilet use (5–10 points), bed-chair transfer (5–15 points), mobility (10–15 points) and stair climbing (5–10 points). The full score is 100 points. According to the final score, the patient’s self-care ability was divided into the following: Grade A (61–99 points) – mild dependence; Grade B (41–60 points) – moderate dependence; Grade C (0–40 points) – severe dependence. The scale has high reliability, with Cronbach’s α = 0.89 [11].

(3) Chronic disease empowerment scale.

(3) Chronic disease empowerment scale.

The scale was compiled by Nicola Small, University of Manchester, UK, and translated into Chinese by Sun Yiqin [12]. It assesses the empowerment level of older patients with chronic diseases and includes self-identification (five items), individual control (four items), participation in decision-making (five items), knowledge and understanding (four items), empowering others (five items); thus, it has five dimensions and a total of 23 items. It uses the Likert 5-level scoring method, ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’ and scoring 1–5 points, with a total score of 23–115 points. In this study, Cronbach’s α = 0.923 and the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin value = 0.91 (Bartlett’s p < 0.001), indicating good reliability and validity.

Data collection method

Before the survey, two participants were uniformly trained. The purpose, significance and confidentiality of the study were explained to the participants before the study, and the questionnaire was issued after obtaining informed consent. The questionnaire was distributed on site and checked and recovered after completion.

Statistical methods

The SPSS 25.0 package and R Studio software were used for data analysis. Univariate analysis (the t-test or F-test) was first used to identify candidate variables, reducing dimensionality for subsequent analyses. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression method was then applied to address multicollinearity and perform variable selection (λ = 0.0529). The random forest (RF) algorithm was employed to examine nonlinear relationships and rank variable importance [13–15]. Finally, the top six variables from the RF ranking entered stepwise multiple regression. Before regression, the linear regression assumptions were tested, including independence (the Durbin–Watson statistic), linearity, homoscedasticity (the Breusch–Pagan test) and normality of residuals (the Shapiro–Wilk test) (see Fig. 1; Table 1). Differences were considered statistically significant with a p-value of < 0.05.

Screening of factors influencing the empowerment of rural disabled older people and the importance ranking of variables

Using the chronic disease empowerment scale score as the dependent variable, the variables with statistically significant differences in the univariate analysis were included in the RF model. Although marital status was not significant in the univariate tests, it was retained because RF can capture potential nonlinear effects. The results show that the variables in descending order of importance are as follows: education, age, main source of income, monthly income, monthly medical expenses, degree of disability, number of chronic illnesses, number of children and marriage, as shown in Fig. 2.

Discussion

The findings of this study offer valuable insights into the health empowerment of disabled older individuals in rural Henan Province. The RF model’s results in this study highlight crucial variables influencing the health empowerment of rural disabled older individuals. Education level emerged as a significant positive predictor, emphasising the need for interventions that enhance health literacy and self-efficacy through educational programmes on chronic disease management and self-care skills. Conversely, the negative association with the number of children indicates the importance of promoting independence alongside family support, suggesting that family education programmes could empower caregivers to facilitate autonomous decision-making and self-management among older people. Additionally, the impact of chronic diseases and disability degree underscores the necessity for comprehensive healthcare services that provide integrated care, addressing both medical and psychosocial needs through multidisciplinary care teams. These findings offer a strategic direction for targeted interventions, guiding healthcare providers and policymakers in developing effective strategies to improve empowerment by focusing on education, balanced family support and comprehensive care tailored to the complex needs of rural disabled older populations.

The empowerment level of disabled rural older people is medium

This study found that the overall level of health empowerment among disabled rural older people was moderately low (mean score 55.43 ± 12.04 out of 115, representing 48.2% of the maximum possible score). This may be related to the relatively low economic level and poor health conditions in rural areas (although there is currently a national emphasis on the need to empower rural public services to promote the extension of ‘Internet + Nursing Services’ to rural areas) [16]. However, under the influence of the urban–rural dual structure in China, there is a big gap between urban and rural health services regarding resource allocation and service quality [17]. Therefore, domestic rural medical personnel may have an insufficient understanding of empowerment and less implementation of empowerment skills in the supply of health services. The average score rates for each dimension were highest for self-identification (2.53 ± 0.78, 50.6% of maximum), followed by individual control (2.42 ± 0.59, 48.4%), knowledge and understanding (2.45 ± 0.69, 49.0%), empowerment of others (2.26 ± 0.65, 45.2%) and decision participation (2.16 ± 0.70, 43.2%). The self-identification score is the highest; one possible reason for this is that disabled older people have accepted their patient status, recognised their sick role and started to find new meaning in life due to their long-term disease state. This may be related to the fact that the disabled older people included in this study have received some positive psychological education from relevant professionals [12] and have gradually come to fully accept their disabled status. Furthermore, because family caregivers mainly provide positive support and emotional companionship to disabled older people in daily life [18], older people expect to be recognised by society, which helps them find their sense of value and purpose in life. Future interventions should pay attention to this phenomenon by encouraging older people to accept their situation and accept change; this taps into their potential for positive behaviour and helps them play an active role in their health management. The decision participation dimension score is relatively low. Studies have shown that although disabled older people often recognise the vulnerability and dependence of their physical function, they are also extremely eager to have the ability to make independent decisions [19]. However, due to the weak health awareness of disabled older people in rural areas and the caregivers often taking the lead in decision-making, it is sometimes difficult for them to participate in medical decisions concerning their own wellbeing [20]. Therefore, in future empowerment interventions, consideration should be given to strengthening this weak point, reducing paternalistic excessive care, establishing an independent and independent life circle for disabled older people and enhancing their health knowledge. Medical staff should promote patient participation in decision-making, leading to better health outcomes through shared communication.

Education level

This study showed that the higher the educational level of a disabled older person is, the higher their empowerment level. This may be because older people with a higher education level have stronger learning ability and are more motivated to accept and acquire health knowledge. Studies have shown that patients with a high level of health knowledge have better health conditions, as sound knowledge can help patients to understand the disease correctly and then implement appropriate health behaviours [21]. Education often improves health literacy, enabling individuals to better understand their conditions, navigate healthcare systems effectively and engage in proactive health behaviours. Additionally, higher education may provide individuals with greater access to resources and networks that support their empowerment efforts, such as information about available healthcare services and community programmes [22]. Therefore, a high level of empowerment requires corresponding health knowledge, literacy and skills. Medical staff should regard the provision and support of knowledge as an early guarantee of empowerment, not only as a simple notification during treatment but also as a systematic dissemination of health knowledge in a prepared, planned and strategic way. In empowerment interventions for disabled rural older people, individual differences among participants should be considered to promote mutual communication and sharing among older people. Health service personnel can help disabled rural older people establish common trait groups to achieve mutual empowerment.

Number of children

This study shows that the more children disabled older people have, the lower their empowerment level (β = −4.042, SE = 0.716, p < 0.001; mean difference of − 27.56 points between those with 0 children vs. ≥ 3 children), which is consistent with the findings of Pan Shuo et al. [23]. This observation appears to diverge from some studies that suggest having more children may provide additional support and resources, potentially bolstering empowerment [24]. The discrepancy could be attributed to several factors unique to the rural Chinese context. First, the presence of multiple children may lead to increased dependency, as older individuals may rely more heavily on their offspring for care and decision-making, thus diminishing their opportunities for self-advocacy and independent health management [25]. Additionally, cultural norms and expectations in rural areas may emphasise filial piety and the responsibility of children to care for their parents, which could inadvertently undermine older people’s autonomy and self-efficacy in health-related matters [26]. It is also possible that the burden of providing for and managing the needs of multiple children may strain the resources and attention of older people, leaving them with less capacity to engage in empowerment-promoting activities [27]. In contrast, other studies have reported similar findings to the current study, indicating that having more children can indeed hinder empowerment among certain populations of older individuals [28]. For instance, research conducted in urban settings has found that the presence of multiple adult children can lead to conflicting expectations and demands, causing stress and reducing older people’s sense of control over their lives [29]. Furthermore, studies examining cultural contexts where intergenerational relationships are characterised by high levels of dependency and limited opportunities for older autonomy have also observed negative associations between the number of children and empowerment [25]. These parallels suggest that the impact of family structure on empowerment may be influenced by a complex interplay of cultural, social and contextual factors, rather than being universally positive or negative.

Number of chronic diseases

The results of this study showed that disabled older people with more types of chronic diseases had significantly lower empowerment levels (mean scores: 0 diseases = 68.57 ± 11.07; 1 disease = 53.24 ± 11.75; ≥2 diseases = 51.01 ± 8.73; F = − 7.274, p < 0.001). This may be because disabled older people with more types of chronic diseases often face many health problems, such as a rapid decline in physical function and quality of life, increased risk of frailty and cross-occurrence of chronic disease symptoms [30]. Soares believes that health status is associated with stress management capacity related to health empowerment and that relatively good health status is a prerequisite for performing disease self-management [31]. Therefore, due to the influence of this factor, disabled older people may have negative emotions towards the people and things around them and are prone to the problems of psychological maladjustment [32]. Thus, it is difficult to adopt effective health management behaviours. The presence of multiple chronic conditions often leads to a decline in physical function and quality of life, making it more challenging for individuals to feel confident and in control of their health. This can result in feelings of helplessness and reduced motivation to engage in self-care and health-promoting behaviours. In conclusion, medical staff designing future intervention programmes for rural disabled older people should place the focus on the patient, offering personalised support plans, knowledge education and psychological counselling activities, thereby encouraging the patient to engage in self-management of their health status.

Degree of disability

Perhaps unsurprisingly, the study also found that a higher degree of disability is associated with lower empowerment levels. The results of this study indicate that the higher the disability of the older person is, the lower their empowerment level (mild dependence: 62.45 ± 12.18; moderate dependence: 52.45 ± 8.37; severe dependence: 12.92 ± 2.21, representing a 79.3% decline from mild to severe dependence). The reason for this may be that with the increase of a disability, the worse the self-care ability becomes, the more their families need to spend time to take care of them and the more the psychological burden increases [33]. Therefore, older people are prone to negative emotions that hinder their active health management behaviours. When an older person’s body function is impaired, the frequency of social collective activities with colleagues, friends, neighbours and relatives can decline; thus, they gradually lose their social support network, leading to a sense of isolation and spiritual emptiness [34]. When disabled older people undergo major changes in their social roles and interpersonal relationships, they may experience psychological problems, such as anxiety and depression, which inhibit their active participation in health-promoting behaviours [18]. Greater disability often results in increased dependency on others for daily living tasks and healthcare decisions, which can diminish an individual’s sense of autonomy and self-efficacy [35]. This highlights the importance of developing targeted interventions that support the empowerment of individuals with higher levels of disability, such as providing additional resources and training to enhance their self-care abilities and decision-making skills [36].

Comparison with recent literature

Our findings align with and extend recent research on health empowerment among older adults in rural China. Consistent with our moderate empowerment levels (mean 2.42 ± 0.53, 48th percentile), Xia et al. (2024) reported that social quality factors significantly influence life satisfaction among rural older adults, with social empowerment being a critical determinant [37]. Similarly, the rural mutual aid older care model described by Yu et al. (2024) emphasises the importance of fostering self-management and active participation, which resonates with our finding that self-identification scored highest among the empowerment dimensions [38].

The positive association between education and empowerment in our study corroborates Zhang et al. (2020), who found that education moderates the relationship between social capital and cognitive function among older adults in China [39]. This suggests that educational interventions could have multiple benefits beyond immediate health literacy improvements. Regarding family dynamics, our finding that a higher number of children correlates with lower empowerment levels reflects broader demographic challenges.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design prevents us from establishing causal relationships between the identified factors and empowerment levels. As data were collected at a single point in time, we cannot determine whether these factors cause or result from the observed empowerment levels. Future longitudinal studies are needed to explore these relationships over time.

Second, our statistical methodology has important limitations. We did not perform bootstrap iterations (100–500 times) to assess the stability of LASSO variable selection, which is recommended to address LASSO’s known instability issues. Variables should ideally be retained only if selected in at least 50% of iterations. Additionally, our RF analysis included only variables significant in univariate analysis rather than all potential predictors, potentially missing important nonlinear relationships and limiting our ability to compare LASSO and RF comprehensively for identifying the most informative predictors.

Third, the study’s focus on a single rural community in Henan Province limits the generalisability of the findings. The unique socio-economic and cultural context of this community may influence the empowerment status and the factors affecting it, making it difficult to apply the results to other regions or countries. Further research in diverse settings is necessary to validate these findings and understand their broader implications.

Finally, the survey tools used may face challenges in cultural adaptability. Although validated in certain contexts, these tools may not fully capture the nuances of empowerment in the rural Chinese setting, potentially affecting the accuracy and reliability of the data collected. Future studies should consider culturally adapting and validating these tools to better reflect the local context and should implement more rigorous statistical approaches, including bootstrap validation for variable selection stability and comprehensive comparison of different machine learning methods.

Conclusions

The empowerment status of disabled older people in rural China is at the middle level, with self-recognition scoring highest. Education level, number of children, number of chronic diseases and disability degree are independent influential factors. To improve empowerment, we recommend (1) implementing village doctor health education programmes targeting chronic disease management, (2) establishing caregiver skills training focusing on promoting older adults’ autonomy and (3) creating county-level disability/chronic disease joint follow-up systems to provide integrated care. Future multicentre studies with larger samples are needed for a more comprehensive understanding.
