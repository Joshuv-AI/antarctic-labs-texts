Paeonol alleviates granulosa cell senescence in laying chickens via the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway
Authors: Yingyu Xiao, Qiongyu Yuan, Xiangyu Cai, Zhaoyu Yang, Wanyue Gao, Jing Dong, Weidong Zeng, Caiqiao Zhang, Yuling Mi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOvarian aging compromises reproductive health and reduces egg production, posing a persistent challenge to the poultry industry. Mitochondrial dysfunction in granulosa cells (GCs) accelerates reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and induces apoptosis in both GCs and oocytes. The impact of Paeonol on mitochondrial function in avian GCs remains unclear.MethodsIn this study, we evaluated the antioxidant activity of Paeonol in a D-galactose (D-gal)-induced senescent GC model and further examined its effects in late-laying hens receiving dietary Paeonol supplementation. Cellular senescence, apoptosis, oxidative stress, mitochondrial status, and PI3K/Akt /mTOR signaling were assessed using molecular and cellular assays, complemented by transcriptomic analysis.ResultsIn vitro, Paeonol treatment significantly mitigated the D-gal-induced imbalance between proliferation and apoptosis in GCs, reduced mitochondrial ROS, increased mitochondrial membrane potential, and improved mitochondrial morphology, consistent with attenuated oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. Importantly, inhibition of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway with LY294002 partially attenuated Paeonol’s cytoprotective effects, suggesting pathway involvement. In vivo, dietary supplementation with Paeonol promoted follicular development and improved egg-laying performance. Paeonol also improved antioxidant capacity and increased the transcription of multiple antioxidant enzyme genes in GCs. In addition, mitochondrial dynamics-related proteins involved in fusion and fission were upregulated, implying improved maintenance of mitochondrial structure and function.ConclusionThese findings indicate that Paeonol may mitigate D-gal-associated GC senescence and mitochondrial impairment, with PI3K/Akt /mTOR signaling potentially contributing to these effects. Paeonol warrants further evaluation as a dietary supplement for supporting reproductive function in late-laying hens.

Introduction

In poultry, ovarian function is tightly coupled to the laying cycle, and maintaining ovarian health is essential for sustained egg production. With advancing age, hens exhibit declining laying rates and variable or reduced egg quality, imposing substantial economic and management challenges on the industry (Sun et al., 2025). Granulosa cells (GCs) play a central role in ovarian function by supporting steroidogenesis, coordinating oocyte growth and maturation, and forming the core of the follicular microenvironment that maintains ovarian homeostasis (Alberico and Woods, 2021). Accordingly, GC dysfunction or premature senescence compromises oocyte competence and adversely affects follicular development, ultimately reducing reproductive performance (Morimoto et al., 2024). In poultry, the ovary serves as the principal organ regulating ovulation and reproductive endocrinology. Thus, egg production is closely linked to ovarian health.

Mitochondria, which serve as central integrators of oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, and cellular aging (Xu et al., 2025), are highly enriched in GCs and provide adenosine triphosphate (ATP) to support proliferation, differentiation, and steroid biosynthesis (Tatarczuch et al., 2025; You et al., 2024). These functions are vulnerable to age-associated damage. Declining GC quality is closely associated with impaired mitochondrial function, suggesting that protecting mitochondrial integrity may help delay ovarian aging (Dong et al., 2022). In avian models, aging ovaries exhibit reduced mitochondrial function and steroidogenesis (Zhan et al., 2024), and oxidative stress-driven ovarian degeneration (Gu et al., 2025). Late-laying hens display swollen mitochondria, disrupted cristae, and an impaired mitochondrial unfolded protein response (Zhan et al., 2024). Nevertheless, the specific contribution of mitochondrial dysfunction to ovarian aging and the decline in laying performance in hens remains poorly defined, highlighting the need for mechanistic studies and targeted interventions to support ovarian function during the late-laying period.

Plant-derived natural compounds have drawn increasing attention for their roles in regulating cellular senescence and oxidative stress (Ebrahimirad et al., 2025). Paeonol, a phenolic compound isolated from Paeonia suffruticosa root bark, alleviates oxidative injury in diverse systems, including reducing hepatic oxidative damage in rats (Chen et al., 2012), protecting endothelial cells from oxidative-stress-induced senescence (Jamal et al., 2014), and delaying dextran sulfate sodium-induced colitis through its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects (Jin et al., 2016). Moreover, a previous study (Yang et al., 2025) demonstrated that Paeonol selectively targets mitochondria and alleviates oxidative stress through mitochondria-targeted protective mechanisms, mitigating dexamethasone-induced kidney injury. However, whether these mitochondria-protective effects extend to GCs in poultry and contribute to delaying ovarian aging remains unknown.

In GCs of laying chickens, stress-associated phenotypes such as altered proliferation, apoptosis, and autophagy are regulated by several conserved signaling modules, including MAPK and the Akt-associated axis (Zhu et al., 2021a). Among these pathways, PI3K/Akt/mTOR has been repeatedly implicated in GC responses, where its dysregulation is accompanied by changes in cell survival programs and stress-adaptive processes (Zhu et al., 2021b). This study investigated the effects of Paeonol in a D-galactose (D-gal)-induced GC senescence model derived from laying hens, with a focus on mitochondrial function and related endpoints. We evaluated whether Paeonol can attenuate D-gal-induced GC senescence by improving mitochondrial function and whether the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway mediates this effect. Furthermore, we assessed whether Paeonol supplementation ameliorates the decline in laying performance observed in late-laying hens. This work aims to provide mechanistic evidence for mitochondrial-targeted strategies to maintain ovarian function and to inform nutritional approaches that extend the productive lifespan and enhance laying performance in hens.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

Hy-Line White chickens were obtained from a commercial poultry farm in Hangzhou, China. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the Zhejiang University Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Zhejiang University Animal Ethics Committee (Approval No. ZJU20250924).

Animals and sample collection

For the in vivo experiment, 180 Hy-Line White chickens (480 days old) were randomly assigned to three dietary treatments. Chickens in the control treatment were offered the basal diet, whereas those in the other two treatment groups received the same diet supplemented with Paeonol at 200 or 500 mg/kg (522-41-0, Herbchem Biotech, Shanxi, China). The supplementation levels were chosen with reference to the oral dose range reported in rats (Adki et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2020). The basal diet was formulated in accordance with the China National Feeding Standard for Chickens (NY/T 33-2004) (Table S1). Each group consisted of 60 chickens housed in 6 pens (10 birds per pen) to ensure uniform stocking density. All chickens were reared in environmentally controlled housing with ad libitum access to feed and water and egg production was monitored daily throughout the experimental period.

After 21 days, six chickens from each pen were randomly chosen. Blood was collected from the wing vein. The plasma was then separated and stored at -20°C. Subsequently, the chickens were anesthetized and euthanized by exsanguination via the jugular vein. The ovary was isolated from the ovarian stromal root, and then washed using phosphate buffer saline (PBS). The number of pre-ovulatory follicles, prehierarchical follicles, and atretic follicles (AF) were recorded. The prehierarchical follicles were further classified by diameter size: large yellow follicles (LYF, 8-12 mm), small yellow follicles (SYF, 6-8 mm), large white follicles (LWF, 4-6 mm), small white follicles (LWF, 2-4 mm). Ovaries and follicles were collected from each individual chicken and GC layer were isolated from SYF after rinsing. The samples were stored at -80°C for subsequent analyses.

Cell culture

For the in vitro experiments, ovaries were collected from peak-laying hens, and SYF were selected. Each follicle was rinsed with PBS, opened, and the granulosa layer was gently peeled from the theca layer to minimize the carryover of theca tissue. The tissues were rinsed with PBS and digested with 1 mg/mL collagenase II (17101015, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) for 5 min at 37°C. The cell suspension was filtered through 200-mesh sieves and centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 5 min. After two additional PBS washes, the pelleted GCs were resuspended in F12/DMEM medium (Hyclone, Tauranga, New Zealand) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (BL201A, Biosharp, Hefei, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (15140122, Gibco). Cell viability was estimated by trypan blue exclusion and GCs were counted in a hemocytometer. The cells were then seeded at 3.5 × 105/ml and cultured at 38.5°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

Estrogen analysis

Serum estrogen (E2) concentrations were measured using a Chicken E2 ELISA kit (XY9CH0095, X-Y Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instruction.

Drug treatment

D-gal (MB1853, MeilunBio, Dalian, China) was prepared by dissolving the powder directly in DMEM/F12 medium. Paeonol (S25511, OriLeaf, Shanghai, China) was first solubilized in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and then diluted with culture medium to different treatment concentration, with the final DMSO concentration kept at < 0.1%. According to the results of a previous study (Dong et al., 2022), cellular senescence was induced by incubating GCs with 200 mM D-gal for 24 h, followed by medium replacement. N-acetylcysteine (NAC, ST1546, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used as a reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenger to evaluate rescue of D-gal-induced damage. The cultures were then treated with varying concentrations of Paeonol for an additional 24 h, with the final treatment concentration for GCs at 1 µM. LY294002 (HY-10108, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) was co-treated with Paeonol at 30 µM for 24 h as a PI3K/Akt/mTOR inhibitor.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was determined with a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; C0038, Beyotime). Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 3.5 × 10⁴/well and treated with various concentrations of the indicated compounds. Following treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added and the cells were incubated for 2 h. Subsequently, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Varioskan Flash, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). To eliminate the interference from culture medium, background was corrected by subtracting the absorbance of blank wells containing medium and CCK-8 but no cells.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase staining

Cellular senescence was evaluated using a senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining kit (G1580, Solarbio, Beijing, China). GCs were seeded into 6-well plates and then exposed to the designated treatments. Following the manufacturer’s protocol, GCs were washed twice with PBS and fixed in the provided β-galactosidase fixative solution. The cells were then rinsed three times with PBS, covered with 1 mL of staining solution, and incubated at 37°C for 12 h. Cells exhibiting diffuse blue precipitates in the cytoplasm were considered SA-β-gal-positive.

ROS assay

Intracellular ROS levels in GCs were quantified using a commercial ROS detection kit (S0033S, Beyotime). Cells were cultured in 6-well plates. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, DCFH-DA was diluted to 10 µM in serum-free medium and added to each well. The cells were incubated for 30 min at 37°C, after which excess probe was removed by washing with PBS. Cell nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (HY-15559A, MCE). Fluorescent signals were captured using an IX70 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and the average fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ software (v1.52i, NIH, USA).

Mitochondrial membrane potential assay

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was assessed using a JC-1 assay kit (C2006, Beyotime) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. For flow cytometry, GCs were washed with PBS and trypsinized with EDTA-free trypsin (C3538, XPbiomed, Shanghai, China) for approximately 2 min. The cells were collected and rinsed twice with PBS. Subsequently, the cell pellet was resuspended in JC-1 working solution and incubated for 20 min at 37°C. After staining, cells were washed twice with 1 × JC-1 buffer, and fluorescence signals were acquired using a BD FACS flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA); data were analyzed using FlowJo software (v10.8.1, BD Biosciences, USA). For fluorescence microscopy, cells were loaded with JC-1 using the same incubation protocol, washed, and observed with an Olympus IX70 microscope. CCCP (10 µM, 20 min) was used as a positive control prior to staining. The red/green fluorescence ratio was analyzed using ImageJ.

Flow cytometric analysis of apoptosis

Apoptosis was determined using an Annexin V-FITC/PI staining kit (210837/210838, NEST, Wuxi, China). GCs were harvested with EDTA-free trypsin (C0205, Beyotime), washed with PBS, and resuspended in 1 × binding buffer. GCs were simultaneously stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI for 15 min at approximately 25°C in the dark, followed by dilution with binding buffer. Fluorescence was recorded on a flow cytometer. 10,000 events were acquired and analyzed per sample. Unstained and single-stained controls were used to set compensation and to define quadrant gates. Cell debris was excluded by gating on FSC-A versus SSC-A.

Measurement of oxidative parameters

After being washed three times with PBS, GCs were collected by low-speed centrifugation. Total protein concentration was determined using a BCA kit (FD2001, Fdbio, Hangzhou, China), and the results were used to normalize subsequent measurements. The cellular levels of malondialdehyde (MDA, A003-4-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China), reduced glutathione (GSH, A006-2-1), and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC, A015-3-1) were subsequently measured according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Transmission electron microscope assay

For ultrastructural analysis, cells were collected and fixed overnight at 4°C with 2.5% glutaraldehyde prepared in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), followed by postfixation in 1% osmium tetroxide. Specimens were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30% to 100%) followed by pure acetone, embedded in epoxy resin, and polymerized at 60°C for 48 h. Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were cut from resin blocks to obtain cross sections of the cell pellet using an ultramicrotome (Leica EM UC7, Germany), collected on copper grids, and double stained with uranyl acetate (in 50% ethanol) and Reynolds’ lead citrate. Sections were examined on a Tecnai G2 Spirit transmission electron microscope (TEM; FEI, Hillsboro, OR, USA) to document mitochondrial ultrastructure. Images were acquired from randomly selected fields from independent biological replicates and at least 15 mitochondria per group were examined.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining

After fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde, tissues were rinsed under running water overnight, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and cleared and paraffin-infiltrated for 40 min prior to embedding. Paraffin blocks were sectioned at 5 μm thickness, and the resulting sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) following standard histological procedures. Stained sections were subsequently examined and imaged using an Eclipse 80i light microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) and reverse-transcribed using the HiScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (R333-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). RNA purity was evaluated by NanoDrop spectrophotometry and only samples with an A260/280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.1 were used. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed on a CFX96 Touch system (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) with 2 × SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711-02, Vazyme). The thermal cycling profile was 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s and 60°C for 30 s. Melt curve analysis was performed at the end of each run to verify amplification specificity. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method, with β-actin serving as the internal control. Primers sequences are listed in Table S2.

TUNEL assay

Deparaffinized sections were processed using a BrightGreen Apoptosis Detection Kit (A112-03, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. In addition, cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (MA0128, MeilunBio). Fluorescent images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Western blot analysis

GCs were lysed in Western/IP cell lysis buffer (P0013, Beyotime) containing 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (A1320914, AmBeed, Shanghai, China) and phosphatase inhibitor (A2679231) cocktail. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C, and the supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA kit (FD2001, Fdbio). Samples were then mixed with loading buffer to a final concentration of 2 µg/µL and denatured at 100°C for 10 min. Equal volumes (10 µL) were loaded onto 4-20% SDS-polyacrylamide gels and electrophoresed at 200 V for 1 h. Proteins were transferred to 0.22 µm PVDF membranes (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA), which were then blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h at room temperature.

Membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies (Table S3). After three washes with TBST, membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with secondary antibodies (Table S4). Following three additional TBST washes, signals were developed using an ECL substrate (SQ203, Epizyme, Shanghai, China). Chemiluminescent signals were captured. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ, and normalized to β-actin.

RNA sequencing

GC samples were collected, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and submitted to Novogene (Beijing, China) for RNA sequencing. Total RNA was extracted, poly(A) mRNA was enriched, and sequencing libraries were constructed, quality-checked, and subjected to high-throughput sequencing on an Illumina platform. Raw reads were adapter-trimmed and quality-filtered using fastp (v1.0.1), then aligned to the Gallus gallus reference genome (Assembly: GCF_016699485.2) with STAR (v2.7.10b). The data were submitted to the National Center for Biotechnology Information with accession number PRJNA1420401 . Gene-level counts were generated using featureCounts (Subread v2.0.3). Differential expression analysis was performed in R (v4.5.1) with DESeq2 (v1.51.0) after removal of low-abundance genes. Significance was set at FDR < 0.05 and |log₂FC| ≥ 1. Variance-stabilized expression values were used for visualization, including heatmaps with hierarchical clustering (pheatmap v1.0.13) and volcano plots (EnhancedVolcano v1.11.3). Functional enrichment of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was conducted using the Gene Ontology (GO) database, and pathway analysis was performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted with at least three independent biological replicates. Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2. Normally distributed data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test or Student’s t-test. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Paeonol alleviates D-gal-induced cellular senescence in GCs

Cell senescence was induced by exposing the cultures to 200 mM D-gal for 24 h. To define the working dose of Paeonol, CCK-8 assays were performed in both untreated and D-gal-induced GCs with Paeonol at different concentrations for 24 h. Paeonol at 1 μM yielded the optimal viability response, improving viability in D-gal-induced GCs while exhibiting no cytotoxicity in untreated cells (Fig. 1A). Specifically, qRT-PCR analysis of key cell-cycle regulators showed a significant increase at 1 μM Paeonol treatment (Figure S1A-B). Accordingly, 1 μM Paeonol was therefore used in subsequent experiments. NAC, a commonly used antioxidant that scavenges intracellular ROS, was used as a reference antioxidant to evaluate the effect of Paeonol. Treatment with 200 μM NAC significantly alleviated D-gal-induced cytotoxicity without affecting basal cell viability (Fig. 1B). To assess cellular senescence, SA-β-gal staining revealed a significant increase in positive cells after D-gal exposure(P < 0.001, Fig. 1C-D). Paeonol co-treatment significantly reduced this fraction (P < 0.01), whereas Paeonol alone showed no difference from the untreated control. By contrast, Paeonol treatment downregulated both genes toward control levels and Paeonol alone produced no measurable change (Fig. 1E-F).

Paeonol alleviates D-gal-induced apoptosis in GCs

qRT-PCR indicated that D-gal exposure significantly upregulated the pro-apoptotic genes Caspase-3, while concurrently downregulating the anti-apoptotic gene BCL2 (Fig. 2A-B). Paeonol supplementation significantly reversed these changes and was more effective than NAC (P < 0.05). Regarding proliferative markers, D-gal exposure markedly reduced the transcript levels of PCNA, CDK2, and CCND1 (P < 0.05, Fig. 2C-E). These decreases were partially rescued by co-treatment with NAC or Paeonol, with consistently greater recovery in the Paeonol group. Western blotting (Fig. 2F) showed that D-gal reduced PCNA and CDK2 protein abundance in GCs (P < 0.05), whereas Paeonol supplementation restored both proteins toward control levels (Fig. 2I, K). Meanwhile, D-gal exposure increased the protein abundance of BAX and Caspase-3 (P < 0.05, Fig. 2G, J), while decreasing BCL-2 (P < 0.01, Fig. 2H), indicating a shift toward apoptosis. Annexin V/PI flow cytometry further confirmed that D-gal markedly increased both early and late apoptotic populations (P < 0.05). Post-treatment with Paeonol substantially reduced the proportion of apoptotic cells (P < 0.05), achieving a greater decrease than NAC (Fig. 2L-M).

Paeonol alleviates D-gal-induced oxidative stress in GCs

ROS measurements showed that D-gal exposure substantially elevated ROS levels in GCs, as evidenced by increased green fluorescence intensity and a higher proportion of ROS-positive cells (P < 0.001). Treatment with Paeonol markedly attenuated the D-gal-induced elevation (P < 0.01), bringing ROS levels closer to control values and to levels comparable to those in the NAC treatment group (Fig. 3A-B). Notably, Paeonol alone also produced a modest reduction in the proportion of ROS-positive cells relative to the control. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that D-gal downregulated multiple antioxidant defense genes (SOD, CAT, Mgst, Gsta, and Gsr) (P < 0.05, Fig. 3C-G). Paeonol alleviates D-gal-induced mitochondrial dysfunction in GCs

To characterize D-gal-induced alterations in mitochondrial ultrastructure, TEM analysis revealed pronounced structural abnormalities following D-gal exposure. These included substantial mitochondrial swelling, disruption and dilation of the inner and outer membranes suggestive of increased permeability, loss or focal disruption of cristae, and decreased electron density with a rarified matrix, all of which are hallmarks of mitochondrial dysfunction. Paeonol supplementation markedly alleviated these alterations, restoring mitochondrial morphology toward the normal, compact architecture observed in the control group (Fig. 4A). To further probe oxidative stress associated with D-gal-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, we quantified MDA, a lipid peroxidation end product, and GSH, a key mitochondrial antioxidant. D-gal exposure markedly elevated intracellular MDA while depleting GSH, reflecting disturbed cellular redox homeostasis. Paeonol treatment effectively counteracted these changes and restored both MDA and GSH to control levels (Fig. 4B-C). To evaluate D-gal-induced alterations in mitochondrial mass and dynamics, Western blot analysis showed that D-gal exposure markedly decreased the levels of the mitochondrial outer membrane protein TOMM20 (P < 0.001), as well as the mitochondrial dynamics regulators (Drp1, MFN2, and OPA1) (P < 0.05, Fig. 4D-H). In parallel, qRT-PCR revealed significant downregulation of nuclear-encoded regulators of mitochondrial DNA replication (TFAM, TFB2M, and POLRMT), alongside reduced expression of the mitochondrial ATP synthase subunit 8 (ATPase8), indicating impaired mitochondrial biogenesis and mitochondrial gene expression. Paeonol supplementation significantly upregulated these transcripts in D-gal-induced cells (P < 0.05), returning their expression toward control values (Fig. 4I-L).

To assess ΔΨm in GCs, the JC-1 probe was employed. In D-gal-induced GCs, red fluorescence (JC-1 aggregates) was markedly diminished, whereas green fluorescence (JC-1 monomers) was increased, indicating a pronounced loss of ΔΨm. Paeonol supplementation substantially reversed this pattern, with restored red fluorescence accompanied by reduced green fluorescence, thereby restoring ΔΨm toward control levels (Fig. 4M-N). In addition, JC-1 flow cytometry demonstrated that D-gal exposure significantly decreased the aggregate-to-monomer ratio, reflecting mitochondrial depolarization. Paeonol treatment markedly elevated this ratio, in some cases surpassing that of the control group, suggesting an enhancement of mitochondrial polarization beyond baseline (Fig. 4O-P).

Screening of pathways mediating Paeonol's alleviation of D-gal-induced senescence in GCs

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which Paeonol mitigates D-gal-induced senescence in GCs, RNA-seq was performed across all treatment groups. Volcano plot analysis identified 611 upregulated genes and 533 downregulated genes in the D-gal group compared with the control group (Fig. 5A). Meanwhile, the D-gal+Paeonol group exhibited 292 upregulated genes and 485 downregulated genes compared with the D-gal group (Fig. 5B). Additionally, GO enrichment analysis showed that the DEGs were mainly associated with immune responses, transmembrane transport, proteolysis, and G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathways (Fig. 5D). KEGG pathway enrichment further revealed multiple significantly enriched signaling pathways. Notably, these pathways shared extensive upstream crosstalk and collectively converged upon the central PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling axis, a key integrator of inputs from growth factors, adhesion receptors, and extracellular matrix signals. Discussion

In laying chickens, ovarian aging is characterized by progressive follicular atresia, dysregulated GC apoptosis, and impaired steroidogenesis, ultimately resulting in reduced reproductive performance and a shortened laying cycle (Lebedeva et al., 2010; Zhan et al., 2024). These degenerative changes are tightly related to mitochondrial dysfunction and increased oxidative stress (Bao et al., 2022; Gu et al., 2025), which together disrupt energy metabolism and accelerate cellular senescence. Here, we integrated a D-gal-induced GC senescence model with dietary supplementation in late-laying hens to delineate the mitochondrial changes associated with ovarian aging. Our findings provide evidence that Paeonol improves mitochondrial function and redox homeostasis in D-gal-induced laying-hen ovarian GCs and alleviates senescence-related phenotypes, with PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling emerging as a key mechanistic node. In vivo, the high-dose Paeonol group (500 mg/kg) showed a greater increase in egg production and a more pronounced rise in prehierarchical follicles, suggesting a dose-related benefit in late-laying hens.

In late-laying poultry, ovarian regression is increasingly linked to mitochondrial dysfunction, as proteomic profiling highlights mitochondrial pathways as major nodes in ovarian degeneration and histological observations describe swollen mitochondria and disrupted cristae in aged ovarian tissue (Gu et al., 2025; Zhan et al., 2024). Our data support a mitochondrial mode of action for Paeonol in GCs, characterized by a coordinated recovery of mitochondrial structure and redox balance. In senescent GCs, Paeonol restored mitochondrial membrane potential and alleviated oxidative burden, together with an ultrastructural rescue that is consistent with preserved cristae integrity and improved organelle competence. Mechanistically, accumulating evidence indicates that Paeonol can act at multiple, but convergent, layers of mitochondrial regulation. Recent work suggests that Paeonol may enter or accumulate within mitochondria through a Translocator protein (TSPO)-associated route, enabling rapid organelle-level repair, including restoration of respiratory chain function and suppression of cytochrome c efflux in stress-injured cells (Yang et al., 2025). In parallel, Paeonol has been repeatedly linked to mitochondrial dynamics control. In diabetic cardiomyopathy models, Paeonol promoted OPA1-mediated inner membrane fusion, reduced mitochondria-derived superoxide, and improved ATP production and respiratory capacity (Liu et al., 2021). These fusion-promoting mechanisms provide a coherent explanation for why Paeonol increased mitochondrial renewal markers and fusion-related proteins in our GCs, because reinforced fusion can stabilize cristae organization, support electron transport efficiency, and reduce the probability of ROS amplification.

PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling integrates growth cues with stress adaptation, and its output is increasingly recognized as a determinant of whether damaged cells undergo apoptosis or enter a senescent state. In cell cycle-arrested cells, mTOR activity can reinforce senescence programs, underscoring strong context dependence (Blagosklonny, 2023). In ovarian aging, GCs are particularly vulnerable because they rely on high mitochondrial activity to sustain follicular support and endocrine competence, and senescence related changes in these cells are now considered a core component of reproductive aging rather than a passive consequence (Secomandi et al., 2022). Ovarian injury models further suggest that oxidative stress perturbs the coupling between PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling and mitochondrial quality control. In a premature ovarian insufficiency model, impaired PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling occurs together with abnormal autophagy and mitochondrial dysfunction, and pathway reactivation is required for cytoprotection, since LY294002 abolishes the protective effect (Dou et al., 2022). PI3K/Akt may also support mitochondrial stability via Nrf2-linked antioxidant programs (Xu et al., 2022). Against this literature background, our findings support a model in which Paeonol mediated protection in laying hen GCs depends on reestablishing a physiological PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling tone under stress, rather than nonspecific pathway activation. This framework links mitochondrial rescue to restrained stress-associated senescence and offers a mechanistic rationale for the improved follicular reserve and laying performance observed in vivo. Notably, the data are consistent with a balance model in which stress dampens PI3K/Akt/mTOR activity at the expense of mitochondrial maintenance, whereas Paeonol partially restores signaling toward a homeostatic range that favors mitochondrial quality control and cellular resilience.

While our findings support a protective role of Paeonol in the aging ovary, several questions remain. Ovarian exposure in birds, including potential passage across the blood ovarian barrier, is not yet defined, and future work should quantify Paeonol and major metabolites in plasma, ovary, and follicular fluid to establish ovarian delivery. Finally, the direct molecular targets underlying the mitochondrial improvements remain unclear, and mechanistic work integrating mitochondrial functional assays with analyses of mitochondrial dynamics and mitophagy will be required.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that Paeonol alleviates GC senescence by restoring mitochondrial homeostasis and reducing oxidative stress through activation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway (Fig. 10). It improves mitochondrial membrane potential, preserves structural integrity, and enhances antioxidant capacity, collectively strengthening cellular resilience. In vivo, dietary Paeonol supplementation also mitigates the decline in egg production and enhances the antioxidant capacity of the granulosa layer. Overall, these findings identify Paeonol as a promising antioxidant capable for alleviating ovarian aging and promoting reproductive longevity in laying chickens.Fig. 10Mechanism of Paeonol in alleviating GC senescence through the regulation of mitochondrial dysfunction in laying chickens.Fig 10 dummy alt text
