Economic evaluations of outpatient stroke rehabilitation models: a systematic review of evidence, methods, and gaps
Authors: Ahmad Al Miaraj, Sonila M Tomini, Nick Ward, Rodolfo Catena
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundStroke imposes an ongoing burden on patients and health systems, making outpatient rehabilitation essential. The economic efficiency of alternative outpatient models remains uncertain, complicating resource allocation. We systematically reviewed and critically appraised full economic evaluations of outpatient stroke rehabilitation to synthesise evidence on reported cost-effectiveness, assess how costs and outcomes have been measured, and identify key methodological limitations and evidence gaps.MethodsWe conducted a PRISMA-guided systematic review (PROSPERO: CRD420251151991) of full economic evaluations of outpatient stroke rehabilitation. Embase, Emcare, Medline, CINAHL, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and PubMed were searched, with publication coverage including all available studies published prior to September 2025. Eligible studies included adults with ischemic or haemorrhagic stroke undergoing outpatient rehabilitation and reporting full economic evaluations. We excluded inpatient-only rehabilitation, transient ischaemic attack, partial evaluations, reviews, and non-English publications. Two reviewers independently screened, extracted, and appraised studies. Risk of bias (RoB 2, ROBINS-I) and methodological quality (Drummond checklist) were assessed, with data narratively synthesised by rehabilitation model and evaluation type.FindingsThe search yielded 1800 records, of which 27 studies met inclusion criteria. Across studies, community-based, home-based, and early supported discharge rehabilitation models frequently demonstrated favourable cost-effectiveness when compared with inpatient or outpatient clinic-based rehabilitation. Study settings were global, with over one-third from the UK. Most were randomised controlled trials (56%), with cost-effectiveness analyses predominating (63%). Community- and home-based rehabilitation were most common (each 30%), followed by outpatient clinic-based (22%), early supported discharge (11%), and telerehabilitation (7%). Outcomes clustered into health-related quality of life (41%), functional recovery (37%), and process measures (22%). Methodological quality was mostly moderate (16/27), with seven high and four low. RCTs showed some reporting concerns, while most non-randomised studies carried serious risk of bias.InterpretationEvidence from existing economic evaluations suggests that shifting stroke rehabilitation toward outpatient and community-based rehabilitation models may demonstrate more favourable cost-effectiveness profiles in certain contexts, while evidence on telerehabilitation remains limited. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to substantial heterogeneity across study designs, comparators, outcomes, and economic evaluation methods. To better inform decision-making, future studies should adopt standardised outcome measures, extend follow-up, and generate context-specific economic evidence, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.FundingThere is no funding source for this study.Keywords: Stroke, Outpatient rehabilitation, Health economic evaluation

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Economic evaluations of stroke rehabilitation exist, but the literature has been fragmented and uneven. Most studies have centred on inpatient or acute care, leaving outpatient models comparatively underexplored. Prior systematic reviews are few and often limited in scope, lacking comprehensive synthesis or rigorous appraisal. Collectively, the evidence base before this study provided little clarity on the economic value of outpatient rehabilitation.

Added value of this study

This review is the first to systematically synthesise the economic evaluations of outpatient stroke rehabilitation models across diverse settings and study designs. By applying a structured framework to examine study characteristics, methodological quality, and outcome measures, the review provides a clearer picture of the strengths and limitations of the current evidence base.

Implications of all the available evidence

The findings reveal important evidence gaps with direct implications for policy and practice. While outpatient rehabilitation appears to be evaluated most often within traditional cost-effectiveness frameworks, there is limited consideration of long-term societal impacts, patient outcomes beyond health-related quality of life, and implementation in resource-constrained settings. Policymakers and health systems should be cautious in extrapolating the available evidence to diverse contexts.

Introduction

Stroke remains a leading cause of long-term disability worldwide, with survivors often experiencing enduring impairments in mobility, communication, and cognition that necessitates ongoing rehabilitation support.1 Outpatient rehabilitation, delivered in hospital clinics, community settings, or at home, is central to promoting recovery, independence, and quality of life, yet it is resource-intensive and operationally complex.2, 3, 4, 5 Models of outpatient rehabilitation differ substantially in intensity, duration, and mode of delivery,6 raising important questions about their cost-efficiency and sustainability within constrained health systems.7,8

Economic evaluations play a pivotal role in guiding the efficient allocation of scarce rehabilitation resources. Their value is underscored by the considerable costs of post-stroke rehabilitation and the heterogeneity of service delivery models. Nonetheless, their interpretation is often complicated by methodological heterogeneity and incomplete measurement of long-term outcomes.

The prolonged nature of stroke recovery means that short analytic horizons may underestimate the long-term benefits of rehabilitation.9,10 Similarly, outcome measures focused solely on impairment may overlook broader improvements in daily functioning and health-related quality of life.10,11 The choice of costing perspective further influences conclusions, as analyses limited to healthcare expenditures omit indirect effects such as caregiver burden, productivity losses, and social care, which are particularly salient in stroke and may vary by the rehabilitation model.11,12

While several reviews have addressed the economics of stroke rehabilitation, their scope has often been narrowed or inconsistent with respect to outpatient rehabilitation. Earlier work concentrated on home-based programmes compared with hospital care,13,14 whereas others combined inpatient and outpatient rehabilitation, typically reporting cost-consequence or cost-effectiveness findings without a structured appraisal of methodological quality.11,15 More recent studies have shifted attention to the overall economic burden or cost-of-illness,8,16 which, while informative, do not constitute full economic evaluations. Reviews extending to acquired brain injuries more broadly further limit applicability to stroke-specific outpatient rehabilitation.17,18

Given these limitations, there is a clear need for a comprehensive synthesis of economic evaluations focused specifically on outpatient stroke rehabilitation. This systematic review aims to synthesise and critically appraise the existing evidence of economic evaluations and its methodological quality in outpatient rehabilitation models. It examines key design features such as cost perspective, time horizon, and outcome measures, and applies the Drummond checklist to assess reporting quality. By identifying strengths, limitations, and evidence gaps, this review seeks to support interpretation of the current evidence-base and inform future research and policy discussions on outpatient stroke rehabilitation.

Methods

Study design, protocol and registration

This systematic review examined full health economic evaluations of outpatient rehabilitation models for stroke survivors following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. These steps ensured adherence to recognised standards of transparency and reproducibility. A detailed study protocol was developed in advance and registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251151991).

The search strategy was developed in consultation with an experienced librarian to ensure methodological rigour and comprehensive coverage of relevant literature. Searches were conducted across Embase, Emcare, Ovid MEDLINE, CINAHL, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, which were selected for their relevance to health services, rehabilitation, and economic evaluation research.19 To ensure completeness, supplementary searches were conducted in PubMed to capture any recent unindexed records. The searches covered all eligible studies from database inception to September 2025, with publication coverage including all studies available up to that date.

A combination of free-text keywords and controlled vocabulary terms (e.g., MeSH, Emtree) was applied using Boolean operators (AND, OR), truncation symbols (e.g., ∗), and quotation marks to capture synonyms, variants, and exact phrases.20 The search strategy encompassed four main conceptual domains: population (e.g., stroke survivors), intervention (e.g., rehabilitation), economic evaluation (e.g., cost-effectiveness), and setting (e.g., outpatient). A detailed account of all search strings, including keywords and database-specific adaptations, is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Data analysis

Data synthesis aimed to integrate findings systematically to address the review objectives by assessing the scope, methods, and results of economic evaluations of outpatient stroke rehabilitation models. A qualitative narrative synthesis was conducted, as meta-analysis was not feasible due to substantial heterogeneity in study designs, intervention types, and outcome measures. The included studies showed marked variability in economic methodologies, including cost perspectives, time horizons, discounting practices, and sensitivity analyses as well as in rehabilitation models and outcome indicators.

Studies were organised according to two dimensions: type of outpatient rehabilitation and economic evaluation methodology. Types of outpatient rehabilitation were grouped into five categories: Early supported discharge rehabilitation (ESD), home-based rehabilitation (HBR), community-based rehabilitation (CBR), outpatient clinic -based rehabilitation (OCBR), and telerehabilitation (TR). This allowed descriptive synthesis of findings across models and contextual factors. Economic evaluation methods were classified as Cost-Effectiveness Analysis (CEA), Cost-Utility Analysis (CUA), Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA), Cost-Minimisation Analysis (CMA), or Cost-Consequence Analysis (CCA), providing a framework to explore how methodological differences influence reported findings.

The review did not aim to undertake direct or indirect comparisons between outpatient rehabilitation models. Instead, it documented the comparators used within each economic evaluation and synthesised findings descriptively by rehabilitation model. Where studies included multiple comparators (e.g., alternative interventions or usual care within the same rehabilitation model), cost-effectiveness was interpreted with respect to the primary intervention under evaluation. Therefore, studies were classified once according to the main intervention of interest, consistent with the analytic focus of the original economic evaluation; details of the specific interventions and comparators are provided in Supplementary Table S6.

Results were summarised in structured tables detailing study characteristics, model features, economic and clinical outcomes, and methodological aspects (Supplementary Table S5). The synthesis emphasised description over direct comparison, highlighting methodological patterns and evidence gaps relevant to the economic evaluation of outpatient stroke rehabilitation (Supplementary Table S7). Methodological gaps were extracted as reported by the original study authors and synthesised thematically.

Risk of bias and quality assessment

The risk of bias (RoB) of included studies was assessed according to study design. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool whereas the non-randomised studies (retrospective and/or prospective cohorts) were assessed using the Risk of Bias in Non-randomised Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool. Detailed results are presented in Supplementary Tables S3 and S4.

Methodological quality was assessed using Drummond's checklist, as outlined in Methods for the Economic Evaluation of Health Care Programmes.21 This comprehensive tool apprises ten domains of economic evaluation across 33 sub-criteria, providing a structured framework for assessing, transparency, validity, and analytical rigour. Each study scored accordingly to the proportion of applicable criteria met, and quality was classified as high (≥85%), moderate (61–84%), or low (<60%) consistent with previous reviews.22, 23, 24, 25 The full checklist and results are presented in Supplementary Tables S8 and S9.

Two reviewers (AAM, SMT) independently conducted the assessments to minimise subjectivity, with disagreements resolved through discussion and, where necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (RC). This dual approach enabled a comprehensive evaluation of both the internal validity of study designs (via RoB 2 and ROBINS-I) and the methodological rigour of economic evaluations (via Drummond).

Ethics

As the review involved the analysis of previously published studies, ethical approval was not required.

Role of the funding source

There was no funding source for this study.

Results

Literature search

The database search identified 1800 records, of which 166 duplicates were removed (Fig. 1). After screening titles and abstracts (n = 1634), 1500 records were excluded. Among 134 full-text articles assessed for elgibility, 107 were excluded, most commonly for inappropriate study design (n = 69), not stroke-specific focus (n = 11), or inpatient-only rehabilitation (n = 4). Many excluded papers were classified as inappropriate study design because they represented partial evaluations, cost descriptions, or clinical effectiveness studies that did not meet the criteria for a full economic evaluation. In total, 27 studies met the inclusion criteria, underscoring the scarcity of full economic evaluations examining outpatient rehabilitation for stroke. A summary of the excluded full-text reports with reasons for exclusion is available in Supplementary Table S2.

Characteristics of included studies

The 27 included studies varied in terms of geography, study design, evaluation type, and rehabilitation model (Table 2). The UK accounted for over one-third of studies, with the remainder spread across Europe, Asia, North America, Africa, and Australia. Randomised controlled trials were most common (56%), followed by retrospective and prospective cohort studies. Cost-effectiveness analysis predominated (63%), while cost-consequence, cost-minimisation, and cost-benefit analysis were less frequent. Rehabilitation models were diverse: community- and home-based programmes were most common (each 30%), followed by outpatient clinic-based rehabilitation (22%), early supported discharge (11%), and telerehabilitation (7%). Most studies adopted a time horizon exceeding 12 months (63%), while fewer examined shorter follow-up periods. The healthcare perspective was dominated (70%), with fewer studies incorporating societal or patient-level costs.

Outcome measures reported across rehabilitation models

Across the included studies, outcome measures were clustered into three domains: health-related quality of life (HRQoL; 41%), functional outcomes (37%), and process measures (22%) (Table 3). HRQoL outcomes captured patient-reported wellbeing and daily functioning, functional outcomes included clinical or performance-based indicators of physical recovery and independence, and process measures assessed service delivery parameters such as length of stay, readmissions, or resource use. Community- and home-based rehabilitation contributed across multiple domains, with home-based programmes showing the greatest focus on functional outcomes (n = 6). Outpatient clinic-based rehabilitation models were mainly associated with HRQoL (n = 5), while early supported discharge was associated with process outcomes (n = 3). Telerehabilitation was represented in only two studies, reflecting limited evidence across outcome categories.

Evidence on cost-effectiveness across models

The included studies varied in how cost-effectiveness was assessed (Table 4). While many cost-effectiveness analyses reported incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs), others relied on p-values or cost comparisons, particularly within cost-minimisation or cost-consequence frameworks. This heterogeneity reflects methodological variations and inconsistent reporting standards across settings. Comparators also differed, ranging from inpatient rehabilitation to alternative outpatient or community-based models, highlighting that economic conclusions depend not only on the model under evaluation but also on the comparator used. For instance, home-based and community-based rehabilitation were generally cost-effective compared to inpatient or clinic-based care, whereas outpatient clinic-based models were favourable when compared with lower-cost community programmes. Where ICERs were reported, cost-effectiveness was interpreted against country-specific willingness-to-pay thresholds, meaning the interventions or models deemed cost-effective in one setting might not meet thresholds elsewhere. Early supported discharge typically demonstrated favourable outcomes compared with inpatient rehabilitation, while telerehabilitation evidence remained sparse and inconsistent. Although some studies compared more than one outpatient rehabilitation model, Table 4 classifies each study according to the primary outpatient model evaluated. Where the intervention and comparator are classified under the same rehabilitation model (e.g., CBR vs CBR), this reflects comparisons between different interventions or service organisation configurations within that category, rather than comparisons of identical services, with further details provided in Supplementary Table S6.

Methodological quality assessment

Using the Drummond checklist, most studies were rated as of moderate quality (n = 16), with fewer classed as high (n = 7) or low quality (n = 4) (Table 5). High-quality studies primarily employed cost-effectiveness analyses, most often evaluating community-based rehabilitation, with some home-based, outpatient, and ESD models. Moderate-quality studies showed greater methodological variations, including cost-consequence, cost-benefit, and cost-minimisation analysis, where home-based rehabilitation featured most prominently. Low-quality studies largely focused on outpatient clinic-based models and generally lacked methodological rigour or complete reporting.

Risk of bias assessment

Among the randomised control trials RCTs (n = 15), none were judged to have a high risk of bias, however, most were rated as having some concerns, primarily due to incomplete reporting of the randomisation process or selective outcome reporting (Fig. 2). These limitations may reduce confidence in both clinical and economic outcomes. Recent RCTs generally demonstrated lower risk of bias, suggesting improved methodological quality in contemporary outpatient rehabilitation evaluations. In contrast, most non-randomised studies (n = 12) were judged to have a serious risk of bias (Fig. 3), primarily due to confounding, from inadequate adjustment for baseline differences such as stroke severity, comorbidities, or social support. Additional issues were identified in outcome measurement and reporting consistency, while missing data and intervention fidelity were less frequently problematic. Identified evidence gaps

Recurring methodological and evidence gaps were identified across the included studies (Table 6). The most frequent limitations related to small sample sizes and short follow-up duration, which constrained statistical power and limited the ability to capture long-term costs and outcomes. Many studies also relied solely on a healthcare provider perspective, overlooking wider societal costs such as caregiver burden, productivity loss, or patient out-of-pocket expenses. Concerns regarding generalisability were common, as findings often derived from single-centre studies or highly specific health-systems context, limiting external validity. Several studies also used outcome measures that did not fully reflect stroke-specific recovery domains or health-related quality of life. Data quality issues, particularly reliance on secondary or approximated cost data, introduced further uncertainty. Other recurring limitations included simplified assumptions in economic models, restricted intervention scope, and the omission of caregiver and system-level opportunity costs. Full details of the evidence gaps for each study are presented in Supplementary Material S5.

Discussion

This systematic review offers an up-to-date and comprehensive synthesis of full economic evaluations of outpatient rehabilitation for stroke survivors. Taken together, the evidence suggest that community-based, home-based, and early supported discharge models may demonstrate more favourable cost-effectiveness than inpatient or conventional clinic-based rehabilitation. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to substantial heterogeneity in study designs, comparators, outcome measures, and methodological approaches. Evidence for telerehabilitation remains limited and methodologically heterogeneous, signalling an important but underdeveloped domain for future enquiry. These patterns matter for health systems seeking to expand access to effective rehabilitation within constrained budgets, and they underline the practical value of economic evidence for service planning and prioritisation. Prioritising these findings within the extant literature clarifies their contribution.

Earlier reviews commonly combined inpatient and outpatient care, or focused narrowly on home-based models, which limited inference about the relative value of distinct outpatient strategies. A 2005 review15 concluded that evidence was insufficient to determine the cost-effectiveness of community-based rehabilitation, but more recent studies have begun to address this gap.27,29,38,48 Our findings build on this trajectory, showing that when assessed across diverse settings, community- and home-based programmes consistently demonstrate favourable economic profiles. By reducing reliance on institutional care and supporting recovery in familiar environments, these models offer both clinical and economic advantages.

At the same time, our review highlights that cost-effectiveness is not uniform across all contexts. Some studies reported little difference between models or even favoured outpatient clinic-based rehabilitation. For instance, an evaluation from Thailand found hospital-based outpatient care to be more cost-effective than home visits, largely due to lower travel costs and service delivery patterns in that setting.42 Such findings underscore that economic outcomes are shaped not only by the rehabilitation model itself but also by contextual factors such as health system organisation, workforce availability, and the relative costs of transport and infrastructure.

Placing these results in a broader rehabilitation context further strengthens their credibility. Similar trends have been observed in cardiac rehabilitation, where home-based programmes are frequently cost-effective compared with centre-based services, particularly when outcomes are measured in QALYs.53 Musculoskeletal rehabilitation has shown comparable results, with home and community programmes delivering equivalent or superior outcomes at lower cost.54,55 Pulmonary rehabilitation for chronic lung disease has likewise benefited from community and home delivery, improving accessibility while maintaining cost-effectiveness.56,57 Although the present review focuses on stroke, the recurrence of these findings across conditions suggests a broader principle: where clinically appropriate, shifting rehabilitation away from hospital-centric models towards community- and home-delivered care can maintain or improve outcomes while enhancing cost-efficiency.

These patterns must, however, be interpreted in light of methodological diversity across the included studies. A notable strength is that many evaluations were embedded within randomised controlled trials or robust cohort designs, allowing outcomes and resource use to be assessed simultaneously. Several studies also reported QALYs and ICERs, enabling direct comparison with standard willingness-to-pay thresholds. In addition, a minority of evaluations adopted a societal perspective, accounting for indirect costs such as informal care, productivity losses, and patient travel expenses, which are particularly relevant in stroke. These methodological features enhance the validity and policy relevance of the evidence base.

Nevertheless, several recurring limitations temper the strength of the conclusions. Short follow-up horizons were common, with some studies measuring costs and outcomes only during the rehabilitation phase or up to 6–12 months post-stroke. This likely underestimate longer-term economic benefits such as reduced readmissions or delayed institutionalisation. Considerable heterogeneity was also evident in outcome measures, ranging from QALYs to functional scores and service use indicators, which limited comparability across studies and precluded meta-analysis. In addition, definitions of community-based rehabilitation varied widely, encompassing programmes of very different intensity and content, complicating interpretation of results.27,29,31,32,34,37,38,48 Evidence on telerehabilitation was particularly sparse, with few studies providing robust cost and outcome data, and almost none incorporating QALY estimates. These methodological gaps highlight areas where future research is urgently needed. A recent review identified only three telerehabilitation studies that reported both cost and clinical outcomes, highlighting a significant evidence gap and the need for further high-quality economic evaluations in this emerging area.58

These findings have important implications for policy and service planning. Economic evaluations play a central role in guiding decisions about which rehabilitation models receive funding and wider implementation. Demonstrating cost-effectiveness enables health systems to invest in models that provide the greatest value for both patients and society. Early supported discharge (ESD) illustrates this clearly by combining hospital and home-based care, ESD has consistently produced favourable economic outcomes and directly influenced national policy. In the United Kingdom, for example, ESD is now embedded as a cost-effective component of the stroke care pathway, reflecting how robust economic evidence can shape service delivery at scale.

Moreover, health systems should increasingly prioritise outpatient, community-, and home-based rehabilitation models, supported by early supported discharge, as part of routine stroke care. Realising this shift will require reforms to reimbursement structures that favour non-institutional care, investment in training and sustaining multidisciplinary community teams, and stronger integration of outpatient rehabilitation within national stroke strategies and performance frameworks. Embedding these models could not only improve efficiency but also enhance equity by making rehabilitation more accessible and patient-centred.

Equity considerations are central here. While high-income countries have generated valuable data to guide policy, the evidence base remains heavily concentrated in these settings, with only isolated studies from middle-income regions and none from low-income countries. This imbalance leaves major uncertainties about the cost-effectiveness of outpatient rehabilitation for stroke survivors in resource-constrained settings, where the majority of the global stroke burden lies. In such environments, access to institutional rehabilitation is often limited and community- or home-based programmes may represent the only feasible approaches. Without locally generated economic evidence, policymakers in LMICs face considerable challenges in making informed decisions about scaling and financing rehabilitation services. Addressing this gap will require targeted investment in LMIC-specific trials, capacity-building for health economic evaluation, and the development of context-appropriate costing methods that reflect local health system structures, wage levels, and reliance on informal caregiving.

In interpreting these findings, the limitations of this review itself must also be acknowledged. Considerable heterogeneity in study designs, interventions, and outcome measures prevented meta-analysis and limited the ability to conduct direct comparisons across studies. The restriction to English-language publications may have excluded relevant evidence from non-English-speaking countries, particularly in regions where health system structures differ substantially. Although the Drummond checklist provided a structured framework for assessing methodological quality, some subjectivity was unavoidable in interpreting partially met criteria, which may have influenced quality categorisation. These factors should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Recognising these limitations also helps to define priorities for future research. Addressing the gaps highlighted in this review requires methodological advances and broader contextual evidence. Priorities include the development of standardised outcome measures tailored to outpatient stroke rehabilitation, ideally through consensus-driven core outcome sets that capture functional independence, participation, and caregiver impact. Evaluations should extend follow-up periods to capture sustained clinical and economic benefits and incorporate comprehensive sensitivity analyses to improve confidence in findings. More evidence is also needed for emerging and under-researched models, particularly telerehabilitation, as well as for rehabilitation in low- and middle-income countries where health system contexts and resource constraints differ significantly. High-quality, multicentre trials adopting both healthcare and societal perspectives will be crucial for building a stronger and more globally relevant evidence base.

Overall, this systematic review suggests that home-based, community-based, and early supported discharge models of outpatient rehabilitation are generally more cost-effective than conventional clinic-based approaches, while telerehabilitation remains underexplored. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to substantial heterogeneity in study designs, comparators, outcome measures, and economic evaluation methods. Strengthening future evaluations through standardised outcome measures, longer-term follow-up, and inclusion of diverse health system contexts, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, will be critical to inform global policy. Addressing these gaps will ensure that stroke rehabilitation is not only clinically effective but also economically sustainable, equitable, and responsive to the needs of patients and health systems worldwide.
