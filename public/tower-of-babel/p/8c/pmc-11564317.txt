Tanshinone IIA Exerts Cardioprotective Effects Through Improving Gut-Brain Axis Post-Myocardial Infarction
Authors: Tong Zhu, Jie Chen, Mingxia Zhang, Zheng Tang, Jie Tong, Xiuli Hao, Hongbao Li, Jin Xu, Jinbao Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Myocardial infarction (MI) is a lethal cardiovascular disease worldwide. Emerging evidence has revealed the critical role of gut dysbiosis and impaired gut-brain axis in the pathological progression of MI. Tanshinone IIA (Tan IIA), a traditional Chinese medicine, has been demonstrated to exert therapeutic effects for MI. However, the effects of Tan IIA on gut-brain communication and its potential mechanisms post-MI are still unclear. In this study, we initially found that Tan IIA significantly reduced myocardial inflammation, apoptosis and fibrosis, therefore alleviating hypertrophy and improving cardiac function following MI, suggesting the cardioprotective effect of Tan IIA against MI. Additionally, we observed that Tan IIA improved the gut microbiota as evidenced by changing the α-diversity and β-diversity, and reduced histopathological impairments by decreasing inflammation and permeability in the intestinal tissues, indicating the substantial improvement of Tan IIA in gut function post-MI. Lastly, Tan IIA notably reduced lipopolysaccharides (LPS) level in serum, inflammation responses in paraventricular nucleus (PVN) and sympathetic hyperexcitability following MI, suggesting that restoration of Tan IIA on MI-induced brain alterations. Collectively, these results indicated that the cardioprotective effects of Tan IIA against MI might be associated with improvement in gut-brain axis, and LPS might be the critical factor linking gut and brain. Mechanically, Tan IIA-induced decreased intestinal damage reduced LPS release into serum, and reduced serum LPS contributes to decreased neuroinflammation with PVN and sympathetic inactivation, therefore protecting the myocardium against MI-induced injury.

Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI), a critical illness in cardiovascular disease (CVD), is defined as cardiomyocyte death induced by a sudden reduction in coronary blood supply, usually based on coronary atherosclerosis and thrombosis formation [1]. Over the past 30 years, there has been a continuous increase in the global prevalence of MI, which is also associated with high mortality and hospitalization rates, resulting in significant social and economic burdens [2]. In clinical practice, despite the implementation of early mechanical reperfusion strategies, such as percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), which has achieved a 60% decrease in mortality post-MI, many MI patients are still unable to receive interventional treatments due to the limited indications. In addition, the reperfusion itself might exacerbate the cardiac secondary injury [3]. Additionally, the commonly used drug treatments, such as captopril (an angiotensin-converting-enzyme (ACE) inhibitor), might also result in several side effects, including paroxysmal cough, proteinuria and renal disorders [4]. Therefore, developing effective therapeutics with minimal side effects is essential to improve the prognosis of MI patients. Recently, emerging studies have revealed the critical role of gastrointestinal dysbiosis in the pathological progression of MI, encompassing morphological alterations, impaired intestinal barrier integrity, gut microbiome dysfunction, and gut-brain axis disruption. For example, MI patients exhibited remarkable alterations in gastrointestinal morphology and microbiota, and the intestinal microbial metabolites are a potential indicator for the severity of MI [5]. Furthermore, restoring the intestinal microbiome has been demonstrated to affect the host immune composition and ameliorating cardiac dysfunction, suggesting the protective effects of intestinal structure on cardiac repair capacity against MI. Together, improving the gut dysbiosis emerges as a promising therapeutic strategy for combating MI.

Tanshinone IIA (Tan IIA), a representative fat-soluble component of Salvia miltiorrhiza (a traditional Chinese herbal remedy), has been shown to exhibit extensive immunomodulatory properties, including antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities [6]. Significantly, numerous studies have established the fundamental role of Tan IIA in cardiovascular protection for treating MI, and its cardioprotective effects are believed to be achieved through various mechanisms including anti-atherosclerosis, anti-arrhythmia, and repair of vascular endothelial cells [7]. For example, a study reported by Chen et al. revealed that Tan IIA exerts cardioprotective effects by reducing the inflammatory responses following MI, and the anti-inflammatory effects might be attributed to its capacity for decreasing the TNF-α/NF-κB signaling pathway [8]. In addition, Zhou et al. showed that facilitating the migration of BMSCs to the ischemic region via the SDF1/CXCR4 axis may be involved in the protective effects of Tan IIA on tissue repair and regeneration post-MI. [9]. However, the effects of Tan IIA on gut dysbiosis post-MI were not yet clear. Intriguingly, the neuroprotective properties of Tan IIA in mitigating Diabetes-induced cognitive impairment were highly associated with its restorative impact on gut pathology, including improvement in intestinal barrier integrity, reduction in the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and modulation of gut microbiome [10]. Thus, these findings raised the possibility that the cardioprotective effects of Tan IIA might be attributable to its capacity to repair gut impairments post-MI.

A substantial body of evidence has demonstrated that MI induces the hyperactivity of the sympathetic nervous system, which in turn contributes to the occurrence of malignant ventricular arrhythmias (VAs) and the development of cardiac electrophysiology disorder. Rebalancing the sympathetic system is considered a critical strategy to improve cardiac structure and function post-MI [11]. In addition, research by Yan et al. has identified that upregulated inflammatory cytokines in the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) is critical in promoting sympathetic overexcitation following MI [12]. Furthermore, amelioration of gut dysbiosis has demonstrated inhibitory effects on the inflammation response within PVN under hypertensive conditions [13]. Nevertheless, the potential impact of Tan IIA treatment in PVN inflammation and sympathetic activity post- MI remains unclear. Consequently, this research aimed to examine the Tan IIA’s effect on gut microbiota, the gut-brain axis, cardiac function, and its regulatory mechanisms after MI.

Materials and Methods

Animals

All male C57BL/6 J mice (8–10 weeks, weighting 25 ± 2 g) were obtained from Xi’an Jiaotong University Animal Laboratory. All mice were randomly housed in a controlled environment (23 ± 2 °C, 55 ± 5% relative humidity) under a 12 h light and dark cycle. Standard laboratory water and food were available ad libitum. All the animal experiments were approved by Xi’an Jiaotong University Laboratory Animal Administration Committee and were carried out strictly in accordance with Xi’an Jiaotong University Guidelines for Animal Experimentation [13].

MI Model

The MI mice model was created by ligating the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery, as previously reported [14]. Under sterile conditions, the mice were anesthetized by mechanical ventilation with 2% isoflurane at a flow rate of 2 L/min. After disinfection, the third to fourth intercostal space of each mouse was opened by a blunt thoracotomy to expose the heart. Then, the left anterior descending (LAD) was ligated with a 6–0 suture needle at about 2 mm below the pulmonary artery conus, followed by repositioning the heart. After repositioning the heart and compressing the chest to remove air from the cavity, a 3–0 suture needle was used to suture the thoracic cavity and skin. The Sham mice were subjected to the same surgery without ligation.

Experimental Design

The mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 24): Sham group, Tan IIA group, MI group and MI + Tan IIA group. Tan IIA (purity > 98% as determined by HPLC analysis) was obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (S31459). In the Sham group, mice were orally administered with 0.9% saline (10 mL/kg/day) daily for 60 days after a thoracotomy surgery without ligation. On the last day of 60-day period, mice were subjected to echocardiography assessment and then sacrificed; In the Tan IIA group, mice were treated with Tan IIA (30 mg/kg/day) dissolved in 0.9% saline by intragastric administration for consecutive 60 days, following a thoracotomy surgery without ligation. On the last day of 60-day period, mice underwent echocardiography assessment and were subsequently sacrificed; In the MI group, mice were given 0.9% saline (10 mL/kg/day) daily for 60 days following MI. On the last day of 60-day period, echocardiography was performed, and the mice were then sacrificed; In the MI + Tan IIA group, mice were given Tan IIA (30 mg/kg/day) intragastrically daily for 60 consecutive days post-MI. On the last day of 60-day period, mice underwent echocardiography assessment before being sacrificed.

Histological Analysis

The heart, brain and proximal colon tissues were quickly removed and cleaned by phosphate-buffered saline (Solarbio). Then, the tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde followed by embedded with paraffin. Sections were serially sectioned at 6 μm thickness. The slices (6 μm) were incubated with Hematoxylin–Eosin (HE) and Masson’s Trichrome staining as reported previously [13, 15]. The stained tissue slices were imaged under a light microscope (Nikon, Japan) to detect the histopathological alterations and cardiac fibrosis. For WGA staining, heart tissues were rapidly frozen in OCT at − 80 °C and then sliced into 10 μm thickness. Then, Slices were incubated with WGA-FITC (sigma # L4895) according to the manufacturers’ instruction as previously described [14]. The WGA-stained slices were observed under a confocal laser microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Heart Weight/Body Weight

At the end of experiment, the mice were anesthetized with isoflurane. Then, the heart was quickly excised, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (Solarbio), and heart weight was measured. The ratio of heart weight/body weight (HW/BW) was calculated to represent the level of cardiac hypertrophy [16].

Echocardiography

Echocardiographic analysis was performed according to a previously described protocol [17]. After being anesthetized with isoflurane, mice were put on a heating plate and then subjected to detection of cardiac function using Echocardiography equipment system (Visual Sonic Vevo 2100, Toronto, ON, Canada). Two-dimensional M-mode images were obtained to detect the systolic functional parameters such as left ventricular end-diastole diameter (LVEDd) and left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVESd). Fractional shortening (LVFS) and ejection fraction (LVEF) were measured using the Vevo 3100 software. All parameters were obtained and averaged from three consecutive cardiac cycles.

Stool Collection, DNA Extraction, Library Preparation, and Sequencing

Sterile tubes were used to collect fecal samples from six mice per group, which were then immediately stored at − 80 °C for further sequencing as previously described. Total genomic DNA was isolated from these samples with the Zymoresearch Fecal/Soil DNA isolation kit (Zymoresearch, Irvine, CA). DNA concentration was measured using NanoDrop One (Thermo Fischer, USA). The hypervariable V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using an ABI GeneAmp® 9700 PCR thermocycler. All PCR reactions were performed in triplicate. The PCR products were then extracted from 2% agarose gel and underwent gel purification using Axygen Biosciences (USA) before being quantified with Quantus™ Fluorometer (Promega, #E615) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. These purified amplicons were sequenced using an Illumina Miseq system (Illumina, USA). The sequencing data were processed using the Quantitative Insights in Microbial Ecology (QIIME II) software package (version 2021.11). Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were identified using QIIME II and taxonomy was assigned with Silva (version 138.1) as the reference database. Alpha and beta diversity analyses were conducted using QIIME, with alpha diversity calculated per sample via the R package Vegan and beta diversity displayed as 3D ordination plots based on unweighted UniFrac principal coordinate analysis [18].

Western Blotting

The mouse heart tissues were isolated and homogenized in ice-cold Radio Immunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China) containing the protease inhibitors Phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, Beyotime). The protein concentration was measured by using NanoDrop 2000C (Thermo Scientific, USA). After equal quantities, total proteins were separated on 8% or 10% SDS-PAGE by electrophoresis, followed by electroblotting onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). The membranes were blocked in 5% bovine albumin (BSA) in TBST for 2 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the PVFDs were incubated with the following primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C: anti-Bax (1:1000, 50599-2-Ig, Proteintech), anti-Bcl 2 (1:1000, 68103-1-Ig, Proteintech), anti-caspase 3 (1:1000, sc-56036, Santa Cruz), anti-TNF-α (1:1000, 60291-1-Ig, Proteintech), anti-IL-1β (1:1000, sc-52012, Santa Cruz), NF-κB p65 (1:1000, AF5006, Affinity)、p-NF-κB p65 (1:1000, AF2006, Affinity), anti-β-actin (1:1000, 20536-1-AP, Proteintech). Then, membranes were incubated with the anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:1000, A0216, Beyotime) or anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:1000, A0208, Beyotime) at room temperature for 1 h. The bands were detected using the ECL chemiluminescence system (Clinx Science Instruments, China). Densitometric analysis was performed using NIH Image J software (Bethesda, MD, USA), and data were normalized to β-actin [19].

Immunofluorescence Staining

The heart, brain and proximal colon slices were washed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 5 min, permeabilized in 0.3% TritonX-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Sigma) for 1 h. Then, tissue slices were incubated with the following antibodies overnight at 4 °C: anti-TNF-α (1:200, 60291-1-Ig, Proteintech), anti-IL-1β (1:1200, sc-52012, Santa Cruz), anti-Iba-1(1:200, GB11105-100, Servicebio), anti-IL-6 (1:200, A0286, Abclonal), anti-ZO-1 (1:200, 21773-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-Occludin (1:200, 27260-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-Cingulin (1:200, 21369-1-AP, Proteintech), anti- Tyrosine Hydroxylase (1:200, AB152, Millipore Sigma). Subsequently, the slices were then incubated in 594- Goat Anti-Rabbit (1:100, GB28301, Servicebio) or 594-Goat Anti- Mouse (1:100, GB25303, Servicebio) for one hour, and washed with PBS three times. Then, the slices were stained with DAPI (1:1000, Beyotime) for five minutes. Tissue slices images were captured with a fluorescent microscope (Nikon, DS-Ri2) and quantified using NIH Image J software. A confocal laser microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) was used for imaging [20].

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Blood samples were obtained from the abdominal aorta and centrifuged to prepare plasma samples according to a standard separation procedure involving centrifugation at 4000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. Norepinephrine (NE) and lipopolysaccharides (LPS) in plasma were determined using a norepinephrine ELISA kit (Abnova, Taiwan) and a limulus amebocyte lysate kit (Xiamen Bioendo Technology, #EC80545S) respectively, according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. To measure LPS concentrations, the optical density at 545 nm was recorded using a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, United States). For NE detection, the absorbance at 450 nm was analyzed [21, 22].

Statistical Analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± SD, and P-value < 0.05 was statistically significant. Data analysis was determined using one-way ANOVA followed by Turkey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 software [23].

Results

Tan IIA Improved Cardiac Function in Mice with MI

To determine the effects of Tan IIA treatment on the cardiac function of mice, the mice were treated with Tan IIA by oral gavage daily from 1 to 60 days post-MI (Fig. 1A). The echocardiography was conducted and revealed that MI led to a remarkable decrease in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS) and a significant increase in left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) and left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV), compared with Sham group (Fig. 1B–F). However, all these parameters were reversed by Tan IIA intervention, suggesting the cardioprotective effects of Tan IIA against MI. Then, we found that Tan IIA notably reduced MI-induced cardiac hypertrophy, as evidenced by the results of the appearance and transverse section of hearts and heart/body weight from all groups (Fig. 1G, H, L). In addition, Masson staining was performed and showed that the percentage of fibrotic area in the MI + Tan IIA group was lower than that in the MI group (Fig. 1I, J). Furthermore, HE and WGA staining results revealed that Tan IIA significantly reduced the MI-caused increase of cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area, compared with MI group (Fig. 1I, K). Tan IIA Ameliorated Myocardial Inflammation and Apoptosis Level Post-MI

To assess whether the anti-inflammatory action was involved in the protective effects of Tan IIA against MI, we detected the expression of inflammation-related proteins (TNF-α, IL-1β, p-NF-κB p65, and NF-κB p65) in the cardiac tissues from all groups. The results of immunofluorescence staining showed that Tan IIA remarkably alleviated MI-caused increase of TNF-α and IL-1β in the heart tissues, compared with MI group (Fig. 2A–D). Furthermore, Western blotting results revealed that the MI + Tan IIA group exhibited a lower level of p-NF-κB p65/ NF-κB p65, compared to the MI group (Fig. 2E, F), indicating the anti-inflammatory effects of Tan IIA post-MI. In addition, we also found that the apoptosis level was significantly reduced by Tan IIA treatment, as evidenced by the results that the ratio of Bax/Bcl2 and cleaved Caspase 3/Caspase 3 in the MI + Tan IIA group were lower than that in the MI group (Fig. 2E, G, H). Tan IIA Alleviated the Pathological Changes of the Intestine in MI Mice

Since the fundamental role of intestinal injury in the pathogenesis of MI, we evaluated the gut pathology changes in the proximal colon from all groups post-MI or Tan IIA treatment. HE staining was conducted and showed that MI induced a decrease in the villi length and an increase in the edema and thickness of the muscle layer, compared with the Sham group. However, the above changes were reversed by Tan IIA (Fig. 3A, B, D). Furthermore, the increased number of goblet cells was observed in the MI + Tan IIA group, compared with MI group (Fig. 3C). In addition, Masson staining results demonstrated that MI + Tan IIA mice exhibited less fibrotic area in the gut than that in MI mice, indicating that Tan IIA has the function of alleviating gut fibrosis following MI (Fig. 3E). The immunofluorescence staining results demonstrated that Tan IIA remarkably reduced MI-induced increase of TNF-α and IL-1β in the proximal colon, compared with the MI group (Fig. 5A–D). In line with the immunofluorescence results, we also found that MI + Tan IIA mice exhibited a remarkable reduction of TNF-α and IL-1β concentration in the intestinal tissues, compared to that of the MI mice (Fig. 5E–G). In addition, Western blotting results also revealed that p-NF-κB p65 level in the MI + Tan IIA group was significantly lower than that in MI group (Fig. 5E, H). Tan IIA Reshaped the Gut Microbial Microbiota Following MI

Given the pivotal role of gut microbiota in the progression of MI, 16S rDNA sequencing was conducted to investigate the effects of Tan IIA on the gut microflora composition. Consistent with previous research [13, 24], the MI group exhibited reduced Chao 1 richness (Fig. 6A) and Shannon diversity (Fig. 6B) compared to the Sham group. However, Tan IIA treatment significantly reversed the MI-induced reduction in both Chao 1 richness and Shannon diversity, indicating a notable improvement in α-diversity post-MI. Furthermore, β-diversity was assessed using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) of unweighted UniFrac distances, with results displayed in 3D charts (Fig. 6C). This analysis demonstrated a clear separation between the Sham and MI groups (ANOSIM, R = 0.442; P = 0.005) (Table 1). Importantly, the gut microbiota of the MI + Tan IIA group also showed a distinct separation from the MI group (ANOSIM, R = 0.676; P = 0.002) (Table 1). Discussion

In this study, we investigated whether Tan IIA’s cardioprotective effects against MI were due to its ability to restore the gut dysbiosis and gut-brain axis. Firstly, we found that Tan IIA treatment exerts profound effects on improving cardiac function, reducing cardiac fibrosis and alleviating cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, indicating the potent cardiovascular protective effect post-MI. Then, MI-induced disruption of gut microbiota and gut pathological impairments were effectively ameliorated by Tan IIA intervention, suggesting the positive regulatory effect of Tan IIA on alleviating intestinal pathologies following MI. Furthermore, our investigations also assessed Tan IIA’s effects on brain alterations, revealing a notable reduction in sympathetic nerve activity and neuroinflammation within PVN. Additionally, the LPS in the serum may function as a link within the gut-brain axis. Overall, these findings demonstrated that the cardioprotective effects of Tan IIA against MI occur, at least in part, through restoration of gut-brain axis, with serum LPS potentially serving as a crucial linking factor. MI is a serious cardiovascular condition that occurs when there is a reduction or complete blockage of blood flow to the heart, eventually resulting in inflammation, fibrosis and loss of cardiomyocytes. And, it is typically caused by a blood clot forming in the coronary artery due to a vulnerable plaque rupturing [25]. From a pathologic perspective, the sudden ischemic insult induces remarkable metabolic and ionic perturbations in the ischemic area as well as in remote zones, which eventually triggers a “wavefront” of cardiomyocyte death spreading from the subendocardium to the subepicardium [26]. Among multiple cell death pathways, apoptotic cell death has been found to play a crucial role in determining the final size of the infarct in the ischemic area. And, it is also closely linked to the progression of heart failure in the myocardium far from the ischemic area [27]. In addition, various damage-associated molecules released by dying cells can trigger prolonged or excessive inflammatory cascade responses, which is critical in converting necrotic tissue into the scar and promoting improper infarct healing [28]. As a critical factor of infarct healing, cardiac fibrosis, characterized by the excessive accumulation of noncontractile extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, plays a significant role in exacerbating myocardial damage and facilitating the progression toward end-stage heart failure [29]. Therefore, targeting the above biological processes, such as apoptosis, inflammation, and fibrosis, could be a potential therapeutic strategy for mitigating cardiac damage and improving the prognosis of MI. In line with prior studies [8, 30], this research demonstrated that Tan IIA induced a significant inhibition in myocardial apoptosis, inflammation, and fibrosis (Figs. 1 and 2), which provides further evidence for the remarkable cardioprotective effects of Tan IIA against MI.

Considerable evidence indicates that sympathetic nerve hyperactivity plays a pivotal role in the pathological progression of MI [31]. The ischemic insult can damage the cardiac sympathetic nerves, leading to the loss of sympathetic innervation within the viable myocardium in the infarct zone. Notably, the viable but denervated myocardium exhibits denervation supersensitivity contributing to the risk of arrhythmias post-MI [32, 33]. In addition to cardiac denervation, sympathetic reinnervation is also critical in promoting sympathetic hyperactivity and cardiac arrhythmia [34]. Following ischemic injury, sympathetic axon fibers undergo infarction and degeneration, succeeded by nerve sprouting and neural remodeling. Although sympathetic reinnervation aids in the performance of surviving myocardium, excessive nerve sprouting might cause heterogeneous patterns of sympathetic innervation. Furthermore, these heterogeneity patterns could be able to elevate repolarization dispersion, exacerbate sympathetic overexcitation, and trigger lethal arrhythmias. Consequently, inhibiting sympathetic nerve hyperactivity is considered a potent strategy for treating MI [33, 35]. In this study, we found that Tan IIA significantly inhibited the expression of sympathetic activity indexes, including norepinephrine (NE) and tyrosine hydroxylase (TH, an enzyme critical for NE synthesis) (Fig. 8). These findings suggested that the beneficial effects of Tan IIA were partially attributed to its capacity for suppressing sympathetic activity.

PVN is a critical cardiovascular regulatory brain region, which functions as integrating sensory input and projecting to the sympathetic system [36]. Importantly, accumulating studies have established a strong association between PVN inflammation and sympathetic hyperactivity [12, 37]. For example, Zhu et al. reported that microinjection of the pro-inflammatory cytokines (PIC), TNF-a or IL-1β, into the PVN could notably increase the sympathetic activity, blood pressure and cardiac sympathetic afferent reflex (CSAR), aggravating the myocardial impairments and dysfunction post-MI [38]. Furthermore, inhibiting the microglia activation and PVN inflammation via knockdown of microglial toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) exerted remarkable inhibitory effects on sympathetic overexcitation and subsequent ventricular arrhythmias VAs post-MI [12]. Additionally, Wang et al. also demonstrated that the treatment with SN50 (a selective inhibitor of NF-κB) in the PVN led to a remarkable inhibition in sympathetic overactivation as evidenced by the reduction in NE and glutamate (indicators of sympathetic activity) [39]. Therefore, we then investigated the effects of Tan IIA on PVN inflammation and found that Tan IIA led to a remarkable inhibition of inflammatory response in the PVN, as demonstrated by a reduction in the levels of inflammatory markers (TNF-α and IL-1β) and percentage of activated microglia (Fig. 7B–I). Thus, it is reasonable to speculate that the inhibition effects of Tan IIA on sympathetic activity might be mediated via the regulation of neuroinflammation in PVN.

It is now widely acknowledged that the gut microbiome, due to its astounding variety and number of microorganisms, possesses significant potential for generating various functional metabolite activities. Physiologically, gut microbiota is crucial in maintaining host homeostasis through various biological reactions, such as metabolic exchange and co-metabolism of substrates, eventually developing a mutualistic relationship with the host [40, 41]. In addition, the gut microbiota has been shown to regulate the host immune system, such as the composition, migration, and function of multiple immune cell types, indicating its crucial role in repairing the damaged tissues in numerous immune-mediated diseases [42]. Importantly, emerging studies have revealed that gut dysbiosis is highly involved in the pathological conditions of MI [14, 43]. For example, Ju and colleagues have found a decrease in the phylum Firmicutes and an increase in the phylum Bacteroidetes from Chinese MI patients compared with healthy controls, providing solid evidence of the alterations in gut microbiota post-MI [44]. Additionally, recent research has firmly established a strong connection between the gut microbiome and the severity of MI, and the potential mechanisms underlying this association may involve the impact of microbiota and its metabolites on atherosclerosis progression and immune regulation. Furthermore, Xie et al. revealed that loss of gut microbiota could induce a remarkable increase in the rates of ventricular rupture and death, and restoring gut microbial communities could facilitate the cardiac repair capacity and improve the survival of mice, suggesting that restoring gut dysbiosis was considered a potent strategy to combat MI [42]. However, it remains largely unclear whether the cardioprotective effects of Tan IIA following MI are mediated by improving gut microbiota. Our study revealed that Tan IIA significantly remodeled the gut microbiota post-MI as evidenced by the significant changes in the α-diversity and β-diversity (Fig. 6). Overall, this research indicated that improvement in the microbiota dysbiosis may be the key factor for Tan IIA treatment’s effectiveness against MI.

It should be noted that the intestinal epithelial barrier plays a crucial role in maintaining gut homeostasis by regulating the complex interactions between gut microbes and the host immune system. Its primary function is to shield the host from luminal antigens, pathogens, and toxins while permitting selective permeability to water, nutrients, and electrolytes, thus forming the first line of defense against harmful microorganisms and pathogenic antigens. Tight junctions, essential components of the intestinal epithelial barrier, are located at the apex of the intestinal epithelium and consist of proteins such as ZO-1, Occludin, and Cingulin. Disruption of these intercellular tight junction complexes can lead to intestinal epithelial barrier breakdown and the translocation of luminal contents into the lamina propria, resulting in gut dysbiosis, uncontrolled immune and inflammatory responses, and ultimately various pathological states [45, 46]. Following MI, reduced cardiac output causes increased intestinal wall ischemia and edema, further damaging the intestinal epithelial barrier and enhancing epithelial permeability, ultimately resulting in gut dysbiosis [47]. Moreover, increased intestinal permeability facilitates the translocation of gut-derived bacterial endotoxins into the bloodstream and other organs, including the heart, ultimately exacerbating inflammation and apoptosis in cardiac tissues [48]. In this study, we investigated the effects of Tan IIA on intestinal epithelial barrier, and found that Tan IIA reversed the MI-induced increase in permeability and inflammation in intestinal tissues, evidenced by that increased levels of ZO-1, Occludin, and Cingulin, and decreased levels of TNF-α, IL-1β and p-NF-κB p65 in the MI + Tan IIA group, compared with MI group, demonstrating the substantial improvement effects of Tan IIA on the intestinal pathological changes (Figs. 4 and 5). Overall, this study suggests that the beneficial effects of Tan IIA on gut microbiota against MI may stem from its role in maintaining the integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier.

Notably, sharply increasing evidence revealed that there is a strong link between gut dysbiosis and PVN neuroinflammation [24, 49]. For example, Kang et al. have shown that reshaping the gut pathology wall via fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) led to a persistent decrease in the percentage of activated microglial cells and mRNA levels of inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-1β) in the PVN. Since our results have revealed that Tan IIA could induce an improvement in gut dysbiosis post-MI, we hypothesized that the modulations in gut structure might be partially involved in the Tan IIA’s inhibitory effects on PVN inflammation [13]. Significantly, LPS has been considered as the major harmful component of gut microbiota metabolite and a non-negligible contributor to the aggravation of inflammatory-related responses following MI [50, 51]. Upon the increased gut permeability and disruption in the gut microbiota induced by MI, LPS derived from intestinal pathogenic bacteria, a crucial factor in the gut-brain neural circuit, was translocated into the circulation and increased the activation of monocytes and neutrophils by Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-mediated pathway, which plays a critical role in triggering the occurrence and development of inflammatory cascade reactions [28]. Interestingly, our results revealed that Tan IIA significantly inhibited the MI-induced increase of LPS level in serum post-MI (Fig. 7A), suggesting that the improvement of Tan IIA in restoring the gut-brain axis post-MI is possibly mediated via the modulation of LPS.

Our study still has several fundamental limitations. Firstly, while our research demonstrated that Tan IIA treatment leads to marked enhancements in gut microbiota, PVN inflammation and cardiac function post- MI, we did not definitively exclude the possibility that the cardioprotective effects of Tan IIA might be independent of gut microbiota and instead arise from alternative pathways. Therefore, in the subsequent experiments, we will investigate whether the depletion of gut microbes caused by antibiotic treatment eliminates the cardioprotective effects of Tan IIA, and whether fecal microbiota transplantation from MI + Tan IIA mice to MI mice can mitigate cardiac pathology and improve cardiac function. Moreover, it is essential to verify whether the observed brain alterations following Tan IIA treatment are indeed mediated by improvements in gut microbiota, thereby substantiating the protective influence of Tan IIA on the gut-brain axis. Thirdly, the exclusive use of male mice in this study also poses a significant limitation. Women experiencing MI not only present distinct symptoms compared to men, but also face unique complications associated with hormonal factors such as estrogen. In addition, older women lose the protective effects of estrogen, contributing to the complexity of MI mechanisms in women. However, historical research has primarily focused on male subjects, leading to a gap in understanding of the mechanisms underlying MI in women [52]. Thus, future research should include the study of female subjects and incorporate vascular measurements, such as pressure/wire myography or pulse wave velocity (PWV), to address this critical oversight. Moreover, due to the inherent limitations in mice models, future experiments are required to translate these findings into clinical applications for treating MI. Despite these limitations, our findings also highlight a noteworthy contribution of Tan IIA in ameliorating gut dysbiosis and in the recovery of the gut-brain axis post-MI.

In Conclusion, we investigated the effects of Tan IIA on gut-brain axis and its underlying mechanisms post-MI in this study. Firstly, we found that Tan IIA is critical in alleviating cardiac dysfunction, ameliorating cardiac pathology and inhibiting cardiac fibrosis after MI, suggesting the beneficial effects of Tan IIA against MI. Then, MI-induced gut dysbiosis, including disruption of gut microbiota, impaired intestinal barrier integrity as well as increased inflammation, was restored by Tan IIA treatment, indicating its strong regulatory effects on the gut disturbances. Additionally, Tan IIA also led to a remarkable decrease in PVN inflammation and sympathetic hyperexcitability, demonstrating the profound modulation of Tan IIA on MI-induced brain alterations. Furthermore, serum LPS may act as a pivotal mediator within the gut-brain axis. Collectively, these findings revealed that Tan IIA’s cardioprotective properties against MI are mediated, at least in part, by the restoration of the gut-brain axis, with serum LPS potentially acting as a pivotal connecting factor. Mechanistically, Tan IIA-induced enhancements in the gut microbiota and intestinal structural integrity result in decreased release of LPS into the bloodstream, which subsequently inhibits neuroinflammation within PVN and prevents sympathetic inactivation, thus mitigating myocardial damage and enhancing cardiac function.
