DeSpaST: deconvoluting spatial transcriptomics signals to cell-level resolution using histology images
Authors: Qin Zhou, Shidan Wang, Yi Jiang, Yang Liu, Tingyi Wanyan, Kenian Chen, Zhuoyu Wen, Zhikai Chi, Peiran Quan, Kevin Lutz, Ruichen Rong, Lin Xu, Guanghua Xiao, Yang Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvances in spatial transcriptomics (ST) technologies enable spatially resolved gene expression profiling, yet most platforms remain limited to spot-level resolution, where each measurement aggregates signals from multiple cells and obscures cell-specific programs and microenvironmental interactions. Existing computational approaches either provide limited sub-spot refinement or rely on matched single-cell RNA sequencing references that are costly and difficult to obtain. Here, we present DeSpaST (Deconvoluting Spatial Transcriptomics Signals to Cell-Level Resolution Using Histology Images), a dynamic edge–conditioned graph convolutional network that deconvolves spot-level ST data into cell-level gene expression profiles using only paired histology images. DeSpaST extracts nucleus-level morphological features, constructs directed cellular interaction graphs, and integrates spatial and transcriptional information through message passing. Validated across four cancer datasets with orthogonal Xenium, immunofluorescence, ablation, and interslice evaluations, DeSpaST enhances spatial resolution, facilitates downstream cellular-level analyses, and provides deeper insights into tissue biology.Keywords: spatial transcriptomics, graph convolutional network, cell-level deconvolution, histology image analysis, tumor microenvironment, gene expression prediction

Background

Spatial transcriptomics (ST) technologies have made significant progress in transitioning transcriptomic research from the cell level to a 2D spatial cell system. This transition is crucial for investigating cell–cell communication, tissue heterogeneity, and the tumor microenvironment, offering new insights into intricate biological processes. In contrast to single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), which requires tissue dissociation, ST technologies profile gene expression and preserve the spatial composition of cells within the native tissue environment [1]. ST technologies (e.g. 10× Genomics Visium [2], Nanostring GeoMx DSP [3], HDST [4], and Slide-seq [5]) utilize unique spatial barcodes to label the spatial locations and capture the gene expression at thousands of barcoded spatial locations (referred to as spots) within one experiment [6]. While these methods achieve high detection efficiency, their spatial resolution is inherently limited. The barcoded spot area (Spot diameter: 10× Genomics Visium: 55 μm, Nanostring GeoMx DSP: 10 μm [7], and Slide-seq: 10 μm [8]) encompasses a diverse population of cells, ranging from a few cells up to 200 cells [9]. To accurately distinguish the mixed-cell populations and uncover biological inferences from the ST data, it is essential to develop computational models that can enhance the resolution of ST data.

Several computational methods have been developed to improve the spatial resolution of ST data. Bayesian-based methods such as BayesSpace [10] refine gene expression to sub-spot resolution, only yielding modest improvement. Deep-learning-based approaches such as SPOTlight [11], Tangram [12], cell2location [13], and CARD [14] integrate ST data with scRNA-seq data, mapping related gene expression cells to ST coordinates or reconstructing the ST landscape from various cell combinations. However, the reliance on scRNA-seq data significantly raises the experimental costs and introduces constraints, including limited cell types and potential biases in spatial reconstruction accuracy.

Instead of relying on the scRNA-seq data, integrating histology images with ST data presents a more feasible and promising way to enhance ST resolution. Most ST platforms, such as Visium and HDST, typically provide corresponding histology images, like hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining images, which offer high-resolution morphological and spatial information for individual cells across the tissue sections. Deep-learning-based methods such as TESLA [15], XFuse [16], and iStar [17] have been developed to integrate the histology image with ST data to enhance ST resolution. However, these methods primarily enhance resolution at the pixel level but fall short of achieving cell-level gene expression resolution. Furthermore, these methods focus on extracting pixel level histological features [e.g. pixel intensities in red, green, blue (RGB) channels], neglecting true cell features and cell–cell interactions, which compromises model accuracy. A specialized model that directly incorporates actual cell-level features is necessary to improve the biological application of ST data.

Many fundamental biological questions—including the delineation of cell-type boundaries, characterization of the tumor microenvironment, and inference of cell–cell communication—inherently require cell-level resolution. However, true single-cell spatial assays such as Xenium or MERFISH remain expensive, limited in gene panel size, and not universally available, making spot-level ST the dominant modality in practice. Moreover, matched scRNA-seq data from the same tissue, which several existing deconvolution methods depend on, is not always obtainable and may introduce additional biases when tissue dissociation alters cell-type composition. A computational approach that can reliably infer cell-level expression directly from spot-level ST data and routine histology images therefore addresses a practical and widely encountered gap in the field.

In this work, we developed an innovative model called DeSpaST (Deconvoluting Spatial Transcriptomics Signals to Cell-Level Resolution Using Histology Images), which incorporates the cell level features from histology image with ST data using dynamic edge–conditioned graph convolution neural network (Fig. 1, and Supplementary Fig. S1). Starting with a histology image, we perform cell segmentation and explicitly extract cell morphological and spatial interaction features, followed by constructing the cellular graphic interaction structures of the tissue. We then build a graph deconvolution model that combines ST data with these morphological features and interaction structures to deconvolute spot-level gene expression into cell-level gene expression profiles. In contrast to the previous methods that primarily rely on pixel-level information and use convolutional neural network (CNN)-like structures to encode the features, which often failing to capture cell–cell interaction information. DeSpaST leverages the graph convolutional networks (GCNs) to fully capture the cellular interaction topologies by constructing cellular interaction graphic structures. Unlike conventional CNNs, GCNs operate on non-Euclidean data structures, enabling comprehensive modeling of diverse cellular interaction topologies.

DeSpaST offers several practical advantages. First, it does not require scRNA-seq references, reducing experimental cost and avoiding dependence on matched dissociated-cell datasets. Second, it leverages morphology and local tissue architecture to improve spatial localization and denoising within spots, which is particularly valuable in heterogeneous tumors where boundaries and niches vary over short distances. Third, DeSpaST’s inferred cell-level expression supports downstream analyses that are difficult at spot resolution, such as cell-type mapping at cellular granularity, spatial domain characterization, and trajectory inference. In our experiments, these advantages manifest as improved agreement with orthogonal measurements (e.g. Xenium), clearer spatial patterns, and improved recovery of spatial domains, with robustness supported by ablations demonstrating that performance depends on both transcriptional input and morphology rather than visual cues alone. Together, these considerations motivate DeSpaST as a practical and interpretable approach for cell-level resolution inference from spot-based ST, while clarifying the conditions under which it is most reliable.

Result

Overview of the DeSpaST model

DeSpaST requires only two inputs: a histology image and ST data (Fig. 1a). Histology images offer the individual cell spatial organization and morphology characterization, while the ST data provide gene expression at the spot level. To explicitly extract the cell spatial and organization characterizations from the histology image, we first employed the HD-staining deep-learning algorithm [18, 19] to segment and classify the cell nucleus from the tissue image. Alternatively, other methods for cell or nuclei segmentation could also be applied at this step. We then extracted the morphology features for each cell to construct the cellular graphic structures (see Fig. 1a and Method section for details). DeSpaST builds the graphic convolution networks by performing the four following steps: (i) extract image patches based on the spot coordinates in the ST data; (ii) construct a directed cellular graphic structure for each patch using nearest neighbors based on the Euclidean distance from a cell to its neighbors; and (iii) derive features from the directed cellular graphic structures to incorporate the spatial and morphology information of the cells into the graph convolutional network. Each node in the graph represents a cell. Node features include cell size, shape, and other morphological features. Edges denote spatial and morphological relationships between cells. Edge features include cell spatial proximity, cell connectivity angle, and interaction edge categorization (Supplementary Fig. S2) [4]. The model is then constructed using two convolutional layers and one fully connected layer (Supplementary Fig. S1). All cell nodes are updated through a message passing from all neighboring nodes in each convolutional layer, which incorporates both morphological and cell–cell spatial interaction features of neighboring cells. In this study, we demonstrate DeSpaST’s ability in accurately capturing cell-level gene expression to overcoming spatial resolution limitations in ST data, using breast, lung, and ovarian cancer datasets as examples. DeSpaST offers a new convenient tool for advancing biomedical research, with broad applicability to diverse spatial transcriptomics datasets.

DeSpaST effectively enhances gene expression resolution

To assess the efficacy of DeSpaST, we applied the model to a well-studied breast cancer 10× Visium dataset [20]. We then conducted a comparative analysis of DeSpaST’s performance against the TESLA model [15], which is a machine-learning framework designed to enhance gene expression to pixel-level resolution [15]. DeSpaST demonstrated notable advancements in elucidating gene expression patterns. To illustrate, we present two marker genes, MS4A1 and ERBB2, as representative examples (Fig. 1b). The MS4A1 gene encodes the surface molecule CD20, which serves as a widely recognized marker indicative of B-cell lineage [21]. ERBB2 encodes the human epidermal growth factor receptor-2 (HER2) protein [22], which is used as a tumor marker in breast cancer diagnosis [23, 24]. For each gene, the cell-level gene expression profile from DeSpaST, spot-level gene expression pattern from Visium ST data, and the pixel-level gene expression patterns from TESLA (Fig. 1b), iStar, and XFuse (Supplementary Fig. S3a and b) were plotted separately. Compared to Visium, TESLA, iStar, and XFuse, DeSpaST showed the gene expression profiles with highest clarity and precision for both genes, successfully enhancing the gene expression resolution. Notably, the gene expression patterns from DeSpaST correlated well with the pathologist-annotated regions. DeSpaST high ERBB2 gene expression cells closely matched the pathologist-annotated tumor regions. Also, the DeSpaST high MS4A1 expressed cells were well aligned with the annotated immune cell regions. In contrast, Visium, TESLA, iStar, and XFuse did not achieve the same level of visual clarity as DeSpaST in these regions. The MS4A1 gene expression profile is particularly noteworthy because it is nearly imperceptible in the Visium ST data and in the reconstructions produced by TESLA (Fig. 1b, right), iStar, and XFuse (Supplementary Fig. S3b), whereas DeSpaST accurately discerns the MS4A1 gene expression pattern and immune cell regions.

Remarkably, DeSpaST’s cell-level gene expression predictions demonstrate its capability and reliability in distinguishing between different cell layers. In three specific areas (Fig. 1c and d, and Supplementary Fig. S4), pathologists meticulously annotated the invasive carcinoma and immune cell regions to delineate their boundaries in the H&E images. The gene expression patterns predicted by DeSpaST effectively differentiated between these immune cell and tumor cell regions. DeSpaST accurately captured the elevated expression of ERBB2 in tumor regions and the elevated expression of MS4A1 in immune cell regions. The distinct gene expression patterns of MS4A1 and ERBB2 predicted by DeSpaST (Fig. 1c and d, and Supplementary Fig. S4b) precisely delineated the tumor and immune boundaries. In contrast, Visium, TESLA, iStar, and XFuse could not accurately delineate these cell layers and boundaries (Supplementary Fig. S3a and b); instead, they only provided ambiguous patterns, which indicates a lack of precision in defining the regions. Moreover, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S4c, DeSpaST demonstrated exceptional potential in precise cell type annotation. Beyond the highly expressed ERBB2 tumor region, DeSpaST identified an additional layer outside the tumor region with abundant MS4A1 expression, which had not been previously annotated. Upon closer examination of the H&E image by the pathologist, the layer was found to be a lightly stained layer, containing fibrous connective tissues interspersed with inflammatory cells, crucial for restricting tumor expansion. This finding of interspersed immune cells is consistent with the MS4A1 gene expression pattern predicted by DeSpaST.

DeSpaST accurately predicts cellular-level gene expression

The above comparison between pathology annotation and DeSpaST highlights the resolution enhancement power of DeSpaST. To further evaluate the model’s performance, we conducted a direct and comprehensive comparison of cellular-level expression for individual genes. In addition to the Visium spot-level gene expression measurement, Janesick et al. [25] also measured the cellular-level gene expression of hundreds of genes through the Xenium experiment. We applied DeSpaST to this breast cancer Visium dataset from Janesick et al. [25], segmented and classified the cell nucleus from the H&E image (Supplementary Fig. S5), and deconvolving spot-level ST signals into cell-level gene expression profiles. Despite the inherent limitations of adjacent-section comparisons (e.g. local distortion, cell displacement, and microenvironmental differences), DeSpaST showed strong concordance with Xenium in spatial distribution patterns. For example, for the breast cancer marker gene CCND1 [26, 27] (Fig. 2a) and T-cell marker gene CD69 [28, 29] (Fig. 2b), DeSpaST accurately predicted the gene expression patterns, in agreement with the observed patterns in the Xenium. Both Visium and TESLA failed to resolve these patterns adequately, especially in the complex tissue regions. Consider the top of the tissue section (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. S6a) where intricate tumor and immune cell domains are present. Only DeSpaST successfully captured gene expression within intricate microenvironments (Fig. 2d and e), accurately reflecting the patterns observed in the Xenium experiment. Conversely, TESLA and Visium failed to capture these spatial variations in gene expression, resulting in unclear expression patterns. In the case of the CD69 gene (Fig. 2b), the Visium ST data are noisy and lack a clear expression pattern. Although TESLA reduces some noise, it fails to capture the inherent gene expression patterns accurately. In contrast, DeSpaST effectively eliminates noise and accurately discerns the true gene expression pattern.

With the gene pattern identified by DeSpaST, our next step involves a quantitative assessment of the performance enhancement achieved by the DeSpaST model. To facilitate this assessment, spot-level gene expression was reconstructed by taking the average expression of cells inside each spot. For quantitative comparison across methods, we reconstructed spot-level expression by averaging the predicted cellular expressions within each Visium spot and then computed gene-wise Pearson correlations against Xenium for DeSpaST, raw Visium, TESLA, iStar, and XFuse (Supplementary Fig. S3). DeSpaST exhibits a stronger correlation to Xenium (r = 0.754 for CCND1 gene and r = 0.665 for CD69 gene) than both Visium (r = 0.477 for CCND1 gene, and r = 0.239 for CD69 gene) and TESLA (r = 0.720 for CCND1 gene, and r = 0.424 for CD69 gene) (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. S3c). Similar enhancements in clarity and noise reduction in gene expression patterns were observed in other genes as well, such as the ERBB2 and MS4A1 genes (Supplementary Fig. S7). DeSpaST demonstrated strong consistency with Xenium experiments for both of these genes, achieving a correlation of r = 0.850 for the ERBB2 gene and r = 0.610 for the MS4A1 gene (Supplementary Fig. S7c and d). In addition to the above genes, to assess transcriptome-wide generalizability, we computed spot-level Pearson correlations for all examined Xenium genes and summarize the overall trend using correlation density plots (Fig. 2g; Supplementary Fig. S3c): DeSpaST achieves higher overall agreement with Xenium (median r = 0.518) than Visium (median r = 0.198) and TESLA (median r = 0.479), and the top 20% highest-density region summary consistently favors DeSpaST, indicating systematic improvement beyond a small set of marker genes.

To address the concern that histology-guided models might rely on visual cues and to validate key graph-construction hyperparameters, we performed a comprehensive set of ablation and sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Fig. S8). First, randomizing the input transcriptional counts (“Random count”) caused a substantial collapse in performance—gene-wise correlation with Xenium dropped (CD69: r = 0.188; CCND1: r = 0.379), the overall summary decreased (top 20% high-density mean correlation: 0.174), and downstream spatial domain agreement deteriorated (NMI: 0.033; ARI: 0.0196)—demonstrating that accurate transcriptional signals are essential and that morphology alone cannot recover meaningful spatial expression. Second, randomizing morphology features (“Random image info”) also reduced performance relative to the full model (e.g. CD69: r = 0.423 versus 0.665; top 20% high-density mean: 0.202 versus 0.582; NMI: 0.169 versus 0.194; ARI: 0.0910 versus 0.1167), indicating that morphology provides complementary information but is not solely responsible for DeSpaST’s gains. Finally, we evaluated the robustness of the k-nearest-neighbor (kNN) cell-graph parameter by varying on the breast dataset: extreme values were suboptimal (e.g. top-20%-density mean correlation = 0.161 at and 0.220 at ), while intermediate values (–12) consistently achieved higher performance (0.58–0.64) and stable domain-detection agreement, supporting our choice of as a balance between locality and connectivity (Supplementary Fig. S8). Together, these analyses show that DeSpaST integrates both transcriptional and morphological information and that its performance is stable across a biologically reasonable range of neighborhood sizes.

To further test whether the deconvoluted outputs preserve biologically meaningful spatial structure beyond individual marker genes, we evaluated each method using a downstream spatial domain detection task on the breast cancer 10× Visium dataset [20]. As shown in (Supplementary Fig. S9), we used the manually annotated domains as ground-truth labels. For each tool, we took the predicted cell-by-gene expression matrix together with per-cell spatial coordinates as input, constructed (i) an expression kNN graph in PCA space and (ii) a spatial kNN graph in 2D coordinate space, fused them into a hybrid connectivity matrix, and inferred domains using Leiden community detection (parameters reported in Supplementary Information). We then quantified concordance with manual annotations using NMI and ARI (Supplementary Fig. S9b; higher is better). DeSpaST achieved the best agreement with manual labels (NMI = 0.194; ARI = 0.117), outperforming iStar (NMI = 0.182; ARI = 0.056), XFuse (NMI = 0.033; ARI = 0.011), and TESLA (NMI = 0.143; ARI = 0.031), indicating that DeSpaST’s deconvolution better preserves domain-level organization for downstream spatial analyses. Supplementary Fig. S9c further visualizes the inferred domains from each method, where DeSpaST produces domain boundaries that are qualitatively more consistent with the expert annotations.

The remarkable cell-level resolution achieved by DeSpaST creates new possibilities for examining cell–cell communications within a spatial context. For instance, within the cellular milieu surrounding the breast ducts (Supplementary Fig. S10), we detected elevated expressions of breast cancer marker genes CCND1 and ERBB2 (Supplementary Fig. S10a and c). In contrast, immune cell marker genes CD69 and MS4A1 (Supplementary Fig. S10b and d) exhibited high expression in the outside layer adjacent to the tumor cells. The gene expression patterns predicted by DeSpaST align with the Xenium gene panel, while TESLA failed to predict them accurately. DeSpaST’s findings suggest possible interactions between immune and tumor cells, highlighting its potential for further studies on the tissue microenvironment and cell–cell communication.

DeSpaST accurately captures the cell-level protein expression

As products of gene expression, protein expressions are generally correlated with differentially expressed mRNAs [30, 31]. Next, we evaluate whether the gene expression patterns obtained by DeSpaST could reflect protein expressions at the cell level [25]. We plotted and compared the MS4A1 and ERBB2 gene expression patterns with the corresponding HER2 and CD20 protein immunofluorescence (IF) signals (Fig. 3a and b). Interestingly, the gene expression patterns predicted by DeSpaST closely align with protein expression profiles, while Visium and TESLA are not able to capture them. We calculated the correlations between predicted MS4A1 gene expression and Xenium IF CD20 protein signals. DeSpaST exhibits superior performance compared to TESLA and Visium with higher correlation (r = 0.622 for DeSpaST versus r = 0.420 for Visium and r = 0.401 for TESLA; Fig. 3c). DeSpaST also demonstrated a notably higher correlation of ERBB2 gene expression with HER2 protein expression (r = 0.839) when compared to Visium (r = 0.821) and TESLA (r = 0.660), as illustrated in Fig. 3d. Although Visium and TESLA also showed good correlations with the HER2 IF signals, DeSpaST significantly outperformed them in predicting fine-grained details gene expression patterns (Supplementary Fig. S11). Notably, only DeSpaST accurately captured the comprehensive pattern of ERBB2 gene expression, depicting the tumor’s shape around the breast duct in alignment with IF protein signals. In contrast, Visium and TESLA predicted a vague outline of ERBB2 expression, lacking finer details and failing to depict a clear pattern.

Application of DeSpaST for cell type identification

Given the highly precise cell-level gene expression predicted by DeSpaST, we employed DeSpaST for downstream analysis. This involved leveraging the predicted cell-level gene expression to delineate the spatial distribution of various cell types. Unlike ST data, where one spot may contain multiple cells, DeSpaST’s cellular-level resolution offers a superior depiction of cell-type distribution. Utilizing DeSpaST’s gene expression predictions as input, we applied the cell-type identification algorithm ScType [32] to assign the cell type to each single cell. The resulting cell-type profiles were mapped back onto the spatial location and detected locations for tumor cells, lymphocytes, fibroblasts, and macrophages (Supplementary Fig. S12a). To validate the cell-type distribution by DeSpaST, we implemented the ScType algorithm with the Xenium data and plotted the cell-type distribution in Supplementary Fig. S12b. The spatial distributions of annotated cell types from DeSpaST closely resemble the cell types obtained from Xenium, which further substantiate the accuracy of DeSpaST. To reveal the tumor’s immunological response, we specifically focus on tumor cells and immune cells (Fig. 4a and b). Both DeSpaST (Fig. 4a) and Xenium panel (Fig. 4b) delineated similar tumor and immune cell regions. Heterogeneous areas are found at the upper and middle portion of the tissue where tumor and immune cells closely interact, which indicates the recognition and attack of activated immune cells that impede tumor spread. In contrast, tumor regions at the lower portion of the slides appear less confined, forming dense and more aggressive tumor areas where there is a lack of immune cell control. Both Xenium and DeSpaST accurately classify these complex cell environments. While Xenium served as a valuable reference here, DeSpaST exhibits greater potential and offers the flexibility to include more marker genes for cell type classification analysis. Unlike Xenium's panel of 306 genes, DeSpaST can leverage the transcriptomic level gene expression data from ST data. This enables the inclusion of a wider range of cell-type markers, leading to more accurate classification.

Application of DeSpaST in uncovering pseudo temporal trajectories of tumor progression

Following the annotation of cancer cells, we calculated the pseudo-trajectories timeline of tumor development based on the inferred cell level gene expression predicted by DeSpaST. The partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA) method was used to construct the topological graph and a cluster with high expression of cancer stem cell marker ALDH1A3 [33, 34] was selected as the root node (Supplementary Fig. S13). Pseudotime values were estimated and mapped back to each cell's spatial location to infer the dynamic trajectories of tumor progression (Fig. 4c). Interestingly, the clusters of early pseudotime cancer cells and late pseudotime cancer cells occupy markedly distinct regions in the tissue section. These distributions align precisely with the DCIS (ductal carcinoma in situ) and invasive tumor regions annotated by expert pathologists (Fig. 4d). Tumor cells with early pseudotime exhibit a denser and more uniform composition characterized with reduced variability in size and shape, corresponding to DCIS regions (Fig. 4d). In contrast, late pseudotime tumor cells, located in the invasive tumor region, show increased invasivenexss and a more loosely organized structure.

Utilization of DeSpaST across diverse tissue datasets

In addition to the above two breast cancer datasets, DeSpaST can be broadly applied to various tissue types and datasets. We further applied DeSpaST to Visium datasets from human ovarian cancer (Fig. 5) and human lung cancer (Supplementary Fig. S16). DeSpaST extracted the cellular morphology features from the H&E image and deconvoluted the cell-level gene expression for all the Visium genes across each cell. For the human ovarian cancer dataset, we selected the ovarian cancer marker gene BRCA1 [35, 36] (Fig. 5b) and ovarian normal tissue marker FOXL2 [37, 38] (Fig. 5c) as illustrative examples. DeSpaST significantly improved gene expression pattern resolution compared to the Visium spot-level expression (Supplementary Fig. S14b and c). High BRCA1 expression (Fig. 5b) corresponds with dark-purple regions in the H&E-stained image, which were annotated as tumor cells, while high FOXL2 expression corresponds to pink color areas indicative of normal ovarian tissue (Fig. 5c). We zoomed in on five specific regions to showcase the resolution of DeSpaST’s output and highlight the differences in gene expression (Fig. 5d–f, Supplementary Fig. S15). DeSpaST BRCA1 and FOXL2 exhibit distinct gene expression patterns between tumor and normal ovarian tissue regions. In contrast, Visium fails to clearly visualize these gene expression patterns (Supplementary Fig. S14d–f, Supplementary Fig. S15).

Similar cell-level resolution improvement of DeSpaST is also observed in the human lung cancer Visium dataset (Supplementary Fig. S16). We illustrated three marker genes as examples of the resolution improvement compared to the Visium spot level resolution (Supplementary Fig. S17): cancer marker gene EGFR [39], T lymphocyte marker CD4 [40], and fibroblast maker SFRP2 [41]. DeSpaST further clarified and enhanced the resolution of these patterns (Supplementary Fig. S16). As depicted in Supplementary Fig. S16e, DeSpaST accurately demonstrated high EGFR expression in tumor regions and elevated SFRP2 expression in fibroblast areas. Regions like Supplementary Fig. S16f and g illustrate the tumor microenvironment with interactive immune cell and tumor cell regions. DeSpaST successfully distinguished immune cells and tumor cells with high EGFR expression in tumor regions, as well as elevated CD4 expression in corresponding immune cell regions. The strong agreement between DeSpaST’s output and pathological annotations in both ovarian and lung cancer tissues supports its accuracy and reliability in uncovering cell-level gene expression from spot-level ST data for multiple tissues.

Finally, to assess cross-region generalizability, we spatially split the Visium spots into training and held-out subsets. On the held-out portion, DeSpaST maintained substantially higher agreement with Xenium than raw Visium (e.g. CD69 Pearson r: 0.653 versus 0.192; CCND1 Pearson r: 0.614 versus 0.208; Supplementary Fig. S18), confirming that the model generalizes beyond the training region.

Discussion

In this paper, we presented DeSpaST, which is a graphic deconvolution model that integrates the cell morphology features from histology images with spatial transcriptomic data to predict the cellular resolution gene expression for cells across the tissue section. Unlike other methods that only enhance the ST resolution, DeSpaST stands out as the first model to directly infer cell-level gene expression from histology images and ST data. DeSpaST is innovative and beneficial in that it takes advantage of the histology images and explicitly models the spatial organization of cells. By constructing the cellular graphic structure, cell morphology and interaction information from histology images were successfully input to the GCN model as nodes and edges, respectively. GCN, with its capability to handle complex non-Euclidean structures, enables deconvolution at cell-level resolution by well aligning with the diverse and intricate cellular interaction topologies. We demonstrated the high accuracy, resolution, robustness, and efficacy of DeSpaST in this paper. This model can be easily applied to diverse types of tissues and ST data.

The cellular-level gene expression inferred from DeSpaST offers powerful support to many biomedical applications. In this study, we applied DeSpaST’s output to the spatial cell type annotation and inferred the pseudotime trajectories of tumor development. DeSpaST offers the cellular resolution for cell type identification compared to traditional spot-level cell type identification. DeSpaST clearly illustrates the cell types for individual cells, enabling the revelation of cell–cell interaction in the tumor microenvironment and providing valuable insights into tumor immunology studies. Pseudotime analysis further uncovers the pseudo-temporal trajectories of tumor progression, revealing distinct clusters of early- and late-stage cancer cells. The high precision of DeSpaST establishes a reliable foundation for subsequent spatial transcriptomics analyses, such as deriving differentially expressed genes and exploring cell–cell communication inference.

A key limitation of our evaluation is that the Xenium measurements used for validation are obtained from an adjacent FFPE section, rather than the exact same section profiled by Visium. Although adjacent-section validation is often necessary in practice because assays are destructive or mutually exclusive, it is inherently imperfect: consecutive sections can exhibit local tissue distortion during sectioning and mounting, cell displacement, partial-volume differences, and subtle microenvironmental heterogeneity (e.g. immune infiltration near boundaries). These factors can weaken strict quantitative comparability even when broad spatial trends remain visually similar. Therefore, the agreement we observe between DeSpaST predictions and Xenium should be interpreted primarily as qualitative and semi-quantitative evidence that DeSpaST recovers coherent spatial gene-expression patterns and microenvironmental structure, rather than as an absolute quantitative ground truth at single-cell resolution. To mitigate this limitation, we emphasize evaluations that do not require one-to-one cell correspondence across sections—such as region-level pattern concordance, marker-consistent localization, and spot-level correlations computed after aggregating predicted cells within each Visium spot—and we further assess whether predicted cell-level signals support consistent tissue organization using downstream spatial domain analyses.

Beyond the cross-section validation constraints discussed above, several additional limitations should be noted. First, DeSpaST’s accuracy depends on the upstream nucleus segmentation step; segmentation errors such as missed nuclei, over-segmentation, or misclassification propagate to the graph construction and deconvolution. Second, genes with very low or near-zero expression in the Visium data are inherently difficult to deconvolve, as there is minimal transcriptional signal to distribute among cells. Third, standardized simulation frameworks that jointly model realistic histology with spatially structured single-cell expression are not yet available, and matched single-cell spatial datasets with paired Visium and H&E inputs remain scarce, precluding direct ground-truth validation at single-cell resolution. Despite these limitations, DeSpaST demonstrates consistent improvements across multiple complementary evaluation criteria and tissue types.

In addition to uncovering cellular gene expression in ST data, DeSpaST holds the potential for deconvoluting cellular protein expression in spatial proteomics. Recently, numerous spatial proteomic methods have been developed to enable the direct detection of protein molecules with spatial variability. However, the resolution remains limited, typically operating at spot sizes around 20–100 μm [42–45]. The application of DeSpaST to improve the resolution of spatial proteomics encourages further exploration of spatially cellular protein expression.

DeSpaST is designed for broad applicability, leveraging histology images and ST data as input to uncover cellular-level expression. Its capabilities can be further extended by incorporating additional data sources. For example, if single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data are available from the same tissue or a similar sample, DeSpaST can integrate it as a cell type constraint, potentially improving the deconvolution accuracy. This is particularly valuable when dealing with very weak sequencing signals in ST data, as scRNA-seq offers higher sequencing depth. An additional enhancement for DeSpaST could involve its extension to 3D spatial transcriptomes. Recently, spatially resolved transcriptomic atlases in high-dimensional data have emerged to revolutionize the understanding of cell interaction and gene regulation within the organ. Organ-level 3D spatial transcriptomics [46–48] or proteomics [49] were reconstructed from serial 2D tissue sections. DeSpaST, with a potential 3D GCN version, could leverage these data’s complexity to learn 3D spatial cell features and deconvolute gene expression. This is particularly relevant as current 3D spatial transcriptomes often miss data between sections. DeSpaST can potentially address this gap and provide a more complete picture of the 3D spatial transcriptomic landscape.

Method

Datasets

Human breast cancer datasets 1

Two human breast cancer datasets were employed for constructing and validating the DeSpaST model. The first human breast cancer dataset consists of Visium ST data for DCIS and Invasive Carcinoma FFPE human breast cancer tissue. This dataset contains 2518 spots, with 5244 median genes per spot and a sequencing depth of 32 524 reads per spot.

Human breast cancer datasets 2

The second human breast cancer dataset is associated with a recently released Xenium in situ platform [25]. This research conducted a comprehensive analysis with three complementary technologies (Chromium, Visium, Xenium) on a single breast-cancer FFPE tissue. The Visium output ST data were used to deconvolute the cell-level gene expression using DeSpaST, which contains 4992 spots, with 5712 median genes per spot and 40 003 mean reads per spot. The Xenium panels were used as a reference standard to evaluate DeSpaST’s cell-level resolution prediction. Additionally, IF staining was performed after Xenium to assess the expression profiles of HER2 and CD20 proteins. The results from both the Xenium panel and the subsequent IF staining were then used to verify the effectiveness of DeSpaST.

Human ovarian cancer dataset

The human ovarian cancer dataset is obtained from 10× Genomics and contains 4674 spots, with 8792 median genes per spot.

Human lung cancer dataset

The human lung cancer dataset is obtained from 10× Genomics and contains 6195 spots, with 10 087 median genes per spot.

Spatial transcriptomics data preprocessing and image feature extraction

Spatial transcriptomics data were first preprocessed prior to analysis. Gene expression values in the spot barcode profile matrix were normalized to counts per million (CPM). To mitigate the impact of outliers, the normalized CPM values were scaled to the 95th percentile.

The cell morphology features were extracted from H&E images using HD-Staining (Histology-based Digital Staining of Pathology Images) [18], which is an automatic deep-learning model that simultaneously segments and classifies cell nuclei, as well as provides morphology information about each identified cell nucleus. The extracted morphological features encompassed the centroid position, cell area, convex area, eccentricity, extent, filled area, major axis length, minor axis length, orientation, perimeter, solidity, and probability. In addition, we further enriched DeSpaST by incorporating 1024-dimensional intermediate box features and 256-dimensional mask features derived from the HD-Staining model. These cell morphological features were incorporated into the construction of DeSpaST.

Cellular graphic structure construction

We initially divided whole-slide H&E images into 250 × 250-pixel patches centered on the Visium spots. In total, we generated 2518 patches for the 10× Visium human breast cancer dataset, 4992 patches for the Janesick et al. [25] human breast cancer dataset, 4674 patches for the human ovarian cancer dataset, and 6195 patches for the human lung cancer dataset. For each patch, we first obtain each cell location by performing cell segmentation, then construct a directed graph structure by connecting the cells based on their spatial proximity. This graph structure was designed to incorporate at least eight nearest neighbors. Pairwise Euclidean distances between cell coordinates were computed to identify the nearest neighbors. For those patches with very low cell density (<8 cells), DeSpaST dynamically resized the patch to include at least eight neighboring cells. These directed graph structures captured the spatial relationship and connectivity among cells in the tissue.

Each graph structure consisted of two main components: nodes and edges. Nodes represent morphological features extracted through HD-Staining for individual cells. These node features have undergone global scaling as part of the preprocessing. Edges store information on edge categorization and edge spatial representation (Supplementary Fig. S2). Edge categorization is determined based on the types of cells connected by each edge, which provides insight into the type of interactions between the connected cells. Here, we included a total of 49 combinations of edge categorizations across seven cell types (Supplementary Fig. S2a). Edge spatial representation refers to the spatial aspects of cell interactions, including the Cell Connectivity Angle and Spatial Proximity. Cell Connectivity Angle indicates the connectivity between neighboring cells and the directionality of these connections. Spatial Proximity reflects the physical distance between cells. Both nodes and edges collectively constitute the cellular graphic structure, which synergistically contributes to the definition of complex cellular relationships.

Image registration and result comparison

Immunofluorescence image and Xenium panel registration to hematoxylin and eosin images

In order to register the IF image and Xenium panel to the Visium corresponding H&E images and facilitate the comparison to the protein/RNA expression experiment, we employed skimage.transform package for image transformation. To achieve a precise and efficient registration, we systematically tested various combinations of angles and scales between the IF and H&E images. To optimize computing efficiency, we initially conducted tests with 10% scaled-down images to obtain approximate scale and angle adjustments. Subsequently, we fine-tuned the registration using the original scale.

DeSpaST model validation

We validate DeSpaST through the assessment of RNA expression and protein expression by utilizing data from the Xenium panel and IF images, respectively. Using the spatial information from Visium spot locations, we reconstructed the spot-level expression for DeSpaST, TESLA, Xenium panel, and IF protein expression. The accuracies of Visium ST data, TESLA, and the DeSpaST model were evaluated using the Pearson correlation coefficient with the Xenium panel and IF image.

Cell type analysis

To assign cell types to each predicted single cell, we adopted a fully automated cell-type identification algorithm, ScType [32], which utilizes the Xenium-tested marker gene as the selected cell markers. The cellular level gene expression predicted by DeSpaST was used as input. The Seurat package was used to normalize the data with the default LogNormalize method. We then use the Seurat FindNeighbors function with parameter dims = 1:50 and FindClusters function with resolution = 0.9, which results in 23 clusters. ScType was also applied with Xenium Breast Cancer Cell Marker panel. Each cluster was assigned to the cell type with the highest score, and clusters assigned the same cell type were merged into larger clusters.

Pseudotime analysis

The gene expression of tumor cells predicted by DeSpaST was extracted for pseudotime analysis. We first reduced the gene expression dimension using principal component analysis (PCA). Then, cell clustering was performed using the Leiden algorithm, which is a fast approach to ensure that all cells are well connected for downstream optimal trajectory inference [55]. Partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA) [56] is used to construct a topological graph based on the cell partitions. The cluster with high expression of ALDH1A3 [33, 34], a gene marker of cancer stem cells, was selected as the trajectory root node. The Diffusion Pseudotime (DPT) method [57] was then applied to estimate pseudotime values, which represented the tumor cell dynamics. All analyses were performed using the SCANPY [58] Python packages.
