Efficacy of Novel Techniques Using a 4-F, 130-cm JR4.0 Catheter for Challenging Guiding Catheter Engagement
Authors: Nobuo Ishiguro, Akimitsu Tanaka, Kohei Kitamura, Miyabi Ishikawa, Teppei Funasaka, Mami Fukujyu, Takeshi Mori, Makito Kaneshiro, Miyuki Ando, Kazuo Kato
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Percutaneous coronary intervention is a mature procedure with a high success rate. However, there are rare situations in which a guiding catheter cannot engage the coronary artery ostium, such as with an anomalous aortic origin of a coronary artery or interference from a transcatheter aortic valve implantation. This report presents 4 cases in which novel techniques using a 4-F, 130-cm JR4.0 catheter were successfully applied. This technique is especially valuable in patients with anomalous coronary artery origins or after transcatheter aortic valve implantation. Its use may improve procedural success, reduce contrast volume, and enhance wire control without the need for custom-shaped guiding catheters.

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is a mature procedure, with a high success rate. Advances in PCI equipment, such as guiding catheters (GCs), guidewires, and balloon catheters, have considerably improved procedural success and safety. However, GC engagement may occasionally fail in challenging anatomical settings, such as severely tortuous access vessels, anomalous aortic origin of a coronary artery (AAOCA), or interference from a transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI). These scenarios often require multiple catheters for coronary angiography (CAG), leading to large iodinated contrast volumes (CVs), increased radiation exposure, and delayed treatment in acute coronary syndrome.1 Here, we introduce novel techniques using a 4-F, 130-cm JR4.0 catheter in 4 complex cases, highlighting the versatility and potential utility of this catheter in difficult GC engagement scenarios.Take-Home Messages•The 4-F, 130-cm JR4.0 catheter serves as an effective inner catheter to reshape guiding catheter tips in challenging coronary engagement, particularly in patients with anomalous coronary artery origins or after TAVI.•This technique may improve procedural success, reduce CV, and enhance wire control, without requiring custom-shaped guiding catheters.

Case 1

A 68-year-old woman with a history of PCI involving an anomalous right coronary artery (RCA) arising from the left sinus of Valsalva (LSOV) and proximal left anterior descending artery (LAD) 7 months prior was referred for inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. During the prior PCI, a guidewire was placed in the LAD, and another guidewire was advanced into the RCA using a floating GC owing to AAOCA. An arterial access was attained through right femoral artery puncture. CAG with a 6-F IL3.5 GC (Asahi Intec) failed to visualize the RCA (Figure 1A). After crossing a guidewire into the LAD and floating the GC, a manipulated, large, bent guidewire could not advance into the RCA. Therefore, a 4-F, 130-cm JR4.0 catheter was inserted into the GC, and another guidewire was manipulated with the JR4.0 tip directed toward the RCA, enabling successful guidewire advancement (Figures 1B and 1C). The wire was advanced distally into the RCA, and the 4-F JR4.0 catheter was exchanged for a 6-F guide extension catheter (GEC) using an extension wire. CAG via GEC revealed severe mid-RCA stenosis (Figure 1D). Following drug-eluting stent implantation and post-dilatation, final angiography demonstrated excellent outcomes (Figure 1E). The total CV was 67 mL, and the procedure time from puncture to guidewire passage into the RCA was 30 minutes. Creatine kinase (CK) and CK-MB peaked at 575 U/L and 67 ng/mL, respectively. Case 2

A 52-year-old man with a history of chronic kidney disease and hyperuricemia was referred for inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. An arterial access was attained through right radial artery (RA) puncture. CAG with 4-F JR 4.0, AL 1.0, and AL 1.5 catheters failed to reveal the RCA, and aortography using a 4-F pigtail catheter placed in the left coronary cusp did not identify the RCA (Figure 2A). CAG was discontinued, and cardiac computed tomography (CCT) was performed owing to concerns about CV. CCT showed that the RCA originated from the LSOV, indicating AAOCA (Figures 2B and 2C). Accordingly, PCI was reattempted. A second arterial access was attained through left RA puncture. CAG using 6-F JL4.0, AL1.5, and AL2.0 GCs (Terumo) again failed to reveal the RCA. After crossing a guidewire to the LAD and floating the 6-F JL4.0 GC, a manipulated large, bent guidewire with slight clockwise GC rotation could not advance into the RCA. A 4-F, 130-cm JR4.0 catheter was advanced through the LAD guidewire using an extension wire (Figure 2D), and a bent guidewire inserted via the JR4.0 was successfully directed into the RCA (Figure 2E). After removing the LAD wire, another guidewire was advanced to the distal RCA with a microcatheter (Zizai; Terumo) support, and the GC was positioned near the RCA ostium (Figure 2F). The microcatheter and 4-F JR4.0 were exchanged for a 6-F GEC. CAG via the GEC demonstrated proximal RCA occlusion (Figure 2G). Following pre-dilatation and drug-eluting stent implantation, final angiography showed excellent results (Figure 2H). CK and CK-MB peaked at 2,081 U/L and 165 ng/mL, respectively. The total CV was 257 mL, with no contrast-induced nephropathy. The procedure time from puncture to guidewire passage into the RCA was 74 minutes. Case 3

A 71-year-old man with hypertension was referred for unstable angina pectoris. CCT showed that the LAD and left circumflex artery (LCX) originated separately from the LSOV, with severe stenosis in the proximal LAD (Figures 3A and 3B). An arterial access was attained through right RA puncture. CAG confirmed separate LAD and LCX origins from the left aortic sinus and severe proximal LAD stenosis (Figures 3C to 3E). A 6-F JL3.5 GC (Asahi Intec) was selectively engaged in the LCX, but could not be positioned in the LAD. After crossing a guidewire into the LCX and floating the GC, a manipulated large, bent guidewire failed to advance into the LAD, though it easily entered the high lateral branch (Figure 3F). Therefore, a 4-F, 130-cm JR4.0 catheter was inserted into the GC, and another guidewire was manipulated with the JR4.0 tip directed toward the LAD, allowing successful advancement (Figure 3G). After removing the LCX wire and directing it into the first diagonal branch, the bifurcation was treated using proximal optimization and kissing balloon techniques. Final angiography revealed favorable results without first diagonal branch occlusion (Figure 3H). The procedure time from puncture to guidewire passage into the LAD was 51 minutes. Case 4

An 84-year-old man with a history of PCI for an ostial RCA lesion (Figure 4A) and TAVI for severe aortic stenosis was referred for CAG owing to exertional dyspnea 6 months prior and ischemia in the RCA territory on myocardial perfusion imaging. An arterial access was attained through right RA puncture. CAG using a 4-F JR4.0 diagnostic catheter demonstrated in-stent RCA occlusion with good collateral flow from the LCX (Figures 4B and 4C). PCI for the in-stent occlusion was planned. A second arterial access was obtained via left RA puncture. Owing to limited space, manipulating a 6-F JR3.5 GC (Asahi Intec) inside the EVOLUT R valve proves challenging. Although the 6-F JR3.5 GC was passed through the EVOLUT R strut nearest the RCA ostium, the guidewire could not be advanced into the RCA (Figure 4D, Video 1). Possible causes included poor coaxial GC positioning or obstruction by a commissural post. To maintain coaxiality and avoid interference from the 3 commissural tabs, the GC tip shape needed to have a three-dimensional shape. A 4-F, 130-cm JR4.0 catheter was inserted into the GC and manipulated until it engaged the RCA (Figure 4E, Video 2). The GC was subsequently advanced through the EVOLUT R strut along the 4-F JR4.0 catheter. A Finecross GT microcatheter (Terumo) and XT-R guidewire (Asahi Intec) were advanced via the 4-F JR4.0 and successfully crossed the occluded in-stent lesion (Figures 4F and 4G). After pre-dilatation and drug-coated balloon angioplasty, the lesion was revascularized. Final angiography revealed good results without residual stenosis (Figure 4H). The procedure time from puncture to guidewire passage into the RCA was 29 minutes. Discussion

This report presents 4 cases using novel techniques with a 4-F, 130-cm JR4.0 catheter, which enables easy and rapid GC engagement in various challenging GC engagement situations. Each scenario was reproduced using a coronary model (Figure 5). The incidence of an anomalous RCA originating from the LSOV is rare, reported in 0.17% to 0.31% of cases.2 The atypical ostial location in AAOCA poses considerable technical challenges, including large iodinated contrast use, elevated radiation exposure, and delays in acute coronary syndrome interventions. In case 1, PCI for an AAORCA lesion was performed 7 months prior, and previous coronary information was available. However, in case 2, CAG could not identify the RCA, and CCT revealed AAORCA. Compared with case 1, the total iodinated CV was significantly greater in case 2. CCT should be considered when AAOCA is suspected and targeted angiography is unsuccessful, even in emergencies. Although custom-shaped or steam-deformed catheters can aid engagement, they may not be readily available or consistently reliable.1 Inserting a 4-F, 130-cm JR4.0 catheter into a GC provides a three-dimensional tip shape suitable for most AAOCA cases. This also applies to cases lacking a left main coronary artery, such as case 3 (Figures 5A and 5B). Advancing the 4-F JR4.0 catheter through the LAD guidewire, as in case 2, helps reorient the GC tip and guide another guidewire into the anomalous RCA (Figures 5C and 5D).

In cases undergoing PCI after TAVI, GC engagement remains one of the most serious challenges, particularly in patients who received TAVI with the Evolut series (Medtronic).3,4 The frame of the Evolut series consists of a diamond-shaped mesh. The valve's inflow is wrapped with a sealing skirt and includes 3 bioprosthetic commissural posts. The spatial relationship between the coronary ostium and the orientation of the diamond struts, sealing skirt, and commissural posts can complicate coronary access.4,5 Depending on aortic size and the coronary takeoff angle, selective GC engagement may be impossible if the coronary ostium faces a commissural post (Figures 5E and 5F). If selective engagement is needed, a guidewire must be manipulated into the coronary artery, followed by advancement of a GEC into the ostium. However, if the RCA is proximally occluded, as in case 3, GEC advancement with guidewire support alone may not be feasible. In such situations, inserting a 4-F, 130-cm JR4.0 catheter into the GC helps provide a three-dimensional tip shape that can successfully engage the RCA ostium (Figures 5G and 5H).

These techniques can alternatively be performed using a 4-F, 100-cm JR4.0 catheter inserted into a 90-cm GC. The 4-F, 130-cm JR4.0 catheter demonstrates high versatility, with the novel technique proving effective in cases where standard GC engagement is challenging.
