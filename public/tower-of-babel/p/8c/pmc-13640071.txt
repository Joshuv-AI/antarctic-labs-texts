Non-laboratory-based cardiovascular risk assessment: 10-year absolute risk, predicted heart age and intervention requirements in a Nigerian population
Authors: Oluseyi Adegoke, Oluwadamilola Omolara Ojo, Ayesha Akinkugbe, Njideka Ulunma Okubadejo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPopulation-based data on cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk burden are essential for effective planning of CVD reduction strategies. There is paucity of such data in Nigerians. We aimed to determine the 10-year absolute CVD risk and heart age profile of adult Nigerians; to identify the most frequent absolute CVD risk-determining factors and the required treatment interventions.MethodsThis was a secondary analysis of data of 3620 adults aged 30–80 years (51% females) from a population-based cross-sectional hypertension survey in Lagos, Nigeria. A non-laboratory-based CVD risk-assessment framework was used to estimate the 10-year absolute CVD risk and predict the heart age profiles. The WHO PEN was used to identify the treatment intervention requirements.ResultsA total of 316 (8.7%) persons, 218 (12.3%) of males and 98 (5.3%) of females had a 10-year absolute CVD risk ≥ 20%, p < 0.0001. Elevated systolic BP (918 (25.4%)) and excess BMI ((overweight: 1072 (29.6%), obesity: 638 (17.6%)) were the most frequent absolute CVD risk determining factors in the population. Excess BMI occurred more frequently in females (p < 0.0001) while the frequency of elevated systolic BP was similar in both sexes (p = 0.091). High excess heart age (EHA) (≥ 5 years) was present in 1531(42.3%) persons; 798 (45.0%) of males and 733 (39.7%) of females, p < 0.0001. Only 521(14.4%) of the total population needed any treatment intervention, compared to 269 (85.1%) of persons with absolute CVD risk ≥ 20%, and 469 (30.6%) with high EHA. Antihypertensive treatment alone (n = 149 (47.2%)) or in combination with statin and aspirin (n = 85 (26.9%)) were the most frequently required interventions in individuals with CVD risk ≥ 20%.ConclusionA significant proportion of our population has high CVD risk. Strategic policies promoting healthy lifestyles, strengthening the health system for focused risk-based screening, and prioritization of treatment interventions could help lower this risk.Keywords: Predicted Heart age, Excess Heart Age, Absolute CVD risk, Hypertension, BMI, Obesity, WHO PEN treatment intervention, Nigeria

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) are the leading cause of morbidity, disability and premature mortality globally, with its prevalence increasing in low and middle income countries (LMICs) where 80% of the CVD related premature mortality also occur [1–3].

The burden of CVD is largely driven by the globally increasing prevalence of risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, tobacco smoking, obesity, dyslipidaemia, sedentary lifestyle, etc [4, 5]. About 90% of the absolute risks for all CVDs as well as for its fatal and non fatal events are attributable to these common CVD risk factors [4, 5].

The most rapid increases in the prevalence of these CVD risk factors have also occurred in LMICs in recent times, albeit with some regional variability in the distribution of these risk factors across populations [4, 5]. Fortunately, almost all these risk factors are potentially modifiable and population-specific strategic interventions directed at these factors may help modulate the burden of CVD. However, prior information on the estimated population based CVD risk burden are required to direct such intervention strategies. In Nigeria, reports on population-based CVD risk profiling are limited. Different risk factor assessment frameworks have been used to estimate risk for individual CVDs. In recent years, risk assessment frameworks that are based on identified shared common risk factors have been developed which estimate the absolute risk of an individual for developing any CVD and fatal or non-fatal CVD event. Such absolute CVD risk assessment frameworks include both the laboratory based and non-laboratory based CVD risk assessment frameworks [6–8]. The CVD risk so estimated may also be variously expressed in terms of absolute percentages or predicted heart age (PHA). The non-laboratory framework has been found to be useful and is validated for use in LMICs where including laboratory assessment in population-based studies and preventive strategies would be expensive [7, 8].

In Nigeria, the combined assessment of the absolute 10 year CVD risk, PHA, and the WHO PEN intervention requirements has not been described.

This study aimed to determine the 10 year absolute CVD risk and PHA profiles of adult Nigerians including sex-based differences in risk; to identify the most frequent absolute CVD risk determining factors; and identify the required treatment interventions. This information would help to quantify CVD risk burden, identify individuals who are at increased risk, inform treatment intervention strategies and guide health policies.

Methods

This was a secondary analysis of data from the cross-sectional population-based hypertension prevalence survey conducted in densely populated mixed income urban areas of Lagos State, Nigeria; the detailed methodology of which has been previously described [9, 10]. The original survey recruited 5365 participants aged 16 to 92 years but our study utilised data from only participants who were aged 30 to 80 years in conformity with the age range included in the CVD risk estimator chart used in this study [6]. Data of 1745 persons were excluded as follows: 1578 persons aged less than 30 years, 21 persons older than 80 years, and 146 persons that had no weight and height records required for BMI computation. Hence, only 3620 persons with complete data were included in this analysis.

Data management

In the absence of country-specific risk scores we utilised the non-laboratory-based Framingham CVD risk assessment framework to estimate the sex-based 10 year absolute CVD risk profile and the sex-based predicted heart age (PHA) of our population [6]. This framework though derived primarily from a US based, predominantly white population; has been previously utilised and adapted to estimate CVD risk and predict heart age in other West African populations [11–14]. Components of the framework include age, BMI, SBP, history of antihypertensive treatment, known diabetes status, and current tobacco use [6, 7]. The calculated 10 year absolute CVD risk profile estimates were categorised as < 5%, 5 to < 10%, 10 to < 20%, and ≥ 20%; and stratified by sex [6]. We defined high risk individuals as those with absolute CVD risk ≥ 20% [6]. We derived excess heart age (EHA) expressed in years, as the difference between the PHA and the individual’s chronological age; and this was categorised as < 0, 0 to < 5, 5–9, and ≥ 10 years; and was stratified by sex. High EHA was defined as EHA ≥ 5 years based on previously used categorisation and definition [13, 14]. We utilised the World Health Organization package of essential non-communicable disease interventions (WHO PEN) to identify the need for treatment interventions in the population and this need was stratified by sex, absolute CVD risk ≥ or < 20%, and EHA ≥ or < 5 years [15].

Elevated SBP was defined as levels ≥ 140mmHg [16]. BMI (Kg/m2) was further categorised into underweight (< 18.5), normal (18.5–24.9), overweight (25.0–29.9), and obese (≥ 30.0) [17]. Data on diabetes status and tobacco use were based on self-report. We assessed the frequency in the population, of other modifiable adverse lifestyle practices (current alcohol consumption, low physical activity, low fruits and vegetable consumption) that were not included in the CVD risk estimation framework used. Physical activity was defined as low if there was less than the recommended moderate activity of > 2.5 h/week or vigorous activity lasting >1 h 15 min/week [18]. Low fruit/vegetable consumption was defined as consumption of less than the recommended daily consumption of 2 cups of fruit and 2.5 cups of vegetables [19].

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 20 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). We tested for normality of distribution of the data using Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Age (in years), SBP and BMI are summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and comparison between groups was done using the Student’s t-test. CVD risk profile, EHA profile, treatment intervention requirement, current tobacco use, elevated SBP, treatment for hypertension, history of diabetes, age category, BMI category, level of education and other modifiable adverse lifestyle practices are presented as frequencies (percentages) and intergroup comparison was done using chi square analysis. Simple linear regression was performed to examine the association between EHA (years) and 10-year CVD risk score. The independent variable was the EHA and the dependent variable was the 10-year CVD risk score. The unstandardised regression coefficient (B) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) is reported. The model fit was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R2). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

This study included 3620 participants aged 30–80 years, and comprised 1774 males (49.0%) and 1846 females (51.0%).

The mean age of the male (43.3 ± 10.6) and female (43.7 ± 11.2) participants were similar. Majority of the participants (3323 (91.8%)) were aged 30–60 years; while 2784 (76.9%) attained at least secondary school education. A significantly higher proportion of individuals with CVD risk ≥ 20% (n = 269 (85.1%)) compared to those with CVD risk < 20% (n = 252 (7.6%)) required treatment, p < 0.0001. Need for antihypertensive treatment alone was the most frequent requirement in individuals with CVD risk ≥ or < 20%, n = 149 (47.2%) and n = 233 (7.1%) respectively. While 85 (26.9%) and 14 (4.4%) individuals with CVD risk ≥ 20% required antihypertensive - statin-aspirin and antihypertensive-statin-aspirin-ACEI combinations respectively; no individual with CVD risk < 20% (n = 0 (0.0%)) required these treatment combinations, p < 0.0001.

Four hundred and sixty nine (30.6%) individuals with high EHA ≥ 5 years required treatment, compared to 52 (2.5%) of individuals with EHA < 5 years, p < 0.0001. Antihypertensive treatment alone or in combination with statin and aspirin were also the most frequently required n = 357 (23.3%) and n = 70 (4.6%) respectively in individuals with high EHA ≥ 5 years. The proportion of individuals requiring individual treatment or in combination were significantly lower in individuals with EHA < 5 years compared to those with EHA ≥ 5 years, p < 0.0001.

Discussion

This study found that 8.7% of our population had a high (≥ 20%) absolute risk of developing a CVD or event in 10 years. This is lower than the 18% and 13.9% reported for urban South African and Ghanaian populations respectively, but slightly higher than the 6.5% and 6% reported in Kenya and Sierra Leone [7, 11, 12, 20]. These differences may be a reflection of the different levels of transitional lifestyles (such as sedentary behaviour, tobacco use, consumption of highly processed foods, etc.) that promote CVD risk in such countries [11]. A study reported higher prevalence of high absolute CVD risk in Ghanaians residing in Europe than in those residing in Ghana [12]. Similarly, the South African study comprised mixed race population at varied levels of urbanisation [7].

In Nigeria, the reported prevalence of high absolute CVD risk varied widely from 0.7% to 22.9% [21–24]. Those studies were however conducted in small sample sizes of restricted population groups such as among healthcare workers, civil servants and small specific community groups. Nonetheless, those findings and ours represent marked increase in the risk for CVDs beyond the rarities that were reported in our population before the 1990s [25]. The frequency of high absolute CVD risk found in this study is lower than that reported in some high income countries (HIC) [26]. However, while these values reportedly represent a downward trend in the CVD risk burden in those HICs, it echoes the increasing burden of CVDs reported in LMICs [27, 28].

Our finding that males were at significantly higher risk for CVD than females is comparable to that reported in other populations [22, 26]. It is also consistent with the identification of the male sex as an important risk predictor in many individual CVD prediction frameworks [29, 30]. This may also be related to the greater likelihood of males to engage in risky lifestyles and have poorer health seeking behaviour [31, 32]. In this study, the current smokers of tobacco and alcohol drinkers were almost predominantly males. Additionally, a significantly lower proportion of males were on antihypertensive treatment despite the high prevalence of elevated SBP. However, this seemingly lower prevalence of high CVD risk in our females is still concerning because there is a greater risk of death and worse prognosis for females for each level of CVD [33].

We found that elevated SBP, excess BMI (being overweight or obese) were the most frequent modifiable absolute CVD risk-determining factors while known diabetes status and current tobacco smoking occurred less commonly; a pattern similar to that reported in studies from Nigeria and other LMICs [24, 28, 34]. This finding is consistent with the high prevalence of hypertension and obesity in our general population, as well as the synergistic relationship between hypertension and obesity [35, 36]. It underscores the role of hypertension as the bedrock of CVD morbidity and mortality in Nigerians, and it is consistent with its being the leading risk factor for major cardiovascular diseases and events in sub-Sahara Africa (SSA) [37, 38]. While evidence suggests a decline in average SBP with resultant decline in absolute CVD risk in HICs in the last two decades, prevalence of uncontrolled hypertension is rising in LMICs notably due to rising prevalence of obesity, sedentary lifestyle, high dietary sodium and limited healthcare resources [27, 39]. Equally worrisome is the observed low level (6.2%) of hypertension status awareness despite elevated SBP being present in about a quarter of the population. This low level of hypertension awareness and treatment status in LMICs has persisted over decades with no evidence of improvement yet [28, 34]. The frequency of overweight and obesity in this study is consistent with the current average of 28% and 15% respectively reported for urban Nigerian population; and it is in the range of that reported for some HICs [40–42]. Sedentary lifestyle and dietary indiscretion have been associated with increasing prevalence of overweight and obesity. Consumption of at least two servings of vegetables per day is associated with less likelihood of weight gain [43, 44]. In our study, four-fifth of the population had no/low physical activity and about a third had low consumption of fruits and vegetables. Similar to previous reports, we observed sex-related differential distribution of CVD risk factors with females having more obesity and physical inactivity while males had more lifestyle related risks such as tobacco use [24, 28, 34]. This finding suggests the need for sex-specific intervention strategies for lowering the burden of CVDs.

The frequency (1.1%) of diabetes in this study is lower than the average frequency of 3% to 7% reported in urban Nigeria and other African studies [45–47]. This value may however represent an underestimation of the true frequency of diabetes in the population since it was based on self-reporting. This may be particularly so considering that greater than 50% of Nigerians with diabetes are reportedly unaware of their status [48]. The proportion of current tobacco smokers in our study is within the prevalence range of 5 to 10% reported in urban Nigeria but lower than the average of 25% reported across European countries [49, 50]. However this seemingly relatively low frequency of tobacco smokers in our population is still concerning as tobacco smoking is associated with a doubling of the 10 year risk for CVDs and fatal events [51]. While early quitting of smoking before the age of 40 is related with risk reduction or reversal, in Nigeria, smoking often begins in youth and continues into adulthood, by which time the harm has been done due to a lack of awareness of the negative effects [49, 51–53]. Although the proportion of female tobacco smokers in our population is small compared to what obtains in other climes, this lifestyle remains undesirable in this sex as the tobacco associated impact on CVD risk is about 25% higher in females given the same level of exposure [51].

The frequency of individuals with high EHA in our study is higher than the average reported across some African countries but similar to the average reported across some HICs [13]. However, the reported frequencies of high EHA in individual African countries varied by region, with our finding being similar to those reported in more urbanized countries like Botswana and Algeria but higher than those reported in Malawi and Ethiopia with fewer urbanization and dietary transition [13]. To the best of our knowledge there are no previous data on predicted heart age of Nigerians, or on the relationship between 10-year CVD risk and PHA. The higher proportion of males with high EHA in our study is consistent with the reported higher odds of having EHA in males [13]. While PHA does not replace calculated absolute CVD risk score, high EHA (≥ 5years) is an indicator of increased CVD risk and it is useful in risk surveillance [54]. It particularly identifies younger individuals who may have been profiled as low or moderate risk on CVD risk estimators (partly due to the prominent role of the age factor used in the calculation) but who actually have an increased CVD risk that require lifestyle modification [55, 56]. Therefore, our finding that 42.3% of the population has high EHA in spite of 8.7% being categorized as having high absolute CVD risk on risk estimator, suggest a public health concern. A possible implication of this being that while only approximately 9% of the population may be immediately flagged as high risk, a much larger proportion (approximately two-fifth) are likely experiencing on-going cardiovascular damage and disease at younger ages than expected and may require intervention. Our finding also suggests that for every year increase in EHA, there is 0.588% point increases in CVD risk score. Additionally, the observed similarly high prevalence of high EHA in individuals with CVD risks between 10 to < 20% and those with ≥ 20%, may suggest that both individuals with intermediate or high CVD risks have significant accelerated cardiovascular aging. These findings underscore the need for early screening, risk factor management and targeted interventions focused on lifestyle modifications [57]. Furthermore, PHA could be a more effective way of communicating CVD risk and to motivate lifestyle modification, as would be expected of a 40-year old for example, who learns that his heart is equivalent to that of a 60 year old [55, 56]. It is also hoped that the realization that approximately two-fifth of its workforce may be in danger of early mortality from rapidly aging heart health could motivate political will in the government to prioritise cardiovascular health.

The majority of our population however require only lifestyle modification in order to achieve CVD risk reduction, as shown by the finding that only about 14% of the total population required treatment intervention. In contrast majority (about 85%) of individuals with high absolute CVD risk ≥ 20% required at least one treatment intervention. This pattern is similar to that reported in other population-based CVD risk screening studies [20, 58]. This finding is quite informative as it may help guide CVD risk reduction strategies that are specifically tailored to our population. Therefore, effective strategies for reducing the risk of CVD in this population may be those that emphasise encouraging healthy lifestyle choices and modifications in the general population along with focused case finding surveillances to identify high-risk individuals who may require immediate treatment intervention.

The case finding surveillances should however be preferably, absolute CVD risk-estimation-based for a more holistic approach. These targeted approaches have been found to produce greater health gains and to be more cost effective [58, 59]. CVD risk reduction strategies that focused on the identification and treatment of individual risk factors have been associated with high rates of individuals with persistently undetected other risk factors resulting in less satisfactory overall CVD risk reduction [60]. The equal proportion of males and females that required treatment intervention in this study despite the lower prevalence of high CVD risk in females may also be a reflection of the greater impact of CVD risks on females [33] Among those that required treatment interventions, antihypertensive treatment alone or in combination with statins and aspirin were the most commonly required; a finding similar to the pattern reported in many other studies [20, 59, 61]. Our observation that while 13.7% of the population required antihypertensive treatment only 6.2% was on treatment is alarming. While the explanations for this observation may be multi-factorial, notable ones include wide knowledge gaps in the populace about hypertension and CVDs, as well as high cost and difficulty in obtaining required treatment [62]. This finding suggests that antihypertensive treatment and statins/aspirins may require prioritisation in our population by making it more affordable and available, as have been variously advocated by previous researchers [62].

Individual-level implications

Every patient-healthcare giver contact should be utilised for unrelenting repeated health education which emphasises the benefits of healthy lifestyles or modifications irrespective of the CVD risk status of the individuals. While continuous surveillance for early detection and management of hypertension should be intensified; a more holistic intervention may be achieved by utilising absolute CVD risk estimation methods to identify, communicate, and treat individuals at high CVD risk.

Population-level implications

The government should prioritise cardiovascular health through strategic health policies which may avert CVD-related health crisis. All levels of the health-system, but particularly the primary health care, may benefit from being equipped for risk surveillance, absolute CVD risk estimation and treatment interventions with provision of antihypertensive, statins and aspirin.

Study limitations

First, we acknowledge that this study was conducted in one Nigerian city hence the findings of this study may not be generalisable to the whole country. The advantage of the selected population however is that the city of Lagos is the most cosmopolitan Nigerian city with a good representation of all ethnic groups, mixed-income, urban and peri-urban settings. The findings of this study should be interpreted in this context for future comparisons.

Second, being a cross sectional study, a cause and effect relationship is not inferred. Third, a primary limitation was the absence of a country-specific risk score, hence our utilisation of a framework that was derived from a US based, predominantly white population, which may not perfectly generalise to West African Nigerians. However, this framework has been previously utilised and adapted to estimate CVD risk and predict heart age in other West African populations [11–14]. Consequently, our findings should be interpreted in this context. Fourth, we acknowledge that basing diabetes status on self report rather than on laboratory confirmation may be associated with the risk of under-reporting which may potentially lead to underestimation of the overall CVD risk profile. Additionally, some of the other risk factors (e.g. tobacco and alcohol use) were self reported and may have been subject to social and recall bias with possible underestimation of the risks. Fifth, the inclusion of only persons with complete data in this study may pose potential the risk of selection bias. However, the 146 persons that were excluded due to missing data (weight and height) were few relative to our large sample size.

The strength of our study includes the population based sampling and the robustness of our data. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to describe the absolute CVD risk profile and PHA in Nigerians on this scale. We believe that our findings will provide baseline information for future comparisons.

In conclusion, approximately 9% of our population has high absolute CVD risk and about 42% has high EHA. Hypertension and excess BMI are the most frequent modifiable absolute CVD risk determining factors in this population and these should be prioritised. Almost all those at high CVD risk required treatment intervention while majority of the larger population would benefit from lifestyle modifications only. Antihypertensive, statins and aspirin were the most commonly observed required treatment interventions.
