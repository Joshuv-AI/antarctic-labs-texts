Burden of overdoses among persons with opioid use disorder in the United States, 2025–2028: a simulation modeling study
Authors: Nisha Nataraj, Yijie Chen, Andrea Stewart, S Michaela Rikard, Kun Zhang, Ketra Rice, Xinyi Jiang, Christine L Mattson, Jean Ko, Gery P Guy, Jan L Losby
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundDespite recent declines in fatal overdoses in the United States, the overdose crisis remains a persistent public health challenge. Research in context

Evidence before this study

Opioid overdose rates in the United States have been decreasing since August 2023 following years of escalating rates. Still, the overdose crisis could plausibly worsen in future years. Simulation models can be a useful tool to estimate the impact on overdose-related outcomes and associated economic costs. Modeling can also inform how prevention efforts could scale to counteract the effects of a worsening crisis. We searched PubMed on December 01, 2025, for publications between December 1, 2020, and December 1, 2025, using the search terms “opioid use disorder” AND “overdose” AND “prevention” AND “model” in the title or abstract with no restrictions on language or geographical location. Of the 47 publications identified, none aimed to assess national opioid overdose-related outcomes and economic burden in the United States among individuals with opioid use disorder (OUD) if overdose rates worsened or estimate the level of prevention effort needed to offset increases in overdoses under such a scenario.

Added value of this study

This is the first study thus far to assess overdose-related outcomes among US individuals with OUD between 2025 and 2028 under hypothetical conditions of short-term increases in opioid-overdose rates. It also identifies the level of public health prevention strategies that can offset the estimated increases in fatal opioid overdoses, specifically describing the annual levels of linkage to, initiation of, and retention in medication for OUD treatment and bystander naloxone administration during fatal drug use. Additionally, it quantifies the outcomes and benefits of planning for increased prevention in the scenario that overdose rates continue to decrease in future years.

Implications of all the available evidence

The findings from this study highlight the substantial economic burden associated with overdose and underscore the efficiency of timely administration of naloxone and initiating individuals into MOUD treatment in counteracting potential short-term increases in fatal overdoses. Further, our results demonstrate the considerable benefits of scaling prevention efforts regardless of the future trajectory of the overdose crisis. These findings have important implications for the value of continuing to invest in evidence-based public health strategies to prevent overdose.

Introduction

Fatal opioid-involved overdoses decreased nationally from a peak of 81,806 deaths in 2022 to an estimated 55,616 deaths in 2024, based on provisional data.1 Several factors have likely contributed to these declines, such as investments in public health prevention including access to opioid use disorder (OUD) medication, naloxone availability, and improved opioid prescribing2; changes in the illegal drug supply3; and potential waning of the COVID-19 pandemic impact which exacerbated overdose rates.4

Scenarios where the overdose crisis could worsen again in future years might arise—provisional data from April 2025 suggest slowing of the rate of decline in fatal overdoses.1 While difficult to ascertain exactly how the crisis might worsen given rapidly changing dynamics, simulation models can be a useful tool to estimate the impact of changing conditions on overdose-related outcomes and associated economic costs. Modeling can also inform how much prevention efforts could scale to counteract the effects of a worsening crisis.

This study updates an existing national simulation model of individuals with OUD—MODIPHI (Modeling OUD Dynamics Informing Public Health Interventions)5—to simulate multiple hypothetical scenarios. Our objectives are twofold—first, to estimate how overdose-related outcomes and costs might change over a four-year period between 2025 and 2028 assuming a modest short-term, sustained annual 5% increase, reflecting a rebound, or 5% decrease, reflecting a steady decline, in opioid-involved overdose rates among individuals with OUD; second, to estimate the level of evidence-based prevention strategies to counteract increases in fatal overdoses observed in rebounding scenarios with increased opioid-involved overdose rates. In steady decline scenarios with continued decreases in fatal overdose rates, we also estimate fatal overdoses potentially averted through these increased prevention efforts.

Methods

Study design

This modeling study uses MODIPHI, an existing, national-level system dynamics simulation model of the estimated US population aged ≥12 years with OUD.5 MODIPHI represents individuals with OUD (not receiving medication for OUD [MOUD]), with OUD receiving MOUD (stratified by treatment duration), in sustained remission from OUD (receiving MOUD or counseling-only treatment for ≥360 days and no past 90-day opioid use), nonfatal and fatal opioid-involved overdose, and other-cause death (Supplementary Methods-A1). For this study, we updated and recalibrated MODIPHI-2.0 with 2021–2024 data (Supplementary Methods-A2, Tables S1–S4) and projected outcomes over a four-year period between 2025 and 2028. Analyses were conducted using AnyLogic (version 8.9.0).6

This study was reviewed by CDC, deemed research not involving human subjects, and conducted in accordance with applicable federal law and CDC policy, outlined in 45 CFR §46. Institutional review board approval was not sought and the need for informed consent did not apply because deidentified, retrospective, aggregate data were used in this study. This study followed the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) reporting guideline.7

Data sources and parameter estimation

Primary data sources include 2021–2024 data from the State Unintentional Drug Overdose Reporting System (SUDORS)8 at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the National Survey on Drug use and Health (NSDUH)9, 10, 11, 12 (Supplementary Methods-B1, Table S5). Additional data sources include administrative claims, published literature, and the CDC MOUD study.13 MODIPHI-2.0 was initialized using 2020 estimates from MODIPHI-1.05 (Supplementary Methods-B2, Table S6). Evidence suggests that OUD prevalence has been decreasing prior to 202014, 15, 16; plus, there are no available estimated national adjustments to 2021–2024 NSDUH estimates of OUD. Consequently, MODIPHI-2.0 was updated conservatively, assuming the 2020 adjusted OUD prevalence5 decreased between 2021 and 2024 by 5% annually, reflecting the overall change in NSDUH overall OUD prevalence estimates between 2021 and 2024 (Supplementary Methods-A2, Table S1). MODIPHI-2.0 represents individuals in remission since 2019, i.e., the start of the modeling period in MODIPHI-1.0.

Model calibration

We calibrated using 2021–2024 data (Supplementary Methods C1–C5, Tables S7–S12) from SUDORS and the National Vital Statistics System (NVSS) to the estimated annual fatal opioid-involved overdoses among persons with OUD (Supplementary Methods C1, Table S8). Additional calibration targets included the population with OUD estimated based on an annual 5% decrease (Supplementary Methods A2, Table S1; Supplementary Methods C2, Table S9) and NSDUH estimates of populations receiving MOUD (Supplementary Methods-C3, Table S10).

Baseline and hypothetical scenarios

The baseline status-quo model (Scenario SB), calibrated to the assumptions of 5% decrease in OUD prevalence between 2021 and 2024, was projected over a four-year period from 2025 to 2028. Two hypothetical scenarios were examined—1) Rebounding, assuming an annual 5% increase in fatal overdose rates among individuals with OUD or in remission from OUD (S+5), and 2) Steady decline, assuming an annual 5% decrease in fatal overdose rates (S−5) (Supplementary Methods-C6, Table S13). NVSS data indicate that opioid overdose deaths involving fentanyl decreased 2.2% between 2022 and 2023.1 Provisional 12-month mortality data from April 2025 indicate a nearly 25% decrease in the number of fatal drug overdoses1; however, these data are reflective of the general population and not specifically among individuals with OUD. Given uncertainty about how fatal opioid overdose rates changed among individuals with OUD, we chose to model a more conservative 5% change, relative to the previous year, starting with baseline rates in 2024 (Supplementary Methods-C6, Table S13). This choice was further informed by an average 3.7% decrease in fatal opioid overdoses1 between 2021 and 2024 (Supplementary Methods-A2, Table S2). We assumed other parameters and calibrated conditions remained constant between 2025 and 2028. The hypothetical scenarios only assumed a change in the fatal overdose rates, with nonfatal overdose rates remaining the same. This allowed us to independently understand the impact of changing fatal overdose rates.

Outcomes

Fatal and nonfatal opioid-involved overdoses among individuals with OUD were estimated between 2025 and 2028. Other health outcomes reported include the number of individuals with OUD (not receiving MOUD), in MOUD treatment, and in remission at the end of 2028.

Additionally, to estimate the economic burden of fatal overdose and OUD from a societal perspective in the baseline and hypothetical scenarios we applied per overdose and per OUD case cost estimates from a prior study,17 adjusted to 2025 dollars18 (Supplementary Methods-C7). These estimates represent criminal justice, lost productivity, and healthcare costs (including costs associated with medically treated nonfatal overdoses, medication for OUD and general health care costs from increased office visits).17,19 We compared economic burden in the two hypothetical scenarios to the baseline assuming status quo. Scenarios with negative costs relative to baseline represent decreased economic burden related to fatal opioid-involved overdose and OUD. Those with positive costs relative to baseline represent increased economic burden.

Prevention strategies and optimization

Our second objective was to estimate the level of prevention strategies (Supplementary Methods-D1) to counteract increasing fatal overdose rates. Specifically, each strategy was optimized in the model to identify the intervention level to minimize the number of additional overdose deaths observed in the rebounding scenario S+5 relative to baseline (Supplementary Methods-D2, Table S14). Applying the optimized prevention levels from S+5 to the steady decline scenarios with decreased fatal overdose rates provided estimates of additional fatal overdoses potentially averted through increased prevention.

We examined four prevention strategies (Supplementary Methods-D1): 1) MOUD Initiation: the annual proportion of individuals with OUD currently not receiving medication initiated into MOUD treatment; 2) Linkage to MOUD: the annual proportion of individuals experiencing a nonfatal overdose initiated into MOUD treatment within 24 h; 3) MOUD Retention: the annual proportion of individuals retained in MOUD treatment for ≥6 months (i.e., reduced OUD recurrence5); 4) Bystander Naloxone: the annual proportion of potential fatal overdoses among individuals with OUD (current or in remission) receiving successful administration of naloxone by a bystander.

Sensitivity analyses

We examined two additional calibrated baseline models, assuming OUD prevalence A) increased 5% annually, or B) remained constant between 2021 and 2024. The same scenarios and optimized prevention strategies were further examined for each of these additional calibrated baseline models (Supplementary Results-F).

Role of the funding source

No funding was received for this work.

Results

Assuming an annual 5% decrease in OUD prevalence between 2021 and 2024, the baseline model, SB, projected an estimated 96,799 fatal and 3,396,080 nonfatal opioid-involved overdoses among persons with OUD between 2025 and 2028 (Table 1). The baseline model additionally estimated 7,324,208 individuals with OUD not receiving MOUD (i.e., 40.3% of the population with current or in remission from OUD), 1,591,635 individuals in MOUD treatment (8.8%), and 9,239,216 individuals in remission (50.9%) at the end of the four-year period (Supplementary Results-E, Tables S15 and S16). The overall estimated economic burden of fatal overdose and OUD between 2025 and 2028 in the baseline scenario was $3.94 trillion (in 2025 dollars), with $1.43 trillion in fatal overdose costs and $2.52 trillion in OUD costs.

The steady decline scenario S−5, simulating an annual 5% decrease in fatal overdose rates between 2025 and 2028, estimated 14.1% (13,678) fewer fatal overdoses than the baseline scenario over four years. This scenario estimated slight increases in individuals with OUD (0.09%; 6,609), receiving MOUD (0.08%; 1,249), and in remission (0.06%; 5,632), representing individuals who may have otherwise experienced fatal overdoses if the status-quo persisted. With fewer fatal overdoses, the economic burden was estimated to decrease by $199 billion from baseline.

The rebounding scenario S+5, simulating an annual 5% increase in fatal overdose rates between 2025 and 2028, estimated 16.5% (15,997) additional fatal overdoses relative to baseline. There were marginal decreases in populations with OUD (0.10%; 7,348), receiving MOUD (0.09%; 1,408), and in remission (0.08%; 7,035) since we observed an increase in the number of fatal overdoses. The economic burden in this scenario was estimated to be an additional $233 billion compared to baseline.

Sensitivity analyses assuming no change in OUD prevalence or annual 5% increases in OUD prevalence in additional baseline models between 2021 and 2024 estimated 107,627 and 119,531 fatal overdoses, respectively (Supplementary Results-F, Table S17) between 2025 and 2028, given the larger OUD population at risk under these assumptions. Scenarios under these assumptions estimated similar effects as our main analyses, indicating that baseline OUD prevalence does not substantially influence relative changes in overdose-related outcomes under scenarios of changing fatal overdose rates. However, economic costs were substantially higher in sensitivity analyses than main analyses when OUD prevalence decreased ($3.9 trillion), totaling $5.1 trillion when OUD prevalence remained level and $6.7 trillion when OUD prevalence increased. The additional economic burden from rebounding scenarios was also higher—$275 billion when OUD prevalence remained level and $368 billion when OUD prevalence increased.

The annual level of interventions (I) to counteract the 15,997 additional overdoses observed under scenario S+5 with increasing overdose rates were estimated (Fig. 1). Interventions that retained all individuals in MOUD for 6 months or linked all individuals to MOUD after a nonfatal overdose were insufficient in counteracting the additional overdoses observed; these interventions were estimated to decrease fatal overdoses by 6.5% (1,042) and 31.9% (5,110), respectively, relative to lives lost under S+5. Applying the same level of interventions in a counterfactual assuming decreased fatal overdoses rates, i.e., S−5, was estimated to further decrease fatal overdoses by an additional 6.1% (839) and 30.0% (4,109).

Scaling prevention strategies including annual MOUD initiation (I = 62.6%) and bystander naloxone administration during potentially fatal drug use (I = 14.2%) were independently sufficient to potentially counteract increased fatal overdose rates estimated in the rebound scenario S+5. Applying the same level of interventions in the steady decline scenario S−5 assuming decreasing fatal overdose rates could further reduce fatal overdoses by 95.3% (13,038) and 86.0% (11,768), respectively.

Results from sensitivity analyses assuming no change or annual 5% increases in OUD prevalence between 2025 and 2028 indicated an increase in intervention levels needed across most prevention strategies compared to the main analysis (Supplementary Results-F, Figures S2 and S3). Analyses assuming no change in OUD prevalence corresponded with much of the main analysis, however, only bystander naloxone administration (I = 15.0%) was sufficient to counteract increasing fatal overdose rates. Substantial increases in MOUD initiation (I = 100%) were able to counteract almost all (98.5%) of the additional overdoses in S+5. Similarly, analyses assuming an increase in OUD prevalence showed that despite maximizing MOUD initiation (I = 100%), this single strategy could counteract 82.2% of the additional overdoses in S+5. Importantly, bystander naloxone remained highly efficient despite assumed increases in OUD prevalence (I = 15.2%).

Discussion

This study estimated opioid overdose-related outcomes under hypothetical scenarios assuming future increases or decreases in fatal overdose rates among individuals with OUD between 2025 and 2028. Among individuals with OUD, our baseline model estimated that if the 2024 status quo remained from 2025 through 2028, we would anticipate nearly 97,000 fatal opioid-involved overdoses, nearly 3.4 million nonfatal opioid-involved overdoses, and an economic burden of fatal overdose and OUD of $3.94 trillion.

Findings showed that if fatal overdose risk among individuals with OUD increased 5% annually between 2025 and 2028, we would estimate an additional 16.5% fatal overdoses relative to the status quo baseline model, corresponding to a $233 billion increase in overdose-related costs. Conversely, an annual 5% decrease in fatal overdose rates would result in 14.1% fewer fatal overdoses than status quo, and $199 billion in reduced economic burden. Importantly, both scenarios showed antagonistic, or non-additive effects on fatal overdoses over four years (i.e., a 5% annual increase did not directly translate to a ∼20% change (specifically, 100−(100±5)4)% change in fatal overdoses). This is likely because fatal overdose risk is much lower among individuals in treatment and remission (Supplementary Methods-A2 and B1, Table S5). Over four years, the population recovering from OUD through MOUD and in remission grows to represent nearly 60% of the modeled population (Table S15), limiting the effects of small increases or decreases in fatal overdose rates. Still, the large population in recovery underscores the importance of ensuring that individuals receiving MOUD and in remission continue to have access to and education about naloxone.

Sensitivity analyses highlighted the staggering costs associated with higher OUD prevalence. The substantially higher fatal overdose and cost burden associated with baseline models assuming level OUD prevalence (107,627 deaths, $5.2 trillion) and increasing OUD prevalence (119,531 deaths, $6.7 trillion) emphasize the importance of continued prevention efforts to reduce the size of the population with OUD and at risk for fatal overdose. Specific examples include educating the public about the risks of OUD through the CDC Stop Overdose campaign,20 limiting exposure to opioids through evidence-based prescribing practices as outlined in the 2022 CDC Clinical Practice Guideline for Prescribing Opioids for Pain,21 implementing linkage to care strategies as outlined in programs like CDC's Overdose Data to Action (OD2A),22 and continuing efforts to prevent youth substance use through evidence-based strategies as outlined in CDC's ENGAGE resource for action23 and through community mobilization via the Drug-Free Communities (DFC) Support Program.24

Results also estimated the level of four broad prevention strategies to counteract potential small and sustained increases in fatal overdose rates over four years. Maximizing MOUD retention for at least six months or linkage to MOUD immediately after a nonfatal overdose were alone insufficient to counteract rebounding fatal overdose rates. However, increasing MOUD initiation among the OUD population not in treatment and bystander recognition of overdose and naloxone administration during potentially fatal drug use were able to counteract these increases. Increasing annual MOUD initiation rates among individuals with OUD not currently receiving treatment to 62.6% was able to offset an annual 5% increase in fatal overdose rates. Yet, MOUD treatment continues to remain underutilized, with only 1-in-4 individuals who need MOUD reporting receiving it.25 Buprenorphine dispensing rates have slowed in recent years despite policy changes including the elimination of the waiver requirement needed to prescribe buprenorphine for OUD in 2022.26 Given these challenges, such a scale up in treatment initiation would not be immediate and may require a comprehensive approach focused on reducing stigma for seeking MOUD, addressing clinician barriers, implementing systems-level improvements, and strengthening payment policies to incentivize prescribing.26

Given the relatively lower level of effort needed for implementation, bystander recognition of overdose and successful administration of naloxone was the most efficient of the single interventions modeled to counteract short-term increases in fatal overdose. Compared to other strategies modeled, findings showed that relatively lower levels of annual naloxone administration during fatal drug use (14–15%) were sufficient to counteract increased fatal overdose rates, regardless of underlying trends in OUD prevalence. SUDORS data indicate that in 2024, among fatal overdoses with known circumstances, fatal drug use was witnessed in 8.1% of cases, and a potential bystander who had opportunity to intervene was present nearby in 44.1% of cases.27 These data suggest that scaling up naloxone administration through additional overdose education and naloxone distribution is a promising strategy—one study found that for every $1 spent on community-based naloxone education and distribution programs, there was $2,742 of benefit from fatal opioid overdoses averted.28 Taken together, these findings underscore the critical importance of widespread access to naloxone in the community and educating laypersons on identifying signs of an opioid overdose and administering naloxone to facilitate timely overdose reversal.

Despite linkage to MOUD after nonfatal overdose and MOUD initiation among individuals with OUD having similar aims to increase the population in treatment, the former alone was insufficient in offsetting small short-term increases in fatal overdose rates. This is because MOUD initiation reduces the overdose risk in the larger population with OUD versus populations experiencing a nonfatal overdose. Further, linkage to MOUD after a nonfatal overdose might have a larger effect size in scenarios with increased nonfatal overdose rates, which we did not model. We caution that these findings should not be misinterpreted as there being limited value in prevention strategies increasing retention or linkage to MOUD immediately after a nonfatal overdose. To the contrary, nonfatal opioid overdose can serve as a critical opportunity for treatment engagement and better outcomes—receipt of MOUD after nonfatal overdose has been associated with decreases in opioid-related29 and all-cause mortality, even after adjusting for effects of treatment discontinuation.30 Rather, the interpretation from our model is that linkage to, initiation of, and retention in MOUD both after nonfatal overdose and among the population with OUD are complementary strategies, demonstrated to be vital in increasing the size of the population receiving treatment, reducing the population at risk for overdose, and ultimately, reducing longer-term overdose-related mortality.5 Given the established benefits of linkage to care,30,31 OD2A supports these activities and could help increase the population receiving MOUD.

This analysis did not identify specific reasons for increased fatal overdose rates among individuals with OUD but instead modeled hypothetical scenarios representing rebounds or steady declines in opioid overdoses. Despite overall decreases in fatal opioid overdose rates, some Western and Midwestern states are seeing small increases in fatal overdoses1 where fentanyl was introduced later.32 Further, changes in the illegal drug supply could also result in increasing fatal overdose rates—recent reports have indicated the reemergence of carfentanil, a fentanyl analog that is 100 times more potent than fentanyl, in fatal overdoses.3 Other scenarios that could result in increasing fatal overdose rates might arise from decreased public health efforts such as reduced funding or capacity for treatment, fewer organizations that provide and support linkage to care, or decreased public health and public safety collaboration and diversion programs. Importantly, our analysis also highlighted the substantial benefits of scaling up prevention strategies even if fatal overdose rates continue to decrease–separately scaling up MOUD initiation and bystander administration of naloxone could each result in over 13,000 and nearly 12,000 deaths averted over four years, respectively.

These findings are subject to limitations. First, these are what-if scenario analyses that are not designed to forecast the future of the overdose crisis but rather serve to project changes in outcomes under plausible conditions. Second, our model makes key assumptions regarding underlying OUD prevalence due to lack of continuity in data sources. While sensitivity analyses demonstrated the robustness of our main findings, further research is needed to accurately establish recent trends in OUD prevalence and incidence. Third, these findings are based on specific hypothetical scenarios assuming small, sustained increases or decreases in fatal overdose rates among individuals with OUD over a four-year period and may not be generalizable to other scenarios. Fourth, proxy variables were used to assess evidence of prior OUD among decedents in SUDORS data (Supplementary Methods-C1). Importantly, SUDORS data now additionally captures history of fentanyl use in addition to prescription opioid misuse and heroin, making the data more complete than the previous MODIPHI model. Also, the most recent provisional data1 show a nearly 27% decrease in drug overdose deaths but do not distinguish between fatal overdose trends among the general population versus individuals with OUD—further research might help determine whether the latter population experienced a similar magnitude of decrease. Fifth, fentanyl availability in the supply is a key driver of fatal overdoses33 and the model does not explicitly simulate changes in the lethality of the drug supply including the plateauing of deaths involving fentanyl suggesting that illegally-made fentanyl saturation of the illegal opioid market may have been reached in 2024.1 Sixth, the specific risk of fatal overdose among individuals in remission remains unknown. We addressed this by calibrating a substantially decreased risk among individuals in remission, however, this may not be entirely representative, especially during the fentanyl era. Further, the model only captures the estimated population in remission since 2019. Seventh, we did not simulate combinations of prevention strategies due to substantial computational complexity. Given the complementary nature of these strategies and past studies demonstrating the importance of combinations of interventions, a conceivably lower level of each of these strategies might be sufficient to counteract increases in fatal overdoses.5,34,35 Additionally, this study did not incorporate the cost of implementing these strategies, given the wide and varied nature through which strategies may be applied. Future research could examine which combinations of intervention strategies might maximally improve fatal overdoses and the estimated costs associated with scaling these evidence-based prevention efforts.

Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths. We updated a previously validated model of individuals with OUD to examine overdose-related outcomes under different scenarios using the most recently available national data. We conducted sensitivity analyses accounting for uncertainties in the underlying prevalence of OUD. Lastly, in addition to being the first and only model to explicitly distinguish health states based on duration of MOUD treatment, MODIPHI is the first model to report individuals in remission from OUD.

US overdose deaths remain staggeringly high–provisional data ending in April 2025 indicate nearly 138 opioid-involved fatal overdoses each day.1 This modeling study examined overdose-related outcomes among individuals with OUD between 2025 and 2028 under status quo and hypothetical counterfactuals scenarios assuming small and sustained short-term increases and decreases in fatal overdose rates. Even if fatal overdose rates remained unchanged, we estimated the economic burden of the overdose crisis to be over $3.9 trillion between 2025 and 2028. Our results underscore the efficiency of timely administration of naloxone and initiating individuals into MOUD treatment in counteracting potential increases in fatal overdoses. Further, findings demonstrate the substantial benefits of scaling prevention efforts regardless of the future trajectory of the overdose crisis. Through multiple waves of this epidemic, approximately 806,000 lives have been lost to opioid overdose between 1999 and 2023.36 These findings have important implications for the value of continuing to invest in evidence-based public health efforts to prevent overdose.
