General practitioners’ perspectives on blood biomarkers for Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Thomas Claessen, F. C. W. Visser, B. C. van Munster, W. M. van der Flier, M. Jiménez‐Mausbach, A. C. van Harten, C. E. Teunissen, L. N. C. Visser
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDAlzheimer's disease (AD) blood biomarkers could improve the diagnostic process for patients with cognitive complaints presenting to primary care. We investigated general practitioners’ (GPs’) perspectives on facilitating and hindering factors associated with the use and implementation of AD blood biomarkers.METHODIn this qualitative, semi‐structured interview study, we used purposive sampling to recruit a heterogeneous group of 18 Dutch GPs. Qualitative data analysis of interview transcripts was based on the Theoretical Domains Framework.RESULTSGPs believed that AD blood biomarkers could have both positive and negative consequences. Mentioned positive consequences included facilitating an accessible diagnostic process, clarity about the etiology, and life planning. Mentioned negative consequences were imposing a psychological burden on patients and possible AD over‐ or underdiagnosis.CONCLUSIONWhile AD blood biomarkers may have potential benefits according to GPs, additional evidence, definition of context of use and logistics, and adoption of guidelines are needed for eventual implementation.Highlights GPs experience a knowledge gap regarding AD and AD blood biomarkersAD blood biomarkers could inform GPs, enabling decision‐making in primary careGPs perceive various harms and benefits associated with the use of AD biomarkersInformation, education and guidelines are key factors for successful implementation

INTRODUCTION

Currently, 101 million individuals worldwide live with Alzheimer's disease (AD) in the mild cognitive impairment or dementia stage. 1 In recent years, AD has evolved from a diagnosis based solely on symptomatology, to a disease for which biomarkers can help to support the diagnosis. 2 , 3 Recent emergence of disease‐modifying therapies (DMTs) may increase the urgency for an accurate and timely AD diagnosis. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 In specialized settings, amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis can aid in diagnosing AD. 2 , 8 Accurate AD diagnosis in primary care can be challenging as general practitioners (GPs) do not have access to pathology‐specific diagnostic tests. 9 , 10

The frequency of under‐ and misdiagnosed AD(‐dementia) in primary care is high, causing delay in diagnosis, care arrangement, and potential treatment. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Blood biomarkers represent a major development in the possibility to accurately support an AD diagnosis. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 Use of AD blood biomarkers in primary care may significantly increase GPs’ diagnostic accuracy of AD patients, while caregiver attitudes towards AD blood biomarker testing are mostly positive. 19 , 20 In addition, blood biomarkers could be easily accessible in primary care, are less invasive, and are likely to be more cost effective than amyloid PET and CSF biomarker analysis. 21 , 22 Therefore, good‐performing blood biomarkers might facilitate an accurate and timely AD diagnosis in primary care. 7 , 16 , 19

Currently, little is known regarding GPs’ views on the impact and desirability of AD blood biomarkers. 23 Implementing research findings and innovations in routine health care is a difficult and unpredictable process, and exploring facilitating and hindering factors is important to better understand the context and needs of a specific health‐care setting. 24 , 25 We aimed to explore GPs’ perspectives on facilitating and hindering factors associated with the use and implementation of AD blood biomarkers in primary care.

METHODS

Study design

In this qualitative, semi‐structured interview study, we explored GPs’ perspectives regarding the use and implementation of AD blood biomarkers in primary care. We aimed to interview a minimum of 15 GPs and continued interviewing until data saturation was achieved, meaning that we gained no new relevant insights in three consecutive interviews (stopping criterion). 26 , 27 We used the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) to guide our directed content‐coding approach, mapping the qualitative data onto the 14 TDF domains. The TDF was developed and validated by behavioral scientists and implementation researchers to help identify, understand, and map factors that influence existing practices. 28 We used the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) checklist to ensure comprehensive reporting of this study (see supporting information 1). 29 Ethical approval was obtained from the Amsterdam UMC Research Ethics committee (METC Amsterdam UMC), project number: 2023.0112, CANTATE‐Perspectives.

Participants

We recruited a heterogeneous group of Dutch GPs for one‐on‐one semi‐structured interviews conducted between July 2023 and November 2023. In the Netherlands, every citizen has a GP, who decides whether a patient should be referred, thus fulfilling a gatekeeping role, while also fulfilling an important role in the AD diagnostic pathway. 30 Purposive sampling was used to ensure a variety in perspectives, taking into account years of experience, sex, and practice location (rural vs. urban). 31 Participants were recruited through Dutch GP networks and also by inviting GPs who had existing connections with the research team. Potential participants were approached by telephone or e‐mail. All participants provided written informed consent prior to the interviews. The interviews were audio recorded, transcribed verbatim, and field notes were made.

Data collection

The interview guide was based on the TDF, drafted by one junior researcher (T.C., male, junior researcher, medical doctor), and improved based on feedback of a senior researcher (L.V., female, psychologist, PhD). 28 This guide was then reviewed by four other researchers: A.H. (female, neurologist, PhD), B.M. (female, internist–geriatrician, PhD), C.T. (female, head of neurochemistry lab, PhD) and F.V. (female, junior researcher, medical doctor), and thereafter piloted in three interviews. GPs did not receive any information regarding a biological definition of AD or AD blood biomarkers prior to the interviews to facilitate an open and unbiased opinion.

During the interviews, we presented two hypothetical scenarios which included basic information regarding AD blood biomarkers and potential blood biomarker accuracy to GPs to set the stage and stimulate discussion. Two questions were added to the interview guide after the pilot interviews resulting in a final version (supporting information 2—including the scenarios). The interviews were conducted online by one researcher (T.C.) using Microsoft Teams software. The length of the interviews ranged between 30 and 50 minutes, which was in accordance with recommendations from Atkins et al. 28 Data saturation was achieved after conducting 18 interviews, thus fulfilling the stopping criterion.

Data analysis

The transcribed interview data were analyzed by a team of two coders (T.C. and F.V., supervised by L.V.) using a directed content‐coding approach supported by MaxQDA software. 32 Both coders familiarized themselves to the data by reading all transcripts. For nine (50%) of the interviews, the coders independently identified and coded all fragments of relevance, that is, a part of a sentence, a whole sentence, or a few sentences that described a single factor associated with the use and/or implementation of AD blood biomarkers. Fragments were categorized into one or more of the 14 existing TDF domains and if possible further categorized into subcategories defined during data analysis according to a published guideline. 28 Fragments that could not be mapped onto an existing TDF domain were coded inductively. The coding tree was refined through two consensus sessions between the two coders, supervised by L.V. After a high inter‐observer agreement was reached, the remaining nine interviews were coded by T.C.

This decision was informed by the following process: a first subset of six interviews (33%) was independently coded and discussed by the two coders. The initial agreement in the first subset was 78%, while a percentage > 60% indicates acceptable agreement. 33 Some changes to the coding tree were made based on our consensus meeting and we decided to select an additional subset of three interviews to be independently coded by two coders. The agreement between coders was 90% for this second set. Disagreements were resolved again by the two coders in a second consensus meeting and no major changes were made to the coding tree. After coding all 18 interviews, the two coders and the other authors reviewed the resulting coding tree and identified overarching themes.

Reflexivity

We are aware that most authors work in an environment where cutting‐edge research on biomarkers is performed. Therefore, we might have a certain perspective, potentially more progressive, when considering the use and implementation of AD blood biomarkers. We reflected on that through discussions in our team, and by making certain methodological decisions. By using a semi‐structured interview design, we ascertained that the same questions were asked to all participants. Additionally, we complemented the perspective of the first author and main coder (T.C.), with the perspective from a coder from a different environment and with more experience in research among GPs (F.V.), to reduce the risk of bias in data collection, coding, and analysis. Based on these aspects, we trust that our findings are a good representation of GPs’ perspectives regarding the use and implementation of AD blood biomarkers in primary care in the Netherlands.

RESULTS

We invited and interviewed 15 GPs and 3 GPs in training (61% female). Years of experience ranged from 0 to 30 years, 39% of the participants were based in an urban area, and 33% felt confident diagnosing cognitive complaints. GPs’ knowledge, skills, beliefs about capabilities, and emotions could both facilitate and hinder the use of AD blood biomarkers

GPs often made statements that were found to be a direct contraposition in our data. For example, in the domains skills and knowledge and perception, only a few interviewed GPs were aware of the existence of AD blood biomarkers or could provide information about such biomarkers to patients (quote I and II, Table 1) which hinders the use of AD biomarkers in clinical practice. For the few GPs who were aware, their knowledge could facilitate the use of AD blood biomarkers. Table 1 shows all identified personal‐level factors, which were part of the domains skills, knowledge and perception, beliefs about capabilities, and emotions. Below we discuss our most relevant findings.

Most GPs perceived AD primarily as a clinical syndrome, not a biological diagnosis, and regarded AD as synonymous with AD dementia (quote III and IV, Table 1). All GPs were aware of the Dutch primary care guideline for dementia, which states that GPs can diagnose (AD) dementia themselves. 30 Interestingly, only half of the participants had confidence in their ability to accurately diagnose AD (dementia) and almost all consulted a specialist for confirmation of their diagnosis (quote V and VI, Table 1). Hardly any of the GPs we interviewed felt comfortable with a pre‐dementia AD diagnosis (quote VII, Table 1).

AD blood biomarkers could have negative consequences for patients and GPs

Negative beliefs about consequences were that AD blood biomarkers could: (1) put a burden on patients, for example by imposing a psychological burden because patients would know they have AD in an early stage of the disease (quote I, Table 2); (2) result in AD over‐ or underdiagnosis due to potential false positive and negative test results or by diagnosing AD more frequently (quote II, Table 2); (3) put patients at a disadvantage, for example by getting treated differently by (health) insurance agencies or banks (quote III, Table 2); (4) put a burden on GPs, because explaining AD biomarkers to patients could be time consuming (quote IV, Table 2); and (5) lead to inappropriate use and unnecessary costs as patients could demand AD biomarkers testing even though they do not have cognitive complaints (quote V and VI, Table 2). In addition, some GPs were unsure or did not believe that AD blood biomarkers would have clinical benefit, or change clinical practice or decision making in primary care (quote VII and VIII, Table 2). These beliefs could also hinder the use of these biomarkers in primary care. “If everyone with Alzheimer's disease gets diagnosed, it [Alzheimer's disease] will seem to occur more often. You might have patients who would rather not have known that they have Alzheimer's disease and who would have died of something else. Just like with prostate cancer.” — GP 4

“If everyone with Alzheimer's disease gets diagnosed, it [Alzheimer's disease] will seem to occur more often. You might have patients who would rather not have known that they have Alzheimer's disease and who would have died of something else. Just like with prostate cancer.” — GP 4

AD blood biomarkers could have several benefits for GPs and patients

In contrast, other interviewed GPs believed that AD blood biomarkers would have clinical benefit or would change clinical practice and decision making (quote I and II, Table 3). “Often, there is a lot of doubt regarding the diagnosis for the patient and the general practitioner. A blood test to diagnose Alzheimer's disease can be beneficial in those cases.” — GP 9

“Often, there is a lot of doubt regarding the diagnosis for the patient and the general practitioner. A blood test to diagnose Alzheimer's disease can be beneficial in those cases.” — GP 9

Positive beliefs about consequences were that AD blood biomarkers could: (1) provide GPs and patients clarity about the etiology because biomarkers facilitate a biological AD diagnosis in primary care while this currently is not possible (quote III, Table 3); (2) facilitate arranging care or earlier treatment so patients can get timely (home) care or treatment with DMTs (quote IV, Table 3); (3) make the diagnostic process in primary care more efficient by reducing diagnostic delay and referrals to memory clinics (quote V, Table 3); (4) facilitate life planning because patients know their diagnosis in an earlier stage of the disease and have more time to plan their life (quote VI, Table 3); and (5) make the diagnostic process more accessible because patients do not have to go to a specialized care setting for an accurate and biomarker‐based AD diagnosis (quote VII, Table 3).

GPs and patients should decide whether to test for AD together; which patient categories should be offered AD blood testing is not yet clear

GPs emphasized that the decision whether to test for AD should be made together with patients and their relatives (quote I, Table 4). However, GPs feared that patients or their relatives could demand AD blood biomarker testing, which could increase patient pressure (quote II, Table 4). “Whether to test for Alzheimer's disease depends on the patient. Some patients want to know that they have Alzheimer's while this knowledge does not influence their quality of life. Other patients say there are no consequences and I can manage now, so I would rather not know.” — GP 10

“Whether to test for Alzheimer's disease depends on the patient. Some patients want to know that they have Alzheimer's while this knowledge does not influence their quality of life. Other patients say there are no consequences and I can manage now, so I would rather not know.” — GP 10

GPs had different opinions on which patients should be eligible for AD biomarker testing (quote III–VIII, Table 4) and who would benefit most from testing (quote IX–XI, Table 4). “Perhaps we should use this Alzheimer's blood test in patients with a family history of Alzheimer's disease or in patients with early symptoms. I find that difficult to determine with the little knowledge I have about these tests.” — GP 7

“Perhaps we should use this Alzheimer's blood test in patients with a family history of Alzheimer's disease or in patients with early symptoms. I find that difficult to determine with the little knowledge I have about these tests.” — GP 7

Nearly all GPs agreed that these biomarkers should not be used to screen for AD in patients without cognitive complaints (quote VII and VIII, Table 4).

Key factors for implementation on health‐care policy or the national level were accuracy, access, education, and information

Factors identified within the environmental context and resources domain are on a health‐care policy or national level and thus go beyond GPs and/or their patients. Key factors were: (1) blood test accuracy: most GPs preferred to rule out AD while also keeping the rate of false positive AD diagnoses as low as possible due to the stigma surrounding AD (quote I, Table 5) and GPs stated that 80% sensitivity and specificity would not be accurate enough when using these biomarkers—an increase to 90% would be more desirable; (2) availability of treatment: treatment would increase the urgency to diagnose AD in primary care, thus also increasing the added value of biomarkers (quote II and III, Table 5); (3) information and education: GPs stated information should be easily accessible and provided to patients and GPs to learn more about the characteristics and limitations of AD blood biomarkers (quote IV, Table 5); (4) access: AD blood biomarkers should be accessible in local labs and should not have too many preconditions for GPs and patients (e.g., timing and storage of samples) or pre‐analytical variance (quote VI, Table 5); and (5) integration in GP guidelines: the eventual role of AD blood biomarkers in primary care should be established, via evidence evaluation for guideline development (quote VII and VIII, Table 5). “A sensitivity or specificity of 80% are not exactly values that I think I would like for a test like this. Mainly the sensitivity should be higher since I want to be able to rule out Alzheimer's disease.” — GP 15.

“A sensitivity or specificity of 80% are not exactly values that I think I would like for a test like this. Mainly the sensitivity should be higher since I want to be able to rule out Alzheimer's disease.” — GP 15.

DISCUSSION

We identified multiple overarching themes and factors associated with the use and implementation of AD blood biomarkers in primary care. On a personal level, the most important factors were GPs’ perception of AD as a clinical diagnosis and their unawareness of AD blood biomarkers. GPs generally regarded AD as synonymous with AD dementia, suggesting that they are unfamiliar with the concepts of preclinical and prodromal AD, which has also been described before. 34 Diagnosing AD based on symptomatology, as expressed by interviewed GPs, is in accordance with the current Dutch primary care dementia guideline. 30 However, AD is often not accurately diagnosed when based on clinical symptoms only. 19 Symptoms commonly observed in AD may be caused by other (neurodegenerative) diseases, early AD symptoms might be missed, and AD is often found concurrent with other neurodegenerative pathologies. 11 , 13 , 35 The presence of blood biomarkers that allow support of an AD diagnosis in a non‐invasive manner, together with the recently revised criteria that define AD as a (clinical–)biological construct and the emergence of DMTs, highlight the need to think about integrating biomarkers in the diagnostic work‐up. 2 , 3

On a health‐care policy and national level, key factors we identified were: (1) accessible information and education for GPs and patients. GPs indicated that they would need information about consequences of AD biomarker testing, as well as clear GP guidelines about timing and eligible patient groups. Availability of information, education, and guidelines could help to mitigate harms and carefully balance ethical considerations. However, more evidence regarding diagnostic accuracy, clinical consequences, counseling, and utility is needed before this can be provided. 19 , 36 (2) Availability of treatment for AD: after conducting this study, the European Medicines Agency recommended approval of lecanemab for clinical use in the European Union. 5 , 37 (3) Accuracy: most GPs preferred to be able to rule out AD, while keeping false positive results as low as possible due to the stigma surrounding AD. Nearly all GPs preferred a sensitivity of > 90%, which is in line with other recommendations. 38

Consequences of AD blood biomarkers for the diagnostic pathway could be both beneficial and harmful according to GPs. Interviewed GPs often did not have confidence in their diagnosis and referred patients who presented with cognitive complaints to specialty care for further assessment and diagnosis, which was also recently reported in another study. 39 We found that GPs believed that AD blood biomarkers might provide clarity about the diagnosis by detecting the etiology of patients’ symptoms, possibly enhancing diagnostic confidence and accuracy. Recent literature indeed shows that AD blood biomarkers can enhance diagnostic accuracy in patients presenting to primary care. 19 Literature regarding the impact of amyloid PET and AD CSF biomarker analysis on clinical practice (in specialty care) also reports an increase in diagnostic accuracy and confidence. 40 , 41 , 42 However, GPs believed that a (false) negative test result could lead to patients not being referred to specialty care even though this is indicated in their case. 43 Additionally, we found that GPs believed that that there is a chance that AD blood biomarkers might be used when there is no proper indication or with inadequate counseling, which could be harmful. Current international AD (blood biomarker) working groups and World Health Organization criteria state that these biomarkers should only be used in patients with objective cognitive decline, because the preclinical stage of AD has an uncertain prognosis and tests may have low positive predictive value due to lower prevalence of AD in this group. 3 , 38 , 44

It may be difficult to strike a balance between aforementioned perceived harms and benefits associated with the use of AD blood biomarkers in primary care at present. We refer to the difficulty of weighing benefits (e.g., clarity about the diagnosis, accessible diagnosis, and arranging care) and harms (e.g., emotional distress and risk of false positive results) of using blood biomarkers in the diagnostic process for AD. Harms and benefits identified in our study align with the recent findings of O'Brien et al. 23 To minimize harms, evidence‐based information and guidelines should be available and individual preferences of patients and their relatives should be taken into account because these are highly personal. 45 This process involves shared decision making, in which patients receive information about available options and their associated benefits and harms. Linking these choices to personal values contributes to deliberate and informed decisions. 46 We found that GPs take wishes and preferences of patients and their relatives into account to determine whether and when biomarker testing is preferable, to respect patient autonomy, which is in line with previous studies. 23 , 34 , 39 , 45 , 47

Still, GPs indicated that they would find it difficult to determine the right timing of AD blood biomarker testing and had different views on which patients should be offered AD blood biomarker testing, based on their personal weighing of various ethical considerations. Beneficence was one ethical principle to consider, which would be achieved by providing clarity about the diagnosis, and facilitating life planning. Non‐maleficence was another ethical principle that GPs considered, as GPs did not want to impose a psychological burden on patients (e.g., diagnosing AD in a preclinical stage). GPs agreed that testing asymptomatic AD patients is not desirable, which was previously found in another study among Dutch GPs and is in line with all current international guidelines. 34 , 38 , 44 It is important that all (ethical) considerations are weighed explicitly before implementing and using AD blood biomarkers for patients’ well‐being. 23 , 48 , 49

Strengths of this study are: (1) use of the TDF, which facilitates a validated and science‐based approach to identify determinants of behavior, while still allowing a broad perspective using content analysis during coding; and (2) purposive sampling of participants, with variation in sex, years of experience, and location of GPs. Limitations of this study include: (1) not all results might be applicable to other countries because this study focused on Dutch GPs’ perspectives and the organization of primary care differs greatly across countries, most factors we identified (e.g., cost, access, accuracy) are relevant regardless of how primary care is organized; (2) we mainly identified personal‐level factors due to our approach: asking individual GPs about their thoughts regarding the use of AD blood biomarkers in their practice; (3) some GPs might be better informed concerning AD research developments and staging because they were recruited from our own network. However, a large portion of GPs was not recruited from our network and by using purposive sampling we recruited a heterogeneous group of GPs, ensuring variation in perspectives.

To better understand determinants of behavior associated with the use and implementation of AD blood biomarkers in primary care, we propose further (international) exploration of facilitating and hindering factors on a health‐care policy and national level. This would provide a more comprehensive overview of all factors associated with the use and implementation of AD blood biomarkers in primary care. Additionally, quantification is needed to determine the relative importance of identified factors, for example by conducting a questionnaire study in a larger sample. In addition, currently expressed needs may be met by further exploration of the clinical utility of AD blood biomarkers in primary care and—when evidence‐based—careful integration in guidelines.

In conclusion, we identified various personal‐level factors and some healthcare policy‐ and national‐level factors associated with the use and implementation of AD blood biomarkers in primary care. Several challenges need to be addressed before the use of AD blood biomarkers in primary care would be beneficial. Our findings suggest that more information, for example, on logistics, and scientific evidence regarding diagnostic accuracy and clinical consequences of AD blood biomarker testing in primary care is needed. This evidence would facilitate future GP (counseling) guidelines and provide the information that is required to make an informed and deliberate decision based on individual preferences.
