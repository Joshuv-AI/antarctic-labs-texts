Crystal structures of Campylobacter jejuni CadF reveal a potential role for the arginine residue R268 in peptidoglycan recognition and pocket formation
Authors: Han Byeol Oh, Sung-il Yoon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCampylobacter jejuni is a pathogenic gram-negative bacterium that primarily causes foodborne enteritis in humans. The C. jejuni protein CadF is located in the outer membrane and is essential for the adhesion of C. jejuni to human cells. CadF contains an outer membrane-embedded domain and a periplasmic domain (CadFPD). The transmembrane domain is well known to bind fibronectin, contributing to host cell recognition. However, the exact function of the CadFPD has never been assessed experimentally. In this study, we present the crystal structures of the CadFPD and demonstrate that CadF may function as a peptidoglycan-binding protein. The CadFPD adopts a two-layer α/β structure and harbors a narrow pocket. The CadFPD interacts with a peptidoglycan-derived peptide by accommodating m-diaminopimelic acid (m-DAP), a non-proteinogenic amino acid of the gram-negative peptidoglycan peptide, into the pocket. The positively charged R268 residue of CadF forms the base of the pocket and specifically interacts with the carboxyl group of m-DAP via salt bridges. This observation combined with the ligand-less structure of the CadFPD mutant R268E highlights the potential role of the R268 residue in maintaining the intact pocket in the CadFPD structure and mediating peptidoglycan recognition.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20604-7.Keywords: CadF, Periplasmic domain, Crystal structure, Peptidoglycan, m-Diaminopimelic acidSubject terms: X-ray crystallography, X-ray crystallography

Introduction

Campylobacter jejuni is a pathogenic bacterium that typically causes foodborne diarrhea and enteritis in humans and is considered one of the leading causes of diarrheal diseases worldwide, particularly in developed countries1,2. C. jejuni can also cause Guillain–Barré syndrome, an autoimmune neuropathy, as a postinfectious disorder, accounting for one-third of cases3,4. C. jejuni typically colonizes the intestines of poultry as a resident microbial flora and can be transmitted to humans through the consumption of undercooked poultry products5. C. jejuni has been shown to bind human or avian cells using adhesins, such as CadF, FlpA, and JlpA, for the initiation of infection6–8. In particular, CadF and FlpA interact with the host protein fibronectin, which is noncovalently attached to cellular integrins, contributing to the adhesion of C. jejuni to host cells9,10.

The CadF protein is localized to the outer membrane of the cell wall of the gram-negative C. jejuni bacterium and extends from the periplasmic space through the outer membrane to the extracellular space11,12. CadF contains two domains, an N-terminal transmembrane domain (CadFTD) and a C-terminal periplasmic domain (CadFPD), as observed for its homologous protein OmpA, which is found in other gram-negative bacteria. The OmpA protein is embedded in the outer membrane via its transmembrane domain, which adopts a β-barrel structure, and it binds to peptidoglycan in the cell wall via its periplasmic domain (OmpAPD), which is exposed to the periplasmic space13,14. OmpA mediates the anchorage of the outer membrane to the peptidoglycan layer in the cell wall of gram-negative bacteria, thereby contributing to the structural integrity of the cell wall in these bacteria. Moreover, OmpA has been shown to function in biofilm formation15.

Structural and biophysicochemical studies on OmpA have provided insight into its peptidoglycan- and outer membrane-binding mechanisms. Previous structural studies on Acinetobacter baumannii OmpA (abOmpA), Samonella enterica OmpA (seOmpA), and Borrelia burgdorferi OmpA (bbOmpA) have shown that the OmpAPD adopts a mixed α/β structure and exists as either a monomer or a dimer depending on the bacterial species14,16. Analysis of the structure of A. baumannii OmpAPD (abOmpAPD) in complex with a pentapeptide revealed that the abOmpAPD can accommodate the peptide portion of the peptidoglycan in its pocket14. Additionally, a molecular dynamics study revealed that OmpA changes its outer membrane-binding pattern depending on the oligomeric state of the OmpAPD when it is bound to peptidoglycan17. However, these studies were performed on several bacterial species from the phyla Pseudomonadota and Spirochaetota, and in particular, the peptidoglycan-binding mode of OmpA has only been determined for A. baumannii, a bacterium belonging to the phylum Pseudomonadota. Therefore, it is unclear whether the peptidoglycan-binding pattern of the abOmpAPD is conserved across diverse OmpA proteins as well as CadF from C. jejuni, which belongs to the Campylobacterota phylum.

CadF has been extensively studied as a virulence factor that recognizes the host protein fibronectin. CadF binds to fibronectin via a specific motif (FRLS sequence) in its transmembrane domain9. However, the peptidoglycan recognition function of CadF has never been experimentally characterized despite its presumable key role in maintaining the structural integrity of the cell wall. Therefore, it is even unclear whether CadF interacts with peptidoglycan. Furthermore, owing to the lack of structural information on the CadFPD, the precise mechanism by which CadF recognizes peptidoglycan remains to be elucidated.

To answer these questions regarding peptidoglycan recognition by CadF, we determined the crystal structures of the CadFPD in complex with a peptide (CadFPDPeptide) or glycine (CadFPDGlycine). The CadFPD features a narrow pocket and uses it to accommodate the amino acid residue meso-diaminopimelic acid (m-DAP) observed in the peptidoglycan peptide but not in regular proteins, suggesting that CadF may function as a peptidoglycan-binding protein in the bacterial cell in addition to its role as an adhesin protein inside the host. We found that the CadFPD residue R268, located at the pocket base, is involved in specific interactions with m-DAP, and its potential role in pocket formation and peptide recognition was structurally examined using the R268E mutant (CadFPD−R268E). Furthermore, our comparative analysis of the CadFPD and abOmpAPD highlights the similar but distinct peptidoglycan-binding mechanisms of the CadF and abOmpA proteins.

Results and discussion

Overall structure of the CadFPD

As a first step to elucidate the structural features of CadF needed for its function, the CadFPD protein (residues 208–319) was recombinantly expressed in E. coli cells and purified to homogeneity by chromatography. The CadFPD protein was crystallized in the presence of PEG 2000 at pH 7.5. The crystal structure of the CadFPD was determined by molecular replacement using an AlphaFold2 model of the CadFPD (residues 208–319; NCBI accession number, EAJ9119466) as a search model18 and refined to an Rfree value of 23.7% using X-ray diffraction data up to a resolution of 1.80 Å (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table S1). Because this CadFPD structure was refined with a bound peptide (see below), we refer to it as the CadFPDPeptide structure. The asymmetric unit of the CadFPDPeptide crystal contains two CadFPD polypeptide chains (chains A and B), which exhibit essentially identical structures with a root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) value of 0.19 Å (Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2). Chain A has lower B-factors than chain B and will be mainly described here, unless specified otherwise.

The CadFPD from the CadFPDPeptide structure, as well as the glycine-bound CadFPD structure (see below), forms a single-domain structure, consisting of four β-strands (β1-β4) and three α-helices (α1-α3), as observed in a prototypical OmpAPD fold14 (Fig. 1). The β-strands and α-helices are sequentially arranged in an alternating pattern (β1-α1-β2-α2-β3-α3-β4). The four β-strands are organized into a β-sheet with the arrangement β1-β4-β2-β3. The α1 and α2 helices are aligned with the β-strands in the longitudinal direction, covering one face of the β-sheet primarily via hydrophobic interactions. The α1-α2 layer and the β1-β4-β2-β3 sheet together form a two-layer α/β structure as the core structure of the CadFPD, which is decorated by linking loops at the top and bottom. Notably, although the α2 helix constitutes the core structure with its C-terminal segment, it projects its N-terminal segment beyond the core structure, thereby forming the bottom of the CadFPD structure in conjunction with the β1-α1, β2-α2, and α3-β4 loops as well as the α3 helix, which extends from the β4 strand.

Accommodation of a peptidoglycan peptide by the CadFPD pocket

The CadFPDPeptide structure harbors a narrow pocket, that is ~ 10 Å long, at the bottom of its core structure (Figs. 1A and 2A and B). The pocket is generated by eleven CadF residues from the protruding α2 helix (Y260, N261, L264, and R268) and its adjacent β1-α1 loop (F218 and D219), β2-α2 loop (T252, D253, I255, and G256), and α3-β4 loop (R310) (Figs. 1 and 2). The buried portion of the pocket is composed of the residues T252, D253, N261, L264, R268, and R310, which are highly conserved across the CadF and OmpA proteins (Figs. 1B and 2A and C). Notably, the positively charged guanidino group of the R268 residue is positioned at the base of the pocket and forms positive electrostatic potential surfaces along with the R310 side chain (Fig. 2B, C). Unlike the base of the pocket, the sidewall of the pocket displays a negative electrostatic potential due to oxygen atoms from the side chains of D219, D253, and N261 and the main chains of I255 and D253 (Fig. 2B, C). The pocket rim consists of the residues F218, D219, I255, G256, and Y260 and exhibits lower sequence conservation than the buried portion does, except for G256 (Figs. 1B and 2A and C).

Interestingly, a strong electron density was identified inside the pocket of the CadFPD as an independent entity that is not covalently linked to the CadFPD polypeptide chain (Supplementary Fig. S3A). This additional density completely fills the entire space of the pocket and further extends beyond the pocket. L-lysine was initially identified as a potential ligand using the phenix.ligand_identification program20; however, it did not fit the additional electron density well. Based on the shape of the density and the surrounding chemical environment, L-lysine was modified to m-DAP, which provided an excellent fit for the density. m-DAP corresponds to the third residue in the pentapeptide portion of peptidoglycan (L-Ala-γ-D-Glu-m-DAP-D-Ala-D-Ala). Consistently, gram-negative bacteria, such as C. jejuni, E. coli, and A. baumannii, typically contain the pentapeptide L-Ala-γ-D-Glu-m-DAP-D-Ala-D-Ala in the peptidoglycan layer instead of L-Ala-γ-D-Glu-L-Lys-D-Ala-D-Ala, which is generally found in gram-positive bacteria21. Furthermore, the abOmpAPD protein, which is homologous to the CadFPD, was shown to directly interact with the pentapeptide portion of peptidoglycan (L-Ala-γ-D-Glu-m-DAP-D-Ala-D-Ala), and one of the abOmpAPD structures was determined in complex with this pentapeptide14. Based on this information and the extended shape of the additional electron density, the tripeptide m-DAP-D-Ala-D-Ala, corresponding to the three C-terminal residues of the peptidoglycan pentapeptide, was ultimately modeled into the CadFPDPeptide structure (Supplementary Fig. S3A). The CadFPD protein appears to have captured the peptidoglycan peptide during recombinant expression in E. coli cells14.

The non-proteinogenic m-DAP residue found in the gram-negative peptidoglycan consists of a side chain and a main chain as observed for regular amino acids that constitute proteins (Fig. 2A). The elongated side chain of m-DAP can be divided into a main chain-linked hydrophobic portion and a subsequent hydrophilic portion. The hydrophobic portion of the m-DAP side chain consists of three sequential methylene groups and is located in the middle of m-DAP, whereas the hydrophilic portion of the side chain is located at the distal end of m-DAP and contains a carboxyl group and an amino group. In the structure of the CadFPD in complex with a tripeptide (CadFPDPeptide), the m-DAP residue of the tripeptide occupies most of the internal space of the pocket (Fig. 2A, B). The side chain of m-DAP is deeply embedded in the CadFPD pocket, with its hydrophilic portion pointing toward the base of the CadFPD pocket. The main chain of m-DAP is located at the opening of the pocket and is linked to the D-Ala residue by a peptide bond (Fig. 2C). Therefore, the two D-Ala residues of the tripeptide are located primarily outside the pocket, but on the CadFPD surface.

The m-DAP residue is responsible for most of the interactions between the CadFPD and the tripeptide and makes contacts with each pocket residue via diverse interactions, including hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions, hydrophobic interactions, and van der Waals interactions (Fig. 2C). The distal hydrophilic tip of m-DAP plays a key role in CadFPD binding, given that it mediates nine out of the eleven total hydrogen bonds and salt bridges between CadF and m-DAP. The negatively charged carboxyl group of the m-DAP side chain contacts the positively charged R268 side chain at the pocket base via salt bridges (interatomic distances, 2.8 and 3.1 Å) and forms hydrogen bonds with the main-chain nitrogen atoms of the CadF residues D219 and D253 (interatomic distances, 2.8 and 3.1 Å, respectively). Therefore, CadF seems to be more optimized to bind to m-DAP than to L-lysine, which lacks one of the carboxyl groups of m-DAP and is typically present in the peptidoglycan of gram-positive bacteria in place of the m-DAP of gram-negative peptidoglycan21, suggesting that the CadF protein from the gram-negative bacterium C. jejuni has evolved to specifically recognize the peptidoglycan of gram-negative bacteria.

The amino group of the m-DAP side chain is deeply embedded inside the ligand-binding pocket of CadF, as observed for the carboxyl group of m-DAP, and it is hydrogen bonded to the D253 and N261 side chains (interatomic distances, 2.8 and 2.7 Å, respectively) and to the D253 and I255 main chains (interatomic distances, 3.0 and 2.8 Å, respectively) (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. S4A). In crosslinked peptidoglycan, the amino group of m-DAP is covalently linked to a D-Ala residue from another peptide to bridge two peptides in most gram-negative bacteria21. However, the amino group of m-DAP is fully covered by the CadF residues inside the pocket without extra space for other atoms, suggesting that the CadFPD preferentially interacts with non-crosslinked peptides rather than crosslinked peptides. Therefore, CadF, which is embedded in the outer membrane11, appears to contribute to cell wall integrity by anchoring the outer membrane primarily to nascent or damaged peptidoglycans, which contain non-crosslinked peptides21. Consequently, CadF may mitigate the harmful effects of penicillin- or cephalosporin-type antibiotics, which cause the accumulation of non-crosslinked peptidoglycans by inhibiting the peptidoglycan-crosslinking activity of penicillin-binding proteins22–24.

In the CadFPDPeptide structure, the remaining part of m-DAP, including a central hydrophobic portion and a main chain, also contributes to CadFPD binding although it mediates fewer interactions than the distal hydrophilic end of m-DAP (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. S4A). The hydrophobic portion of the m-DAP side chain is surrounded mainly by hydrophobic residues, including F218, I255, and Y260, thereby facilitating hydrophobic interactions with these residues. The main chain of m-DAP participates in two hydrogen bonds with the D219 side chain and the G256 main chain (interatomic distances, 3.3 and 3.4 Å, respectively) at the pocket rim. In contrast to the m-DAP residue, the two D-Ala residues of the tripeptide make limited contacts with CadFPD residues, interacting with only I255 and F218. Consequently, they appear more disordered than m-DAP, as their electron density is less well defined than that of m-DAP in the CadFPDPeptide crystal structure (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Taken together, we conclude that the CadFPD forms a narrow pocket designed to optimally accommodate peptidoglycan-specific m-DAP and thus exclusively recognizes peptidoglycan.

Interaction of the CadFPD with glycine

The recombinant CadFPD protein obtained via the E. coli expression system contained the peptidoglycan peptide, allowing us to visualize the critical interaction of CadF with peptidoglycan. To further determine the CadFPD structure without the peptidoglycan peptide, the CadFPD protein produced by the E. coli expression system was denatured for the dissociation of CadF from the peptidoglycan peptide and then refolded. The structure of the refolded CadFPD protein was obtained at a resolution of 1.75 Å (Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Table S1). This structure did not contain a peptide, as expected. Instead, an additional electron density resembling glycine was observed, and a glycine molecule was modeled into the structure of the refolded CadFPD protein at the position of m-DAP from the CadFPDPeptide structure (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. S3B). Consistently, abOmpAPD, a homolog of CadFPD, was shown to bind glycine with a Kd of 0.59 µM, and one of its crystal structures was determined in complex with a glycine molecule14. The structure of the glycine-bound CadFPD (CadFPDGlycine) is similar to the CadFPDPeptide structure, with an RMSD of 0.41 Å, and it harbors an elongated narrow pocket at the same location (Fig. 3B). In the CadFPDGlycine structure, the glycine molecule is deeply inserted into the CadFPD pocket and overlaps well with the hydrophilic distal end of m-DAP from the CadFPDPeptide structure (Fig. 3). Because the glycine molecule has its carboxyl and amino groups at the same positions as those of the m-DAP tip, it forms multiple hydrogen bonds and salt bridges with CadFPD residues at and near the base of the CadFPD pocket as observed for m-DAP in the CadFPDPeptide structure. Minor structural differences are observed only at the opening of the CadF pocket because glycine is smaller than m-DAP and thus cannot arrange its atoms near the pocket opening. The CadFPD pocket rim residues D219 and G256, which form hydrogen bonds with the main chain of m-DAP in the CadFPDPeptide structure, do not contact the glycine molecule in the CadFPDGlycine structure. As a result, the CadF D219 side chain and its neighboring F218 side chain from the β1-α1 loop exhibit positional differences at pocket opening between the CadFPDPeptide and CadFPDGlycine structures. This comparative structural analysis suggests that the CadFPD pocket is optimized to recognize m-DAP or peptidoglycan peptides rather than glycine, although it can still accommodate the glycine molecule as a pseudoligand.

Comparison of the peptidoglycan peptide-binding patterns of the CadFPD and abOmpAPD

Overall, the CadFPD structure is similar to the previously reported structures of several OmpAPD homologs [abOmpAPD, PDB ID 3TD514; S. enterica OmpAPD (seOmpAPD), PDB ID 4RHA16; B. burgdorferi OmpAPD (bbOmpAPD), PDB ID 3OON16], with RMSD values of 1.06–2.10 Å (Fig. 4A). The α1 and α2 helices and the β1-β4 strands that constitute the two-layer α/β structure of the CadFPD are generally found at similar positions in the CadFPD and OmpAPD structures. However, the seOmpAPD and bbOmpAPD exhibit local conformational differences from the CadFPD throughout their structures. For example, the α3 helix of the CadFPD is absent or located differently in the seOmpAPD and bbOmpAPD structures.

Among the OmpAPD structures, only the abOmpAPD was structurally characterized with a biological ligand, the peptidoglycan pentapeptide L-Ala-γ-D-Glu-m-DAP-D-Ala-D-Ala (Fig. 4A)14,16. The peptide-complexed abOmpAPD (abOmpAPDPeptide; PDB ID 3TD5) has a two-layer α/β structure similar to that of the CadFPDPeptide (RMSD, 1.06 Å) (Fig. 4A)14. As observed for the CadFPDPeptide structure, the abOmpAPDPeptide structure harbors a pocket that accommodates m-DAP in an orientation in which the hydrophilic tip faces the base of the pocket (Fig. 4B). The hydrophilic tip of m-DAP in the abOmpAPDPeptide structure adopts a conformation similar to that of the CadFPDPeptide structure and interacts with the pocket residues that are identical to those of CadF in terms of residue type (Fig. 4B, C).

In contrast to the m-DAP tip, the remaining portion of the m-DAP residue in the abOmpAPDPeptide structure exhibits structural deviations from that of the CadFPDPeptide structure, and its adjacent abOmpA residues at the pocket rim are generally modified to different types of residues in CadF, unlike identical residue types in the buried region of the pocket (Fig. 4B, C and Supplementary Fig. S4). In particular, the abOmpA residues T236 and T273, which contact the main chain of m-DAP, are replaced by larger residues in CadF (F218 and I255, respectively). These larger CadF residues push the m-DAP main chain to the center of the pocket opening in the CadFPDPeptide structure in comparison with the abOmpAPDPeptide structure, allowing the m-DAP main chain to be additionally hydrogen bonded to CadF D219 and G256. Accordingly, the D-Ala residues that are linked to m-DAP also shift. Furthermore, owing to the different residue types at the pocket opening between the CadFPDPeptide and abOmpAPDPeptide, the pocket opening of the CadFPD becomes narrower than that of the abOmpAPD. These structural differences imply that the CadFPD may interact with a more limited range of peptide types and permit less conformational flexibility of the bound peptide than the abOmpAPD. Taken together, we conclude that the CadFPD and abOmpAPD share the m-DAP tip-binding mechanism in the deeper region of the pocket, whereas the m-DAP main chain-binding mechanisms at pocket opening diverge between the two proteins.

Potential role of CadF R268 in m-DAP recognition and pocket formation

In the CadFPDPeptide structure, the CadF residue R268 is located at the base of the CadF pocket and specifically interacts with the carboxyl tip of m-DAP from the peptide. To investigate the critical role of the CadF residue R268 in m-DAP recognition, we mutated the positively charged R268 residue to a negatively charged glutamate residue, which has two fewer atoms than arginine does, and determined the crystal structure of the CadFPD-R268E at a resolution of 1.50 Å (Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Table S1). In the CadFPD-R268E structure, the electron density corresponding to m-DAP or its linked D-Ala residues was not observed; instead, three water molecules were detected in place of the distal tip of m-DAP, indicating that the positively charged tip of the CadF R268 residue makes a substantial contribution to accommodating m-DAP into the pocket (Fig. 5A). Notably, CadF R268 is absolutely conserved as an arginine in OmpA proteins, highlighting its critical role across the CadF and OmpA proteins (Fig. 1B). However, the absence of m-DAP in the crystal structure of CadFPD-R268E does not necessarily indicate that CadFPD-R268E is not able to bind m-DAP in solution. It is possible that CadFPD-R268E interacts with m-DAP with reduced affinity.

Interestingly, although the CadFPD-R268E adopts a two-layer α/β structure, as observed for its wild-type (WT) counterpart (CadFPDPeptide), and locates its shorter glutamate side chain at a position similar to that of the side chain stem of R268, the CadFPD-R268E exhibits substantial structural differences from the CadFPDPeptide throughout the region from the N-terminus to the β1-α1 loop (Fig. 5). Compared to the CadFPDPeptide structure, the β1-α1 loop moves up to ~ 13 Å toward the space between the α1 and α2 helices, away from the pocket region, in the CadFPD-R268E structure. This conformational change is attributed to the loss of interaction between residue R268 (mutated to Glu) and residues G217 and K220 at the β1-α1 loop. In the CadFPDPeptide structure, the guanidino group of the CadF residue R268 forms three hydrogen bonds with the carbonyl oxygen atoms of the G217 and K220 residues at the β1-α1 loop, positioning the tip of the β1-α1 loop in close proximity to m-DAP and thereby contributing to the assembly of the intact pocket observed in the CadFPDPeptide structure (Fig. 5B). However, the glutamate residue at position 268 in the CadFPD-R268E inherently lacks the capacity to form hydrogen bonds with the carbonyl oxygen atoms of G217 and K220 at the β1-α1 loop, thereby causing the β1-α1 loop to disengage from the pocket region and adopt a looser conformation that protrudes from the core structure of CadFPD. The loss of the intramolecular hydrogen bonds in the CadFPD-R268E likely renders the β1-α1 loop highly flexible in solution. However, in the crystal structure, the β1-α1 loop appears to be stabilized in a single conformation by crystal packing. The structural alteration of the CadFPD into a less compact form by the R268E mutation is supported by gel-filtration chromatography analysis of the CadFPDPeptide and CadFPD-R268E proteins. In gel-filtration chromatography, the CadFPD-R268E protein eluted earlier than the CadFPDPeptide protein, suggesting a larger hydrodynamic radius for the CadFPD-R268E (Supplementary Fig. S5). Taken together, the R268E mutation causes residue 268 to lose the intramolecular interactions and leads the β1-α1 loop to adopt a loose single conformation or highly flexible conformations, contributing to pocket disintegration and further loss of the interaction between the CadFPD and m-DAP. Therefore, the CadFPD-R268E cannot interact with m-DAP in the crystal structure for two reasons: indirectly, due to mutation-mediated pocket disintegration, and directly, due to the loss of the m-DAP-attracting capacity of residue 268.

The critical role of the interactions between residue R268 and the main-chain oxygen atoms of G217 and K220 in pocket formation is supported by the observation that the three intramolecular hydrogen bonds and the β1-α1 loop conformation are mostly conserved in OmpAPD (PDB ID 3TD514, 5VES16, and 4RHA16) and distant homologs of CadFPD, such as Pal (PDB ID 1OAP25), TagL (PDB ID 7BBA26), and MotB (PDB ID 3CYP27). In these proteins, an arginine residue at the pocket base forms hydrogen bonds with neighboring main-chain oxygen atoms at the β1-α1 loop, irrespective of ligand binding, even in apo structures. As a result, their β1-α1 loops consistently adopt a conformation identical or similar to that of the CadFPDPeptide structure, but not that of the CadFPD-R268E structure, contributing to pocket formation.

Surprisingly, in the structure of the CadFPD-R268E mutant, the β1-α1 loop withdraws the adjacent β1 strand away from its original position observed in the CadFPDPeptide structure, shifting it by two residues closer to the pocket region (Fig. 5B). Consequently, the β1-α1 loop of the CadFPD-R268E mutant is two residues longer than that of the CadFPD WT protein. Therefore, the structural rearrangement of the CadFPD mediated by the single mutation R268E can be widespread, extending from the N-terminus through the β1 strand to the β1-α1 loop along the vertical direction of the CadFPD structure.

In conclusion, the CadFPD forms a single domain, α/β structure containing a narrow pocket and seems to recognize a peptidoglycan peptide by accommodating the m-DAP residue of the peptidoglycan in the pocket. In particular, the R268 residue of CadF contributes to pocket formation in the CadFPD and facilitates m-DAP recognition at the pocket base. Based on our findings and previous reports, we propose that CadF highly likely functions in maintaining the integrity of the cell wall as an anchor protein that links the outer membrane to the peptidoglycan by embedding the CadFTD into the outer membrane and simultaneously binding to the peptidoglycan via the CadFPD.

Methods

Construction of the CadFPD protein expression plasmid

The plasmid used to overexpress the soluble protein of the CadFPD (residues 208–319; NCBI accession number, EAJ9119466) was constructed through PCR amplification, restriction enzyme digestion, and ligation (Fig. 1B). First, the DNA fragment encoding CadF residues 208–319 was amplified via PCR from the genomic DNA of C. jejuni ATCC 33291. The PCR product was digested using the restriction enzymes BamHI and SalI and ligated using T4 DNA ligase into a modified pET49b vector, which was engineered to express the CadFPD protein with an N-terminal His6 tag and a subsequent thrombin cleavage site28. The ligation product was transformed into the E. coli DH5α strain, and the cells of the transformants were selected on an LB agar plate containing kanamycin. The CadFPD expression plasmid was extracted from transformant cells, and its nucleotide sequence was verified by DNA sequencing (NCBI accession number, PX283190). The plasmid overexpressing the CadFPD−R268E was obtained via PCR-based site-directed mutagenesis (Agilent) using the WT CadFPD expression plasmid as a template.

CadFPD protein expression and purification

For recombinant CadFPDPeptide protein overexpression, the CadFPD expression plasmid was transformed into the E. coli BL21 (DE3) strain. The transformant was then grown at 37 ℃ in LB broth containing kanamycin until the optical density of the culture at 600 nm reached 0.6. The culture was supplemented with 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside for the induction of CadFPD overexpression and then grown at 18 °C for 18 h. The resulting cells were lysed by sonication in a solution containing 50 mM Tris, pH 8.0, and 200 mM sodium chloride, and the cell lysate was cleared by centrifugation. The His6-tagged CadFPD protein was purified from the supernatant of the cell lysate by Ni-NTA affinity chromatography with imidazole-mediated elution. The eluted His6-tagged CadFPD protein was dialyzed against a solution containing 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, and 150 mM sodium chloride. The His6 tag was cleaved from the CadFPD protein using thrombin. The tag-free CadFPD protein was purified using a Superdex 200 16/600 column (GE Healthcare) in a solution containing 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, and 150 mM sodium chloride. The CadFPD−R268E mutant protein was expressed and purified in a manner identical to that of the CadFPDPeptide protein.

The CadFPDGlycine protein was obtained by refolding. The supernatant of the cell lysate containing the CadFPD protein was treated with urea at a final concentration of 6 M for protein denaturation. The denatured CadFPD protein was subjected to on-column refolding in the first purification step mediated by Ni-NTA affinity chromatography. The refolded protein was eluted from the column using a urea-free imidazole solution containing 50 mM Tris, pH 8.0, and 200 mM sodium chloride. The resulting CadFPD protein was treated with thrombin and further purified via gel-filtration chromatography as described for the CadFPDPeptide protein.

Gel-filtration chromatography analysis of the CadFPD protein

Gel-filtration chromatography was performed by loading the CadFPD protein (200 µg) in 300 µl of a solution containing 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, and 150 mM sodium chloride onto a Superdex 75 10/300 column (GE Healthcare). Elution was carried out using a solution containing 20 mM Hepes, pH 7.4, and 150 mM sodium chloride, and protein elution was monitored by measuring the UV absorbance at 280 nm. Independently, 0.2, 6.5, 14, 29, and 43 kDa gel-filtration standards (CellMosaic) were loaded onto the column for reference.

Protein crystallization and X-ray diffraction

The CadFPD protein was crystallized via a sitting-drop vapor-diffusion method at 18 °C in a 1 µl drop consisting of 0.5 µl protein solution and 0.5 µl reservoir solution (22% PEG 2000 and 0.1 M Hepes, pH 7.5, for the CadFPDPeptide; 30% PEG 4000 and 0.1 M MES, pH 5.0, for the CadFPDGlycine; 20% PEG 3350 and 0.2 M ammonium sulfate for the CadFPD−R268E). The CadFPD crystal was flash-frozen at -173 °C under a nitrogen cryostream in the presence of 25% glycerol and mounted on a goniometer using a nylon loop for X-ray diffraction. X-ray diffraction data were collected at the Pohang Accelerator Laboratory, beamline 7 A, and processed using the HKL2000 program29 (Supplementary Table S1).

Structure determination and analysis

The structure of the CadFPDPeptide was determined by molecular replacement with the Phaser program30 using an AlphaFold2 model18 of the CadFPD (residues 208–319; NCBI accession number, EAJ9119466) as a search model. The final structure of the CadFPDPeptide was obtained through iterative cycles of model building and refinement using the Coot31 and Phenix.refine20 programs, respectively (Supplementary Table S1). The structures of the CadFPDGlycine and CadFPD−R268E were solved by molecular replacement with the Phaser program30 using the coordinates of the CadFPDPeptide structure as a search model. The final structures of the CadFPDGlycine and CadFPD−R268E were obtained through iterative cycles of model building and refinement using the Coot31 and Phenix.refine20 programs, respectively (Supplementary Table S1). Protein structures were superimposed using the Coot program31, and secondary structures were assigned using the DSSP program32. Structure visualization was performed using the PyMol program (https://pymol.org/).
