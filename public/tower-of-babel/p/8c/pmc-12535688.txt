Effects of phosphorus deficiency on lipid composition and photosynthesis process in Zygophyllum xanthoxylum
Authors: Xiaowei Hu, Lijing Zhang, Hua Fu, Xiangyu Ma, Jiajia Liu, Congyan Zhang, Bowen Duan, Hao Guan, Pei Wang, Qingping Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Under phosphate (Pi) limiting conditions, lipid remodeling serves as a critical mechanism for enhancing phosphorus (P) use efficiency in plants. This process also affects the photosynthetic process simultaneously, thereby influencing the accumulation of biomass. Our previous studies have proved that Zygophyllum xanthoxylum had a remarkable P remobilization capacity, and could maintain a high biomass under Pi deficiency environment. However, the specific patterns of membrane lipid remodeling and their regulatory effects on photosynthetic performance remain to be elucidated. In this study, the changes of photosynthetic parameters, chlorophyll fluorescence parameters, leaves lipid compositions, Pi content and ATPase activity of chloroplast were determined after 1D, 10D and 40D of Pi sufficient and Pi deficient treatments. We found that Pi deficiency did not cause a significant decrease in photosynthetic indices (except 40D treatment) and did not weaken the photosynthetic electron transport process. Under Pi deficiency treatment, the glyceroglycolipid content in leaves showed significant increase at 10D and 40D treatments, but the phospholipid content remained stable. The concentration of Pi and the activity of ATPase in chloroplasts at 1D and 10D treatments were significantly increased, but there was no significant difference between 40D treatment and that of the CP. The results showed that under Pi deficiency environment, Z. xanthoxylum provided structural and functional protection for electron transport process by maintaining the content stability of phospholipids and increasing the glyceroglycolipid content. In addition, more Pi was allocated to chloroplasts, enhancing ATPase activity and providing continuous and stable assimilatory power for the photosynthetic process.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07193-3.

Introduction

Phosphorus (P) is an essential element in compounds such as ATP, NADPH, nucleic acids, and phospholipids, all of which play important roles in photosynthesis [17, 48]. Therefore, even marginal P deficiency has a major impact on plant growth and development. More than 30% of the arable land worldwide requires supplemental P fertilization [37]. However, due to chemical fixation and microbial immobilization, P fertilizer is rapidly immobilized in the soil, therefore, excessive P application is usually required to ensure ample P availability to plants. But less than 20% of P fertilizer added to the soil are taken up by the crop [10]. This persistent limitation drives excessive P application in agricultural systems to ensure plant requirements are met. Paradoxically, such over-application contributes to aquatic ecosystem degradation through eutrophication of lacustrine and coastal marine environments [31]. This obviously low P use efficiency is unsustainable.

P deficiency has been demonstrated to induce a weakening of the photosynthesis process in plants, thus affecting the formation of plant biomass [25, 49]. The photosynthetic membrane around chloroplasts and thylakoids in higher plants is a lipid bilayer membrane, which provides a platform for photosynthesis [49]. The photosynthetic membranes contain four major lipids: glycerophospholipid (PG), accounted for 85% and 5–12% of the total PG and of higher plants and the total lipid of thylakoids, respectively. Monogalactosyl diglyceride (MGDG) and digalactosyl diglyceride (DGDG) is generally only present in photosynthetic membranes, accounting for ~ 50% and ~ 30% of total lipids in thylakoids, respectively. Sulphoquinovosyl diglyceride (SQDG) is usually found only in the thylakoids and accounts for ~ 5–12% of the total lipid of the thylakoids [4, 14, 24, 53, 61]. However, it should be noted that changes in lipid composition can be observed in response to alterations in the environment [42]. As much as 30% of organic P exist in the form of phospholipids in the cell membrane of well-nourished plants, constituting these as important organic P reservoirs. These organic P pools are mobilized through phospholipid hydrolysis to liberate bound-P reserves when cellular inorganic phosphorus (Pi) levels become insufficient to meet metabolic demands, thereby enhancing P use efficiency via Pi recycling [12]. At this time, the stability of the cell membrane is compromised, while plants can maintain the stability of the cell membrane by synthesizing P-free galactolipids, i.e. MGDG and DGDG [12]. However, studies have shown that those lipids are not only essential for maintaining membrane structural stability, but also they are closely bound to the pigment protein complex, which play an important role in the photosynthesis process [14, 59]. Consequently, under Pi deficiency condition, membrane lipid remodeling, that is, synthesis of MGDG, DGDG and SQDG to make up for the decrease in PG content, is very imperative to ensure the photosynthesis of plants under stress [44]. However, since PG may mediate the interaction between protein complexes in the thylakoid membrane, and it may play a special role in photosynthesis and therefore cannot be replaced totally by glyceroglycolipids [59]. Plant mutant experiments showed that the absence of MGDG, DGDG, SQDG or PG in plants would weaken or even block the electron transfer process during photosynthesis [22, 25]. However, the photosynthetic process of plants is still inhibited compared with normal conditions even when membrane lipid remodeling occurs under Pi deficiency condition [67]. Low Pi contents, in addition to affecting the lipid composition of the membrane, also affect ATPase activity, limiting the carboxylation capacity of Rubisco and RuBP regeneration, negatively affecting the rate of CO2 assimilation and therefore biomass accumulation [6, 23]; In the dark reaction stage, Pi deficiency reduces the activity of certain enzymes involved in carbon fixation, such as ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (Rubisco), this prevents the regeneration of ribulose-1,5-disphosphate (RuBP). All of the above processes ultimately affect the formation of plant biomass.

Zygophyllum xanthoxylum is a shrub that belongs to Zygophyllaceae, which is endemic to the desert areas of Central Asia, and a dominant species in the deserts of Western China [7, 68]. The available phosphorus content of its native soils is approximately 5 mg/kg, which classifies them as extremely Pi-deficient soils [36]. Previous studies have indicated that P concentration of both the leaves and roots, biomass, and root/shoot ratios in Z. xanthoxylum are not significantly altered under different soil Pi levels [21]. After the supply of sufficient Pi nutrition and then transfer to the Pi starvation environment, the biomass of Z. xanthoxylum would not be changed [20]. Z. xanthoxylum maintain a stable biomass, because it has a high capacity for Pi remobilization and does not obtain more Pi nutrients by consuming carbohydrate. This demonstrate that Z. xanthoxylum adopt an efficient P utilization strategy to sustaining high growth rates under Pi deficiency conditions [20]. Photosynthesis is the main process of biomass formation, and we proposed that the key mechanisms enabling Z. xanthoxylum to sustain a high growth rate under Pi deficiency conditions are the prioritization of Pi allocation to chloroplasts and membrane lipid remodeling, which maintain photosynthetic stability.

Material and method

Plant cultivation and sampling

Seeds of Z. xanthoxylum were collected from plants growing in the Alxa League (N: 39°08’, E: 106°01’; elevation: 1118 m) within the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, China. A voucher specimen (No. 00078287, http://www.nsii.org.cn/2017/specimen.php?id=14878833), identified by Feng Hai, is deposited at the Inner Mongolia Agricultural University. No specific permission was required for the experimental use of these materials. For the experiments we selected seeds that were uniform and full, without defects. The seeds were surface sterilized for 1 min with a 5% (v/v) NaClO and rinsed five times with sterile water, soaked in sterile water at 4◦C for one day, and then germinated at 25◦C in the dark for 2 days. After 4-day germination, seedlings of uniform size and health were transplanted into pots (10 × 10 cm), which were filled with coarse quartz sand and irrigated with a modified ½ strength Hoagland nutrient solution that contained 0.5 mM KH2PO4, 2 mM KNO3, 0.5 mM Ca(NO3)2·4H2O, 0.5 mM MgSO4·7H2O, 0.5 mM CaCl2, 50 µM H3BO3, 0.04 mM EDTA-Fe, 1.6 µM ZnSO4·7H2O, 10 µM MnCl2·4H2O, 0.05 µM Na2MO4·2H2O, and 0.1 µM CuSO4. After 4 weeks, all seedlings were treated as follows: CP (control P level): seedlings were irrigated with modified ½ strength Hoagland nutrient solutions (500 µM P), this is the optimal P level for Z. xanthoxylum [20]; -P: seedlings were treated with modified ½ strength Hoagland nutrient solutions without P (0 µM P), the P was added as KH2PO4. During the experiments, the solutions were renewed every 2 days and the pH of the nutrient solutions were adjusted to 5.8 ± 0.2. The seedlings were grown in a greenhouse at a temperature of 28 ± 3◦C, with a photoperiod of 16 h/d (we used the artificial light with an illumination intensity of ∼10,000 lx.), and relative humidity of ∼30%. Plants were cultured for 1 day (1D), 10 days (10D) and 40 days (40D) before harvesting roots, stems, and leaves. A selection of them were oven-dried to constant weight for biomass and P concentration, another set of them were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent physiological analyses. All parameters had five biological replicates.

Leaf area measurement

Leaf area was measured using an Epson 1680 scanner at 200 dpi and was analysed using the routine procedure in the WinRhizo version 4.1c scanner program [45].

Total phosphorus concentration

The total plant P concentration (% of dry mass) were measured following digestion in a di-acid mixture at an 8:1:1 ratio (HNO3:HClO4:H2SO4) employing standard methods [2].

Lipid extraction and lipid standards

Lipid extraction and Lipid Standards – The method was modified in reference to Welti [63], as follows: the leaves for each sample were cut at the sampling time and transferred to 3 ml of isopropanol with 0.01% butylated hydroxytoluene at 75 °C. After 15 min, 1.5 ml of chloroform and 0.15 ml of water were added. The tubes were shaken for 1 h, followed by centrifuge at 4000 rpm for 10 min, and then transfered the upper level extract to the new tube. The chloroform/methanol (2:1) was added in the new tube with 30 min of agitation each time, until all of the remaining plant tissue appeared white. The remaining plant tissue was heated overnight at 105 °C and weighed. The weights of these dried, extracted tissues are the “dry weights” of the plants. The combined extracts were washed once with 1 ml of 1 M KCl and once with 2 ml of water, followed by centrifuge at 4000 rpm for 10 min, and discarded the upper level. The solvent was evaporated under nitrogen, and the lipid extract was dissolved in 1 ml of chloroform. Phospholipid standards were purchased from Kansas Lipidomics Research Center.

ESI–MS/MS– An automated ESI–MS/MS approach was used and data acquisition and analysis were performed as described previously [63]. Lipid profiling was conducted using an Orbitrap Exploris 480 (Thermo Scientific) equipped with electrospray ionization. From 1 ml of plant lipid extract, two aliquots were taken for mass spectrometry analysis: one sample for phospholipid analysis and one for galactolipid analysis. For phospholipid analysis, 150 μl of plant lipid extract in chloroform was combined with solvents and internal standards, such that the ratio of chloroform/methanol/ammonium acetate (300 mM) in water was 300:665:35, the final volume was 1.8 ml, and the sample contained di14:0-PC (6.6 nmol), di24:1-PC (6.6 nmol), 13:0-lysoPC (6.6 nmol), 19:0-lysoPC(6.6 nmol), di14:0-PE(3.6 nmol), di24:1-PE (3.6 nmol), 14:0-lysoPE (3.6 nmol), 18:0-lysoPE(3.6 nmol), di14:0-PG(3.6 nmol), 3.6 nmol of di24:1-PG(3.6 nmol), 14:0-lysoPG(3.6 nmol), 3.6 nmol of 18:0-lysoPG(3.6 nmol), di14:0-PA (3.6 nmol), di20:0 (phytanoyl)-PA (3.6 nmol), di14:0-PS (2.4 nmol), di20: 0(phytanoyl)-PS (2.4 nmol), 16:0–18:0-PI (1.97 nmol), and di18:0-PI (1.63 nmol). For galactolipid analysis, 80 l of plant lipid extract in chloroform was combined with solvent and internal standards, such that the ratio of chloroform/methanol/sodium acetate (50 mM) in water was 300:665:35, the final volume was 0.8 ml, and the sample contained MGDG isomers (16:0–18:0 at 44.55 nmol,di18:0 at 35.45 nmol) and DGDG analogs (16:0–18:0 at 8.65 nmol; di18:0 at 31.28 nmol). The source temperature was 100 °C, the desolvation temperature was 250 °C, 2.8 kV was applied to the electrospray capillary, the cone energy was 40 V.

Acyl Group Identification–The acyl groups of the major phospholipid species in a lipid extract were identified as acyl anions from the appropriate negative ion precursors. The collision energies were 20–55 V. The solvent was chloroform/methanol/ammonium acetate (300 mM) in water (300:665:35). PG, PI, PE, lysoPE, and PA were analyzed as [M-H]− ions, MGDG and DGDG were analyzed as [M-H]− and [M + acetate]− ions, and PC and lysoPC were analyzed as[M + acetate]− ions [63].

Data Analysis–Data processing was performed using Masslynx software. In each spectrum, the background was subtracted, the data was smoothed, and then peak height was determined with the centroiding routine. Identification of the peaks of interest and calculation of lipid species amounts were performed using Microsoft Excel. Corrections for overlap of isotopic variants (A + 2 peaks) in higher mass lipids were applied.

Photosynthetic capacity

Photosynthetic gas exchange parameters: A, Net CO2 assimilation rate (µmol CO2 m−2 s−1); E, Transpiration rate (mmol H2O m−2 s−1); Gs, Stomatal conductance (mmol m−2 s−1); Ci, Intercellular CO2 concentration (ppm) were measured using GFS-3000 gas- exchange system (WALZ, Germany) at 9:00–11:00 a.m. The leaves growing in the middle and upper part of the plants were selected for living determination, 3 leaves were determined for each plant.

Chlorophyll fluorescence index: Maximum efficiency of PSII photochemistry (Fv/Fm), effective PSII quantum yield (Y(II)), Photochemical quenching coefficient (qP), Non-photochemical quenching coefficient (NPQ) and Photosynthetic eletron transport rate (rETR) were measured and calculated using MINI-PAM2500 (WALZ, Germany). Determination method refered to Lu [34], follow as: the minimum fluorescence level (Fo), corresponding to all Photosystem II (PSII) reaction centers being open, was measured using a low-intensity modulated light. Subsequently, a high-intensity saturating light pulse was applied to dark-adapted leaves to induce closure of all PSII reaction centers and determine the maximum fluorescence level (Fm). Following this, leaves underwent light-adapted, upon reaching a steady-state fluorescence level (Fs), a second saturating pulse was administered to establish the maximum fluorescence level under light-adapted conditions (Fm'). The actinic light was removed and the minimal fluorescence level in the light-adapted state (Fo’) was determined by illuminating the leaf disc with 3 s far-red light. By using fluorescence parameters determined in both light- and dark-adapted leaves, calculations were made of: (1) Fv/Fm = (Fm-Fo)/Fm; (2) Y(II) = (Fm’-Fs)/Fm’; (3) rETR = PAR × Y(II) × 0.84 × 0.5; (4) qP = 1-(Fs-Fo)/(Fm’-Fo’); (5) NPQ = (Fm-Fm’)/Fm’. The leaves growing in the middle and upper part of the plants were selected for living determination, 3 leaves were determined for each plant.

Chloroplast Pi concentration and ATPase activity

The leaves from plants subjected to photosynthesis measurement were subsequently harvested in their entirety for determination of chloroplast Pi concentration and ATPase activity.

Chloroplast extraction [6]: Plants were adapted in the light at growth conditions for 4 h prior to isolation. Subsequent manipulations were maintained at 4 °C. The samples were segmented into 3–4 cm fragments and immediately immersed in 200 mL of ice-cold HS buffer (50 mM HEPES and 330 mM sorbitol, adjusted to pH 8 with KOH pellets) and rapidly blended with three quick bursts. The resultant slurry underwent primary clarification through dual-layered 22–25 μm porosity filtration membranes (Merck Miracloth) followed by differential centrifugation at 3,300 × g (2 min). The supernatant was carefully discarded, and the tubes were rolled on ice to loosen the pellets. Then, 1 mL of HS buffer was added, and the suspension was layered onto a precooled Percoll pad containing 2 mL of 53 HS buffer, 3.5 mL of Percoll, and 4.5 mL of Milli-Q water and then centrifuged at 1,400 g for 8 min in a swing rotor. The green middle layer (containing thylakoids and broken chloroplasts) and the Percoll were removed carefully. The intact chloroplast fraction was subsequently washed through sequential resuspension in 10 mL HS medium and centrifuged at 3,000 g for 2 min in a swing rotor. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was suspended in 700 mL of HS buffer.

Determining of Chloroplast Pi concentration by referring to the instructions of Pi assay kit (Suzhou comi, China); Determining of chloroplast ATPase activity by referring to the instructions of the instructions of the chloroplast ATPase activity assay kit (FHTE-1-Y, Suzhou comi, China).

Quantification analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Based on published transcriptome sequencing data [20], gene expression differences were analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as means ± SE based on five independent biological replicates for each sample. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 20 software and SigmaPlot 12.5. The data between CP and -P treatment at the same time point was assessed using a t-test, at P < 0.05.

Differential expression pathway analysis under Pi deficiency treatments

In the photosynthetic pathway, Pi-deficient conditions induce 43 differential expression genes (DEG) following 1D and 10D treatments, of which 40 DEGs exhibited significant upregulation. Only 1 and 2 DEGs exhibit down-regulation at 1D and 10D treatments, respectively (Fig. 4). Under 1D treatment, the upregulated genes comprise (i) encoding PsbC, PsbO, PsbP, PsbS, PsbW, PsbY, and Psb27 proteins, which components of photosynthetic complexes in the PSII complex; (ii) encoding PetC protein in the cytochrome b6/f complex (Cyt b6/f); (iii) encoding PsaA, PsaC, PsaD, PsaE, PsaF, PsaG, PsaH, PsaK, PsaL, PsaN, and PsaO proteins in the PSI complex; (iv) encoding PetE, PetF (isoforms b, c, d), PetH, and PetJ proteins involve in photosynthetic electron transport; (v) encoding F-type H⁺-transporting ATPase subunit (F-type H+-transporting ATPase subunit gamma and F-type H+-transporting ATPase subunit delta). Discussion

Pi deficiency had no significant effect on growth of Zgophyllum xanthoxylumy

Pi deficiency is a major factor limiting plant growth and crop productivity [51]. While Pi deficiency typically inhibits shoot growth more severely than root growth, resulting in an increased root-to-shoot ratio as a common adaptive response [35, 41], this adaptive response helps plants expand their phosphorus foraging reach in nutrient-barren environments [47]. This study uncovered a remarkable phenomenon in Z. xanthoxylum: despite we prolonge Pi deprivation for 40 days (with tissue P concentration declining to < 2 mg/g), neither plant height nor biomass shows significant differences compared to the corresponding CP (Table 1). This observation demonstrate the absence of significant growth inhibition despite severe Pi deficiency conditions. Concurrently, the root/shoot ratio exhibites no significant alteration compared to the CP, the response mode diverges from previous reports on other species under Pi limitation, but consistent with previous findings in Z. xanthoxylum [20, 21]. Furthermore, maintaining photosynthetic capacity is the key to avoiding growth inhibition. Pi deficiency restricts leaf expansive growth by reducting in leaf area and decelerating of growth rate. These morphological changes diminish the photosynthetic area, thereby impairing photosynthetic capacity and ultimately reducing plant productivity and crop yield [9, 55]. In this study, Z. xanthoxylum could maintain unaffected leaf area across treatments (except at 40D, Table 1). This critical physiological trait likely enables it to maintain relatively high biomass accumulation under Pi stress. Zygophyllum xanthoxylum might ensure normal leaf development and photosynthetic function in low-Pi environments through highly efficient P acquisition and recycling mechanisms. Notably, a well-established link exists between plant nutritional status and resistance to herbivory, as nutrient elements significantly influence insect host selection [30]. For instance, Fall armyworm (FAW) (Spodoptera frugiperda) preferentially selected host plants with high P:C ratios [60]. Crucially, the physiological characteristics of Z. xanthoxylum demonstrated here—specifically maintaining high biomass despite extremely low internal P concentration—result in tissues with low P:C ratios. This nutritional profile may theoretically confer significant ecological advantages by reducing attractiveness to P-preferring insects, rendering it a suboptimal host. Consequently, the capacity of Z. xanthoxylum to endure extreme Pi deficiency without compromising growth not only provides competitive advantages in Pi limite habitats but might also indirectly enhance resistance to herbivorous insects, representing an integrated adaptive strategy to dual environmental constraints.

Zygophyllum xanthoxylum can still maintain stable photosynthesis under Pi deficiency treatment

Photosynthesis is the ultimate source of plant biomass production [33, 69], this process largely relies on P-containing compounds [57]. Pi plays a pivotal role in chloroplast structure and photosynthetic processes, directly participating in photophosphorylation and carbon assimilation during photosynthesis [3]. Consequently, photosynthesis is particularly sensitive to Pi deficiency. Previous studies have demonstrated that Pi deprivation reduces the net photosynthetic rate [9, 32], which is primarily attributed to non-stomatal limitation by reducing stomatal conductance, increasing or maintaining intercellular CO2 concentration stable [9, 67]. In this study, however, Pi deficiency for 1D and 10D treatments does not induce A reduction in Z. xanthoxylum, which contrasts with conventional observations. Notably, when subjected to Pi deficiency for 40D treatment, Z. xanthoxylum exhibite decreased A, increased Gs and unchanged Ci (Table 2), the result indicates that the photosynthetic inhibition under long-term Pi stress is still mediated through non-stomatal factors. This pattern suggests a unique physiological regulation in Z. xanthoxylum to mitigate photosynthetic impairment during Pi limitation. Existing studies have demonstrated that Pi deficiency typically impairs photosynthetic performance through the following mechanisms:Impacting on the energy transduction system of thylakoids. Low Pi stress specifically damages the PSII photochemical process, manifesting by reduced actual quantum yield and decreased proportion of open reaction centers in PSII. This impairment subsequently decelerates photosynthetic electron transport rates, leading to excessive light energy dissipation through thermal pathways. These physiological alterations are quantitatively reflected in decreased photosynthetic parameters including Fv/Fm, rETR, qP, and Y(II), and increased NPQ [19, 52, 58, 64]. However, in this study, there are no significant differences in Fv/Fm values between Pi stress treaments and the CP (Table 2), demonstrating that Pi deficiency do not cause structural damage to PSII reaction centers. Meanwhile, both the Y(II) and rETR exhibite significantly increased under Pi deficiency environment (Table 2, except for the 40D treatment), indicating that the Pi deficiency treatments enhance the actual quantum yield and open ratio of the PSII reaction center, and increase the electron transport rate. These physiological observations align with transcriptomic evidence show upregulation of genes associated with the photosynthetic electron transport chain during 1D and 10D treatments (Fig. 4). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the energy transduction processes in thylakoid membranes remain unimpaired under Pi deficiency conditions in this species.The decrease of intracellular Pi concentration leads to the reduction of ATPase activity in thylakoid membranes. Pi deficiency normally diminishes ATP synthase activity through substrate limitation (Pi depletion in chloroplasts), consequently reducing ATP biosynthesis and ultimately constraining carbon assimilation processes [17]. In Z. xanthoxylum, however, chloroplast Pi concentration under 1D and 10D treatments are significantly higher than those in CP, concomitant with upregulated expression of ATPase subunit-encoding genes and enhance ATPase activity (Figs. 2 and 4). The triose-phosphate/phosphate translocator (TPT) mediates bidirectional transport, exporting photoassimilated carbon from chloroplasts to the cytosol and importing cytosolic Pi into the chloroplast stroma [27]. Previous study of transcriptomic analyse revealed distinct TPT upregulation during 1D and 10D treatments in Z. xanthoxylum [20], facilitating efficient photoassimilate export and maintaining continuous Pi influx into chloroplasts, which is consistented with chloroplast Pi accumulation in this study. While impaired ATP synthase activity has been recognized as a critical factor in thylakoid energy transduction failure (attributing to diminished proton flux from thylakoid lumen to stroma and then causing lumen acidification) [6], our finding suggests that preferential Pi allocation to chloroplasts is a key adaptive strategy to preserve thylakoid energy transduction efficiency under Pi stress condition in Z. xanthoxylum.Inhibiting the activity of enzymes in the Calvin cycle. Pi limitation typically suppresses photosynthetic carbon assimilation by inhibiting Calvin cycle enzymes, primarily through reduced Rubisco content/activity and impaired RuBP regeneration [9, 29]. While these specific parameters are not assessed in our study, we find that during 1D and 10D treatments, significant increases are observed in Y(II), rETR, chloroplast Pi concentration and ATPase activity compared to that of CP (Table 3, Fig. 3), but there is no significant difference between the net photosynthetic rate and that of CP (Table 2). Under 40D treatment, there are no significant differences in Y(II), Fv/Fm, chloroplast Pi concentration and ATPase activity compared with that of CP, indicating that the energy transduction process of the thylakoid and ATPase activity are not limited by Pi deficiency. But the net photosynthetic rate is significantly reduced (Table 2), this divergence suggests that Pi deficiency primarily limites Calvin cycle functionality rather than thylakoid energy transduction. Intriguingly, the observed photosynthetic rate reduction at 40D does not correspond to measurable growth inhibition (Fig. 1, Table 1).

Phylogenetic divergence in P allocation strategies reveals distinct evolutionary adaptations to nutrient limitations. Generally, monocots preferentially distribute Pi to mesophyll cells, while in eudicots, they preferentially distribute Pi to epidermal cells [18]. However, Proteaceae, which is the eudicot from severely Pi-deficient habitat, has the same P distribution pattern as monocot. This evolutionarily selected trait enables Proteaceae to maintain exceptionally high PPUE, constituting a key adaptation to chronically Pi-deficient soils [18, 46]. Chloroplasts, the core sites of photosynthesis, are rich in P-containing compounds. Maintaining relative structural and functional integrity of chloroplasts is a crucial strategy for plant survival under stress [50]. The results demonstrated that under Pi deficiency (1D and 10D treatments), Z. xanthoxylum prioritized the allocation of P to chloroplasts (Fig. 3). Compared wurh the CP, photosynthetic parameters showed no significant changes (Table 2), indicating that this allocation strategy was a key mechanism enabling Z. xanthoxylum to maintain relatively stable photosynthesis during the early stages of Pi deficiency. However, as the stress duration extended to 40D, this preferential allocation to chloroplasts ceased, and photosynthetic parameters subsequently declined. We speculate that this occurred due to the progressive depletion of internal P reserves, triggering an adaptive adjustment shift in Z. xanthoxylum towards a P remobilization strategy. Furthermore, the natural habitat of Z. xanthoxylum is a Pi-deficient environment, such selective chloroplast-targeted Pi allocation might constitute an evolutionarily optimized survival strategy, which effectively utilizes the scarce soil Pi reserves through metabolic priority of photosynthetic mechanisms.

Pi deficiency-induced membrane lipid remodeling of Zygophyllum xanthoxylum: elevated glyceroglycolipid and stable phospholipid content

Phospholipids constitute approximately one-third of plant organic phosphorus reserves. The plant maintain a stable phospholipid content is very important for the normal growth [13]. Membrane lipid remodeling emerges as a crucial adaptive mechanism to Pi limitation [1], primarily involving two biochemical processes:Promoting the metabolism of phospholipids to release Pi bound. The Pi remobilization phase is mediated by Pi deficiency-induced PLD, PLC, PAP. PLD/PLC catalyzes the hydrolysis of phospholipids to generate PA/DAG, PA is subsequently dephosphorylated by PAP to yield DAG [43]. However, in this study, phospholipid content remains stable across different Pi treatments (Fig. 3), while the gene expression levels of PLD and PLC are downregulated and PAP is upregulated (Fig. 5). Notably, the generation of PA is not strictly dependent on the PLD/PLC pathways. Instead, PAP likely functions by utilizing PA derived from alternative sources. For instance, PA generated by through the enzymatic reactions of glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase with glycerol-3-phosphate can provide PAP with sufficient substrate [56]. These result indicates that Pi deficiency does not enhance phospholipid metabolism in Z. xanthoxylum, yet still promotes DAG production. Additionally, previous studies have demonstrated that under Pi-limited conditions, PLD and PLC are not only involved in membrane lipid remodeling but also regulate root architecture [11]. This regulatory mechanism may be attributed to PA, which participates in modulating primary root elongation and root hair density [28, 54]. Under Pi-limited conditions, the root morphology of Z. xanthoxylum remains unchanged [62], and we speculated that this phenomenon might be associated with the expression patterns of PLD and PLC.Increasing the content of glyceroglycolipid. Under Pi stress conditions, plants compensate for the reduction in phospholipids by enhancing the synthesis of MGDG and DGDG. Concurrently, the decrease in PG content disrupts the anionic balance of membrane lipids, necessitating an elevation in SQDG to maintain intracellular anionic homeostasis [5, 40, 67]. Key enzymes MGD, DGD and SQD1 play important roles in this way [5, 40]. In this study, under Pi-limited conditions, the key genes involvd in glyceroglycolipids biosynthesis are upregulated (Fig. 5), accompanied by a significant increased in MGDG, DGDG, and SQDG content, consistent with previously reported findings. Collectively, Z. xanthoxylum exhibite a unique membrane lipid remodeling strategy characterized by increased glyceroglycolipid levels and maintained phospholipid content stability. We hypothesized that even without enhanced phospholipid turnover, the accelerated conversion of PA to DAG provided abundant substrates for the biosynthesis of glyceroglycolipids.

Additionally, unsaturated fatty acids (UFA) in cellular membranes play critical roles in photosynthetic functionality. For instance, fad2/fad6 double mutant of Arabidopsis thaliana, which exhibits significantly reduced membrane UFAs, shows a marked decline in chlorophyll content, impairment of photosynthetic complexes, and an inability to sustain autotrophic growth [39]. In this study, lipid profiling reveals that under 10D and 40D treatments, the most prominently increased fatty acid species are 36:6-MGDG, 36:6-DGDG, and 34:3-SQDG (Table S1- S2), all of which are highly unsaturated forms. These findings suggest that the elevated glyceroglycolipids under Pi-limited conditions in Z. xanthoxylu are predominantly enriched in UFAs. We proposed that this UFA-rich glyceroglycolipid remodeling might crucially support photosynthetic electron transport maintenance under low Pi stress.

The relationship between the lipid composition and photosynthesis

Membrane lipid remodeling not only facilitates the releasing and recycling of intracellular Pi but also critically regulates photosynthetic processes. PG, MGDG, DGDG and SQDG serve dual roles: they constitute the lipid bilayer matrix of photosynthetic membranes and participate in photosynthesis at multiple levels [14]. Notably, PG is the only major phospholipid exclusively localized to thylakoid membranes. Studies have demonstrated that treating thylakoid membranes with PLA or PLC degrads 70% and 50% of PG, respectively, and nearly abolishes photosynthetic electron transport in PSII. Furthermore, the digestion of PG might disrupt the binding of extrinsic proteins to PSII dimers, leading to monomer accumulation [26]. Therefore, PG plays an important role in the structure and function of thylakoid membranes [16, 59]. Studies on A. thaliana galactolipid-deficient mutants reveale that reduced levels of MGDG or DGDG lead to decrease chlorophyll content, impaired photosynthetic activity, altered chloroplast ultrastructure, and inhibited cell growth, manifesting as stunted growth, reduced chlorophyll accumulation, and compromised photosynthetic capacity [14, 25]. The mgd1 mutant demonstrates that DGDG is essential for maintaining the structural integrity of the PSII donor side, facilitating light-harvesting complex II trimer assembly. DGDG deficiency also reduces chlorophyll-protein complexes associated with PSI and restricts intersystem electron transport [22]. In MGD-overexpressing plants, the chlorophyll content and photosynthetic rate were significantly higher than in wild-type plants under low-Pi conditions, it demonstrates that enhanced glyceroglycolipid biosynthesis can effectively protect the function of photosynthetic machinery, thereby aiding plant survival under Pi deficiency [66]. Partial SQDG-deficient mutant exhibites no significant impact on photosynthetic performance [15], while complete SQDG loss results in a slight decline in the effective quantum yield of PSII [65]. However, under Pi stress conditions, SQDG partially replaces PG as an anionic lipid, maintaining total anionic lipid content and thereby preserving photosynthetic functionality [14]. Pi deficiency typically triggers membrane lipid remodeling in plants, characterized by increased non-phospholipids (MGDG, DGDG and SQDG) to supplement the reduction of phospholipids [1, 8, 40]. However, under Pi deficiency environmtents, even if glyceroglycolipids increase to compensate for the membrane lipid remodeling caused by the reduction of phospholipids, photosynthetic processes are still inhibited compared to normal conditions. Notably, low-Pi-tolerant cultivars exhibite greater glyceroglycolipid accumulation, less phospholipid depletion, and milder photosynthetic inhibition than low-Pi-sensitive cultivars [67]. In this study, significant alterations in membrane lipid composition are observed under Pi-limited conditions. The content of DGDGs MGDG and SQDG are markedly higher in Pi-deficient plants than the corresponding CP (except 1D treatment), while PG levels remain stable. Correspondingly, the expression of key genes involved in glyceroglycolipids biosynthesis (MGD, DGD and SQD) are upregulated, whereas genes associated with phospholipid degradation (PLD, PLC and PLA) are downregulated (Fig. 5). The photosynthetic electron transport process is also adjusted, with Y(II) and rETR enhanced (except under 40D treatment), and the gene expression levels involved in encoding the Chl protein complex related to PSI and PSII are upregulated (Fig. 4). This adaptive response diverges from the typical photosynthetic suppression are observed in most plant species under Pi stress conditions. We speculated that the increased total lipid content – particularly the increased glyceroglycolipid levels without PG depletion – enhances structural and functional support for photosynthetic electron transport. This enhancement facilitates efficiency of photosynthetic electron transport chain and ultimately preventing photosynthetic inhibition (Table 2). Therefore, the maintenance of stable phospholipid levels combined with enhanced glyceroglycolipids synthesis constitutes one of the key mechanisms underlying Z. xanthoxylum sustained biomass production under Pi stress conditions. While membrane lipid remodeling to release Pi for redistribution and reuse is a common Pi-saving mechanism in plants, Z. xanthoxylum appeares to bypass phospholipid degradation for Pi mobilization. We speculated that it has other Pi release mechanisms to achieve efficient reuse of Pi.

Conclusions

The sustained biomass production of Z. xanthoxylum under Pi-limited conditions could be attributed to two key adaptive mechanisms: 1) maintenance of stable phospholipid levels coupled with enhanced glyceroglycolipid synthesis, which ensures structural integrity and functional optimization of photosynthetic electron transport systems; and 2) preferential Pi reallocation to chloroplasts, where elevated ATP synthase activity provides generates sustained assimilatory power (ATP and NADPH) to drive carbon fixation processes.
