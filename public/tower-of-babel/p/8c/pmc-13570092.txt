Oral antibiotics prescribing patterns and associated diarrhea risk: Public health implications
Authors: Zorica Naumovska, Emilija Atanasovska, Beti Djurdjic, Kristina Mladenovska, Tanja Petreska Ivanovska, Nikola Geskovski, Katerina Anceska Netkovska, Katerina Goracinova, Renata Slaveska Raichki, Dushko Shalabalija, Ljubica Mihailova, Teodora Tasevska, Lina Livrinska Trpeska, Maja Simonoska Crcarevska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInappropriate antibiotic use contributes to antimicrobial resistance and adverse drug reactions (ADR), including antibiotic-associated diarrhea (AAD), underscoring the importance of WHO AWaRe recommendations for rational prescribing. Clinical evidence supports the use of specific probiotic strains to reduce AAD risk, with strain-specific recommendations that should be adapted to regional contexts.ObjectivesTo assess oral antibiotic consumption in the Republic of North Macedonia (2011–2022), evaluate prescribing patterns according to the WHO AWaRe classification, identify antibiotics associated with a higher burden of AAD, and determine the availability of evidence-supported probiotics for AAD prevention.DesignObservational pharmaco-epidemiological study integrating antibiotic utilization data, pharmacovigilance signals, and evidence synthesis from systematic reviews and market analysis.MethodsReimbursed oral antibiotic data (ATC J01) were obtained from the National Health Insurance Fund. Gastrointestinal ADR reports were extracted from EudraVigilance and FAERS. Diarrhea risk was estimated using SmPC frequency categories and normalized pharmacovigilance indicators. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses were used to identify probiotic strains with evidence for AAD prevention, and online pharmacy surveys assessed market availability.ResultsPenicillins and cephalosporins predominated in antibiotic use. Amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, cefixime, and clindamycin ranked highest for diarrhea risk based on combined utilization and pharmacovigilance indicators. Saccharomyces boulardii and Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG showed the strongest evidence for AAD prevention. Considerable variability was observed in probiotic product composition and reporting across the market.ConclusionFindings highlight antibiotics with higher AAD-related public health burden and support antimicrobial stewardship strategies, including evidence-based probiotic co-administration.

1. Introduction

Antibiotics remain among the most frequently prescribed medicines worldwide, yet their misuse and overuse have contributed to the escalating threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). This global issue has significant clinical, public health, and economic implications, leading the World Health Organization (WHO) to introduce the AWaRe classification (Access, Watch, Reserve) of antibiotics in 2017 as a stewardship tool to monitor and assess their use. The AWaRe framework promotes preferential use of “Access” antibiotics, agents with narrower spectrum, lower resistance potential, and are more affordable, while discouraging unnecessary use of “Watch” and “Reserve” antibiotics that are associated with higher resistance risk and cost. In 2024, the United Nations Political Declaration on AMR reaffirmed the WHO goal that at least 70% of total antibiotic consumption should derive from the “Access” group by 2030, as part of global efforts to preserve antimicrobial efficacy and ensure equitable access.1,2 Despite these initiatives, progress remains inconsistent, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The WHO Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System (GLASS) 1 reported that in 2022, “Access” antibiotics accounted for only 52.7% of global consumption, while “Watch” antibiotics comprised 45.3%, with several LMICs showing excessive reliance on broad-spectrum agents.

Data on antibiotic consumption from the Republic of North Macedonia remain limited.3,4 In an Evidence brief for policy issued by the WHO in 2020, it is stated that precise data on total antibiotic consumption (in particular self-medication and hospital consumption) for the country are lacking, however, based on experience, it is estimated that the total consumption of antibiotics is up to 35 defined daily doses (DDDs) per 1000 inhabitants per day, which is much higher than the EU and EEA population weighted mean consumption of 21.9 DDDs per 1000 inhabitants per day. 5 Several previous reports have described general antibiotic utilization trends, but no systematic long-term evaluation of oral antibiotic prescribing has been conducted using the AWaRe framework. Understanding national prescribing practices is essential not only to inform stewardship and policy but also to assess broader consequences related to adverse drug reactions (ADRs) and healthcare costs. The inappropriate or excessive use of broad-spectrum antibiotics is associated with higher rates of ADRs, including antibiotic-associated diarrhea (AAD), which represents one of the most common treatment-related complications6,7 posing significant health burdens ranging from mild, self-limiting illness to severe, life-threatening complications like Clostridium difficile infection that can prolong hospital stay, increase mortality, and impose substantial economic costs on healthcare systems.8–10 Moreover, antibiotic misuse can cause long-lasting disturbances in the intestinal microbiota, increasing patients’ susceptibility to subsequent infections and other diseases. 11

Recent publications suggest that aligning national prescribing patterns with the AWaRe classification can have dual benefits in both reducing antimicrobial resistance and optimizing healthcare resource utilization. 12 However, achieving these outcomes requires detailed insight into local antibiotic prescribing practices, their alignment with AWaRe categories, and the associated risk of ADRs. Moreover, although the co-administration of probiotics or their derivatives has been shown to reduce the risk of AAD, these preventive strategies remain underused and inconsistently incorporated into clinical practice. 13

The present study provides the first systematic assessment of oral antibiotic consumption in the Republic of North Macedonia across 12-year period (2011–2022), with detailed analysis of the most recent five years (2018–2022) according to the WHO AWaRe classification. By integrating data from the National Health Insurance Fund (NHIF) with pharmacovigilance indicators and known ADR profiles, this study aimed to: (1) characterize prescribing patterns in the context of regional and global trends; (2) evaluate the balance between “Access” and “Watch” antibiotics; (3) identify antibiotics most associated with AAD risk, thereby informing evidence-based preventive measures; and (4) identify probiotic strains with evidence supporting AAD prevention and assess their availability in the national community pharmacy market.

2. Methods

The reporting of this study was prepared in accordance with the RECORD-PE statement for pharmaco-epidemiological studies using routinely collected health data. 14 The completed RECORD-PE checklist is provided as supplementary file.

2.1. Antibiotic consumption

Antibiotic consumption data (ATC J01) were obtained from the NHIF website (https://fzo.org.mk/potroshuvachka-na-lekovi, accessed August 17, 2025) for 2011–2022. Total prescriptions and expenditures for all oral dosage forms, identified by international nonproprietary names (INN), were collected. Aggregate values were calculated to analyze trends and correlate with adverse events.

2.2. Antibiotic-associated adverse drug reactions

Data on reported adverse drug reactions (ADRs) associated with each antibiotic, including those specifically related to the gastrointestinal (GI) tract and diarrhea, were retrieved from: EudraVigilance, the European database for suspected adverse drug reaction reports, 15 and FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS), the U.S. Food and Drug Administration pharmacovigilance database. 16 The Reporting Odds Ratio (ROR) was calculated as a measure of disproportionality. This represents the odds of reporting a specific ADR (e.g., GI disorder or diarrhea) for a given antibiotic compared to all other antibiotics analyzed. For each antibiotic, the following parameters were determined: total number of reported ADRs, number of GI related ADRs, number of reports involving diarrhea, number of other ADRs (excluding GI and diarrhea).

Based on these data, 2×2 contingency tables were constructed as follows:a = number of reports for the specific ADR (GI or diarrhea) for the antibiotic of interest;b = number of other ADRs for the same antibiotic;c = number of reports for the same ADR for all other antibiotics;d = number of other ADRs for all other antibiotics.

This analysis allowed comparison of the relative risk of GI and diarrhea ADRs among different antibiotics, independent of their total number of reports.

2.3. Estimation of diarrhea risk based on SmPC data

To assess the potential of different antibiotics to cause diarrhea, frequency data were extracted from the Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPC) for each antibiotic available in the National Drug Register of the Republic of North Macedonia (https://lekovi.zdravstvo.gov.mk/ last accessed August 22, 2025). Antibiotics were classified according to the standard frequency categories for adverse reactions: Very common: >1/10; Common: ≥1/100 to <1/10; Uncommon: ≥1/1000 to <1/100; Rare: ≥1/10,000 to <1/1000; Very rare: <1/10,000 and Unknown frequency. For each antibiotic, the expected number of patients who could develop diarrhea was estimated by multiplying the total number of prescriptions by the lower and upper probability limits from the SmPC, producing a range of possible outcomes. Additionally, a mean probability value within each frequency category was applied as an approximate statistical measure: Very common (>10%) → 15%; Common (1–10%) → 5%; Uncommon (0.1–1%) → 0.5%; Rare (0.01–0.1%) → 0.05%; Very rare (<0.01%) → 0.005%; Unknown frequency → not estimated.

2.4. Integrated risk ranking of antibiotics based on consumption and diarrhea risk

The potential for AAD was estimated for antibiotics with more than 120,000 prescriptions issued during the last five years (2018–2022), provided that complete data were available. This time frame was selected to reflect the most recent and relevant trends in antibiotic consumption while minimizing bias due to older data or market availability changes. For each antibiotic, the total number of prescriptions was used to calculate the estimated number of diarrhea cases (mean), obtained by multiplying the number of prescriptions by the mean probability of diarrhea from the SmPC. As the analysis included three distinct variables (1) estimated mean number of cases, (2) diarrhea ROR from EudraVigilance, and (3) diarrhea ROR from FAERS all expressed on different scales, min–max normalization was applied. A combined score was then calculated based on these normalized values, and antibiotics were ranked according to the resulting score to estimate their relative risk for inducing diarrhea.

2.5. Systematic reviews, meta-analyses and clinical guidelines for probiotics use in antibiotic associated diarrhea

A computerized PUBMED search (2015–2025) was conducted using the MESH terms “probiotic” and “AAD” to review probiotic effectiveness in preventing and treating AAD. Only English-language systematic reviews and meta-analyses were included. Two investigators independently screened and extracted data, identifying 30 publications, 29 of which contained relevant effectiveness data. In addition, clinical practice guidelines of the European Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (ESPGHAN) Working Group 17 and the World Gastroenterology Organization (WGO) were considered. 18

2.6. Market overview of registered probiotics in the Republic of North Macedonia

To overview the probiotic market in the Republic of North Macedonia, four major online pharmacies’ websites (Zegin, Eurofarm, Anni Farm, 24Apteka) were surveyed. Using the keyword “probiotics,” product types, formulations, and colony-forming units (CFU) per dose were systematically extracted to capture a representative snapshot of available products.

Overall, the total number of dispensed oral antibiotic prescriptions in the Republic of North Macedonia showed a decreasing trend over the 2011–2022 period. The highest consumption was observed in the earlier years of the study period (2011–2016), followed by a gradual decline with the lowest levels recorded in 2020–2021. The majority of the total number of prescriptions (2011-2022) referred to amoxicillin with clavulanic acid 6,049,645 prescriptions (28.7%), followed by ciprofloxacin with 2,379,995 prescriptions (11.3%). In contrast, the fewest prescriptions were recorded for cloxacillin, a total of 10 prescriptions over 12-year period (nine in 2011 and one in 2012), after which no further prescriptions were reported. Regarding the financial burden, during the analyzed 12 year period, the NHIF of the Republic of North Macedonia allocated a total of 3,600,426,810 MKD (≈58,543,525.36 EUR), corresponding to an average annual expenditure of 300,035,567.5 MKD (≈4,878,627 EUR). 3.4. Analysis of diarrhea potential

The analysis aimed to estimate the number of patients likely affected by diarrhea during antibiotic therapy. Assuming each prescription represents a single patient due to short-term acute therapy, Table S2 presents approximate incidence based on SmPC reported frequency and prescription numbers. The results suggest diarrhea is relatively common with certain drugs, especially amoxicillin+clavulanic acid and amoxicillin, the most frequently prescribed. While not reflecting actual incidence in the Macedonian population, these estimates indicate that, at the population level, exposure to commonly prescribed antibiotics may result in a higher probability of diarrhea, highlighting the potential burden of this adverse effect in clinical practice.

Antibiotics were ranked by Total Score, with higher scores indicating greater diarrhea risk. Rank 1 was amoxicillin+clavulanic acid (largest estimated affected patients, high ROR), followed by cefixime (rank 2) and clindamycin (rank 3). Low scores, e.g., benzathine phenoxymethylpenicillin, indicated low potential risk. The ranking integrates real-world consumption and pharmacovigilance signals, enabling quantitative comparison and identification of antibiotics with higher public health impact for diarrhea.

3.6. Evidence from systematic reviews, meta-analyses and clinical guidelines for probiotics use in antibiotic associated diarrhea

A total of 29 systematic reviews and meta-analyses were included in the final analysis. These consistently demonstrated that probiotics reduce the risk of AAD, although the magnitude of effect varies by strain, population, and timing of administration. The strongest and most consistent evidence was observed for Saccharomyces boulardii CNCM I-745 and Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG, as well as selected multi-strain Lactobacillus–Bifidobacterium formulations. Overall, meta-analyses reported a reduction in AAD incidence of approximately 30–50% compared with control groups. Clinical guidelines (ESPGHAN and WGO) support the use of specific probiotic strains, particularly in pediatric populations, while emphasizing the importance of tailored recommendations based on indication in clinical practice.17,18

3.7. Market overview of registered probiotics in the Republic of North Macedonia

A total of 174 probiotic products were initially identified from the surveyed websites. After removing duplicates, 112 unique products remained (Supplementary Table S3). Common dosage forms included capsules, sachets, tablets, and drops; less common were lozenges and gummies. Four products lacked compositional information, and 54 (48.2%) did not report CFU strength. The most common genera were Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Streptococcus, and Saccharomyces. Many products contained multiple strains, up to 14, while others provided only general descriptions like “well-known lactic acid bacteria”. CFU counts ranged from 0.024 × 109 to 50 × 109 per dose, with some reaching 2 × 1011 CFU/g.

4. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive analysis of antibiotic consumption for oral use in the Republic of North Macedonia over a decade-long period. The study offers a detailed overview of national prescribing patterns and their implications for antimicrobial stewardship, focusing on the 2018–2022 period as most reflective of the current practice. During this interval, antibiotic prescribing was dominated by amoxicillin+clavulanic acid, azithromycin, ciprofloxacin, amoxicillin, and cefixime. Among these, three belong to the WHO “Watch” group (azithromycin, ciprofloxacin, and cefixime) and two to the “Access” group (amoxicillin+clavulanic acid and amoxicillin). The relative share of “Access” antibiotics in the Republic of North Macedonia for the period 2018–2022 was 53.1%, while “Watch” antibiotics accounted for 46.9%, reflecting concerns highlighted in the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) country visit report on AMR. 19 The report notes frequent use of broad-spectrum agents, gaps in infection prevention and control, and limited implementation of antimicrobial stewardship programs. As it was noted in the introduction according to GLASS data, “Access” antibiotics accounted for only 52.7% of global use in 2022, far below the 70% target, while “Watch” antibiotics comprised 45.3%. Although the Republic of North Macedonia was not included in the WHO Report on Surveillance of Antibiotic Consumption 2016–2018 20, comparison with neighboring, regional, and developed European countries provides useful context. In that report, “Access” antibiotic use exceeded 50% in most countries. Among high-income, developed European countries, “Access” shares were particularly high: Iceland (78%), Sweden (64.7%), Slovenia (63.2%), and the Baltic states (Lithuania 68.1%, Latvia 67.7%), exceeding or approaching the WHO target of >70%.1,20 Within the Western Balkans and regional upper-middle-income countries, Serbia (64.5%) showed favorable pattern compared to Montenegro (56.1%), whereas Albania (37.9%) and Bulgaria (45.2%) relied more heavily on “Watch” antibiotics. 20 Within this context of sustained high WATCH antibiotic use, the observed peak in azithromycin consumption in 2020 in the Republic of North Macedonia is particularly notable and is consistent with global evidence of disrupted antimicrobial prescribing during the COVID-19 pandemic. This pattern has been attributed to early therapeutic uncertainty and widespread empirical use of azithromycin across different healthcare settings. Evidence from multiple countries, including Jordan, Croatia and Brazil, confirms similar increases in azithromycin consumption during 2020.21–23 In addition, systematic review data indicate that such prescribing behavior contributed to antimicrobial stewardship disruption and was associated with a significant increase in macrolide resistance (OR = 2.71, 95% CI: 2.04–3.59), highlighting important public health consequences beyond consumption trends. 24 Overall, the relatively high proportion of “Watch” antibiotics in the Republic of North Macedonia indicates the need for continued monitoring, additional work on education of the healthcare professionals and optimization of prescribing practices. These trends underscore the importance of strengthening antibiotic stewardship and ensuring equitable access to essential, narrow-spectrum agents. Compliance with the AWaRe recommendations can therefore contribute not only to AMR mitigation but also to more efficient use of healthcare resources, a particularly important consideration for the Republic of North Macedonia, where the national NHIF faces significant budgetary constraints.

Integration of national NHIF prescription data with pharmacovigilance and safety indicators provides a comprehensive view of both antibiotic utilization and its potential adverse outcomes. When prescription volumes are cross-referenced with disproportionality signals (RORs) from EudraVigilance and FAERS, antibiotics most strongly associated with diarrhea emerge as amoxicillin+clavulanic acid, cefixime, clindamycin, azithromycin, and cefuroxime. Notably, only amoxicillin+clavulanic acid belongs to the “Access” group, highlighting that a large proportion of high-risk antibiotics fall within the “Watch” category. Since these same antibiotics rank among the top prescribed agents, their combined prescribing frequency and adverse-event potential, substantially influence patient outcomes and impose preventable costs on the healthcare system. 25

The relevance of AWaRe adherence therefore extends beyond resistance control to encompass the economic and clinical burden of ADRs. Among these, AAD represents one of the most frequent and costly complications. The occurrence of AAD prolongs the recovery time, increases the economic cost, decreases the quality of life, and even increases mortality. 26 Severe infections, such as Clostridium difficile infection, substantially increase hospital stays by an estimated 3–20 extra days per patient and drive significant economic burden, with annual costs in the US rising from US$1.3 billion in 2002 to US$6.3 billion in 2016.11,27 In resource-limited health systems such as that of the Republic of North Macedonia, where antibiotics can be issued through both reimbursed and out-of-pocket prescriptions, these avoidable complications exert a disproportionate economic burden. Strengthening rational antibiotic use in accordance with AWaRe principles could therefore yield dual benefits, limiting resistance development and reducing the economic burden of avoidable ADRs.

Emerging evidence suggests that probiotics and their derivatives synbiotics, ghost probiotics, and postbiotics support antimicrobial stewardship by preventing and treating AAD, especially in patients on high-risk antibiotics. 28 By modulating gut microbiota, enhancing barrier integrity, producing antimicrobial substances, and promoting immune function, probiotics counteract diarrhea pathophysiology and inhibit pathogens such as Escherichia coli, Salmonella enteritidis, and Clostridium difficile, reducing additional antibiotic use. 29 A meta-analysis of 17 RCTs (n = 3,631 adults) showed AAD incidence fell from 17.7% in controls to 8.0% with probiotics (RR 0.49, 95% CI 0.36–0.66; I2 = 58%). 30 Another meta-analysis of 36 RCTs (>9,000 adults) found a 38% reduction in AAD when probiotics were started early during antibiotic therapy, 31 confirmed for older adults when administered within two days of treatment. 32

Clinically, Saccharomyces boulardii CNCM I-745, Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG, and multi-strain Lactobacillus–Bifidobacterium formulations reduce AAD incidence by 30–50% in pediatric and adult populations.11,30,33–37 Multi-strain products colonize different gut regions, modulate distinct immune pathways, enhance colonization resistance, shorten diarrhea duration, delay onset, reduce severity, and restore native microbiota faster.7,38 A meta-analysis of five trials (n = 742) reported a 46% AAD risk reduction with L. acidophilus LA-5 combined with Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis BB-12 (RR 0.54, 95% CI 0.33–0.91). 39 Adding S. boulardii to Lactobacillus–Bifidobacterium formulations is rational due to complementary activities,40,41 and combined L. rhamnosus GG and S. boulardii is favorable as they do not compete metabolically. 42 However, L. rhamnosus GG shows inconsistent adult efficacy. McFarland et al., 43 (228 trials) found no significant AAD protection in adults (RR 0.55, 95% CI 0.25–1.18), whereas S. boulardii CNCM I-745 is effective in both adults and children. Doar and Samuthiram 44 ranked S. boulardii CNCM I-745/ATCC 74012 as the most effective and safe probiotic for AAD prevention, followed by L. casei CNCM I-1518/DN114-001 and L. acidophilus CL1285. Sniffen et al., 45 confirmed strong evidence for L. casei DN (+2 RCT score), while L. acidophilus CL1285 showed no significant benefit (–2 RCT score, six trials).

Based on overall data, the ESPGHAN Working Group recommends Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG or Saccharomyces boulardii for children at risk of AAD. 17 The WGO identifies effective strains including L. rhamnosus GG (level 1, highest evidence on scale 1-5), L. reuteri DSM 17938 (level 3), S. boulardii CNCM I-745 (level 1), and multi-strain formulations such as yogurt with L. casei DN114, L. bulgaricus, and Streptococcus thermophilus (level 2), and Lactobacillus acidophilus CL1285 and Lactobacillus casei (Bio-K+ CL1285) (level 2). 18 According to WGO global guidelines, recommended doses for AAD prevention include Bio-K+ CL1285, ≥1010 CFU, once daily; yogurt containing L. casei DN114, L. bulgaricus, S. thermophilus, ≥1010 CFU, twice daily; L. rhamnosus GG, ≥1010 CFU, twice daily; S. boulardii CNCM I-745, 5 × 109 CFU, twice daily; and L. reuteri DSM 17938, 108 CFU, twice daily. 18

These data collectively emphasize that probiotic benefits are both strain- and dose-dependent, demonstrating that nonspecific recommendations to “use probiotics” are scientifically insufficient and economically inefficient. The absence of national or institutional guidelines in the Republic of North Macedonia specifying probiotic strains, optimal dosing, and treatment duration further limits consistent clinical implementation. A practical example of evidence-based guidance comes from a recent Dutch meta-analysis, which systematically evaluated clinical trials and probiotic products available on the market to the population to issue recommendations for AAD prevention. 11 The authors mapped effective strains (Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG, L. acidophilus LA-5, B. lactis BB-12, Saccharomyces boulardii) to specific commercial products, highlighting which formulations could be reliably used in clinical practice. In the Republic of North Macedonia, the market overview reveals that, although a wide range of probiotic products is commercially available, significant variability exists in strain composition, dosage forms, and CFU reporting. Approximately 48% of all products do not specify CFU, and among pediatric formulations, this proportion is even higher, with 12 out of 21 products lacking this information. This heterogeneity underscores the challenge of providing evidence-based recommendations for clinicians and patients in the absence of national guidelines. To generate a level of guidance similar to the Dutch model, which mapped clinically effective strains to specific commercial products for AAD prevention, 11 a more comprehensive and systematic survey of the market in the Republic of North Macedonia is needed. Such a survey should expand beyond the currently analyzed websites of online pharmacies to include additional major community pharmacies to ensure that all relevant probiotic products are captured and assessed. This would enable the detailed identification of commercially available formulations with documented efficacy and support the development of national recommendations for evidence-based probiotic use, helping to bridge the current gap in guidance and allowing clinicians to recommend products with proven effectiveness.

5. Strengths and limitations

Strengths include the integration of national consumption of antibiotics with two international pharmacovigilance systems and SmPC-derived risk metrics to produce a policy-relevant priority list. In addition, the study provides a first comprehensive overview of commercially available probiotic products in the Republic of North Macedonia, including strain composition, dosage forms, and CFU reporting, which can help generate evidence-based guidance. Limitations are the use of reimbursed NHIF data only (excluding out-of-pocket dispensing), ecological assumptions (one prescription ≈ one treated patient), and the interpretive limits of spontaneous reporting (ROR signals indicate disproportional reporting, not incidence). Consequently, our findings are best used to prioritize interventions and surveillance rather than to estimate precise incidence rates.

6. Conclusion

This study demonstrates that antibiotic use in the Republic of North Macedonia is disproportionately weighted toward broad-spectrum “Watch” agents, with “Access” antibiotics comprising just over half of total consumption. These patterns emphasize the urgent need to strengthen antimicrobial stewardship, enhance prescribing oversight, and align national practices with WHO AWaRe targets to limit resistance and safeguard patient safety. Integrating evidence-based measures, such as targeted probiotic use, can help prevent antibiotic-associated complications while reducing healthcare costs. Developing national guidelines for probiotic selection and dosing, ensuring product quality, and fostering collaboration between physicians and pharmacists are essential to translate these strategies into routine practice. Such a combined approach offers a practical, cost-effective pathway to optimize antibiotic use, improve clinical outcomes, and mitigate the wider consequences of inappropriate antibiotic prescribing in the Republic of North Macedonia.
