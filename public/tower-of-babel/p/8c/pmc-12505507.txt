HPV vaccination willingness and behavior among nursing female students in China based on the protection motivation theory: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Rong Nie, Zhuohan Ma, Xiaoqin Rui, Tingting Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFemale nursing students show high intention yet low uptake of HPV vaccination, which undermines cervical cancer prevention efforts. To examine the mechanisms influencing their vaccination behavior, this study developed and validated an intention-behavior transition model based on Protection Motivation Theory, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for designing targeted interventions and improving vaccination coverage. A total of 631 female nursing students from universities in Wuhan were selected by the convenience sampling method and were surveyed using a General Information Questionnaire, Protection Motivation Questionnaire, and HPV Vaccination Willingness and Behavior Questionnaire, and a structural equation model was constructed using AMOS 24.0 software. 69.3% of the 631 female nursing students had HPV vaccination intention, and 13.3% had vaccination behavior. The results of structural equation model fitting showed that threat appraisal significantly affected female nursing students’ willingness to receive HPV vaccination (β = 0.209, P < .002) and behavior (β = -0.198, P = .002). Coping appraisal significantly influenced their willingness to vaccinate (β = -0.085, P < .008) and behavior (β = -0.170, P < .001). Willingness to vaccinate significantly influenced vaccination behavior (β = 0.627, P < .001). The Protection Motivation Theory is applicable for explaining the decision-making mechanism regarding HPV vaccination among female nursing students, as both threat appraisal and coping appraisal collectively influence the intention-behavior transition process. This study provides theoretical support for formulating immunization promotion policies targeted at this population; however, future research should expand sample diversity to enhance the generalizability of the findings.

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is one of the most common sexually transmitted infections in the world,1 and approximately 70–80% of sexually active women are infected with at least one HPV virus during their lifetime. Persistent infection with the virus can lead to a variety of cancers, including condyloma acuminatum, anal, vaginal, vulvar, oropharyngeal, and cervical cancer.2–4 Nowadays, the HPV vaccine is one of the most specific and effective measures to prevent HPV infection, which can fundamentally hinder the transmission of HPV.5 The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends HPV vaccination for women over 9 years of age, and 9–26 years of age is considered the most optimal age for vaccination.6 Therefore, female college students are at the optimal age for vaccination.7 Previous studies have shown that healthcare providers’ attitudes toward vaccination and their recommendations significantly influence the general public’s trust in vaccines and their willingness to receive HPV vaccinations.8,9 As future frontline workers in the healthcare field, nursing students’ thorough understanding of HPV and related vaccines, coupled with their positive attitudes toward them, is crucial to advancing vaccination efforts. Therefore, female nursing students not only bear the responsibility of promoting public health but also play an indispensable role in the promotion and dissemination of HPV vaccines.10

In recent years, several epidemiologic surveys have shown that the incidence of premarital sex among Chinese college students has been increasing, increasing the risk of HPV infection among female college students.11 However, a meta-analysis showed that the awareness rate of HPV among female college students in mainland China was only 24%.12 Insufficient knowledge of HPV and vaccines among the population significantly reduces their willingness to be vaccinated, thereby affecting subsequent vaccination behavior. Data show that the HPV vaccination rate of female college students in China is less than 15%,13–17 far below the vaccination rates of over 80% among female college students in countries such as Australia, Portugal, and the United Kingdom.18,19 Despite the proven availability and efficacy of the HPV vaccine, the vaccination rate among female college students in China remains unacceptably low. This not only highlights the severe challenges currently facing HPV vaccination promotion efforts but also underscores a critical public health issue that urgently requires resolution.

Existing studies have shown that a guiding framework based on health behavior theory can systematically explain the influencing factors of protective behaviors and provide a scientific basis for the development of effective health intervention strategies.20 However, most of the current empirical studies on HPV vaccination only stop at measuring college students’ knowledge level, attitudinal tendencies, and willingness to be vaccinated and lack the support of a well-developed theoretical framework,21,22 and there are few effective models that use modeling and analytical methods to assess and predict HPV vaccination decisions among female nursing students. Protection Motivation Theory (PMT) was first proposed by Rogers in 1975 and has been widely used to predict health protective behaviors, such as sexually protective behaviors,23 cervical cancer screening,24 and influenza vaccination,25 among many other health domains. PMT explains the adaptive behaviors adopted by individuals in the face of threats through cognitive regulatory processes, including both threat appraisal and coping appraisal.26,27 Threat appraisal is an individual’s perception of a health threat, including severity, vulnerability, intrinsic and extrinsic rewards. Coping appraisal is the perception of threat-handling ability, including response efficacy, self-efficacy, and response cost. Minimizing threat appraisal while maximizing response appraisal helps to shape the intention to act protectively, which in turn promotes actual protective behaviors,28 and PMT explains the mechanisms and processes inherent in behavioral change.

Therefore, this study uses protection motivation theory as a reference framework and fully incorporates cognitive modulators to explain the internal mechanisms of the change in intention-behavior toward HPV vaccination among female nursing students. This will provide a reference basis for the future HPV vaccination of female nursing students. We hypothesize that both threat appraisal and coping appraisal, as defined by Protection Motivation Theory, are associated with HPV vaccination willingness and behavior among female nursing students.

Methods

Study participants and sampling

From September to December 2023, this study employed a convenience sampling method to conduct a questionnaire survey among female nursing students from Wuhan Polytechnic University, Jianghan University, and Wuhan Social Work Professional College. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age 18–26 years old; (2) informed consent and voluntary participation in this study. Female nursing students participating in similar studies within the last year were excluded.

According to the sample size calculation formula n=z1−a/22π1−πδ2 reviewing the relevant literature,29 the HPV vaccination rate of female undergraduates in the central region was 10.6%, and taking π = 10.6% as the HPV vaccination rate of female nursing students, with Z(1-α/2) = 1.96, and the permissible error δ = 0.03, and taking into account the 20% loss of visit rate. The minimum required sample size was calculated to be 485.

Questionnaire design and content

The questionnaire included sections to assess demographic characteristics related to HPV infection and vaccination, knowledge levels of HPV and its vaccine, Protection Motivation Theory (PMT) entries, HPV vaccine uptake, and willingness to receive the HPV vaccine.

Demographic characteristics and HPV-related knowledge

Demographic characteristics include age, grade, monthly family income, and parents’ educational attainment; key information related to HPV includes awareness of the vaccine, whether the individual has had sexual intercourse, whether a doctor has recommended vaccination, and family history of cervical cancer.

PMT scale

The protective motivation theory questionnaire used in this study was developed as the HPV Vaccination Intention and Behavior Questionnaire by Xiaoqin Rui.30 The overall Cronbach’s α coefficient of the questionnaire stands at 0.932, with the coefficients for each dimension being 0.852, 0.625, 0.968, 0.944, 0.920, 0.896, and 0.970, respectively. The content validity index is 0.963, and the cumulative variance explanation rate amounts to 78.425%. Consequently, the scale exhibits favorable reliability and validity.

The questionnaire contained seven theoretical dimensions of protection motivation, totaling 32 items. Severity (4 items), which assessed nursing students’ perception of the severity of cervical cancer; Vulnerability (4 items), which assessed nurses’ perceived likelihood of being infected with HPV and of developing cervical cancer; Intrinsic rewards (3 items), which evaluated the perceived rewards of not receiving the vaccine; and Extrinsic rewards (4 items), which assessed nurses perceived objective benefits of not receiving the vaccine. Response efficacy (4 items) assessed nursing students’ recognition of the benefits of HPV vaccination; Self-efficacy (7 items) assessed nursing students’ perceived confidence in overcoming barriers to vaccination; Response costs (6 items) assessed nursing students’ perceived difficulties and barriers they may face. Each entry was scored on a 5-point Likert scale, with the “severity,” “vulnerability,” “response efficacy,” and “self-efficacy” dimension entry values ranging from 1 to 5 for scoring (strongly disagree to agree strongly), and the “intrinsic rewards,” “extrinsic rewards,” and “response costs” dimensions had the opposite rating range.

HPV vaccination intention and vaccination behavior

Question 1, “Would you like to be vaccinated against HPV in the future?” (yes/no), assessed female nursing students’ willingness to receive HPV vaccination. Question 2, “Have you received HPV vaccination in the past (yes, made an appointment but not yet vaccinated, made no appointment and not yet vaccinated)?” evaluated their vaccination behaviors, where vaccination behavior was defined as either having received the HPV vaccine or having made a confirmed appointment for vaccination.

Data collection

In the classroom setting, researchers provided female nursing students with a detailed explanation of the study’s main content, objectives, and significance. To ensure the validity of the data, no intervention measures were implemented during the study. After ensuring that the research subjects were fully informed, the researchers solemnly promised to keep the survey data strictly confidential and obtained written consent from the participants before conducting the survey using paper questionnaires. The questionnaire was completed independently and anonymously, taking approximately 15 minutes to complete. Upon completion, the questionnaires were collected on-site, and the completeness and quality of the responses were checked.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS). Cases and component ratios described the count data, and the measurement data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Chi-square tests and t-tests were used for one-way analysis and multifactorial logistic regression to determine factors influencing HPV vaccination. In PMT structural equation modeling (SEM) of female nursing students’ HPV vaccination behavior, all latent variables (threat/coping appraisal dimensions) were modeled at the item level. Measurement models were tested via confirmatory factor analysis prior to SEM, with no cross-loadings or item parcels used. The SEM was constructed using AMOS 24.0, with relationships between variables fitted by the maximum likelihood method at the α = 0.05 significance level.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki, and all studies involving human participants were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuhan Polytechnic University (BME-2023–1-06). All study participants gave informed consent and volunteered to participate in this study.

Results

Demographic characteristics and HPV vaccination intention and behavior

A total of 650 questionnaires were distributed in this study. After rigorous screening, 19 invalid questionnaires (primarily due to logical inconsistencies) were excluded, ultimately yielding 631 valid questionnaires with an effective response rate of 97.08%. Among 631 female nursing students, the willingness to vaccinate against HPV was 69.3%, the vaccination behavior was 13.31%, and the vaccination rate was 8.1%. The average age of the participants was 21.24 ± 2.175 years, with sophomores accounting for 42.95%. Nearly half of the participants’ parents had junior high school education levels (fathers: 47.7%; mothers: 50.24%), and 77.18% of families had a monthly income of 3,000–5,000 yuan. While 72.74% were aware of HPV vaccines and 84.0% reported no sexual experience, only 16.3% had received a doctor’s recommendation for vaccination. The chi-square test revealed statistically significant differences (P < .05) in HPV vaccination willingness across different academic years, vaccine awareness, and whether a doctor had recommended vaccination. Similarly, significant differences (P < .05) were observed in vaccination behavior based on age, academic year, parental education level, HPV vaccine awareness, family history, and doctor’s recommendation. Structural modeling of PMT for HPV vaccination of female nursing students

Reliability and validity testing of the measurement model

The reliability and validity test results of the PMT scale showed that all items had significant factor loadings on their corresponding dimensions (P < .001), indicating good construct validity. The overall Cronbach’s α was 0.852, and the Cronbach’s α for each dimension was greater than 0.6, exceeding the minimum standard of 0.6,31 demonstrating good internal consistency. The composite reliability (CR) values all exceeded 0.6, confirming the questionnaire’s good reliability. The AVE and its square root were both greater than 0.5, indicating good convergent validity, and the correlation coefficients between dimensions were all smaller than the corresponding square roots of the AVE.31 Therefore, the measurement model used in this study was validated. Structural modeling

Structural equation modeling was constructed with threat appraisal, coping appraisal, willingness to vaccinate, and vaccination behavior. The results of the modified model were as follows: χ2/df = 2.522, less than the critical value of 5; CFI = 0.900, equal to the critical value of 0.900; IFI = 0.901, more significant than the critical value of 0.900; GFI = 0.936, more significant than the critical value of 0.90; RMSEA = 0.053, less than the recommended value of 0.08, and the model’s various fitted values are within the acceptable range. Discussion

The results of this study indicate that the proportion of female nursing students who had HPV vaccination behavior is 13.3%, while the vaccination rate is 8.1%. This vaccination rate not only lags significantly behind that of Western countries (e.g., Spain at 73% and Italy at 23.9%)32,33 but also falls below the 11.0% vaccination rate among female college students in China,29 reflecting the current unsatisfactory status of HPV vaccination among female nursing students. Notably, female nursing students exhibit relatively high vaccination intent (69.3%), a proportion that exceeds both that of South Indian female nursing students (65.2%)9 and that of female college students in other regions of China (42.9% − 63.2%).15,34,35 This disparity between high vaccination intent and low vaccination rates may be closely related to practical factors such as high vaccine prices, concerns about vaccine safety, and cumbersome vaccination procedures. This suggests that simply increasing intent is insufficient to drive behavioral change, and further exploration of the psychological mechanisms influencing the transition from intent to behavior is necessary.

To address this, this study further employed structural equation modeling combined with the protective motivation theory to analyze female nursing students’ HPV vaccine vaccination willingness and behavior. The results revealed that threat assessment and coping assessment within the theoretical framework significantly influence the formation of vaccination willingness, and that willingness can further positively predict actual vaccination behavior. These findings not only validate the applicability of protective motivation theory in explaining vaccination behavior but also indicate that the theoretical model possesses predictive capacity for female nursing students’ HPV vaccination decisions, providing a theoretical basis for understanding the psychological drivers underlying the “intention-behavior gap.”

This study found that threat assessment (including intrinsic rewards, extrinsic rewards, severity, and vulnerability) significantly influences female nursing students’ willingness and behavior toward HPV vaccination. When female nursing students fully recognize the high risk of HPV infection and the severe health consequences of infection, their willingness to vaccinate significantly increases, leading them to be more inclined to adopt vaccination behavior. However, the mechanism through which threat assessment influences vaccination behavior is not simply “fear-driven.” If individuals simultaneously perceive that “not getting vaccinated” can bring more psychological or social benefits (i.e., higher intrinsic or extrinsic rewards), then high threat assessment may actually inhibit their vaccination behavior. This suggests that individuals’ vaccination decisions are essentially the result of a trade-off between risk perception and the “benefits” of not getting vaccinated. Previous studies have also pointed out that when individuals are unaware of the benefits of vaccination for their own health or lack social support and encouragement, they are more likely to delay or refuse vaccination.36

Notably, intrinsic rewards play a critical role in this process. If female nursing students believe that avoiding vaccination can help them evade potential side effects or mistakenly assume that “natural immunity is healthier,” they may forgo vaccination due to these perceived “intrinsic benefits,” even when aware of the severe threats posed by HPV infection. This cognitive bias directly explains why high threat appraisal can paradoxically lead to behavioral avoidance when the “perceived benefits of non-vaccination” outweigh the “protective benefits of vaccination.” These findings suggest that schools and public health authorities should prioritize strategies to mitigate the negative impact of such internal rewards while strengthening positive guidance. For instance, offering elective courses on HPV, organizing expert lectures, and conducting case-based analyses can help correct misconceptions such as “avoiding vaccination is safer” using empirical evidence. Real-world cases can be leveraged to enhance risk perception of HPV infection, thereby improving students’ knowledge about HPV. Additionally, through social media campaigns, campus-wide promotions, and peer-led education, vaccination can be framed as a socially responsible act, fostering an environment where vaccination is perceived as a positive choice. Such efforts can break down the psychological defense rooted in the “perceived benefits of non-vaccination” and ultimately facilitate the transition from intention to action.

This study confirms that coping appraisal (comprising response efficacy, self-efficacy, and response cost) has a direct impact on female nursing students’ willingness and behavior to receive HPV vaccination. When students perceive high vaccination costs (e.g., expensive vaccines, cumbersome appointment procedures), doubt the effectiveness of the vaccine, or lack confidence in their ability to complete the vaccination process, their vaccination intention significantly decreases, thereby hindering actual uptake. The study reveals a negative correlation between coping appraisal and both vaccination intention and behavior. The underlying mechanism is that even if individuals perceive a certain level of health threat, if they consider the protective measure (vaccination) to be ineffective, too costly, or beyond their personal capability, their protective motivation is weakened. In such cases, “non-action” is perceived as a more “rational” choice, thus inhibiting the adoption of health behaviors.

Multiple studies have indicated that self-efficacy is a decisive factor predicting vaccination intention and behavior.37 Research by Yang et al.38 suggested that severity, response efficacy, and self-efficacy collectively serve as strong predictors of HPV vaccination. Similarly, Liu R20 found in a hepatitis B vaccine study that response efficacy and susceptibility are significant cognitive factors influencing vaccination behavior, while self-efficacy and susceptibility markedly affect vaccination intention. Furthermore, structural barriers in reality, such as vaccine cost, accessibility, and concerns about vaccine safety and effectiveness, have been widely confirmed as critical factors influencing vaccination behavior.39,40 This suggests that schools and public health departments should aim to reduce response costs and enhance response efficacy and self-efficacy among female nursing students. For instance, health authorities could promote the inclusion of HPV vaccines in national immunization programs or reduce costs through financial subsidies to alleviate economic burdens. Schools could collaborate with disease control departments to introduce on-campus vaccination services, set up vaccination sites within campuses, and simplify appointment and vaccination procedures to reduce both economic and time burdens. Lastly, integrating scientific evidence on the efficacy and safety of HPV vaccines into relevant courses such as “Epidemiology” could help correct cognitive biases among students, while peer support education could enhance their confidence and self-efficacy in completing vaccination.

The results indicate a significant correlation between vaccination intention and vaccination behavior, which supports previous findings that intention is often the most critical determinant of such behavior.41 In the absence of protective motivation, when the recommended protective behavior is perceived as ineffective or unfeasible in mitigating threats, individuals are unlikely to form an intention to act.42 Given that vaccination intention strongly predicts actual vaccination behavior, intervention programs should place particular emphasis on the close relationship between intention and behavior.

This study has the following limitations: First, the study used a cross-sectional survey design, which does not allow for causal inference. Second, all data used in the study were self-reported, which may be subject to social desirability bias and recall bias. Third, the survey participants were limited to female nursing students in Wuhan, China, resulting in a limited sample representativeness, which may restrict the generalizability and applicability of the study findings to other regions in China and abroad. Fourth, the PMT theory itself has certain limitations, the influence of social and cultural factors was not fully incorporated, and demographic variables were not included in the model, which may result in the model lacking sufficient comprehensiveness when explaining female nursing students’ HPV vaccination decisions.

Future research could focus on improving these shortcomings. On one hand, expanding the sample size and further validating the model in a more diverse sample is recommended. On the other hand, incorporating demographic factors and social and cultural factors into the analytical framework to explore their potential influence on the model’s pathways could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms, thereby laying a stronger foundation for further research into female nursing students’ HPV vaccination decisions.

Conclusions

Promoting HPV vaccination intention and actual uptake among female nursing students is a critical step in advancing cervical cancer prevention efforts. This study revealed a significant gap between vaccination intention (69.3%) and behavior (13.3%) in this population. Based on the Protection Motivation Theory (PMT), the analysis demonstrated that both threat appraisal and coping appraisal collectively influence behavioral intention. Specifically, intrinsic rewards inhibited vaccination behavior through cognitive bias, while high response costs and perceptions of efficacy hindered the transition from intention to action. It is therefore recommended to integrate vaccine education into nursing curricula, implement peer-led campaigns and on-campus vaccination services, and promote the inclusion of HPV vaccines in national immunization programs to reduce financial barriers. This study extends the theoretical mechanisms of PMT in health behavior research and provides a basis for developing immunization promotion policies targeted at female nursing students. Future research should expand to include other populations and employ longitudinal designs.
