A conserved mammalian mecciRNA, mecciATP6, regulates mitochondrial homeostasis through interaction with HNRNPA3
Authors: Xiaoyang Cao, Juzheng Sui, Ge Shan, Xu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Circular RNAs (circRNAs) are covalently closed, single-stranded RNA molecules generated from both nuclear and mitochondrial genomes. Several nuclear-encoded circRNAs have been reported to be conserved in sequence and function across animals. However, the evolutionary conservation and physiological significance of mitochondria-encoded circRNAs (mecciRNAs) remain largely unexplored. Here, by analyzing mitochondrial RNA sequencing data from human and mouse cells, we identify a conserved mecciRNA derived from the MT-ATP6 locus, termed mecciATP6. We show that mecciATP6 modulates mitochondrial homeostasis by binding and regulating the protein abundance of an evolutionarily conserved RNA-binding protein (RBP) HNRNPA3. Our data provide a conceptual framework for defining mecciRNA conservation across mammals and uncover a conserved mecciRNA-protein regulatory mechanism linked to mitochondrial homeostasis.

Introduction

Circular RNAs (circRNAs) are covalently closed, single-stranded RNA molecules generated from both nuclear and mitochondrial genomes and are widely present in animal cells [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Nuclear genome-encoded circRNAs are generally stable and have been shown to participate in diverse physiological and pathological processes, functioning as microRNA sponges, transcriptional regulators, protein-binding scaffolds, and, in some cases, templates for translation [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. Mitochondria-encoded circRNAs (mecciRNAs) have recently been identified as a distinct subclass of circRNAs [3,4]. In contrast to most nuclear circRNAs, mecciRNAs exhibit rapid turnover, which is mediated by the SUPV3L1/ELAC2 complex [4,9]. Emerging studies have revealed that mecciRNAs are functionally linked to mitochondrial homeostasis [3,4,[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Several mecciRNAs, including mecciND1, mecciND5, and mcPGK1, have been shown to shuttle between mitochondria and the cytosol, facilitating the mitochondrial import of nuclear-encoded proteins [4,10]. In addition, mecciRNAs have been reported to modulate mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) activity and mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) release through interactions with mPTP components, such as ATP5B, or regulatory factors, including TRAP1 [9,11].

Although a large number of circRNAs have been cataloged in mammalian cells, the extent to which circRNAs are conserved across species and whether such conservation reflects shared biological functions remains incompletely understood [13]. Several nuclear-encoded circRNAs have been reported to be conserved across animal species and to exert similar physiological functions. For example, CDR1as is conserved in mammals and regulates brain development and function by sponging miR-7 [14,15]. CircBoule, which is conserved in metazoans, plays a conserved role in regulating male reproductive function and stress responses across species by interacting with heat shock proteins (HSPs) [16]. In addition, the mammalian conserved circRNA circLARP1B interacts with HNRNPD and modulates lipid metabolism [17].

Mammalian mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is a highly conserved ∼16.5 kb circular double-stranded genome that encodes a fixed set of 37 genes, including 13 protein-coding genes, 22 tRNAs, and 2 rRNAs across mammalian species [18]. Given the high degree of conservation of the mitochondrial genome, it is possible that mtDNA derived mecciRNAs may also exhibit evolutionary conservation across mammals. MecciRNAs have been identified at multiple loci across the mitochondrial genome in both human and mouse, and individual mitochondrial genes can give rise to distinct mecciRNAs from different regions [4]. However, the evolutionary conservation of these mecciRNAs has not been defined, and their conserved physiological functions have not been systematically examined.

In this study, we analyzed mitochondrial RNA sequencing data (RNA-seq) from human and mouse cells and identified a conserved mecciRNA derived from the MT-ATP6 locus, termed mecciATP6. We show that mecciATP6 modulates mitochondrial homeostasis by regulating the protein abundance of the RNA-binding protein (RBP) HNRNPA3. These findings provide a conceptual framework for defining mecciRNA conservation across mammals and uncover a conserved mecciRNA-protein regulatory mechanism linked to mitochondrial homeostasis.

Results

Defining and identifying conserved mecciRNAs across mammals

To investigate the evolutionary conservation of mecciRNAs, we performed RNA-seq on isolated mitochondrial RNA from human PLC cells and mouse 3T3 cells. Subsequent bioinformatic identification of mecciRNAs was conducted using these data together with published mitochondrial RNA-seq datasets from human HeLa and 293T cells and mouse N2a cells (GSE249786) [9]. As a result, 77 human and 71 mouse mecciRNAs were consistently detected across all three or two respective cell lines (Fig. 1A). The size distribution of the identified mecciRNAs was predominantly concentrated between 150 and 450 nt (Fig. S1A). Among the 77 shared human mecciRNAs, 10 exhibited lengths greater than 4000 nt, while among the 71 mouse mecciRNAs, 2 exceeded 4000 nt in length (Fig. S1A). These long mecciRNAs were therefore filtered out from subsequent analyses. Given the compact and highly conserved organization of mammalian mitochondrial genomes, we focused on mecciRNAs derived from homologous mtDNA loci. MecciRNAs originating from antisense strands of mitochondrial protein-coding genes were further excluded to ensure unambiguous locus assignment. These remaining consistently detected mecciRNAs were derived from 12 mitochondrial gene loci, except MT-ND3, from which no mecciRNAs were detected under our criteria, possibly due to cell type-specific expression and/or limited sequencing depth (Fig. S1B). To define the evolutionary conservation of mecciRNAs more precisely, we employed a restricted positional conservation-based approach (Fig. 1C). For each mecciRNA, its genomic position was mapped to the corresponding mitochondrial gene, and the relative position within the gene was normalized to gene length. Human and mouse mecciRNAs originating from homologous genes were then pairwise matched, and positional conservation was quantitatively evaluated based on the extent of overlap between matched mecciRNA pairs (Fig. 1C). Based on positional conservation scores, all human–mouse mecciRNA pairs were ranked (Table S1). After excluding pairs with a score of 0, the top 15 ranked pairs were selected from the remaining 127 pairs as the most highly conserved group of mecciRNA pairs. These high-scoring pairs were derived from the MT-CO1, MT-ND1, MT-ND6, MT-ATP6, and MT-ND2 loci, with eight originating from the MT-CO1 locus (Fig. 1D, Fig. S1C). Notably, these mecciCO1s exhibited highly similar start and end positions and comparable lengths in both human and mouse, resulting in substantial overlap among individual circular species and thereby limiting experimental discrimination for downstream functional investigation (Fig. S1C). As a result, these mecciCO1s were not pursued for further functional investigation in the present study. Sequence alignment was then performed for the left top-ranked mecciRNA pairs to evaluate their sequence conservation across species. Among them, the mecciRNA pair derived from the MT-ATP6 and MT-ND1 loci exhibited high conservation at both the positional and sequence levels (Fig. 1D). In combination with expression levels across the five analyzed cell lines, one mecciRNA, named mecciATP6 (ranked sixth by positional conservation and showing approximately 61% sequence identity between human and mouse), was selected for further investigation (Fig. 1D and Fig. S1D). Collectively, we provide a positional overlap-based scoring strategy for the systematic assessment of mecciRNA conservation across species and identify a conserved mecciATP6 in humans and mice.

Validation and characterization of mecciATP6

The existence of mecciATP6 was verified by PCR amplification with divergent primers in HeLa and N2a cells (Fig. 2A). The putative junction fragment was further confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Fig. 2B). RNase R digestion confirmed the circular nature of mecciATP6 in HeLa and N2a cells (Fig. 2C). After transcription was blocked with actinomycin D, which suppresses both mitochondrial and nuclear transcription [9], the half-life of mecciATP6 was estimated to be less than 2 h, comparable to that of its linear counterpart (Fig. 2D, Fig. S1E). This observation is consistent with previous reports that mecciRNAs undergo rapid degradation [9]. Single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization (smFISH) revealed that the signals of mecciATP6 partially overlapped with mitochondria in both HeLa and N2a cell lines (Fig. 2E). Some portion of mecciATP6 signals did not overlap with the mitochondria, indicating that a substantial fraction of mecciATP6 was localized outside mitochondria and in the cytosol. In summary, these data confirm the circular nature and rapid turnover of mecciATP6. MecciATP6 interacts with HNRNPA3 in both human and mouse cells

To further elucidate the physiological function of mecciATP6, we performed RNA pulldown to identify its potential partners. Pulldown was conducted in HeLa and N2a cell lysates with a 3′-biotinylated antisense probe against the junction site of mecciATP6 (Fig. 3A–F). The effective and specific capture of mecciATP6 in both HeLa and N2a cells was validated by RT-qPCR (Fig. 3A and D). Proteins co-pulled down with mecciATP6 were separated by SDS–PAGE and visualized by silver staining. Bands specifically enriched in the mecciATP6 pulldown were excised and subjected to mass spectrometry (MS) analysis (Fig. 3B and E). HNRNPA3, an RNA-binding protein (RBP), was identified as the specific interacting protein of mecciATP6. A strong ∼43 kDa band was detected in silver-stained gels following mecciATP6 pulldown in mouse N2a cells. Mass spectrometry analysis of this band revealed predominantly abundant cytoskeletal proteins, such as actin, suggesting a nonspecific association. HNRNPA3 is predominantly localized in the nuclei, shuttles between the nucleus and cytoplasm, and plays a key role in mRNA trafficking and stability [[19], [20], [21]]. Moreover, a previous study reported that mecciND5 binds to HNRNPA3 and facilitates its mitochondrial import; however, the functional role of HNRNPA3 within mitochondria has not been defined [4]. To facilitate visualization of cytosolic HNRNPA3, immunofluorescence (IF) analysis with mild permeabilization conditions was applied to reduce the dominant nuclear signal by minimizing nuclear disruption. Quantitative colocalization analysis of HNRNPA3 signals and mitochondrial signals revealed that over 50% of cytosolic HNRNPA3 signals overlapped with mitochondrial signals in HeLa and N2a cells (Fig. 3K).

The splicing profile of HNRNPA3 is highly conserved between humans and rodents [22,23]. The human HNRNPA3 gene encodes four protein isoforms with approximate molecular weights of 38, 39, 41, and 41.5 kDa, whereas the murine Hnrnpa3 gene is annotated to produce two isoforms in current databases. Consistent with this complexity, we detected three major HNRNPA3 bands in human HeLa cells and two major bands in murine N2a cells by Western blotting (Fig. 3C and F), which may be due to differential expression of HNRNPA3 isoforms across species and tissues [[22], [23], [24]]. The longest isoform of HNRNPA3 exhibits a high degree of protein sequence conservation between human and mouse, with only one amino acid difference (Fig. S2A). Collectively, our data demonstrated that mecciATP6 specifically binds to a highly conserved RBP HNRNPA3, and the interaction between mecciATP6 and HNRNPA3 indicates that mecciATP6 may exert conserved functions across human and mouse.

MecciATP6 regulates HNRNPA3 protein abundance and modulates mitochondrial energy metabolism and redox homeostasis

We then investigated the regulatory relationship between HNRNPA3 and mecciATP6. We found that under mecciATP6 knockdown in HeLa and N2a cells, the protein level of HNRNPA3 was significantly decreased (Fig. 4A and B). The HNRNPA3 mRNA level remained unchanged (Fig. S2B and C). Conversely, overexpressing mecciATP6 increased HNRNPA3 protein abundance without changing its mRNA level (Fig. 4C and D, Fig. S2D–F). In addition, neither knockdown nor overexpression of mecciATP6 altered the MT-ATP6 mRNA level (Fig. S2B–F). IF analysis of HNRNPA3 showed that knockdown of mecciATP6 in HeLa and N2a cells led to a significant reduction in overall HNRNPA3 signal, accompanied by a marked decrease in mitochondrial HNRNPA3 fluorescence intensity (Fig. 4E–H). To investigate the mechanism by which mecciATP6 regulates HNRNPA3 protein abundance, mecciATP6-knockdown HeLa and N2a cells were treated with MG132, a proteasome inhibitor. In mecciATP6-knockdown cells, MG132 treatment largely attenuated the reduction of HNRNPA3 protein levels (Fig. S2G and H). In both HeLa and N2a cells, mecciATP6 knockdown was associated with reduced ATP levels and elevated intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), whereas overexpression resulted in opposite changes (Fig. 5A–D). HNRNPA3 is known to regulate the stability of nuclear-encoded mRNAs; therefore, we asked whether it similarly functions in regulating mitochondrial transcripts. After siRNA-mediated knockdown of HNRNPA3 for 48 h, the RNA levels of all 13 mitochondrial protein-coding genes were reduced in both HeLa and N2a cells (Fig. 5E–H). Following actinomycin D treatment for 2 h, accelerated degradation of several mitochondrial mRNAs, including MT-ATP8, MT-ATP6, MT-CO3, and MT-ND3, was observed in both species (Fig. 5I and J). However, other transcripts showed no obvious changes or exhibited inconsistent trends between human and mouse, likely due to differences in mRNA stability and expression levels (Fig. S3A and B). Mitochondrial mRNAs encode essential components of the oxidative phosphorylation machinery and are therefore directly linked to mitochondrial function [25]. Dysregulation of mitochondrial mRNA expression can lead to impaired ATP production and excessive ROS accumulation [26,27]. Moreover, we also observed a significant reduction in mecciRNA levels following HNRNPA3 knockdown, suggesting that HNRNPA3 may participate in the regulation of mecciRNA turnover or biogenesis (Fig. S3C and D). These results indicate that HNRNPA3 contributes to the regulation of mitochondrial mRNA stability. Discussion

In this study, we provide a positional overlap-based scoring strategy for the systematic evaluation of mecciRNA conservation across species and identify a conserved mecciATP6 in humans and mice. MecciATP6 is highly expressed and positionally conserved, and exerts conserved functions through interaction with the evolutionarily conserved RBP HNRNPA3. MecciATP6 binds to HNRNPA3 and regulates its protein abundance, thereby influencing the mitochondrial level of HNRNPA3. Mitochondrial HNRNPA3 contributes to the stabilization of mitochondrial mRNAs. A simple summary for this study. We provide a positional overlap-based scoring strategy for the systematic evaluation of mecciRNA conservation across species and identify a conserved mecciATP6 in humans and mice. MecciATP6 binds to HNRNPA3 and regulates its protein abundance, thereby influencing the mitochondrial level of HNRNPA3. Mitochondrial HNRNPA3 contributes to the stabilization of mitochondrial mRNAs. High levels of mecciATP6 promote mitochondrial mRNA stability and maintain mitochondrial homeostasis, whereas reduced mecciATP6 leads to decreased HNRNPA3 abundance, impaired mitochondrial mRNA stability, reduced ATP levels, and elevated intracellular ROS.

In mecciATP6-knockdown cells, MG132 treatment largely attenuated the reduction of HNRNPA3 protein levels (Fig. S2G and H), suggesting that mecciATP6 contributes to the stabilization of HNRNPA3, at least in part, by limiting proteasome-dependent degradation. Future studies are still required to elucidate the precise mechanism by which mecciATP6 regulates HNRNPA3 protein abundance. It remains unclear whether the interaction between mecciATP6 and HNRNPA3 occurs in mitochondria, the cytosol, or both compartments. Previous studies have shown that mecciND5 interacts with HNRNPA3 and facilitates its import into mitochondria [4]. It is therefore possible that binding of mecciATP6 to HNRNPA3 may similarly contribute to its mitochondrial trafficking, although this requires further investigation. In addition, the precise binding region of HNRNPA3 on mecciATP6 has not yet been defined. Notably, HNRNPA3 has been reported to preferentially bind G-rich RNA sequences in the nucleus [28], raising the possibility that specific sequence or structural features of mecciATP6 may mediate this interaction.

Circular RNAs have been reported to form R-loops with nuclear DNA, termed circRNA-associated R-loops (ciR-loops), which play important roles in transcriptional regulation and genome stability [29,30]. Dysregulation of ciR-loop homeostasis can compromise genomic integrity, indicating that ciR-loops are subject to tight cellular control. Recent studies have identified the RNA helicase Brr2 as a critical regulator of ciR-loop homeostasis by resolving circRNA–DNA hybrids. Given that mecciRNAs originate from the mitochondrial genome, it is conceivable that mecciRNAs may similarly engage in R-loops with mtDNA. In this context, our observation that decreasing HNRNPA3 leads to a global reduction in mitochondrial mRNA abundance raises the possibility that HNRNPA3 may contribute to the maintenance of mtDNA transcriptional activity, potentially by supporting mtDNA stability or resolving aberrant RNA–DNA hybrids. Although direct evidence for mecciRNA-associated R-loops in mitochondria is currently lacking, these findings suggest a potential parallel between nuclear ciR-loop regulation and mitochondrial mecciRNA function.

Notably, although most of the conserved mecciRNAs identified in this study, including mecciATP6, exhibit strong positional conservation across species, their junction sequences are not identical. This suggests that strict sequence identity at the junction may not be essential for the conserved function of mecciRNAs. The high degree of positional and size similarity among conserved mecciCO1 species raises the possibility of functional clustering or coordinated regulation, which will require the development of more refined experimental approaches to resolve. Our findings demonstrate that mecciRNAs are evolutionarily meaningful regulatory molecules rather than byproducts of RNA processing. Given the highly compact organization of the mtDNA, mecciRNAs appear to be maintained through evolution. These results substantially expand the biological significance of mecciRNAs.

Methods

Cell culture, transfection, and MG132 treatment

HeLa, PLC/PRF/5 (PLC), N2a, and 3T3 cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). All the cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C under 5% CO2. Mycoplasma detection was performed by DAPI staining to confirm the absence of mycoplasma contamination. Plasmids and small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) were transfected with Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, 11668027) following the manufacturer's protocol. For MG132 treatment, 48 h post-transfection, cells were treated with 50 μM MG132 (APExBIO, A2585) for 8 h to inhibit proteasome activity, then harvested for protein extraction and analyzed by Western blot. Untreated cells served as controls.

Mitochondria isolation

Mitochondria were isolated from cultured cells as previously described [31]. Briefly, cells were collected by centrifugation. The cell pellets were resuspended in ice-cold IB-1 buffer (225 mM mannitol, 75 mM sucrose, 0.1 mM EGTA, and 20 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.4). Cells were homogenized using a 7 mL Dounce homogenizer with a B pestle (Kimble Kontes, 885300–0007) for 40 strokes. Nuclear debris was removed by three consecutive centrifugation steps at 1800×g. The supernatant was then centrifuged at 12,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C to pellet crude mitochondria. The pellets were gently resuspended in ice-cold IB-2 buffer (225 mM mannitol, 75 mM sucrose, and 20 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.4) and transferred into a 1.5 mL tube. Mitochondria were centrifuged again at 12,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. Loose ER contaminants surrounding the light-yellow mitochondrial pellet were carefully washed away with IB-2. The remaining pellet was resuspended in 200 μL IB-2 containing 1 mg/mL digitonin (Sigma-Aldrich, D141) and incubated on ice for 10 min to remove residual ER contamination. For subsequent RNA isolation, RNase A (1 μg/mL) was added together with digitonin. Purified mitochondria were pelleted at 13,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C and used for downstream experiments.

RNA sequencing

Mitochondrial RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, 15596018CN) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Ribosomal RNA was removed using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Gold kit (Illumina). Sequencing libraries were then constructed using the NEB Next Ultra Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs) following the manufacturer's protocols. All libraries were subjected to 150-nt paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Novogene), generating approximately 10 Gb of data per library. For downstream analyses, raw sequencing reads were processed by removing adaptor sequences using Cutadapt (version 1.15).

MecciRNA identification

MecciRNAs were identified as previously described [9]. Paired-end RNA-seq clean reads were first aligned to ribosomal DNA (rDNA) sequences corresponding to pre-rRNAs (18S, 5.8S, 28S, MT-RNR1, and MT-RNR2) using Bowtie2 (version 2.3.2) to remove rRNA-derived reads. The remaining ribo-minus reads were then aligned to the reference genomes (human: hg38; mouse: mm10) using BWA (version 0.7.17). Soft-clipped reads mapped to the mitochondrial genome were subsequently extracted and analyzed to identify mecciRNA junctions. A read was defined as mecciRNA junction only if it satisfied at least one of the following criteria: (1) both of the paired-end reads supported the same circular junction breakpoint; or (2) one of the paired-end reads contained a junction breakpoint, while the other end mapped to the corresponding circularized junction region.

Identification of conserved mecciRNA pairs

The length of the homolog mitochondrial gene in both human and mouse was scaled to 100 relative units. Each mecciRNA was then mapped back to its corresponding parental mitochondrial gene, and its relative positional interval was determined based on the normalized gene length. Human and mouse mecciRNAs derived from the same mitochondrial gene were compared in a one-to-one pairing manner. For each mecciRNA pair, the degree of overlap between their relative positional intervals was calculated ([overlapped length]/[average length of mecciRNA pair]). Higher overlap values indicate stronger positional conservation along the parental mitochondrial gene. Based on these positional conservation scores, all human–mouse mecciRNA pairs were ranked. After excluding pairs with a score of 0, the top 15 pairs were defined as the most highly conserved candidates according to positional conservation and were selected for further analyses. Sequence conservation was then evaluated by applying the Needleman–Wunsch global alignment algorithm and the Smith–Waterman local alignment algorithm to obtain the optimal alignments. Sequence conservation was defined as the percentage of successfully paired nucleotides within the optimal alignment. In parallel, the expression levels of mecciRNAs across multiple cell lines were quantified as reads per million (RPM). Considering positional conservation, sequence conservation, and high expression levels, the mecciATP6 pair was selected for further investigation.

Reverse transcription (RT) and PCR reaction

Total RNA (500 ng) or mitochondrial RNA (100 ng) was used as templates for cDNA synthesis. Reverse transcription was performed using the GoScript Reverse Transcription System (Promega, A5001) according to the manufacturer's instructions, with random hexamer primers. PCR was performed using 2 × TransTaq-T PCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech, AS122-11) according to the standard protocol. Quantitative PCR was performed using GoTaq SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Promega, A6001). Reactions were run on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR system (Thermo Scientific) following standard procedures. All primer sequences were included in Table S2 of the Supporting Information.

Western blotting

For Western blotting analysis, samples were separated by SDS-PAGE. Proteins were transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore). Membranes were processed using the Pierce ECL Western Blotting protocol (Thermo Scientific, 32106). Chemiluminescent signals were captured with an ImageQuant LAS 4000 Biomolecular Imager (GE Healthcare). Gray-scale intensities were quantified using ImageJ software. The following antibodies were used in Western blotting: anti-β-actin (Transgene, HC201) and anti-HNRNPA3 (Proteintech, 25142-1-AP).

Genomic DNA and total RNA isolation

For genomic DNA isolation, 1 × 107 cells were lysed in Genomic Lysis Buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 400 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA pH 8.0, 1% SDS, 100 μg/mL proteinase K) at 56 °C for 4-12 h, followed by sequential phenol-chloroform extraction. DNA was further purified by an additional phenol-chloroform extraction and isopropanol precipitation. For total RNA extraction, TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596018CN) was used according to the standard protocol, followed by DNase I treatment to remove residual genomic DNA.

RNase R treatment

RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, 15596018CN) according to the manufacturer's instructions. For RNase R treatment, 20 μg total RNA was treated with 20 U RNase R (Epicentre, RNR07250) for 40 min at 37 °C to digest linear RNAs [32].

mRNA stability assay

For the mRNA stability assay, cells were cultured in fresh DMEM at 37 °C for 12 h and then treated with 5 mg/mL actinomycin D (Sigma, A4262). Samples were collected at the indicated time points. mRNA levels were subsequently analyzed by RT-qPCR.

Single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization

Single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization (smFISH) was performed as previously described with some modifications [33]. Briefly, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature for 10 min, followed by two washes with DPBS. Permeabilization was then carried out by incubating the cells in pre-chilled 70% ethanol at −20 °C overnight. Before hybridization, cells were washed twice with 2 × SSC buffer. A pre-hybridization step was performed by incubating the samples in hybridization buffer (containing 30% formamide, 5 × SSC, 9 mM citric acid, pH 6.8, 0.1% Tween-20, 50 μg/mL heparin, 1 × Denhardt's solution, and 10% dextran sulfate) at 37 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, hybridization was performed overnight at 37 °C with 1.2 pmol of each probe in 300 μL hybridization buffer. For the amplification stage, fluorescently labeled hairpins (18 pmol each) were heated at 95 °C for 90 s and cooled to room temperature in the dark for 30 min. Samples were incubated overnight at room temperature with the snap-cooled hairpins in 300 μL amplification buffer (5 × SSC, 0.1% Tween-20, and 10% dextran sulfate). After amplification, samples were stained with DAPI (5 μg/mL) and washed accordingly. Finally, samples were mounted using mounting medium for imaging. The images were captured on the laser confocal microscopy LSM980 (Zeiss).

Confocal microscopy and fluorescence analysis

SmFISH and immunofluorescence images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM980 confocal microscope equipped with a 63 × oil-immersion objective at a resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels. Image processing and quantification were performed with ImageJ. Cytoplasmic fluorescence intensity was calculated by subtracting the Hoechst-stained nuclear area from the whole-cell area. Fluorescence intensity was defined as the ratio of cytoplasmic integrated fluorescence density to the cytoplasmic area. For smFISH and immunofluorescence (IF) analyses, the colocalization ratio between smFISH/IF signals and mitochondria was quantified using the Coloc2 plugin in ImageJ. To quantify the integrated density of smFISH signals within mitochondrial regions, confocal images were imported into ImageJ and split into individual channels. Mitochondrial regions were identified based on mitochondrial marker signals using an optimal threshold, and the integrated fluorescence density of smFISH signals within mitochondrial areas was calculated using the corresponding mitochondrial masks.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) was performed as previously described with some modifications [30]. Briefly, cells were crosslinked using a UV crosslinker (UVP) at a dose of 120 mJ/cm2, followed by lysis in RIPA buffer (containing 50 mmol/L Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mmol/L NaCl, 5 mmol/L EDTA, 1% NP-40, 0.1% SDS) supplemented with 200 units of RNasin Ribonuclease Inhibitors (Promega, N2615), 2 mmol/L DTT, and 1 × protease inhibitor cocktail (Transgen, DI111). The cell suspension was sonicated for 10 min at 4 °C using a Sonics Vibra-Cell sonicator and then centrifuged at 13,000 g for 15 min to collect the supernatant. Specific antibody or control IgG (Proteintech, 30000-0-AP) was added to the supernatant and incubated at 4 °C for 4 h to allow antigen binding. Protein G Dynabeads (Life Technologies) were then added and incubated at 4 °C for at least 2 h. The protein-antibody-beads complexes were washed twice with RIPA buffer and once with RIPA buffer supplemented with 500 mmol/L NaCl. After the final wash, one-fifth of the beads were resuspended in SDS loading buffer and heated at 100 °C for 10 min for subsequent Western blot analysis. The remaining complexes were digested with proteinase K at 55 °C for 30 min, and RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596018CN). Antibodies used included: anti-HNRNPA3 (Proteintech, 25142-1-AP).

RNA pulldown

RNA pulldown was performed as previously described with minor modifications [4]. Cell lysates were prepared using the same protocol as the RIP assay. The supernatant was collected, and 100 pmol of biotinylated antisense oligonucleotide was added, followed by incubation at room temperature for 2 h. Concurrently, M-280 streptavidin magnetic beads (Life Technologies) were blocked by incubating with 500 ng/μL yeast tRNA and 500 ng/μL BSA at room temperature for 1 h, washed with RIPA buffer, and resuspended. For every 100 pmol of biotin-DNA oligonucleotide, 50 μL of the blocked beads were added to the mixture, which was then incubated with rotation at room temperature for 2 h. The beads were collected using a magnetic stand (Life Technologies) and washed twice with RIPA buffer, followed by two washes with RIPA buffer supplemented with 500 mmol/L NaCl. RNA and proteins were eluted from the beads for subsequent analysis. Proteins pulled down by the antisense oligonucleotide were separated by SDS-PAGE and visualized by silver staining.

Plasmid construction

All plasmids were constructed using either restriction enzyme digestion followed by ligation or recombinant cloning methods. Restriction enzymes were purchased from Thermo Scientific (FD series). Ligation reactions were performed using T4 DNA ligase (Promega, A3600), and recombinant cloning was carried out using a seamless cloning kit (Vazyme, C112-02). All plasmids were verified by Sanger sequencing.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were stained with MitoTracker (invitrogen, M22426) diluted 1:5000 in serum-free DMEM and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min to label mitochondria. The cells were then fixed using a freshly prepared methanol-glacial acetic acid (3:1) fixative. Following fixation, cells were treated with a mild permeabilization buffer containing 0.01% Triton X-100 on ice for 20 min to minimize the disruption of the nuclei. After washing twice with PBS, cells were blocked with blocking buffer (PBS containing 0.1% BSA) at room temperature for 30 min. Primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer were added and incubated with the cells at 4 °C for 4 h. Cells were then washed twice with PBS, followed by incubation with corresponding Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies (invitrogen, A-11008) in the dark at room temperature for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 980 confocal microscope. For transfection experiments, cells were transfected one day prior to plating on coverslips, and immunofluorescence assays were performed one day after plating.

ROS and ATP measurements

Intracellular ROS and ATP levels were measured using the Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0033) and the ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0026), respectively, strictly following the manufacturer's instructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses for both main and supplementary figures were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. P values were calculated using Student's t-tests, as indicated in the figure legends. The values shown in the graphs represent the means of at least three independent experiments, with error bars indicating the standard deviation (SD). Statistical methods are specified in the corresponding figure legends.
