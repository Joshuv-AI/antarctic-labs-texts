Rare Delayed Perforation of Central Venous Catheter in Persistent Left Superior Vena Cava: A Case Report
Authors: Shuyi Lyu, Zhou Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn patients with vascular anomalies (e.g., isolated persistent left superior vena cava [IPLSVC]), real‐time ultrasound guidance during central venous catheter (CVC) placement reduces immediate procedural risks but cannot prevent delayed vascular perforation. Post‐procedural imaging remains critical to verify catheter tip position and mitigate complications.

Introduction

Central venous catheterization (CVC) is a cornerstone intervention in critical care management, enabling hemodynamic monitoring, delivery of parenteral nutrition, and administration of vasoactive medications. It is an important diagnostic and therapeutic measure [1]. Clinicians are very familiar with common complications of CVC, such as pneumothorax, catheter‐related infections, and intravascular thrombosis. However, there are few reports on the complications of CVC vascular erosion, with an incidence rate less than 0.4% [2]. The rarity of this complication makes diagnosis more difficult, and misdiagnosis and delayed diagnosis often occur [3]. We herein report a rare case of delayed central venous catheter malposition with milky pleural effusion due to perforation of an innominate vein.

Case History

The patient was admitted to the hospital with complaints of bloating for 1 week, which worsened over the previous day. She denied fever, chills, respiratory symptoms, abdominal pain, or rectal bleeding. One month earlier, she underwent total gastrectomy with D2+ lymphadenectomy, Roux‐en‐Y oesophagojejunostomy, and adhesiolysis for a pathologic diagnosis of poorly differentiated gastric carcinoma (T4aN1M0, pN1[14/24]; components: signet‐ring cell and poorly cohesive subtypes). No history of chronic comorbidities was noted.

Investigations and Treatment

Upon admission, the patient exhibited severe hypoalbuminemia (< 20 g/L) with feeding intolerance and malnutrition. Abdominal ultrasound revealed massive ascites requiring therapeutic paracentesis. A central venous catheter (single lumen, 16G × 20 cm; Guangdong Medical Devices, China) was inserted under real‐time ultrasound guidance via the right internal jugular vein for total parenteral nutrition (TPN) administration, with confirmation of proper placement by blood return. On post‐procedural Day 1, she was given a chest drain because of the increased amount of pleural effusion on her right side, which was also done under ultrasound guidance. Initial drainage yielded clear, yellowish fluid.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

On post‐procedural Day 7, loss of CVC patency (no blood return) coincided with new milky‐white right pleural effusion (biochemically consistent with TPN). The asymptomatic patient underwent emergent cervicothoracic CT, revealing mediastinal gas/fluid and catheter migration. Imaging identified isolated persistent left superior vena cava (IPLSVC) with anomalous 90° angulation between right internal jugular and innominate veins (Figure 1). Contrast‐enhanced CT confirmed mediastinal extravasation without thoracic cavity leakage (Figures 2 and 3), verifying delayed vascular perforation. The CVC was discontinued and replaced by a left cephalic vein peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC).

Milky effusion decreased post‐CVC removal, transitioned to serous fluid by Day 4 and resolved completely by Day 10. The catheter was electively removed on Day 14 without complication. Follow‐up CT demonstrated complete resolution of mediastinal gas/fluid. The patient was discharged asymptomatic on Day 21 with no sequelae.

Discussion

While CVC is a common and relatively safe invasive procedure, it carries risks of serious complications such as vascular erosion and catheter malposition [4, 5]. The present case exemplifies delayed innominate vein perforation in a patient with isolated persistent left superior vena cava (IPLSVC), demonstrating three critical clinical considerations:

First, anatomical anomalies significantly increase procedural risks. IPLSVC occurs in 0.3%–0.5% of the general population [6]. It creates mirror‐image vascular architecture. In this case, the right superior vena cava was absent. This occurs in ≈10% of IPLSVC cases [7]. The absence caused a 90° angle between the right internal jugular vein and innominate vein. This unusual path combines with left mediastinal shift and asymmetric pleural vault levels [8]. Together, they increase catheter tip migration risk. This risk persists even with real‐time ultrasound guidance.

Second, hyperosmotic infusates potentiate vascular injury. TPN solutions increase endothelial permeability and exert chronic mechanical stress on vessel walls through cardiorespiratory motion [9]. These factors likely contributed to the 7‐day delayed perforation manifesting as milky pleural effusion—a pathognomonic sign of TPN extravasation documented in prior reports [10, 11].

Third, standard verification protocols require augmentation in high‐risk scenarios. While ultrasound guidance reduces immediate complications and obviates routine pre/postoperative imaging for radiation/cost reduction [12], this case demonstrates its limitation in detecting delayed migration. The absence of contrast extravasation into the pleural cavity on CT (potentially due to insufficient contrast volume) underscores the diagnostic value of effluent biochemical analysis when radiographic findings are equivocal.

Notably, four factors may have mitigated worse outcomes: (1) low‐pressure venous perforation, (2) use of a narrow‐bore catheter (6F), (3) prompt catheter withdrawal upon symptom recognition, and (4) minimal mediastinal contamination. This favorable outcome emphasizes that when IPLSVC is suspected preoperatively (e.g., from aberrant ultrasound anatomy), echocardiography should be considered to evaluate vascular architecture. Postprocedurally, unexplained clinical changes—particularly new pleural effusions during TPN administration—necessitate immediate imaging to exclude catheter malposition.

Conclusion

Delayed catheter perforation may occur in IPLSVC despite ultrasound guidance. New milky effusion during TPN mandates immediate imaging. Recommendations: (1) Preoperative echocardiography for high‐risk anatomy; (2) Prompt imaging for unexplained symptoms.
