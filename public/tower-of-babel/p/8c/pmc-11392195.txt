Letter to Editor: ‘Association of follow-up neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and systemic inflammation response index with stroke-associated pneumonia and functional outcomes in cerebral hemorrhage patients: a case controlled study’
Authors: Yanfei Yang, Yang Wen, Jun Li
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

We recently read with great interest this article entitled ‘Association of follow-up neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and systemic inflammation response index with stroke- associated pneumonia and functional outcomes in cerebral hemorrhage patients: a case controlled study’. This article focuses on a single-centre study of neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI) as independent diagnostic biomarkers of stroke-associated pneumonia (SAP). The associations between NLR, SIRI, and SAP have been determined and followed up for functional outcome. Finally, a predictive column chart for SAP has been constructed. The NLR and SIRI proposed in this study are important for assessing the diagnostic value of SAP occurring in acute cerebral haemorrhage [intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH)] patients after admission to the hospital1. Here, we still want to go for some insightful thoughts on this.

First of all, this paper is a single-centre clinical study, and this prediction study also has constructed a dynamic nomogram at the end, whose NLR and SIRI have played a good predictive performance for the prediction of SAP, but a single-centre clinical study, whose predictive model has a low clinical reproducibility. Moreover, in this study, a total of 451 patients were included in this study, and according to the Japanese Akaike information principle, the sample of its clinic should be 10 times the included screening predictive variables2. Obviously its sample size is low, the reliability of the data results must be considered, and the predictive performance will be reduced.

Furthermore, the correlation with SAP has been statistically analysed based on neutrophils in this study. According to the latest study, Inflammatory Burden Index (IBI) has the highest C-statistic for predicting the prognosis of patients with cancer, reaching 0.640 (0.617, 0.663). IBI is C-reactive protein (mg/l) × neutrophil (/μl)/lymphocyte (/μl)3. IBI has a significant impact on tumour prognosis, and its impact is the greatest compared to other bioinflammatory markers. If researchers can include it in multicentre studies and explore the level of inflammation in patients in a multifaceted way, the predictive properties of SAP in ICH patients will be qualitatively improved.

Last but not least, the researchers in this paper have shown that NLR and SIRI have important clinical significance in the assessment of inflammation in SAP, which is a more scientific measure of the level of inflammatory load in patients, which is conducive to the clinical guidance of the use of antibiotics and other anti-infective treatments for patients. If the researchers can take the above two factors into account, the clinical persuasiveness of their clinical prediction model is greatly enhanced. We thank the authors once again for their great contribution.
