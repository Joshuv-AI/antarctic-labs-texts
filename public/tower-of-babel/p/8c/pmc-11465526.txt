High prevalence of colonization with extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing and multidrug-resistant Enterobacterales in the community in Addis Ababa Ethiopia: risk factors, carbapenem resistance, and molecular characterization
Authors: Dessie Abera, Abel Abera Negash, Surafel Fentaw, Yonas Mekonnen, Raffaele Joseph Cataldo, Ashenafi Alemu Wami, Adane Mihret, Woldaregay Erku Abegaz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobally, extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales are major causes of hospital-acquired infections and there are increasing concerns about their role in community-acquired infections.ObjectiveWe aimed to investigate the prevalence of extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing Enterobacterales (ESBL-PE) and Carbapenemase-producing-Carbapenemresistant-Enterobacterales (CP-CRE) and associated factors in community settings in Gulele sub city, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 261 healthy individuals. Stool samples were collected and processed using standard microbiological methods. Antimicrobial susceptibility and phenotypic ESBL and carbapenemase tests were performed. Antibiotic resistance genes were detected by Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR).ResultsThe colonization rate of ESBL-PE and CP-CRE were 31.4% (82/261, 95% CI: 25.91–37.48) and 0.8% (2/261, 95% CI: 0.13–3.1), respectively by phenotypic method. Molecular detection of genes for ESBL-PE was 27.9% (73/261, 95% CI:22.7–33.9), and for CP-CRE was 0.8% (2/261, 95% CI: 0.13–3.1). The most prevalent genes were blaTEM [76.7% (56/73)] and blaCTX-M [45.2% (33/73)]. Previous antibiotic use (AOR:2.04, 95%CI: 1.35–4.41, P:0.041) and age between 42 and 53 years old (AOR:3.00, 95%CI:1.12–7.48, P:0.019) were significantly associated with ESBL-PE colonization.ConclusionIntestinal colonization by ESBL-PE harboring the associated antibiotic resistance genes was substantially high but with low CP-CRE. Continued surveillance of community-level carriage of antimicrobial resistance Enterobacterales is warranted.

Introduction

Enterobacterales are the largest family of gram-negative bacteria that live harmlessly as normal flora in the human and animal gut [1]. In particular, E. coli and K. pneumoniae are the predominant Enterobacterales in the gut, which can cause opportunistic nosocomial and community-acquired infections [2]. Mostly, asymptomatic intestinal colonization of such opportunistic bacterial species has the potential to spread to others [3]. One of the main global public health issues is antimicrobial resistance (AMR) of K. pneumoniae and E. coli to β-lactam antibiotics [4]. The mechanism of resistance is primarily by producing extended-spectrum βeta-lactamase (ESBL) and carbapenemases. These enzymes are commonly found on plasmids, which are of particular concern due to the high rate of horizontal gene exchange within the gut bacterial community [5]. Temoneira (TEM), cefotaximase (CTX-M), and sulfhydryl variable (SHV) are frequently reported ESBL genes [6].

Colonization with carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) represents one of the most complex problems in healthcare facilities because of limited treatment options. However, community-associated CRE colonization is very low compared to the healthcare settings [7]. Carbapenemase-producing Carbapenem resistant-Enterobacterales (CP-CRE) are the priority concern due to the ability of the enzyme to degrade most beta-lactam antibiotics [8]. The three most common CP-CRE genes are Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemase (KPC), New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase (NDM) and oxacillinase β-lactamase 48 (OXA-48) [9].

Gut colonization with ESBL-producing E. coli and K. pneumoniae among patients was the focus of many studies until recently [10, 11]. However, current evidence indicated an increased prevalence of gut colonization with ESBL-producing E. coli and K. pneumoniae among healthy individuals. These asymptomatic individuals can serve as a source for antimicrobial resistance transmission and a risk factor for healthcare and community-acquired infections [12]. The misuse and overuse of antibiotics, previous admission history, and travel history have been associated with fecal carriage of ESBL-producing E. coli and K. pneumoniae [13]. The global fecal carriage of ESBL-producing E. coli in healthy individuals ranges from 16.5 to 21.1% [14]. The prevalence of ESBL-producing E. coli and K. pneumoniae ranges from 5.6 to 33.3% among food handlers and hospitalized patients in Ethiopia [15–17]. However, there is limited data on healthy individuals in the community. Therefore, this study sought to determine the prevalence of ESBL-PE and CRE and associated factors in the community settings in the Gulele sub-city, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

A community-based cross-sectional study was carried out from February 2023 to March 2024 in the Gulele sub-city in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Gulele is one of the largest sub-cities with 10 woreda (woreda is the smallest unit of a sub-city), and has 385,332 population, according to the sub-city administrative information.

Sample size determination

A single population proportional formula was used to determine the minimum sample size with 95% confidence interval, 5% precision, and 21.7% prevalence from the previous report in Gondar [15]. Therefore, the final sample size was 261.

Sampling method and data collection

Gulele sub city and the specific study site (Woreda 9) were selected purposively based on its close proximity to the Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI) for ease of specimen transportation. From a total of 20 kebele (the smallest unit of Woreda) one kebele was selected randomly by lottery method. The study households were selected using a systematic random sampling technique from the total household sampling frame which was obtained from kebele administrative office. The first household was randomly selected, and the subsequent households were included based on the kth interval. One individual was selected from the household members, an alternate was made randomly for those who declined to participate or did not meet eligibility criteria.

The primary investigator and two health extension workers participated in the data collection. The study populations selected were all apparently healthy individuals at the ages of 18 years and above. After obtaining informed consent, a face-to-face interview was carried out to collect the socio-demographic characteristics of the participants, as well as factors related to ESBL-PE and CRE colonization such as antibiotic treatment and history of hospital exposure in the past 6 months. People with known chronic illness, diarrhea, cough at the time of the interview, and those with antibiotic treatment during data collection were excluded. The study participants were instructed to give about 20gm of stool samples into a clean leak-proof container. Each stool specimen within the container was gently agitated using a sterile cotton swab applicator stick, after which approximately 1 gram of the stool specimen was placed into Cary-Blair medium (Oxoid Ltd., Basingstoke, UK). The specimens were then transported in Cary-Blair medium in an ice-cold box to the Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI) within four hours of collection, and a revisit was made for those who were unable to give a sample at the time of the interview.

Bacterial isolation and identification

Stool specimens were inoculated onto a MacConkey agar plate (Oxoid Ltd., Basingstoke, UK) and incubated aerobically for 24 h at 37 °C. Bacterial colonies were identified based on lactose fermentation, which could be pink, mucoid, or non-mucoid, and non-lactose fermentation, which was colorless. Additionally, their shape and size were also considered in the process. A single colony from the predominant morphologically similar lactose fermenter colonies was picked on the MacConkey agar and sub cultured onto nutrient agar for biochemical test. Moreover, non-lactose fermenter colonies were sub cultured onto XLD agar (Oxoid Ltd., Basingstoke, UK) for 24 h to observe further characteristics. Species identification was confirmed by isolating a pure colony from the nutrient agar and conducting biochemical tests (Liofilchem Ltd, Roseto degli Abruzzi, Italy) including indole, citrate, lysine, Kligler’s Iron Agar, mannitol, and urea [18]. In addition, bacterial identification was also performed using the VITEK 2 compact system (bioMe´rieux, France).

In this procedure, gram-negative bacterial identification card (ID-GN86) was used for the identification of isolated bacteria based on the manufacturer’s instruction. The bacterial suspension was prepared using an aseptic technique by transferring 3 ml of sterile saline (0.45%) into two clean test tubes. About 3–5 pure colonies were taken from the nutrient agar and transferred into the first saline tube using sterile loop. Then the density of the suspension was measured using turbidity meter until it became equal to 0.50 McFarland standard. Transfer 145 µl of the prepared suspension from the first tube into the second tube. The first bacterial suspension tube was used for identification and the second tube was used for AST.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was done using AST-GN86 card (bioMe´rieux SA, France). Antimicrobials used were: ampicillin, amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, ampicillin/sulbactam, cefazolin, cefuroxime, cefuroxime axetil, ceftazidime, ceftriaxone, cefepime, ertapenem, imipenem, gentamicin, tobramycin, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, tetracycline, nitrofurantoin, and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole. Pure colonies were taken from the nutrient agar and transferred into the test tube using a sterile loop. Then the density of the suspension was measured using a turbidity meter until it became equal to 0.50 McFarland standard. The VITEK 2 compact system performed minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and the results were interpreted as sensitive, intermediate, and resistant.

Phenotypic detection of ESBL production

ESBL production was identified with the VITEK 2 compact system per manufacturer instructions. AST-GN86 cards contain cefotaxime (0.5 µg/ml), ceftazidime (0.5 µg/ml), cefepime (1 µg/ml), and β-lactam inhibitors such as cefotaxime/Clavulanic Acid (0.5/4 µg/ml), ceftazidime/Clavulanic Acid (0.5/4 µg/ml) and cefepime/Clavulanic Acid (1/10 µg/ml).

Phenotypic detection of carbapenemase production

Imipenem and/or ertapenem-resistant isolates were candidates for testing carbapenemase production by modified Carbapenem Inactivation Method (mCIM) and result interpretation was made based on CLSI 2023 guideline [19]. Briefly, one to four loops full of CRE isolates were suspended in 2 mL of tryptic soy broth; and 10 µg meropenem disk was submerged in each tube and incubated at ambient temperature for 4 h ± 15 min. Fresh carbapenem susceptible ATCC25922 E. coli was suspended with sterile saline and checked for its equivalence to a 0.50 McFarland standard, which was followed by spreading the suspension onto Muller-Hinton agar. After that, the meropenem disk was removed from each tube and placed on the inoculated Muller-Hinton agar, and incubated at 35 °C ± 2 °C for 18–24 h. Inhibition zone with a diameter of 6–15 mm or the presence of pinpoint colonies within16-18 mm were determined as positive for carbapenemase enzyme production. A zone of inhibition ≥ 19 mm was considered to be negative for carbapenemase enzyme production. A zone diameter of 16 to 18 mm or a zone diameter of ≥ 19 mm and the presence of pinpoint colonies within the zone were classified as indeterminate, requiring additional testing to determine whether carbapenemase production is present or absent.

Molecular detection of ESBL and CP-CRE genes

DNA extraction

DNA was extracted from ESBL and CRE-positive fresh isolates by heat lysis method [20]. A loop full (1µL loop) of fresh colonies was suspended in 100 µL Tris-acetate-EDTA (TAE) buffer and heated at 100 °C for 10 min; then, placed at room temperature for 5 min to cool down; and centrifuged at 14,000 g for 5 min at 4 °C. About 50 µL of the supernatant was transferred into a nuclease-free Eppendorf tube and stored at-20 °C until analysis for a short time and at -80 °C for a long period.

PCR amplification of resistance genes

Conventional multiplex PCR was used to detect ESBL and carbapenemase resistance genes at the Armauer Hansen Research Institute (AHRI), Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. We used the PCR protocol from previous works [21, 22]. Eight β-lactamase genes were grouped into two, and were detected by multiplexing four genes, and eleven carbapenemase genes were also multiplexed by four genes. Amplification reactions were performed in a final volume of 25µL containing 12.5µL 2x Tag plus PCR Master Mix (Thermo Scientific, Lithunia, EU), 1µL of DNA primers, 5.5µL of nuclease-free water, and finally, 5µL of the template DNA preparation were added to the reaction mixture. Reactions were run in a T100 DNA thermal cycler (BIO-RAD) under the following conditions: denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 20 s, 61 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min with a final extension of 72 °C for 7 min. After PCR amplification, 2.5µL of each reaction was removed for separation and gel detection of the amplicons by electrophoresis which was run in 1.5% agarose gel for 50 min at 120 V in 0.5 × TAE buffer. DNA was stained with ethidium bromide (1.5 µg/mL), and the bands were detected using a UV transilluminator (Cleaver Scientific Ltd, Rugby, UK).

Quality control

Expiry date of the media, reagents, and antibiotic disks was checked before use, and culture media were inspected visually for cracks and thickness; and for the presence of freezing, bubbles, and contaminants. Media sterility was checked by incubating E. coli 25,922 and K. pneumoniae 700,603 ATCC controls at 35–37 °C for 24 h of incubation. ESBL detection was also controlled using ATCC 25,922 E. coli and ATCC 700,603 K. pneumoniae as negative and positive control, respectively. For carbapenemase detection, K. pneumoniae ATCC BAA-1705 as positive and K. pneumoniae ATCC BAA-1706 as negative control were used based on CLSI guideline [19]. E. coli strains bearing blaTEM, blaSHV and blaCTX-M were used as positive controls. In addition, known K. pneumoniae strains carrying blaKPC, blaNDM and blaOXA-48 were used as positive controls.

Operational definitions

CP-CRE

Enterobacterales that can produce carbapenemase enzymes and are resistant to carbapenem antibiotics.

Colonization rate or carriage rate

The presence of ESBL-PE and CRE in the gut of adults in the absence of infection.

ESBL-PE

Enterobacterales that can produce extended spectrum beta-lactamase.

Healthy individuals

Individuals who did not show any clinical symptoms of the disease during the study period.

MDR

Multidrug resistance is defined as non-susceptible to at least 1 agent in > 3 different antimicrobial classes [23].

Statistical analysis

All data were encoded in Microsoft Excel 2016 and exported to SPSS version 25 software. We used descriptive statistics to describe the proportion of bacterial isolates. A binary logistic regression model was performed to determine the association between ESBL colonization and associated factors. Moreover, all variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable analysis to account for possible confounding variables. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants

A total of 261 study participants were recruited, 59.8% (156/261) were females, and the participants’ median age was 44. The majority of the study participants, 40.6% (106/261) were in the age group of 42–53 years. Regarding the marital status of the study participants, 79.3% (207/261) of them had married. About 62% (162/261) of them had 2–3 family size. Most of the study participants, 57.1% (149/261), had college and above level of education (Table 3).

Distribution of Enterobacterales

A total of 273 Enterobacterales isolates were obtained from the 261 healthy participants. The common bacteria were E. coli, 92.6% (253/273); and K. pneumoniae, 4% (11/273); followed by Enterobacter cloacae, 1.5% (4/273); Citrobacter freundii, 0.7% (2/273) and Proteus mirabilis 0.4% (1/273). Mixed bacterial species were also found among two study participants (1 E. coli and 1 K. pneumoniae from one participant, 1 C. freundii and 1 E. coli from another participant) (Fig. 1).

The carriage rate of ESBL-PE and CP-CRE

The overall intestinal ESBL-PE and CP-CRE colonization rate were 31.4% (82/261,95%CI: 25.91–37.48) and 0.8% (2/261, 95%CI: 0.13–3.1) by phenotypic detection, respectively. From the total 82 ESBL-positive isolates, E. coli was the predominant ESBL producer, 96.3% (79/82) followed by K. pneumoniae at 3.7% (3/82). Out of 253 E. coli isolates, 31.2% (79/253) were ESBL positive. About 27.3% (3/11) of K. pneumoniae isolates were ESBL-positive, and carbapenemase production was found in two K. pneumoniae isolates (Fig. 2).

Frequency of ESBL-producing and non-ESBL-producing E. coli and K pneumoniae and CP-CRE among healthy individuals from February 2023 to March 2024 at Gulele sub city, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia (n = 261)

Antimicrobial susceptibility profile

ESBL-producing E. coli and K. pneumoniae were 100% resistant to cefazolin and cefuroxime. ESBL-producing E. coli was 100% sensitive to ertapenem and/or imipenem, while two [66.7% (2/3)] ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae showed resistance to these antibiotics. These two CRE isolates were tested for carbapenemase production and both were found positive. Furthermore, ESBL-producing E. coli and K. pneumoniae were less sensitive to non-beta-lactam antibiotics such as tetracycline at the rate of 81% (64/79) and 66.7% (2/3), respectively. However, non-ESBL-producing E. coli and K. pneumoniae showed low resistance to all tested antibiotics which range from 0 to 25% (Table 4).

Multidrug resistance

Out of the total E. coli and K. pneumoniae isolates, 28.8% (76/264) of ESBL and non-ESBL-producing E. coli and K. pneumoniae were resistant to three or more classes of antimicrobial agents (MDR). Out of these ESBL-producing MDR E. coli and K. pneumoniae, 88% (67/76) was E. coli, while 2.6% (2/76) was K. pneumoniae. A higher proportion of MDR was found among ESBL-producing E. coli isolates [84.8% (67/79)]. On the other hand, the remaining [9.2% (7/76)] of the total MDR isolates were non-ESBL E. coli isolates (Table 5).

Frequency of antibiotic resistance genes

Among the total 82 phenotypic ESBL-PE, 89% (73/82) of the isolates were positive for at least one ESBL gene. The predominant gene was blaTEM [76.7% (56/73)] followed by blaCTX-M [45.2% (33/73)] and blaSHV [20.5% (15/73)]. The coexistence of ESBL genes was detected in the proportion of 23.2% (17/73), 10.9% (8/73), and 2.7% (2/73) for blaTEM + blaCTX-M, blaTEM + blaSHV and blaCTX-M + blaSHV, respectively (Fig. 3). blaNDM [66.7% (2/3)] and blaOXA-48 [66.7% (2/3)] genes were identified only in K. pneumoniae (Fig. 3). Bands from amplicons produced from genes of interest using gel of electrophoresis were demonstrated (Fig. 4).

Factors associated with fecal ESBL-PE colonization among healthy individuals

In this study, different possible factors for intestinal carriage of ESBL-PE were assessed. The majority of the factors such as sex, family size, occupation, education status, marital status, previous hospital exposure, source of drinking water, and habit of raw meat consumption were not significantly associated with the carriage of ESBL-PE (P > 0.05). However, previous antibiotic use (COR = 2.21, 95%CI: 1.53–4.65, P = 0.038) and age group of 42–53 years old (COR = 3.16, 95% CI = 1.92–7.23, P = 0.013) showed significant association with ESBL positivity (Table 6).

Multivariate analysis was performed for factors that showed significant association at the crude odds ratio. Accordingly, people with a history of antibiotic consumption in the last 6 months were 2.04 times more likely to be ESBL-PE positive than those who had no history of antibiotic consumption (AOR = 2.04, 95% CI = 1.35–4.41, P = 0.041). A significant association was also found between the age group of 42–53 years old and ESBL-PE positivity (AOR = 2.99, 95% CI = 1.12–7.84, P = 0.019) (Table 7).

Discussion

The escalation of ESBL-PE and CRE in healthy individuals is a growing global concern, as it can poses a threat to hospital patients and people in the community [24]. Therefore, detection of intestinal carriage of ESBL-PE and CP-CRE at the community level is significant in reducing the transmission of antibiotic-resistant bacteria.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first community-level research regarding the detection of intestinal ESBL-PE and CP-CRE colonization among apparently healthy individuals in Ethiopia. In this study, the colonization rate of ESBL-PE was 31.4% (82/261, 95% CI: 25.91–37.48) by phenotypic method, which is comparable to previous studies among food handlers from Dilla, Ethiopia (25.3%) [16]; in Chad among healthy people (38%) [11], Nepal, among university students (29.5%) [24] and Taiwan, among healthy adults (27.4%). However, it was higher than a report among food handlers in Gondar, Ethiopia (21.7%) [15]; in Tanzania among community individuals (16.5%) [25], Côte d’Ivoire among healthy individuals (14.6%) [26], in Burkina Faso among healthy people (22%) [27], and Spain among health workers (3.1%) [28]. On the other hand, it was much lower than the rate reported in Tanzania among pregnant women at 64.3% [29], and Indonesian study among healthy people (66.7%) [30]. The discrepancy could be due to differences in study population and geographical location. For example, the previous report recognized E. coli and K. pneumoniae as the most common ESBL-PE, and their distribution varied from place to place even within the same geographical location [31]. Another probable reason might be due to the variability and inconsistent ESBL detection methods [32].

The present study’s detection rates of ESBL-producing E. coli [96.3% (79/82)] and K. pneumoniae [3.7% (3/82)] were in line with a study in Nepal among university students where E. coli was 95.7% and K. pneumoniae 4.3% [24]. In our finding, ESBL-producing E. coli detection was much higher than that of ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae (96.3%vs3.7%). However, the discrepancy between the detection rate of ESBL-producing E. coli from other studies was not as big as it was in the present study from Gondar among food handlers (ESBL-producing E. coli 68% vs. K. pneumoniae 27% [15] and from Côte d’Ivoire among healthy people (ESBL producing E. coli 46.7% vs. K. pneumoniae 17.3%) [26]. The higher prevalence of ESBL-producing E. coli in our finding might be related to food sources and person-to-person transmission through the fecal-oral route [33]. ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae has been also associated with these routes of transmission but at a low rate compared to ESBL-producing E. colie [34].

The rise of CP-CRE has been connected to healthcare-associated infections [35, 36]. A systematic review study in Ethiopia from isolates obtained from different clinical samples reported a pooled prevalence of CP-CRE at 5.44%, ranging from 1 to 30.5% [37]. In our result, the phenotypic detection rate of CP-CRE was 0.8% (2/261, 95% CI: 0.13–3.1); and the only carbapenemase producer was found to be K. pneumoniae. This low detection rate was also observed in previous studies such as from Gondar, Ethiopia (2.4%) among food handlers [15]; India (6.1%) among healthy individuals [36] and China among healthy people (4.26%) [35]. The variation could be due to differences in sample size, study population and geographical location where India and China are countries with high antimicrobial consumers and high antibiotic resistance reports [38].

The present study has also assessed the phenotypic antimicrobial resistance pattern of E. coli and K. pneumoniae to different antimicrobial agents. Accordingly, ESBL-producing E. coli and K. pneumoniae were highly resistant [80.5% (66/82)] to antimicrobial agents compared to the non-ESBL-producing E. coli and K. pneumoniae [20.3% (37/182)]. ESBL-producing E. coli was 95% and 100% resistant to cefuroxime and cefazolin, respectively, whereas K. pneumoniae was 100% resistant to both of these antibiotics. This high resistance rate could be due to the frequent use of β-lactam antibiotics without prescription in the community, and this practice can create selective pressure on the gut flora to develop antibiotic resistance [2].

The ESBL-producing E. coli and K. pneumoniae isolates from this study were also resistant to non-beta lactam antibiotics such as tetracycline (80.5%), cotrimoxazole (26.8%) and ciprofloxacin (29.3%), which was in line with previous report [16]. The possible reason for this resistance could be related to overuse and misuse of antibiotics or it might be because the resistance genes to these antibiotics are also found on the same plasmid [39].

In this study, Imipenem and/or Ertapenem were 100% effective against both ESBL and non-ESBL-producing E. coli, which was in line with previous reports in Nepal among healthy individuals [24, 40]. In contrast, ESBL and carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae from the current study were resistant to Ertapenem and imipenem (66.7%); however, this may not indicate the true picture due to the small number of isolates detected. This result was much lower than a study in India among healthy people where 89% (57/64) isolates were shown to be carbapenemase positive [36]. The difference between our report and the one from India shown above might not be surprising since the number of CP-CRE isolates in our study was very small.

The overall prevalence of MDR from this study was 28.8% (76/264, 95% CI: 23.49–34.72), of which 88% (67/76) was due to ESBL-producing E. coli. This result was similar to a report from Nepal in healthy adults, 23% [41], but lower than from Gondar among food handlers (42.3%) [15] and 99.9% among the healthy community in Malaysia [42].

Phenotypic detection of ESBL-PE and CP-CRE is still significant in low-income countries like Ethiopia; however, it lacks specificity as a result of which it requires the use of molecular methods as a confirmatory test [43]. In our study, the detection rate of ESBL-PE intestinal colonization rate by the molecular method was 27.9% (73/261, 95% CI:22.7–33.9). From the total 82 phenotypically ESBL-positive isolates, 9 isolates were negative by molecular testing.

Antibiotic resistance genes such as TEM, SHV, and CTX-M are the predominant ESBL genes and were mainly present in E. coli and K. pneumoniae [44]. Like the case in the phenotypic detection, E. coli was the preeminent bacteria in the current study that harbored these ESBL genes followed by K. pneumoniae, although at a very small rate. The most frequently detected ESBL gene from both E. coli and K. pneumoniae was TEM (76.7%) followed by CTX-M (45.2%) and SHV (20.5%). However, CTX-M was reported as widely distributed in healthcare and community settings [45, 46]. Our results were concordant with the findings from Nepal among university students, where they reported TEM (92%), CTX-M (60%), and SHV (4%) [41], and from Indonesia among school children, TEM (92.6%), SHV (38.8%), and CTX-M (4.3%) [47]. In contrast, other studies showed CTX-M as the most prevalent gene from rural Nepal (80%) [46], and from school children in China (27.1%) [48]. These inconsistent results could be due to geographical location and study population.

Moreover, coexistence of ESBL genes was observed to be a common occurrence in this study, where 23.2% of the isolates carried both TEM + CTX-M and 10.9% of the isolates carrying TEM + SHV. Such findings with the co-existence of ESBL genes were also reported from Nepal with frequencies of TEM + CTX-M (48%), TEM + SHV (4%) [41], and from Indonesia with TEM and SHV (29%) [47]. These combinations can be explained by the fact that several genes can be encoded in a single plasmid. We also observed that only two K. pneumoniae isolates were found to have carried NDM, OXA-48, and VIM genes, whereas none were found to harbor KPC gene. This was in line with previous findings that NDM was a predominant carbapenemase gene in K. pneumoniae [35].

International travel has been associated with ESBL-PE colonization [49], however, age, sex, previous antibiotic use, and previous hospital exposure have inconsistent associations with ESBL-PE colonization [12]. In the present study, those who took antibiotics in the past 6 months were two times more likely to harbor ESBL-PE than those who didn’t take antibiotics (AOR = 2.04, 95% CI = 0.95–4.4, P = 0.041). Misuse and overuse, purchasing antibiotics without prescription, and lack of awareness about antibiotic resistance in the community could contribute to this significant association. In line with this thought, a study in Ethiopia has reported that 67.7% of pharmacy professionals during the study period sold antibiotics without a prescription [50]. While a systematic review has linked previous antibiotic intake to be significantly associated with ESBL-PE carriage [51], a different study among healthy individuals from Indonesia contradicted this finding [30]. Moreover, 42–53 years participants were three times more likely to acquire ESBL-PE (AOR = 3.00, 95% CI = 0.96–7.48, P = 0.019). This association could be due to the majority of our study participants being in this age group. The result was incongruent with a study in India which reported age not to be a factor in ESBL-PE colonization [52].

Limitations

This study has used a questionnaire to collect potential factors of ESBL-PE colonization, but care must be taken in interpreting and drawing conclusions because of the recall bias of the respondents. In addition, findings that showed association with ESBL-PE positivity must be interpreted cautiously because of the cross-sectional study design which makes it difficult to attribute causality to the observed association. The inability to include Acinetobacter species and Pseudomonas aeruginosa was another limitation of this study, which may have contributed to the low prevalence of CP-CRE.

Conclusion

The prevalence of gut colonization with ESBL-producing E. coli and K. pneumoniae among healthy adults in our study was significantly high. TEM and CTX-M, as well as carbapenemase genes such as NDM and OXA-48, were detected in these healthy participants, which may indicate a potential threat to the community for developing infections by drug-resistant members of the Enterobacterales. ESBL-PE colonization was significantly associated with history of antibiotic use in the previous 6 months and ages between 42 and 53 years old. The fact that nine phenotypically ESBL-positive isolates did not carry the genes tested might indicate the involvement of unknown genes in this situation, which calls for further investigation using sequencing to understand the type of clones that are spreading within the community.
