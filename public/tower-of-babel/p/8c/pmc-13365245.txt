Elovanoid neuroprotection targets cell transcriptomics and proteomics to sustain synaptic integrity after brain injury
Authors: Brian L Giles, Surjyadipta Bhattacharjee, Jeff X Ji, Pranab K Mukherjee, Ludmila Belayev, Carlos M Vieira, Nicolas G Bazan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraumatic brain injury (TBI), a leading cause of death and disability, is the largest non-genetic, non-aging-related contributor to cognitive impairments. Currently, there are limited effective therapies to protect neurons after acute brain injury. Our results suggest that intranasal-delivered (IN) elovanoid (ELV) shortly after TBI elicits neuroprotection that involves synaptic and mitochondrial pathways that mediate neuroprotection. Using a single-cell multiome approach, we found an upregulation of genes involved in synaptic integrity. Furthermore, we discovered that ELVs improve synaptosomal mitochondrial function, reduce lipid peroxidation, and increase the activity of antioxidant transcriptional programs, including the NRF2 pathway, in neurons. We suggest that these changes, together with the induction of cell-type-specific gene regulation in glutamatergic neurons and other cells, underlie ELV-elicited neuroprotection.Subject terms: Neuroscience, Diseases of the nervous system

Introduction

Each year, ~70 million people worldwide suffer from traumatic brain injuries (TBI)1–5. There are no effective TBI therapies available4 and current treatment involves occupational therapy and medication to control symptoms such as headaches or anxiety. After TBI, more than one-third of individuals develop long-term disabilities6–9, being the largest non-genetic, non-aging-related cognitive impairments10–12, and Alzheimer’s disease (AD)-like dementia10.

Elovanoids (ELVs) are stereospecific dihydroxylated derivatives of 32:6, omega-3 or 34:6, omega-3 fatty acids that yield ELV-N32 and ELV-N34, respectively13–15. ELV precursors are biosynthesized by neuronal-specific ELOVL4 (elongation of very long-chain fatty acids protein 4) and utilize docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) or eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) to form ≥28-carbon fatty acids. Moreover, ELOVL4 mutations are associated with seizures, intellectual disability, and neurodegenerative disorders12,14–22. ELVs elicit protection in uncompensated oxidative stress (UOS), oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD), and N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA)-induced excitotoxicity in primary cultures of human neurons/astrocytes14,15.

The development of neuroprotective drugs has proven difficult due to, among other factors, the blood–brain barrier (BBB), which limits drug penetration. To overcome this limitation, we administered ELV intranasally (IN)4,23, which uses the olfactory and trigeminal nerve tracts as a transport route to the brain23. IN delivery has been utilized effectively in animal models of TBI24. Additionally, IN-delivered ELV precursors activate integrated inflammatory signaling, improve behavior, decrease lesion size after experimental stroke in rats25, and protect retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells and photoreceptors15. Using two rat TBI models, intravenous (IV) or IN delivery of ELVs reduced lesion size, improved neurobehavioral outcomes, and corpus callosum (CC) integrity as evidenced by ex vivo MRI26. Specifically, IN-ELV reduced neurological deficits, with improvements as early as 1 day and sustained up to 14 days post-TBI. T2-weighted imaging revealed a reduction in ventricle enlargement and cortical thinning following IN-ELV. Notably, ELV concentrations were higher in the ipsilateral cortex compared to the contralateral cortex after IN delivery, highlighting the targeted delivery and accessibility of this method26. Furthermore, ELV treatment was found to reduce axonal damage and preserve white matter tracts26.

The therapeutic potential of nutritional interventions of omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (n-3-PUFAs) and their derivatives, such as DHA, for TBI by mitigating neuroinflammation, necroptosis, and mitochondrial dysfunction has been suggested27,28. Repeated and prolonged supplementation enhances long-term cognitive recovery and tissue repair, with broader implications for aging, neurodegeneration, and visual and emotional deficits29. Nutritional strategies, including DHA-rich interventions, are recognized as valuable adjuncts in TBI treatment30–35.

Building upon these findings, the current study examines the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying ELV’s neuroprotective effects at the single-cell level to understand gene regulatory changes that promote synaptic protection and neuronal survival. Given that ELOVL4 is a neuronal-specific enzyme that generates ELV precursors, we hypothesize that ELV exerts its protection by modulating neuronal responses to injury. Therefore, we focus on gene regulation and protein abundance underlying the acute neuroprotection mediated by IN-ELV delivered shortly after TBI in rats. The broader neuroprotective relevance of our findings after TBI is supported by the effect of ELV on proinflammatory immune cell infiltration, lipid peroxidation, gene transcription related to synapse integrity, and synaptosomal mitochondrial function. In addition, our results suggest that ELVs increase the activity of Nuclear Factor Erythroid-2-related factor 2 (NRF2) and the Activator Protein-1 (AP-1) family transcription factor (TF) in glutamatergic neurons post-TBI.

Results

The results are from intranasally (IN) delivered ELVs (Elovanoid-N34) after a controlled cortical impact (CCI) model of TBI, which we refer to as TBI.

Reduced neurobehavioral deficits, neuroinflammation, lipid peroxidation, and neuronal loss

Sprague-Dawley (SD) male young rats were subjected to TBI to the cortex using a CCI model and received vehicle (Veh) or IN 20 µg of ELV at 1 and 24 h after injury. Behavioral scoring, assessed by a composite neurological assessment (normal = 0, maximal deficit = 12), was performed 1 h after CCI and then every 24 h for up to 3 days. We observed statistically significant improvements in ELV-treated rats on Days 2 and 3 after TBI compared to Veh-treated controls (Fig. 1A). There was no significant difference between Veh and ELV at 1 h after injury, which suggests that the acute injury severity was equivalent between Veh and the ELV group prior to treatment.

To evaluate immune cell infiltration, brain sections at the injury site were immunostained for CD68, a marker for monocytes and macrophages. Representative images from Veh (Fig. 1B) and ELV (Fig. 1C) show a qualitative reduction in CD68+ cells in the ELV group 3 days after TBI. Quantification of CD68+ cell counts in the lesion and perilesional area confirmed a reduction in total immune cells in the injury area of ELV-treated animals compared to vehicle-treated animals on day 3 (Fig. 1D).

Then, we assessed lipid peroxidation and neuronal survival by immunostaining brain sections with 4-Hydroxynonenal (4HNE), an indicator of lipid peroxidation, and NeuN, following Veh and ELV treatment (Fig. 1E and F). We observed a reduction in 4HNE immunoreactivity within the lesion area in the ELV group compared to the vehicle group 3 days after TBI (Fig. 1G). Additionally, in the area of neuronal loss, characterized by the absence of NeuN+ cells in the cortex (Fig. 1G), we noted a similar reduction 3 days after TBI. These behavioral and immunostaining data support ELV neuroprotection after TBI by decreasing immune cell infiltration, preventing lipid peroxidation, and promoting neuronal survival.

Single-cell transcriptomics reveals cell-specific changes that sustain synaptic integrity and improve mitochondrial function

To gain an understanding of the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying ELV action following TBI, we performed single-cell multiome transcriptomic and epigenetic evaluation. The right (injured) cortex was harvested from TBI rats receiving vehicle or ELV, or from sham-operated rats, at 3 days post-injury. Nuclei were isolated and processed (10x Genomics Multiome protocol, Fig. 1I). After quality control (Supplementary Fig. 1A–F), a total of 25,148 cells were analyzed, comprising 8056 cells from Sham, 10,698 cells from Veh, and 6394 cells from ELV treatment. Cell clustering was performed in Seurat after SCTransform normalization, and cell type annotation was done by mapping the sequencing data to the Allen Brain Atlas using the Whole Mouse Brain database and a hierarchical clustering method. Seurat clustering results are available in Supplementary Fig. 1G–L. The automated cell annotation output was manually adjusted to reveal broad cell classifications, including glutamatergic neurons (Glut Neurons), GABAergic neurons (GABA Neurons), glial cells, immune cells, and vascular cell clusters, alongside specific annotations from the atlas (Fig. 1J). No batch effects were noted, and no batch correction integration procedure was performed. Clustering for each individual sample is available in Supplementary Fig. 2. To validate these cell type annotations, the top three gene markers for each identified cell type were determined and plotted (Fig. 1K). The top markers for each cluster included: Tafa1, Fstl4, and Cntn3 for L2/3 Glut; Slc1a3, Atp1a2, and Gja1 for Astrocytes; Mog, Apod, and Plp1 for Oligodendrocytes (Olig); Grm8, Ano3, and Pde1a for L6 Glut; Kcnh5, Rorb, and Zmat4 for L4/5 Glut; Ctss, C1qa, and C1qb for Microglia; Vcan, Sox6, and Cdh9 for Oligodendrocyte Progenitor Cells (OPCs); Nxph1, Rbms3, and Cntnap4 for Parvalbumin-expressing GABAergic interneurons (Pvalb GABA); Sst, Grin3a, and Synpr for Somatostatin-expression GABAergic interneurons (Sst GABA); Tox, Etv1, and Sorl1 for L5 Glut; Rarb, Rgs9, and Gnal for Striatum GABAergic interneurons (STR GABA); Vip, Adarb2, and Slc44a5 for vasoactive intestinal peptide-expressing GABAergic interneurons (Vip GABA); Cdc45.1, Pltp, and Sclo1a4 for endothelial cells (Endo); Gad1, Zfp385d, and Grik1 for lysosome-associated membrane protein 5-expressing GABAergic interneurons (Lamp5 GABA); Bmp6, Dab2, and Cped1 for vascular leptomeningeal cells (VLMC); Chst9, Pbx3, and Scn5a for a general cluster of GABAergic neurons (GABA); Galnt14, Nr4a2, and Tmem163 for a cluster of subcortical glutamatergic neurons (Subcortex Glut); and Ebf1, Vtn, and Atp13a5 for pericytes (Peri).

Focused comparative analysis was conducted on microglia, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, OPCs, glutamatergic neurons, and GABAergic neurons. Differential expression (DE) analysis was made to identify the top differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each cell type by comparing ELV to Veh or Sham to Veh. Volcano plots for each comparison highlighted the significance, direction, and magnitude of change (Fig. 2A–F). In microglia (Fig. 2A), genes such as Camk2n1, Cox4il, Calm1.1, Stmn3, and Ano3 were decreased, while Erbb4 was increased in Veh compared to the Sham. With ELV treatment, Cntn5, Unc5d, and Tafa2 were decreased, while Camk2n1, Fth1, Spp1, and Adgb were increased compared to the vehicle control. Similar detailed DEG changes were observed for Astrocytes (Fig. 2B), Oligodendrocytes (Fig. 2C), OPCs (Fig. 2D), Glutamatergic neurons (Fig. 2E), and GABAergic neurons (Fig. 2F). The total number of significant genes between Sham and Veh, as well as between Veh and ELV for each major cell type, was tabulated (Fig. 2G, H). Notably, Glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons generally exhibited the highest number of DEGs. All statistical outputs, such as average Log2FC, p-value, and p-adjusted value for the DEGs, are available in Supplementary Dataset 1.

Cell type-specific functional pathway analysis depicts mitochondrial synaptic dysfunctions and uncovers synaptic rescue

To define the functional relevance of the DEGs within each cell type, pathway analysis was conducted by Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Processes (BP) database within the ClusterProfiler package. The top 10 significant pathways from DEGs were defined for Sham vs. Veh comparisons and Veh vs. ELV comparisons in Microglia (Fig. 3A), Astrocytes (Fig. 3B), Oligodendrocytes (Fig. 3C), OPCs (Fig. 3D), Glutamatergic Neurons (Fig. 3E), and GABAergic Neurons (Fig. 3F) to identify biological processes affected by TBI after Veh or ELV intervention. Across all cell types, the top pathways altered in Veh compared to Sham were primarily related to cognition, synaptic function, and mitochondrial function. Importantly, the top pathways altered in ELV compared to Veh were predominantly related to cognition and synaptic function, indicating that ELV action recovers the transcriptomic signatures of synaptic function that TBI disrupted.

ELV rescue of cell-type-specific pathways, including mitochondrial and synaptic dysfunctions after TBI.

Gene Ontology (GO)–Biological Process (BP) pathway analysis results in Microglia (A), Astrocyte (B), Oligodendrocytes (Oligo) (C), Oligodendrocyte Precursor Cells (OPC) (D), Glutamatergic (Glut) Neuron (E), and GABA Neuron (F) clusters for Vehicle (Veh) vs. Sham (left of each panel) and for ELV vs. Veh (right of each panel).

In glutamatergic neurons, top pathways in TBI vs. Sham comparisons included oxidative phosphorylation and aerobic respiration, while ELV vs. Veh comparisons involved pathways of synapse organization and assembly (Fig. 3E). Across cell types, there was a consistent recovery of synapse-related genes in the ELV group that were downregulated in TBI vs. Sham. Furthermore, additional genes were activated by IN ELV that were not highly expressed in Sham, suggesting that ELV intervention upregulates genes that sustain synaptic function even in the presence of TBI-induced neuronal and axonal injury.

To establish specific genes in synaptic dysfunction following TBI and synaptic recovery with ELV treatment, genes from relevant GO pathways were identified, and their scaled expression was plotted across conditions for microglia, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, OPCs, glutamatergic neurons, and GABAergic neurons (Supplementary Fig. 3). In microglia, Apoe and Trem2 were upregulated in Veh vs. Sham, and ELV restored lower expression. Furthermore, genes critical to synapse integrity, such as Nrg1, Efna5, Nrcam, Sparcl1, and Grin2b, are downregulated in the Veh group compared to the Sham group but restored by ELV. Additionally, other genes critical to synapse integrity, such as Erbb4, Nrxn1, Nrxn3, Ntrk2, and Ptprd, were upregulated in ELV compared to both Sham and Veh. In astrocytes, Apoe was upregulated in Veh compared to Sham, and its lower expression was restored by ELV, similar to what was observed in microglia. Some genes from the GO pathways that were decreased in Veh compared to Sham but restored with ELV were Efna5, Nrg1, Sparcl1, Asic2, and Grin2a. Additionally, ELV-induced genes in astrocytes involved in synaptic integrity included Ntrk2, Nrxn1, Nrxn3, Robo1, and Nrcam. In oligodendrocytes, expression was lost in the vehicle (Veh), but restored by ELV in genes including Sparcl1, Nrg1, Nrxn1, Ntrk2, and Efna5. Upregulation of Robo1, Erbb4, Ptprd, Ptk2, and Cttnbp2 was observed in ELV compared to both Sham and Veh. In OPCs, the expression of genes including Nrg1, Efna5, and Sparcl1 was decreased in the Veh group compared to the Sham group, but was restored by ELV. Upregulation of Ptk2, Asic2, Nrxn3, Ntrk2, and Robo1 was observed in ELV compared to both Sham and Veh. Across glial populations, as seen above, gene changes are conserved, indicating that ELV acts on synaptic processes and does not individually target a specific cell type.

In glutamatergic neurons, analysis of synaptic pathways revealed that ELV induced genes involved in synaptic maintenance and integrity including Nrxn1, Nrcam, Abgrb3, Ctnnd2, Ctnna2, Clstn2, Cntnap2, Ptprd, Ptprt, and Efna2 involved in synaptic adhesion and cell–cell interactions, Gria4, Grin2b, Grin1, Asic2, Gpr158, Ntrk2, and Nrg1 that participates in neurotransmitter function, Grip1, Ppfia2, Sipa1l1, Dgkb, and Cacna1a involved in synaptic trafficking, Map2, Rock1, Pak2, Arhgap22, Fat3, and Cux1 engaged in spine morphogenesis, and Caprin1, Rbfox2, Tnik, Hecw1, and Camk2a involved in regulation of synaptic proteins and plasticity. In GABAergic neurons upregulation in ELV of Nrcam, Adgrb3, Ntrk2, Ctnna2, Rock1, Ctnnd2, Ppfia2, Arhgap22, Gria4, Nrxn1, Clstn2, Grip1, Cntnap2, Gpr158, Ptprd, Caprin1, Grin1, Sipa1l1, Asic2, Rbfox2, Ptprt, Hecw1, Map2, Cacna1a, Grin2b, and Pak2 was observed similarly to glutamatergic neurons. Robo1, Efna5, Ptk2, and Cttnbp2, which were observed in glial cells, were upregulated in ELV in GABAergic neurons, but not glutamatergic neurons.

Overall, pathway analysis conducted in individual cell types revealed gene expression differences associated with the maintenance and restoration of synaptic integrity induced by ELV.

Improvement of synaptosomal mitochondria function

Given that our single-cell pathway analysis indicated mitochondrial disruptions in TBI, we investigated whether synaptic mitochondrial function would be improved after TBI following IN ELV compared to Veh. The data on respirometry analysis on isolated synaptosomes from rats 1 day after TBI using the Agilent Seahorse XF96 system is shown in Fig. 4A. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were measured by the Agilent Seahorse XF96 workflow in the cortex (Fig. 4B) and hippocampus (Fig. 4C). Both regions demonstrated an increase in OCR and ECAR with ELV treatment compared to Veh. Specifically, basal respiration (Fig. 4D), maximal respiration (Fig. 4E), and ATP production (Fig. 4F) measurements revealed a significant increase in response to ELV treatment compared to the vehicle, indicating an improvement in mitochondrial function.

ATAC-seq transcriptomics reveals activation of antioxidant defenses and neuronal survival

As shown above, ELV reduces lipid peroxidation, enhances synaptic mitochondrial respiration, and improves neuronal survival. We sought to define the transcriptional regulators engaged. Using single-cell ATAC-seq, TF binding was indirectly assessed by evaluating chromatin accessibility at known TF consensus sequence motifs found in the JASPAR2020 vertebrate database. The top 15 differentially accessible motifs between Veh and ELV in neurons were mapped (Fig. 5A). For the antioxidant defense gene NRF1/NRF2, its consensus motif (Fig. 5B) was found to be most active in Glutamatergic neurons compared to other cell types (Fig. 5C).

To further analyze NRF1/NRF2, we employed TF foot printing, which surveys chromatin accessibility of known TF motifs. Areas of high predicted TF activity show elevated Tn5 enrichment in genomic regions adjacent to a trough corresponding to poor Tn5 enrichment due to TF binding at its motif36. Across the different groups, NRF1/NRF2 activity was increased in ELV samples compared to Veh and Sham (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, the differences in TF activity of NRF1/NRF2 were consistently observed across all Glutamatergic neuron subtypes in feature plots for ELV (Fig. 5E), Veh (Fig. 5F), and Sham (Fig. 5G) samples. A similar analysis was performed for the FOS::JUNB consensus motif (Fig. 5H). The activity of FOS::JUNB was greatest in glutamatergic neurons and, within glutamatergic neurons, was highest in ELV compared to Veh and Sham (Fig. 5I and J). The activity of FOS::JUNB was also plotted on feature plots for ELV, Veh, and Sham, revealing consistency across all Glutamatergic neuron subtypes (Fig. 5K–M). The activation of NRF1/NRF2 and AP-1 structural components, FOS/JUN, plays important roles in responding to oxidative stress and neural injury37,38. Their activation may contribute to the survival of Glutamatergic neurons after TBI.

Activation of oxidative stress defenses reflected by NRF2 and cell survival by AP-1 and synapse markers

Following our epigenetic analysis, we assessed NRF2 activation in the injured (right) cortex 3 days post-TBI ± IN ELV. NRF2 is bound in the cytoplasm and is activated by phosphorylation at Ser40, which triggers its nuclear translocation39. Once in the nucleus, NRF2 upregulates antioxidant defense pathways, thereby supporting mitochondrial function40. Immunohistochemistry (IHC, n = 4–5) and Western Blot analysis using the JESS system (n = 6–7) underwent subcellular fractionation to isolate cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins. Validation of fractionation was conducted using GAPDH as a marker for cytoplasmic protein and HDAC2 as a marker for nuclear proteins (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Fig. 4). This revealed increased total NRF2 and phosphorylated NRF2 (p(ser40)-NRF2) in the nuclear fractions (NF) from after ELV treatment, 3 days after TBI (Fig. 6B and C). Additionally, IHC analysis of phosphorylated NRF2 (p-NRF2) co-stained with NeuN revealed an increase in fluorescence intensity of p-NRF2 in surviving neurons located in the lesion periphery after ELV-treatment 3 days post-TBI (Fig. 6D–F). To assess oxidative stress in the injury area, IHC was performed for Heme Oxygenase 1 (HO-1) and imaged within the damaged region (Fig. 6G). HO-1 was co-stained with NeuN to ensure that the image was captured within the damaged area, where no living neurons were present. Immunostaining of HO-1 in ELV-treated samples showed decreased fluorescence intensity in the injury center (Fig. 6G–I) at day 3. Previous studies have demonstrated that HO-1 is rapidly induced after intracranial hemorrhage and is co-localized in microglia and macrophages41. The observed decrease in HO-1 by ELV may reflect a reduction in microglia/macrophage and, consequently, a reduction in oxidative reactions. In addition to the oxidative stress response, phosphorylated Fos proto-oncogene, AP-1 transcription factor subunit (p-c-FOS) was elevated after ELV treatment 3 days post-TBI (Fig. 6J and K). Furthermore, synaptic proteins, such as postsynaptic density 95 (PSD95) and synaptophysin (SYP), were elevated after ELV treatment compared to Veh on day 3 (Fig. 6J and K). In addition to these synaptic proteins, Calmodulin, a regulator of calcium signaling and synaptic function and plasticity, was also increased after ELV treatment compared to Veh on day 3, which supported transcriptomic results (Supplementary Fig. 5). Full blots for all representative images and total protein normalization are in Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7.

Discussion

Our previous study demonstrated ELV-mediated protection after TBI, as evaluated by behavioral assessment and ex vivo MRI26. IN-ELV reduced neurological deficits from days 1 to 14 post-TBI, and T2-weighted MRI showed less ventricular enlargement and cortical thinning compared with vehicle-treated rats26. Furthermore, ELVs were higher in the ipsilateral cortex compared to the contralateral cortex after intranasal delivery26. Additionally, ELV treatment displayed reduced axonal damage and preserved white matter tracts.

In the present study, we examined the cellular and molecular mechanisms by which IN ELV exerts neuroprotection against TBI, utilizing a combination of molecular assays, single-cell multiomics, protein assessments, and mitochondrial function approaches. A proposed cellular cascade of events leading to recovery is illustrated in Fig. 6L. The severity of damage was evaluated through behavioral cognitive testing caused by TBI. The damage was assessed by immunostaining for the inflammatory cell marker CD68, the lipid peroxidation indicator 4-HNE, and the neuronal marker NeuN. Taken together, these molecular signatures demonstrated improvement by IN-ELV. The single-cell multiome approach evaluated genome-wide transcriptomics to identify the pathological mechanisms by which IN ELVs confer neuroprotection after TBI. Our results determined that ELV restores gene networks related to synaptic integrity across multiple cell types and activates TFs contributing to oxidative stress response and neuronal survival. The oxidative stress response and overall mitochondrial function were further investigated in synaptosomes, revealing that ELV increased mitochondrial function compared to Veh as early as one day after TBI. Based on our ATAC findings, we found greater NRF2 activity in the ELV group, which likely contributes to the improvement of mitochondrial function and neuronal survival after neural injury.

One of the key hallmarks of TBI is neuroinflammation. In general, PUFAs have been established as molecules that promote brain anti-inflammation42,43. Beyond inflammation, other hallmarks of TBI are mitochondrial dysfunctions and lipid peroxidation44–46. Lipid peroxidation occurs when free radicals target PUFAs, leading to cell dysfunction and cell death via ferroptosis47. Our study demonstrates that lipid peroxidation, as evaluated by 4HNE, within the damaged cortex is reduced after ELV treatment, and the total area of injury is also reduced, as assessed by NeuN staining. Thus, ELV protects cells from ferroptosis processes by reducing lipid peroxidation and sustaining cell survival. This mechanism occurs by limiting the production of reactive oxygen lipid species after oxidative stress, which TBI enforces. ROS levels are imbalanced and upregulated following TBI, which has led to antioxidants as a potential therapeutic strategy for TBI48. Our study investigated antioxidant defense through the activation of NRF2, a master regulator of the antioxidative response37,39,40,49–52, by ELV after TBI. In the ELV group, we observed increased activation of NRF2 in glutamatergic neurons located on the borders of the damaged areas inflicted by TBI. This suggests that ELVs contribute to enhancing the brain’s antioxidant defenses, mitigating oxidative damage, and preserving neuronal integrity. NRF2 and its chaperone KEAP1 sense and respond to oxidative stress53, and its TF activity regulates transcription of antioxidant inducers and iron homeostasis genes contributing to protection from ferroptosis54,55. NRF2 enhances proteasome activation under oxidative stress, which stimulates survival and delays senescence56. Additionally, NRF2 interacts with TXNRD1, which is involved in antioxidant defenses following stress57. Our previous work has shown that ELV modulates TXNRD1 at the onset of uncompensated oxidative stress, establishing a potential connection between ELV and NRF2 activation58.

NRF2 is highly active in the mitochondria as a regulator of energy production, ROS, calcium signaling, and ferroptosis59. Mitochondrial dysfunction is associated with TBI pathology, including alterations in metabolic homeostasis, a decrease in energy production, a reduction in mitochondrial size, and an increase in oxidative stress60,61. Furthermore, cardiolipin oxidation in the mitochondria contributes to TBI pathology62. Taken together, this suggests that mitochondria may be a therapeutic target for TBI. Previous studies have shown that after CCI, acute mitochondrial dysfunctions in the impacted cortex peak within 24 h and return to near baseline after 3 days63,64. Our respirometry analysis of mitochondrial function in isolated synaptosomes one day after TBI shows that ELV elicits improvement in OCR, ECAR, respiration rates, and ATP production in both the area of injury in the cortex and in the hippocampus. The observed improvement in mitochondrial function during the early acute phase of TBI at the synapse likely contributes to synaptic integrity and cell survival. Single-cell gene expression at 3 days post-TBI revealed alterations in gene pathways and reduced mitochondrial activity. Although we did not assess these gene expression changes between ELV and Veh, we saw an upregulation of pathways associated with increased synaptic assembly and activity after ELV treatment, 3 days after TBI. We propose that early protection against mitochondrial stress by ELV contributes to sustaining synaptic activity post-TBI. Furthermore, we investigated HO-1 as an oxidative stress response and found an overall decrease in abundance with ELV treatment. HO-1 is an essential oxidative stress response protein, but prolonged increased levels of HO-1 are associated with AD pathology65–67.

Cognitive decline following TBI is attributed to multiple factors, including neuronal death and loss of synapses68–70. Improved mitochondrial function at the synapse protects synapses against amyloid pathology71.

In addition, our single-cell multiome DE and pathway analysis revealed recovery and induction of genes by ELV involved in synaptic integrity across multiple cell types, further highlighting the recovery induced by ELV treatment. Across glial populations, we uncovered an upregulation in ELV compared to Veh in Ntrk2, a receptor for brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) that is implicated in synaptogenesis and synaptic modulation72. Similarly, there was an upregulation of Nrxn1, Nrxn3, Nrg1, Nrcam, and Sparcl1 across glial populations, which are involved in cell adhesion and synapse formation73–76. This gene expression behavior in glial cells indicates synaptic maintenance and restoration induced by ELV. The behavior can be attributed to the decrease in oxidative stress and inflammation, which prevents proinflammatory phenotypes in microglia and astrocytes, leading to sustained damage77–79. In neurons, DEGs were associated with synaptic maintenance and integrity, synaptic adhesion and cell–cell interactions, neurotransmitter reception and synaptic transmission, synaptic trafficking, spine morphogenesis, and regulation of synaptic proteins and synaptic plasticity, as outlined in the Results. A single-cell multiome offers the advantage of revealing the genome-wide transcriptomic profile, thereby providing numerous targets for follow-up. The consistency of upregulation across synaptic processes compared to Veh indicates synaptic protection induced by ELV. In addition, we observed an increase in p-c-FOS in the nuclear fraction in the lesioned cortex after ELV treatment. Phosphorylation stabilizes the c-FOS protein and enables it to bind with c-JUN to form the AP-1 complex80. Previous studies have shown that AP-1 activation occurs after TBI81,82 and upregulates the transcription of a host of genes, including those needed for synaptic remodeling and plasticity83,84. Thus, the increase in FOS::JUN motif accessibility in Glutamatergic neurons and the elevated p-c-FOS protein in cortical tissue after ELV treatment suggest that increased AP-1 activity may contribute to the upregulation of gene pathways associated with synaptic assembly and function seen in the ELV group.

Given that TBI is a risk factor for AD-like dementia, the neuroprotective actions of ELVs could translate into amelioration of AD pathology. Chronic neuroinflammation is a hallmark of AD. Microglia undergo phenotypic transcriptional changes, causing damage85. By modulating the neuroimmune environment, ELVs could prevent sustained inflammation initiated by TBI from becoming a chronic driver of AD pathology. The restoration of synaptic and mitochondrial gene programs by ELVs addresses deficits seen in AD by preserving neuronal integrity and function post-TBI; ELVs would protect against neuronal loss and synaptic dysfunctions of AD.

In determining the genetic and epigenetic modulation caused by IN-ELV, the single-cell multiome offers high-resolution analysis at the single-cell level of specific transcription and regulatory mechanisms, including chromatin accessibility and indirect measures of TF binding. Matching transcription with chromatin regulation and TF activity provides integration of how ELV acts in specific cell types. Single-cell chromatin accessibility has been a useful tool in determining AD disease progression86. Gene expression revealed differential expression patterns in cell types that displayed synapse loss following TBI and synapse recovery with ELV treatment. Moreover, ATAC-seq revealed increased accessibility at NRF1/2 and AP-1 family TF motifs by ELV treatments. Together, these findings suggest that ELV promotes cell survival through multiple mechanisms involving mitochondrial function, oxidative stress responses, and synaptic integrity. We coupled cell-type-specific ATAC with validation at the protein level using bulk cortical tissue. TF differences were measured by protein abundance of phosphorylated protein targets in nuclear fractions. To measure synaptic integrity in the damaged region, the abundance of a presynaptic marker (SYP) and postsynaptic marker (PSD95) validates that the gene expression changes resulted in an increase in synaptic maintenance and integrity at the protein level. Both SYP and PSD95 have been shown to be decreased in mild cognitive impairments and AD, contributing to neurodegeneration and pathology87–92.

Coupling Multiome transcriptomics and epigenomics with protein validation overall ensures that the specific gene, pathway, and regulatory changes observed are also present at the protein level, thereby validating the findings as potential mechanisms of ELV neuroprotection following TBI.

IN-ELV provides a rapid, noninvasive route to administer acute neuroprotection after TBI. We have previously shown using liquid-chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) that IN ELV-N34 delivered 1 h post-TBI was found in higher levels in the injured cortex and subcortex compared to the uninjured cortex and subcortex26. Studies using lipid-based nanoparticles have identified three pathways for IN transport: olfactory pathway, trigeminal pathway, and the blood circulation pathway93. Both the olfactory and trigeminal pathways deliver directly to the brain through intracellular endocytosis–exocytosis in neurons or paracellular transport across the gaps in the olfactory epithelium. The blood circulation relies on the abundant blood and lymphatic vessels in the nasal mucosa to uptake the molecule into circulation and transport it to the brain by crossing the BBB93. Although it is still unclear which route IN-ELV enters the brain, our lab and others have shown that lipid mediators, such as NPD194 and vectorized DHA95, delivered IN improve cortical (cognition) and hippocampal (memory) function in murine models of AD. In TBI, the breakdown of the BBB due to inflammation of the brain may further facilitate entry of ELV through either the olfactory/trigeminal route or through the circulatory system96.

In conclusion, our histological evaluation revealed a reduction in immune cell abundance and decreased lipid peroxidation at the lesion site following ELV treatment, which corresponded to enhanced gene transcription associated with synapse integrity, as revealed by single-cell multiome analysis. Single-cell ATAC analysis also revealed increased chromatin accessibility at NRF2 and AP-1 TF motifs in glutamatergic neurons, together with increased NRF2/p-NRF2 and p-c-FOS in bulk cortical tissue, suggesting the activation of oxidative stress defenses in the enduring neurons after TBI with ELV intervention. The increased expression of mitochondrial and synaptic genes in the ELV group supported the relative increase in mitochondrial function and increased abundance of synaptic proteins in ELV compared to Veh. These findings shed light on the acute mechanisms through which ELV protects the brain after TBI and have implications for neuroprotection in other neurodegenerative diseases.

The current study has some limitations as areas for further investigation. The single-cell multiome approach, which allowed for both genetic and epigenetic examination in the same cell, lacks spatial information. As a future direction, combining spatial transcriptomics and proteomics approaches would be beneficial for precise observation of damage in the lesion and perilesional regions and for providing cell-specific changes in transcripts and protein levels with ELV treatment after TBI. Furthermore, this study identifies potential oxidative stress response and mitochondrial function mechanisms that should be further investigated to identify the cascade of cellular events leading to neuroprotection. In order to define ELV action on immune responses, it would be valuable to explore the dynamics of microglial activation that influence damage by the neuroimmune environment. Moreover, top-down lipidomics using LC–MS/MS and MALDI would provide specific information on peroxidized lipids. Due to the limited sample size in the single-cell experiment, we were unable to use a pseudobulking approach for data aggregation analysis and instead made comparisons on a per-cell basis. The per-cell approach is more vulnerable to inflation of p-values, and the significance of individual genes/TF motifs should be interpreted with this limitation in mind97. The Seahorse XF synaptosome results did not include a sham group, and normalization of synaptosomal extracts was done using total protein estimation instead of mitochondrial proteins. Lastly, only young adult male rats were included in the study. Future studies should be done to determine if ELV also protects against TBI in female and aged animals.

Methods

All animal experiments were conducted in strict compliance with Institutional, National, and International Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Louisiana State University Health Sciences Center, New Orleans, under Protocol Number 9341. All procedures were performed in accordance with the institution’s ethical regulations. The study also adhered to the ARRIVE 2.0 reporting guidelines to ensure transparent and comprehensive documentation of animal Research. A schematic summary of the timeline for treatments, behavioral measurements, and tissue collection is provided in Supplementary Fig. 8.

Controlled cortical impact (CCI) as a TBI model

Sprague-Dawley (SD) male young rats (3–4 months old, weight 300–500 g) were used for all experiments. Traumatic brain injury was induced using controlled cortical impact (CCI) on the right hemisphere98–100. Rats were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane, 70% nitrous oxide, and a balance of oxygen, intubated, and catheters were inserted into the femoral artery and vein for blood pressure monitoring, IV infusion, and blood sampling for arterial gases, pH, hematocrit, and plasma glucose. Cranial and rectal temperature was monitored during the surgical procedure. The animals then underwent impact using a magnetic-guided stereotactic apparatus pre-programmed to a depth and speed of impact (velocity: 5 m/s, dwell time: 0.2 s, depth: 2.5 mm). The craniotomy window was not sealed and was loosely covered by sewing over the underlying fascia as well as the scalp. An illustration of the craniectomy and CCI location can be found in Supplementary Fig. 8B. After TBI, rectal temperature and body weight were monitored daily during the 3-day survival period.

Drug administration and experimental groups

ELV-N34 (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) was dissolved in saline + 10% ethanol to be delivered intranasally (IN) according to the study protocol. Rats in the Veh and Sham group received an intranasal delivery of saline + 10% ethanol at 1 and 24 h after TBI. Rats in the ELV group received ELV-N34:6 (20 µg/dose) dissolved in saline + 10% ethanol at 1 and 24 h after TBI. Rats were lightly anesthetized with isoflurane to minimize variability in the delivery. 10 µl of solution was delivered to each nostril, with a rest of 30 s between nostrils, for a total volume of 20 µl delivered per treatment. IN delivery was given after behavioral scoring at 1 and 24 h. The dose and timing of ELV delivery were chosen from our previously published studies14,25,101. The generation of surgical animals and tissue collection, administration of treatment, drug preparation, and behavioral tests were performed by separate experimenters. The order of treatments was randomized.

Behavioral scoring

An observer blinded to the experimental groups performed all behavioral tests. The following tests were conducted102,103: A composite neurological battery that consists of a postural reflex test to examine forelimb and upper-body posture and an elicited forelimb placing test to study sensorimotor integration by assessing placing reactions to visual, tactile, and proprioceptive stimuli. Scores ranged from 0 (normal) to 12 (maximal deficit). Behavioral assessments were conducted 1 h after trauma and then every 24 h up to 3 days post-injury. Neurological scores at each time point (1 h, 1, 2, and 3 days) were compared between ELV and Veh using the Student’s t-test with Bonferroni correction for multiple testing. Sample sizes of 6–7 rats/group were based on our previous study using ELV26, which showed robust neurobehavioral differences using similar sample sizes.

Multiome sequencing and bioinformatics

Tissue collection and nuclei isolation

Lesional cortex from Sham, Veh, and ELV rats was dissected and snap-frozen at –80 °C. More information about the tissue dissection can be found in Supplementary Fig. 8C. Two biological replicates/animals were used for each group. The tissue was thoroughly homogenized, and single-nuclei were isolated using the 10x Genomics protocol (Pleasanton, CA, CG000505). The protocol involved lysis and dissociation of the tissue, followed by filtration on the Nuclei Isolation Column using centrifugation. Nuclei were resuspended in 50 µL of resuspension buffer with debris removal buffer to ensure purity. Nuclei concentration was determined with a Luna-FL cell counter (Logos Biosystems, Annandale, VA).

Library preparation and sequencing

Using the 10x Genomics Chromium Controller, RNA and ATAC libraries were generated on a cell-by-cell basis using microfluidic partitioning to capture single cells and prepare barcoded, next-generation sequencing (NGS) libraries. All samples were processed in the same batch. The pooled mRNA and ATAC libraries from all six samples were sequenced separately with a NextSeq 2000 system using two flow cells (one for ATAC and one for mRNA) (Illumina, San Diego, CA), targeting a minimum of 20,000 read pairs per cell for the mRNA library and 25,000 read pairs per cell for the ATAC library. Cell Ranger ARC 2.0.2 was used to annotate, process, and aggregate the mRNA and ATAC sequencing data using the mRNATBN7.2 genome assembly.

Bioinformatic analysis

Preprocessing and quality control

Aggregated filtered_feature_bc_matrix h5 and atac_fragments files were imported into a Seurat object in R. A percentage feature set of mitochondrial gene expression was created, and all cells with nFeature_RNA > 200 and percent.mt < 15 were retained for further analysis. The mRatBN7.2 genome was added to the EnsDb.Rnorvegicus.v110 genome annotation, and transcription start site (TSS) enrichment was performed using the TSSEnrichment function from Signac. Final quality control metrics were executed, keeping single cells with nCount_ATAC < 100,000 and >500, TSS enrichment score > 0.5, nFeature_RNA < 6000, and percent.mt < 15. Peaks were called using MACS2 in the CallPeaks function from Signac.

Normalization, clustering, and cell type annotation

Normalization was performed using SCTransform on RNA data. Dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component analysis (PCA) by RunPCA, and an elbow plot was used to determine significant PCs for clustering. Clustering and uniform manifold approximation projection (UMAP) were generated using PCs 1:50 and a resolution of 1. Normalization was performed using frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) and singular value decomposition (SVD) for ATAC data, utilizing RunTFIDF and RunSVD, respectively, to generate latent semantic indexing (LSI) dimensionality reduction. Clustering and UMAP projection were generated using LSI dimensions 2:20 and a resolution of 1. Weighted nearest neighbor (WNN) analysis was performed to integrate RNA and ATAC clustering using PCs 1:50 for RNA and LSI dimensions 2:20 for ATAC. Automated cell type annotation was performed using the Allen Brain Cell (ABC) Atlas (RRID:SCR_024440) 10x Whole Mouse Brain taxonomy using the MapMyCells tool and the Hierarchical Mapping algorithm. Although the reference taxonomy is mouse rather than rat, there is a high degree of conservation in the canonical marker genes for different cell types in the brain between rat and mouse, and the hierarchical mapping approach used for MapMyCells is robust for cross-species mapping. Following MapMyCells, the class_name and subclass_name were extracted and added to the metadata in Seurat. For each cluster identified after running Seurat’s FindCluster function (Supplementary Fig. 1), we identified the predominant cell class or subclass annotated according to the ABC atlas. For cell clusters containing a mixture of many cell types seen primarily in neurons, we assigned broader classification schemes (such as “subcortex glut”) to group sparse subtypes together.

Differential expression and pathway analysis

Differential expression (DE) testing was performed for each cell type to determine gene expression differences between conditions. Mitochondrial genes and unnamed genes were not considered for DE analysis. DE testing was performed on SCT-normalized RNA data using Seurat FindMarkers between conditions for each cell type using the Wilcoxon Rank Sum Test implemented by Presto within Seurat. Volcano plots were generated from EnhancedVolcano for each comparison using fold change (FC) and p-value to highlight significance, direction, and magnitude of change. The top 300 significant (p_val < 0.05) differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each comparison were used for pathway analysis by Gene Ontology (GO) using biological process (BP) ontology in the enrichGO function from clusterProfiler. DEGs contributing to specific synaptic pathways were extracted from enrichGO results and plotted using pheatmap with scaled expression per row to prevent high-expression bias.

Motif enrichment and TF binding analysis

Using ATAC data, an indirect analysis of TF binding sites was performed to gain insight into the epigenetic changes caused by ELV following TBI. TF consensus motifs were added to the mRatBN7.2 genome using the JASPAR2020 core vertebrates’ taxonomy group database. Motif variability scores were generated using the ChromVAR package104 by measuring the chromatin accessibility at consensus motifs. Measures of chromatin accessibility at TF binding sites are highly correlated with TF binding but are an indirect assessment. Differences in motif variability between conditions were assessed in each cell type using Seurat’s FindMarkers function on ChromVAR output, with row means to calculate the average difference. TF Footprints were generated using the Footprint function and plotted using PlotFootprint from Signac.

Molecular assays

Synaptosomes isolation and Seahorse respirometry

Synaptosomes were isolated from the cortex and hippocampus of rats 1 day after TBI using Syn-PER synaptic protein extraction reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and mitochondrial function was assessed via respirometry analysis using the Agilent Seahorse XFe96 Analyzer and the Seahorse XF Mito stress test kits as per the manufacturer’s protocol105–113. More details on the tissue dissection are provided in Supplementary Fig. 8C. An aliquot of the isolated synaptosomes was used to measure the total protein concentration using the Pierce BCA protein quantification kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and 10 μg of synaptosomes were loaded per well. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were measured following the Agilent Seahorse XF96 workflow. The OCR and ECAR were measured three times per stage (basal respiration, after injection of oligomycin, after injection of FCCP, and after injection of rotenone and antimycin A) in ~7 min intervals. Each biological sample contained at least six technical replicates (wells). The technical replicates were averaged for each sample at each timepoint and then the biological mean and SEM for each sample were plotted in Fig. 4B and C. Basal respiration, maximal respiration, and ATP production were calculated from these measurements in Fig. 4D.

Subcellular protein fractionation

The right cortex was freshly collected as described in Supplementary Fig. 8. Subcellular protein fractionation was conducted using the subcellular protein fractionation kit for tissues from Thermo Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Briefly, 100 mg of fresh tissue was collected and homogenized in a Dounce tissue homogenizer containing a cytoplasmic extraction buffer with protease inhibitors. Homogenized tissue was strained, centrifuged, and the supernatant containing cytoplasmic protein was collected. The pellet was resuspended in membrane extraction buffer containing protease inhibitors, then centrifuged, and the supernatant, containing membrane protein, was collected. This process was repeated using a nuclear extraction buffer containing protease inhibitors for soluble nuclear protein, and a nuclear extraction buffer containing protease inhibitors, calcium chloride, and micrococcal nuclease for chromatin-bound nuclear protein. Soluble nuclear protein and chromatin-bound nuclear fraction were combined to create a total nuclear extract. Extracts were stored at –80 °C until further use. Purity of the nuclear and cytosolic fractions was assessed by WB analysis for markers HDAC2 (nuclear fraction) and GAPDH (cytosolic fraction).

Cryosectioning, immunohistochemistry, and microscopy

Rats were terminally anesthetized and perfused using a gravity perfusion setup first with saline for 5 min, followed by 25 min with 10% formalin. The fixed brain was embedded in room-temperature OCT before rapidly freezing in isopentane chilled on dry ice. The tissue was stored in –80 °C. Subsequently, the brain was cryosectioned (Epredia NX50) at 12 μm, mounted onto microscope slides, and stored at –20 °C until further processing. Slides stored at –20 °C were thawed for 15 min at room temperature and fixed for 10 min using 4% PFA at room temperature. After fixation, slides were washed in a large volume of 1X phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 15 min and then washed twice over the slide for 5 min each with 1X PBS. Tissues were blocked using 1X PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 (PBST) in a humidified chamber at room temperature for 30 min. Primary antibodies were diluted in an incubation buffer consisting of PBST and 1% goat serum and incubated with tissues at 4 °C overnight. Secondary antibodies were diluted in the same incubation buffer at a 1:1000 dilution and added to the tissues for a 2-h incubation at room temperature. For nuclear staining, Hoechst was diluted to 1:5000 in 1X PBS and added to tissues for 10 min of room temperature incubation. Slides were washed three times with 1X PBS, and coverslips were mounted using gold antifade. Slides were stored at 4 °C until imaging.

Imaging for 4HNE/NeuN, CD68, and HO-1/NeuN was conducted using the Olympus VS120 microscope. An overview image for each sample was captured at 2x on the Hoechst stain. Regions of interest were defined by editing the scan area. Consistent exposure time was maintained between samples for each channel. 4HNE/NeuN and CD68 stained samples were imaged at 10x, while HO-1/NeuN was imaged at 20x.

Imaging for p-NRF2/NeuN staining was conducted using the Olympus Fluoview FV4000 confocal microscope. For each sample, three separate images were taken peripherally from the damaged region, defined by NeuN staining, using a 40x long-range objective. Z-stacks with a 1 μm step size were acquired, and a maximal projection was obtained for image analysis.

All imaging data were analyzed in Fiji. To quantify immunostaining results for immune infiltration, the ratio of CD68+ cells to the total number of nuclei identified by Hoechst staining was calculated. We noted that NeuN staining in the injured cortex revealed a clear boundary in the cortical tissue where there was an absence of NeuN-positive staining compared to the uninjured cortex, and we defined the region of neuron loss as the lesion area. To quantify immunostaining results for lipid peroxidation, the total area of the lesion was determined by manually tracing along the border of living neurons marked by NeuN, and a percentage of that area in which 4HNE fluorescence was present at a determined threshold was measured. To quantify p-NRF2 and HO-1, fluorescence intensity was measured within specific regions and averaged for each sample. The following antibodies were used at the following dilutions; NeuN (Cell Signaling Technologies, catalog # 94403) 1:300, 4HNE (Abcam, catalog # ab46545) 1:200, CD68 (Cell Signaling Technologies, catalog # 97778) 1:500, p-NRF2 (ThermoFisher Scientific, catalog # PA5-67520) 1:200, and HO-1 (Cell Signaling Technologies, catalog # 43966) 1:200.

Capillary JESS system for protein analysis

Protein concentration from the nuclear, cytosolic, and total protein extracts was determined by BCA assay. The protein evaluation was performed using the Jess Automated Western Blot System, with a 12–230 kDa separation module and chemiluminescence kit, following the manufacturer’s recommended protocols. For data analysis, the area of spectra for the target protein was normalized to the total protein value. The following antibodies were used at a dilution of 1:50; HDAC2 (Cell Signaling Technologies, catalog # 57156), NRF2 (Cell Signaling Technologies, catalog # 33649), p-NRF2 (ThermoFisher Scientific, catalog # PA5-67520), GAPDH (Abcam, catalog # ab37168), p-c-FOS (Cell Signaling Technologies, catalog # 5348), PSD95 (Abcam, catalog # ab2723), SYP (Abcam, AB52636), and Calmodulin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog # MA3-917).

Statistics and reproducibility

Plots were generated for multiome data in R using either the ggplot2 package or Seurat, with custom code modifications for data visualization. All p-values reported in this study were adjusted for false discovery rate (FDR) using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure for differential expression (DE) analysis. P-values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Statistical analyses for data on synaptosome respirometry using Agilent Seahorse XFe96, capillary Western Blot using JESS, and IHC were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10, using the Student’s t-test. All group data are presented as mean ± SEM. Significance was defined when p < 0.05.
