Effects of exercise interventions on core health outcomes in patients with SLE: systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zehui He, Shuqi Jia, Mengsi Chen, Xinxin Shi, Lunuo Hu, Fei Li, Hui Shi, David C Nieman, Yue Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis meta-analysis evaluated the effects of exercise interventions on key health outcomes in adults with SLE, including fatigue, physical function, aerobic capacity, disease activity, depression and pain.MethodsFollowing the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines, we systematically searched four databases up to February 2025 for randomised controlled trials (RCTs) in adults diagnosed with SLE according to the American College of Rheumatology/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (ACR/EULAR) criteria. Eligible studies examined aerobic, resistance or combined exercise as the sole intervention. Meta-analyses were conducted using Hedges’ g, the Risk of Bias 2 (ROB-2) tool and the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) system used to assess bias and evidence quality.ResultsTwelve RCTs involving 470 participants were included. Exercise showed small-to-moderate improvements in fatigue (g=−0.53, 95% CI −0.81 to −0.25, p<0.001), physical function (g=0.46, 95% CI 0.23 to 0.68, p<0.001), aerobic capacity (g=0.47, 95% CI 0.22 to 0.71, p<0.001), depression (g=−0.36, 95% CI −0.64 to −0.09, p=0.01) and pain (g=−0.53, 95% CI −0.86 to −0.20, p<0.001), with no significant change in disease activity (g=0.18, 95% CI −0.16 to 0.51, p=0.30). Subgroup analyses suggested greater benefits with moderate-intensity programmes lasting 8–12 weeks. GRADE rated the evidence as moderate for fatigue, physical function, depression and pain, and low for other outcomes.ConclusionExercise is an effective non-pharmacological therapy in SLE management, improving several physical and psychological outcomes. Individualised, moderate-intensity and supervised programmes are recommended. Further research should optimise intervention protocols and evaluate long-term safety.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251115953

Introduction

SLE is a chronic autoimmune disease characterised by persistent inflammation and multisystem involvement,1 with a global prevalence of approximately 43.7 per 100 000 people.2 The disease causes diverse symptoms including fatigue,3 reduced physical function,4 impaired aerobic capacity,5 mood disorders (eg, depression)6 and chronic pain,7 substantially impairing quality of life. While glucocorticoids and immunosuppressants effectively control disease activity,8 their long-term use carries risks including osteoporosis, metabolic disturbances and increased infection susceptibility,9 highlighting the need for safe non-pharmacological interventions. Exercise interventions, with low risk and broad applicability, have proven advantageous in other chronic diseases and are increasingly recognised as a promising adjuvant in SLE management.10 11

Previous systematic reviews support exercise benefits for SLE but show methodological limitations. Most focus primarily on fatigue and aerobic capacity,1214 with limited analysis of functional impairment, depression, pain and disease activity. Lu et al15 found no significant exercise intervention effects on pain or mental health improvements using the SF-36 health survey, while da Hora et al16 noted quality-of-life gains without analysing individual outcomes. Broad inclusion criteria and heterogeneous interventions have produced inconsistent findings, particularly regarding pain, disease activity and mental health.14

To address these gaps, this systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated exercise effects on six core health outcomes—fatigue, functional capacity, cardiorespiratory endurance, disease activity, depression and pain—in patients with SLE. Subgroup analyses examined how exercise type, duration, frequency, session length and intensity influenced outcomes to help clarify optimal dose–response relationships.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement.17 The study protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO.

Literature search strategy

We searched PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase and Web of Science through February 2025 using terms related to ‘systemic lupus erythematosus’ and ‘exercise’. Search strategies are in online supplemental table S1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Following the PICOS framework, inclusion criteria were (1) adults diagnosed with SLE per established criteria (eg, American College of Rheumatology/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (ACR or EULAR))18; (2) exercise interventions (aerobic, resistance or combined training); (3) usual care controls without additional exercise or active interventions; (4) RCT design; (5) reporting of at least one core SLE outcome; (6) no significant baseline group differences.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) conference abstracts, protocols or incomplete reports; (2) mixed populations without separate SLE results; (3) interventions with non-exercise components or active control interventions; (4) non-controlled designs; (5) undefined or unextractable outcomes; (6) studies demonstrating critical methodological flaws that prevented data extraction or meaningful interpretation.

Study selection and data extraction

All records were managed in EndNote V.X9 with duplicates removed. Two reviewers (ZH and MC) independently screened titles/abstracts and full texts against predefined criteria; a third reviewer (SJ) resolved disagreements. The selection process is shown in the PRISMA flow diagram.

Two reviewers (ZH and MC) independently extracted data into a standardised Excel form, including study characteristics (author, year, design, country, sample size, sex, SLE criteria, age), intervention details (type, duration, frequency, session length, intensity; online supplemental table S2) and outcomes (measures, tools, pre and postintervention means and SDs). When SDs were unavailable, they were derived from SEs, CIs or medians (online supplemental data S1). Values from figures were extracted using WebPlotDigitizer. Change scores were calculated following methods in online supplemental data S1. In three-arm trials, each intervention group was extracted separately. A third reviewer cross-checked all data for accuracy.

Definition and assessment tools for primary outcomes

Fatigue, a common symptom in SLE characterised by persistent exhaustion unrelieved by rest, was assessed using the Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) and the LupusQoL-fatigue domain. The FSS is a 9-item, 7-point Likert scale with higher scores indicating greater fatigue, while the LupusQoL-fatigue (0–100 scale) assigns higher scores to less fatigue.

Physical function, reflecting the ability to perform daily activities, was measured using the SF-36 Physical Functioning subscale and the LupusQoL physical health domain, with higher scores indicating better function in both surveys.

Aerobic capacity, an indicator of cardiorespiratory endurance, was evaluated via field tests (12 min walk test, 2 km walk test or laboratory tests (maximal symptom-limited cycle ergometry, cardiopulmonary exercise testing, treadmill testing and the Quark PFT ergospirometric system). Outcomes were expressed as aerobic capacity or exercise tolerance, including VO₂ max/peak (mL/kg/min).

Disease activity, reflecting current inflammatory disease activity, was assessed using Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) and SLEDAI-2000 (SLEDAI-2K), with higher scores indicating greater activity.

Depression symptoms were measured using the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale depression subscale (HADS-D), both validated for depression severity grading.

Pain, a key determinant of quality of life, was assessed by the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS, 0–10 or 0–100) and the SF-36 Bodily Pain domain.

Risk of bias assessment and evidence quality assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane ROB V.2.0 tool across five domains: randomisation process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement and selection of reported results. Overall judgments were classified as ‘low risk’, ‘some concerns’ or ‘high risk’. Evidence certainty was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach via GRADEpro, assessing risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias, with evidence graded as ‘high’, ‘moderate’, ‘low’ or ‘very low’. Two reviewers (ZH and MC) independently conducted all assessments, with a third reviewer (SJ) resolving disagreements. Risk of bias ratings were not used as an independent exclusion criterion; studies were excluded only when critical methodological flaws prevented data extraction or meaningful interpretation, consistent with exclusion criterion (6). Studies rated as ‘some concerns’ or ‘high risk of bias’ were retained if they met PICOS criteria and provided extractable data, with their methodological limitations addressed through ROB V.2.0 reporting, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses and outcome-level GRADE assessment.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using Stata V.17.0. Change scores from baseline to postintervention were pooled across RCTs. Given variability in measurement tools, Hedges’ g with 95% CI was calculated as the standardised mean difference to correct for small-sample bias. Effect sizes were interpreted as: <0.2 trivial, 0.2–0.5 small, 0.5–0.8 moderate and ≥0.8 large.19 Heterogeneity was assessed using I² statistics: ≤ 25% (none), 25%–50% (low), 50%–75% (moderate) and >75% (high).20 A fixed-effects model was applied when I²<50% and p>0.1; otherwise, a random-effects model was used. Subgroup analyses explored heterogeneity sources. Sensitivity analyses sequentially removed each study to assess robustness. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s test, with trim-and-fill adjustment applied when bias was detected. All tests were two-tailed with significance at p ≤0.05.

Results

Search results

A total of 3815 records were identified across four databases. After removing 847 duplicates, 2968 records were screened, with 2889 excluded at title/abstract screening. Of 79 full texts assessed, 67 were excluded, yielding 12 studies for inclusion. The selection process is shown in figure 1, with included studies listed in online supplemental data S2.

Flowchart of literature selection and inclusion.

Basic information and intervention characteristics

Twelve RCTs2132 involving 470 adults with SLE (265 exercise, 205 control) met inclusion criteria. Participants were predominantly female (only two trials included males: n=2 and n=4), aged 20–50 years and met established diagnostic criteria (ACR or EULAR). Interventions included aerobic training (n=5), combined aerobic and resistance training (n=6) and resistance training alone (n=2), delivered over 8–16 weeks (typically 12 weeks), 2–3 times/week, 30–60 min/session, at moderate intensity (MI). Exercise classifications are in online supplemental data S3. Outcomes assessed were fatigue (n=4), physical function (n=6), aerobic capacity (n=8), disease activity (n=7), depression (n=4) and pain (n=6). Controls received routine care or health education without exercise. Study details are in table 1.

Methodological quality and evidence certainty assessment

Most included RCTs (69.2%) had an overall risk of bias rated as ‘some concerns’, while 15.4% each were rated as ‘low’ or ‘high’ risk (online supplemental figure S1A). Low risk was most frequent for ‘selection of the reported result’ (92.3%), followed by ‘missing outcome data’ (84.6%) and ‘deviations from intended interventions’ (76.9%), whereas the ‘randomisation process’ showed higher risk, with only 23.1% rated as low. Detailed risk-of-bias judgments for each study are provided in online supplemental figure S1B. Evidence quality (online supplemental table S3) was moderate for fatigue, physical function, depression and pain; low for aerobic capacity and disease activity, indicating moderate certainty for the first four outcomes and substantial uncertainty for disease activity and pain. Downgrading for aerobic capacity and disease activity was driven primarily by risk of bias, reflecting insufficient reporting of randomisation and allocation concealment in the contributing studies.

Meta-analysis

Research findings

All pooled analyses used a random-effects model. Detailed forest plots are presented in figure 2.

Forest plots of the effects of exercise interventions on (A) fatigue, (B) physical function, (C) aerobic capacity, (D) disease activity, (E) depressive symptoms and (F) pain in patients with SLE. Effect sizes are presented as Hedges’ g with 95% CIs. Meta-analyses were conducted using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method.

Fatigue (figure 2A): four studies showed exercise significantly improved fatigue (Hedges’ g=−0.53, 95% CI −0.81 to −0.25, p<0.001) with no heterogeneity (I²=0.0%, p=0.32).

Physical function (figure 2B): six studies demonstrated significant improvement in physical function (Hedges’ g=0.46, 95% CI 0.23 to 0.68, p<0.001) with no heterogeneity (I²=0.0%, p=0.38).

Aerobic capacity (figure 2C): eight studies showed significant enhancement in aerobic capacity (Hedges’ g=0.47, 95% CI 0.22 to 0.71, p<0.001) with mild heterogeneity (I²=16.21%, p=0.33).

Disease activity (figure 2D): seven studies found no significant improvement in disease activity (Hedges’ g=0.18, 95% CI −0.16 to 0.51, p=0.30) with moderate heterogeneity (I²=46.66%, p=0.08).

Depression (figure 2E): four studies showed significant reduction in depressive symptoms (Hedges’ g=−0.36, 95% CI −0.64 to −0.09, p=0.01) with no heterogeneity (I²=0.0%, p=0.53).

Pain (figure 2F): six studies demonstrated significant pain reduction (Hedges’ g=−0.53, 95% CI −0.86 to −0.20, p<0.001) with moderate heterogeneity (I²=50.16%, p=0.07).

In summary, the meta-analysis indicated that exercise interventions significantly improved fatigue, physical function, aerobic capacity, depression and pain in patients with SLE, while effects on disease activity remained inconclusive.

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

Sensitivity analysis (online supplemental figure S2) using Stata V.17.0 showed that sequential removal of individual studies did not substantially alter effect sizes and 95% CIs for fatigue, physical function, aerobic capacity, disease activity, depression and pain, confirming the robustness of the results. Publication bias was evaluated via funnel plots and Egger’s test (online supplemental figure S3), indicating no significant bias for most outcomes, except a potential trend for pain (p=0.0644).

Adverse events

No adverse events were reported in the included studies.

Discussion

Main findings

The findings suggest that structured exercise may be a useful non-pharmacological strategy for improving patient-reported symptoms and physical capacity in patients with SLE, while its effect on disease activity remains uncertain.

These findings align with prior meta-analyses by Frade et al12 and Wu et al,13 which reported similar benefits. However, our subgroup analysis indicated that moderate-intensity programmes of ≤12 weeks could achieve meaningful improvements, contrasting with Frade et al’s suggestion that >12 week interventions yield stronger effects.

Sensitivity analyses confirmed robustness of all estimates, with no clear evidence of publication bias was observed, although the number of studies was small. GRADE ratings indicated moderate-quality evidence for fatigue, physical function, depression and pain. These results affirm the multidimensional benefits of exercise in SLE and highlight intensity and duration as modifiable parameters for evidence-based exercise prescriptions.

Explanation of mechanisms and indicators

Mechanistic insights into fatigue

Fatigue in SLE extends beyond simple ‘physical deconditioning’ and reflects dysregulation of immune, neural and metabolic pathways. First, chronic low-grade inflammation is a major driver of central fatigue. Persistent elevation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (eg, IL-6, TNF-α) can act on the central nervous system, disrupt neurotransmitter homeostasis (dopamine, norepinephrine) and induce ‘inflammation-related behaviours’ such as motivational decline and exhaustion.33 Aerobic training can downregulate these cytokines and increase anti-inflammatory mediators (eg, IL-10), thereby reshaping the inflammatory milieu.

Second, mitochondrial dysfunction underlies peripheral fatigue. Patients with SLE often display reduced ATP synthesis, excess reactive oxygen species and loss of mitochondrial membrane potential in skeletal muscle.34 Moderate-intensity exercise can activate the PGC-1α–NRF1–TFAM axis, enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy, improving energy production and reducing oxidative stress.35 It can also increase capillary density, improve oxygen delivery and activate central reward circuits (eg, dopamine D2 receptor upregulation), jointly mitigating energy deficits and emotional exhaustion.

Mechanistic insights into physical function and aerobic capacity

Patients with SLE often exhibit reduced muscle strength, cardiopulmonary endurance and exercise tolerance due to inflammatory, metabolic and cardiovascular dysregulation. Physical function impairments result from muscle loss driven by chronic inflammation-mediated protein degradation, glucocorticoid-induced atrophy and metabolic disturbances.36 RE activates the mTORC1 pathway, promoting protein synthesis while suppressing catabolic markers (myostatin, MuRF1, Atrogin-1), restoring anabolic–catabolic balance.37 It also improves joint stability and stimulates muscle-bone crosstalk, with bone-derived myokines (osteocalcin, irisin), enhancing neuromuscular function.

Aerobic capacity limitations in SLE include reduced VO₂peak, delayed heart rate recovery and exertional dyspnoea, linked to autonomic dysfunction, inflammation, endothelial injury and myocardial involvement. Approximately 25%–40% of patients show cardiovascular limitations, including reduced HRV and exercise capacity even in remission.38 AE improves myocardial compliance, cardiac output and autonomic balance via vagal reactivation, upregulates endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) to enhance endothelial function and promotes angiogenesis while elevating red blood cell 2,3-DPG for improved oxygen delivery. These adaptations enhance performance and are associated with lower mortality and cardiovascular risk. Cardiopulmonary endurance is a key modifiable protective factor in systemic rheumatic diseases.39 Combined training synergistically improves cardiorespiratory fitness, strength and movement safety.

Mechanistic insights into depression

Depression affects 40%–60% of patients with SLE, substantially exceeding general population rates. Beyond psychosocial factors, this results from inflammatory, neural and neuroendocrine disturbances that cross the blood–brain barrier, initiating neuroimmune responses that disrupt neurotransmitter balance and reduce neuroplasticity—an ‘immune-mediated depression’ process.40

Exercise offers distinct mechanistic benefits as a ‘multisystem plasticity modulator’. It upregulates brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, promoting neurogenesis and synaptogenesis.41 It also restores dopamine and serotonin activity by modulating the tryptophan–kynurenine pathway: aerobic training increases kynurenine aminotransferase expression, converting kynurenine to neuroprotective kynurenic acid while reducing neurotoxic metabolites.42 Additionally, exercise modulates the HPA axis, lowering cortisol levels and restoring circadian rhythms, while exerting immunomodulatory effects through increased regulatory T cells and IL-10 production.43

These interconnected mechanisms—inflammation attenuation, neurotransmitter modulation, neurotrophic support and neuroendocrine stabilisation—constitute a robust non-pharmacological pathway for improving mood in SLE.

Mechanistic insights into pain

Pain is highly prevalent in SLE, arising from musculoskeletal, immune and central nervous system interactions beyond inflammation alone. Chronic inflammation induces synovitis and myofascial tension imbalance, producing persistent mechanical pain; prolonged activity sensitises the central nervous system, lowering pain thresholds and causing neuropathic pain with widespread hyperalgesia.44

Resistance training alleviates musculoskeletal pain by increasing muscle fibre cross-sectional area, enhancing postural control and improving load distribution, thereby reducing joint shear stress in vulnerable sites. In addition, exercise activates multiple endogenous analgesic pathways including the opioid peptide system, in which upregulation of proopiomelanocortin increases β-endorphin levels that bind μ-opioid receptors to downregulate nociceptors such as transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1).45 Another pathway activated by exercise includes the endocannabinoid system, where elevated anandamide and 2-AG activate CB1/CB2 receptors to produce both central and peripheral analgesia.46 A third pathway includes the noradrenaline-serotonin axis in which enhanced monoamine signalling augments descending pain inhibition at the spinal dorsal horn.

AE improves blood perfusion and tissue temperature, restoring myofascial–neurovascular sliding capacity. The ‘muscle–nerve–immune axis’ serves as a key mediator, whereby exercise-induced myokines (irisin, IL-6, BDNF) exert anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects, modulating hyperexcitability in spinal neurons and central pain-processing regions.47

Mechanistic insights into disease activity

Although this review found no significant improvement in disease activity after exercise in SLE (Hedges’ g=0.18, p>0.05), this may reflect study design and disease characteristics rather than ineffectiveness. Most participants were clinically stable, and SLEDAI scores exhibit floor effects at low values, limiting sensitivity to change. Tench et al similarly reported improved fatigue without corresponding disease activity score changes.28 Exercise enhances regulatory T cell function, reduces Th17 proportion and lowers pro-inflammatory cytokines. These effects are indicative of ‘immune rebalancing’ rather than conventional immunosuppression.48 Such shifts are less likely to be detected by short-term clinical scoring systems. Multi-omics studies show exercise can downregulate neutrophil extracellular trap formation, interferon (TNF)-stimulated gene expression and CD38+ B cell activity,49 indicating deeper modulation of inflammatory pathways.

Moderate exercise is safe and does not increase SLE flare risk or complement consumption and does not complicate kidney health.28 It may delay disease progression by reducing IFN-α expression and enhancing NK cell function.50 While short-term effects on activity scores may be minimal, exercise likely supports long-term stability via chronic immune regulation and restructuring of inflammatory networks.

Dose–response relationship and optimisation of intervention parameters

Subgroup analyses revealed dose–response relationships influenced by exercise type, intensity, frequency, duration and intervention period.

Exercise type: aerobic training consistently improved fatigue, aerobic capacity and depression through mitochondrial enhancement and anti-inflammatory effects. Resistance training more effectively improved muscle strength, physical function and pain via mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) activation and fibre remodelling. Combined training showed no synergistic benefits, possibly due to reduced adherence.

Intensity: Moderate-intensity protocols (50–70% HRmax or 60–75% 1RM) produced consistent effects by balancing inflammation suppression with metabolic activation. High-intensity training showed potential but requires further investigation for safety and adherence.

Frequency and duration: sessions of 2–3 times weekly for 30–60 min provided optimal balance with adequate recovery. An 8–12-week intervention period was sufficient for clinically meaningful improvements in aerobic capacity, fatigue and function.

In summary, moderate-intensity aerobic training, optionally supplemented with low-frequency RE, performed 2–3 times per week for 30–60 min over 8–12 weeks, appears most effective and feasible for stable-phase SLE.

Study limitations

This analysis followed rigorous methodological standards (PRISMA, ROB-2, GRADE) but has important limitations. Included studies had small samples and methodological concerns, particularly related to insufficient reporting of randomisation, allocation concealment, blinding and adherence. Although all included RCTs were assessed using the Cochrane ROB V.2.0 tool, several outcomes were informed by studies with some concerns or high risk of bias. Risk of bias was not used as an exclusion criterion; instead, its potential influence was addressed through leave-one-out sensitivity analyses and incorporated into outcome-level GRADE ratings, leading to the downgrading of evidence certainty for outcomes such as aerobic capacity and disease activity. These findings should, therefore, be interpreted with caution.

Intervention parameters were inconsistently defined, and most studies lacked medium-term to long-term follow-up data on benefit durability and flare prevention. Participants were predominantly middle-aged women, limiting generalisability to other populations. These constraints underscore the need for larger, standardised trials with diverse populations before broad clinical implementation.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis demonstrated that exercise interventions provide significant multidimensional benefits for patients with SLE, particularly improving fatigue, depression, pain, physical function and aerobic capacity. Moderate-intensity aerobic or resistance training performed 2–3 times weekly for 8–12 weeks is recommended as the optimal intervention model. These findings provide evidence-based guidance for SLE rehabilitation and emphasise the need for individualised, professionally supervised exercise prescriptions to ensure safety and efficacy. Emerging technologies such as wearable devices and artificial intellgence (AI)-driven models may enhance personalisation and adherence in future programmes.
