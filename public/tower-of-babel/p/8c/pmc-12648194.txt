Role of Polyamines in Mitigating Salinity Stress in Ornamental and Food Crops
Authors: Soheyla Mohammadi Alagoz, Behnam Asgari Lajayer, Zahra Azimzadeh, Younes Kheirizadeh Arough, Bernard Dell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSoil and irrigation salinity continue to have a major impact on the world's agriculture and horticulture, and loss of plant production is likely to worsen with global warming and climate change. Efforts to mitigate salinity stress and breed better salt‐tolerant plants rely on our knowledge of plant response to abiotic stress at the physiological and molecular levels. Salinity usually leads to the accumulation of free and conjugated polyamines (PAs) in plant tissues. Putrescine (Put), and its derivatives spermine (Spm) and spermidine (Spd), perform critical functions by activating biochemical, physiological and molecular defense systems, thus reducing damage caused by salinity stress. Promoting endogenous levels of PAs can improve the salt tolerance of plants. Furthermore, the application of exogenous PAs has been shown to effectively mitigate salt stress across a range of commercially important plant species. This review critically examines the biosynthesis of PAs and their associated physiological, phytochemical, and molecular responses in plants under saline conditions. In addition, it evaluates the potential of PAs as a strategic tool for enhancing salinity tolerance. The review also highlights key gaps in current knowledge and proposes directions for future research to optimize the use of PAs in salinity stress management.

Introduction

Unfavorable environmental conditions from salinity, drought, temperature extremes and heavy metals adversely affect crop yields by restricting plant growth, development, and survival (Napieraj et al. 2023). These abiotic stresses disrupt physiological, biochemical and molecular processes, which often lead to the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative stress (Zulfiqar and Ashraf 2021). Production of ornamental and food crops is increasingly vulnerable to global warming and the associated changes in precipitation and extreme weather events. Approximately 3% of the world's agricultural land and 20% of irrigated land are affected by salinity (FAO 2020; Harper et al. 2021); thus, salinity is a serious threat to sustainable agriculture, horticulture, and food security (Mukhopadhyay et al. 2021; Tarolli et al. 2024). Furthermore, soil salinity is predicted to increase due to global warming, climate change, sea level rise, and increased evaporation (Zaman et al. 2018). In most crop plants, damage from salinity stress results from osmotic stress, ion toxicity, and oxidative stress (El‐Beltagi et al. 2022) or a combination of these (Ramadan et al. 2022; Raza et al. 2023).

Polyamines (PAs) are low‐molecular‐weight, pleiotropic amines with diverse biological activities (Blázquez 2024; Kolesnikov et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2024), including regulating abiotic and biotic stress responses in plants (Hasan et al. 2021). Putrescine (Put), and its derivatives spermine (Spm) and spermidine (Spd), perform critical functions in physiological and developmental processes in response to abiotic stress (Hasan et al. 2021; Li et al. 2024; Rahman et al. 2024; Toledo et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2024). The presence of PAs as polycations at physiological pH makes them attractive to nucleic acids and cell membrane phospholipids (Aktar et al. 2021). Polyamines regulate replication and transcription processes by improving enzyme activity, modulating cell division and membrane stability, and they are involved in biological activities dependent on cell growth (Mustafavi et al. 2018; Pál et al. 2018). In general, the levels of PAs increase in plants exposed to stress (Alcázar et al. 2020) and externally applied PAs have a beneficial effect on stressed plants (Amiri et al. 2024). Also, PAs exhibit poly‐pathway reactions in plant response and tolerance to stress (Du et al. 2022). In addition to plant stress responses, many processes of plant growth and development, fruit ripening and senescence are associated with the presence of PAs (Lenis et al. 2017).

Given the present impact of soil salinity and irrigation salinity on food production and increasing risks from global warming and climate change, addressing salinity stress and breeding more salt‐tolerant crop varieties are vexing problems facing scientists. Hence, the role of polyamines in increasing stress tolerance in plants is an important topic not only for horticulturalists, agronomists, and agriculturalists, but also for crop physiologists and molecular plant breeders. This review covers the biosynthesis of polyamines and their role in mitigating salinity stress. It identifies research gaps and research directions that can advance our understanding of PAs in overcoming salinity stress.

Polyamine Biosynthesis in Plants

The formation of Put is the first step in the synthesis of polyamines (Figure 1). Put production in plants and animals can be produced by the direct carboxylation of ornithine (Orn) through the activity of ornithine decarboxylase (ODC) (Blázquez 2024). Plants can transform arginine (Arg) into Put through a pathway that involves three successive steps: Arg is decarboxylated to agmatine (Agm) by arginine decarboxylase (ADC), agmatine iminohydrolase (AIH) converts Agm to N‐carbamoylputrescine (NCP), and finally N‐carbamoylputrescine aminohydroxylase (CPA) acts to produce Put. In plants, the specific role of arginase in the conversion of Arg and Orn has not been studied in detail, but the importance of this pathway lies in the synthesis of other metabolites and signaling molecules beyond polyamines (Siddappa and Marathe 2020). Put is then used by several plant lineages to initiate the synthesis of a specific family of alkaloids through the activity of Put N‐methyltransferase (Teuber et al. 2007) or to generate H2O2 and 4‐aminobutanal, and ultimately 1‐pyrroline, in a reaction catalyzed by diamine oxidase (DAO). Most importantly, Put is the initiator for the synthesis of triamine and tetraamine via aminopropyltransferases. First, Spd synthase (SPDS) transfers the aminopropyl moiety of decarboxylated S‐adenosyl methionine (dcSAM) to Put, and then Spm is formed by the symmetrical addition to Spd of another aminopropyl group by Spm synthase (SPMS). Alternatively, the attachment of the aminopropyl group to the opposite end of the Spd molecule is carried out by Tspm synthase (TSPMS), which also converts the Spm isomer. The most important part of this step, for reactions catalyzed by aminopropyltransferases, is the production of dcSAM as an aminopropyl donor via S‐adenosylmethionine decarboxylase (SAMDC) (Roje 2006). One of the mechanisms for maintaining polyamine homeostasis and generating H2O2 for different cellular processes is the oxidation of Spd and tetraamines by polyamine oxidases (PAOs) (Salvi and Tavladoraki 2020). It has been suggested that there are two physically separate pathways for Put biosynthesis within cells, as ODC is associated with the cytosol as a cytosolic enzyme, whereas ADC is present in photosynthetic tissues, including the thylakoid membrane of chloroplasts (Borrell et al. 1995), and the nucleus of non‐photosynthetic tissues (Bortolotti et al. 2004). By contrast, DAO and PAO are localized in the cytosol and in peroxisomes (Salvi and Tavladoraki 2020) and can also be found associated with cell walls (Kaur‐Sawhney et al. 1981). The interaction of SPDS and SPMS with each other has been shown and may, under certain conditions, drive the conversion of Put to Spm by forming a multimer complex (Panicot et al. 2002). Studies have shown that the nucleus is the site of interaction between aminopropyltransferases (Belda‐Palazón et al. 2012), but this does not involve TSPMS.

Polyamines and Salinity Stress Tolerance

Polyamines are essential for growth and physiological processes and modulate the defense response of plants to abiotic stress (Choudhary et al. 2023). Polyamines play a role in cell wall stabilization under stress conditions and they can have strong antioxidant activities (Anam et al. 2024). The composition of PAs appears to be important in the plant's response to and tolerance of stress. In barrel medic ( Medicago truncatula ), Antoniou et al. (2021) showed that highly polycationic PAs were associated with genotype sensitivity to salinity. Using a combined metabolomics and lipidomics approach, Buffagni et al. (2022) identified specific PA effects in tomato plants applied with foliar sprays of 0.2 mM Put, Spd, and Spm. Promoting endogenous synthesis and use of exogenous PAs, as well as genetic engineering of PA biosynthetic genes, provides flexibility in breeding and managing crops for stressful conditions (Tyagi et al. 2023; Yang et al. 2024). The roles of endogenous and exogenous PAs in salinity tolerance are considered separately in the following two sections.

Salinity Tolerance From Endogenous Polyamine Content

Polyamines play a role in reducing the effects of salinity stress by activating biochemical, physiological, and molecular defense systems (Rathinapriya et al. 2020). In general, PAs increase the survival of plants in unfavorable environmental conditions (Kuznetsov and Shevyakova 2007). Many studies have investigated the accumulation of stress‐related PAs (Alet et al. 2012; Blázquez 2024; Kolesnikov et al. 2024). Under salinity stress, the concentration of PA changes; in many cases, Put is decreased, while the Spd and/or Spm concentrations are enhanced (Zapata et al. 2004). Tolerance to salt stress is related to the increase in the (Spd + Spm)/Put ratio. In arabidopsis ( Arabidopsis thaliana ) and sunflower ( Helianthus annuus ), the concentration of endogenous PA increased with exposure to salinity (Quinet et al. 2010). Rapid and temporary accumulation of Put and Spm occurred in roots of yellow lupin ( Lupinus luteus ) seedlings under salt stress (Legocka and Kluk 2005). During PA catabolism, the formation of amine oxidases, including copper‐bound DAOs and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD)‐bound amine oxidase, led to increased stress tolerance in plants (Gupta and Huang 2014).

Adaptation to salinity stress is partly achieved by reducing Na+ absorption and K+ leakage, which is aided by PAs, regulating ion channel activities via direct binding, such as K+ channels in guard cells (Alcázar et al. 2010). However, not all PA‐mediated effects on ion channels involve direct interaction; in certain plant species, cytoplasmic signaling mechanisms have been implicated (Alcázar et al. 2010). For instance, the inhibition of inward K+ channels (IKin) in guard cells of broad bean ( Vicia faba ) (Liu et al. 2000) and nonselective cation channels (NSCCs), which are permeable to both K+ and Na+, in mesophyll protoplasts of pea ( Pisum sativum ) (Shabala et al. 2007) appears to be mediated through cytoplasmic pathways rather than direct channel blockage. In these cases, PAs function as signaling molecules rather than mere substrates (Liu et al. 2000; Shabala et al. 2007). The regulation of ion channels by PAs is integral to their broader role in abiotic stress responses. Notably, PAs at micromolar concentrations have been shown to inhibit both inward and outward currents through NSCCs in pea mesophyll protoplasts, thereby contributing to salinity tolerance by limiting Na+ influx and K+ loss (Shabala et al. 2007). In cucumber ( Cucumis sativus ), Ma et al. (2022) reported that, under salt stress, stomatal aperture is regulated by Put due to induction of the H2O2 signal and degradation of PA by DAO, increasing glutathione (GSH) levels and disrupting accumulation of the phytohormone abscisic acid (ABA).

A positive correlation has been observed with an increase in the level of endogenous PAs, especially Spd, and an increase in antioxidant capacity (Amri et al. 2011). It has also been reported that plant growth is improved under saline conditions through antioxidants' activation by Put, which prevents oxidative stress, and inhibits ROS production and lipid peroxidation (Amri et al. 2011).

Enhancing Salinity Tolerance From Exogenous Application of Polyamines

Exogenous PAs have been shown to improve the salinity tolerance of many plants (Amiri et al. 2024; Shabani Fard et al. 2024) (Table 1). In general, under stress conditions, the application of exogenous PAs increases the level of endogenous PAs. As highlighted by Shabala and Munns (2017), the exogenous application of PAs, through seed treatment or foliar spraying, has been shown to enhance plant tolerance to salinity stress. Empirical evidence indicates that externally applied PAs contribute to the stabilization of plant cell membranes under stress conditions. Simultaneously, endogenous PAs play a critical role in maintaining membrane integrity, underscoring their dual function in stress protection. The application of exogenous PAs has been demonstrated to strengthen crop resilience against a broad spectrum of abiotic stresses. For instance, exogenous Put has been reported to improve plant tolerance to salinity, low and high temperatures, drought, heavy metal toxicity, osmotic stress, waterlogging, and flooding. In addition, genetic engineering approaches involving the overexpression of PA biosynthetic genes, such as those encoding ADC, ODC, SAMDC, and SPDS, have been effective in enhancing stress tolerance across various plant species. Transgenic plants overexpressing ADC, SPDS, or SAMDC have exhibited substantial resistance to multiple environmental stresses, including salinity, drought, temperature extremes, and heavy metal exposure, suggesting a robust and multifaceted defense mechanism. This capacity for multi‐stress tolerance is particularly relevant given that plants often face simultaneous environmental challenges throughout their developmental cycle, as illustrated in Figure 2 (Gill and Tuteja 2010).

Under experimental conditions, PAs have been applied to the foliage of containerized plants in growth chambers or greenhouses, to seed just prior to germination, and to the roots of plants grown in hydroponic culture. For example, soaking tomato ( Solanum lycopersicum ) seed with 0.25 mM Spd increased the conversion of Put to Spd and Spm in roots and reduced damage from salinity stress (Hu et al. 2012). In similar stress conditions, 0.25 mM Spd applied to the foliage reduced SOD production and lipid peroxidation in tomato seedlings (Zhang et al. 2016).

Many physiological and biochemical effects have been observed across a range of stressed plant species. Treatment with different types and concentrations of PAs reduces tissue damage from salinity stress by regulating ion exchange and enhancing the accumulation of proline and antioxidants (Lou et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2018). Correlations have been observed between PA‐treated plants and maintenance of membrane integrity, accumulation of compatible osmolytes, reduction in ROS, decreased organelle Na+ and Cl− concentrations, and less negative effects on the photosynthetic apparatus compared to non‐treated plants (Gupta and Huang 2014) (Table 1). Improvement in ion balance and increased resilience to salinity may occur at high levels of cellular PA due to modulation of plasma membrane ion channel activity, preventing K+ efflux from the leaf mesophyll (Shabala et al. 2007).

In canola ( Brassica napus ) experiencing salinity stress, foliar Spd significantly increased plant growth, and this was attributed to enhanced chlorophyll content, improved photosystem II (PSII) efficiency, and reduced ROS and lipid peroxidation (ElSayed et al. 2022). Reduced lipid peroxidation, enhanced activity of ROS‐inhibiting enzymes, and quenching of free radicals due to binding of PAs to membranes have been reported in a number of other studies (Talaat 2015). More recently, similar findings have been reported for Bougainvillea buttiana Lin et al. (2021) and nasturtium ( Tropaeolum majus ) Santos Filho et al. (2022).

Under saline conditions, cereal growth is enhanced by the application of exogenous PAs. In barley seedlings, exogenous Spd and Put partially reduced salinity damage by preventing decreases in phospholipid and PA concentrations in tonoplast vesicles (Zhao and Qin 2004). Zhao et al. (2023) found that 0.5–3.0 mM Put enhanced tolerance to salinity, and this was associated with stronger ascorbate (ASC) and glutathione metabolism, increased photosynthesis and Na+/K+ homeostasis in wheat ( Triticum aestivum ). Application of 0.25 mM Spd to sweet sorghum ( Sorghum bicolor ) seedlings increased the transcript level and activity of Rubisco, the key CO2 assimilation enzyme, thus enhancing photosynthetic efficiency (El Sayed et al. 2019). Furthermore, Spd, as a signal transducing molecule, induced the expression of stress‐related protein genes, which subsequently increased the soluble protein content in leaves of oat ( Avena sativa ) seedlings under salt stress (Hai et al. 2022). In addition, Spd appears to play a role in protein post‐translational processing via covalent binding to the original protein in the cell (Hai et al. 2022). It may stabilize the structure of proteins and slow down their degradation (Kasukabe et al. 2004). Foliar application of Spd increased the soluble sugar content of oat seedlings under stress by increasing carbohydrate metabolism, and glucose, fructose, and sucrose synthase activities (Yan et al. 2012).

In salinity‐sensitive rice ( Oryza sativa ) cultivars, application of Put led to a reduction in Na+ accumulation, an increase in Put biosynthesis, and activation of genes encoding amine oxidases (Quinet et al. 2010). More recently, Shen et al. (2024) found that Spd reduced malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in rice. They concluded that Spd alleviates salt stress by boosting antioxidant enzyme activity and decreasing levels of peroxides in roots. In cucumber plants, exogenous Put improved photochemical efficiency and photosynthesis, reducing the harmful impact of salinity (Zhang et al. 2009).

In grapevine ( Vitis vinifera ), 1 mM Spd reduced salt stress and this was associated with decreased tissue Na+ and Cl− concentrations, decreased membrane permeability and lower MDA in a greenhouse trial (Shokri et al. 2024). In another grapevine experiment, exogenous Spd increased the activity of antioxidant enzymes, ion transport, and endogenous PA content, which led to enhanced salinity stress tolerance (Xu et al. 2024). Exogenous PAs (2 mM Put, Spd) partially protected rooted pomegranate ( Punica granatum ) cuttings from salinity (Amri et al. 2011). Salt‐stressed tea ( Camellia sinensis ) cuttings benefitted from foliar‐applied 5 mM Put, which promoted photosynthesis and increased ROS scavenging [enhanced levels of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and peroxidase (POD)] (Xiong et al. 2018). Also, foliar PA had a beneficial effect on the growth of containerized chili ( Capsicum annuum ) (Subhi and Kisko 2024).

Exogenous PAs may offer benefits for turf farming and golf‐course management. In Kentucky bluegrass ( Poa pratensis ), 1 mM Spd applied as a foliar spray reduced oxidative damage and promoted salinity tolerance (Puyang et al. 2015). Spraying 0.15–0.45 mM Spd increased the activities of ADC, SAMDC, and DAO, leading to enhanced salt tolerance in hydroponic zoysia grass ( Zoysia japonica ) (Li et al. 2017). Shi et al. (2013) used proteomics to investigate polyamine‐mediated responses in Bermuda grass ( Cynodon dactylon ). Among the 36 proteins identified, PAs led to the enrichment of proteins related to electron transport and energy pathways. Also, three antioxidant enzymes and nucleoside diphosphate kinase (NDPK) were significantly regulated (Shi et al. 2013). These authors concluded that PAs have the ability to activate multiple pathways and improve the adaptation of Bermuda grass to salinity and drought stress.

An efficient method to increase stress tolerance is seed priming with PAs because it may protect young plants against salinity stress at a low cost (Paul and Roychoudhury 2017). The negative effects of salinity on germination and early seedling growth in belladonna ( Atropa belladonna ) were alleviated by seed pretreatment with 0.1 mM Put, which enhanced the accumulation of endogenous PA and secondary metabolites (Ali 2000). Soaking glutinous rice seeds with Spd increased germination and seedling growth (Chunthaburee et al. 2014). This was attributed to a reduction in chlorophyll damage, increases in anthocyanin and other phenolic compounds, and decreases in H2O2 and proline levels.

Polyamines and Gene Expression Under Salinity Stress

Several genes related to PAs have been found to be effective in salinity tolerance. The content of PA in rice plants increased along with a rise in ADC activity under salt stress, followed by improved plant growth (Todorova et al. 2013). Shen et al. (2024) reported that exogenous Spd enhanced the resilience of rice at both the physiological and transcriptomic levels under salinity stress. In particular, Spd improved plant tolerance by affecting key gene pathways, including mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling, hormone signal transduction, and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis. In addition, affinity assays revealed that Spd enhances OsbZIP73's protein–RNA binding, suggesting the formation of protective biomolecular condensates that compartmentalize and shield biomolecules from stress‐induced damage. Cis‐element and promoter analyses identified OsPYL1, OsPYL3, OsPYL4, OsPYL6, OsSPARK1, and various small auxin up‐regulated RNA (SAUR) family genes containing OsbZIP73‐responsive sequences, highlighting their roles in maintaining the resilience of rice to salinity stress. It can be concluded that, in rice and other crops, the application of exogenous Spd increases salt stress resilience and reduces its effects by modulating OsbZIP73 activity (Shen et al. 2024).

In rice, tobacco ( Nicotiana tabacum ) and arabidopsis, increased salinity tolerance and consequently enhanced plant growth have been observed with overexpression of SAMDC (Alcázar et al. 2020). Increasing Na+ accumulation and induction of oxidative stress under salinity conditions in the mutants acl5‐1, spms‐1, and double acl5‐1/spms‐1 [synthesis of spermine and thermospermine (tSpm) genes] indicate that PAs play a role in increasing salt tolerance (Alet et al. 2012). Xu et al. (2024) reported that in grapevine leaves under salinity–alkali stress, exogenous Spd reduced Na+ accumulation and K+ content by increasing the expression level of VvNHXP (Na+/H+ antiporter) and VvHKT2 (K transporter) genes. Furthermore, endogenous free Spd and Spm accumulated in response to exogenous Spd application. In grapevine seedlings, the free Put concentration in leaves decreased under saline–alkaline conditions (Xu et al. 2024). Overall, these results indicate that the effect of salinity–alkalinity stress in grapevine seedlings was reduced by exogenous Spd.

Increased activity of antioxidant enzymes and decreased MDA accumulation have been observed with overexpression of SPDS1 in apple ( Malus domestica ) seedlings under salinity stress (Neily et al. 2011). A key polyamine biosynthesis gene is polyamine oxidase 5 (PAO5). In arabidopsis, loss‐of‐function mutations in atpao5‐2 and atpao5‐3 have been associated with elevated PA accumulation and enhanced tolerance to salinity stress (Zarza et al. 2017). In contrast, reduced activity of ADC, in combination with mutations in spe1‐1 and spe2‐1, leads to diminished PA levels and a corresponding decrease in salt stress tolerance. These findings underscore the critical role of PA biosynthesis and catabolism in modulating plant responses to salinity (Zarza et al. 2017). In cucumber, exogenous PAs (particularly Put) induced ADP‐glucose pyrophosphorylase (AGPase) and β‐amylase (BAM) gene expression and enhanced endogenous PA content via the ADC pathway, which led to regulation of AGPase and β‐amylase enzyme activity, and as a result decreased metabolism (Shen et al. 2019).

Excessive accumulation of exogenous PAs in leaves protects photosynthetic pigments and leads to tolerance to salinity stress (Shen et al. 2019). In arabidopsis, activation of AtADC2 under salinity stress results in an increase in Put content and contributes to stress tolerance (Duan et al. 2008). In salt‐stressed arabidopsis, increased AtADC2 gene expression led to the accumulation of endogenous Put (Urano et al. 2004). However, under saline conditions, there was a 25% reduction in Put content in adc2‐1 mutants, and damage was reversed with the exogenous application of Put (Urano et al. 2004). Jasso‐Robles et al. (2024) found that the Atadc2 arabidopsis mutant, characterized by reduced endogenous Put levels, showed insensitivity to Put priming without growth enhancement, which was linked to significant imbalances in N metabolism, including a high Gln/Glu ratio. In contrast, Atnata2 mutants showed significant growth improvement and upregulated AtADC2 expression, particularly under Put priming, highlighting the involvement of these genes in regulating plant development. Put‐priming enhanced plant growth by inducing the accumulation of specific polyamines (free, acetylated, conjugated, or bound) and improving light‐harvesting efficiency, particularly in the Atnata2 line. Jasso‐Robles et al. (2024) concluded that in chloroplasts, AtNATA2 may negatively regulate Put synthesis and accumulation via AtADC2 and affect light harvesting in photosystem II (PSII). Furthermore, the Atadc2 mutant line exhibited upregulated AtADC1 but reduced AcPut levels, pointing to cross‐regulation among these genes. The regulation by AtNATA2 on AtADC2 and AtADC2 on AtADC1 could be crucial for plant growth and overall stress tolerance by interacting with polyamine catabolism, which shapes the plant metabolic profile under different growth conditions.

In addition, due to the induction of the antioxidant defense system, damage to the photosynthetic system was reduced when salinity‐stressed rice was exposed to Spd (Jiang et al. 2020). Applying Spd increased the expression of genes involved in gibberellin (GA) biosynthesis and activation, including the GT‐3b transcription factor, resulting in stimulation of endogenous GA (Wang et al. 2020). Moreover, spraying GA made it easier for cucumber plants to tolerate Spd‐induced salinity (Wang et al. 2020). Further interactions between PAs and phytohormones such as GA are explored in more detail in the next section. Examples of improvements in salt tolerance using exogenous Spd are shown in Figure 3. By activating autophagy‐related genes (ATG), exogenous Spd enhanced salt tolerance; furthermore, Spd promoted respiratory burst oxidase homologs (RBOHs), which increased H2O2 production (Zhang et al. 2021). Moreover, exogenous Spd greatly diminished the negative impacts of salinity on tomato seedlings by activating the H2O2‐mediated signaling pathway, which is responsible for raising RBOH1 expression and the SlMYB102 (SlHKT1), SLRKY1, and SLDDREB2 genes, by enhancing osmotic compounds and antioxidation (Raziq et al. 2022). Salinity tolerance in cucumber has been positively associated with the exogenous application of PA. Specifically, Put has been shown to modulate stomatal behavior through the activation of H2O2‐mediated signaling pathways. This signaling cascade facilitates the catabolism of PAs via DAO enzymes, resulting in elevated levels of GSH and a concomitant reduction in ABA accumulation under salt stress conditions (Ma et al. 2022). These biochemical adjustments contribute to improved photosynthetic efficiency in cucumber leaves by optimizing stomatal movement.

Exogenous Spd enhances salt tolerance via modulation of GA and PA biosynthesis pathways. Under salt stress, exogenous Spd elevates endogenous GA3 levels by up‐regulating gibberellin biosynthetic genes and enhancing GA3‐oxidase and GA20‐oxidase enzyme activities. Spd also induces GT‐3b expression, which interacts with the GT‐1 element in the promoter region of the S‐adenosylmethionine synthase gene (CsSAMS) in cucumber, thereby promoting polyamine biosynthesis. The coordinated regulation of gibberellins and polyamines mitigates damage to the photosynthetic apparatus and strengthens plant resilience to salinity stress (Wang et al. 2020).

Polyamines function as signaling molecules that modulate the expression of various stress‐responsive genes. In the ornamental species Hibiscus rosa‐sinensis , the salt‐tolerant cultivar ‘Porto’ exhibited distinct gene expression profiles compared to the salt‐sensitive cultivar ‘Sunny Wind’ under saline conditions (Trivellini et al. 2023). Notably, the AP2/EREBP family transcription factor ERF‐1 was significantly upregulated in the tolerant cultivar, indicating a potentially more robust regulatory network for stress adaptation (Trivellini et al. 2023). In other plant species, the accumulation of Spd has been demonstrated to enhance the expression of stress‐responsive transcription factors such as DREB (Dehydration Response Element Binding Factor) (Kasukabe et al. 2004). Furthermore, the gene NCED, which plays a key role in ABA biosynthesis, was downregulated in the tolerant Hibiscus cultivar, corresponding with reduced ABA levels in leaf tissues (Trivellini et al. 2023). The accumulation of Spd has also been associated with the upregulation of genes encoding stress‐protective proteins, including RD29A (Response to Desiccation) (Kasukabe et al. 2004).

Exogenous application of Spd has been shown to mitigate salt‐induced damage in Gladiolus gandavensis seedlings by enhancing physiological and molecular responses. These include increased relative electrical conductivity (REC), elevated proline content, improved gas exchange parameters, and heightened antioxidant enzyme activities. Additionally, Spd treatment upregulated the expression of several stress‐related genes, including CAT1 (Catalase), POD (Peroxidase), MnSODM (Manganese superoxide dismutase), P5CS (1‐Pyrroline‐5‐carboxylate synthetase), and BADH (Betaine‐aldehyde dehydrogenase) (Qian et al. 2021).

Interplay Between Polyamines, Phytohormones and Salinity Stress

Adverse environmental conditions, such as salinity, have been shown to induce changes in PA levels in many plant species (Tiburcio et al. 2014). There is considerable crosstalk between phytohormones and PAs in stressed plants, and interactions vary with individual PAs and phytohormone type. For convenience, phytohormones can be placed in two groups: the first comprising positive growth regulators, mainly auxins, cytokinins (CKs), gibberellins (GAs), and brassinosteroids (BRs); and the second containing stress‐related compounds such as abscisic acid (ABA), jasmonic acid (JA), salicylic acid (SA), and ethylene (ETH) (Li et al. 2018). Interactions between polyamines and phytohormones in response to salinity stress.

Considering the stress‐related phytohormones first, many studies have investigated ETH and PAs in plants experiencing salinity stress. The increase in tolerance of arabidopsis to salt stress by application of ETH or ACC is mediated via the expression of genes related to the inhibition of ROS (Peng et al. 2014), while no increase in plant sensitivity to salinity results from inhibition of ETH biosynthesis (Tao et al. 2015). The PA biosynthetic pathway is connected with ETH biosynthesis as S‐adenosyl‐L‐methionine is a common precursor of both. Ethylene induction is associated with PAO, suggesting that the ETH biosynthetic apparatus can be affected by deregulation of PA homeostasis (Hou et al. 2013). Thus, it is of interest to know whether ETH biosynthetic genes under salt stress are affected by PAO deregulation. Li et al. (2004) reported that, during osmotic stress, a reduction in H2O2 production occurs through PAO‐mediated inhibition of ACC synthase, and ETH strongly induces PAO (Gil‐Amado and Gomez‐Jimenez 2012).

In plants exposed to salinity, the concentrations of Na+ and K+ are sensitive to the ETH content in xylem tissue (Sharma et al. 2019). Quinet et al. (2010) showed that in saline conditions, exogenous Put increased the synthesis of ETH in two rice cultivars. Under salinity stress, the content of ETH, Spd, and Spm increased in salinity‐sensitive species (Zapata et al. 2017). Also, high levels of SAM support the biosynthesis of both PA and ETH during salinity stress (Zapata et al. 2017). Because PA catabolism is important in plant stress responses, crosstalk between PA catabolism and ETH signaling has been investigated. Transcript levels of genes encoding 1‐aminocyclopropane‐carboxylate synthase (ACS) and 1‐amino‐cyclopropane‐carboxylate oxidase (ACO) were higher in transgenic tobacco plants showing lower PAO activity compared to plants with higher PAO activity (Gémes et al. 2017). The conversion of SAM to 1‐aminocyclopropane‐1‐carboxylic acid (ACC) is catalyzed by ACS, whereas the conversion of ACC to ETH is carried out by ACO (Yang and Hoffman 1984). Freitas et al. (2018) revealed the complex interplay between ETH and PA expression in salt‐tolerant maize ( Zea mays ). Briefly, during the first phase of exposure to NaCl, there is a peak in ETH synthesis; ETH then binds to receptors eliciting DAO gene expression, and the subsequent H2O2 production induces the formation of PAs and activation of the PA anabolic pathway.

There are also documented interactions between PAs and the other stress‐related hormones, particularly JA and ABA. Arabidopsis has been used as a model to reveal how PAs affect the regulation of JA content in salt‐stressed plants. Zarza et al. (2017) looked at the PA back‐conversion pathway using several loss‐of‐function polyamine oxidase (PAO) mutants and found that the atpao5‐3 mutant showed significantly increased JA accumulation under salt stress conditions (Zarza et al. 2017). Furthermore, there was a direct effect of exogenous tSpm on the expression of JA‐related genes. In cucumber plants, the increases in tissue levels of JA and SA from salt stress were reversed by applying exogenous Spd (Radhakrishnan and Lee 2013).

Under salinity stress, ABA triggers stomatal closure and helps to maintain osmotic homeostasis (Zhao et al. 2021). Pál et al. (2018) applied Put and ABA to wheat plants experiencing osmotic stress (PEG‐induced) to study interactions of PAs, ABA, and proline; and found that ABA is involved in the regulation of PA and proline biosynthetic pathways. They concluded that ABA signaling “leads to the controlled regulation and maintenance of polyamine and proline levels under osmotic stress conditions in wheat seedlings”. In a later study, Pál et al. (2019) found that PA metabolism differs between two reduced height (Rht) isogenic wheat lines. The observed genotypic differences in PA metabolism were closely associated with the activity of antioxidant enzymes, such as G‐POD and APX in Rht3 roots, as well as with variations in phytohormone levels, including SA, IAA, and ABA. These findings offer valuable insights into the regulatory role of PAs in modulating plant growth and stress responses in gibberellin‐insensitive Rht mutants.

Many studies have investigated links between PAs and plant growth hormones. For example, BRs and PAs can have a positive interaction in plants affected by salt stress (Sharma et al. 2014). In lettuce ( Lactuca sativa ), tolerance to salinity increased due to modulation of homeostasis and distribution of PAs as a result of the exogenous application of a BR analog (Serna et al. 2015). The interaction between PAs and BRs can elicit differential physiological responses depending on the severity of salt stress. Under high‐salinity conditions, the application of 24‐epibrassinolide (EBL) has been shown to enhance salt tolerance in canola by attenuating PA‐mediated H2O2 accumulation and reducing DAO activity. In contrast, under low‐salinity stress, the inhibitory effects of EBL on canola growth were alleviated through the downregulation of Put biosynthesis and the associated reduction in H2O2 production. These findings underscore the context‐dependent nature of PA–BR interactions in modulating plant stress responses (Liu et al. 2020).

Changes in GA synthesis and/or degradation can strongly affect plant growth under stress (Banerjee and Roychoudhury 2019), and changes in GAs biosynthesis can affect PA homeostasis (Alharbi et al. 2020). Furthermore, GAs and PAs can interact when plants are exposed to salinity. Priming with Spd and GA3 partially reversed increases in the Na+/K+ ratio, proline and H2O2 contents in salt‐tolerant and salt‐sensitive rice cultivars (Chunthaburee et al. 2014). In fennel (Foeniculum vulgar), Put, Spd, and Spm were elevated in seedlings exposed to 100 mM NaCl, and seed priming with 3 μM GA3 reduced PA levels in salt‐stressed seedlings (Attia et al. 2022).

The extent to which CKs and PAs crosstalk in plant response to salt stress is not known. Natural CKs such as zeatin (Z) and zeatin ribose (ZR) promote plant morphogenesis, growth and leaf longevity and protect plants against stress (Pavlů et al. 2018). In wheat, cowpea ( Vigna sinensis ) and luffa ( Luffa acutangular ), tolerance to salinity was enhanced by the simultaneous application of kinetin (KN, a synthetic CK) and PAs (Aldesuquy et al. 2014; Kapoor and Hasanuzzaman 2020). In wheat, KN and Spm had a positive effect on photosynthesis under salinity conditions. Also, seed priming with KN, Spm or KN + Spm reduced the negative impact of salinity stress in wheat by increasing leaf area, improving chloroplast ultrastructure and photosynthetic activity (Aldesuquy et al. 2014). In luffa, the co‐application of KN and Put reduced salinity stress, notably affecting the activity of enzymatic and non‐enzymatic antioxidants Kapoor and Hasanuzzaman (2020). The effect of using KN and Put together was higher than using each alone, which suggests there are mechanisms of regulatory interplay between KN and Put (Kapoor and Hasanuzzaman 2020).

Future Research and Applications in Ornamental and Food Crops

Polyamines play an important role in improving the growth and economic yield of ornamental and food crops experiencing salinity stress. Nevertheless, significant gaps remain in our understanding of PA regulation and modulation of molecular mechanisms. Crucial participants in PA signal reception and transduction, such as sensors, transporters, transcriptional factors, proteins, and metabolites, remain unexplored. In spite of these gaps in knowledge, it is well established that the use of exogenous PAs or PA synthesis stimulants, and the overexpression of key genes in plants, leads to increased tolerance of biotic and abiotic stresses (Pál et al. 2018). So far, most studies showing increased tolerance to salinity stress from exogenous PAs have been undertaken using seedlings or rooted cuttings in laboratory or greenhouse settings. Therefore, we do not know how long foliar applications remain effective or whether they are worthwhile in mature plants under field conditions of salinity stress. Hence, further studies are needed to optimize their field application and to fully understand their mechanisms of action. Conclusion

Abiotic and biotic stresses cause changes in the natural physiological processes of plants and reduce their productivity. Salinity stress is one of the major abiotic stresses that reduce the growth and productivity of many crops around the world. Maintaining crop yield under adverse environmental conditions is a major challenge facing modern agriculture, and polyamines play an important role in helping to alleviate stress. It is well established that natural intracellular polyamines such as spermine, spermidine, and putrescine are involved in eliminating free radicals and improving the tolerance of plants grown in saline soils or irrigated with saline water. It is evident that the precise regulation of polyamine metabolism plays an important role in growth and tolerance to environmental stress. However, more studies on the synthesis and metabolism of these compounds are needed. Expanding our understanding of biosynthesis and metabolic pathways, as well as the processes and mechanisms affected by polyamines, has the potential to provide new ways to increase plant tolerance to salt stress via molecular plant breeding and the use of new horticultural/agricultural sprays.
