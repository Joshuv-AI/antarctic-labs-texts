NUDT1 drives osteosarcoma progression and serves as a robust predictor of clinical outcomes
Authors: Zhe Zhang, Lu Wan, Kai Ai, Jiqiang Liu, Xianming Tang, Zhixi Duan, Ruiqi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteosarcoma (OS) is a primary bone malignancy characterized by high metastatic potential and poor prognosis. Nudix hydrolase 1 (NUDT1) plays a key role in preventing oxidative DNA damage, yet its functional significance in OS remains to be fully defined. By integrating transcriptomic data from the TARGET, GEO, and an independent cohort from our institution, we found that NUDT1 is significantly upregulated in OS tissues and cell lines. High NUDT1 expression serves as an independent predictor of unfavorable overall survival. Functional assays demonstrated that NUDT1 promotes OS cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro and accelerates tumor growth in vivo. Mechanistically, GSEA indicated that NUDT1 is associated with metabolic pathways including glycolysis and glutathione metabolism. We confirmed that NUDT1 maintains OS cell fitness by restraining ROS accumulation and limiting oxidative DNA damage, effects that were partially reversed by the antioxidant N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC). Furthermore, NUDT1 expression levels were found to correlate with the tumor immune microenvironment, specifically showing associations with the infiltration levels of CD8+ T cells and Naïve B cells. Finally, drug sensitivity profiling and experimental validation suggested that NUDT1 levels may predict response to topoisomerase inhibitors, such as Topotecan and Irinotecan. In conclusion, NUDT1 is a key oncogenic factor involved in redox homeostasis and is closely associated with the immune microenvironment in OS, representing a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-026-04347-7.Keywords: Osteosarcoma, Prognosis, NUDT1, Immune microenvironment

Background

Osteosarcoma (OS) is the most common primary malignant bone tumor in children and adolescents, characterized by its high recurrence rate, early metastasis, and poor clinical prognosis despite advances in multimodal therapy [1–3]. Although the introduction of neoadjuvant chemotherapy has improved patient survival over the past few decades, the five-year survival rate for localized OS remains stagnant at approximately 60–70%, while the five-year survival rate for metastatic or recurrent OS is less than 20% [4–6]. This limited progress underscores the urgent need to elucidate the molecular mechanisms driving OS progression and to identify reliable biomarkers for prognosis and therapeutic intervention.

Nudix hydrolase 1 (NUDT1, also known as MTH1) is a cancer-associated enzyme that hydrolyzes oxidized purine nucleotides, such as 8-oxo-dGTP, thereby preventing their incorporation into DNA and protecting cells from oxidative damage-induced lethality [7–9]. Accumulating evidence indicates that NUDT1 exerts oncogenic functions in multiple malignancies. For instance, a quantitative analysis of colorectal cancer (CRC) tissues found elevated 8-oxo-dGTPase activity of NUDT1 in tumor specimens, suggesting that NUDT1 contributes to oxidative-damage tolerance in CRC cells [10]. In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), overexpression of NUDT1 was associated with worse overall survival and progression-free survival, implicating it as a prognostic indicator in lung malignancies [11]. Furthermore, comprehensive pan-cancer profiling demonstrated that NUDT1 is frequently overexpressed in tumor tissues compared to normal counterparts, thereby functioning as an adaptive mechanism by which cancer cells counter increased ROS production and genome stress during proliferation. Importantly, both pharmacological and genetic disruption of NUDT1 have been shown to impair tumor growth by promoting accumulation of oxidized nucleotides and DNA damage, and in several contexts this approach enhances tumor responses to ROS-generating treatments or radiotherapy. For example, the small-molecule NUDT1 inhibitor TH1579 (karonudib) induces 8-oxo-dG accumulation, reduces proliferation and angiogenesis, and increases apoptosis in mesothelioma xenografts, supporting therapeutic activity of NUDT1 inhibition in vivo [12]. In OS specifically, the NUDT1 inhibitor TH1579 (karonudib) delays tumor growth and inhibits metastasis [13], while delivery of NUDT1 inhibitors via functionalized nanoparticles enhances chemo-photodynamic therapy efficacy [14], suggesting that NUDT1-targeted approaches may have therapeutic potential in this malignancy. Despite these promising observations, the expression patterns, prognostic significance, and mechanistic roles of NUDT1 in OS remain incompletely characterized. Previous studies primarily focused on proliferation and metastasis in limited models, and the relationships between NUDT1 expression, the tumor immune microenvironment, and drug sensitivity have not been systematically evaluated. Moreover, whether NUDT1 directly regulates oxidative stress and DNA damage to drive OS cell proliferation has not been established.

With the rapid advancement of high-throughput sequencing and computational biology, integrative multi-cohort analyses provide a powerful framework for identifying consistently dysregulated genes with clinical relevance in OS [15–17]. By combining bioinformatic analyses across multiple datasets with in vitro and in vivo experimental validation, it is possible to uncover biologically meaningful drivers of tumor progression and identify candidate prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

In this study, we applied a comprehensive bioinformatics workflow across multiple OS cohorts to investigate NUDT1. We demonstrate that NUDT1 is consistently upregulated, predicts poor prognosis, and promotes malignant phenotypes. Mechanistically, we reveal that NUDT1 sustains OS cell proliferation at least in part by restraining ROS accumulation and limiting ROS-associated DNA damage. Additionally, NUDT1 expression is associated with immune infiltration patterns and differential drug sensitivity. These findings consolidate and extend previous observations, providing novel mechanistic and therapeutic insights into the role of NUDT1 in OS.

Materials and methods

Data collection

The expression levels of NUDT1 across 33 cancer types and their corresponding normal tissues were analyzed using the Xiantao Academic Online Tool (https://www.xiantao.love/products). Gene expression data for OS were obtained from the GSE16088 dataset, which includes 14 tumor samples and 6 normal samples, and the GSE33382 dataset, which includes 84 tumor samples and 3 normal bone samples, from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). In addition, the GSE21257 dataset, comprising 53 OS patients, was utilized exclusively for survival analysis. Clinical information, including survival outcomes and other patient characteristics, as well as gene expression profiles for OS patients, were retrieved from the Therapeutically Applicable Research to Generate Effective Treatments database (TARGET, 88 tumor samples; normal tissue information not available, https://ocg.cancer.gov/programs/target). Additionally, long-read transcriptome and next-generation sequencing data from our own OS cohort (tumor samples:23, normal tissues:13) have been deposited in GEO under accession number GSE218035 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?&acc=GSE218035). When combing data from multiple cohorts, batch effects were corrected using the ComBat function from the sva R package prior to downstream analyses to reduce technical variability.

Prognostic analysis of NUDT1 in OS

The prognostic value of NUDT1 in OS was assessed using survival data from public databases. For survival analyses, patients were stratified into high- and low-NUDT1 expression groups based on the optimal cut-off point determined by maximally selected rank statistics for overall survival. Log-rank tests and Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to evaluate the predictive capacity of NUDT1 for patient survival. Both univariate and multivariate Cox analyses were performed to determine whether NUDT1 could serve as an independent prognostic factor. To enhance prognostic accuracy, a nomogram was constructed using the R package “rms”, enabling prediction of 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival rates for OS patients. The performance of the nomogram was further evaluated through calibration curves, time-dependent ROC curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA) using the R packages “caret”, “timeROC”, and “rmda”, respectively, to assess its predictive reliability and clinical utility.

Co-expression and functional enrichment analysis

Pearson correlation analysis was performed to quantify the strength of associations using correlation coefficients. The top five positively and top five negatively correlated genes were selected for further evaluation and visualized using circos plots and scatter plots to illustrate the co-expression patterns. To explore the potential biological functions and pathways associated with NUDT1, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted on high- versus low-NUDT1 groups defined by the median expression value. Analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler R package, with curated gene sets obtained from MSigDB. This approach allowed the systematic identification of enriched pathways and biological processes potentially underlying NUDT1-mediated effects in OS.

Analysis of immune cell infiltration

To investigate the relationship between NUDT1 expression and the tumor immune microenvironment in OS, patients were stratified into high- and low-NUDT1 expression groups based on the median expression value. Stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores were first calculated for all samples using the estimateScore algorithm. Differences and correlations of these scores between the high- and low-NUDT1 groups were subsequently assessed to evaluate how NUDT1 expression relates to the overall composition of the tumor microenvironment. The relative abundance and composition of tumor-infiltrating immune cells were further analyzed using the CIBERSORT algorithm, and comparisons were made between the high- and low-NUDT1 groups to identify specific immune cell populations associated with NUDT1 expression. In addition, single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) was performed using the GSVA R package to quantify infiltration levels of diverse immune cell types and assess differences between expression groups. Finally, the association between NUDT1 expression and key immune checkpoint genes was examined.

Drug sensitivity analysis

Drug sensitivity was evaluated using the pRRophetic R package, which integrates gene expression data with pharmacogenomic profiles from the GDSC (Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer) database to predict tumor responses to various chemotherapeutic agents. Patients were stratified into high- and low-NUDT1 expression groups based on the median expression value. The pRRophetic function was used to estimate IC50 values for multiple drugs based on a ridge regression model trained on the GDSC dataset, with batch effect correction and power transformation applied to improve prediction accuracy. Differences in predicted drug sensitivity between the high- and low-NUDT1 groups were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (P < 0.05). Results were visualized using ggplot2. All analyses were performed using publicly available GDSC data (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/), providing insights into the potential impact of NUDT1 expression on chemotherapy efficacy and its implications for personalized treatment strategies in OS.

Cell culture

Human OS cell lines 143B, U2OS, MG63, SJAS and HOS, as well as HEK-293T cells, were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). The human fetal osteoblastic cell line hFOB1.19 was used as a non-tumorigenic control. 143B and HEK-293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S) at 37℃ with 5% CO₂. U2OS cells were maintained in McCoy’s 5 A medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. MG63 and HOS cells were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. SJAS cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 Medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. hFOB1.19 cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 medium containing 0.3 mg/mL G418, 10% FBS, and 1% P/S at 34 °C, whereas all other cell lines were cultured at 37℃.

Lentiviral infection

NUDT1 knockdown cells were established using a lentiviral system [18]. Briefly, shRNA plasmids targeting NUDT1 (shNUDT1) or control plasmids (shNC) were co-transfected with packaging plasmids (pSPAX2) and envelope plasmid (pMD2.G) into HEK293T cells. Lentiviral supernatants were collected at 48 and 72 h post-transfection and stored at -80℃. Target OS cells were infected with the viral particles in the presence of 10 µg/mL polybrene overnight. The following day, the medium was replaced with fresh culture medium containing 2 µg/mL puromycin for selection. Experiments were conducted once all control cells had died, typically within 72–96 h of puromycin treatment.

Real-time PCR

Total RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) were performed according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Total RNA was isolated using the RNA Express Total RNA Kit (M050, NCM Biotech, China). Reverse transcription was carried out with the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (K1622, Thermo Scientific, USA). RT-qPCR was performed using Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (High Rox Plus) (11203ES, YEASEN Biotech, China). The primer sequences were as follows: NUDT1: forward 5′-GCTCATGGACGTGCATGTCTT-3′, reverse 5′-GTGGAAACCAGTAGCTGTCGT-3′. GAPDH: forward 5′-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3′, reverse 5′-GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCAT GG-3′. Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH as the internal reference.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, China) supplemented with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) and a protease inhibitor cocktail (MedChem-Express, China). Protein concentrations were quantified with a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Solarbio) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at 37℃ for 1 h and subsequently incubated overnight at 4℃ with the following primary antibodies (1:1000): NUDT1 (#16705-1-AP, Proteintech) and β-actin (#66009-1-Ig, Proteintech). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, including HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (#SA00001-1, Proteintech) and HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (#SA00001-2, Proteintech), at 37℃ for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (APExBIO, USA).

Cell proliferation assay (CCK8)

Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK8 (Dojindo, Japan) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, OS cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10³-3 × 10³ cells per well in 100µL of complete medium and allowed to adhere overnight. At the indicated time points (0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h), 10µL of CCK8 reagent was added to each well and incubated at 37℃ for 1–2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader to quantify cell viability.

Colony formation assay

OS cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10³ cells per well and maintained in complete medium for 7–10 days, allowing visible colonies to form. At the endpoint, cells were gently washed with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, and stained with crystal violet for an additional 30 min. Plates were imaged, and the number of colonies was quantified using ImageJ software.

Wound healing assay

A wound healing assay was performed to evaluate the migratory capacity of OS cells. Briefly, cells were seeded into 6-well plates and grown to a confluent monolayer. A straight scratch was then generated across the cell layer using a 200µL sterile pipette tip. Detached cells were removed by washing twice with PBS, and serum-free medium was added to minimize proliferation-related interference. Images of the wound area were captured at 0 h and at the indicated time points using an inverted microscope. The wound width was measured using ImageJ, and the migration rate was calculated by comparing the wound closure relative to the initial scratch width.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Transwell assays were performed to evaluate the migratory and invasive capacities of OS cells. For the migration assay, 1 × 10⁴ transfected cells were resuspended in 200µL serum-free medium and seeded into the upper chamber of a Transwell insert. The lower chamber was filled with 750µL medium containing 20% fetal bovine serum as a chemoattractant. After incubation for 24 h, cells on the upper surface of the membrane were removed with a cotton swab. Cells that had migrated to the lower surface were fixed in methanol for 30 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min, washed with PBS, and imaged. Cell numbers were quantified by counting randomly selected microscopic fields. For the invasion assay, the upper chamber was precoated with a layer of Matrigel (Corning, USA) diluted according to the manufacturer’s instructions and allowed to polymerize at 37℃. A total of 2 × 10⁴ cells in 200µL serum-free medium were seeded into the Matrigel-coated insert, and the remaining steps were performed as described for the migration assay.

In vivo xenograft models

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the second Xiangya Hospital. Four-week-old female BALB/c nude mice were used for the establishment of the xenograft model. Stable OS cell lines with NUDT1 knockdown were generated via lentiviral infection and puromycin selection prior to implantation. For subcutaneous xenografts, a total of 5 × 10⁶ cells suspended in 100 µl of PBS/Matrigel (1:1) were injected into the right flank of each mouse. Tumor volume was measured every 3–4 days using a digital caliper, and calculated with the formula: Volume = (length × width²)/2. At the predetermined endpoint, animals were euthanized, and tumor tissues were excised, weighed, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent histological analyses.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

To visualize DNA damage and ROS levels, OS cells were seeded on coverslips in 24-well plates. For ROS detection, cells were incubated with 5µM CellROX™ Deep Red ( C10422, American) at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. For γ-H2AX staining, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% BSA. Cells were then incubated with primary antibody against γ-H2AX (1:500, #ab81299, Abcam) overnight at 4 °C, followed by Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 (Yeasen, 40731ES10). Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Pharmacological reagents

Topotecan (#HY-13768) and Irinotecan (#HY-16562) were purchased from MedChemExpress. N-acetylcysteine (NAC, #S1623) was obtained from Selleck.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis and visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 and R (v4.2.1). Two-group comparisons were evaluated by two-tailed Student’s t-test, and multiple-group differences by one-way ANOVA. Pearson’s correlation was used for association analyses. Survival curves were plotted using Kaplan-Meier estimates and compared with the log-rank test, while Cox regression models assessed prognostic factors. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 and P > 0.05 as not significant.

Results

Expression of NUDT1 in OS

To characterize the expression profile of NUDT1, we first conducted a pan-cancer analysis, which revealed that NUDT1 is markedly upregulated across multiple tumor types, including BLCA and BRCA (Fig. 1A). We then examined NUDT1 mRNA expression in OS using datasets from GEO (GSE16088, GSE33382) and the TARGET database. Consistently, NUDT1 mRNA levels were significantly higher in OS tissues than in adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1B-D). These findings were further validated using our in-house sequencing data, which corroborated the results from public datasets (Fig. 1E). In addition, analysis of paired clinical samples demonstrated that NUDT1 expression was substantially elevated in tumor tissues compared with the matched adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 1F). We next assessed NUDT1 expression in vitro by RT-qPCR. As shown in Fig. 1G, NUDT1 expression was significantly elevated in OS cell lines (MG63, HOS, U2OS, and 143B) relative to the normal osteoblast cell line hFOB1.19. Western blot analyses further confirmed elevated NUDT1 protein levels in these OS cell lines (Fig. 1H). Finally, Western blot of clinical samples revealed that NUDT1 protein abundance was markedly higher in OS tissues compared with normal tissues (Fig. 1I).

The prognostic significance of NUDT1 in OS

To investigate the potential clinical relevance of NUDT1 in OS patients, we analyzed the relationship between its expression level and clinical outcomes. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that elevated NUDT1 expression was significantly correlated with shorter overall survival in OS patients across both in TARGET and GSE21257 cohorts (Fig. 2A-B). Specifically, patients in the high-expression group exhibited a markedly lower survival probability compared to those in the low-expression group. To determine whether NUDT1 serves as an independent prognostic factor, we performed univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. As shown in Fig. 2C-D, NUDT1 expression remained independently associated with overall survival rate even after adjusting for age and gender (HR > 1, P < 0.05), suggesting that NUDT1 is a robust predictor of unfavorable prognosis in OS. Subsequently, we constructed a prognostic nomogram by integrating NUDT1 expression with relevant clinical variables (gender, age, metastasis status, and risk score) (Fig. 2E). The nomogram demonstrated strong predictive capacity for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS. Calibration plots indicated excellent concordance between the predicted and observed survival probabilities (Fig. 2F), affirming the reliability of the model. Furthermore, decision curve analysis (DCA) demonstrated that the NUDT1-based nomogram conferred a greater net clinical benefit over a wide range of threshold probabilities compared to individual clinical variables (Fig. 2G). Consistently, time-dependent ROC analysis showed favorable predictive accuracy, yielding AUC values of 0.898, 0.709, and 0.771 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rate, respectively (Fig. 2H). Collectively, these findings identify NUDT1 as a robust independent prognostic biomarker and suggest that incorporating NUDT1 expression substantially enhances the predictive performance of survival models in OS.

Analysis of the NUDT1-associated gene network

To explore the molecular mechanisms underlying NUDT1 function in OS, we analyzed its co-expression network and prognostic relevance. A chord diagram highlighted significant interactions between NUDT1 and key regulators such as SPRED2, UBE2G1, SSC5D, PSMG3, SNX8, and TOGARAM1 (Fig. 3A), implicating NUDT1 in cell proliferation and cell cycle control. Further correlation analysis confirmed significant negative associations between NUDT1 and SPRED2, UBE2G1, SSC5D, TOGARAM1, and FOXP1, alongside positive associations with PSMG3, SNX8, EIF3B, MAD1L1 and MRM2 (Fig. 3B). These data strongly support the involvement of NUDT1 in cell cycle and DNA repair pathways. Survival analysis further underscored the clinical relevance of this network. The results indicated that high expression of the majority of positively correlated genes (PSMG3, SNX8, EIF3B, MAD1L1 and MRM2) was associated with poorer overall survival. Notably, high expression of several negatively correlated genes (SPRED2, UBE2G1, SSC5D, TOGARAM1, and FOXP1) were also linked to adverse survival outcomes (Fig. 3C). Overall, most NUDT1-associated genes possess potential adverse prognostic significance in OS.

Correlation between NUDT1 expression and the tumor immune microenvironment in OS

To explore the relationship between NUDT1 expression and the tumor microenvironment (TME), we performed computational analyses using ESTIMATE, CIBERSORT, and ssGSEA. Patients with high NUDT1 expression exhibited lower StromalScores and ESTIMATEScores, suggesting higher tumor purity, and correlation analysis confirmed an inverse relationship between NUDT1 and stromal content (Fig. 4A, C-D). CIBERSORT revealed reduced fractions of anti-tumor immune cells, including Naive B cells and CD8+ T cells, in the NUDT1-high group, which was further supported by correlation analyses (Fig. 4B, E-F). ssGSEA indicated a distinct immune profile in these patients, with lower scores for B cells and Mast cells and higher scores for immunosuppressive regulatory T cells (Tregs) (Fig. 4G). Additionally, several key immune checkpoint molecules (e.g., CTLA4, CD80, CD244, TNFRSF9) were expressed at lower levels in the high-NUDT1 group (Fig. 4H). Collectively, these results suggest that elevated NUDT1 expression is associated with reduced stromal content and altered immune infiltration.

Evaluation of NUDT1-associated drug sensitivity and therapeutic potential in OS

To investigate potential therapeutic vulnerabilities linked to NUDT1 expression in OS, we performed drug sensitivity prediction using the using the pRRophetic algorithm. The estimated IC50 values were compared between groups with high and low NUDT1 expression. Several agents, including Foretinib, Irinotecan, Palbociclib, Sabutoclax, and Topotecan, were predicted to have lower IC50 values in the high-NUDT1 group, whereas Doramapimod, Nelarabine, Ruxolitinib, and Selumetinib showed lower predicted IC50 values in the low-NUDT1 group (Fig. 5A-L). These findings suggest that NUDT1 expression is associated with differential drug response patterns in OS. Given the inherent limitations of computational modeling, we performed further experimental validation focusing on the clinically relevant agents Topotecan and Irinotecan. In vitro cytotoxicity assays demonstrated that NUDT1 overexpress significantly reduced the experimentally determined IC50 values of both agents (Fig. 5M-P), thereby increasing cellular sensitivity. Taken together, our results indicate that NUDT1 expression is associated with distinct drug response profiles. However, as many of these findings are derived from predictive modeling, further preclinical and clinical validation will be required to confirm their translational utility in therapeutic decision-making.

Downregulation of NUDT1 inhibits the proliferation, migration, and invasion of OS cells

To fully elucidate the functional role of NUDT1 in the malignant progression of OS, we constructed a stable NUDT1 knockdown model in the 143B and U2OS cell lines. RT-qPCR and Western blot analysis confirmed that the NUDT1 mRNA and protein levels in the shNUDT1 group were significantly lower than those in the control group (shNC) (P < 0.0001, Fig. 6A-B), verifying the reliability of the interference efficiency. Based on this, CCK8 cell viability assays showed that NUDT1 deficiency inhibited cell growth in a time-dependent manner, with significant separation of growth curves on days 3 and 4 (Fig. 6C). Plate colony formation assays further corroborated this anti-proliferative effect; compared with the control group, the knockdown group showed a sharp decrease in the number and size of colonies (Fig. 6D-E), indicating that NUDT1 is crucial for maintaining the colony-forming ability of OS cells. Furthermore, assessments of cell metastasis potential revealed that in scratch healing assays, the NUDT1 silencing group showed a significantly lower wound healing rate after 24 h compared to the control group, indicating impaired lateral cell motility (Fig. 6F-G). Transwell assays provided more direct evidence of longitudinal migration and invasion, demonstrating that NUDT1 knockdown led to a substantial decrease in the number of cells crossing the chamber membrane (migration) and Matrigel matrix (invasion) (Fig. 6H-K). To further validate the oncogenic role of NUDT1 in a physiological context, we established a subcutaneous xenograft model in BALB/c nude mice. OS cells transfected with shNC or shNUDT1 were injected subcutaneously, and tumor growth was monitored. As shown in Fig. 6L, the physical size of the excised tumors was markedly smaller in the NUDT1 knockdown groups compared to the control group. Quantitative analysis revealed that both tumor volume and tumor weight were significantly reduced following NUDT1 silencing (Fig. 6M). These in vivo results, consistent with our in vitro findings, demonstrate that NUDT1 acts a crucial pro-cancer role in tumorigenesis and development.

Overexpression of NUDT1 promotes proliferation, migration, and invasion of OS cells

To further validate the oncogenic role of NUDT1 in OS, we established stable NUDT1-overexpressing models by transfecting 143B and U2OS cells with a NUDT1 overexpression plasmid (NUDT1) or empty vector (Vector). Western blot analysis confirmed that NUDT1 protein levels were significantly elevated in the overexpression group compared to controls (Fig. 7A), indicating successful model construction. Functional assays based on these models demonstrated that NUDT1 overexpression markedly enhanced the malignant phenotype of OS cells. Specifically, CCK8 proliferation assays showed that cells in the NUDT1 overexpression group exhibited significantly higher proliferation rates compared to controls (Fig. 7B-C). Colony formation assays further revealed that NUDT1-overexpressing cells formed more numerous and denser colonies, with quantitative analysis showing highly significant differences (Fig. 7D-F). Regarding motility, wound healing assays indicated that the NUDT1-overexpressing cells closed scratches significantly faster than control cells after 24 h (Fig. 7G-I). Transwell assays corroborated these findings, demonstrating that NUDT1 overexpression significantly increased both migration through the membrane and invasion through Matrigel-coated chambers (Fig. 7J-M). Taken together, these gain-of-function experiments, consistent with our previous knockdown results, provide strong evidence that NUDT1 significantly promotes proliferation, migration, and invasion in OS cells.

NUDT1 promotes OS cell proliferation through regulation of ROS and DNA damage

To explore the potential biological functions and signaling mechanisms governed by NUDT1 in OS, we performed GSEA based on KEGG pathway gene sets. As shown in Fig. 8A, gene sets enriched in the NUDT1-low expression group were primarily associated with cell-environment interactions and signal transduction, including Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, Focal adhesion, Regulation of actin cytoskeleton, and general Pathways in cancer. In contrast, the NUDT1-high expression group exhibited significant enrichment in metabolic and biosynthetic processes (Fig. 8B), particularly pathways related to Glycolysis/Gluconeogenesis, Glutathione metabolism, Glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, and the Proteasome. These enrichment patterns suggested a potential association between NUDT1 expression and metabolic regulation, especially redox-related processes. Given the established role of NUDT1 in sanitizing oxidized dNTP pools, we next investigated whether NUDT1 influences oxidative stress in OS cells. ROS measurements demonstrated that NUDT1 knockdown markedly increased intracellular ROS levels compared with control cells (Fig. 8C-D). Consistently, increased γ-H2AX nuclear foci formation was observed following NUDT1 knockdown, reflecting enhanced DNA damage (Fig. 8E-F).

To further determine whether the impaired proliferation was ROS-dependent, rescue experiments were conducted using the ROS scavenger N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC). Confirmed, NAC treatment significantly suppressed the elevated ROS levels induced by NUDT1 depletion, indicating that the excessive ROS accumulation was reversible (Figure S1). Functionally, CCK8 and colony formation assays showed that NUDT1 depletion significantly impaired OS cell proliferation, whereas NAC treatment largely restored proliferative capacity in NUDT1-silenced cells (Fig. 8G-H). Together, these findings indicate that NUDT1 supports OS cell growth, at least in part, by restraining ROS accumulation and limiting ROS-associated DNA damage, thereby promoting proliferation in a ROS-dependent manner.

Discussion

OS remains the most common primary malignant bone tumor in children and adolescents, characterized by high metastatic potential and poor clinical outcomes, particularly in patients with recurrent or metastatic disease [4, 19]. Despite advances in multimodal therapy, long-term survival has plateaued over the past decades, highlighting the urgent need to identify molecular determinants that drive tumor progression and could inform more effective therapeutic strategies [20–23]. Moreover, the TME of OS is frequently immunosuppressive and often described as “cold,” which may contribute to the limited efficacy of current immunotherapeutic approaches [24, 25]. In this context, our study presents a comprehensive and integrative analysis of NUDT1 in OS, combining multi-cohort transcriptomic profiling with functional and mechanistic validation to systematically investigate its role in tumor biology and the TME.

Importantly, while the oncogenic role of NUDT1 has been previously reported in OS and other malignancies, our study extends existing knowledge in several aspects. First, we performed a multi-cohort validation across TARGET, GEO, and an in-house dataset, strengthening the robustness of NUDT1 as a prognostic biomarker. Second, beyond phenotypic characterization, we provide experimental evidence supporting a role for NUDT1 in regulating redox homeostasis and DNA damage in OS cells. Third, we systematically link NUDT1 expression with immune infiltration patterns and predicted drug sensitivity, thereby integrating tumor-intrinsic and microenvironmental features into a unified framework. Collectively, these findings position NUDT1 not only as a functional oncogene but also as a potential bridge between metabolic adaptation, genomic stability, and tumor-microenvironment interactions.

Consistent with previous reports in multiple cancer types, we observed that NUDT1 is significantly upregulated in OS tissues and cell lines and is associated with poor clinical outcomes [11, 26–28]. Mechanistically, our data suggest that NUDT1 supports OS cell proliferation and survival, at least in part, by maintaining redox balance. Specifically, NUDT1 depletion led to increased intracellular ROS accumulation and enhanced DNA damage, as evidenced by elevated γ-H2AX levels. Importantly, antioxidant treatment with NAC partially rescued the proliferation defects induced by NUDT1 knockdown, indicating that the growth-suppressive effect is at least partially ROS-dependent. These findings are consistent with the established biochemical function of NUDT1 in sanitizing oxidized nucleotide pools and preventing their incorporation into DNA [8, 9, 29]. However, while our results support a role for NUDT1 in regulating oxidative stress and genomic stability, more detailed mechanistic studies-such as the direct measurement of oxidized dNTPs or metabolic flux-are required to fully elucidate this process in OS.

Beyond these cell-intrinsic functions, our study unveils a provocative link between NUDT1 expression and the TME. Utilizing multiple deconvolution algorithms (ESTIMATE, CIBERSORT, and ssGSEA), we consistently observed that elevated NUDT1 expression correlates with a “cold” immunophenotype, characterized by diminished infiltration of CD8+ T cells and an enrichment of regulatory T cells (Tregs). This spatial exclusion of effector lymphocytes suggests that NUDT1 may contribute to an immunologically “silent” niche that facilitates tumor evasion [24, 30, 31]. Intriguingly, we observed a paradoxical downregulation of several canonical immune checkpoints in NUDT1-high tumors. In the context of a “cold” tumor, this likely reflects a state of “primary resistance”, where the absence of a pre-existing inflammatory infiltrate precludes the necessity for checkpoint-mediated exhaustion [32]. Consequently, NUDT1 expression may not only serve as a prognostic indicator but also as a potential biomarker to identify patients who are unlikely to benefit from monotherapeutic immune checkpoint blockade (ICB). However, it must be emphasized that these immunological insights are currently derived from computational deconvolution of bulk transcriptomic data and remain speculative. Future investigations utilizing multiplex immunofluorescence, single-cell RNA sequencing, and functional T-cell co-culture assays are indispensable to validate these correlations and to definitively establish the causal role of NUDT1 in modulating the OS immune landscape.

From a translational perspective, our drug sensitivity analysis provides preliminary insights into potential therapeutic vulnerabilities associated with NUDT1 expression. Based on GDSC-derived predictive modeling, patients with different NUDT1 expression exhibited distinct estimated IC50 values for several agents, including the topoisomerase inhibitors Topotecan and Irinotecan. Topotecan has been explored as a therapeutic option in relapsed or refractory OS, particularly in combination regimens [33, 34]. Its mechanism involves stabilizing the DNA-topoisomerase I cleavage complex, thereby inducing replication-associated DNA damage [35]. Importantly, we further validated these findings experimentally, demonstrating that NUDT1 knockdown enhances sensitivity to both agents in vitro. These results suggest that NUDT1 expression may influence the response to DNA-damaging therapies, potentially through modulation of genomic stress tolerance, oxidative stress response, or DNA repair pathways. Nevertheless, it is important to emphasize that most drug sensitivity results are derived from computational predictions, and their clinical applicability remains to be established through further preclinical and clinical validation. Future studies integrating patient-derived xenografts or organoid models, as well as clinical correlation analyses, would be critical to confirm the predictive value of NUDT1 as a biomarker for therapy selection. Additionally, exploring combination strategies targeting NUDT1 and DNA-damaging agents may provide a rational approach to enhance therapeutic efficacy in OS patients with high NUDT1 expression.

Despite the clinical and functional insights provided by our findings, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, our prognostic model was developed using retrospective public datasets with a relatively modest sample size and incomplete annotation of established clinical variables; thus, validation in large-scale, multi-center prospective cohorts remains essential. Second, our immunological characterization-relying on computational deconvolution of bulk RNA-seq data-is inherently correlative and requires further experimental validation. Furthermore, while xenograft experiments in BALB/c nude mice confirmed the oncogenic role of NUDT1 in vivo, the lack of a functional adaptive immune system precluded a direct evaluation of tumor-immune interactions. It is also important to note that our current models did not assess metastatic potential, such as lung metastasis, which is a critical clinical feature of OS. Future studies employing syngeneic, immunocompetent, and metastatic models will be necessary to fully elucidate the multifaceted role of NUDT1 in OS progression and immune modulation.

Conclusion

In summary, our study identifies NUDT1 as a clinically relevant regulator in OS. NUDT1 contributes to tumor progression through modulation of redox balance and is associated with immune features and therapeutic responses, highlighting its potential as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target.
