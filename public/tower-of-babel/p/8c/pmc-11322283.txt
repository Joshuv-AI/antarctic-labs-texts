Harnessing the potential of acyl triazoles in bifunctional cobalt-catalyzed radical cross-coupling reactions
Authors: Chang-Yin Tan, Sungwoo Hong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPersistent radicals facilitate numerous selective radical coupling reactions. Here, we have identified acyl triazole as a new and versatile moiety for generating persistent radical intermediates through single-electron transfer processes. The efficient generation of these persistent radicals is facilitated by the formation of substrate-coordinated cobalt complexes, which subsequently engage in radical cross-coupling reactions. Remarkably, triazole-coordinated cobalt complexes exhibit metal-hydride hydrogen atom transfer (MHAT) capabilities with alkenes, enabling the efficient synthesis of diverse ketone products without the need for external ligands. By leveraging the persistent radical effect, this catalytic approach also allows for the development of other radical cross-coupling reactions with two representative radical precursors. The discovery of acyl triazoles as effective substrates for generating persistent radicals and as ligands for cobalt catalysis, combined with the bifunctional nature of the cobalt catalytic system, opens up new avenues for the design and development of efficient and sustainable organic transformations.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Photocatalysis, Synthetic chemistry methodology

Introduction

Persistent radical-mediated cross-coupling has emerged as a powerful tool in organic synthesis for forging new C−C bonds. The success of these transformations relies on the persistent radical effect, which requires pairing a persistent radical with a transient radical at comparable rates1,2. Despite the discovery of new patterns of persistent radicals, such as cyano-stabilized dihydropyridine radicals or azolium-stabilized ketyl radicals, the limited availability of known persistent radicals compared to transient ones restricts the practical applicability and scope of radical/radical cross-coupling reactions3–17. Lewis acid transition metals show promise in overcoming the limited availability of persistent radicals by employing coordination groups in the substrate18–21. These Lewis acid-coordinated groups, such as imidazole, pyridine, N-acyl hydrazone, and others, enable substrate activation for single-electron transfer (SET), generating persistent radical intermediates22–29. This approach expands the scope of persistent radicals available for radical/radical cross-coupling reactions, potentially increasing their practical applicability. However, their limited reactivity, along with the difficulty in removing these groups from the final products, constrain their utility and applicability30–33. To address this issue, it is imperative to design new leading groups that ensure both removability and versatility. The development of such leading groups would enable the synthesis of valuable end products with diverse potential applications, expanding the scope of persistent radicals available for cross-coupling reactions, and ultimately enhancing the overall utility, practicality, and efficiency of the entire procedure.

Cobalt has emerged as a versatile and indispensable metal in contemporary catalysis due to its widespread availability, low toxicity, and effectiveness across various chemical conversions34–45. It catalyzes hydrogen transfer reactions for alkene functionalization, where high-valent cobalt hydrides coordinated with tailored ligands play a central role (Fig. 1a)46–52. The rise of reductive cobalt hydride catalysis, aligning with green chemistry principles, has garnered significant attention53–67. By undergoing reduction to Co(I), this species readily engages protons, giving rise to active Co(III) hydrides, which contrasts with oxidative cobalt catalysis that relies on stoichiometric oxidants or electrochemical anodic oxidation with expensive silane hydride reagents68–76. Furthermore, cobalt’s notable proficiency as a Lewis acid catalyst, especially compared to heavier traditional transition metals, highlights its potential for cost-effective and versatile catalysis77–82. Despite these advantages, the potential of cobalt as a bifunctional catalyst within a single reaction system remains largely unexplored due to the scarcity of tailored reaction designs. This presents a new opportunity for transformative advancements in chemical synthesis by developing systems that harness cobalt’s dual catalytic capabilities.

In pursuit of our research objective to develop novel catalytic platforms for efficient radical cross-coupling reactions, we propose employing cobalt as a Lewis acid catalyst with carefully tailored coordinating groups to generate potentially persistent radical intermediates via SET. Moreover, we hypothesize that these substrate-coordinated cobalt complexes could concurrently function as efficient metal hydride catalysts, facilitating metal-hydride hydrogen atom transfer (MHAT) with alkenes to produce alkyl radical intermediates46–52. The critical aspect lies in designing substrates with N or O donors as weak field ligands for cobalt, which is essential for forming weaker M−H bonds that promote rapid HAT processes with alkenes47.

In this work, we identify acyl triazole as a novel and versatile group that enables cobalt catalysts to exhibit MHAT capabilities without necessitating external ligands while simultaneously serving as persistent ketyl radical precursors for subsequent radical cross-coupling reactions (Fig. 1b, top)83,84. Building upon this cobalt and acyl triazole system, we further demonstrate two representative radical cross-coupling reactions with other radical precursors by leveraging the persistent radical effect (Fig. 1b, bottom).

Results

Reaction discovery and optimization

To verify the feasibility of our working hypothesis, we selected styrene 2a as the model substrate for the cobalt hydride catalytic reaction. After thoroughly exploring various conditions, including acyl substrates and cobalt catalysts, we discovered that the hydroacylation product 3a could be obtained in an 88% isolated yield. This was achieved utilizing acyl triazole substrate 1a, a commercially available Co(BF4)2•6H2O catalyst, and an organic photocatalyst 4CzIPN, with iPr2NEt serving as a reductant in MeCN with H2O (3 equiv) as an additive under visible light irradiation (Table 1, entry 1). Investigation of different acyl substrates revealed the remarkable efficiency of the triazole moiety in facilitating this transformation. Notably, the isomer of the standard acyl triazole 1ab demonstrated high efficiency, affording an 80% yield. Control experiments revealed that 1ab could efficiently transfer to 1a in the presence of iPr2NEt prior to the radical-coupling reaction, indicating that 1a is the active substrate in this reaction (See Supplementary Fig. 6 for more details)84. In contrast, acyl imidazole 1ac, acyl chloride 1ad, and anhydride 1ae failed to furnish the desired product. Furthermore, common coordination groups in Lewis acid chemistry, such as pyrazole (1af and 1ag), imidazole (1ah) and pyridine (1ai) were also tested in this reaction; however, no reactivity was observed with these substrates (entry 2). This difference can be attributed to the triazole ring’s properties: its superior electron-donating ability, better coordination potential due to its three nitrogen atoms, and greater resonance stabilization. These characteristics enhance its ability to form stable Co-ligand complexes and delocalize spin density in ketyl radicals. These observations underscore the pivotal role of the acyl triazole moiety in this dual cobalt-catalyzed radical-coupling reaction, setting it apart from other commonly employed directing moieties. Further investigations revealed a significant decrease in yield when the acyl triazole substrate was employed as the limiting reagent, implying the necessity of additional 1a to function as a ligand for the effective formation of the cobalt hydride complex (entry 3). Among alternative metal catalysts, a nickel catalyst could produce the desired product, albeit with a lower 15% yield, which is reasonable given the similar metal hydride characteristics of nickel and cobalt85. However, the traditional Lewis acid Yb(OTf)3 failed to yield any desired product due to its insufficient hydrogen atom transfer ability (entry 4). It was found through solvent screening that THF and acetone, while viable alternatives, resulted in notably lower product yields (entry 5). Among the amine reductants tested, iPr2NEt and TEA proved suitable for the reaction, whereas Hantzsch ester yielded a diminished yield (entry 6). The Co(salentBu,tBu) catalyst exhibited reduced efficiency, yielding only 29% of the desired product, attributed to its comparatively lower Lewis acid capacity, resulting in inefficient activation of acyl triazole 1a (entry 7). High yield could be achieved without water as an additive, suggesting the potential utilization of the amine reductant as the proton source for cobalt hydride (entry 8)86. Finally, control experiments underscored the indispensable role of the cobalt catalyst, amine reductant, photocatalyst, and light in driving this reaction forward (entry 9).

Substrate scope studies

With the optimized conditions in hand, we systematically explored the scope of the hydroacylation reaction, as detailed in Fig. 2. We began by investigating various para-substituents on the aryl ring of styrene and observed robust reactivity in generating coupling ketone products, regardless of their electronic properties (3a–3i). The protocol exhibited excellent tolerance towards diverse functional groups in the alkene, including trimethylsilyl (3e), ester (3f and 3g), cyano (3h), and amide (3i) moieties, facilitating smooth product formation. Next, we assessed the impact of alkene steric bulkiness, revealing that ortho-methyl substituted styrene yielded the product 3j with high efficiency, while even ortho-disubstituted styrenes served as viable substrates irrespective of electronic properties (3k and 3l). Notably, naphthyl-substituted styrene and benzodioxole scaffold also furnished the desired ketone products with notable yields (3 m and 3n). We then examined 1,1-disubstituted styrenes for synthesizing ketones with quaternary carbon centers (3o–3q), which are traditionally challenging with metal hydride chemistry. Although internal alkenes proved unsuitable under standard conditions, employing commercial Co(salentBu,tBu) catalyst enabled product formation (3r). To investigate the applicability of this strategy to complex substrates, we effectively synthesized cross-coupling products derived from biomolecules such as nerol and indomethacin (3s and 3t). Importantly, the internal alkene of nerol remained unaffected following the reaction. Furthermore, treatment of cyclopropane-derived alkene 2u with the reaction conditions resulted in anticipated ring opening, yielding alkene-containing ketone 3u in high yield. Subsequent investigation into the scope of acyl triazoles revealed the smooth formation of corresponding products with high yields, accommodating various substituents on the aryl ring (3v–3aa). Pleasingly, ortho-chloro-substituted acyl triazoles and one bearing heteroaryl group could deliver the desired products 3ab and 3ac. Attempts with aliphatic acyl triazoles and aliphatic alkenes were unsuccessful in producing the desired products.

Motivated by these findings, we envisage that the stable ketyl radicals generated through the interaction of the cobalt catalyst and acyl triazole could potentially participate in free radical cross-coupling reactions with other radical precursors via SET. This expansion of radical partners not only broadens the scope of the reaction but also underscores the advantages of this new platform for persistent ketyl radicals. As outlined in Table 2, our initial choice of 1,4-dihydropyridines (DHP) as a radical precursor was guided by their capacity to generate diverse radicals, coupled with their capability to be photoexcited as potent reductants87–90. Delightfully, the cross-coupling product was successfully synthesized in an isolated yield of 83% using photoexcited DHP 4a as the substrate under visible light irradiation without a photocatalyst (Table 2, entry 1). Exploration of other first-row transition metal catalysts revealed nickel and iron catalysts to be effective, whereas copper yielded a significantly lower yield (entry 2). Notably, traditional Lewis acid metals also demonstrated high efficiency in catalyzing this reaction (entry 3), suggesting cobalt’s role as a Lewis acid catalyst rather than the insertion of the C–N bond in substrate 1a. The solvent evaluation indicated that the reaction is not particularly sensitive to solvent choice, although DMSO, DMF, and DCE yielded slightly lower yields compared to MeCN (entries 4). The optimal light wavelength for this reaction was found to be 427 nm, as shorter wavelengths accelerated DHP decomposition, while longer wavelengths were ineffective at efficiently exciting the DHP (entry 5)90. Notably, trifluoroborate 4aa also proved to be a viable substrate with the aid of a photocatalyst (entry 6). Control experiments revealed a dramatic decrease in yield to 18% in the absence of the cobalt catalyst, suggesting that uncoordinated substrate 1a could undergo reduction to generate the radical intermediate with much lower efficiency (entry 7). The light was established as a crucial factor in this reaction, highlighting its indispensable role in driving the desired transformation (entry 8).

Substrate scope studies

After obtaining optimized conditions, we further explored the applicability of acyl triazole in this redox-neutral cross-coupling reaction (Fig. 3). We were pleased to observe the minimal impact of electronic effects on the aryl ring, allowing various para-substituted groups to smoothly generate the corresponding target products (5a–5i). Additionally, meta-chloro-substituted aryl rings and ortho-substituted acyl triazoles also yielded the target products efficiently, overcoming steric hindrance (5j–5m). Furthermore, naphthalene rings and benzene rings bearing benzyl chlorides were compatible in this system (5n and 5o). Heterocyclic substrates could also serve as substrates for synthesizing various ketone products (5p–5r). To highlight the compatibility of this strategy, we efficiently achieved late-stage derivatization using probenecid-derived acyl triazole substrate 1s. In our continued investigation, we delved into the utilization of aliphatic acyl triazole in this reaction—a notably more challenging endeavor due to the absence of a conjugate π system to stabilize the ketyl radical intermediate. As anticipated, the yield of the desired product 5t dropped to 34%, even with a photocatalyst. To prolong the lifespan of the ketyl radical intermediate, we proposed the addition of an extra ligand to augment the steric bulkiness surrounding the radical, thereby enhancing its stability. To our satisfaction, employing ligand L1 led to a significant improvement, boosting the yield to 72%. Under these conditions, we proceeded to examine additional aliphatic substrates—terminal chloro (5 u), cyclohexyl (5 v), and flurbiprofen-derived acyl substrates (5w)—all of which yielded the desired products successfully. In our subsequent investigations, we explored the versatility of alkyl radical precursors. We found that primary benzylic radicals generated from DHP or trifluoroborate smoothly yielded the ketone product 5x with reliable yields, highlighting the robustness of our approach. Additionally, α-amino radicals bearing a Boc protecting group and phthalimide, derived from DHP, proved to be effective coupling partners (5y and 5z), offering a straightforward route to the desired α-amino ketone scaffold with notable efficiency. Notably, our methodology showed adaptability by accommodating non-stabilized alkyl radicals, thereby broadening the range of accessible substrates (5aa–5ad). Importantly, even in the presence of such challenging radicals, the internal alkene remained unaltered, indicating the selectivity and reliability of our method (5ae). To underscore the practical applications of our cobalt-catalyzed radical-coupling reaction, we turned our attention to its potential in late-stage functionalization of various pharmaceutical compounds. Encouragingly, alkyl radicals sourced from commonly used pharmaceuticals such as ibuprofen (5af), flurbiprofen (5ag), and naproxen (5ah) readily participated in our reaction, underscoring their practical utility in medicinal chemistry. Furthermore, we observed an intriguing application of our methodology in a three-component radical cascade reaction involving styrene (5ai and 5aj), suggesting the potential for utilizing our strategy in the difunctionalization of styrene, thereby offering a pathway to access structurally complex molecules with enhanced efficiency and precision.

Control experiments and proposed mechanism

To further demonstrate the practicality of this approach, a one-pot method for achieving the cross-coupling reaction from readily available acyl chloride 1ad and anhydride 1ae was successfully achieved (Fig. 4a, b). Moreover, both cobalt-catalyzed HAT and redox-neutral reactions can be scaled up to a 2 mmol scale with high yields (Fig. 4c). The reaction mechanism was further investigated, and in both reactions, proposed ketyl radical intermediates and alkyl radicals were successfully trapped by TEMPO, without detection of any desired products, demonstrating a radical cross-coupling process (Fig. 4d). When D2O was used instead of H2O, 10% of deuterated products were observed in the reaction mixture, indicating that water serves as one of the sources of protons in cobalt hydride species (Fig. 4e). Based on previous literature reports, we propose that the radical cation formed after amine reduction is the main proton source, aligning with the observed high product yields even under anhydrous conditions. Cyclic voltammetry studies revealed that the acyl triazole substrate 1a has an E1/2 value of –1.69 V (vs SCE), too low for direct SET with most photocatalysts or reductants. However, upon addition of the cobalt catalyst, the reduction potential shifts to a more positive value (E1/2 = –1.31 V vs. SCE), suggesting that cobalt acts as a Lewis acid, activating acyl triazole 1a and facilitating its reduction to generate the persistent ketyl radical (Fig. 4f). Stern-Volmer quenching experiments indicate that the excited photocatalyst 4CzIPN is initially quenched by iPr2NEt, after which the reduced 4CzIPN donates electrons to the substrate-coordinated cobalt complex. To investigate the possibility of alkene reduction to the radical anion by a strong reductant and subsequent formation of the same benzylic radical after protonation, additional electrophiles such as methyl iodide, ketones, and CO2 gas were added to the reaction mixture91,92. However, no radical anion addition products were observed, suggesting that the radical anion intermediate is unlikely to be formed during the reaction (Fig. 4h). This observation supports the conclusion that the benzylic radical in the MHAT reaction is formed by the cobalt hydride intermediate.

Based on the aforementioned observations, we propose a plausible mechanism (Fig. 5). Initially, according to the fluorescence quenching results, the excited state of 4CzIPN undergoes SET from iPr2NEt, generating iPr2NEt radical cation and 4CzIPN radical anion. Simultaneously, the cobalt catalyst coordinates with substrate 1 to form intermediate A. Although the reducing potential of 4CzIPN•− (E1/2(PC•−/PC) = –1.24 V vs. SCE in MeCN)93 is insufficient to reduce intermediate A (E1/2red = –1.31 V vs. SCE in MeCN), recent reports highlight consecutive photoinduced electron transfer (conPET). The excitation of 4CzIPN•− results in a significantly more strongly reducing state, 4CzIPN•−* (Eox* = –2.24 V vs. SCE), facilitating SET to intermediate A94–98. Upon reduction, intermediate A generates cobalt-coordinated persistent ketyl radicals B and CoI species. The CoI species binds with a proton to form a CoIII–H intermediate capable of HAT, which then reacts with styrene to produce a benzyl radical53–67. The benzyl radical undergoes radical cross-coupling with the radical intermediate B to form a C–C bond, followed by the departure of the triazole leaving the group to yield the target ketone product 3 and regenerate the cobalt catalyst. The proton source is attributed to iPr2NEt radical cation and water86. The mechanism of the redox-neutral reaction is relatively straightforward and is further elucidated in the Supplementary Fig. 21.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a novel platform for generating persistent ketyl radicals, facilitated by a cobalt Lewis acid catalyst and versatile acyl triazoles. The acyl triazole-coordinated cobalt complexes exhibit dual functionality: they can be reduced to form persistent ketyl radicals and serve as MHAT catalysts, enabling the generation of alkyl radical species with alkenes. The triazole moiety presents excellent leaving ability, allowing the formation of diverse ketone products without the need for further post-reactions. Furthermore, this innovative platform has been successfully employed in radical cross-coupling reactions with other representative precursors, such as DHPs and trifluoroborates, capitalizing on the persistent radical effect. By leveraging the advantages of cobalt catalysis and the persistent radical effect in bond formation, this flexible platform offers opportunities for exploring new radical reaction mechanisms and designing more efficient catalytic systems. Building on the insights gained from our current work, we are currently exploring ways to extend our methodology to include unactivated alkenes and develop asymmetric versions.

Methods

Representative procedure for the MHAT reactions

In an oven-dried test tube (12 mL) equipped with a magnetic stir bar, acyl triaozle 1 (0.2 mmol, 2.0 equiv) and 4CzIPN (0.8 mg, 0.001 mmol, 1 mol%), Co(BF4)2•6H2O (3.4 mg, 0.01 mmol, 10 mol%) were added. The test tube was sealed with PTFE/rubber septa, and dry MeCN (2.0 mL) was added under an Ar atmosphere, then alkene (0.1 mmol, 1.0 equiv) and N,N-diisopropylethylamine (26.0 μL, 0.15 mmol, 1.5 equiv), and H2O (5.4 μL, 0.3 mmol, 3.0 equiv) were added via microsyringe, and the reaction mixture was irradiated by Kessil PR160-456 nm blue LED with 25% intensity and stirred for 22 h cooled by strong fan at room temperature. After reaction completion, the residue was purified by flash column chromatography on silica gel to give the desired product.

Representative procedure for redox-neutral reaction with DHPs

In an oven-dried test tube (12 mL) equipped with a magnetic stir bar, DHP 4 (0.4 mmol, 2.0 equiv), acyl triaozle 1 (0.20 mmol, 1.0 equiv) and Co(BF4)2•6H2O (3.4 mg, 0.01 mmol, 5 mol%) were added. The test tube was sealed with PTFE/rubber septa, and dry MeCN (2.0 mL) was added under an Ar atmosphere, and the reaction mixture was irradiated by Kessil PR160-427 nm blue LED with 50% intensity and stirred for 12 h cooled by strong fan at room temperature. After reaction completion, the residue was purified by flash column chromatography on silica gel to give the desired product.
