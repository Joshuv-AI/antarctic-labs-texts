Zero-valent iron regulates hydrothermal carbonization pathways toward Fe–C interfacial hydrochar formation
Authors: Liangyan Jiang, Jin Li, Haiyan Li, Yue Cao, Muhammad Arslan, Mohamed Gamal El-Din, Yuxian Wang, Yali Zhan, Qinghong Wang, Chunmao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydrothermal carbonization (HTC) offers a sustainable route for converting wet biomass-derived precursors into functional carbonaceous materials, yet mechanistic control over carbonization pathways, heteroatom incorporation, and metal–carbon interfacial evolution remains challenging. Here, we use glucose and glycine as representative carbohydrate and amino acid precursors, respectively, to elucidate how zero-valent iron (Fe0) regulates HTC chemistry under mild hydrothermal conditions. Rather than serving only as a performance-enhancing additive, Fe0 acts as a multifunctional regulator that directs hydrothermal transformation, enabling the controlled construction of Fe–C interfacial architectures. At 200 °C, Fe0 promotes the transformation of labile hydroxyl groups into more stable oxygen-containing interfacial moieties associated with C–O–C/Fe–O–C environments, enriches pyridinic-N species, and facilitates the formation of Fe3O4 nanophases embedded within the carbon matrix. Combined solid- and liquid-phase analyses reveal a Fe-mediated dissolution-recrystallization process, in which dissolved Fe species migrate from the aqueous phase to the growing hydrochar and participate in Fe–C interface construction. Adsorption experiments using cationic and anionic probes further demonstrate that Fe0-regulated HTC increases the specific surface area and changes the surface chemistry of the hydrochar, leading to selective affinity toward cationic species. This work provides mechanistic insights into Fe0-mediated hydrothermal carbonization and clarifies how iron regulates carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen redistribution during Fe–C interfacial hydrochar formation.

Introduction

Hydrothermal carbonization (HTC) is a thermochemical conversion process that transforms wet biomass into carbon-rich hydrochar through aqueous-phase reactions under relatively mild conditions, typically at 180–260 °C under autogenous pressure for several minutes to hours.1–3 Because HTC can directly process high-moisture feedstocks without energy-intensive drying, it is particularly attractive for sludge valorization.4 In addition to reducing the volume and environmental burden of sludge disposal, HTC enables the conversion of sludge-derived organics into functional carbonaceous solids and liquid co-products. The resulting hydrochar typically exhibits improved carbon density and abundant surface functionalities, rendering it potentially valuable for applications such as adsorption, catalysis, solid fuel production, and soil amendment.5 However, HTC still suffers from insufficient control over hydrochar yield, surface chemistry, and structural evolution under non-optimized conditions.6

Catalyst incorporation is widely acknowledged as a potent strategy for regulating hydrochar formation kinetics in HTC, enabling the tailored fabrication of functional carbon products with a high added value.7–10 Iron-regulated HTC and Fe–C hydrochar materials have been widely investigated over the past decade.11–18 Previous studies have demonstrated that iron salts, iron oxides, zero-valent iron, and iron-containing biomass-derived systems can regulate hydrochar formation, improve magnetic separability, modify porosity, and enhance adsorption or catalytic performance.11,12,14,17,18 In particular, many reported Fe–C hydrochars exhibit much higher BET surface areas and adsorption capacities than directly carbonized hydrochars, especially when chemical activation, template-assisted pore construction, or high-temperature post-carbonization is employed.13,15 These studies have established important synthetic and application-oriented foundations for Fe–C hydrochar development.

During the hydrothermal treatment, iron can participate in the transformation of biomass-derived intermediates as well as in the nucleation, deposition, and phase evolution of Fe-containing solids, thereby influencing both the carbonization pathway and the final structure of the resulting Fe/C material.19–21 Existing studies suggest that iron species can affect dehydration, condensation, nitrogen migration, and the redistribution of oxygen-containing functionalities during hydrothermal conversion.22 In addition, the iron oxides formed in situ can catalyze key reactions critical for hydrochar formation.16,23 However, as most of the studies used complex biomass or waste feedstocks as the HTC carbon precursors, the coupled contributions of carbohydrate dehydration, amino acid conversion, Maillard chemistry, nitrogen incorporation, iron migration, and Fe–C interfacial formation remain difficult to resolve. Moreover, the intrinsic roles of Fe0 in regulating HTC reaction pathways and Fe–C interface evolution remain unclear.16 The resulting iron leaching, over-reduction, agglomeration, uncontrolled phase transformation, and insufficient regulation of Fe–C interfacial structures and carbon ordering under hydrothermal conditions may reduce active-site density and accessibility, weaken the stability of active iron species, and hinder precise control over the structure–property relationship of Fe/C materials.24–26

Given the relatively minor contribution of lipids to HTC in many sludge-like systems, in this study, we selected glucose and glycine as representative carbohydrate and amino acid model compounds, respectively, to investigate the formation of a Fe-functionalized hydrochar. This model-system design was not intended to mimic all the compositional complexity of real biomass, but to decouple the key reactions involved in carbohydrate-amino-acid HTC, including sugar dehydration, amino acid conversion, Maillard-type reactions, nitrogen incorporation, oxygen redistribution, and carbon framework evolution.27,28 Several iron-based catalysts were introduced to systematically compare their effects on hydrochar yield, elemental composition, structural evolution, and liquid-phase organic distribution. Through combined characterization of the solid and liquid products, this work aims to clarify how Fe0 regulates the hydrothermal transformation of glucose-glycine and how Fe-containing interfacial structures evolve within the hydrochar matrix. The results are expected to provide mechanistic insights and experimental guidance for the directed preparation of Fe/C hydrochar materials for value-added utilization.

Materials and methods

Hydrothermal carbonization experiment

Commercial glucose (purchased from Innochem) and glycine (purchased from Aladdin) were used as substrates. Three types of iron-based catalysts, namely Fe0, Fe2O3, and Fe3O4, all of which are nanoscale, were purchased from Aladdin. The initial properties of the catalysts were characterized (Fig. S1 and Table S1). Two types of homogeneous iron catalysts, namely FeCl3·6H2O and FeSO4 (analytical grade, Aladdin, China), were also investigated.

The HTC process was conducted in a high-pressure hydrothermal reactor (Beijing Zhongteng Kexing Machinery Equipment Co., Ltd, China) with an effective volume of 1000 mL, which was equipped with temperature-sensing probes and pressure gauges to monitor the operational conditions in real time. The working volume for each run was 300 mL. The reactor was magnetically stirred at 130 rpm. Heat-up rate was 2.5 °C min−1. Immediately after the reaction was completed, cooling water was introduced to rapidly reduce the system temperature.

Glucose (0.1 mol L−1) and glycine (0.1 mol L−1) were added to the reactor along with 0.5 g of catalyst (heterogeneous: Fe0, Fe2O3, or Fe3O4; homogeneous: anhydrous FeSO4 or FeCl3·6H2O corrected for water of crystallization). The initial pH of the solution was adjusted to 7.0 using dilute HCl or NaOH. The mixture was heated at 165 °C, 200 °C, and 260 °C for 30 min, respectively. After cooling to room temperature, a 0.45 µm filter membrane was used to separate the solid and liquid phases. The solid phase was dried at 105 °C to constant weight and weighed, while the liquid phase was stored at 4 °C before analysis.

The products were named according to “temperature–catalysts”. For example, the hydrochar produced from the Fe-catalyzed HTC process at 200 °C is labeled 200 °C–Fe, while the products generated from the HTC process without catalysts at 200 °C are labeled 200 °C-blank. Each experiment was performed in at least duplicate to ensure reproducibility, and average values are reported.

Calculation of hydrochar yield

The hydrochar yield of the blank group was calculated using eqn (2.1):2.1YieldHydrochar(%) = WHydrochar/(WGlucose + WGlycine)where WHydrochar is the mass of the dry hydrochar and WGlucose and WGlycine are the masses of the substrates (glucose and glycine, respectively). To calculate the hydrochar weight in the catalyst-containing system, the total solid-phase weight was corrected by subtracting the catalyst weight. The iron catalyst weight was determined directly from the iron content determined by ICP-OES. The masses of Fe2O3 and Fe3O4 are converted into their respective oxidation states based on the measured iron content and then subtracted to obtain the final WHydrochar value.

Characterization of the hydrochar

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to qualitatively and quantitatively analyze the C, O and Fe contents in the samples by using an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (Axios mAX, Netherlands), and the peaks were deconvoluted via the XPSPEAK 4.30 software.29 The detailed parameters for the XPS fitting analysis were provided in the Text S1. The contents of C, H, N, and O in the hydrochars were determined with an elemental analyzer (Vario EL cube, Germany). The surface functional groups and chemical structures of the samples were analyzed using a Fourier-transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR, Bruker V80, Germany) with a wavenumber scanning range of 400–4000 cm−1. FTIR spectra collected across the temperature series were background-corrected, baseline-adjusted, and normalized, then subjected to synchronous and asynchronous 2D-COS analyses using Origin 2021.30 Peaks corresponding to key functional groups (e.g., O–H/N–H, CO, C–O–C, CC) were identified, and the coupling patterns were interpreted to infer the order of chemical transformations. A Raman microscope (Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution, France) equipped with a green laser (532 nm) was used to determine the molecular vibrational modes and chemical composition/structure of the hydrochar. X-ray diffraction (XRD, DX-2700BH, China) was performed to characterize the phase structure of the hydrochar using filtered Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å) with an accelerating voltage of 40 kV and a current of 30 mA. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was conducted using a JEOL JEM-2100F microscope (JEOL, Japan) operated at 200 kV. The specific surface area and pore volume of the samples were determined using an ASAP 2000 Accelerated Specific Area and Pore Volume Analyzer (Micromeritics, Norcross, GA, USA). Zeta and pHpzc were measured using a BeNano 90 Zeta potential analyzer (Dandong Bate Instrument Co., Ltd, China).

The total Fe content in the hydrochar was determined as follows. First, the weighed hydrochar (0.03–0.06 g) was digested in a polytetrafluoroethylene digestion tube with 5 mL of concentrated nitric acid and 1 mL of hydrofluoric acid at 190 °C for 8 hours. The cooled liquid was subsequently transferred to a 50 mL plastic volumetric flask to bring the volume to a standard level. Inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, Thermo ICAP PRO, Thermo Fisher Scientific, United States) was used to determine the Fe concentration of the digested liquid. The iron content in the hydrochar was calculated using eqn (2.2):2.2Ciron (mg kg−1) = Cliquid (mg L−1) × 50 (mL) × 10−3/(M (g) × 10−3)where Ciron is the iron content in the hydrochar, Cliquid is the iron concentration in the liquid (mg L−1), and M is the mass of the dry hydrochar (g).

Adsorption behavior evaluation

Hydrochar was first stirred with 100 mL of ethanol for 30 min, followed by rinsing with deionized water until the filtrate became colorless, and then dried at 55 °C for 12 h. In the adsorption test, the treated adsorbent was added to the target pollutant solution at a dosage of 0.5 g L−1. The concentrations of methylene blue (MB) and methyl orange (MO) were measured by ultraviolet spectrophotometry at 664.0 nm and 464.5 nm, respectively, while the concentrations of Cu2+ and Ni2+ were determined using ICP-OES. The adsorption capacity was calculated using eqn (2.3):2.3where the volume of pollutant (L) is V, the original/equilibrium concentration (mg L−1) is C0/Ce, respectively, and the mass (g) of the hydrochar is expressed by m. Normalized adsorption capacities (Qe, mg m−2) were obtained by dividing qe (mg g−1) by the BET surface area (g m−2) of each sample.31

For the pollutant solution with an initial concentration of 20 mg L−1, adsorption kinetics were investigated at different times (0, 10, 20, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min). The time-dependent data were fitted to both pseudo-first-order (PFO) and pseudo-second-order (PSO) kinetic models to elucidate the rate-controlling mechanisms.

Composition analysis of HTC liquid

The concentration of Fe(iii) and Fe(ii) in the liquid phase was determined via the o-phenanthroline spectrophotometric method at a wavelength of 510 nm. Details of the quantitative analysis methods for Fe(ii) and Fe(iii) determination are provided in Text S2. The organic composition of the HTC mixture was analyzed using an Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph-5977B mass spectrometer (GC-MS, Santa Clara, USA). The separation system was equipped with an Agilent DB-35 chromatography column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 µm). Using the liquid–liquid extraction method, the dissolved organic pollutants (DOPs) in 100 mL of each sample were extracted with dichloromethane (DCM, HPLC grade).32 The carrier gas was ultrapure He (flow rate of 1 mL min−1). The mixture was heated at 50 °C for 5 minutes, then the temperature was increased to 300 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1. Electron impact (EI) ionization mode with an electron energy of 70 eV was used for MS. The ion source temperature and quadrupole temperature were set at 250 °C and 150 °C, respectively. The molecular weights were acquired in full-scan (m/z 50–550) mode. The GC-MS data were processed via Agilent Qualitative Analysis B.07.00 software with the “NIST14. L” library.

Results and discussion

Effect of temperature and catalyst on the yield and elemental composition of the hydrochar

Temperature is a key parameter governing the reaction pathways of HTC. In HTC studies, temperatures of 165 °C, 200 °C, and 260 °C are commonly associated with three characteristic stages: dehydration–decarboxylation, condensation-dominated reactions, and intensive carbonization, respectively. To isolate the effect of temperature, the reaction time was fixed at 30 min for all experiments. Under this condition, the differences in hydrochar yield among temperatures and catalytic systems could be directly compared while avoiding additional energy input associated with prolonged treatment. Fig. 1a shows the hydrochar yields for all the systems at the three temperatures. At 165 °C, the HTC system remained in an early conversion stage, where dehydration and decarboxylation occurred but were incomplete. Glucose-derived intermediates and glycine-derived species underwent limited dehydration and partial fragmentation, while the formation of insoluble carbonaceous nuclei remained restricted. Because the thermal energy at 165 °C is insufficient to drive extensive polymerization and aromatization, all the systems exhibited relatively low hydrochar yields.

(a) Hydrochar yield under different catalysts and temperatures; (b) Fe content in HTC solid; (c–e) composition of C, H, N, and O elements; and (f) van Krevelen diagram.

At 200 °C, the system entered a condensation-dominated stage, in which soluble intermediates were more effectively converted into insoluble polymerized products. Dehydrated sugar derivatives, low-molecular-weight oxygenates, and nitrogen-containing intermediates can undergo condensation, cyclization, and subsequent aromatization, leading to the formation of hydrochar. Under these conditions, the heterogeneous Fe catalyst achieved the highest yield (13.5%), which was 1.4% higher than that of the blank (12.1%). In contrast, the homogeneous catalysts FeCl3 (11.2%) and FeSO4 (12.3%) show no significant advantage over the blank. These results suggest that 200 °C provides a favorable balance between intermediate formation and solid-phase carbon accumulation within 30 min. At 260 °C, HTC proceeds into an intense carbonization stage. In addition to further aromatization and structural condensation of the carbon matrix, the solid carbon framework is partially consumed, and the product distribution shifts toward liquid and gaseous fractions, leading to lower hydrochar yields.33

Fig. 1b shows the retention and distribution of iron in the solid products. As the temperature increases, the iron content in the solid products of the Fe2O3 and Fe3O4 groups decreases. In contrast, the Fe, FeCl3, and FeSO4 groups exhibit iron enrichment with increasing temperature; among these, the Fe group achieves the highest iron content in the hydrochar at high temperature, with iron likely re-depositing as iron-containing phases on or within the hydrochar structure. Such re-deposition may promote secondary reactions and contribute to the lower hydrochar yield observed at 260 °C.34 Thus, under the present experimental conditions, 200 °C appears to be the most favorable temperature for hydrochar formation.

It should be noted that, although the heterogeneous Fe catalyst improved hydrochar formation at 200 °C under the present conditions, the absolute hydrochar yield in this work is relatively moderate compared with those reported for some HTC systems in the literature (Table S2). This difference is likely related to the choice of catalyst and, more importantly, to the functional role assigned to Fe in the present system. Rather than maximizing hydrochar yield alone, Fe was introduced here to regulate the hydrothermal conversion pathway and the structure of the resulting carbonaceous solids.

In the hydrothermal media, Fe species can participate in multiple processes, including the conversion of oxygenated intermediates, the redistribution of surface oxygen functionalities, and the deposition or anchoring of iron-containing phases on hydrochar. Therefore, the role of Fe should not be evaluated solely in terms of hydrochar yield, but also in terms of its influence on surface chemistry and microstructure. In particular, Fe incorporation is expected to modify the local bonding environment of the carbon matrix and the distribution of oxygen-containing groups, which may further alter the electronic structure of the hydrochar surface. Such structural and electronic differences are closely related to the potential performance of Fe-functionalized hydrochar in practical applications. For this reason, detailed characterization of the hydrochar structure is necessary.

To further investigate the composition of the hydrochar, the elemental composition and van Krevelen diagrams of the hydrochar produced under different catalysts and temperatures were compared (Fig. 1c–f). Hydrochar is predominantly composed of C (ranging from 50.1% to 72.7%), followed by O (16.6% to 38.1%), whereas the contents of N and H are relatively low. The HTC process leads to oxygen consumption and carbon enrichment with increasing reaction temperature. As shown in the van Krevelen diagram, the decrease in both H/C and O/C atomic ratios with elevated temperature indicates that dehydration and decarboxylation are the main reaction pathways in the absence of a catalyst.35,36 Notably, the introduction of Fe-based catalysts significantly altered this trajectory. When Fe was used as the catalyst, the hydrochar showed lower C content and higher O content compared to the blank control, resulting in consistently higher O/C ratios. This suggests that the Fe-catalyzed HTC process incorporates more oxygen into the solid product, likely in the form of oxygen-containing functional groups (e.g., –COOH, –OH) anchored on the hydrochar surface, while Fe species themselves are loaded as oxides. Accordingly, Fe did not simply promote carbon deposition; it also modified the surface chemistry of the hydrochar. Such Fe-induced structural regulation is important because the catalytic or interfacial properties of carbon materials are closely related to the local electronic environment of surface atoms. The introduction of Fe species may change the electron distribution of adjacent C/O sites through coordination interactions at Fe–O–C interfaces, thereby generating chemically distinct surface sites. However, the exact nature of these sites and their roles in specific reactions cannot be concluded from elemental analysis alone. Therefore, further structural characterization is required to clarify how Fe affects the surface functional groups, iron speciation, and the electronic structure of the hydrochar.37

Evolution of the hydrochar structures and dynamic reaction pathways

To investigate the temperature-dependent structural evolution of the hydrochar and the role of the Fe catalyst, the morphology, carbon structural order, phase composition, and surface chemistry of the samples at 165 °C, 200 °C, and 260 °C were compared. TEM images (Fig. 2a) reveal the formation of carbonaceous particles with Fe species distributed on or within the carbon matrix. At 165 °C, Fe catalyst particles agglomerate, the carbon matrix remains amorphous, and the iron signal is concentrated. This observation suggests that, at low temperature, the Fe-catalyzed hydrochar formation may proceed through adsorption-assisted interactions between Fe and intermediate species.38 However, at 165 °C, due to severe agglomeration and insufficient contact, this adsorption-catalytic effect is not fully realized. When the temperature rises to 200 °C, Fe particles become more uniformly dispersed and partially embedded within the carbon matrix. The overlap between iron and carbon signals increases, enhancing the uniformity of the Fe–C system and increasing the contact area between catalytic active sites and reactants. High-resolution TEM shows distinct lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.293 nm, closely matching the theoretical d-spacing (0.296 nm) of the (220) plane of Fe3O4.39 EDS elemental mapping (Fig. 2b) confirms the uniform distribution of C, Fe, and O, along with a significant N signal (14.6%). This indicates that nitrogen originating from glycine is incorporated into the carbonaceous solid. Nitrogen atoms incorporated into a carbon matrix, particularly in the form of pyridinic-N and pyrrolic-N, possess lone-pair electrons that can serve as Lewis basic sites. These sites enhance the local electron density of the carbon framework and provide additional coordination positions for metal ions or adsorption of polar molecules.40 The introduction of Fe did not significantly alter the specific surface area (SSA) of the hydrochar, with values of 2.39 m2 g−1 for 200-Blank and 3.59 m2 g−1 for 200-Fe (Table S3). Notably, these SSA values are much lower than those of many activated, templated, or post-carbonized Fe–C hydrochars reported in the literature because of the lack of chemical activation, gas activation, hard/soft templates, or high-temperature post-carbonization.11,13,41 Although post-treatments can substantially increase porosity, they would also alter the primary hydrothermal product and obscure the intrinsic role of iron species during HTC.

Structural characterizations of the Fe-based hydrochar prepared at different temperatures: (a) TEM images, (b) EDS elemental mapping images, (c) XRD patterns and (d) Raman spectra. XPS Fe 2p peak deconvolution results for (e) 165 °C, (f) 200 °C, and (g) 260 °C.

To identify the specific iron phases and their interaction with the carbon skeleton, XRD patterns (Fig. 2c) show weak diffraction peaks for Fe3O4 (2θ ≈ 35.6°) and Fe0 (2θ ≈ 44.7°) in the 200 °C – Fe sample. These peaks become sharper at 260 °C, confirming that the carbothermal reduction reaction intensifies with temperature. No iron phase peaks are detected at 165 °C, consistent with TEM observations of amorphous agglomeration. At 200 °C, moderate reduction preserves the carbon skeleton while forming Fe3O4 magnetic centers.42 Raman spectroscopy (Fig. 2d) was used to evaluate carbon order.43 As the temperature increases from 165 °C to 260 °C, the ID/IG ratio decreases from 2.15 to 1.19, indicating that high temperature promotes the transformation of amorphous carbon into an ordered structure. Notably, at 200 °C, the Fe-catalyzed sample exhibits a lower ID/IG (1.55) than the blank (1.67). This lower ID/IG ratio suggests that the presence of Fe facilitates the structural ordering of the carbon matrix under the same hydrothermal conditions, which may result from Fe-promoted condensation and aromatization reactions.44

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to further probe the chemical state of iron on the hydrochar surface. The XPS Fe 2p spectra (Fig. 2e–g) reveal the evolution of iron species valence states during the hydrothermal process. As the temperature increased from 165 °C to 260 °C, Fe(iii) was gradually reduced to Fe(ii), with the Fe(iii)/Fe(ii) ratio decreasing from 1.7 : 1 at 165 °C to 0.7 : 1 at 260 °C. The Fe 2p3/2 binding energies and satellite features are consistent with the presence of Fe3O4 as the dominant iron phase at elevated temperatures, in agreement with the XRD results (Fig. 2c). The higher hydrochar yield obtained with Fe0 compared to directly adding Fe2O3 or Fe3O4 (Fig. 1a) may be attributed to the in situ dissolution-reduction-recrystallization of Fe0, which likely generates smaller and more dispersed Fe3O4 nanoparticles on the carbon matrix. Combined with the yield data, the increased proportion of Fe3O4 may be a contributing factor. At 260 °C, Fe3O4 likely functions as a solid-phase catalytic center, facilitating the conversion of solid hydrochars into the liquid phase and thereby diminishing the hydrochar yield.

FTIR spectroscopy was used to compare the surface functional group distribution of HTC produced without and with the Fe catalyst (Fig. 3a). To enable a reliable comparison of peak intensities, all the spectra were normalized using the aromatic CC stretching band at 1398 cm−1 as an internal reference, which remained relatively unchanged during carbonization. The Fe-catalyzed HTC showed peak positions similar to those of the blank HTC, but with different intensities, indicating the presence of similar functional groups and chemical scaffolds, while the distributions of oxygen-containing groups and nitrogen-containing groups varied due to Fe. Overall, the broad band from 3600 cm−1 to 3050 cm−1 corresponded to O–H and N–H stretching of phenolic, alcoholic, and carboxylic functional groups. The CO stretching band at 1640 cm−1 indicated the presence of carbonyl-containing groups (carboxyl, ketone, and aldehyde). In the Fe-catalyzed samples, the normalized CO intensity reached its highest value at 200 °C, exceeding that of the blank at the same temperature. This suggests that at 200 °C, Fe facilitates the conversion of hydroxyl groups into carbonyl species, leading to a net increase in carbonyl content. The C–O–C stretching band at 1045 cm−1 (ethers and esters45) decreased with temperature but showed a higher relative intensity when the Fe catalyst was present, implying that ether/ester functionalities were more stably retained on the hydrothermal carbon under Fe, providing persistent functional sites. The peaks at 3050 cm−1 (aromatic C–H) and 1398 cm−1 (aromatic CC) confirmed the presence of an aromatic structure in the hydrothermal carbon. The addition of Fe did not significantly change the relative intensity of these aromatic bands, but clearly changed the distribution of oxygen-containing functional groups, as further evidenced by XPS C 1s and O 1s analyses (Fig. 3c, d and S2). The coexistence of Fe–O and C–O features in the XPS O 1s spectra suggests possible interfacial interactions between iron species and the carbon matrix.

(a) FTIR spectrum of the hydrochar in the range of 4000–400 cm−1, and (b) synchronous and asynchronous two-dimensional correlation maps. XPS C 1s peak deconvolution results for (c) blank hydrochar, (d) Fe-catalyzed hydrochar, and XPS N 1s peak deconvolution results for (e) blank hydrochar and (f) Fe-catalyzed hydrochar.

To further elucidate the dynamic carbonization mechanism catalyzed by Fe, two-dimensional correlation spectroscopy was applied to analyze the evolution sequence of functional groups (Fig. 3b). In the blank group, functional group evolution follows a disordered sequence, reflecting a random reaction pathway. In contrast, a well-defined reaction sequence emerges upon Fe addition: O–H → CO → C–O–C → CC. This sequence provides for the Fe-modulated reaction pathway: the initial weakening of O–H bonds corresponds to the dehydration stage, followed by CO reduction accompanied by C–O–C enhancement, indicative of decarboxylation. The final formation of CC bonds marks the completion of aromatization. This clearly demonstrates that a precisely regulated reaction pathway progresses sequentially through dehydration, decarboxylation, and aromatization.

XPS C 1s peak fitting (Fig. 3c and d) reveals the temperature evolution of oxygen-containing functional groups. With increasing temperature, sp2-C content increases, consistent with Raman trends. At 200 °C, the Fe-catalyzed sample shows much lower C–OH content (18.1%) than the blank (33.9%), indicating that Fe promotes dehydration. Simultaneously, the Fe-catalyzed sample retains higher proportions of CO and O–CO (increasing from 3.4% and 2.9% in the blank to 5.1% and 5.7%, respectively). The enhanced decarboxylation observed by FTIR spectroscopy reflects the release of unstable bulk carboxyls as CO2, whereas the elevated CO and O–CO fractions in XPS originate from hydroxyls converted into more stable carbonyl, carboxyl, and ester groups that remain anchored on the hydrochar surface. This means that Fe does not simply remove all oxygen groups but converts –OH into more stable C–O–C (ether/ester), thereby retaining oxygen within the carbon skeleton.46,47 This precisely explains the higher O/C ratio of the Fe-catalyzed hydrochar. At 260 °C, the contents of CO and O–CO decrease, indicating that, at this elevated temperature, even the more stable oxygenated groups are progressively removed, which contributes to the reduced hydrochar yield. XPS O 1s analysis (Fig. S2) further supports this interpretation. In the Fe-catalyzed hydrochars, a distinct Fe–O component appears and increases with temperature, consistent with Fe3O4 formation. The O–CO reaches its maximum at 200 °C, in agreement with the C 1s results. The coexistence of Fe–O and C–O/O–CO features implies spatial proximity between iron oxides and oxygen-containing functional groups, consistent with possible Fe–O–C interactions at the Fe–C interface.

The XPS N 1s spectra (Fig. 3e and f) indicate that nitrogen species evolve from thermally labile amino-N toward more stable heterocyclic nitrogen configurations within the carbon skeleton. As the temperature increased, the amino-N content in each group declined, and the introduction of Fe accelerated this consumption. At 200 °C, the amino-N fraction in 200-Fe is only 13.5%, substantially lower than that in 200-Blank (26.9%), whereas the fraction of pyridinic-N reaches 31.1% in 200-Fe compared with 26.7% in 200-Blank. Fe species in the hydrothermal medium can act as Lewis acid centers due to the electron-deficient nature of Fe(iii)/Fe(ii) ions, which can accept electron pairs from amino groups and thereby lower the activation energy for deamination.48 The consumption of amino-N accompanied the enrichment of nitrogen-containing heterocycles, such as pyridine-N/amine-N and pyrrole-N/imidazole-N, which promoted the deep conversion of glycine-like substrates. The resulting pyridinic-N and pyrrolic-N species possess lone-pair electrons that can participate in π-conjugation with the carbon matrix, thereby modulating the local electronic structure. Pyridinic-N sites, in particular, exhibit Lewis basicity and have been shown to act as active centers for adsorption and catalytic reactions, such as oxygen reduction or persulfate activation.49,50

These results suggest that, at 200 °C, the Fe catalyst exhibits relatively optimal catalytic performance. It promotes carbon skeleton ordering (decreased ID/IG) and selective dehydration (reduced C–OH), while preserving CO/C–O–C moieties to maintain a high O/C ratio and abundant active sites. Meanwhile, the carbothermal reduction proceeds moderately without excessive carbon consumption, thus affording the highest yield. In contrast, at 260 °C, despite further enhanced carbon ordering, carbothermal reduction induces extensive oxidative degradation of the carbon skeleton and substantial loss of oxygen-containing functional groups, ultimately leading to a lower yield.

Evolution of dissolved organic matter and hydrochar formation mechanism in the liquid phase

Chemical characterization of the liquid-phase products revealed the complex reaction network during hydrothermal carbonization (Fig. 4a and b). The compounds detected by GC-MS encompassed various functional group categories, including nitrogen-containing compounds, aldehydes, ketones, esters, carboxylic acids, alcohols, and phenols (Fig. S3). In the blank group, the liquid phase was rich in oxygenated small molecules: organic acids (22.6%), alcohols (21.4%), and ketones (12.1%), while nitrogen-containing compounds accounted for only 26.3% at 165 °C. This composition is consistent with incomplete dehydration and limited condensation at low temperature, consistent with the low hydrochar yield and high O/C ratio observed at this temperature. As the temperature increased to 200 °C and further to 260 °C, the abundance of oxygenated aliphatic species (acids, alcohols, aldehydes) in the liquid phase progressively declined, while the proportion of nitrogen-containing heterocycles increased. Concurrently, the hydrochar exhibited decreasing H/C and O/C ratios (Fig. 1e), indicating progressive dehydration and aromatization. The depletion of small oxygenates from the liquid phase and the enrichment of nitrogenous heterocycles (e.g., pyrazine derivatives) correspond to their incorporation into the growing hydrochar framework via condensation and Maillard-type reactions. Thus, in the blank system, carbon is gradually transferred from the liquid phase (as reactive intermediates) to the solid phase (as aromatic structures) with increasing temperature.

Organic composition of the carbonization liquor and Fe distribution between solid and liquid phases: (a) organic composition; (b) composition of the precursors for condensation-polymerization reactions; (c) iron concentration in the liquid phase; and (d) iron distribution in the solid and liquid phases.

The addition of the Fe catalyst markedly altered this solid–liquid partitioning. At 165 °C, the Fe-catalyzed carbonization liquor showed a dramatic increase in nitrogen-containing compounds to 76.2% of the GC-MS peak area, driven primarily by a surge in pyridine derivatives (from 7.4% to 53.2%) and an increase in pyrazole compounds (to 12.8%). This enrichment of N-heterocycles in the liquid phase is consistent with increased nitrogen retention in the hydrochar. Fe acts as a Lewis acid to promote the Maillard reaction between glucose and glycine, accelerating the formation of nitrogenous intermediates that partition between the liquid and the growing solid.51–53 At 200 °C, nitrogen-containing compounds dominated the liquid phase (95.3% peak area), while the hydrochar achieved its maximum yield and retained a high O/C ratio due to Fe-stabilized oxygen functional groups. This indicates that Fe not only accelerates condensation but also modifies the nature of the solid product, retaining more oxygen and nitrogen within the carbon matrix. At 260 °C, the aqueous product contains an increased fraction of alcohols and ketones compared with the 200 °C liquor.

These species arise from the partial oxidative degradation and hydrolytic cleavage of the hydrochar framework under the high-temperature hydrothermal conditions. In the presence of Fe, the Fe3O4 crystallites embedded in the carbon matrix may act as solid-phase catalysts that facilitate the conversion of the carbonaceous solid into water-soluble organic fragments, thereby transferring carbon from the solid phase back into the liquid phase.54 The carbon lost from the solid hydrochar thus appears in the liquid phase predominantly as carboxylic acids, phenolic compounds, and low-molecular-weight oxygenates, suggesting that Fe-containing phases may facilitate fragmentation or transformation of the carbon framework.55 Therefore, the yield reduction at 260 °C is a consequence of carbon redistribution toward the aqueous phase, driven by Fe-catalyzed degradation of the hydrochar framework. This Fe3O4-catalyzed solid-to-liquid conversion accounts for the reduced hydrochar yield at 260 °C.

Integrating the composition of the carbonization liquor with the properties of the hydrochar, the mechanism of Fe dissolution followed by recrystallization during HTC regulated the hydrochar morphology (Fig. 4c and d). In the initial stage, the dissolution of nano-iron powder was facilitated by the weakly acidic hydrothermal environment (Fig. S4), leading to the oxidative release of Fe(iii)/Fe(ii) ions into the liquid phase. As the temperature rose from 165 °C to 260 °C, dissolved iron content decreased while solid-phase iron content increased, indicating a phase transfer from liquid to solid. Hydroxyl-rich glucose and its hydrolysis products acted as reductants, reducing Fe(iii) to Fe0 and Fe3O4.26 Compared with direct addition of Fe2O3 or Fe3O4, the use of Fe0 led to a higher hydrochar yield (Fig. 1a) and more uniform Fe distribution (Fig. 2a and b). It is proposed that the in situ dissolution-reduction-recrystallization process of Fe0 generates smaller and better-dispersed Fe3O4 nanoparticles than direct addition of pre-synthesized oxides. These finely dispersed Fe3O4 nanoparticles likely provide more accessible catalytic sites for condensation and aromatization, thereby enhancing the yield and structural uniformity of the Fe–C hydrochar. This process is further enhanced by a synergistic effect between glycine and glucose: glycine improves electron-transfer efficiency, complementing the electron-donating capacity of the hydroxyl groups of glucose, thereby accelerating iron conversion.56 Driven by self-generated pressure and concentration gradients, reduced iron species nucleated and uniformly precipitated within the carbon matrix as Fe3O4. These iron species adsorbed aromatic compounds from the liquid phase, facilitating surface growth and forming the iron-core-carbon-shell structure observed by TEM.

Based on the above results, a plausible four-stage reaction mechanism is proposed for Fe-catalyzed HTC of glucose-glycine (Fig. 5). Hydrolysis and activation predominate at 165 °C. Dissociated hydronium ions (H3O+) from water molecules synergize with the Lewis acid sites of Fe, coordinating with the (–OH) groups in glucose to lower the activation energy for dehydration. Dehydration/decarboxylation and the Maillard reaction predominate at 165–200 °C. This stage is critical for determining the properties of the final products. Glucose undergoes dehydration to yield furan derivatives such as 5-hydroxymethylfurfural. Concurrently, the Fe catalyst substantially accelerates the Maillard reaction between glycine and glucose, promoting the formation of nitrogenous heterocyclic intermediates. The non-catalyzed system favors pyrazine-based compounds, whereas the Fe-catalyzed system preferentially generates pyridine and pyrrole derivatives. These N-heterocyclic intermediates undergo condensation and cross-linking to construct the hydrochar framework. During this temperature window, carbon progressively accumulates in the solid phase, while the liquid phase becomes enriched in N-heterocycles that remain in dynamic exchange with the growing solid matrix. The concurrent decrease in dissolved Fe(iii) concentration and the appearance of Fe3O4/Fe0 crystallites in the solid phase indicate that Fe(iii) is reduced under these hydrothermal conditions. Polymerization and aromatization predominate at 200 °C. The generated nitrogenous heterocycles (e.g., pyrazine, pyridine) undergo cross-linking polymerization, constructing a hydrochar skeleton rich in aromatic structures.57 Iron species crystallize into uniformly dispersed Fe3O4 nanoparticles within the carbon matrix. These nanoparticles subsequently act as nucleation cores for carbonization precursors, which deposit and encapsulate them to form a core–shell structure. Consistent with the XPS O 1s results, Fe–O bonds may form possible Fe–O–C linkages with the C–O–C framework, which may enhance the structural order of the hydrochar. Fe-catalyzed hydrochar exhibits Fe–O–C characteristics, with pyridinic-N embedded in the carbon skeleton and incorporated Fe3O4 crystallites, structures that can influence adsorption and catalytic behavior.58 Partial degradation and carbon redistribution predominate at 260 °C. At elevated temperatures, the hydrochar framework becomes susceptible to hydrolytic and oxidative cleavage. Fe3O4 crystallites catalyze the fragmentation of the carbon matrix into water-soluble organic species, which appear in the liquid phase. This process transfers carbon from the solid back to the liquid phase, resulting in a reduced hydrochar yield, despite further increases in the structural order of the remaining solid.

Reaction mechanism of the iron-catalyzed HTC process.

In summary, iron serves as a multifunctional catalyst: it selectively accelerates the formation of nitrogen-containing heterocycles, particularly pyridinic nitrogen, while simultaneously promoting dehydration (reducing –OH groups). Concurrently, it may stabilize a portion of carboxyl functional groups through Fe–O–C bonding and complexation while simultaneously directing solid-phase structural organization via Fe3O4-mediated cross-linking and nucleation. This synergistic effect significantly enhances the active sites and structural order of the hydrochar.

Adsorption behavior as a probe for Fe-induced interfacial chemistry

Adsorption experiments were conducted to probe the structure–function relationship of Fe-induced interfacial chemistry. Fig. 6a compares the adsorption capacities of 200-Blank and 200-Fe hydrochars at equilibrium (120 min). The results show that the addition of Fe enhanced the adsorption performance for the cationic dye MB and heavy metal ions (Cu2+ and Ni2+), while inhibiting the adsorption of the anionic dye MO. The 200-Fe sample exhibited the highest adsorption capacity for MB, reaching 42.8 mg g−1, significantly higher than that of the blank group (29.9 mg g−1). The pronounced difference in surface charge between 200-Blank and 200-Fe hydrochars provides a clear explanation for the contrasting adsorption behavior toward cationic and anionic species. Zeta potential measurements revealed a strongly negative surface charge for 200-Fe (ζ = −31.9 mV, Table S3), whereas 200-Blank exhibited a less negative value. This increased negative surface charge upon Fe addition originates from the altered distribution of oxygen-containing functional groups on the hydrochar surface.

(a) Comparison of the adsorption capacities of the hydrochar for methylene blue, methyl orange, Cu2+, and Ni2+ (C0 = 20 mg L−1, t = 120 min, and 25 °C). (b) Adsorption kinetic curve of the hydrochar. Fitting of the adsorption kinetics curve: (c) methylene blue, (d) methyl orange, (e) Cu2+, and (f) Ni2+.

As demonstrated by XPS C 1s and FTIR analyses (Fig. 3), Fe catalyzes the conversion of hydroxyl (–OH) groups into more stable carboxyl (–COOH) and ester (C–O–C) moieties. Under the adsorption conditions (pH 5.0–6.0), a substantial fraction of these surface carboxyl groups exists in the deprotonated carboxylate form (–COO−), which imparts a net negative charge to the hydrochar surface. The higher O/C ratio of 200-Fe further corroborates the greater abundance of oxygen-containing functionalities that serve as negative charge carriers. Additionally, the point of zero charge (pHpzc) of 200-Fe was determined to be 2.7, substantially lower than that of 200-Blank (5.1) (Fig. S5). The 200-Fe surface carries a net negative charge, favoring electrostatic attraction toward cationic MB, Cu2+, and Ni2+, while electrostatically repelling anionic MO. In contrast, the 200-Blank surface, with a higher pHpzc, possesses fewer negative sites at the same pH, resulting in weaker electrostatic interactions with cationic adsorbates. This aligns with the enhanced adsorption of MB and the inhibition of MO. Concurrently, the increase in sp2-C content from XPS C 1s analysis and the decrease in the ID/IG ratio from Raman spectroscopy collectively confirm a higher degree of graphitization and aromaticity in 200-Fe. This conjugated π-system may further enhance MB adsorption through π–π interactions.59 Similar π–π interactions have been reported in Fe3O4/carbon composites for the adsorption of aromatic pollutants.60 Therefore, the adsorption contrast between MB and MO provides evidence that Fe0 regulation modifies the hydrochar interface.

Regarding heavy metal adsorption (Cu2+ and Ni2+), the 200-Fe hydrochar showed a higher adsorption capacity for Cu2+ (16.29 mg g−1) than for Ni2+ (8.93 mg g−1), consistent with the Irving–Williams stability series.61 This trend suggests the formation of inner-sphere complexes during adsorption. The higher proportion of pyridinic-N in 200-Fe (confirmed by the XPS N 1s results) provides abundant active sites for heavy metal adsorption. Acting as a Lewis base, pyridinic-N can strongly coordinate with Lewis acidic metal ions via surface complexation, constituting the primary active sites for heavy metal uptake.

Based on the BET surface areas, surface-area-normalized adsorption capacities were calculated (Table S4). For MB, the normalized capacity of 200-Fe (11.93 mg m−2) was comparable to that of 200-Blank (12.49 mg m−2), indicating that the higher MB uptake is mainly due to the increased surface area. In contrast, for Cu2+ and Ni2+, the normalized capacities of 200-Fe were 1.7- and 2.2-fold higher than those of 200-Blank, respectively, suggesting that the improved heavy metal adsorption arises from Fe-induced surface chemical modifications (e.g., more negative surface charge and higher pyridinic-N content). This unit-area adsorption efficiency for Cu2+ is substantially higher than that of a previously reported Fe–C hydrochar prepared under hydrothermal conditions,14 further confirming the effectiveness of Fe-induced surface chemistry. It is worth mentioning that the obtained adsorption capabilities towards dyes and heavy metal cations in this study are lower than the reported values. And the adsorption tests are used as structure-function probes of Fe-induced interfacial chemistry rather than as a demonstration of optimized adsorption capacity.

The adsorption kinetic curves (Fig. 6b) indicate a rapid process, with equilibrium reached within approximately 30 minutes. Kinetic fitting results (Fig. 6c–f and Table S5) show that the PSO model provides higher correlation coefficients (R2) than the PFO model, offering a more accurate description of the adsorption behavior. This suggests that the adsorption process is predominantly governed by chemisorption, suggesting that the rate-determining step involves coordination/complexation and π–π interactions between the hydrochar and the adsorbates (MB, Cu2+, Ni2+), rather than mere physical diffusion. A benchmark comparison with representative Fe–C hydrochars reported in the literature is provided in Table S6. Many previously reported Fe–C hydrochars exhibit substantially higher BET surface areas and adsorption capacities than 200-Fe, particularly when activation, templating, or high-temperature post-treatment is employed. In contrast, the present 200-Fe hydrochar was obtained directly from mild HTC without additional porosity engineering. Therefore, its lower BET surface area and moderate adsorption capacity are not unexpected. This comparison further validates the critical role of Fe in altering the interfacial chemical selectivity of the hydrochar.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that zero-valent iron acts as a multifunctional regulator during glucose-glycine hydrothermal carbonization. Rather than simply improving hydrochar yield or adsorption capacity, Fe0 directs the HTC reaction pathway by promoting dehydration/decarboxylation, accelerating Maillard-type nitrogen incorporation, redistributing oxygen-containing functional groups, and inducing Fe dissolution-recrystallization. At 200 °C, these coupled processes lead to the formation of Fe3O4-containing Fe–C interfacial hydrochars enriched with pyridinic-N and stabilized oxygen-containing moieties. Adsorption tests toward cationic and anionic probes further confirm that Fe0-regulated HTC alters the surface charge, pHpzc, and coordination environment of the hydrochar, resulting in selective affinity toward cationic species. The Fe-regulated hydrochar exhibits enhanced adsorption of methylene blue (42.8 mg g−1) and Cu2+ (20.93 mg g−1) compared to the unregulated hydrochar. These findings provide insight into how Fe0 regulates carbohydrate-amino-acid HTC chemistry and Fe–C interfacial architecture formation and provide a rational basis for designing iron catalysts to produce application-tailored hydrochars.
