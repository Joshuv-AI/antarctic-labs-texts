A Retrospective Cohort Study to Determine Real-World Treatment Patterns in Patients with Sézary Syndrome in the United States
Authors: Robert Ristuccia, Yang Zhao, Chunlan Chang, Huanxue Zhou, Takeshi Takahashi, Takanobu Nomura, Eslie Dennis, Oleg Akilov
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSézary syndrome (SS) is a rare leukemic cutaneous T cell lymphoma. This study was conducted to examine the real-world treatment patterns among patients with SS in the USA from 2018 to 2020.Methods This was a retrospective cohort study using the Symphony Health Solutions claims database. Adult patients with ≥ 1 diagnosis code for SS were classified into three non-mutually exclusive cohorts: 2018, 2019, and 2020. Patient characteristics and treatment patterns were examined across the 3 years of study and reported descriptively for each year. Annual treatment patterns were also described for the five states with the highest proportions of SS patients in 2020.ResultsOverall, 869, 882, and 853 SS patients were identified in 2018, 2019, and 2020, respectively (median age: 70 years for each year; male: 54.4%, 54.8%, and 55.6%, respectively). The use of any systemic and parenteral systemic treatments increased over time. While utilization rates for many specific systemic therapies decreased over the study period, mogamulizumab use increased, making it the most commonly used systemic treatment in 2020 (29.2%) among patients with any systemic treatment. The five states with the highest proportions of SS patients in 2020 were Florida, New York, California, Texas, and Pennsylvania. Systemic treatment patterns varied considerably by state.ConclusionSome systemic therapies showed decreased usage over time while a few showed increased utilization, with mogamulizumab showing the largest increase. Treatment patterns for SS varied by region. Further research is needed to examine the factors that drive treatment selection for patients with SS.

Introduction

Cutaneous T cell lymphomas (CTCL) are a heterogenous group of non-Hodgkin lymphomas that originate in the skin [1, 2] and typically occur in older adults [3]. CTCLs are relatively rare, with an annual incidence of 6–10/million persons and prevalence of approximately 40,000 persons in the US [2, 4]. Sézary syndrome (SS) is an advanced leukemic subtype of CTCL and accounts for approximately 5% of CTCL cases in the US [5, 6]. SS is characterized by erythroderma and diffuse lymphadenopathy and is associated with leukemic blood involvement [7, 8]. Upon progression, patients with SS usually experience a worsening state of immune suppression, leading to an increased risk of infections and secondary malignancies [3]. The prognosis for patients with SS remains poor, with a median survival of 3.1 years [3, 8–10].

The therapeutic landscape for SS consists primarily of systemic therapy (e.g., extracorporeal photopheresis [ECP], parenteral systemics, and oral systemics) and skin-directed therapy (SDT; e.g., topical agents, localized radiation, total skin electron beam therapy [TSEBT], and phototherapy) [11–13]. To date, the only therapeutic modality with curative intent is bone marrow transplantation (BMT), which is associated with high rates of mortality, morbidity, and relapse and is only possible in a minority of patients [14]. Nonetheless, the approval of novel systemic therapies such as mogamulizumab offers the potential for improved patient outcomes [1].

Given the number of available therapies and a treatment history that usually includes multiple lines of therapy, therapeutic decision-making for SS can be complex. Real-world evidence on treatment patterns among patients with this rare disease is limited [8, 13, 15, 16]. The objective of the current study was to examine the real-world treatment patterns among patients with SS in the US from 2018 to 2020 as well as to assess whether these treatment patterns varied between five key states.

Methods

Data Source

This retrospective cohort study used patient-level data from a large, de-identified, claims database, Symphony Health Solutions’ Integrated Dataverse® (SHS). Within SHS, data from medical practices, pharmacies, and hospitals are linked to allow longitudinal assessment of healthcare utilization. The database contains approximately 274 million active patients in the US and is considered to be representative of the US population in terms of sex, age, geographic distribution, and insurance coverage [17]. Data for this study were collected over the period from January 2018 to December 2020. All patient records were de-identified and fully compliant with US patient confidentiality requirements. Given that this study was a non-interventional, retrospective analysis involving de-identified claims data, neither ethical review nor informed consent was required.

Patient Selection

Eligible patients were those with at least one medical claim with an International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) diagnosis code for SS (C84.1x) in 2018, 2019, or 2020. Outcome measures were examined separately for each year; thus, there were three non-mutually exclusive cohorts of patients with at least one diagnosis code for SS in 2018, 2019, and 2020.

Outcomes and Measures

Demographic characteristics were examined for each cohort [18]. Treatments of interest included all options recommended by the NCCN Guidelines® [12] and were grouped as follows: systemic therapies, SDTs, and BMT. Systemic therapies included ECP, parenteral systemics (alemtuzumab, bortezomib, brentuximab vedotin, cyclophosphamide, etoposide, gemcitabine, interferon alfa-2b, interferon gamma 1b, liposomal doxorubicil, methotrexate, mogamulizumab, nivolumab, pembrolizumab, pentostatin IV, pralatrexate, romidepsin), and oral systemics (acitretin, bexarotene, chlorambucil, cyclophosphamide, etoposide, isotretinoin, lenalidomide, methotrexate, temozolomide, vorinostat). SDTs included topical therapy, phototherapy, local radiation (involved-site radiation therapy), and TSEBT.

The annual treatment rate was defined as the number of patients with at least one claim for each type of treatment in each year, divided by the total number of patients with SS in that year. These rates were examined across the 3 years of the study to observe whether there were any notable changes over time. The most commonly prescribed systemic therapies were also determined by dividing the number of patients with at least one claim for each systemic therapy in each year by the number of SS patients treated with any systemic therapy in that year.

Treatment rates were additionally examined at the quarterly level to assess the impact of COVID-19. Quarterly treatment rates and most common systemic therapies were defined as the number of patients with at least one claim for each treatment type by quarter in each year of study, divided by the number of SS patients with at least one claim in that year.

Sub-analyses examined geographic variations in systemic and SDT patterns across the five US states with the highest proportions of patients with SS in the most recent year of data capture (2020). Annual treatment rates and most commonly prescribed systemic therapies were examined at a state level for these five states.

Analyses

All analyses were descriptive, with means and standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables and frequency counts and percentages for categorical variables.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Overall, 869, 882, and 853 patients with at least one SS diagnosis were identified in 2018, 2019, and 2020, respectively (Table 1). Patient characteristics were similar across the cohorts. Approximately 54–56% of patients were male, and the median age was 70 years in all three cohorts. The greatest proportion of patients was in the South, followed by the Northeast. Annual Treatment Patterns

Overall, 64.6%, 67.7%, and 68.8% of SS patients had claims for at least one form of treatment in 2018, 2019, and 2020, respectively. Nearly 50% of patients had claims for systemic therapy in each year (Table 2). There was a numeric increase in patients receiving both systemic and SDTs, from 41.8 to 46.5% and 48.9 to 52.9%, respectively, between 2018 and 2020. The increase in systemic therapy was mainly driven by an increase in parenteral therapies, from 20.7 to 28.7% over the 3-year period. The increase in SDT appeared to be driven by an increase in topical therapy claims, from 42.3% in 2018 to 48.3% in 2020. Very few patients had BMT (≤ 1.5% per year). Quarterly Treatment Patterns

Parenteral systemic therapy use decreased from 11.6% in Q1 to 10.5% in Q4 of 2018; however, there was a progressive increase in usage observed from Q1 to Q4 in both 2019 and 2020 (15.3–16.9%, and 16.8–17.5%, respectively) (Fig. 1b). Parenteral systemic therapy was the most common systemic therapy (> 14%) in each quarter of 2019 and 2020, followed by ECP in 2019 (ranging from 9.9–11.0%) and oral systemic therapy in 2020 (ranging from 9.4 to 11.1%). Oral systemic therapy use decreased from 11.5% in Q1 to 9.4% in Q4 of 2018, remained relatively stable from 8.8% in Q1 to 9.0% in Q4 of 2019, and increased slightly from 10.2% Q1 to 11.1% in Q4 of 2020. Despite the increasing trend, there was a notable dip in utilization (9.4%) in Q2 2020, coinciding with the start of the COVID-19 pandemic. ECP usage decreased from Q1 to Q4 within each study year; similar to oral systemic therapy, there was a notable dip (8.4%) in Q2 2020.

Geographic Variation in Treatment Patterns

Among the 853 patients diagnosed with SS in 2020, the five US states with the highest proportions of patients were Florida (13.1%), New York (10.7%), California (9.1%), Texas (6.7%), and Pennsylvania (5.6%). The proportion of patients with at least one form of treatment over 3 years in Florida, New York, Texas, and Pennsylvania ranged from 62.2 to 74.7%, with this being slightly lower in California (51.0–64.1%). In New York (Fig. 3b), bexarotene was the most common systemic therapy in 2018 (33.3%) and 2020 (30.4%) and the second highest in 2019 (27.8%) after ECP (30.6%). Mogamulizumab use increased from 2019 (19.4%) to 2020 (23.9%) to become the second most common systemic therapy. Treatment rates for oral methotrexate decreased over time (from 19.0% in 2018 to 8.7% in 2020), while romidepsin rates increased (from 7.1% in 2018 to 13.0% in 2020).

In California (Fig. 3c), romidepsin was the most common systemic therapy in 2018 (28.6%); however, mogamulizumab was most common in 2019 (30.0%) and 2020 (42.4%). Oral methotrexate usage increased from 21.4% in 2018 to 36.4% in 2020, whereas ECP usage decreased over time from 21.4% in 2018 to 6.1% in 2020. Bexarotene usage varied between years (2018: 7.1%, 2019: 15.0%, 2020: 9.1%).

In Texas (Fig. 3d), systemic treatment rates varied by year. In 2018, bexarotene was the most common (29.6%); however, rates decreased in 2019 (10.7%) and 2020 (10.7%). In 2019, romidepsin was most common (28.6%); in 2020, ECP was most common (35.7%). Overall, an increase in rates of ECP was observed from 2018 (22.2%) and 2019 (25.0%). Mogamulizumab usage was relatively low in 2019 and 2020 (10.7% and 10.7%, respectively) compared to other states. Oral methotrexate use remained low over time (from 0.0% in 2018 to 3.6% in 2020).

In Pennsylvania (Fig. 3e), ECP was the most common systemic therapy across all 3 years of the study (2018: 68.8%, 2019: 60.7%, 2020: 64.0%). Mogamulizumab was the second most common in 2019 and 2020 (25.0% and 24.0%, respectively). Bexarotene usage remained stable from 2018 (25.0%) to 2019 (25.0%); however, it decreased slightly in 2020 (20.0%). Romidepsin usage decreased (2018: 18.8%, 2019: 3.6%, 2020: 8.0%), and oral methotrexate use remained low over time (from 0.0% in 2018 to 4.0% in 2020).

Discussion

Using a large claims database, this study examined the real-world treatment patterns among patients with SS in the US. Although SS is a rare disease, over 850 patients were analyzed each year from 2018 to 2020. Utilization of systemic treatments, particularly parenteral systemic treatments, increased over time. Although treatment rates for many specific systemic therapies decreased over the study period, some increased, including mogamulizumab, which became the most common systemic treatment in 2020.

To our knowledge, this is the first study conducted within the contemporary treatment landscape that specifically highlights the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic. This study is also the first to examine the regional variation in treatment patterns among patients with SS in the US. The five states with the highest proportions of SS patients each had different treatment patterns over time. Overall, the findings suggest that there are significant variations in the treatment of patients with SS depending on geographical location, time of the year, and/or the potential impact of drug formulary on treatment decision making.

Approximately half of all patients had claims for SDTs, most of which were for topical agents. Topical therapies are often used in combination with systemic therapies for the treatment of SS [12, 19]. Topical therapies may provide symptomatic relief when patients change from one systemic therapy to another. There was a decreasing trend in the use of phototherapy across the study period. Marked decreases in the use of local radiation and TSEBT were also observed in 2020. This may potentially correspond to treatment modifications made to limit patient exposure to COVID-19.

In 2019, the most used systemic therapy was ECP, followed by mogamulizumab, bexarotene, and romidepsin. While ECP, bexarotene, and romidepsin usage decreased across the study period, mogamulizumab usage increased following its approval in 2018 [20]. Findings from this study differ from those of a previously conducted multicenter study among patients with SS, which identified oral retinoids as the most common first-line systemic therapy [15]. These discrepancies could be due to the differences in time periods between the two studies (1990–2020 vs. 2018–2020), highlighting the rapidly changing treatment landscape.

The quarterly analyses identified a notable drop in ECP and oral systemic therapies in Q2 of 2020. It is possible that the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic could have impacted treatment selection and administration. Previous studies have indicated that the pandemic had a substantial impact on healthcare systems and patient access [21]. During this time, the NCCN issued a series of recommendations for the treatment of patients with cancer on systemic therapy regimens, including longer dosing intervals, switching to equivalent oral therapies, and at-home administration [22]. The decrease in oral therapy usage may at first seem counterintuitive given this guidance, but it is possible that the additional monitoring requirements associated with treatments like bexarotene may have prompted physicians to choose therapies that required in-office visits for treatment but less overall time in treatment centers. It is also possible that some treatments delivered were not captured in the database. While there was a decrease in utilization for ECP and oral systemic therapies in Q2 2020, the increase in topical therapy and parenteral systemic therapies suggests that utilization remained relatively steady. These findings could reflect the severity of SS, as most recommendations for delaying treatment during the COVID-19 pandemic made exceptions for patients with life-threatening conditions.

The relatively high concentration of patients in Florida, New York, California, Texas, and Pennsylvania is likely caused by the high concentration of the US population and the location of specialist “centers of excellence” within these states. Interestingly, however, there were variations in the treatment patterns across these states, which suggests that there is not universal agreement on the most appropriate treatment regimens for patients with SS. There was a marked increase in SDT use over time in California and Pennsylvania and a substantial decrease in Texas. Systemic therapy use increased over time in both California and New York. By contrast, usage increased from 2018 to 2019 but decreased from 2019 to 2020 in Florida, Pennsylvania, and Texas. These varying treatment patterns and utilization of therapies observed across the five states may reflect differences in real-world clinical practice, access/reimbursement, and patient preferences across institutions, and highlights the complexity of therapeutic decision-making and the lack of standardized treatment algorithms for SS. Future research is needed to identify predictors of response to treatment and define optimal treatment sequencing.

There are several limitations to this study, mostly owing to the data source. Given that SHS is an open claims database, the results are limited by the availability, quality, and completeness of the data. Despite the severity of SS, approximately 35% of patients in the study did not have claims for any NCCN-recommended treatment. This may reflect the fact that clinical trials are often recommended for patients with SS [1, 12]. It is therefore likely that at least some patients were receiving treatments in clinical trials or from other sources not captured by SHS. In addition, due to the open claims data used in this study, it was not possible to ascertain whether prescribed medications identified through pharmacy claims were actually initiated. However, due to the serious nature of the disease, it is expected that the number of patients who had claims for treatment but did not initiate treatment would be small. A further limitation of this study is that the data are limited by the accuracy of diagnosis and diagnosis code recording; however, this is a limitation of a clinical nature that is not possible to control for in a retrospective analysis of this nature. The impact on this study is not expected to be large. Lastly, the results may not be generalizable to SS patients in other countries or to populations with different insurance coverage or patient characteristics.

Conclusion

Using a large US claims database to examine treatment patterns for patients with SS from 2018 to 2020, use of systemic therapies such as ECP, bexarotene, and romidepsin decreased while mogamulizumab use increased over time. Treatment patterns for SS varied by region. Further research is needed to explain the regional variability in treatment usage and the factors that drive treatment selection as well as the outcomes for patients with SS.
