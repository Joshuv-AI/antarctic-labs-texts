Safety and feasibility of intermediate-dose post-transplant cyclophosphamide for graft-versus-host disease prophylaxis
Authors: Natasha Venugopal, Andrew McGowan, Yoshitaka Inoue, Hong Zheng, Joseph Cioccio, Kevin Rakszawski, Natthapol Songdej, Myles Nickolich, Seema Naik, Christopher Ehmann, Jeffrey Sivik, Joseph Mierski, Brooke Silar, Caitlin Vajdic, Hiroko Shike, Shin Mineishi, Kentaro Minagawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Post-transplant cyclophosphamide (PTCy) is widely used for graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) prophylaxis in allogeneic hematopoietic stem-cell transplant (HSCT). However, the optimal dosing of PTCy remains uncertain. In this case series, we evaluated intermediate-dose PTCy (25–30 mg/kg/day on Days 3 and 4) in 11 consecutive patients treated at our center between February 2023 and November 2024. Outcomes regarding engraftment were compared to our center’s historical cohort, which received standard-dose PTCy. All 11 patients achieved neutrophil engraftment, and 10 achieved platelet engraftment. Median times to neutrophil, platelet, and lymphocyte engraftment were Day 14, 17, and 21, respectively—significantly shorter than in the standard-dose group (Day 18, 23, and 40; p < 0.05 for all comparisons, Mann–Whitney U test). Complete donor chimerism was observed in all patients by Day 30. Compared to our center’s historical cohort, significantly more patients in the intermediate-dose group achieved complete donor chimerism in both total cells and T cells (p < 0.01 and p = 0.03, respectively; Fisher’s exact test). Among the 11 patients, acute GVHD occurred in 3 patients (one each of grade I, II, and III); two of those 3 developed severe chronic GVHD. One patient died from diffuse alveolar hemorrhage; the others remain alive without relapse. Intermediate-dose PTCy demonstrated favorable engraftment, high donor chimerism, and acceptable toxicity, suggesting it may be a viable option for GVHD prophylaxis, particularly in vulnerable populations such as elderly patients, those with poor performance status, comorbidities, or splenomegaly. Larger prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-025-06657-8.

Main body

The prevention and management of graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) remains a primary concern for patients undergoing allogeneic hematopoietic stem-cell transplant (HSCT). Among prophylaxis strategies, post-transplant high-dose cyclophosphamide (PTCy) has emerged as a preferred option. Initially used in haploidentical HSCT [1], PTCy is now also appropriate for transplants with HLA-matched siblings and unrelated donors based on the Phase III BMT CTN 1703 trial [2]. The standard PTCy regimen involves administering 50 mg/kg/day on Day 3 and 4 post-transplant, in combination with tacrolimus (Tac, 0.05–0.06 mg/kg/day orally or 0.02–0.03 mg/kg/day intravenously) and mycophenolate mofetil (MMF, 15 mg/kg per dose orally or intravenously three times daily) [2]. While now a cornerstone of GVHD prophylaxis, the optimal timing and dosing of PTCy remains uncertain [3]. This regimen is also associated with limitations, such as delayed engraftment, graft failure or poor graft function (GF/PGF), impaired immune recovery, increased infection risks, and organ-specific toxicities, particularly cardiac complications [4, 5].

Several studies have explored reduced-dose PTCy regimens to mitigate toxicities while maintaining efficacy (Table 1). For example, a modified protocol utilizing PTCy at 40 mg/kg/day on Day 3 and 4 post-transplant combined with Tac has been shown to be both safe and effective in certain populations, such as those with advanced age or preexisting cardiac conditions [6]. The optimal dosing strategy of PTCy remains unclear, highlighting the need for further investigation to establish the safest and most effective regimen. We present a case series examining the safety and efficacy of intermediate-dose PTCy. This case series included 11 consecutive patients who underwent HSCT using intermediate-dose PTCy between February 2023 and December 2024. Patient details are shown in Table 2. Statistical analyses are described in the Supplemental Methods. The median age of patients at the time of HSCT was 64 years (range: 36–76 years). The diseases included acute myeloid leukemia in 7 patients (all in remission at HSCT) and myelodysplastic syndromes/myeloproliferative neoplasms in 4 patients (non-remission at HSCT). All patients had poor Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS < 80) at HSCT; Case 2 had a particularly low KPS of 50. Seven patients had splenomegaly before HSCT (Mild: ≥ 12 cm in 5, Moderate: ≥ 15, < 20 cm in 2, and imaging was unavailable in 2 patients). Donors included HLA-matched related in 1, HLA-matched unrelated in 6, HLA 1-allele mismatched unrelated donor in 2, and HLA-haploidentical related in 2 cases. Peripheral blood (PB) was used in all cases. One patient received 30 mg/kg/day on Day 3 and Day 4 (Case 4), while the others received PTCy at a dose of 25 mg/kg/day on Day 3 and 4. Reasons for dose reduction were poor general condition (n = 6: including advanced age ≥ 70 years = 2, cardiac comorbidity = 2, fungal infection = 1, intolerance to chemotherapy = 1), splenomegaly (n = 2), re-transplantation (n = 2), and HLA-matched sibling donor (n = 1, due to the lower risk of GVHD). In Case 4, who had myelofibrosis with splenomegaly, standard-dose PTCy had initially been planned, but after discussion at the group meeting, the attending physician decided on a 40% dose reduction in PTCy (30 mg/kg/day × 2 days), considering the risks of both GF/PGF and GVHD. All but one patient underwent reduced-intensity conditioning with fludarabine plus melphalan-based conditioning (fludarabine: 160 mg/m2, melphalan:100–140 mg/m2); one patient received myeloablative regimen (fludarabine: 160 mg/m2 plus 8 Gy of total body irradiation). Neutrophil engraftment (absolute neutrophil count ≥ 500/µL) was achieved in all patients, including those with splenomegaly before HSCT. Platelet engraftment (platelet count ≥ 20,000/µL) was not achieved only in Case 1. Median time to engraftment was Day 14 (range: Day 12 to 27) for neutrophils, Day 17 (range: Day 16 to 37) for platelets (excluding one patient who did not achieve platelet engraftment), and Day 21 (range: Day 15 to 42) for lymphocytes (absolute lymphocyte count ≥ 200/µL). In our previous study on standard-dose PTCy (50 mg/kg/day on Day 3 and Day 4) involving 57 patients (39 with HLA-mismatched HSCT and 18 with HLA-matched HSCT), the median time to neutrophil, platelet, and lymphocyte engraftment was Day 18 (range: Day 13 to 40), Day 23 (range: Day 12 to 103), and Day 40 (range: Day 23 to 73), respectively [4]. Compared to the previous study cohort, the Mann-Whitney U test showed that patients who received intermediate-dose PTCy had a significantly shorter median time to engraftment for neutrophils (p < 0.01), platelet (p = 0.03), and lymphocytes (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1). Among the 2 patients with relatively delayed neutrophil engraftment, Case 1 (engrafted on Day 23) had a severe systemic fungal infection before HSCT, and Case 4 (engrafted on Day 27) had myelofibrosis with moderate splenomegaly prior to HSCT. Case 1 had disseminated fusariosis prior to HSCT and was receiving voriconazole (VRCZ). To avoid potential drug interactions during PTCy administration, VRCZ was switched to amphotericin B on Day − 7 and resumed on Day + 5 after completion of PTCy. Despite intensive antifungal therapy, including subsequent combination treatment and a switch to posaconazole, the patient developed severe pulmonary infection, which likely contributed to the delayed engraftment. Although we attempted to minimize drug interactions during PTCy administration, they cannot be completely ruled out as a contributing factor. The persistent highly inflammatory status due to severe GVHD might also have impaired platelet engraftment. Typical findings of transplant-associated microangiopathy were not observed. Chimerism analysis at Day 30 showed that complete donor chimerism (>95% donor chimerism) was achieved in all patients for total cells. T-cell chimerism was assessable in 8 of 11 cases (72.7%), all of whom achieved complete donor chimerism. For the remaining 3 patients, Case 1 achieved complete donor T-cell chimerism on Day 72, Case 4 on Day 259, and Case 5 on Day 80. Regarding chimerism analysis, in the previous study, complete donor chimerism at Day 30 was achieved in 23 of 57 patients (40.4%) for total cells and in 16 of 57 patients (28.1%) for T cells. Three out of 11 patients developed acute GVHD (Grade I, II, and III in one each); two of those 3 developed severe chronic GVHD. Case 1, who developed Grade II acute GVHD, subsequently experienced severe pulmonary dysfunction. Chest computed tomography showed diffuse ground-glass opacities, and infection was considered unlikely; therefore, we considered these findings to be a chronic GVHD-related pulmonary lesion. He also had adynamic ileus, acute subdural hematomas, persistent thrombocytopenia, HHV-6 viremia, and acute kidney injury, and he finally passed away on Day 100 due to diffuse alveolar hemorrhage. Case 2, who developed Grade III acute GVHD, was treated with methylprednisolone and then prednisone taper, and the patient is doing well off of steroids with no symptoms of chronic GVHD. Case 3 developed grade I acute GVHD on Day 23 and severe chronic GVHD on Day 139, which was treated with prednisone. Additionally, he had diffuse bilateral infiltrate, diagnosed as an IPS-related to chronic lung GVHD, and worsening skin rash. He was treated with high-dose steroids and had to be intubated but was eventually extubated and recovered his functional status with rehabilitation. The cumulative incidences of grade II–IV acute GVHD at day 100, chronic GVHD at 1 year, and the probability of GVHD-free/relapse-free survival at 1 year were 19.2% vs. 14.0% (p = 0.83), 28.9% vs. 28.9% (p = 0.85), and 59.7% vs. 52.6% (p = 0.95) in the current cohort and the prior cohort, respectively. Severe non-hematologic toxicities are summarized in Table 2. Excluding Case 1, Case 6 had Clostridioides difficile infection on Day 5, and Case 7 had prolonged QTc syndrome on Day 22 and rhinovirus/enterovirus infection on Day 49. In all patients, no disease relapse occurred after HSCT, and 10 of 11 patients are alive (median follow-up among survivors: Day 224).

This case series underscores the benefits of intermediate-dose PTCy, including reduced toxicity, shorter neutropenia, and faster immune recovery without increasing GVHD risk. As shown in Table 1, many studies have reported that the median time to neutrophil engraftment is around Day 14 when the PTCy dose is 25 mg/kg/day for 2 days. Our results are consistent with these results. In large-scale studies on standard-dose PTCy with PB and reduced-intensity conditioning, the median time to neutrophil engraftment was Day 16 in both haploidentical and HLA-matched transplant [2, 7]. Ustun et al. reported higher bacterial infection risk with PTCy versus conventional prophylaxis [8]. Therefore, this two-day difference in the time to neutrophil engraftment is significant, especially for elderly or frail patients at high infection risk. We previously reported that the use of PTCy in patients with pre-transplant splenomegaly increases the risk of GF/PGF [5]. In this study, 7 patients had splenomegaly, including 2 with moderate splenomegaly. Despite this, all cases achieved prompt engraftment. Few studies focus on spleen size and PTCy, and none have evaluated the efficacy of reduced-dose PTCy in patients with splenomegaly. We consider that intermediate-dose PTCy has the potential to reduce the risk of GF/PGF in patients with splenomegaly. Nevertheless, Hyder et al. reported GF in a patient with myelofibrosis who had massive splenomegaly despite the use of intermediate-dose PTCy [9]. Their study, however, included only bone marrow grafts, whereas all patients in our series received peripheral blood stem cell grafts. Given that our study included only a small number of cases and did not involve any patients with severe splenomegaly, who are at the highest risk, further studies with larger cohorts are warranted to clarify this issue. Furthermore, in the cases with intermediate-dose PTCy in this study, the time to lymphocyte recovery and the transition to complete donor chimerism occurred earlier compared to standard-dose PTCy, indicating a more rapid post-transplant immune reconstitution. It has been reported that PTCy had a higher risk of viral infections, such as cytomegalovirus and viral-related hemorrhagic cystitis [10]. In our cohort, viral infections were limited: HHV-6 viremia was observed in one severely ill patient (Case 1), and rhinovirus/enterovirus infection occurred in Case 7, whereas neither cytomegalovirus infection/viremia nor viral-related hemorrhagic cystitis was documented. Rapid lymphocyte recovery might be beneficial in combating these viral infections. A faster transition to complete donor chimerism, particularly T-cell chimerism, has been reported to contribute to prevention of disease recurrence [11]. From this perspective of disease control, intermediate-dose PTCy might be an effective approach.

The primary concern with reduced-dose PTCy is the increased risk of GVHD. In several previous reports, single-day PTCy regimens (25 or 50 mg/kg/day) have shown unacceptably high GVHD and non-relapse mortality [1, 12, 13]. On the other hand, reducing the daily dose and administering PTCy on both Day 3 and Day 4 has been shown to mitigate the risk of GVHD (Table 1). Although the number of cases is limited and the observation period is short, our cases also exhibited a similar incidence of GVHD compared to previous reports using reduced-dose PTCy administered for two days. Therefore, we consider this to be an acceptable approach. There is still a need to establish the optimal reduced-dose PTCy regimen that balances efficacy with toxicity. The long-term impact of reduced-dose regimens on GVHD-free, relapse-free survival, immune recovery, and quality of life also needs to be investigated. Randomized prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings to better characterize the adverse effect profile.
