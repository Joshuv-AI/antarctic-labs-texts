Screening for symptoms of depression, anxiety and associated factors among leprosy patients at referral hospitals in the Amhara region, Ethiopia
Authors: Mihret Melese, Amare Mebrat Delie, Liknaw Workie Limenh, Nigus Kassie Worku, Eneyew Talie Fenta, Mickiale Hailu, Alemwork Abie, Molla Getie Mehari, Tenagnework Eseyneh, Dereje Esubalew, Mohammed Abdu Seid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeprosy, a neglected tropical disease (NTD), is closely associated with stigma and discrimination, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, which significantly worsen mental health challenges. In Ethiopia’s Amhara region, leprosy accounts for 26.1% of cases, often leading to stigma, isolation, and increased depression and anxiety risk. This study aimed to assess depression and anxiety symptoms among leprosy patients in hospitals across the Amhara region, providing a basis for targeted mental health interventions and policies.Methods and materialsAn institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted in three referral hospitals in the Amhara region from December 14 to 20, 2023. The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) assessment tools were used to evaluate symptoms of depression and anxiety, respectively. Binary logistic regression was employed to identify factors associated with symptoms of depression and anxiety, using a significance level of p ≤ 0.05.ResultsAmong 383 participants, 36% reported depressive symptoms, and 32.6% experienced anxiety symptoms. Several factors were found to significantly contribute to mental health symptoms in individuals with leprosy. Symptoms of depression were significantly associated with being female (AOR = 1.20; 95% CI: 1.09–3.42), being aged above 50 years (AOR = 2.42; 95% CI: 1.23–3.22), having a chronic disease (AOR = 1.64; 95% CI: 1.07–3.66), having multibacillary leprosy (AOR = 1.40; 95% CI: 1.08–3.22), and being on multidrug therapy (AOR = 1.20; 95% CI: 1.12–3.22). Similarly, symptoms of anxiety were more likely among individuals who were female (AOR = 1.34; 95% CI: 1.14–2.67), had chronic diseases (AOR = 1.40; 95% CI: 1.05–2.70), smoked (AOR = 1.25; 95% CI: 1.02–2.40), had multibacillary leprosy (AOR = 1.40; 95% CI: 1.10–3.30), or had borderline lepromatous leprosy (AOR = 1.30; 95% CI: 1.05–2.10).Conclusion and recommendationsThis study identified factors influencing anxiety and depression in leprosy patients, including gender, age, disease classification, multidrug therapy, chronic diseases, and smoking habits. It is important to note that the study assesses symptoms, not diagnoses. The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) questionnaires are crucial for early detection, especially in high-risk groups such as females, older adults, and those with chronic or multibacillary leprosy. Routine screening and integrating mental health assessments into care, along with specialized services and awareness campaigns, will improve patient outcomes and reduce the psychosocial impact of leprosy.Keywords: Symptoms of depression, Anxiety symptoms, Leprosy, Referral hospitals, Associated factors, Ethiopia

Introduction

Leprosy, also known as Hansen’s disease, is a debilitating infectious condition induced by the bacterium Mycobacterium leprae [1]. It is a neglected tropical disease of public health importance [2, 3]. Leprosy spreads through respiratory droplets when untreated individuals cough or sneeze, with prolonged close contact posing a risk [4]. This bacterium primarily affects the skin and peripheral nerves, leading to various clinical manifestations [5]. Nerve damage can occur through direct effect of M. Leprae on the nerve or indirectly through immunological reaction causing severe nerve inflammation, and subsequently disability [2].

Leprosy patients experience significantly higher rates of depression and anxiety disorders compared to the general population, with prevalence estimates ranging from 21 to 65% across low-income settings [6, 7]. This mental health burden stems from intersecting factors including disease-related stigma, physical disability, social exclusion, and economic marginalization [8].

In Ethiopia’s Amhara region, where leprosy is still common and mental health services are scarce, understanding psychiatric disorders is essential for creating effective interventions [9]. A study from 2008 to 2012 across 93 regions found leprosy incidence over 1 case per 10,000 people, accounting for 54% of national cases. The highest rates were in Oromia (90%), Benishangul Gumuz (80%), and Amhara (60%) [10].

Symptoms of anxiety and depression in leprosy patients have serious consequences, impacting both their health and financial well-being. These conditions worsen disability, weaken the immune system, and reduce quality of life and work ability, leading to income loss and socioeconomic difficulties [11].

Symptoms depression and anxiety in people affected by leprosy can be influenced by various factors, such as socioeconomic status, sex, age, education level, employment status, marital status, ethnicity, leprosy classification, treatment discontinuation, corticosteroid use, neuritis, and the presence of other health conditions [12–14].

Leprosy patients in Ethiopia’s Amhara region often face significant stigma, leading to social exclusion and limited access to healthcare, employment, and education. This marginalization increases their risk of developing depression and anxiety [15–17]. Despite this, there is a lack of data on the mental health status of leprosy patients in the region. This study aims to assess the prevalence of depression and anxiety symptoms among leprosy patients in Amhara referral hospitals and identify associated factors. The findings will serve as a foundation for developing targeted mental health interventions and policies to address the psychological impact of leprosy.

Methods and materials

Study design and setting

An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted in Comprehensive Specialized and General Hospitals within the Amhara Regional State from September 14 to December 20, 2023. The region is served by eight major hospitals designated at various levels, including Comprehensive Specialized Hospitals such as the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Tibebe Ghion Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, and Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, along with General Hospitals like those in Dessie, Woldia, Debre Markos, Debre Tabor, and Debre Birhan. Among these, Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Dessie General Hospital, and Debre Markos General Hospital were randomly selected for the study.

In the Amhara region, a total of 1,409 leprosy cases have been reported, with patients receiving follow-up care across these healthcare facilities. This systematic follow-up ensures that individuals diagnosed with leprosy continue to access consistent medical care and treatment.

Source and study population

The population source included all adult individuals known to be diagnosed with leprosy and receiving healthcare services in referral hospitals within the Amhara region. The study population consisted of all adults with leprosy having follow-up to the selected hospitals during the data collection period.

The eligibility criteria

The study included all adults aged 18 years and older with a confirmed diagnosis of leprosy. Individuals were excluded if they had severe illnesses.

Sampling techniques

In this study, a total of 383 study participants were included by the quota sampling technique and study populations were divided in to strata (age, sex and treatment centre). All leprosy patients attending follow-up clinics during the study period who met the eligibility criteria were invited to participate.

Study variables

Dependent variable

Symptoms of depression and anxiety were assessed using the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 questionnaires, respectively. The PHQ-9 is a 9-item tool where each item is scored from 0 to 3 (0 = Not at all, 1 = several days, 2 = More than half the days, 3 = nearly every day).

The total score is calculated by summing the responses to all 9 items, resulting in a score range from 0 to 27. Scores from 0 to 4 indicate no symptoms of depression, while scores greater than 5 indicate the presence of depression symptoms. Anxiety was measured using the GAD-7, a 7-item questionnaire with a total score ranging from 0 to 21. Scores are categorized as follows: 0–4 indicates no anxiety symptoms, and scores greater than 5 indicate the presence of anxiety symptoms [18, 19].

Independent variables

The study considered sociodemographic factors (including age, sex, occupation, educational attainment, income, place of residence, and religion), behavioural characteristics (including alcohol consumption, cigarette smoking, and Khat chewing), and clinical factors (such as leprosy classification, treatment dropout, corticosteroid medication, neuritis, and the presence of comorbidities).

Operational definitions

Severe illnesses

Severe illnesses were defined as medical or psychological conditions that significantly impair an individual’s ability to participate in a study. These conditions may include being bedridden, experiencing acute medical emergencies, enduring severe pain or discomfort, having active psychosis, or suffering from cognitive impairments that hinder effective communication or voluntary participation. Such conditions are typically considered exclusion criteria in clinical research to ensure participant safety and the integrity of study outcomes [20].

Data collection tools and procedures

Three BSc nurses, under the supervision of the primary investigators, collected clinical, behavioral, and sociodemographic data using interviewer administered questionnaire from September 14 to December 20, 2023. The questionnaire included standardized and validated assessment tools for anxiety and depression symptoms, specifically the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7) scale. Originally in English, the questionnaire was translated into Amharic for the participants’ convenience and then back-translated into English by fluent speakers to ensure accuracy. To enhance the comprehensiveness of the study, the participants included both newly diagnosed and previously diagnosed cases of leprosy, as determined by a physician assigned to the adult outpatient department (OPD) and integrated into the chronic disease unit for follow-up.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were coded and entered into Epidata version 4.6, then analyzed using SPSS version 25. A normality test (Shapiro–Wilk test) was conducted to assess the distribution of the continuous data (age p = 0.12), which was found to be normally distributed. Summary statistics, including mean ± standard deviation (SD) and proportions and frequencies, were used to summarize the results, which were presented in tables and graphs. Bivariable and multivariable logistic regression models were employed to identify factors significantly associated with the outcome variable. In the bivariable logistic regression, variables with a p-value ≤ 0.25 were considered candidates for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression. The model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test for both depression (p = 0.657) and anxiety outcomes (p = 0.587). The reliability of the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 tools for assessing depression and anxiety symptoms was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, with values above 0.77 indicating good reliability.

For validity, we have included model fit indices to evaluate the measurement model. A CFI value of 0.95 or higher indicates a good fit and suggests the tool reliably captures the intended constructs. An RMSEA value below 0.08 reflects an acceptable level of model misfit, with lower values indicating better alignment with the data. Similarly, an SRMR value below 0.06 suggests minimal discrepancies between the observed and predicted correlations. Meeting these criteria supports the tool’s construct validity, affirming its accuracy in measuring depression and anxiety.

Sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants

In this study, a total of 383 individuals affected by leprosy participated, resulting in a 100% response rate. The mean age of the participants was 31 (± 1.17) years. The majority of the participants were male. Nearly half of the participants were married, and almost half identified as members of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church. Level of depression and anxiety

Of the 383 participants in this study, 138 individuals (36%) reported experiencing symptoms of depression (95% CI: 32.34–39.76%). Meanwhile, the level of anxiety symptoms during the same period was 32.6% (95% CI: 28.5–36.33%).

Factors associated with symptoms of depression among leprosy patients

Factors including income, presence of chronic disease, cigarette smoking status, leprosy classification, multidrug therapy usage, and clinical forms of leprosy were considered for multivariable logistic regression analysis (p ≤ 0.25). The multivariable analysis revealed that sex, age, leprosy classification, multidrug therapy, and clinical forms of leprosy were significant predictors of depressive symptoms. Specifically, female leprosy patients were found to be more likely to have depressive symptoms than their male counterparts (AOR = 1.20; 95% CI: 1.09–3.42). Leprosy patients aged over 50 years were more likely to develop symptoms compared to those aged 15–25, with an adjusted odds ratio (AOR) of 2.42 (95% CI: 1.23–3.22). Furthermore, leprosy patients with chronic illnesses had 1.64 times greater odds of experiencing symptoms of depression compared to those without chronic conditions (AOR = 1.64; 95% CI: 1.07–3.66). Multibacillary leprosy patients were more likely to experience depressive symptoms compared to those with paucibacillary leprosy (AOR = 1.40: 95% CI: 1.08–3.22. Finally, the odds of developing depression symptoms were 1.20 times higher for patients receiving multidrug therapy compared to those on intermediate leprosy therapy (AOR = 1.20; 95% CI: 1.12–3.22) Table 3.

Factors associated with symptom of anxiety among leprosy patients

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, several factors including income, presence of chronic disease, cigarette smoking status, leprosy classification, multidrug therapy usage, and clinical forms of leprosy were initially considered for multivariable analysis at p-value of (≤ 0.25).

The final multivariable logistic regression analysis identified sex, age, leprosy classification, multidrug therapy, and clinical forms of leprosy as significant predictors of depressive symptoms (p < 0.05). Female participants were 1.34 times more likely to experience anxiety than males (AOR = 1.34; 95% CI: 1.09–3.42). Patients with chronic diseases had 1.40 times higher odds of anxiety symptoms (AOR = 1.40; 95% CI: 1.12–1.75), while current smokers showed 1.25 times greater likelihood (AOR = 1.25; 95% CI: 1.23–4.33). Clinically, multibacillary leprosy patients demonstrated 1.40 times higher anxiety risk compared to paucibacillary cases (AOR = 1.40; 95%CI: 1.08–3.22), with borderline lepromatous patients specifically showing 1.30 times greater odds (AOR = 1.30; 95% CI: 1.12–3.22). Patients receiving MDT for multibacillary leprosy were particularly vulnerable to anxiety symptoms compared to those on paucibacillary regimens. Discussion

Leprosy is a Neglected Tropical Diseases (NTDs) known to cause stigma and discrimination in Ethiopia.

The level depression and anxiety symptoms observed in this study were 36% and 32.6%, respectively. These findings are consistent with a study from India, which reported depression symptoms in 33% of participants. The observed prevalence of depressive symptoms among individuals affected by leprosy in this study was notably higher than that reported in the general Ethiopian population, where the national prevalence of depression is estimated to be around 9.1% (WHO, 2017). This disparity underscores the heightened mental health burden experienced by people living with leprosy [21]. Additionally, the prevalence found in this study exceeds that reported in the general Indian population, where depression symptoms range from about 4.4–4.7%, surpassing both global and national estimates [22, 23]. However, the level of depression symptoms in this study is lower than the results reported by the WHO in certain regions, such as Southeast Asia, where depression among individuals with leprosy reaches 59.2% [24], and in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, where it is reported at 52.4% [25]. The discrepancy can be attributed to variations in public health policies, mental health resources, and awareness levels. Regions with robust mental health policies often report lower depression symptoms due to better detection and support systems. Socioeconomic disparities also play a role, as low-income areas face stressors like poverty and instability, which increase depression risk, while affluent regions offer more coping resources. Cultural factors, such as stigma, can lead to underreporting, and exposure to trauma from conflict or disasters can elevate depression rates. These factors highlight the complex interplay of social, economic, and environmental influences on global depression prevalence [26].

The current study found the level of anxiety symptoms to be 32.6% (95% CI: 28.45–36.33), which is closely aligned with a previous study from India that reported a level of 29% [22]. However, this rate is higher than that of another study from India, which found a level of 21% [30], but lower than the findings from a study conducted in Bangladesh, where the level of anxiety symptoms reached 37% [27].

The level of anxiety symptoms varies across countries due to a complex interplay of cultural attitudes, socioeconomic conditions, environmental stressors, genetic predispositions, healthcare access, coping mechanisms, work pressures, and media influence. Cultural stigma, economic inequality, exposure to environmental stress, and access to social support all contribute to these differences. Additionally, work and academic pressures, along with media portrayals, shape how anxiety is experienced and perceived. Understanding these diverse factors is essential for effectively addressing anxiety on a global scale [28, 29].

In this study, symptoms of depression was positively associated with various factors, including sex, age, leprosy disease classification, and the use of multidrug therapy for both multibacillary and paucibacillary leprosy. Additionally, depression was linked to specific clinical forms of leprosy and the occurrence of type 1 and type 2 reactions.

Female patients had a higher level of depression symptoms compared to male patients. This finding aligns with studies conducted in Nigeria [30], Nepal [31], India [22], Bangladesh [32], Brazil [33] and the USA [34]. This disparity can be attributed to hormonal fluctuations during puberty, menstruation, pregnancy, and menopause. Additionally, biological differences in brain chemistry may increase women’s susceptibility to depression. Unique psychosocial stressors, such as societal expectations and caregiving responsibilities, further contribute to this vulnerability. Understanding these factors is essential for creating effective interventions that address the specific mental health needs of women. This suggests that fluctuations in female hormones may act as triggers for the development of depressive symptoms [35].

Patients aged 50 years and older were more likely to develop symptoms of depression compared to those aged 18–25 years. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Nigeria [30], China [36], the USA [34] and Brazil [33].

One possible explanation for this trend is that older adults experience age-related changes in endocrine, inflammatory, cardiovascular, and neuroanatomical systems, which may lead to the development of various comorbidities and accelerate the clinical manifestations of diseases [37].

The prevalence of depression symptoms was higher among patients with multibacillary leprosy compared to those with paucibacillary leprosy, aligning with findings from a study conducted in India [22]. There are two primary reasons why people with multibacillary leprosy are more likely to experience depression. First of all, sensory and motor impairments resulting from multibacillary leprosy’s involvement of peripheral nerves have a direct impact on mental health and aid in the emergence of depressed symptoms. Second, the chronic nature of multibacillary leprosy, which is characterized by the potential for complications and relapses, results in a chronic state of tension and anxiety, which makes people more susceptible to depression [22, 38, 39]. Furthermore, multibacillary leprosy often results in visible skin lesions, nodules, and physical deformities. The stigma associated with these visible changes can intensify feelings of low self-esteem and social isolation, thereby increasing the risk of mental health issues such as anxiety and depression symptoms [26].

This study identified significant associations between symptoms of anxiety and several factors, including sex, the presence of chronic diseases, smoking status, leprosy classification, the use of multidrug therapy, clinical forms of leprosy, and episodes of Type 1 and Type 2 reactions. Notably, participants experiencing Type II leprosy reactions exhibited a higher prevalence of anxiety compared to those with Type I reactions. This finding is consistent with research conducted in the United Kingdom [6].

One possible explanation for the higher prevalence of anxiety symptoms in individuals experiencing Type II leprosy reactions is the severe and systemic nature of these reactions, which can lead to significant distress and psychological impact. Symptoms associated with Type II reactions, including fever, painful skin nodules, joint pain, and eye inflammation, can exacerbate anxiety levels due to their intensity and discomfort. Additionally, the chronic and unpredictable nature of Type II reactions may create heightened concern and anxiety about the progression of the disease and its potential complications. The systemic inflammation and physical discomfort experienced during these reactions can also disrupt sleep patterns and overall well-being, further intensifying anxiety symptoms [40–43].

Individuals affected by leprosy who also have concurrent chronic illnesses exhibited a higher propensity for anxiety symptoms compared to those with leprosy alone, as evidenced by research conducted in Nigeria and a study by PMW Somar et al. [11, 44]. Those managing chronic illnesses face numerous challenges, including financial strain, social isolation, loneliness, physical disabilities, persistent fatigue, and pain. These factors can lead to feelings of distress, anger, hopelessness, and frustration, significantly increasing the risk of developing depressive and anxious symptoms [45, 46].

Limitations of the study

This study has two limitations. The sample size was determined solely based on the proportion of depressive symptoms from a previous study, without calculating the required sample size for assessing anxiety separately, which may have reduced the power to detect significant associations related to anxiety. Future studies should calculate sample sizes for each key outcome to ensure adequate statistical power. Additionally, the use of non-probability sampling and a relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study identified key factors influencing anxiety and depression symptoms in leprosy patients, including sex, age, leprosy classification, multidrug therapy use, clinical forms, chronic diseases, and smoking. It focused on assessing symptoms rather than diagnosing disorders. The PHQ-9 and GAD-7 questionnaires are vital for early identification of patients at risk for depression, anxiety, and related complications like suicide and leprosy reactions. Routine screening, especially for high-risk groups, will enable timely intervention. The study advocates for specialized mental health services for leprosy patients and emphasizes the integration of mental health assessments into routine care to improve patient outcomes and reduce the psychosocial impact of the disease.
