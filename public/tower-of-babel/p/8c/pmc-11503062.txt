Role of beta-hydroxybutyrate measurement in the evaluation of plasma glucose concentrations in newborn infants

Charles Stanley and colleagues provide further information about glucose and ketone metabolism during neonatal transition using data from the Glucose in Well babies (GLOW) study. They aimed to characterise further the duration of the initial transitional phase of hypoglycaemia, during which ketone levels are low, likely explained by relative hyperinsulinism due to persistence of the low fetal glucose threshold for insulin production, and the later phase, when ketone production has become established during the relative fasting while milk supply is becoming established. The later elevated ketone concentrations may help compensate for the low glucose concentrations by providing an alternative fuel for brain metabolism so that low glucose concentrations may represent a greater threat to the brain in the first phase when ketones are absent. Regular measurements of glucose, and beta-hydroxybutyrate (BOHB) were analysed from the first 5 days after birth in 66 healthy newborns. The total insulin-dependent fuel available for the brain was considered to be the sum of the concentrations of glucose and BOHB and a threshold total of 4 mmol/L was used to define the threshold between the hyperinsulinaemic state and non hyperinsulinaemic state. The median duration of the initial hyperinsulinaemic phase when ketones were not available was 40 hours, and was>60 hours in 15%. Most of the infants subsequently developed ketonaemia. The authors argue that point of care analysers for BOHB are available and sufficiently accurate to consider using them as a further adjunct to the interpretation of neonatal blood sugar measurements and indications for further investigation.

Kathryn Beardsall and Ajay Thankamony discuss the paper. The low ketones in the early phase challenge traditional teaching that healthy term infants are protected from hypoglycaemia by the availability of ketones as alternative fuels. Although ketones were low, lactate was high, suggesting that lactate could be a critical cerebral fuel in the first few hours after birth when glycogen stores are being used. The study included healthy babies, and not those generally considered to be at risk from hypoglycaemic injury. Further studies including babies with a range of risk factors and using continuous glucose monitoring as well as point of care measurement of other metabolites will help to further characterise the circumstances of greatest metabolic risk. Respiratory outcomes and survival after unplanned extubation

Helene Yager and colleagues studied unplanned extubation events in 12 NICUs in France over a 20 month period. Among 2280 intubated patients there were 162 episodes of unplanned extubation in 139 (6.1%) patients. Re-intubation occurred within 24 hours in 50% of unplanned extubations and 12.3% of planned extubations. Severe bradycardia was reported in 32 cases and CPR in seven but no patient died of a UE complication. Mortality at discharge did not differ between those with planned and unplanned extubations. While unplanned extubation may be a serious and potentially life threatening event, the substantial proportion of infants who do not require re-intubation after an unplanned extubation events highlights the limitations of assessment of readiness for extubation and the need to examine more critically the balance of risks and benefits of continued intubated respiratory support. Factors that impact second attempt success for neonatal intubation following first attempt failure

Mitchell Johnson and colleagues in the National Emergency Airway Registry for Neonates (NEAR4NEOS) studied the factors associated with second attempt intubation success following a failed first attempt at neonatal tracheal intubation. Data from 18 NICUs in the USA, Canada, Europe and Australia were included. Among 13 126 tracheal intubations performed during the study period, 5805 (44%) required at least two attempts and had complete data. In the majority of second attempts (3855, 66%), neither the intubator nor the equipment was changed between the first and second attempt. However, on multivariate modelling, change in intubator (OR 1.80, 95% CI 1.56 to 2.07), change in stylet use (OR 1.65, 95% CI 1.36 to 2.01) and change in ETT size (OR 2.11, 95% CI 1.74 to 2.56) were associated with second attempt success, suggesting that second attempt success might be improved by making changes, rather than deferring changes until multiple failed attempts. Early and extended erythropoietin monotherapy after hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy

Therapeutic hypothermia treatment is not universally available for infants with moderate to severe hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy (HIE). Reema Garegrat and colleagues examined the feasibility of conducting a placebo controlled trial of extended erythropoietin monotherapy for moderate to severe HIE in a setting in South Asia where therapeutic hypothermia was not in use. They were able to recruit 86% of eligible infants and to complete the protocol satisfactorily in most cases, including performing MRI scanning in 33/34 infants who survived to discharge. They conclude that the Safety and efficacy of early and extended erythropoietin monotherapy should be explored in further clinical trials adequately powered to examine neurodevelopmental outcomes at 18 months or more. Abandoning ‘kitchen sink’ regression

Stefan Kuhle and colleagues discuss what they call ‘Kitchen sink regression’, a term to describe a multivariable regression procedure where all available variables (‘the kitchen sink’) that may or may not be associated with an outcome of interest are entered into a model, followed by some form of automated or manual variable selection strategy based on p values or a model-based information criterion. The variables remaining in the model are then interpreted as ‘independent risk factors’ for the outcome of interest. They use examples from perinatal care to highlight some of the pitfalls of this approach and advocate for the use of Directed Acyclic Graphs to guide variable selection for models that aim to examine associations between a putative risk factor and an outcome. They emphasise very strongly the importance of remembering that the estimates from a prediction model have no meaningful causal interpretation. The complexity of the analytical challenge highlights the importance of obtaining statistical input in planning studies that use anything more than the simplest analytical methods and why the journal benefits from a team of statistical editors to contribute to our peer review process.
