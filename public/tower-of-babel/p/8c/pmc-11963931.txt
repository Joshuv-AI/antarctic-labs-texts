Rationale and Design of the Australasian Registry of Screening ECGs in National Athletes Project
Authors: Jessica J. Orchard, Andre La Gerche, Rajesh Puranik, Hariharan Raju, Angus J. Davis, Simon Eggleton, Tim Driscoll, Michelle Lorimer, Robert N. Doughty, Bruce Hamilton, Jonathan A. Drezner, John W. Orchard
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiac screening of elite athletes is widely recommended by Australasian sporting federations, but data are not structured to be shared. Data are lacking from underrepresented groups to inform ECG interpretation guidelines. The ARENA (Australasian Registry of Screening ECGs in National Athletes) project is a retrospective and prospective, multicenter, longitudinal, observational registry of athlete cardiac screening results and outcomes. The aim is to create a repository to improve our understanding of the diagnoses and outcomes of screening.MethodsParticipating sports that conduct cardiac screening of athletes will contribute data. This includes an initial collection (retrospective data, waiver of consent) and future prospective data (opt‐out consent). Data include sex, age, sport/event, screening date, ECG findings, cardiac test results, follow‐up details, sport participation status, cardiac diagnoses, and major cardiovascular outcomes defined as sudden cardiac arrest/death, cardiac syncope or implanted cardioverter defibrillator shock, cardiac hospitalization, and arrhythmias requiring intervention. Comparisons will be made between diagnoses, outcomes, and ECG features and analyzed by sport and sex. The ARENA project was developed in collaboration with sporting bodies, team physicians, and players association representatives and endorsed by the Australasian College of Sport & Exercise Physicians and Sports Medicine Australia.ConclusionsThe ARENA project will provide a long‐term international data repository to improve our understanding of ECG interpretation, cardiac screening and diagnoses, and the prevalence of cardiovascular outcomes in screened athletes. A unique aim is to address evidence gaps in underrepresented athlete groups, specifically female athletes and Indigenous populations. Results will inform screening policies and guidelines.

Sudden cardiac arrest or death (SCA/D) in athletes is an infrequent but tragic event. SCD is the leading cause of death for people playing sports, with athletes having a 2.5 times higher risk than the general population. 1 , 2 Therefore, cardiac screening of athletes for conditions associated with SCA/D is recommended by bodies such as the European Society of Cardiology 3 and the Australasian College of Sport and Exercise Physicians. 4 Screening generally includes a personal/family history, physical examination, resting 12‐lead ECG, and, for some sports, an echocardiogram. Many sports screen athletes every 2 to 5 years, depending on age or international competition requirements (eg, Olympic Games).

Although most Australian and New Zealand (Australasian) sporting bodies recommend that athletes be screened, 5 data are not structured to be shared, making quality improvement and comparisons across different sports difficult. Limited evidence suggests that Australasian elite sports 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 appear to have a lower SCA/D rate than other regions (eg, North America, 10 Europe 11 ). Long‐term data on cardiac outcomes and diagnoses in screened athletes in Australasia are needed.

Ethnicity, sex, and sport play a role in cardiovascular adaptations to exercise training. 12 , 13 Over the past 15 years, athlete ECG interpretation criteria have been refined as more data have become available. These changes have significantly improved diagnostic yield and reduced the false positive rate, especially in White athletes. 14 However, current guidelines (the International Criteria 15 ) are based on available data, predominantly from male White and Black athletes. Other races and female athletes are underrepresented in these data sets, often due to the screening policies and demographics of the sporting organizations involved. For example, one of the largest published data sets from the UK Football Association with 20 years of cardiac screening data from >11 000 athletes was composed of 95% male athletes and 91% White athletes. 11 , 14 It is acknowledged that race is a social, not biological, construct 16 and that disparities linked to social determinants of health play an important role in cardiovascular outcomes for athletes. 17 , 18 It is likely that further improvements in accuracy are possible with additional data, 19 including from Australasian sports.

There are currently no Australian or New Zealand registries of athlete screening ECGs. Further, there are very few such registries that exist internationally, and generally not at the elite/professional level. The ARENA (Australasian Registry of Screening ECGs in National Athletes) project aims to improve our understanding of the clinical outcomes of screening and the boundaries between normal and abnormal ECG features in athletes across different sexes, ethnicities, ages, and sports.

Methods

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. The project is a retrospective and prospective, multicenter, longitudinal, observational registry of athlete cardiac screening results and outcomes, including major cardiovascular events, for elite athletes in Australia and New Zealand. All sporting organizations that conduct cardiac screening of athletes including ECG are eligible to participate.

The registry has ethical approval from the University of Sydney Human Research Ethics Committee (Project No. 2023/551). Deidentified data from the registry will be available to other national and international researchers on request. This requires an application to the steering committee including details of the proposed study, funding, ethics approval and, if approved, compliance with secure data storage procedures and authorship and publication requirements.

Project Aims

The primary aim is to establish an ECG screening repository to determine the prevalence of conditions associated with SCA/D in screened athletes.

Secondary aims include to: monitor cardiac screening results across sports;assess the accuracy of ECG interpretation across different groups (eg, athletes of various ethnicities, ages, and sexes);assess the influence of sex, sport, and ethnicity on ECG features in athletes;assess the bounds of normal cardiac adaptations to exercise expressed on an ECG across athletes from different Australasian sports to provide relevant benchmarks for normal athlete ECG features and accurate interpretation;document major cardiovascular outcomes in screened athletes;contribute to ongoing quality improvement in the implementation of athlete screening programs via regular reports to participating sporting organizations; andcreate a long‐term resource for national and international research.

Consent Process

Data are collected after written informed consent has been obtained from the participating sporting organization. Data to be collected include an initial collection of completed screenings (retrospective data) and future screenings (prospective data) (Figure). Retrospective data will be collected under a waiver of consent and prospective data will be collected using an opt‐out consent process. Under the opt‐out process, athletes are read a verbal script during screening informing them of the registry, how to obtain further information, and their ability to opt‐out at any time if desired.

Procedures and Data Collection

Athletes will be screened in accordance with their sport's policy. Screening data will be collected centrally in electronic form for all athletes from each sporting organization. This includes an initial collection of retrospective data and ongoing collection of prospective data. Prospective data will be collected annually (or at a frequency that suits the organization) and at least 6 months after screening has been completed to allow time for any required follow‐up to be completed.

Data include sex, ethnicity (self‐report), age, sport/event, date and result of each screening, ECG findings, additional cardiac test results, details of follow‐up, sport participation status, cardiac diagnoses (categorized as “conditions associated with SCD” and “other cardiac diagnoses” 11 ), treatments (including medication, ablation, implantation of an implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator, other cardiac surgery or procedure) and major cardiovascular outcomes 20 , 21 , 22 (Table).

Outcomes and diagnoses will be monitored on an ongoing basis for athletes until retirement, and each participating sporting body will be contacted biannually to query for major cardiovascular outcomes in their athletes. For athletes who have retired or are no longer associated with an elite Australasian sporting organization, SCA/D will be monitored until the age of 50 years. Regular internet (Google) and media (Factiva) searches will be conducted for any reports of Australasian athletes suffering SCA/D, cardiac hospitalization, or other relevant cardiac issues, an approach used in previous studies. 6 , 7 , 9 , 23 Any outcomes identified through these searches will be cross‐checked with the chief medical officer of the relevant sporting body.

All ECGs were reviewed and followed up at the time by physicians conducting the screening program. For the registry, ECGs will be rereviewed in accordance with the most recent athlete ECG interpretation criteria (currently the International Criteria 15 ). Definitions of ECG features, including normal, borderline, and abnormal features, will be in accordance with the International Criteria. The main abnormal ECG findings in athletes during screening include abnormal T‐wave inversion or ST‐segment depression (potentially associated with cardiomyopathies, myocarditis), pathological Q waves (potentially associated with cardiomyopathies, myocarditis, or prior myocardial infarction), prolonged QTc (potentially associated with long QT syndrome), ventricular preexcitation (potentially associated with Wolff‐Parkinson‐White syndrome), and multiple premature ventricular contractions (potentially associated with cardiomyopathies, myocarditis, sarcoidosis).

All abnormal ECGs, unclear/borderline features, and all cases with a cardiac diagnosis will be reviewed by the registry's expert clinical advisors. In the event of a disagreement, the majority view will be used. In the rare event that a previously unrecognized ECG abnormality is identified by the registry team, the chief medical officer of the sporting organization will be notified by email and will then contact the athlete and determine the appropriate follow‐up. Within 2 weeks of the first email, an additional reminder will be sent to confirm that they have received the notification.

Data Storage and Security

Registry data will be securely stored at the South Australian Health and Medical Research Institute. The institute has highly secure data protection systems with appropriate authentication and access controls to limit access to authorized staff and has secure procedures, facilities, devices, servers, and backup systems to ensure data are protected. Further, the South Australian Health and Medical Research Institute will comply with the relevant Commonwealth, State, and Territory legislation and regulations. Specifically, the institute will comply with the Privacy Act (1988), the Privacy Amendment (Enhancing Privacy Protection) Act 2012, the Privacy Regulation 2013, and the 13 Australian Privacy Principles.

Once collected, data will be deidentified, coded, entered, and stored in a Research Electronic Data Capture database hosted at the South Australian Health and Medical Research Institute. A deidentified data set will be developed and will be used for analysis and research purposes. All analyses will be conducted only on deidentified data and athletes will not be identifiable in any research results or publications.

Governance

The registry is supported by a steering committee, working group, and a multidisciplinary investigator team including clinical advisors with expertise in sports cardiology, sports medicine, genetic cardiology, cardiac imaging, and epidemiology. Terms of reference for the steering committee and working group are based on established procedures and are approved by members.

Outcomes

The primary outcome is the prevalence of screened athletes who are diagnosed with a condition associated with SCA/D.

Secondary outcomes include: the frequency of various athlete ECG features, including comparisons by sex, sport, and ethnicity;the proportion of screenings that required clinical follow‐up;the proportion of abnormal ECGs and echocardiograms;the proportion of athletes who received treatment for a cardiac condition;the proportion of athletes who retired from sport due to a cardiac reason; andthe proportion of screened athletes who experience SCA/D or any other major cardiovascular outcome including cardiac syncope or an implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator shock, cardiac hospitalization, or cardiac arrhythmias requiring intervention.

Statistical Analysis

Demographic data will be presented as counts and percentages for categorical data and means±SD for continuous data.

Categorical outcomes will be modeled using either logistic regression or generalized linear models assuming a binomial distribution with log link for dichotomous outcomes and ordinal logistic regression for ordered categorical outcomes. Continuous outcomes will be modeled using linear regression with robust SE estimates. Differences between sports, sexes, and ethnic groups will be considered. All tests will be 2 tailed at the 5% level of significance.

Patient and Public Involvement

The protocol was developed in collaboration with sporting bodies, team physicians, players association representatives and endorsed by the Australasian College of Sport & Exercise Physicians and Sports Medicine Australia. The Australasian College of Sport & Exercise Physicians Position Statement on Pre‐Participation Cardiac Evaluation in Young Athletes (2024) specifically encourages all sporting organizations with screening programs to pool data and contribute to the ARENA project.

Discussion

ARENA is a retrospective and prospective, multicenter, longitudinal, observational registry of athlete cardiac screening results and outcomes. The registry will provide a long‐term international data repository to improve our understanding of ECG interpretation, cardiac screening, rates of diagnoses of conditions associated with SCA/D, and major cardiovascular outcomes in screened athletes. A unique aim of this registry is to address important evidence gaps in underrepresented athlete groups, specifically female athletes and Indigenous populations. This is the first Australasian registry of elite athlete cardiac screening data. Recruitment commenced in November 2023 and is ongoing. Results from studies using registry data will be disseminated through peer‐reviewed publications, conference presentations, and the registry website.

Future Directions

The ARENA project has initially commenced in Australia with the intention to roll out to New Zealand as soon as practical. The inclusion of New Zealand will create a unique Australasian resource.

It is intended that registry data will be available to other researchers in accordance with the Data Access Policy. It is also intended that ARENA data be linked to other databases, such as the EndUCD (End Unexplained Cardiac Death) 24 registry and the ORCCA (Outcomes Registry for Cardiac Conditions in Athletes, 21 which is a US registry with plans for international expansion to Australia. This will foster future research and international collaborations.

An application for statutory protection (known as “qualified privilege”) of the registry as a declared quality assurance activity under Part CV of the Health Insurance Act 1973 (Cth) has been submitted. This legislation grants protection to participants and organizations contributing data to approved registries by prohibiting the release of any information that might identify an individual, patient, or health care professional and protects those taking part in registry activities from legal liability. 25 The aim of the scheme is to encourage health care professionals to contribute data to quality improvement schemes. An application to a similar scheme in New Zealand to become a Protected Quality Assurance Activity under the Health Practitioners Competence Assurance Act 2003 will also be submitted. 26 An application has been submitted for listing on the Australian Register of Clinical Registries.

Limitations

It is possible that we will not be able to capture data on all diagnoses and outcomes, especially those that occur once an athlete has left their sporting organization and that are not reported in the media. Therefore, it is possible that the outcomes will underreport clinically important diagnoses or outcomes. However, we anticipate our methods will reduce this likelihood as much as possible.

Although the cohort is well defined and likely to involve large numbers, we will be unable to report a denominator of athletes eligible for screening and therefore may not be able to report screening compliance or the number of athletes opting out of screening. This is largely because some sporting organizations may not have these records available, especially for retrospective data. This means we may not be absolutely confident in the proportion of athletes captured by the registry. However, we will request this information and include it if available. For prospective data, we will encourage participating sporting organizations to keep and provide records of the total number of eligible athletes as it would be useful to be able to present this information.

Conclusions

There is an important need for data on cardiovascular outcomes of screened athletes. The ARENA project will provide a long‐term international data repository to improve our understanding of ECG interpretation, cardiac screening and diagnoses, and cardiovascular outcomes in screened athletes. A unique aim of this registry is to address important evidence gaps in underrepresented athlete groups, specifically female athletes, and Indigenous populations. We anticipate results will contribute to ongoing quality improvement in athlete screening programs and inform screening policies, ECG interpretation, and management guidelines.
