Traumatic Inflammatory—Coagulation Coactivation Occurs Early After Injury and Impacts Mortality
Authors: Julia Riccardi, Anamaria J. Robles, James T. Ross, Carrie Lewis, Alyssa R. Bellini, Matthew Mell, John B. Holcomb, Rachael A. Callcut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVES:Acute traumatic coagulopathy is known to occur early following severe injury. However, the role and impact of both pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines are unknown. We aimed to investigate the inflammatory (pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines) and coagulation pathways following injury and their relation to 30-day mortality.DESIGN:Patients included in this analysis had complete data available for a panel of 48 coagulation and inflammatory biomarkers obtained from initial emergency department blood draws. Principal component analysis (PCA), a dimensionality-reduction machine learning method, was used to identify biomarker phenotypes present. Principal components with variance (eigenvalues) greater than 1 were selected for inclusion in the analysis. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator regression was performed to identify independent predictors of 30-day mortality. The critical administration threshold (CAT, > 3 units of RBCs in 1 hr) was used to quantify bleeding.SETTING:This is a secondary analysis of the Pragmatic Randomized Optimal Platelet and Plasma Ratio (PROPPR) study.PATIENTS:The PROPPR study was a pragmatic, phase 3, multisite, randomized clinical trial that included 680 severely injured patients at 12 level 1 trauma centers.INTERVENTIONS:None.MEASUREMENTS AND MAIN RESULTS:Inflammatory and coagulation marker phenotypes were associated with 30-day mortality. Among 286 patients included, 30-day mortality was 24.4% (n = 70). PCA found 14 phenotypes that collectively explained 83% of pathophysiologic variability. The top six principal components contributing to the early pathophysiology changes were a coagulopathy phenotype, followed by two distinct mixed inflammatory phenotypes, a platelet phenotype representing platelet activation integrins, von Willebrand Factor (vWF), and a thromboelastography phenotype representing clot strength and thrombin generation. Controlling for age, sex, injury mechanism, and bleeding (CAT+), mortality was predominantly driven by the coagulopathy phenotype 1 (p = 0.0002) and mixed inflammatory phenotype 3 (p = 0.0684).CONCLUSIONS:These findings demonstrate that coagulation and inflammation are coactivated early after severe injury and are associated with poor outcomes. The activation of these pathways is both complex and integrated, involving inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, coagulation factors, platelet activated integrins, vWF, and thrombin. This study uses the largest comprehensive array of coagulation and inflammatory biomarkers to date and identifies key biologic phenotypes linked to mortality after severe trauma.

KEY POINTS

Question: What is the role of coagulation and pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines after injury and how are they associated with mortality?

Findings: This secondary analysis of the Pragmatic Randomized Optimal Platelet and Plasma Ratio (PROPPR) study, which used principal component analysis, demonstrated that a coagulation phenotype and mixed inflammatory phenotype were independently associated with 30-day mortality after severe injury.

Meaning: Coagulation and inflammation coactivation occurs early after severe injury and is associated with worse outcomes.

Traumatic injury remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide and is the leading cause of death for individuals 45 years old and under in the United States (1, 2). Hemorrhage predominates in early death, whereas later causes of death have been linked to several inflammatory-driven conditions including respiratory failure and sepsis. Sepsis has been strongly linked to an abundance of both pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors (3–6). Although similar inflammatory changes have been hypothesized to be a major contributor to outcome in severely injured patients, there remains a lack of specificity in understanding the mechanisms and interactions with the coagulation cascade. Among the many knowledge gaps are the need to define the initial injury profiles associated with pathologic inflammatory signatures, the timing of differing cytokine activation, and course of inflammatory resolution in those suffering from hemorrhagic injury.

For patients who are severely injured, dysregulated coagulopathy and elevated inflammation have been linked to decreased survival; however, the comprehensive inflammatory phenotypes that are associated with mortality are unknown (6, 7). Prior work has shown variable degrees of hyperfibrinolysis, platelet dysfunction, endothelial activation, and cytokine dysregulation after injury. This includes the demonstration that viscoelastic studies used to measure coagulopathy after trauma, such as the maximum amplitude-reaction time (MAR) ratio on thromboelastography, have been tied to significant differences in a small panel of inflammatory markers investigated including interleukin (IL)-6, IL-8, IL-10, and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) in critically injured patients (8). In the Pragmatic Randomized Optimal Platelet and Plasma Ratio (PROPPR) study, a high MAR ratio at admission thromboelastography was also associated with increased mortality, fewer ICU-free days, and fewer ventilator-free days (6, 8). An initial inflammatory reaction to major trauma has also been linked to a higher risk of sepsis (9–13). After major trauma, patients also have elevated syndecan-1 levels drawn within 4 hours of admission (14). Most importantly, dysregulation and/or prolonged inflammation is associated with worse outcomes after injury including increased rates of venous thromboembolism, sepsis, and decreased survival (5, 6, 14-16). These individual findings have not yet been integrated into a coherent mechanistic model of how inflammation and coagulation crosstalk influences survivorship.

Prior work supports the premise that coagulation and inflammation are interactive. However, prior studies focused on singular markers of inflammation or very limited panels of markers. There is significant need to investigate a more comprehensive profile of inflammatory markers to better elucidate the key drivers of outcome. It is well known that cytokines, both pro- and anti- inflammatory, have many complex interactions. Uncovering the early patient phenotypes associated with pathologic inflammatory signatures could help identify opportunities to tailor treatment to help mitigate poor outcomes. The biomarkers investigated in this study were identified from those associated with coagulopathy, inflammation, and endotheliopathy in prior literature. The purpose of this study is to identify the inflammatory and coagulation phenotypes present immediately after injury that are associated with mortality in a severely injured population.

METHODS

This is a secondary analysis of the PROPPR study. The PROPPR study was a landmark randomized, multicenter, prospective trial published in the Journal of the American Medical Association examining the impact of a balance resuscitation strategy 1:1:1 resuscitation vs. 1:1:2 resuscitation (plasma:platelet:RBCs) on mortality in severely injured patients. Patients were enrolled at 12 level 1 trauma centers between August 2012 and December 2013 (17). To be included in the trial, patients had to be a highest level trauma activation and receive at least one unit of blood either prehospital or in the first hour of injury. Patients had to be transported directly from the scene of the injury, and were anticipated to need massive transfusion by exceeding the threshold score on the Assessment of Blood Consumption score of 2 or more. Patients excluded from the PROPPR trial included those with devastating injury expected to die within 1 hour of admission, burns greater than 20% total body surface area, those with inhalation injury, prehospital cardiopulmonary resuscitation greater than 5 minutes, emergency department thoracotomy before receiving blood products, transfer patients from another facility, pregnant women, children younger than 15 years old, and those with a known do not resuscitate order. The dual primary endpoints were 24-hour and 30-day mortality and no difference in survivability was found in the original trial. Secondary endpoints included planned analysis of a comprehensive panel of biomarkers that were drawn at the time of enrollment. The PROPPR trial was approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (Investigational New Drug No. 14929), Health Canada, the Department of Defense, and all site institutional review boards (IRBs Number HSC-GEN-11-0174 at University of Texas, approved on July 12, 2013). This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of responsible IRBs and the Helsinki Declaration of 1975. The study was also monitored by an external data and safety monitoring board appointed by the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute. The study used exception of informed consent, including community consultation with delayed patient or legally authorized representative consent.

Although the primary study endpoints were reported in 2015, the comprehensive biomarker data required a lengthy time for assaying and data harmonization. All assays were performed at a central location under the direction of the Data/Study Coordinating Center. Assays were performed using commercially available reagents/kits. It was anticipated that approximately 5 years would be needed after the trial was complete to perform the biologic assay evaluation. However, additional delays were introduced due to the COVID pandemic impact on laboratory operations and manufacturer assay kit availability. Thus, these complete biologic data only became available to study investigators in the last 18 months.

In the current study, any patient who had blood products prehospital were excluded from the analysis as those products may have impacted biomarker analysis. We also excluded patients who did not have complete biomarker data available. The specimens analyzed were from the first blood draw in emergency department at the time of IV catheter placement. Forty-eight candidate biomarkers and factors were assessed. Those with complete data from the PROPPR study were included in the principal component analysis (PCA). The dimensional-reduction machine learning method was used to identify initial biomarker phenotypes (principal components [PCs]) associated with 30-day mortality. PCA reduces the number of factors in data to a set of linear variables known as PCs. The technique is designed to address collinearity or overlapping relationships between variables. Each PC contains unique information even when the original variables were highly correlated. We included PCs with a variance (eigenvalues) greater than 1 for inclusion in our analysis.

PCA describes the properties of a dataset represented by phenotypes or PCs, but does not describe the relationship of the dataset to an outcome of interest. Additional analysis must be conducted using either clustering techniques or regression to interpret how the PCs relate to specific outcomes of interest. We used the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression to identify independent 30-day mortality predictors. Statistical analysis was completed using Statistical Analysis System (SAS), Version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). Age, injury mechanism, and significant hemorrhage were controlled for with LASSO regression. Significant hemorrhage was quantified as receiving greater than or equal to 3 RBC units in any hour of care in the first 24 hours (critical administration threshold+).

When regression is performed to associate the PC to an outcome of interest, it is common that the highest ranking PCs or phenotypes often will appear “insignificant” or not reach the standard statistical threshold of p value of less than 0.05 because the original rank order of the PCs (before regression analysis) were ordered based on the characteristics of the original dataset, not ranked based on their relationship to the outcome variable of interest (i.e., mortality). For example, PC1 represents the most possible information about the dataset, then PC2 the next most, and PC3 follows. For this reason, it is common to retain and report PC relationships that extend beyond the traditional p value of less than 0.05 value when the PCs were in the top third of the original dataset. We report all PCs that meet a statistical significance threshold of p value of less than 0.10.

RESULTS

A total of 286 patients had the complete biomarker data and were included in this analysis. The 30-day mortality was 24.4% (n = 70) for this cohort. The average age was 38.9 years (± 17.3 yr), and there was a significant difference in age between those who died within 30 days and those who survived (the average age was 45 ± 30 yr in the deceased cohort and 36.9 ± 15.5 yr in the surviving cohort; p = 0.004; Table 1). The cohort was 76% male and there was no survival difference between genders (78% of the deceased cohort and 81.9% of the surviving cohort was male; p = 0.5).

PCA found 14 phenotypes that collectively explained 83% of the difference in mortality. The top six PCs contributing to early pathophysiology changes were a coagulopathy phenotype, followed by two distinct mixed inflammatory phenotypes; a platelet phenotype and a thromboelastography phenotype (Table 1). The platelet phenotype represented platelet activating integrins. Phenotype 5 was driven by von Willebrand Factor (vWF). The thromboelastography phenotype represented clot strength and thrombin generation. After controlling for age, sex, injury mechanism, and significant bleeding (> 3 packed RBCs [pRBCs] in 1 hr), mortality was predominately driven by the coagulopathy phenotype 1 (p = 0.0002) and mixed inflammatory phenotype 3 (p = 0.068; Table 3). No other phenotypes in the top six reached the p value of less than 0.10 threshold.

DISCUSSION

The crosstalk and significance of coagulation and inflammatory pathways after severe injury is complex and not fully understood (3, 5, 6, 15, 8). Nearly all the prior literature on the link between inflammation and coagulation has focused on inflammatory marker detection at later time points, ranging from 2 to 24 hours or later. There is a paucity of data collected before the influence of treatment-related variables. Our current work uniquely identifies that inflammatory markers are present very early following injury and are detectable with the first blood draw. To be included in this analysis, patients could not have received prehospital blood products and the time zero sample was drawn at the time of placement of the initial IV catheter. Therefore, our data reflect the earliest time point one can reasonably assess after trauma for inflammatory pathways.

Our study uses the largest biomarker panel to date to study biologic phenotypes linked to mortality. Our findings demonstrate that 83% of variability of biomarkers after severe trauma is secondary to 14 distinct phenotypes, driven by coagulation markers, mixed inflammatory markers, platelet function markers, and thromboelastography values. Importantly, two phenotypes were independently associated with mortality within 30 days when controlled for age, sex, injury mechanism, and bleeding (> 3 pRBC in 1 hr), a coagulation phenotype 1 and a mixed inflammatory phenotype 3 (Table 3). These findings add to the growing body of evidence that both the inflammatory and coagulation pathways are coactivated imminently after severe trauma. In addition, dysregulation of these pathways is associated with worsened outcomes (6, 14, 16, 8, 7). Further understanding of the interaction between these pathways is imperative to identifying patients at risk, triaging them appropriately, and potentially selecting patients for personalized treatment to mitigate outcome.

Coagulopathy after trauma is thought to be in part secondary to anticoagulant and anti-inflammatory arms of the activated protein C pathway (18–21). In this study, phenotype 1 was driven by antithrombin and coagulation factors II, V, IX, X, XI, and XII. These findings are similar to a prior single institution study that demonstrated a significant association between 28-day mortality and factors V, VIII, X, and protein C (18). Factor V has been associated with hypoperfusion in trauma, as well as poor outcomes in other fields, such as allograft failure and death in liver transplant patients (18, 20, 22, 23). We found phenotype 1 includes factor V, and this is consistent with the prior literature that factor V is associated with poor outcomes in severe trauma (18). Further understanding of the role that factor V plays within the mediation of the coagulation and inflammatory pathways after trauma is imperative for targeting the dysfunction that leads to poor outcomes. Antithrombin is a key mediator in regulating the coagulation cascade and lower levels have been associated with disseminated intravascular coagulation and decreases survival in the trauma population (18, 24–26).

Prior literature has identified cytokines IL-1B, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-23, high mobility group box 1, and MCP as likely involved in the relationship between inflammation and coagulopathy after injury (3, 8, 27–34). Phenotype 3 is a mixed inflammatory phenotype driven by pro-inflammatory factors: cytokines IL-6 and IL-8, chemokine factors: MCP-1, and anti-inflammatory cytokines: IL-10 and IL-1ra. Higher concentrations of IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, and MCP-1 were also identified in patients with a high MAR ratio in a prior single institution investigation by Savage et al (8). Phenotype 2 was driven by pro-inflammatory cytokines: interferon gamma, IL-1B, IL-12p70, IL-5, IL-7 and anti-inflammatory cytokines: IL-4 and IL-13. Although there is a concurrent anti-inflammatory response, our findings support the theory, that after injury there is a dysregulated inflammatory state, with mostly pro-inflammatory cytokines, that is, associated with a poor outcome (3, 6, 8, 30).

Platelets are known to play a key role in inflammation by activating leukocytes, releasing soluble mediators for leukocytes, modulating neutrophil activation, facilitating neutrophil recruitment, and adhesion at sites of inflammation (35, 36). In this work, phenotypes 4 and 5 included platelet activator integrins; glycoproteins (GP) IIB, GP1Ba, and GPIIIA, and vWF. Platelet activated integrins and vWF play active roles in coagulation and inflammation. Platelet integrins glycoprotein IIb/IIIa and GP1ba mediate early and middle stages of platelet adhesion (37). GP1ba is a part of the receptor of vWF and when bound to vWF enables platelets to form thrombotic plugs at sites of vascular injury. GP1ba is also known to interact with macrophage-1 antigen during inflammation. In addition, GPIIb-IIa or GP1b binds to pathogens using vWF or fibrinogen (38). High flow or shear rates are associated with the activation of GP1ba and vWF leading to increased affinity and shear resistance that allows platelet adhesion under stress (39). Platelet activated integrins and vWF are activated early after injury and play an integral role in inflammation and coagulation. Their importance may reflect the interaction between the endothelium with the inflammatory and coagulation pathways.

This study has several limitations. First, the complete biomarker data were only available for 286 of the 680 patients in the PROPPR study. The small number of patients with complete samples reflects challenges in obtaining adequate blood samples in severely injured patients in the chaos of a trauma resuscitation. As a result, there was not adequate volume of blood collected for all assays to be done in all patients. Future clinical trials should consider obtaining extra blood tubes for biobanking for further study as more biomarkers are likely to be discovered from the time of trial planning to trial completion. To account for the risk of sample bias, we compared the demographics and outcomes of those with and without complete biomarker data, and found no significant difference between these groups. We acknowledge that there may still be unrecognized bias in other unmeasured confounders present that we cannot account for.

An additional limitation is in the machine learning approach. Although a powerful analytic technique, PCA is a hypothesis generating technique when applied to clinical trial data, and not able to determine cause and effect directly. It is also not readily generalizable, and we are unable to currently test for the phenotypes in the clinical setting. In addition, in this methodology the researcher can introduce bias by the a priori selection of which biomarkers to provide to the machine learning approach. We based our markers on those that had clinical or known biologic plausibility or a link to outcome. We also recognize that the study may have some limitations in power given the smaller-than-intended sample size, which limits the ability to conduct subset analysis by injury mechanism. Future studies should be carefully designed to further elucidate the complex interplay between coagulation and inflammation and further validate the findings of our study.

Despite these limitations, this is an important advance for the field of trauma care as these inflammatory markers may signal future opportunities for novel therapies to be developed that target pathologic inflammation. Secondary use of biomarkers from clinical trial data are intended to identify future areas of work to return to preclinical models to gain more mechanistic insight into potential causative pathways. Investigations like ours help to focus future work on those biomarkers or mechanistic pathways of highest probability of potential links to clinical outcomes. Such work, if validated in future studies, could eventually lead to the development of point-of-care tests to identify these phenotypes and development of targeted treatments.

CONCLUSIONS

Immediately after severe trauma, coagulation and inflammatory pathways are coactivated and associated with a poor outcome. The activation of these pathways is both complex and integrated involving inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, coagulation factors, platelet activated integrins, vWF, and thrombin. Point-of-care tests with inflammatory and coagulation phenotypes associated with high mortality risk should be developed to identify patients at elevated risk. Future research should focus on attempting to identify targeted therapies that counteract the dysregulated inflammatory and coagulation pathways identified.
