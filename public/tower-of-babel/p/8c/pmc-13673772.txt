Associations of household air pollution from solid cooking fuel use with risk of chronic respiratory diseases: evidence from the CHARLS study
Authors: Luhua Yu, Canlei Liu, Jun Jiang, Peng Guan, Yonghao Wu, Xin Zhang, Shujuan Lin, Jianbing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditional solid fuels remain the primary energy sources for household across China, with particularly widespread use in rural areas. However, evidence linking household use of solid fuels to risk of chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs) remains insufficient.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the association between solid cooking fuel use and risk of CRDs in middle aged and elderly adults.MethodsParticipants were recruited from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Cooking fuel was categorized into solid fuel (e.g., wood and coal) and clean fuel (e.g., petroleum gas and electric). The diagnosis and timing of the first diagnosis of CRDs, mainly including incident chronic lung diseases (CLDs) or asthma, were ascertained by face-to-face computer-assisted personal interviews using standardized, validated questionnaires. Cox proportion hazards models were used to examine the association between solid cooking fuel use and risk of CRDs.ResultsA total of 13,686, 14,986, and 13,414 participants were included in the analysis for the outcome of CLDs, asthma and CRDs, respectively. Compared with clean cooking fuel users, solid cooking fuel users had an adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) of 1.26 (95% confidence intervals [CI]: 1.10–1.45) for CLDs and 1.20 (95%CI: 0.95–1.52) for asthma, respectively. Among all participants, 12.60% (95% CI: 5.39%-19.90%) of CLDs cases could be attributable to participants using solid cooking fuels. Meanwhile, the HRs were higher among users of biomass fuels (e.g., crop residue and wood) than coal users. Of note, participants who switched from solid to clean cooking fuel or persistent clean cooking fuel had a lower risk of CRDs, with the HRs of 0.81 (95%CI: 0.58–1.15) and 0.71 (95%CI: 0.52–0.97) for CLDs, and the corresponding HRs were 0.85 (95%CI: 0.43–1.65) and 1.06 (95%CI: 0.60–1.88) for asthma.ConclusionsUse of solid cooking fuel is associated with a higher risk of CRDs, particularly for CLDs. Our findings suggest that decreasing dependence on solid fuels and transitioning to clean energy sources could contribute to alleviating the burden of CRDs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12931-026-03837-6.Keywords: Household air pollution, Chronic respiratory diseases, Solid cooking fuel use, Cohort study

Introduction

Chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs) refer to a diverse group of disorders that affect the airways and other structures components of the lung, including interstitial lung disease, pulmonary sarcoidosis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma and pneumoconiosis. Of these, COPD and asthma are the most prevalent [1], imposing a substantial burden on global health and the economy. An assessment of the Global Burden of Disease 2019 Study shown that among 454.6 million prevalent cases and 77.6 million new cases, CRDs accounted for 4.0 million deaths, being the third leading cause of death [2, 3]. In the United States, the costs associated with respiratory conditions were estimated at approximately US$171 billion in 2016 [4]. Similarly, among the 28 European Union member states, annual expenditures related to the care of patients with CRDs reached approximately €380 billion annually [5]. Given the considerable public health impact of CRDs, identifying and addressing modifiable risk factors is crucial for effectively mitigating this growing challenge.

Smoking has been reported to be a critical determinant contributing to the persistently risking incidence of CRDs, followed by ambient air pollution (AP) [2, 6]. Currently, household air pollution (HAP) poses significant threats to global environmental health [7], particularly in regions of Asia and Africa [8], which attracted extensive attention. HAP typically arises from pollutants emitted in household environments, primarily due to the burning of solid fuels during the cooking or heating, which releases harmful chemicals (e.g., sulfur dioxide [SO2], carbon monoxide [CO], particulate matter [PM]), leading to HAP levels far exceeding acceptable air quality standards [9]. Notably, an estimated 3.8 billion people living in low- and middle-income countries (LIC and MIC) still relied on solid fuels to fulfill their household energy requirements [10], potentially being exposed to heightened risk of developing serious respiratory and other chronic health conditions [11–13]. Several studies have reported that individuals relying on solid cooking fuels as their primary energy source have an elevated risk of hospitalization or death from both acute and chronic respiratory diseases [14]. In contrast, findings from a specialized cohort assessing the burden of chronic airflow obstruction showed no association of solid fuel use with chronic cough, chronic phlegm, or spirometric restriction [15]. Of note, most of existing studies assessed fuel types only at baseline, failing to capture potential changes in fuel types during the follow-up period. Evidence regarding the link of solid cooking fuels with CRDs remains limited.

To address existing research gaps, we designed a prospective cohort study to explore the association between use of household solid cooking fuels and risk of CRDs among middle aged and elderly adults. Furthermore, we assessed potential effects of differences and transitions in fuel types on risk of CRDs. In addition, we estimated the fractions of CRDs attributable to use of solid cooking fuels.

Methods

Study population

Participants were selected from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally longitudinal survey spanning 450 villages or urban committees and 150 counties or districts in 28 provinces. Briefly, the CHARLS comprehensively and systematically collected demographics, health-related behaviors, health status and functioning, health care and insurance, individual and household assets, community-level information and biomarkers. CHARLS baseline survey enrolled 17,708 participants aged ≥ 45 years in 2011 and subsequent follow-up visits were conducted biennially from 2013 to 2018. Ethical approval was obtained from Peking University Health Center (IRB00001052-11015), and the dataset is publicly accessible at: http://charls.pku.edu.cn/.

This study enrolled participants from waves spanning from 2011 to 2018. To address the distinct research objectives, we structured the participant selection process into two stages. Firstly, to examine the association between exposure to solid fuel use at baseline and incident CRDs, we excluded subjects who did not satisfy the representative sample age of 45 years or older, had missing information on solid cooking fuels use in 2011 survey wave, had missing covariates, and had a prior history of CRDs (namely, CLDs and asthma) at baseline. Secondly, to assess the impact of transitions in cooking fuel use, we further excluded participants who had missing information on solid cooking fuel use in the 2011–2015 survey waves, had a prior history of CRDs (namely, CLDs and asthma) in 2015, and had died before the 2015 survey waves. Exposure assessments

In CHARLS, data on cooking fuel use were collected through standardized structured questionnaires administered at baseline and during subsequent follow-up visits (“What is the main source of cooking fuel?”). According to their self-reported responses, participants were classified into two categories: users of clean cooking fuels (e.g., marsh gas, natural gas, liquefied petroleum gas, or electricity) and users of solid cooking fuels (e.g., coal, solid charcoal, wood, crop residues, or other biomass).

Ascertainment of outcomes

All participants were followed from the initial recruitment in CHARLS until either the incident diagnosis of a relevant respiratory disease, withdrawal from the cohort, death, or December 31, 2018, whichever occurred earlier. The main outcomes in our study were incident CRDs, including chronic lung diseases (CLDs) and asthma. Cases of CLDs and asthma was identified from the health status and functioning questionnaires from the baseline and follow-up survey with questions such as “Have you been diagnosed with chronic lung diseases, such as chronic bronchitis, emphysema (excluding tumors, or cancer), by a doctor?” and “Have you been diagnosed with asthma by a doctor?”. If the respondent replied “Yes”, he or she was defined as suffering from CRDs and needed to provide the first diagnosed time. Meanwhile, participants were re-asked “Our records from your last interview show that you had/not had conditions, is this right?” in a new survey wave. If the respondent replied “Disagree”, the diagnosis was reviewed and the corresponding disease status (CLDs or asthma) was updated/corrected based on the current response and available information.

Covariates

In light of biological and statistical considerations, as well as previous research, potential covariates ——including data on age (continuous), sex (male or female), marital status (single or other, or married/living with partner), physical activity (low, moderate, or high-intensity), alcohol consumption status (none, drinking but less than once a month, or drinking more than once a month), smoking status (never, former, or current), second-hand smoke (yes and no), total household income (by quartiles), education level (illiterate, elementary school or below, junior high school, and senior high school or above), history of hypertension and diabetes (both coded yes or no), independent kitchen (yes or no), geographic regions (North China, Northeast China, East China, Central China, South China, Northwest China, and Southwest China), urban-rural status (yes and no), and particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter ≤ 2.5 μm (PM2.5)——were derived from the baseline questionnaires.

Smoking status was defined based on whether an individual consumed more than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and currently smoked. Second-hand smoke was accessed based on household smoking status. Non-smoking participants were classified as being exposured second-hand smoke if they reported living at least one current smoker in the same household [16]. Physical activity was converted into metabolic equivalent of tasks (METs) relied on the type and duration of physical activities, calculated as MET-minutes/week = MET value × duration (minutes per session) × frequency (days per week). Then, according to the cut-off values (< 600, 600–3000, > 3000 MET-minutes/week) recommended by the globally recognized International Physical Activity Questionnaire, physical activity was categorized into low-intensity, moderate-intensity, or high-intensity (Supplemental Table 1) [17, 18]. Data on PM2.5 were obtained from the China High Air Pollutants (CHAP) dataset (https://weijing-rs.github.io/product.html) with a spatial resolution of 1000 m. The CHAP dataset is a widely recognized source of long-term, spatially resolved air pollution exposure estimates for China, and has been extensively utilized in numerous related studies [19, 20]. Due to the lag effect of air pollutant exposure, each participant was assigned the prior year’s annual average pollutant concentration (e.g., 2010 data matched to the 2011 wave) based on geocoded community-level residence information from the CHARLS PSU dataset [19].

Statistical analyses

Participants’ characteristics were summarized as frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables and means ± standard deviation (SD) or median (25th-75th centiles) for continuous variables. Differences in baseline characteristics between participants using different fuel types were evaluated using two-sample t-tests, Wilcoxon rank sum tests or chi-square tests.

Cox proportion hazards regression models, with age as the underlying timescale, were established to investigate the impact of using solid fuels for cooking on risk of CRDs. Strength of the association were estimated as hazard ratios (HRs) with the 95% confidence intervals (95%CI). Several models were sequentially adjusted for potential confounders, including sex, education level, marital status, total household income, physical activity, alcohol consumption status, second-hand smoke, smoking status, history of diseases, independent kitchen and PM2.5. The assumption of proportional hazard was assessed via Schoenfeld’s residuals (Supplemental Table 2).

To quantify CRDs (including CLDs and asthma) burden attributable to solid fuel use, we employed the R package graphPAF to calculate population attributable fractions (PAFs) [21], and the corresponding 95% CIs were estimated using bootstrapping. Detailed information on the function of PAFs was provided in Supplemental Method 1.

We further investigated the impact of different types and the conversion of cooking fuels on risk of CRDs. Solid cooking fuels were categorized into two types: biomass fuel (e.g., crop residue and wood) and coal. Meanwhile, we assessed the transition in cooking fuel use between the 2011 and 2015 survey waves for each participant and reset the follow-up time starting from the 2015 survey wave in the longitudinal analysis. Participants who consistently used solid fuels during this period (persistent solid fuels) served as the reference group. We compared the CRDs risk (including CLDs and asthma) among individuals who continuously used clean cooking fuels (persistent clean fuels) and experienced transition from solid to clean cooking fuels or from clean to solid cooking fuels [22].

Stratified analyses were conducted to understand potential variations across different groups defined by demographic, lifestyle, socioeconomic, history of diseases and housing characteristics. And a multiplicative interaction term (solid cooking fuel × baseline characteristics) was incorporated into the models in order to examine potential interactions of cooking fuel with baseline characteristics. To address the risk of Type I error inflation due to multiple subgroup comparisons, Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment was applied. To assess the robustness of our results, a series of sensitivity analyses were performed. Firstly, missing values of covariates were imputed using multiple imputation via chained equations (MICE). Secondly, participants with missing BMI data were excluded, and BMI was further adjusted in the full model. Thirdly, to account for spatial clustering among participants, spatial random-effects Cox proportional hazards models were applied to adjust the impact of region and urban-rural status. Fourthly, inverse probability weighting (IPW) was used to reduce the effect of missing data on the use of solid cooking fuels. Fifthly, time-dependent Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess changes in fuel types during the follow-up period. Finally, to address potential reverse causation that participants with respiratory symptoms might have changed fuel use because of their health condition, participants with peak expiratory flow (PEF) below 1 SD from the sex-specific mean were excluded [23–25].

The entire statistical analyses and figures were implemented using R 4.2.1. And statistical significance was set at two-sided P-values < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of participants

Baseline characteristics of the individuals are summarized in Table 1. A total of 13,686, 14,986, and 13,414 participants were included in the CLDs-free, asthma -free and CRDs-free at baseline, respectively (Fig. 1). For the analysis of CLDs, the mean follow-up duration was 6.53 ± 1.40 years, with 1009 incident CLDs cases identified; for the asthma, the mean follow-up duration was 6.69 ± 1.16 years, with 337 incident asthma cases confirmed; for the CRDs, the mean follow-up duration was 6.52 ± 1.41 years, yielding 1017 incident CRDs cases (comprising 893 incident CLDs and 184 incident asthma cases, of which 60 represented concurrent incident diagnoses of both CLDs and asthma). At baseline, 53.56%, 54.34%, and 53.27% of participants reported using solid cooking fuels in the analysis for CLDs, asthma and CRDs, respectively. Individuals adopting solid fuels as primary household energy source for cooking were generally older, had lower educational attainment, had lower household income levels, and had a higher smoking experience. Additionally, participants using solid cooking fuels showed a lower prevalence of diabetes and engaged more frequently in high-intensity physical activity.

Association of cooking fuel use with CRDs

Table 2 shows the adjusted HRs and 95% CIs of CRDs (namely, CLDs and asthma) in relation to solid cooking fuel use. In minimally adjusted models, risk of CRDs, CLDs and asthma increased by 30% (HR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.15–1.47), 36% (HR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.20–1.55) and 37% (HR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.15–1.47), respectively. When sex, marital status, educational level, total household income, second-hand smoke, smoking status, alcohol consumption status, history of hypertension and diabetes, independent kitchen and PM2.5 were included in Model 3, the relationships of solid cooking fuel use with CRDs and CLDs were still statistically significant (CRDs: HR = 1.21, 95%CI: 1.06–1.39; CLDs: HR = 1.26, 95%CI: 1.10–1.45), but similar non-significant association was observed for asthma (HR = 1.20, 95% CI: 0.95–1.52).

Association of different cooking fuel types with CRDs

Table 3 displays the impact of different types of solid cooking fuels on risk of CRDs. Compared with clean cooking fuels, adopting biomass fuels for cooking, such as crop residue or wood, had an elevated risk of CRDs (HR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.06–1.40). Specifically, comparable findings were observed for biomass fuel use in relation to risk of CLDs (HR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.10–1.47), but not for asthma (HR = 1.27, 95% CI: 0.99–1.62). No associations were observed for coal use and risk of CRDs (HR = 1.19, 95% CI: 0.96–1.48).

Association of the conversion of cooking fuel type with CRDs

Table 4 presents the results of cooking fuel transition and CRDs. Compared with persistent solid cooking fuel users, the HRs (95%CI) for risk of CRDs in users who transitioned to cleaner fuels or consistently used clean fuels were 0.83 (95%CI: 0.58–1.16) and 0.73 (95%CI: 0.54-1.00), respectively. Similar results were observed for CLDs and the corresponding HR for transition to cleaner fuels or consistently used clean fuels was 0.81 (95%CI: 0.58–1.15) and 0.71 (95%CI: 0.52–0.97), respectively. However, no associations were observed for the conversion of cooking fuels and risk of asthma.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

In subgroup analyses, physical activity and marital status significantly modified the relationship between use of solid cooking fuel and risk of CRDs (all P interaction<0.05). For CLDs, statistically significant effect modification was observed for sex, drinking status, smoking status, physical activity, marital status, total household income, and education level (all P interaction<0.05). Specifically, the association between solid cooking fuel use and CLD risk was stronger among the elderly, female, never-smokers, nondrinkers, and individuals who were unmarried, have lower household income and education level. Whereas no interaction was observed for asthma. Detailed results are presented in Supplemental Tables 5–7. In additional, compared with participants who had never smoked and used clean cooking fuels, those in other exposure groups, including never smoked and used clean cooking fuels, ever smoked and used clean cooking fuels, and ever smoked and used solid cooking fuels, exhibited significantly elevated risks of CRDs. The corresponding HRs (95% CIs) were 1.25 (95% CI: 1.04–1.49), 1.42 (95% CI: 1.13–1.79), and 1.64 (95% CI: 1.31–2.03), respectively. Similar associations were observed for CLDs and asthma (Supplemental Table 8).

In sensitivity analyses, the results were not materially altered when imputing missing values of covariates, adjusting for BMI, applying spatial random-effects Cox proportional hazards models, applying IPW, and excluding participants with low PEF. However, the association between solid fuel use and asthma became statistically significant when using time-dependent Cox proportional hazards models (primary analysis: HR = 1.20, 95% CI: 0.95–1.52; sensitivity analysis: HR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.02–1.70) (Supplemental Table 9).

Discussion

Using population data from a nationwide longitudinal survey, we observed that participants adopting solid fuels as the primary household cooking energy source had a 26% higher risk of CLDs, but not for asthma. The PAF of solid cooking fuel use for CLDs was 12.60%. Notably, participants who transitioned to cleaner cooking fuels or consistently used clean cooking fuels exhibited a reduced risk of CLDs. In addition, the combustion of biomass fuels (e.g., crop residues or wood) was significantly linked to an increased risk of developing CLDs and asthma, whereas no associations were observed for coal use. Interactions were observed between certain covariates (e.g., physical activity and marital status) and use of solid cooking fuels. The results were largely robust in sensitivity analyses, with the exception of the time-dependent Cox model, which might yield a stronger association.

In this study, our main finding was that solid cooking fuel use was linked to CRDs, particularly for CLDs. A large multinational cohort study found that 41.8% of participants primarily relied on solid fuels for household cooking, with such usage identified as a solid factor for developing fatal or nonfatal respiratory diseases [26]. Another independent study with follow-up of more than 9 years reported that risk of developing COPD was 1.21-fold higher among individuals using solid cooking fuels than clean cooking fuel users [27]. Systematic reviews from India and China have drawn consistent conclusions that high levels of HAP was positively associated with an increased risk of respiratory symptoms, respiratory infections, and COPD [28, 29]. However, systematic reviews and meta-analyses based on studies from Europe, Canada, New Zealand, Australia, and the USA showed that no significant association was observed between household burning of wood and risk of asthma and cough [30, 31]. The collective evidence indicates that HAP from burning of solid cooking fuels has a detrimental effect on CRDs, particularly for CLDs.

Meanwhile, we found that 10.40% (95% CI: 2.86%-17.90%) of CRDs new cases were attributable to solid cooking fuel use. So far, data on the burden of CRDs associated with use of solid fuels remained extremely limited. A Global Burden of Disease study showed that smoking, PM, HAP, occupational particulates, ozone and second-hand smoke accounted for 73.3% (95% uncertainty interval: 65.8–80.1) of disability-adjusted life years related to COPD [32]. A study involving 21 countries indicated that approximately 1.8% of the PAF for death in MIC was attributable to HAP, compared to up to 12% in LIC [33]. Using PAFs to estimate the health impact of solid cooking fuels which was a major source of HAP could offer clear and intuitive public health implications.

The exact mechanisms underlying the impact of solid fuel use on risk of developing CRDs are not well understood. Several studies have indicated that, ideally, the complete combustion of fuels should produce only nitrogen, oxygen, carbon dioxide (CO2) and water (H2O) [34]. However, in reality, this ideal scenario is rarely achieved. On the one hand, household stoves are far from ideal combustors due to their low thermal efficiency and lack of air pollution control devices, which could result in highly incomplete combustion. This incomplete combustion commonly generates harmful pollutants, including CO, PM, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), and volatile organic compounds. In China, the residential combustion of solid fuels is estimated to contribute to approximately 36% of annual primary anthropogenic PM2.5 emissions, 53% of elemental carbon, and 62% of PAHs [35–37]. Furthermore, solid fuels also contain sulfur and nitrogen, as well as various mineral elements such as aluminium, silicium, iron, and phosphorus, along with potentially toxic trace elements (e.g., arsenic, plumbum, mercury, manganese, cadmium, and chromium). As a result, combustion not only releases SO2 and nitrogen oxides, but also gaseous and/or particulate-bound forms of mineral elements, nitrogenous compounds such as ammonia, and elemental/organic carbon [38]. These pollutants may significantly affect household air quality, leading to pathological conditions in individuals such as inflammation, oxidative stress, cellular aging, and impairment of the airway epithelial barrier, thereby posing serious risks to human health [39].

Additionally, the results showed that cooking with biomass solid fuels (e.g., crop residues or wood), compared to use of cleaner fuels (e.g., petroleum gas, electric), was associated with a higher risk of CRDs, whereas cooking with coal showed a positive but non-significant association with CRDs. Similar results were observed for CLDs. Previous studies have also showed that persistent wood users have a greater excess risk of chronic lower respiratory diseases than coal users [14, 27]. However, a cross-sectional analysis from China Kadoorie Biobank has indicated that among women, use of coal had a detrimental effect on risk of airflow obstruction, but wood burning had a protective effect [40]. The etiology underlying the association of CRDs with air pollutants generated from the combustion of different fuels was unclear. Several studies suggested that biomass (crop residues or wood) have contained higher volatile organic compounds, higher moisture content, and lower ash content than coal [27, 34]. Additionally, biomass combustion could produce higher emissions of PM than coal combustion.

Another noteworthy finding of our study was that transition to cleaner fuels could reduce risk of CRDs compared with prolonged use of solid fuels. Although limited evidence can be available, a prior study indicated that transition from solid to clean fuels could substantially reduce emissions of harmful HAP, which was consistent with our study [14]. This finding underscores that the increased risk of diseases caused by historical use of solid fuels could be mitigated through a transition to cleaner fuels later in life.

Notably, the primary findings were robust across multiple sensitivity analyses. However, the time-dependent Cox model yielded a stronger and statistically significant association between solid fuel use and incident asthma (HR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.02–1.70), in contrast to the primary time-fixed analysis (HR = 1.20, 95% CI: 0.95–1.52). In the primary analysis, solid cooking fuel use was treated as a time-fixed baseline exposure. Yet, a substantial proportion of households transitioned from solid to cleaner fuels during the follow-up period, resulting in exposure misclassification that person-time accrued under actual cleaner conditions remained assigned to the “solid fuel” category, biasing the hazard ratio towards the null [41, 42]. By contrast, the time-dependent Cox model updated fuel use status at each survey wave, thereby capturing transitions, particularly from solid to clean fuels, and sharpening the contrast between persistently exposed and persistently unexposed person-time. Although this model strengthens the observed association, we retain the time-fixed baseline exposure as the primary analytical approach for two key reasons: firstly, the study’s central question concerns the long-term respiratory implications of a household’s initial cooking-energy profile; secondly, time-updated exposure definitions introduce methodological complexities, including decisions about survey frequency, and timing of exposure changes, that could not be independently verified in the absence of objective, device-based stove-use data. Thus, the time-dependent analysis is presented as a sensitivity and exploratory assessment. It suggested that using a fixed baseline label alone might underestimate the true burden of solid fuel exposure, particularly when transitions to cleaner fuels occur. Importantly, the results also indicated that transition to cleaner fuels was associated with a reduced risk of CRDs, particularly asthma.

In exploratory subgroup analyses, we observed several nominally significant interactions. While some of these findings are biologically plausible, they should be interpreted with caution given the number of comparisons performed. The heterogeneity analysis suggested the association between solid fuel use for cooking and risk of CLDs was stronger in several subgroups, including females, current smoker, those with lower educational attainment, lower household income, and lower physical activity. These findings were consistent with previous studies. Females usually spent more time on domestic work, such as cooking, leading to higher exposure to HAP [43]. Participants with lower education level and household income might face potential environmental health inequalities, including higher exposure levels, together with deprived material resources, and limited healthcare access, which could amplify their susceptibility to the adverse effects of solid fuel use [44]. Exercise could preserve or improve the function of multiple physiological systems (e.g., neuromuscular and cardiopulmonary function), which might partly offset the harmful effect of biomass fuels [45]. Individuals with low physical activity levels exhibited greater susceptibility to solid fuel-related HAP. And the stronger association observed among current smokers could highlight a potential synergistic effect between active smoking and solid fuel exposure. Notably, a stronger association was among never-drinkers that might reflect a “healthy drinker” bias. Moderate drinkers in observational studies were often healthier at baseline, with better overall health behaviors and socioeconomic status, which could attenuate the measured association in that group. Participants who did not drink were likely to be more sensitive to HAP, which could cause greater damage to their health. Additionally, some evidence suggested that HAP from solid fuel combustion played a more important role in the occurrence of tissue inflammation for participants who did not drink [14, 15, 46, 47].

Several important strengths of this study include a high-quality national longitudinal survey involving participants from 28 provinces in China, a follow-up period exceeding 6 years, and comprehensive assessments of the effects of different cooking fuel types and transitions in cooking fuels on CRDs. Meanwhile, Our Cox models were further adjusted for demographic characteristics, individual-level socioeconomic status, lifestyle behaviors, and history of diseases to account for potential confounding bias. Additionally, considering the potential issue of spatial clustering among participants, we employed spatial random-effects Cox proportional hazards models in sensitivity analyses.

Some limitations should not be ignored in the present study. Firstly, lack of data on heating fuel use, which was also a major source of HAP, restricted our capacity to fully evaluate the combined effects of heating and cooking fuel use on risk of CRDs. Secondly, cooking fuel types was self-reported by participants, and the exposure levels of cooking fuel were not directly measured, which might bring recall bias. Thirdly, although we further adjusted for housing characteristics in analyses (independent kitchen), unmeasured confounders (e.g., ventilation status of the kitchen, duration and frequency of cooking) may still remain. Fourthly, some individuals who currently depend on clean cooking fuels may have previously transitioned from solid fuels prior to baseline. Therefore, risk of developing CRDs among those classified as unexposed may be influenced by prior exposure. This residual exposure could attenuate the observed association, leading to bias toward the null. Finally, due to the absence of post-bronchodilator spirometry or medical-record-validated lung function, the diagnosis of CLDs could not be regarded as clinically confirmed COPD.

Conclusion

Our research furnishes new insights into the link between HAP emitted from the combustion of solid cooking fuels and an elevated risk of CRDs, particularly for CLDs, whereas transitioning to cleaner cooking fuels could reduce this adverse impact on CRDs. As a key component of solid fuels, the burning of wood posed a higher risk than coal use. Our findings could provide important implications that residential combustion should be prioritized in air pollution control, and that multi-pronged strategies to reduce solid fuel exposure —such as restricting use of solid fuels, optimizing renewable energy policies, expanding access to clean fuels, and strengthening regulatory oversight —should be developed, as important approaches for reducing risk of CRDs.
