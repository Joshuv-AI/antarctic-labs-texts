Comparing the effects of virtual reality and traditional balance training on trunk control, sitting balance, and activities of daily living in patients with stroke: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Ting He, Mengjie Zhang, Xinpeng Chen, Yijie Lian, Ziyun Zhang, Wenxin Niu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStroke survivors frequently experience impaired trunk control, affecting sitting balance and daily activities. Virtual reality (VR) balance training presents an engaging alternative to conventional methods, though its comparative effectiveness remains uncertain.PurposeTo compare the effects of VR-based versus traditional balance training on trunk control, sitting balance, and ADL in stroke patients.MethodsThirty-six participants were randomly assigned to either VR-based training or conventional sitting balance training group. Both groups completed a 4-week intervention. Outcome measures including the Modified Barthel Index (MBI), Trunk Impairment Scale (TIS), and Center of Pressure (COP) parameters under static/dynamic sitting conditions were assessed at baseline and post-intervention.ResultsBoth groups showed significant time effects in trunk control (TIS total score, p < 0.001), with the VR group demonstrating greater improvement (d = 1.72 vs. 1.07). A significant time×group interaction was found for dynamic balance (TIS-dynamic, p = 0.013) and dynamic mediolateral displacement (p = 0.016), indicating VR training produced superior gains. While both groups improved in activities of daily living (MBI, p < 0.001), no significant time×group interaction was observed. Only the VR group showed significant improvement in static anterior-posterior displacement (p = 0.039), with no time×group effects found for sway velocity or static mediolateral displacement.ConclusionWhile both interventions similarly improved trunk control, sitting balance, and ADL, VR-based training demonstrated superior efficacy in enhancing lateral trunk control during dynamic tasks, suggesting its potential advantage for facilitating dynamic postural adjustments in stroke rehabilitation.Trial registrationhttps://www.chictr.org.cn (No. ChiCTR2500100936); Registration date: 4-17-2025 (retrospectively registered).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01323-y.

Introduction

Stroke is the second leading cause of death globally and is closely associated with severe long-term disabilities [1]. More than 80% of stroke survivors experience severe balance dysfunction [2], primarily attributed to impaired trunk control. Sitting balance is a key component of the rehabilitation process, playing an indispensable role in improving patients’ quality of life and promoting their functional recovery [3, 4].

Balance dysfunction may limit the patient’s ability to sit independently without back support or make it difficult to maintain trunk stability during daily activities [5, 6]. Sitting balance is an important indicator for predicting mobility, functional prognosis, and length of hospital stay of stroke patients [7–9]. Furthermore, the stability of sitting balance is closely related to the risk of falls during hospitalization and rehabilitation [10]. Falls that occur after stroke can lead to fractures and cause patients to reduce physical activity due to fear of falling again [11].

Traditional post-stroke sitting balance rehabilitation typically relies on repetitive exercises of specific movements aimed at enhancing trunk flexibility, strength, coordination, and symmetry [12]. These methods include neurodevelopmental therapy, task-specific training [13], and trunk-focused exercises [14]. However, these traditional methods often require significant manpower and time investment, and their effectiveness largely depends on the skill level of healthcare professionals. Additionally, many stroke patients find these training methods lack clear goals and fun, which can lead to a decrease in their enthusiasm and persistence in participation [15]. Consequently, there is a pressing need for straightforward and effective sitting balance training that is readily accepted by patients and capable of sustaining high levels of compliance and motivation.

As an emerging technology, Virtual reality (VR) training is gradually showcasing its advantages [16–18]. Non-immersive VR training employs advanced computer hardware and software technology to accurately track patients’ movement trajectories and enable real-time interaction with virtual images on the screen. Moreover, VR training offers real-time visual and auditory feedback, which not only greatly enhances patients’ engagement in rehabilitation tasks but also makes the rehabilitation process more enjoyable. Research has demonstrated that compared to traditional rehabilitation methods, VR training can achieve more frequent repetitive exercises, and reinforcement through repetitive training has been proven to be closely related to improved patient prognosis [19]. It is important to note that VR may not be well suited for patients with severe postural instability who need constant supervision or assistance from a therapist to ensure safety.

Assisted by VR technology, encouraging results have been achieved in the recovery of upper limb function, gait, and standing balance in stroke patients [20, 21]. However, there is still a lack of comparative research on the effects of VR balance training versus traditional balance training on sitting balance post-stroke. A recent systematic review encompassed six randomized controlled trials [22], most of which investigated the effects of integrating VR trunk training into conventional rehabilitation programs, as opposed to traditional treatment of rehabilitation alone. The review findings indicated that VR-based trunk training had significant effects in reducing trunk impairments in patients with subacute and chronic stroke [22]. A randomized controlled trial conducted by Sultan et al. [23] further compared differences in standing balance (Berg Balance Scale, BBS), trunk control, and functional independence between stroke patients receiving VR training using Xbox Kinect and those receiving balance training, upper limb strengthening, and core strengthening. They found that Xbox Kinect-based VR training significantly improved trunk control and functional mobility in stroke patients compared to the control group, though standing balance (BBS) improvements were comparable between groups. Nevertheless, there remains a notable gap in the literature directly comparing the effects of VR-based sitting balance training and traditional balance training on sitting balance and trunk control in stroke patients.

This randomized controlled trial aimed to address the research gap by comparing the effects of VR-based balance training and traditional balance training on trunk control, sitting balance, and activities of daily living in patients with subacute stroke. We hypothesized that VR-based balance training would be superior to traditional balance training in improving trunk control, sitting balance, and ADL in subacute stroke survivors.

Methods

This randomized, single-blinded, parallel-group trial was designed and implemented strictly in accordance with the requirements of the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials Statement [24]. The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Shanghai Yangzhi Rehabilitation Hospital (YZ2020-043). All relevant information regarding this experiment was fully registered on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR), https://www.chictr.org.cn (No. ChiCTR2500100936).

Participants

A total of 125 patients with stroke in Shanghai Yangzhi Rehabilitation Hospital were screened between January 2021 and December 2024. All participants were recruited from the inpatient rehabilitation unit and were in the subacute phase of stroke recovery. No participants had been discharged prior to study enrollment. Following stringent selection processes, 36 patients met the inclusion criteria and were randomly and equally divided into either the VR-based balance training group or the traditional balance training group. All participants signed written informed consent forms. Figure 1 illustrates the detailed process of the involvement in the study.

Inclusion criteria: (1) First occurrence of ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke within the past 6 months; (2) Ability to sit independently for at least 1 min without support and maintain a sitting position for at least 30 min under supervision, ensuring the completion of the scheduled exercise routine; and (3) Good cognitive function (Mini-Mental State Examination > 24). Exclusion criteria: (1) Severe visual, auditory, or sensory impairment; (2) Presence of vestibular deficits or vertigo; or (3) History of epileptic seizures in the last 6 months.

Randomization and sample size calculation

This trial employed a block randomization method with mixed block sizes (2 and 4, respectively) to ensure a balanced distribution of sample sizes between groups while maintaining the unpredictability of the allocation sequence. The specific randomization plan and allocation sequence were generated using the website of Randomization.com. To ensure transparency and fairness in the allocation process, all allocation information was meticulously recorded in opaque, sequentially numbered, tightly sealed envelopes, which were kept by an investigator not involved in the trial.

The sample size estimation was conducted using G*power version 3.1.9.4 software, with a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a power value (1-β) of 0.8. Based on a recent systematic review comparing the effect size of VR-based trunk training with the control group, the effect size (Cohen’s d) for the total score on the Trunk Impairment Scale (TIS) was estimated to be 1.11 [22]. Based on this effect size and preliminary estimates of other statistical parameters, a total of 28 participants were required for the study. Considering a potential 10% dropout rate during the follow-up period and to maintain a 1:1 balanced distribution between the experimental and control groups, at least 16 participants needed to be recruited for each group.

Interventions

All participants received standard rehabilitation including physical therapy (60 min/day), occupational therapy (30 min/day), and traditional Chinese medicine interventions (30 min/day), all administered 5 days/week. In addition to this standard regimen, participants were randomly assigned to receive either VR-based or traditional sitting balance training for 30 min daily, 5 days/week over 4 weeks (20 sessions total). Crucially, both experimental interventions were carefully matched for training intensity: they employed an identical 1:1 therapist-to-patient ratio and followed comparable difficulty progression protocols.

All therapeutic interventions were administered by three licensed occupational therapists, each possessing a minimum of three years of clinical practice in neurological rehabilitation. To guarantee treatment consistency, two board-certified senior therapists (clinical experience > 6 years) provided comprehensive protocol training, including VR system operation standards and conventional sitting balance training guidelines.

Patients in the experimental group received VR-based sitting balance training using the Doctor Kinetic gaming system (iMove 3.0-Standard, DIH Technologies Co., USA). This system incorporated a Kinect sensor, a gaming software platform, and a 48-inch monitor. During training, patients engaged in a series of specially designed sitting balance games, such as Sunflower, Space Rescue, and Nine Square Grid. These games were specifically designed to deliver progressive, multidirectional trunk training tailored to stroke rehabilitation needs. Sunflower targeted lateral weight-shifting to facilitate paretic-side loading; Space Rescue incorporated anterior-posterior movements to challenge sagittal plane control during reaching tasks; Nine Square Grid integrated diagonal movements to develop coordinated multiplanar control for functional activities. Patients performed all training while seated on a standard chair with a backrest for safety, with their feet firmly placed on the ground. The therapist provided verbal guidance to help patients better complete the training tasks. Therapists modified game parameters based on individual patient abilities to ensure engagement and training effectiveness. Patients received real-time feedback from the monitor and game sounds during training, which helped to improve their balance performance and training outcomes.

Patients in the control group received traditional sitting balance training. This involved a series of therapeutic activities, including trunk movements in vertical, horizontal, and diagonal directions. Specific activities included pushing a roller back and forth, stacking cups from different directions, and inserting wooden boards into corresponding holes. As training progressed, the difficulty level was gradually increased. Throughout the training, therapists provided appropriate auditory and visual feedback to patients to facilitate improvements in their balance abilities.

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes were trunk control and sitting balance, assessed by the TIS and Center of Pressure (COP) measurements in static and dynamic sitting. The secondary outcome measure was the ability to perform activities of daily living, as assessed by the Modified Barthel Index (MBI).

The TIS is a comprehensive tool used to evaluate the sitting balance and trunk coordination of stroke patients in both static and dynamic settings. This scale comprises 17 specific items, with total scores ranging from 0 to 23 [25]. Higher scores indicate better trunk control ability. The Chinese version of the TIS exhibits acceptable levels of both intra-rater and inter-rater reliability, as evidenced by Intraclass correlation coefficients ranging from 0.75 ∼ 0.89 and 0.90 ∼ 0.97, respectively. Additionally, the scale has shown satisfactory internal consistency and has been validated for concurrent and construct validity in stroke survivors [26].

To further quantify the trunk control ability, a force measurement platform (AMTI BP400600-2000, Advanced Mechanical Technology, Inc., Newton, MA, USA) was utilized to evaluate the COP displacement. The sampling frequency was 1000 Hz. In practice, vertical uniaxial force sensors were used to measure reaction forces, and the data were recorded in real-time to a computer system. During the evaluation process, participants were seated on a stable and securely fixed chair placed on a Kistler force platform measuring 60 cm by 40 cm. The experimental protocol consisted of three consecutive tasks: (1) a static sitting posture, (2) anterior-posterior trunk movements, and (3) medio-lateral trunk movements, as depicted in Fig. 2.

Prior to initiating any experimental tasks, participants were explicitly instructed to adopt a static sitting position with specific requirements: maintaining an upright trunk posture, ensuring a 90-degree angle between the thighs and trunk; crossing their hands and placing them across the chest; and keeping both feet suspended. Simultaneously, participants were required to keep their eyes open and focus their gaze on a fixed target positioned 2 m ahead, which served to enhance stability. The duration for maintaining this static sitting posture was set at 30 s, during which participants were asked to minimize any unnecessary body movements as much as possible. Following the static sitting posture, participants were further instructed to perform maximum range anterior-posterior and medio-lateral trunk movements. To ensure that participants had an accurate understanding and execution of these dynamic sitting tasks, a practice session was specifically arranged prior to the COP assessment, allowing participants to familiarize themselves with and master the essential techniques of the movements.

After completing the practice, participants immediately proceeded to perform the three prescribed sitting assessment tasks in the stipulated order. Throughout the testing procedure, an experienced therapist conducted on-site observations and real-time monitoring to ensure that participants adhered strictly to the established assessment protocols and completed each task accurately and in accordance with the standards.

The evaluator documented the following measurement parameters: (1) Sway velocity: This parameter measured the average COP velocity. The calculation method involved dividing the total length of the COP trajectory throughout the entire trial (30 s). (2) Sway area: The trial process was accurately divided into two sections two equal stages, each lasting 15 s. For the COP data from each stage, we utilized two-dimensional normal distribution 95% confidence ellipses to depict and calculate the area of these ellipses, thereby quantifying the sway area for each section. Finally, the average of the sway area results from the two stages was taken to obtain the overall sway area for each trial. (3) Medio-lateral displacement: This parameter assessed the maximum extension ability of participants in the frontal plane, which referred to the farthest position difference that the patient can achieve in the medio-lateral direction of the body. The specific quantification method involved calculating the difference between the maximum and minimum offsets of the COP in the medio-lateral direction. (4) Anterior-posterior displacement: This parameter measured the furthest extension in the sagittal plane. The quantification method involved comparing the difference between the maximal and minimal COP excursion in the anterior-posterior orientation.

The MBI was used to measure a person’s ability to complete ten fundamental activities of daily living, such as feeding and bathing. The scoring range was from 0 to 100, where higher scores denoted increased independence.

All assessments were independently conducted by an evaluator who was unaware of the patient groupings at baseline and after intervention, ensuring the objectivity and impartiality of data collection.

Statistical analysis

Data processing and analysis were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26. Characteristics of the subjects and test results were summarized based on variable types. Continuous data was represented by mean and standard deviation, while categorical variables were presented in percentage form. At baseline, comparisons between the two groups were conducted using independent sample t-tests and chi-square tests, respectively. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, homogeneity of variance was examined via Levene’s test, and sphericity was verified with Mauchly’s test. A two-way mixed-design analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to assess intervention effects, with group as the between-subjects factor and time as the within-subjects factor. The model examined main and time*group interaction effects, with effect sizes reported as partial eta squared(η²ₚ) for ANOVA effects and Cohen’s d for pairwise comparisons. Following the detection of a significant interaction effect, simple main effects analyses will be conducted to assess: (1) between-group differences at each time point, and (2) within-group temporal variations. These analyses will be performed using the Estimated Marginal Means module in SPSS, with all pairwise comparisons adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni method. The validity of parametric assumptions was systematically evaluated. Non-normally distributed variables were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (within-group) and the Mann-Whitney U test (between-group), with rank-biserial correlation (r) and Cliff’s delta effect size, respectively. The significance level (α) was set at 0.05. Following the intention-to-treat protocol, missing endpoint data were addressed via last observation carried forward imputation.

For trunk control, both groups showed significant improvement in TIS total score (time effect: F = 150.58, p < 0.001, η²p = 0.82), with the VR group exhibiting numerically greater gains (d = 1.72, 95% CI [1.12, 2.32]) than the control group (d = 1.07, 95% CI [0.55, 1.59]). However, the time × group interaction (p = 0.070) and group effect (p = 0.204) were not statistically significant.

The TIS-dynamic balance revealed more nuanced findings. Our analysis confirmed both a significant time effect (F = 91.64, p < 0.001, η²p = 0.73) and time × group interaction (F = 6.91, p = 0.013, η²p = 0.17), but no significant group main effect (p = 0.335). Simple effects analysis showed both groups improved significantly from baseline (VR: p < 0.001; control: p < 0.001), though direct between-group comparisons at post-intervention were non-significant. To further elucidate these effects, we conducted independent samples t-tests on change scores, revealing significantly greater improvement in the VR group (Δ Mean ± SD = 3.61 ± 1.75) versus controls (Δ Mean ± SD = 2.05 ± 1.80; t (34) = 2.63, p = 0.013). The between-group effect size was d = 0.54 with 95% CI [0.07, 1.01].

The mixed ANOVA revealed significant effects of time (F = 41.75, p < 0.001, η²p=0.55) and a time×group interaction (F = 6.42, p = 0.016, η²p=0.16) on dynamic mediolateral displacement, though no main effect of group was observed (p = 0.16). Follow-up analyses showed both the VR training and control groups demonstrated significant improvements, with VR training producing substantially greater gains (d = 1.53,95% CI [0.95,2.11], p < 0.001) compared to the control group (d = 0.60,95% CI [0.14,1.06], p = 0.009). While groups were equivalent at baseline (p = 0.98), post-intervention comparisons revealed VR training resulted in significantly larger dynamic mediolateral displacement with a medium-to-large effect size advantage (d = 0.75,95% CI [0.23,1.27], p = 0.03) over the control group. However, neither the time × group interaction effect (p = 0.12) nor the group effect (p = 0.50) reached statistical significance.

The within-group comparisons revealed no significant changes in sway velocity or static medio-lateral displacement in either group (both p > 0.05). Notably, the VR-based balance training group showed significant reductions in the COP anterior-posterior displacement during static sitting (Z = -2.06, p = 0.039) after four weeks of intervention, whereas there was no significant change in the control group (Z = -1.89, p = 0.058).

For secondary outcomes, MBI improved significantly in both groups (time effect: F (1,34) = 175.20, p < 0.001), with the VR group showing numerically greater gains (d = 2.15 vs. control d = 1.20) but no significant interaction and group effects (both p > 0.05).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the effects of VR-based balance training compared with traditional balance training on trunk control, sitting balance, and daily living activities among subacute stroke patients. The findings indicated that after four weeks of training, both patient groups exhibited significant enhancements in static and dynamic sitting balance, as well as daily living activities. Specifically, the VR-based balance training demonstrated a significantly superior effect on trunk control of the frontal plane in a dynamic sitting compared to traditional balance training.

Our findings aligned with the research conducted by Lee et al. [27], which revealed that game-based VR canoe paddling training significantly enhanced the sitting balance of subacute stroke patients. In the study by Lee et al. [27], the control group underwent routine rehabilitation, whereas the experimental group incorporated VR canoe paddling training into their standard rehabilitation plan. Previous research has indicated that increasing the intensity of training can lead to functional improvements in stroke patients [28, 29]. Consequently, it remains inconclusive whether the observed improvements in this study can be attributable to the VR training itself or the augmented treatment intensity.

Our findings differed from those of Sheehy et al. [30], who reported that 10 ∼ 12 sessions of 30-minute VR sitting balance training did not significantly enhance sitting balance, as assessed by the Function in Sitting Test, the Ottawa Sitting Scale, and the Reaching Performance Scale. In our study, however, we conducted 30-minute VR training session over a total of 20 sessions, effectively doubling the intervention time. Furthermore, according to Sheehy et al. [30], the control group also participated in VR training, albeit limited to trunk movements, which constrained the evaluation of the overall sitting balance effect of VR training.

The VR-based balance training group demonstrated a significant improvement in frontal plane trunk control ability during the dynamic sitting balance test. However, no significant between-group differences were observed in sagittal plane trunk control during dynamic sitting balance tasks, despite the VR group’s improvements in static sitting. This discrepancy may be attributed to the training posture utilized in VR-based training. Specifically, patients sat on a chair with a backrest, with their feet firmly on the ground, which provided support for sagittal torso control tasks and potentially limited improvement in this area. Conversely, the absence of external support in the frontal plane required higher trunk stability, making dynamic tasks more challenging and resulting in more pronounced improvements through VR-based balance training. Therefore, future research should seek to minimize the potential influence of the training environment configuration on trunk control, while ensuring the safety of participants. Furthermore, VR balance training demonstrates measurable benefits for sagittal plane trunk control during static sitting. This effect likely stems from the system’s ability to provide immediate visual feedback during training activities. As patients perform controlled movements to interact with virtual objects, they develop enhanced awareness and ability to adjust for subtle trunk movements, an effect that conventional balance training with therapist-provided verbal feedback typically cannot replicate.

Frontal trunk control predominantly relies on the lateral flexors of the trunk, which facilitate weight transfer through concentric contractions on the ipsilateral side and eccentric contractions on the contralateral side. Effective control of the lateral trunk muscles is vital for achieving stability during lateral reaching movements. Optimal control of the trunk lateral flexors is necessary to maintain pelvic stability and manage body weight within the limits of balance stability during sideways arm reach-outs [31]. Tessem et al. [5] reported that stroke patients exhibited weight-shifting disorders during sitting extension tasks, characterized by a diagonal forward movement towards the less affected side. Similarly, Wiskerke et al. [32] measured the maximum voluntary weight shifts in both diagonal and lateral directions while sitting and found that the COP distance in non-ambulatory patients was significantly smaller than in the healthy control group. Moreover, they observed a moderate to high correlation between the COP distance and the clinical measurements. Lateral balance in stroke patients was more severely impaired compared to anterior-posterior balance [33]. Compared to healthy controls, ambulatory subacute stroke patients display a reduced ability to move their weight diagonally toward the affected side while standing [34]. Therefore, improving the control of the lateral and diagonal trunk is a critical objective in stroke rehabilitation.

The current study revealed significant improvements in trunk control following both interventions, as indicated by large effect sizes across all TIS components. Notably, VR-based training demonstrated superior efficacy in enhancing dynamic balance, supported by a statistically significant time × group interaction and a moderate between-group effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.54). While between-group differences in total TIS scores did not achieve statistical significance, the VR group exhibited clinically meaningful improvements, with mean gains (+ 5.67 points; d = 1.72) approaching twice the established minimal clinically important difference (MCID = 3 points) [35], compared to conventional training (+ 4.17 points; d = 1.07). These results indicate that VR training appears particularly effective for improving dynamic trunk control, with possible secondary benefits for general postural stability. However, these preliminary findings require validation through larger-scale clinical trials.

VR-based balance training was as effective as traditional balance training in enhancing the sitting balance, trunk control, and activities of daily living of stroke patients. Additionally, VR offers diverse and engaging gaming scenarios, which can increase patients’ motivation and participation, leading to higher training frequency and intensity. The difficulty of VR training can be personalized and adjusted according to each patient’s abilities and needs, making it more targeted. Therefore, VR is a viable and efficient training technique, which is of great significance for improving the sitting balance and trunk control of stroke patients. It is worth being widely adopted in clinical practice.

This study has several methodological limitations. Firstly, participants were not stratified by baseline disease severity or balance dysfunction. The exclusion of individuals unable to maintain sitting for > 30 s resulted in a functionally homogeneous cohort, potentially limiting our assessment of VR training’s therapeutic efficacy across the full spectrum of balance impairments. Secondly, all participants received concurrent inpatient multidisciplinary rehabilitation alongside the assigned VR or conventional balance training interventions. Given the individualized nature of rehabilitation programs, it was not possible to fully control for potential confounding effects on the outcomes. Thirdly, the use of a backrest during VR training, though crucial for safety compliance, may have constrained sagittal-plane trunk mobility, creating a task-specificity discrepancy with the control group’s multidirectional exercises. Finally, the absence of long-term follow-up data limits definitive conclusions regarding the sustained benefits of VR training and its functional gains. Consequently, the generalizability of these findings should be interpreted with caution. Future studies should employ stratified randomization, increase sample sizes to enhance statistical power, and incorporate extended follow-up periods to strengthen the validity of the results.

Conclusion

While both interventions similarly improved trunk control, sitting balance, and ADL, VR-based training demonstrated superior efficacy in enhancing lateral trunk control during dynamic tasks, suggesting its potential advantage for facilitating dynamic postural adjustments in stroke rehabilitation. It offers a perspective for personalized rehabilitation programs tailored to stroke patients with balance dysfunction.
