A Novel Technique Using Flash 2.0 Catheter for Percutaneous Aspiration of Native Tricuspid Valve Endocarditis Caused by a Hemodialysis Venous Catheter
Authors: Kelsey Stanford, Ashar Pirzada, Frederic Paulin, Janet Ngu, Scott Harris, Tiffany Furneaux, Mohammad Almutawa
Published: 2026

Right-sided infective endocarditis (RSIE) secondary to central venous catheter (CVC) infection is traditionally treated with prolonged antimicrobial therapy and, in selected cases, cardiac surgery. Percutaneous mechanical aspiration (PMA) has emerged as a less invasive adjunctive therapeutic option in selected patients. We describe the case of a 43-year-old man with a large right atrial/tricuspid valve vegetation from CVC infection treated with antimicrobials and PMA, achieving ∼ 80% vegetation reduction with continued regression on follow-up transesophageal echocardiography (TEE). The patient remained asymptomatic with negative blood cultures postprocedure and at 6 months. This case adds to the evidence supporting PMA as an adjunctive treatment for CVC-associated RSIE.

CVC used for hemodialysis increases the risk of bloodstream infections, with Staphylococcus aureus being the most common causative organism.1 Catheter-related injury to the tricuspid valve (TV) may predispose patients to RSIE, an uncommon but serious condition accounting for 5%-10% of all infective endocarditis cases.1

Management of RSIE traditionally consists of prolonged antimicrobial therapy with cardiac surgery reserved for select patient cases. However, PMA has emerged as a less invasive adjunctive treatment for RSIE of the TV, with early studies demonstrating favourable procedural success and outcomes comparable with cardiac surgery.2

Case History

A 43-year-old man was transferred to a tertiary care hospital with a large right atrial (RA) and TV vegetation identified on cardiac computed tomography (CT) and TEE (Fig. 1A). His past medical history was notable for hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and posttraumatic stress disorder. He had a 30-pack-year smoking history and reported daily cannabis and tobacco use. He had no history of intravenous drug use.

The patient initially presented with respiratory failure secondary to influenza A infection, requiring intubation and mechanical ventilation. Pre- and postintubation chest radiography and contrast-enhanced CT imaging of the neck and chest showed no pulmonary emboli. The admission was complicated by MRSA ventilator associated pneumonia and an acute kidney injury necessitating intermittent hemodialysis. His course was further complicated by septic shock, infusion reactions to vancomycin and daptomycin, and critical illness myopathy. He recovered and was discharged with a right internal jugular (RIJ) tunneled hemodialysis catheter in situ to continue outpatient hemodialysis.

Approximately 1 month later, he presented again with fever, rigours, fatigue, purulent discharge from his dialysis catheter site, and right-sided ear and jaw pain. Blood cultures again grew MRSA bacteremia and, in consultation with infectious disease, he was treated with a 4-week course of linezolid (600 mg twice daily). Blood cultures were persistently positive despite initial improvement. Follow-up CT chest with contrast demonstrated a large TV vegetation in the setting of persistent catheter-related infection.

TEE revealed a large, mobile vegetation measuring approximately 37 × 15 mm, with attachment to both the RA free wall and TV and mild tricuspid regurgitation. Cardiac CT demonstrated a 41 × 29-mm mass protruding into the right ventricle, along with multiple pulmonary septic emboli. Given the size of the vegetation and ongoing embolic risk, the case was reviewed by the multidisciplinary heart team. The patient was referred for PMA and removal of the tunneled dialysis line.

Procedure Details

PMA was performed under conscious sedation in the cardiac catheterization laboratory of a tertiary care centre. A 10F femoral venous sheath was inserted under ultrasound guidance, and an intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) catheter (Abbott Labs, N Chicago, IL) was advanced into the RA for procedural guidance. After careful review of the preprocedural CT and TEE, the RIJ vein was determined to provide a more coaxial approach to the TV than the femoral route. Under ultrasound guidance, a 6F RIJ sheath was inserted, and 2 Perclose ProGlide (Abbott) devices were predeployed at the 10 and 2 o’clock positions before serial dilation with 8F, 12F, and 14F dilators. An 18F, 33-cm DrySeal sheath (WL Gore & Associates, Newark, DE) was then advanced into the RIJ vein. Systemic anticoagulation was achieved with intravenous heparin to maintain an activated clotting time of > 250 seconds.

A Lightning Flash 2.0 16F aspiration catheter (Penumbra, Alameda, CA) was then advanced under fluoroscopic and ICE guidance. The catheter was positioned along the atrial aspect of the TV free wall, where a large vegetation was identified. Mechanical aspiration was performed, achieving approximately 80% reduction in vegetation burden (Fig. 1B).

After successful vegetation debulking, the tunneled RIJ catheter was removed in the catheterization laboratory. The predeployed Perclose sutures were secured to achieve hemostasis of the RIJ access site, while the 10F femoral venous sheath was removed with hemostasis achieved by manual compression. Estimated blood loss was approximately 500 mL.

Results

The patient tolerated the procedure well, with no procedural complications. Immediate postprocedural limited transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated no significant tricuspid regurgitation, preserved TV structure and function, and no definite residual vegetation.

On day 1 of follow-up, TEE revealed a small residual echogenic focus attached to the RA free wall measuring 9 × 5 mm, representing a substantial reduction compared with preprocedural imaging. The patient was discharged home after 48 hours and followed with serial laboratory testing and imaging. The infectious disease team recommended a 4-week course of antimicrobial therapy after the procedure.

At 6-week follow-up, repeat TEE demonstrated further reduction in the residual echogenic focus to 7 × 3 mm (Fig. 1C), indicating continued interval improvement. The patient remained clinically asymptomatic, with negative blood cultures.

At 1 year postprocedure, the patient has remained free of recurrent infection, with no evidence of relapse on clinical assessment, laboratory testing, or repeat blood cultures.

Discussion

This case demonstrates a novel, minimally invasive approach to the management of a large RA/TV vegetation secondary to hemodialysis-related CVC infection using the Lightning Flash 2.0 16F system for off-label PMA. Notably, the procedure was performed under conscious sedation with continuous ICE guidance, avoiding general anesthesia and providing real-time intracardiac imaging while maintaining hemodynamic stability. Unlike TEE, ICE enabled continuous intraprocedural visualization without the need for endotracheal intubation, while TEE was used before and after the procedure to assess vegetation burden and confirm preservation of TV integrity.

In contemporary practice, procedural success in PMA of valvular vegetations is generally defined as a > 70% reduction in vegetation burden.3,4 A systematic review using the AngioVac system reported complete vegetation removal in 24.6% of cases, > 70% reduction in 33.8%, and < 70% reduction in only 4.6%.1,2 In the present case, approximately 80% debulking was achieved, consistent with a clinically successful outcome and within the higher range of reported efficacy. In addition, in our case, favourable superior vena cava-to-lesion alignment identified on preprocedural CT and TEE allowed the RIJ approach to provide a direct, coaxial trajectory to the vegetation, facilitating catheter engagement without the need for additional steering maneuvers.

Emerging data from other aspiration systems further support the feasibility and safety of PMA in RSIE. A retrospective series of 5 patients treated with the FlowTriever system (Inari Medical, Irvine, CA) reported a 100% procedural success rate without complications.5 Similarly, an AngioVac (AngioDynamics, Latham, NY) series in 11 patients with intravenous drug use–associated infective endocarditis demonstrated a 72% success rate, with only 2 patients requiring subsequent surgical intervention.6 Last, the newer AlphaVac system (AngioDynamics), which uses a cannula similar to the AngioVac but employs a manual aspiration system with a volume-limited aspiration handle, has also shown success in the treatment of RSIE, demonstrating relatively low blood loss.7 In a 2-patient case series of TV IE secondary to intravenous drug use, the AlphaVac achieved complete vegetation removal without worsening tricuspid regurgitation and was associated with a hemoglobin decrease of < 1 g/dL.7 Collectively, these findings suggest that PMA may offer an effective alternative or adjunct to surgery in selected patients with TV endocarditis and may be effective across a spectrum of infective organisms and valve substrates.

Reported complications of PMA include pulmonary embolism, persistent bacteremia, worsening tricuspid regurgitation, recurrence of vegetation, perforation, and bleeding.1 The Lightning Flash 2.0 16F system used in this case incorporates several design features that may help mitigate some of these risks. The system’s stainless-steel, laser-cut hypotube provides near 1:1 torqueability, while the atraumatic tip enhances trackability and facilitates controlled catheter navigation without direct mechanical engagement of the vegetation, potentially reducing the risk of valvular injury or embolization. In addition, the integrated suction-sensing technology modulates aspiration and may limit unnecessary blood loss during noncontact suction.3 These features may be particularly advantageous in cases with large, mobile vegetations, multiple attachment sites, or involvement across the TV, as seen in our patient.

Finally, the ability to perform the procedure under conscious sedation, without the need for extracorporeal circulation or cell salvage systems, represents a practical advantage. This approach may expand procedural feasibility within the cardiac catheterization laboratory while minimizing procedural complexity, resource use, and patient morbidity.

Conclusions

This case highlights a novel application of PMA using the Lightning Flash 2.0 16F system for the management of RSIE involving a large TV vegetation. PMA represents a less invasive approach, with the potential for reduced procedural morbidity, shorter antimicrobial therapy, and more favourable clinical outcomes in appropriately selected patients.
