Growth responses of precious tree species with different plant characteristics to three lithological soil types and their interaction effects
Authors: Xingwu Zhou, Xiaoli Wei, ShiCheng Su, XuTao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBased on specific nutrient requirements, precise tree species-site matching is the cornerstone of effective silviculture. To explore plant-soil interactions, this study investigated the nutrient utilization strategies of three tree species with distinct traits (Ormosia hosiei Hemsl. et Wils, Ormosia henryi Prain, and Phoebe bournei (Hemsl.) Yen C. Yang) cultivated in soils derived from slate, sandstone, and coal-bearing sandy shale. We further analyzed the interactive effects between plant characteristics and lithological substrates. Results indicated that cultivation significantly altered soil physicochemical properties. Notably, O. hosiei significantly increased soil organic matter in slate and sandstone (p < 0.05) while minimizing AN consumption in these two soil types. Lithological soils significantly influenced seedling growth, nutrient utilization, and biomass accumulation. Although all species performed optimally in coal-bearing sandy shale, their driving soil factors varied. Redundancy and correlation analyses revealed that soil AK and BD drove O. hosiei growth by regulating leaf-root resource allocation and nutrient uptake. For O. henryi, growth was co-limited by AK, BD, and AP. It adapted to the soil environment by optimizing an “aboveground nitrogen (SNR) and underground phosphorus (RPR)” allocation pattern to achieve trait differentiation. In contrast, the growth of P. bournei was primarily driven by soil pH. It regulated inter-organ phosphorus partitioning and whole-plant NUE, and PUE via pH-mediated nutrient availability, thereby maintaining growth homeostasis across different environments. This study systematically elucidates the adaptive strategies and nutrient allocation mechanisms of different species across varied lithological soils. These findings provide a robust theoretical framework for precise species-site matching and efficient plantation management during the structural adjustment of degraded coniferous forests in southern China.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09524-4.Keywords: Lithological soil, Precious tree, Plant trait variation, Nutrient utilization characteristics, Interaction effects.

Introduction

Water and nutrients essential for plant life are primarily derived from the soil [1, 2]. Factors such as nutrient availability, temperature, light, and water collectively constrain plant growth and play vital roles in governing plant development and physiological processes [3, 4]. Parent rock lithology serves as the fundamental basis for soil formation. Soils derived from different lithologies exhibit substantial differences in their physicochemical properties and mineralogical composition. These differences influence the inherent fertility of the soil and, consequently, plant growth performance [5–7]. Nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K) are widely recognized as key limiting elements for plant growth and are major determinants of community-level primary productivity [8–10]. Plant roots provide structural stability and are responsible for the absorption and transport of water and nutrients to sustain growth [11, 12]. The root uptake process, mediated by root exudates and associated microbial activity, can induce changes in soil properties—including bulk density, pH, water-holding capacity, and cation exchange capacity. Such alterations subsequently affect the speciation and bioavailability of soil nutrients, thereby creating a critical feedback mechanism that influences plant nutrient acquisition, utilization, and overall growth [13, 14].

In natural environments, plants exhibit remarkable adaptability and employ strategic responses to cope with varied soil conditions. They optimize nutrient uptake and utilization by adjusting morphological and physiological traits such as leaf development, root architecture, and resource allocation [15–17]. For example, under limited soil resource availability, plants increase biomass allocation to roots and expand root surface area to enhance water and nutrient capture [18]. Concurrently, plants translocate nutrients from senescing tissues to new leaves to support carbon assimilation [17]. In arid environments, plants often invest more nitrogen in leaves to offset drought-induced declines in photosynthetic rate [19]. These strategies collectively enhance nutrient use efficiency (NUE), enabling plants to produce more organic matter with fewer nutrients—an important survival and adaptation mechanism in changing environments [17].

“Matching tree species to suitable sites” is a fundamental principle in plantation cultivation. Studying how different tree species respond to soils derived from various lithologies and their interactive effects provides a scientific basis for aligning species with specific nutrient requirements to appropriate lithological soil types—an essential approach to implementing the “right tree for the right site” principle. In forestry practice, understanding species-specific adaptation to lithological soils has commonly been approached by planting the same tree species across different lithological substrates and comparing their growth and physiological performance. A limitation of this method, however, is the difficulty in ensuring comparable site conditions within the same ecological region, which hinders a controlled comparison of lithology-specific effects. A more rigorous alternative is to conduct planting trials under uniform conditions using soils developed from distinct lithological sources. This approach offers an effective means to examine tree responses and adaptations to lithological variation in isolation from confounding environmental factors. To date, this experimental ecological methodology has been applied to study species such as Pinus massoniana Lamb., Gleditsia sinensis Lam., and Camphora bodinieri (H.Lév.) Y.Yang, Bing Liu & Zhi Yang, enabling the identification of suitable lithological soil types for their optimal growth [20, 21].

In China, monoculture plantations of coniferous species such as P. massoniana and C. lanceolata are widely established due to their rapid growth, high commercial value, and substantial carbon sequestration potential. However, their simplified stand structure and low biodiversity often lead to soil degradation, declining productivity, and deterioration of forest habitat quality [22]. Therefore, restructuring existing monocultures by introducing ecologically and economically valuable broadleaved species is crucial for improving stand structure, enhancing plantation quality, and strengthening forest ecosystem services—representing a key step toward multifunctional and sustainable plantation management in China. Valuable native broadleaved species such as Ormosia hosiei Hemsl. et Wils, Ormosia henryi Prain, and Phoebe bournei (Hemsl.) Yen C. Yang are important candidates for improving monoculture plantations of C. lanceolata and P. massoniana in southern China. These coniferous plantations are mainly established on yellow soils derived from slate, sandstone, and coal-bearing sandy shale. However, the adaptation and response of these three broadleaved species to such lithological soils, their influence on soil properties after planting, the interactions between species traits and lithological substrates, and the optimal lithological soil type for each species remain unclear. This knowledge gap severely constrains the effective use of these valuable broadleaved species in transforming low-quality and low-efficiency coniferous monocultures.

Although lithology serves as the foundation for regulating soil physicochemical properties and nutrient availability, the response mechanisms of different tree species to this lithological heterogeneity depend largely on their specific plant characteristics. Plant characteristics refer to any morphological, physiological, or phenological features that directly or indirectly influence individual fitness, resource acquisition, and ecosystem functioning [23]. These features, ranging from aboveground leaf phenology to belowground nutrient absorption by roots, dominate how tree species adapt to heterogeneous environmental pressures and regulate plant–soil feedbacks [24, 25]. While the importance of feedbacks mediated by plant characteristics is recognized as significant, the understanding of their interactions with lithological soils remains insufficient. O. hosiei, O. henryi, and P. bournei are precious broadleaved tree species for stand structural adjustment, and they possess certain variations in their characteristics. Specifically, O. hosiei and O. henryi are leguminous tree species capable of forming symbiotic relationships with rhizobia to achieve biological nitrogen fixation. In contrast, P. bournei is a non-leguminous tree species, which mainly relies on the symbiosis between roots and mycorrhizal fungi to acquire nutrients. In addition, even within the same leguminous genus, the resource allocation of O. hosiei and O. henryi under the same environment will exhibit distinctly contrasting sensitivities. Choosing these three tree species with certain variations in their characteristics provides an ideal comparative framework for testing how different plant characteristics adjust adaptability and nutrient utilization strategies under lithological soil heterogeneity.

To address this, we conducted a controlled simulated field experiment under uniform ecological conditions using two-year-old container seedlings of O. hosiei, O. henryi, and P. bournei planted in soils derived from slate, sandstone, and coal-bearing sandy shale. This study was conducted to examine the growth responses and nutrient accumulation strategies of the three tree species across three lithological soil types, to analyze the effects of planting species with different traits on the properties of these soils, and to reveal the interactions between plant characteristics and lithological substrates while identifying the most suitable soil type for each species. The findings aim to provide a scientific basis for selecting the optimal tree species to improve low-quality C. lanceolata and P. massoniana stands growing on varied lithological soils.

Materials and methods

Study site

The experiment was conducted at the Ecological Experimental Station of the College of Forestry, Guizhou University, located in Huaxi District, Guiyang City in the central part of Guizhou Province, China (104°34′ E, 26°34′ N; elevation: 1100 m). The region features a subtropical humid monsoon climate characterized by mild winters and cool summers. The mean annual temperature is 15.3 °C with average summer and winter temperatures of 23.2 °C and 4.6 °C, respectively. The highest and lowest recorded temperatures are 35.1 °C and − 7.3 °C. The average annual precipitation is 1178.3 mm [26].

Soil types

The experimental soils were sampled in 2018 from the B horizon of three parent materials: slate, sandstone, and coal-bearing sandy shale soil. These substrates are hereafter designated as “slate soil (B),” “sandstone soil (S),” and “coal-bearing sandy shale soil (M),” respectively.

Slate soil was collected from Guankou Village, Jianhe County, Qiandongnan Prefecture, Guizhou Province; coal-bearing sandy shale soil was collected from Pengguan Village, Maiping Town, Huaxi District, Guiyang City; and sandstone soil was collected from Mengguan Township, Huaxi District, Guiyang City. Prior to transportation to the experimental site, all soils were sieved through a 5-mm mesh at the collection sites. Subsequently, based on the experimental design, the three soil types were placed into planting beds (6 m × 1 m × 0.5 m) constructed from cement bricks, respectively. Nine planting beds were prepared for each soil type. The physicochemical properties of the three lithological soils are presented in Table 2.

Experimental design

In November 2021, uniformly growing two-year-old container seedlings of O. hosiei, O. henryi, and P. bournei were transplanted into the three lithological soil types: slate (B), sandstone (S), and coal-bearing sandy shale (M). For each soil type, each species was allocated to three independent planting beds, serving as three biological replicates. Within each independent bed, 20 seedlings were planted at a spacing of 50 × 50 cm to establish a uniform growth environment. During the experiment, seedlings were watered every six days (with scheduling adjusted in case of rainfall) and subjected to routine weeding. To accurately evaluate the effect of natural soil fertility on seedling establishment, no fertilizer was applied throughout the experimental period. After one year of growth (November 2022), seedlings were sampled to determine growth parameters and nutrient status across each treatment.

Indicator measurement

Soil physicochemical properties

In November 2021, soil cores were collected from the planting beds using a cutting ring sampler and transported to the laboratory for the determination of bulk density and field capacity following the method of Bai et al. [27]. At the same time, soil samples from the 0–20 cm depth were obtained using a five-point sampling method. Gravel, plant roots, and other debris were removed, and the samples were homogenized before being brought to the laboratory. The soils were air-dried, ground, and passed through a 0.149 mm sieve. A weighed subsample of this fine soil was used to determine soil organic matter by the potassium dichromate oxidation method. For the analysis of plant-available nutrients, the air-dried soil was ground and passed through a 2 mm sieve. Soil pH, alkali-hydrolyzable nitrogen, available phosphorus, and available potassium were then determined using the potentiometric method, alkali diffusion method, ammonium chloride–hydrochloric acid extraction method, and ammonium acetate extraction–flame photometry, respectively, according to Bao [28] .

Growth parameters and leaf area

At the conclusion of the experiment, the height (measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a measuring tape) and ground diameter (measured to the nearest 0.01 cm using a digital vernier caliper) of all seedlings were recorded. From each independent planting bed (replicate), five representative plants were selected as sub-samples, carefully excavated, and washed with clean water to remove soil from the roots before being blotted dry with absorbent paper. The root systems were scanned using a root scanning system (WinRHIZO Pro 2004), and the total root length and total root surface area per sub-sampled plant were analyzed using the LA-S series plant image analysis system. For each plant, three leaves with consistent growth position, orientation, and maturity were selected, and their leaf area was measured using an EPSON V700 Photo scanner coupled with the WinSEEDLE image analysis system.

Following the root and leaf area scans, the roots, stems, and leaves of each sub-sampled plant were oven-dried at 105 °C for two hours to halt enzymatic activity, then dried at 65 °C to constant mass. The dry mass of roots, stems, and leaves was weighed to the nearest 0.01 g, and the mass ratio of each organ was calculated according to the formulas provided in Table S1. All physiological and morphological data obtained from the five sub-sampled plants within each planting bed were averaged to yield a single representative mean value per replicate (n = 3).

Seedling mineral nutrients

After biomass measurement, the root, stem, and leaf samples were separately ground and passed through a 2 mm sieve. The nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K) concentrations in each organ were determined following sulfuric acid digestion and subsequent analysis by the diffusion method, molybdenum-antimony anti-colorimetric method, and flame photometry, respectively [10, 28]. Based on the nutrient concentrations and organ biomass, parameters including the organ nutrient ratio, nutrient content, whole-plant nutrient concentration, nutrient uptake, and nutrient use efficiency were calculated according to the methods described by Chen et al., Soratto et al., and Zhang et al. [10, 29, 30]. The specific calculation formulas are provided in Table S1.

Data processing

All indicators were subjected to two-way analysis of variance (Two-way ANOVA) and Duncan’s multiple range test using SPSS 20.0. Figures were generated using Origin 2023 and Excel 2023. Redundancy analysis (RDA) was performed using Canoco 5.0 to reveal the relationships between soil environmental factors and tree growth, nutrient utilization, and resource allocation. Concurrently, another RDA coupled with forward selection and permutation tests was executed to systematically screen and identify the critical biological allocation traits and nutrient characteristics driving the growth of each tree species. In this second analysis, seedling growth parameters (height and ground diameter increments) were used as response variables, while organ resource allocation ratios and nutrient traits served as explanatory variables. Prior to the respective RDA executions, environmental variables, plant allocation traits, and nutrient characteristics were all screened using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) to iteratively exclude variables with severe multicollinearity (VIF > 10) (Tables S5, S6). Following these steps, Pearson correlation analysis was performed to untangle the pairwise relationships among soil properties, organ resource allocation, and nutrient use efficiencies. Results

Effects of different lithological soils on the growth of the three seedling species

The growth of seedlings of the three tree species was significantly affected by lithological soil type, tree species, and their interaction (p < 0.05) (Table 3). Specifically, for O. hosiei, the increases in height and ground diameter were highest under the coal-bearing sandy shale soil treatment and lowest in the sandstone soil; the former increased by 44.5% and 68.4% compared with the latter, respectively. Under the slate soil treatment, the leaf area of O. hosiei reached its maximum, whereas its total root length and root surface area exhibited the opposite trend. Significant differences were observed in various growth parameters of O. henryi across the different lithological soils. Except for leaf area, which was lowest in the sandstone soil, all other parameters reached their minimum values under the slate soil treatment, and all parameters peaked in the coal-bearing sandy shale soil treatment. For P. bournei, significant variations were noted in height increase, leaf area, and root development parameters across the different lithological soils; all of these parameters reached their maximum values under the coal-bearing sandy shale soil treatment (10.1 cm, 1303.4 mm², 151.71 cm, and 20.14 cm², respectively), followed by the slate soil. Furthermore, within the same lithological soil type, the height increase and leaf area of O. hosiei were significantly higher than those of O. henryi and P. bournei. Conversely, the total root length and root surface area of P. bournei were significantly greater than those of O. hosiei and O. henryi (except for the root growth parameters for O. hosiei under the sandstone soil treatment).

Effects of different lithological soils on biomass accumulation and allocation of the three tree species seedlings

Effects on biomass accumulation

The biomass accumulation and allocation of seedlings of the three tree species varied significantly across the different lithological soils. Total biomass was also significantly affected by lithological soil type, tree species, and their interaction (Fig. 1) (p < 0.05). For O. hosiei, the stem and root biomass accumulation, as well as total biomass, were on average 28.0% and 11.37% lower, respectively, in the slate soil compared to the other two lithological soils, reaching their maximum values under the sandstone soil treatment. However, its leaf biomass was highest in the slate soil (7.09 g). Except for stem biomass, the biomass accumulation in all organs of O. henryi was highest in the coal-bearing sandy shale soil, followed by the sandstone soil, and lowest in the slate soil. Notably, the root and total biomass accumulation of O. henryi [under the coal-bearing sandy shale treatment] were significantly higher than those in the other two soils. For P. bournei, the biomass of all organs and the total biomass reached their maximum accumulation in the coal-bearing sandy shale soil. Except for leaf biomass, all other biomass components accumulated the least in the sandstone lithological soil. Generally, the organ biomass and total biomass across the three species followed the order of O. hosiei > P. bournei > O. henryi. However, an exception was observed for leaf biomass in the sandstone and coal-bearing sandy shale soils, which exhibited a different order: P. bournei > O. henryi > O. hosiei.

Effects on biomass allocation

The biomass allocation of seedlings of the three tree species was significantly affected by lithological soil type, tree species, and their interaction, with the different tree species exhibiting distinct allocation characteristics (Table 4; S2) (p < 0.05). For O. hosiei in the slate soil, biomass allocation to leaves increased while allocation to roots decreased; compared with the sandstone and coal-bearing sandy shale soil treatments, its leaf mass fraction increased by 3.75 and 5 times, respectively, whereas its root mass fraction significantly decreased by 21.95% and 25.58%. Under the slate soil treatment, the stem mass fraction of O. henryi was significantly higher by 29.55% and 58.33% compared to the sandstone and coal-bearing sandy shale soils, respectively. However, its allocation to leaf and root biomass exhibited the opposite trend (M > S > B). For P. bournei, variations in mass allocation were primarily manifested in the leaves and stems; the leaf mass fraction in the slate soil was significantly lower than that in the sandstone and coal-bearing sandy shale soil treatments, while the stem mass allocation showed the opposite trend. Within the same lithological soil type, significant differences in biomass allocation characteristics were observed among the different tree species: O. hosiei exhibited a significantly higher stem mass fraction, O. henryi had the highest root mass fraction, and P. bournei displayed the highest leaf mass fraction.

Effects of the three tree species seedlings on the physicochemical properties of different lithological soils

Different lithological types, tree species, and their interactions significantly affected soil physicochemical properties (Fig. 2) (p < 0.05). Overall, after planting seedlings of the three tree species, the BD of the sandstone soil, the FC of the slate soil, and the AK content of the coal-bearing sandy shale soil increased significantly. Conversely, the AN and AP across all lithological soils, the BD and AK of the slate soil, and the FC of the sandstone soil decreased significantly.

Different uppercase and lowercase letters indicate significant differences at different lithological soil and tree species according to Duncan test, respectively. (p < 0.05)

Following the planting of O. hosiei seedlings, the SOM content in both the slate and sandstone soils increased significantly. After planting O. henryi seedlings, the changes in SOM across the three lithological soils were not significant; however, after planting P. bournei seedlings, the organic matter in the sandstone soil decreased significantly. Furthermore, the changes in AK in the sandstone soil varied depending on the planted species. Planting O. hosiei significantly decreased the AK content, planting P. bournei significantly increased it, whereas planting O. henryi resulted in no significant change. Notably, although the AN content decreased in all lithological soils after planting the three tree species, the magnitude of these changes varied depending on the tree species and lithological soil type. In the slate and sandstone soils, P. bournei consumed the highest amount of AN, followed by O. henryi and then O. hosiei. In contrast, in the coal-bearing sandy shale soil, O. hosiei consumed the highest amount of AN, followed by P. bournei and then O. henryi.

Nutrient utilization characteristics of the three tree species seedlings in response to lithological soils

Responses of seedling nutrient allocation

Seedling nutrient allocation was significantly affected by lithological soil type, tree species, and their interactions, with the three tree species exhibiting distinct nutrient allocation strategies (Fig. 3; S2). For O. hosiei, the allocation ratios of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium in the leaves were all highest in the slate soil, whereas its roots exhibited the exact opposite pattern (p < 0.05). For O. henryi, the nutrient allocation ratios of leaf N, stem N, stem K, and root P were significantly highest in the slate soil, followed by the sandstone soil, and lowest in the coal-bearing sandy shale soil. Conversely, its allocation ratios for leaf P, root N, and root K demonstrated the opposite trend. For P. bournei, the allocation ratios of stem N and root P in the sandstone soil were significantly higher than those in the other soils, whereas its allocation patterns for leaf P, stem P, and root N exhibited the opposite trend.

Responses of nutrient uptake and utilization

Lithological soil type, tree species, and their interaction significantly affected the nutrient uptake and utilization efficiency of the tree seedlings (p < 0.05). Both O. hosiei and O. henryi exhibited the highest N uptake in the coal-bearing sandy shale soil and the lowest in the slate soil. For P. bournei, N uptake was significantly higher in the sandstone soil than in the other two soils. All three species demonstrated maximum P uptake in the sandstone soil and minimum in the coal-bearing sandy shale soil. However, the difference in P uptake for P. bournei between the slate and coal-bearing sandy shale soils was not significant. In contrast, O. hosiei and O. henryi showed significant differences in P uptake across all lithological soils, ranking as: S > B > M. Under the coal-bearing sandy shale soil treatment, K uptake was highest for all three tree species, though the difference in K uptake for O. hosiei between the slate and sandstone soils was not significant. Similarly, P. bournei showed no significant difference in K uptake between the slate and coal-bearing sandy shale soils. Overall, across the different lithological soils, O. hosiei absorbed the highest amounts of N, P, and K, followed by P. bournei, while O. henryi absorbed the least (Table 5).

Relationships between growth, resource allocation, nutrient uptake and utilization of the three tree species seedlings and soil factors

Redundancy analysis (RDA) exploring the relationships of seedling growth, resource allocation, and nutrient uptake and utilization with soil factors explained 83.27%, 79.14%, and 55.41% of the total variance, respectively (Fig. 3A–C). Specifically, AK, and soil BD were the primary factors influencing the growth, resource allocation, and nutrient utilization of O. hosiei. The growth of O. henryi was predominantly affected by soil AK, BD and AP, whereas P. bournei was primarily influenced by pH, and AK (p < 0.05, Fig. 3D–F).

The three tree species exhibited distinct nutrient allocation traits and use characteristics, which subsequently influenced their growth (Fig. 5, Tables S6 and S7). For O. hosiei, growth was significantly affected by SKR, PU, and NUE (Table S7, p < 0.05). Soil BD was significantly and positively correlated with soil AP, PU, NUE, and SKR, but negatively correlated with soil AN. Soil AK showed significant positive correlations with NUE, LNR, and LPR, but negative correlations with RPR and RNR. In addition, soil AP was positively correlated with PU, NUE, and SKR, whereas soil AN was negatively correlated with PU, SKR, and RPR (Fig. 5A). For O. henryi, SNR and NUE were identified as the key allocation traits and nutrient characteristics driving plant growth (Table S7, p < 0.05). Soil BD, AK, and AP were significantly and positively correlated with RPR and NUE, but significantly and negatively correlated with LPR and RNR (Fig. 5B). For P. bournei, growth was significantly related only to SNR (Table S7, p < 0.05). Soil AK was significantly and positively correlated with RNR, and RNR was significantly and negatively correlated with SNR. Furthermore, soil pH showed significant negative correlations with AN, SPR, LPR, NUE, and PUE, but positive correlations with PU, NU, and RPR. Conversely, soil AN, NUE, and PUE exhibited the opposite correlation patterns with these parameters (Fig. 5C).

Discussion

Nutrient uptake and utilization strategies of the three tree species on different lithological soils

In addition to being influenced by biotic and abiotic environmental factors, plant growth is highly species-specific. Different plant types possess unique traits that regulate their growth by influencing soil microbial assembly, plant growth forms, and the allocation and utilization of resources among various organs [31, 32]. For instance, evergreen and coniferous species adapt to oligotrophic environments by maintaining a high carbon-to-nitrogen (C: N) ratio and high nitrogen use efficiency [33]; conifers further limit nitrogen uptake due to their high content of carbon-based secondary compounds [34]; meanwhile, leguminous plants reduce their reliance on soil nitrogen through the nitrogen-fixing action of rhizobia [35]. In this study, soil available potassium (AK) and bulk density (BD) were the key environmental factors influencing the characteristics and growth of O. hosiei (Fig. 4A and D), while SKR, PU, and NUE were the critical indicators driving its growth (Table S7). Specifically, the enrichment of soil AK significantly reduced the cost and allocation of resources to underground roots, which increased the allocation of nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) to the aboveground parts (LNR and LPR). This shift modified the nitrogen use efficiency and subsequently promoted plant growth (Figs. 3 and 5A). Meanwhile, soil BD was closely related to field capacity (FC), further affecting the availability of soil AN and AP, which in turn influenced P absorption by O. hosiei. Under sufficient P conditions, O. hosiei significantly increased its SKR and enhanced its NUE, thereby promoting growth. O. henryi was jointly influenced by soil AK, BD, and AP (Fig. 4B and E), and SNR, LPR, and NUE were the key factors affecting its growth (Table S7). Distinct from O. hosiei, when soil AK was enriched, O. henryi significantly down-regulated RNR and LPR, but promoted SNR and RPR. Concurrently, soil AP content also increased the allocation to SNR and RPR, which enhanced the NUE and subsequently promoted the growth of O. henryi. Under high BD conditions, O. henryi also tended to up-regulate SNR and down-regulate RNR, thereby maintaining its growth status (Fig. 5B). P. bournei was driven by soil pH and AK (Fig. 4C and F), and SNR was the unique key factor influencing its growth (Table S7). A decrease in soil pH led to an increase in soil AN content, which affected the absorption of N and P by P. bournei. This process significantly up-regulated SPR and LPR, down-regulated RPR, and simultaneously enhanced both NUE and PUE, thus maintaining growth homeostasis across different environments. In addition, low soil AK significantly down-regulated RNR, which subsequently increased SNR, constituting a significant driving force for the macro-growth variations of P. bournei (Fig. 5C). The nutrient utilization mechanisms of the three tree species adapting to lithological soils in this study are consistent with theoretical expectations of plants responding to environmental heterogeneity through phenotypic plasticity [15, 16, 36]. Plants form an adaptive suite throughout the processes of nutrient uptake, transport, storage, and utilization via the dynamic adjustment of inter-organ biomass allocation [37] and the precise regulation of nutrient utilization efficiency [38]. This ultimately achieves an optimal nutritional allocation ratio [39–41] and functional equilibrium to fulfilling their growth and developmental needs [17].

Effects of cultivating different tree species on lithological soils

As the primary sessile organisms in terrestrial ecosystems, plants are core biological elements that regulate soil development processes through life activities such as root growth, exudation, and their own chemical properties, which alter soil physicochemical properties and microbial community structures through multiple pathways [42, 43]. Although most experimental studies conclude that plant cultivation helps improve soil structure and increases organic matter and nitrogen content, Smal et al. investigated the effects of afforestation on soil physicochemical properties and found that within the first decade after afforestation, the initial growth phase of plants requires the absorption of nutrients such as N, P, and K from the soil to maintain metabolism and development, leading to a significant decrease in soil C and N contents [44].

This study indicates that the physicochemical properties of the lithological soils exhibited distinct temporal shifts following one year of seedling cultivation (Fig. 1). These alterations in lithological soil properties may represent a coupled outcome of both tree species regulation and abiotic factors (such as irrigation, rainfall, and subsidence). Generally, plants can significantly decrease soil BD and enhance FC through root system expansion and organic matter input [45, 46]. Conversely, processes including daily irrigation, physical subsidence, abiotic leaching, and natural soil compaction can also inevitably alter soil quality metrics [47]. In the current experiment where an unplanted blank control was omitted, the soil property modulations induced by abiotic factors such as irrigation and subsidence cannot be entirely excluded. For the slate soil, although the bulk density was significantly decreased, it was likely because the biological effects of plant roots overrode the abiotic environmental influences; in contrast, the non-significant shifts observed in the coal-bearing sandy shale soil might be attributed to an equilibrium between these two counteracting forces. Furthermore, the increased bulk density and decreased water retention in the sandstone soil were linked to both abiotic processes and the inherent texture of the sandstone itself. Due to a lack of clay particles for structural support, such light-textured soils respond more rapidly to the impacts of these abiotic factors [48]. Regarding soil nutrients, nutrient uptake after planting the three tree species led to a significant decrease in AN and AP across all three lithological soils (Fig. 2). Notably, in contrast to the cultivation of the non-leguminous species (P. bournei), which resulted in a significant decrease in soil organic matter across all lithological soils, the cultivation of the leguminous species (O. hosiei and O. henryi) increased SOM content.

Regarding soil nutrient dynamics, a significant decrease in AN and AP was observed across all three lithological soils after one year (Fig. 2). This downward trend may be driven by the dual pathways of plant biological absorption and physical migration and loss caused by abiotic factors, where repetitive irrigation events and precipitation leaching in an open growth environment may facilitate the downward migration or localized loss of highly mobile nutrient ions [49]. Notably, in contrast to the cultivation of the non-leguminous species (P. bournei), which resulted in a significant decrease in soil organic matter across all lithological soils, the cultivation of the leguminous species (O. hosiei and O. henryi) increased SOM content. This difference is speculatively attributed to the symbiotic nitrogen fixation unique to legumes. Although root nodulation and actual nitrogen fixation rates were not directly quantified in this study, it is well-established that the nitrogen-fixing action of the root nodules formed by O. hosiei and O. henryi converts atmospheric nitrogen into bioavailable forms [10, 50]. This process provides an alternative nitrogen source for the plants [35, 51] and potentially increases soil carbon storage, as nitrogen input can inhibit the microbial decomposition of organic matter. Furthermore, such potential symbiotic nitrogen fixation is speculated to have partially mitigated the leguminous species’ reliance on soil AN, thereby resulting in a significantly lower AN consumption in the slate and sandstone soils planted with leguminous species compared to the non-leguminous treatments.

However, particularly in the coal-bearing sandy shale soil, the decrease in AN in the soil planted with O. hosiei was significantly higher than that in the P. bournei treatment. This phenomenon represents a plausible ecological trade-off; since the N and SOM contents in the coal-bearing sandy shale soil were sufficiently abundant for O. hosiei, the metabolic cost of nitrogen fixation under such luxury conditions might be higher than the cost of directly absorbing soil N. Consequently, we speculate that this economic threshold prompted the plant to reduce energy allocation to biological nitrogen fixation and shift toward direct soil resource extraction [52, 53], though further experimental quantification is required to definitively validate this energetic shift.

Interaction effects between tree species traits and lithological soils and suitable soil environments for cultivation

As one of the primary factors driving soil variation, lithology dictates soil physicochemical properties [7], which subsequently influence plant growth regarding nutrient limitation, mobility, and plant-soil feedback [54, 55]. In this study, seedling growth was significantly affected by lithological soil type, tree species, and their interactions (Table 3), with the three tree species exhibiting significant growth differences under various soil conditions. All three tree species grew well in coal-series sandy shale soils (Table S3). Plants depend heavily on the water, nutrients, and microbial symbiotic environments provided by the soil. Thus, their growth reflects the combined effects of multiple shifting soil properties. The growth variations among the three species were interactively regulated by soil physicochemical properties and the nutrient traits across different plant organs (Figs. 4 and 5, Table S7).

AK was a common key factor influencing the growth and nutrient utilization characteristics of O. hosiei, O. henryi, and P. bournei (Fig. 4). This commonality stemmed from the differences in initial soil AK fertility across different lithologies, as well as the variations in physiological demands for potassium among the three species. Furthermore, potassium mediates the demands of plants during osmotic regulation [56]. Therefore, the three tree species exhibited distinct resource allocation strategies in response to soil AK content to maintain efficient nutrient utilization (Fig. 5). Concurrently, sandstone soil has a limited water-holding capacity due to its lack of clay particles [48]. The resulting water stress forces plants to dynamically adjust their resource allocation [16, 57]. Moreover, FC is closely correlated with soil BD and related nutrient contents. This correlation leads to differences in nutrient availability among different soil lithologies (Table 2), which further influences seedling growth. For O. hosiei, although its growth was co-driven by soil AK and BD, its absorption of soil nutrients was restricted by the limitations of FC. Specifically, the absorption of AP promoted potassium allocation in the stem and enhanced the NUE, which subsequently accelerated growth. Additionally, in sandstone soil with poor water-holding capacity, O. hosiei increased its root length and root surface area to enhance water and nutrient uptake. It also adopted a strategy of reducing leaf area and shifting resources toward stem investment to decrease water dissipation. Consequently, water content and AK became the primary limiting factors. This finding aligns with Tian’s conclusion that soil water content is the most critical factor during the seedling stage of O. hosiei [41]. Although O. henryi belongs to the same genus as O. hosiei, it displayed distinct variations in resource allocation and growth performance in response to the soil environment (Table 3; Fig. 5). This divergence may be due to adaptive differences caused by provenances, interspecific characteristics, and site conditions [58]. When facing different levels of soil AK and AP, O. henryi regulated its growth by adjusting the allocation of N and P within its body via an “aboveground N and underground P” or “aboveground P and underground N” pattern. In contrast, O. hosiei adjusted the N and P allocation between its leaves and roots in response to soil nutrient contents, a process mediated by potassium allocation in the stem. This triggered either an “aboveground N and P” or “underground N and P” pattern, which subsequently affected its growth. The response of P. bournei to soil AK was opposite to that of the legume species, as a high AK environment potentially inhibited its growth (Fig. 5). However, the growth variations of P. bournei across different soil lithologies were relatively small, which might be driven by soil pH. The nutrient absorption of P. bournei was significantly influenced by soil pH. For instance, a lower pH increased soil AN content, and AN significantly promoted P allocation to the aboveground parts. This process also enhanced N and P use efficiencies, thereby maintaining growth homeostasis and allowing the plant to adapt to different environments. Liang et al. pointed out that P. bournei performs better in slightly acidic soils [59]. They proposed that a lower pH environment might promote the colonization of certain arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi in the root system of P. bournei, which enhances nutrient uptake and drives plant growth. Consistent with this conceptual framework, we speculate that under the coal-series sandy shale and slate soils in this study, P. bournei optimized its underground symbiotic network. This optimization likely promoted root development, improved N and P uptake capacities, and cooperatively optimized P allocation among organs. However, the root mycorrhizal colonization rate was not measured in this experiment, and this hypothesis requires further validation in future studies.

Limitations and future perspectives

Although this study systematically clarifies the interactions and differentiated growth strategies between species with distinct traits across various lithological soils, certain limitations remain. First, due to the practical constraints of the nursery experimental setup, an unplanted blank soil control was omitted. This omission prevents the absolute mathematical decoupling of plant-driven modification effects from purely abiotic factors (such as rainfall leaching and natural subsidence over time). Second, the experimental monitoring period was limited to one year. While this captured the critical initial establishment phase of the seedlings, such a short-term timeframe cannot fully reflect the long-term patterns of forest soil evolution and decades-long ecological succession. Third, while our redundancy and correlation findings strongly point toward species-specific ecological trade-offs and nutrient strategic shifts, key underlying biological traits were not directly quantified. These unmeasured traits include the actual root nodulation and nitrogen fixation rates for the leguminous species, and the arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal colonization rates for P. bournei. Consequently, future research involving multi-factor unplanted blocks and long-term field positioning monitoring is highly warranted. Meanwhile, integrating high-throughput microbial amplicon sequencing with isotope tracing techniques to explicitly untangle the dynamic feedback loops of rhizosphere symbiotic networks will provide a more comprehensive theoretical framework for precise “tree-site matching” and forest quality enhancement in southern China.

Conclusions

This study revealed significant interaction effects between plant species traits and soil lithological properties, which jointly regulated the growth and development of seedlings of three precious tree species: O. hosiei, O. henryi, and P. bournei. Following one year of seedling cultivation, the physicochemical properties across all lithological soils exhibited significant temporal shifts. These alterations were interactively modulated by both tree species identity and abiotic factors. The BD of the slate soil decreased and its FC increased, whereas the sandstone soil exhibited increased BD and decreased FC. Furthermore, the available nutrient contents AN and AP decreased significantly across all lithological soils. This downward trend was driven by the dual pathways of plant biological absorption and physical environmental dissipation. Compared with the non-leguminous species P. bournei, the cultivation of the leguminous species O. hosiei and O. henryi elevated the SOM content in the slate and sandstone soils while maintaining lower consumption of AN. The interaction between soil factors and tree species traits collectively regulated seedling growth, with each species exhibiting a distinct growth strategy. Soil AK and BD drove the growth and biomass accumulation of O. hosiei by adjusting resource investment between leaves and roots, as well as NUE. The growth of O. henryi was co-limited by AK, BD, and AP. It adapted to the soil environment by optimizing the allocation of N and P between aboveground parts (leaves, stems) and roots. Moreover, soil pH played a critical role in the growth and biomass accumulation of P. bournei seedlings. It regulated their inter-organ nutrient allocation and promoted growth by optimizing the distribution of phosphorus to the aboveground parts. By integrating plant trait variations and lithological soil properties, this study systematically elucidates the interactions and differentiated growth strategies between species with distinct traits across various lithological soils. These findings provide a robust theoretical framework for precise site selection and species adaptation strategies during the structural adjustment of degraded coniferous forests in southern China.
