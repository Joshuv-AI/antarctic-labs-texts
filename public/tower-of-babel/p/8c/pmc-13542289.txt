Robust SWCNT-OH/GO membranes for scalable recovery of moxifloxacin from high-salinity organic wastewater
Authors: Yechen An, Liang Mei, Ruijie Yang, Jun Cui, Wenhai Zhang, Jing-Long Han, Wenli Jiang, Ruiyun Ren, Xiaoxu Gao, Zhihui He, Haoran Xu, Ruifeng Yan, Quan-Fu An, Zhiling Li, Haoyi Cheng, Yu Tao, Aijie Wang, Zhiyuan Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMembrane-based separation offers a sustainable route for resource recovery from high-salinity organic wastewater, yet graphene oxide (GO) membranes remain constrained by poor selectivity, limited solvent stability, and challenges in scale-up. Here, we report a hydroxylated single-walled carbon nanotube (SWCNT-OH) reinforced GO membrane, in which SWCNT-OH promotes robust inorganic crosslinking with sodium tetraborate (Na2B4O7) to form rapid transport channels for water and ions, while suppressing organic passage. This architecture enhances the moxifloxacin (MXF)/NaCl separation factor more than fivefold (from 4.2 to 22.2) and increases water permeability over fourfold (from 7.2 to 30.1 l m−2 h−1 bar−1). The membrane was scaled up to 11 m2 and deployed for continuous treatment of MXF crystallization mother liquor over seven days, delivering a stable recovery of 10 kg MXF per day under industrially relevant conditions. These findings establish a scalable strategy for the design of robust nanocarbon-based membranes, advancing resource-efficient treatment of high-salinity wastewater.Subject terms: Chemical engineering, Two-dimensional materials

Introduction

Fine chemicals create the beauty of the world, providing pharmaceutical ingredients, pesticides, dyes, pigments, to name but a few1–3. But they churn out high-salt organic wastewater during their involved processes like halogenation, diazotization, and sulfonation4,5. Every year, the world’s chemical plants produce a substantial volume of these effluents. In China, the number is hundreds of millions of tons6. This is a critical issue, as they normally contain high-risk organic compounds, such as halogenated, nitro, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons7,8.

Traditional techniques, like evaporation and incineration, could reduce those burdens9–11. But they are energy-intensive, and encounter challenges regarding their sharp carbon footprint. Also, they are unable to recycle valuable synthetic products9–11. Biochemical techniques and advanced oxidation could dilute carbon emissions to some extent, but still fail in recycling12,13. Membrane separation offers a promising solution, allowing water and inorganic salts to pass through while blocking target organic molecules as wastewater flows across it14, thereby enabling the recovery of these valuable compounds.

The membrane is the core building block of the membrane filter. Its design and development have received significant attention over the past few decades. Breakthroughs have been made and will continue to advance. Notably, graphene oxide (GO) laminated membrane is one of the most promising candidates, due to its cheap and scalable natures15,16. Still, managing GO layer swelling is challenging, which influences the stability and organic matters/inorganic salts separation efficiency15–17. Although cross-linking methods can enhance water stability, challenges with solvent resistance and long-term stability persist18–21.

Herein, we address this issue by laying tiny hydroxylated single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNT-OH) in the GO membrane. The SWCNT-OH/GO membrane excels in permeability, organic/inorganic separation efficiency, solvent resistance, structure stability, and anti-fouling properties, as SWCNT-OH optimizes the interlayer nanochannels, enhances crosslinking, reinforces the membrane structure, and increases the amounts of hydrophilic hydroxyl groups (-OH) on the membrane surface (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1).

Furthermore, we have scaled up our lab design to pilot-scale demonstrations for resource recovery from real high-salt organic wastewater, utilizing a membrane area of up to 11 m2 (single membrane system is 5.5 m2). Our pilot scale system (Three sets of equipment with a total membrane area of 33 m2) can safely run more than 7 days, with a daily processing capacity of 2 tons (2 t) of moxifloxacin (MXF) crystallization mother liquor (Fig. 1b), recovering 10 kg of MXF, yielding a daily economic return of $1,388 USD (Based on the crude recycled MXF price of $138.8 kg−1). This verified the feasibility of our developed SWCNT-OH/GO membrane filter for industrial-scale high-salt organic wastewater treatment, along with recycling target organic molecules.

Results

Membrane preparation and characterizations

The SWCNT-OH/GO membrane was prepared by a modified pressure-driven self-assembly method, followed by a thermal crosslinking treatment (Supplementary Fig. 2, see Methods for details). Specifically, a certain amount (with mass ratios to GO ranging from 0:1 to 1:1) of SWCNT-OH (Supplementary Figs. 3, 4, Supplementary Table 1) and sodium tetraborate (Na2B4O7, an inorganic crosslinking agent) were added to a GO dispersion and applied to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) substrate by a pressure-driven self-assembly process. The resulting membrane was then heat-treated at 90 °C for 2 hours to form -C-O-B- bonds between GO and SWCNT-OH, thus achieving cross-linking of the membrane22 (see Supplementary Fig. 5 for the detailed crosslinking mechanism).

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) and High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images of the membranes’ cross-section show stable separation layers with different stacking orders in all GO and SWCNT-OH/GO membranes (Supplementary Figs. 6, 7). For SWCNT-OH/GO membranes, SWCNT-OHs were effectively integrated within the GO layer, contributing to an increased thickness of the membrane when compared with the pure GO membrane (from 38 nm to 103 nm, Supplementary Fig. 6). Note that the GO membrane displayed a relatively uniform and compact stacking (Supplementary Fig. 7a), whereas the stacking features of the SWCNT-OH/GO membranes varied with the loading amount of SWCNT-OH. When the mass ratio of SWCNT-OH to GO is 0.1:1, SWCNT-OHs were uniform and orderly distributed within the membrane (Supplementary Fig. 7b). At which point, GO tightly wraps around the SWCNT-OHs, creating high-speed channels that facilitate the transport of water and inorganic salts while blocking the passage of organic molecules. However, tuning the mass ratio of SWCNT-OH and GO to 1:1 causes structural disorganization and bending of SWCNT-OHs, leading to their aggregation into bundles with a diameter increased to 5–20 nm (pristine inner diameter is 1.5 nm) (Supplementary Fig. 7c). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) characterizations indicate that the incorporation of SWCNT-OH (with rich in -OH) increases the membrane’s surface -OH density and enhances cross-linking interactions (Supplementary Figs. 8–11). The SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane exhibits the highest -OH content and strongest cross-linking effect, whereas the SWCNT-OH/GO (1:1) membrane shows reduced -OH content and weakened cross-linking effect due to the aggregation of SWCNT-OH into bundles.

Permeability and separation performances

To systematically evaluate the separation performance of SWCNT-OH/GO membranes, probe molecules with varying molecular sizes and charges were selected to assess their size-sieving and electrostatic interactions. We selected polyethylene glycol (PEG3350) as a representative uncharged organic molecule, and selected MXF, Alizarin red S (ARS), methyl blue (MB), and Congo red (CR) as negatively charged organics to evaluate the rejection characteristics of GO and SWCNT-OH/GO membranes. Inorganic salts (Na2SO4, MgSO4, MgCl2, and NaCl) were also included to assess separation performance.

We first systematically investigated the effect of GO nanosheet size on membrane performance. GO nanosheets with larger lateral dimensions (1-5 μm) (Supplementary Fig. 12) were selected to fabricate GO membranes, which exhibited higher organic rejection (Supplementary Fig. 13) and thus facilitated organics/inorganics separation. Subsequently, the influence of the SWCNT-OH/GO ratio on membrane performance was evaluated. Under cross-flow filtration with applied hydraulic pressure (0.5 MPa), a sharp increase in water permeability and organic/inorganic separation efficiency was observed by increasing the SWCNT-OH/GO ratio from 0:1 to 0.1:1 (Fig. 2a, b). The SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane exhibits the highest water permeability (30.1 l m−2 h−1 bar−1), about four times greater than that of the pristine GO membrane (7.2 l m−2 h−1 bar−1) (Fig. 2a). The enhanced water permeability is attributed to the introduction of SWCNT-OHs, which create frictionless, high-speed one-dimensional (1D) channels for water transport. The SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane also demonstrates the highest separation factor between organic matters and inorganic salts, achieving the highest organic rejections (92.8% for PEG3350, 90.2% for MXF, 97.2% for ARS, and 99.9% for both MB and CR) while maintaining the lowest inorganic salt rejections (5.1% for NaCl, 5.9% for MgCl2, 6.2% for MgSO4, and 19.6% for Na2SO4) (Fig. 2b). Further increase the SWCNT-OH/GO ratio from 0.1:1 to 1:1 leads to SWCNT-OH’s aggregation into bundles within the GO layers, which induces SWCNT-OH/GO membrane’ structural disorder and reduces its water permeability and organic/inorganic separation performances (Fig. 2a, b).

Given the sizes of GO interlayer channels (~1 nm) and SWCNT-OH channels (~1.5 nm), the SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane can effectively reject organic molecules larger than 1.5 nm, such as Coomassie brilliant blue (CBB) and Evans blue (EB), with rejection rates above 99% (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 14 and Supplementary Table 2). For molecules smaller than 1.5 nm, size effect towards rejection rate is minimal, but charge effect is large. For example, for negatively charged molecules, including Chromotropic acid disodium (CAD), Eriochrome black T (EBT) and Methyl Orange (MO), the rejection rates are over 90% (Fig. 2c, and Supplementary Fig. 14). Whereas for positively charged molecules, such as Rhodamine B (RB) and Methylene blue (MLB), the rejection rates are much lower (37.1% and 32.8%, respectively). This indicates that the Donnan effect dominates the rejection of negatively charged organic molecules but is less effective for positively charged ones. Hence, when tested in mixed solutions of organic matters and inorganic salts, the SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane not only delivers excellent water permeability (30.1 l m−2 h−1 bar−1) but also achieves the highest rejection rates for negatively charged molecules MXF, MB and CR, thus the separation factors for MXF/NaCl (22.2), MB/NaCl (25.6), and CR/NaCl (24.4) are also the highest (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Table 3). In addition to MXF, MB and CR, the SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane also exhibits high rejection toward a broad range of antibiotics and per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFASs) (>90%) (Supplementary Fig. 15). The combination of outstanding water permeability and exceptional organic/inorganic separation performance makes the SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane the best among all previously reported membranes, including 2D material-based membranes like GO23–28, MoS229, and Mexene30; porous material-based membranes like covalent organic framework (COF)31, metal organic frameworks (MOF)32,33, polymeric membranes34–36; and even commercial polymeric membranes, including nanofiltration (NF270) and ultrafiltration (UE001) membranes (Supplementary Table 4).

Stability is crucial for the long-term operation of GO-based membranes. The SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane demonstrates exceptional robustness, enduring one day of harsh chemical (NaOH/Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate) cleaning without degradation while maintaining effective separation of target organic compounds from organics/inorganics mixed solutions (MB>99% and NaCl<4%) (Supplementary Figs. 16, 17). It also sustains continuous long-term cross-flow filtration operation for 30 days without any performance loss (Supplementary Fig. 18). This outstanding stability is attributed to the cross-linking accomplished by Na2B4O7, it is further reinforced by SWCNT-OHs, which function like steel bars in reinforced concrete to strengthen the separation layer (Supplementary Fig. 5). Moreover, considering the actual operating conditions encountered in real wastewater treatment, varying pH conditions (3-11), salinities (75-300 mM), hydraulic pressures (1-9 bar), and cycle numbers (0-20) were selected to verify the role of SWCNT-OHs in enhancing the structural stability of the SWCNT-OH/GO membrane, confirming the robustness of the SWCNT-OH/GO membrane (Supplementary Figs. 19–25).

We then tested secondary effluent from microbial treatment, a typical MXF-containing wastewater from the chemical pharmaceutical industry with a low pH value (pH 4.26) and high inorganic salts concentration (conductivity 9.35 mS cm−1). Note that since lots of organic matters existed in wastewater, the rejection rates were calculated based on the chemical oxygen demand (COD) before and after membrane filtration. The SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane shows a high organic matter rejection of 75.6%, that comparable to commercial membranes NF270 (77.8%) and UE001 (74.3%) (Fig. 2e). But, it exhibits a much lower inorganic salt rejection rate (8.9%) than NF270 (58.4%) and UE001 (50.8%). As a result, the SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane achieves a much higher separation factor (8.5) for organic matters/inorganic salts than NF270 (1.3) and UE001 (1.5). Unlike commercial NF270 and UE001 membranes that simultaneously concentrate both organic molecules and inorganic salts—thereby complicating the recovery of organics from high-salinity organic wastewater, our SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane can selectively separate target organic molecules from high-salinity organic wastewater, offering an effective strategy for their subsequent recovery and recycling.

Membrane fouling remains a major challenge in separation processes. Practical wastewater (typical MXF-containing wastewater) fouling tests show that the SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane maintains a stable flux during filtration, in contrast to widely used commercial organic membranes (NF270 and UE001), which suffer from severe membrane fouling problem and significant flux decline (Fig. 2f). In organic/inorganic combined fouling scenarios, the SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane also demonstrates superior resistance, with less than 16% flux decrease for the 1st cycle and the 2nd cycle, and then the water flux can recover to 90% of the initial flux after rinse treatment (Supplementary Fig. 26). By comparison, SWCNT/GO (0.1:1) membranes prepared with SWCNT-COOH or SWCNT-NH2 (see method for details) exhibit severe membrane fouling, underscoring the crucial role of SWCNT-OH in enhancing antifouling performance (Supplementary Fig. 26). This is attributed to the abundant and stable -OH on the SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane surface (Supplementary Fig. 27), which form a water molecular shield that prevents the adsorption of organic pollutants, thereby mitigating fouling37. Additionally, the SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane possesses more negative charge and a smoother surface (−40.2 mV, 46.7 nm) than that in the GO membrane (−30.1 mV, 59.2 nm) (Supplementary Figs. 28–30), further reducing the adhesion of organic contaminants. The hydrophilic surface also facilitates easier cleaning, enabling effective performance restoration and extended membrane lifespan (Supplementary Text 1).

Membrane structure characterization for mechanism clarification

To elucidate the separation mechanism of these membranes, low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) was employed to characterize the membrane structures using water as a probe molecule. The transverse relaxation time (T2) distribution can provide insights into the pore architecture (Fig. 3a; detailed results in Supplementary Text 2)32,38. All membranes exhibit similar T2 distributions in the range of 10-10000 ms, indicating nanoscale confinement (10–1000 ms) and free water (1000–10000 ms) (Supplementary Fig. 31)39. In the sub-nanoscale confinement (0.1–10 ms), the GO membrane exhibits a T2 population centered at approximately 0.81 ms (0.1–2 ms range) (Fig. 3a), corresponding to its 2D interlayer channels (channel I)32. In comparison, all SWCNT-OH/GO membranes display an additional T2 population at 2–10 ms range (channel II), attributing to the 1D tube-like pores of SWCNT-OH. For reference, the pristine SWCNT-OH membrane shows a single T2 peak at approximately 3.65 ms. These results revealed that the formation of 1D transport channels capable of transporting water molecules upon incorporation of SWCNT-OHs into the GO membrane.

Compared to the SWCNT-OH membrane, the SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane shows no change in the relaxation time of channel II, while the T2 value of channel I decreased from 0.81 to 0.70 ms. This reduction is consistent with its smaller interlayer spacing of SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane than the GO membrane in the hydrated state, as revealed by the X-ray diffraction (XRD) results (Fig. 3b). At a higher SWCNT-OH content (1:1 ratio), the relaxation time of channel II begins to decrease from 3.16 to 2.74 ms, likely due to SWCNT-OH aggregation. Meanwhile, the channel II peak area increases, indicating the formation of additional tube-like channels. In contrast, the channel I peak area further diminishes due to interlayer blockages, which impede water and ion transport.

The pristine GO membrane exhibits pronounced swelling in both aqueous and organic environments, with interlayer spacing expanding from 7.9 Å (11.24°) to 12.4 Å (7.14°) in pure water and 11.4 Å (7.74°) in a mixed solvent (Fig. 3b, c, Supplementary Figs. 32–34). This structural instability is attributed to the weak crosslinking strength, as revealed by XPS (Fig. 3d), results in limited rejection performance − 72.3% for neutral PEG3350 and 74.2%, 81.5%, 81.1% and 80.6% for negative charged organics MXF, ARS, MB and CR (Fig. 2b). The relatively low rejection of negatively charged species is governed by weak Donnan exclusion effects due to the low negative charged GO membrane (Zeta potential: −30.1 mV) (Supplementary Figs. 28, 29).

In contrast, the SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) composite demonstrates superior anti-swelling properties, with minimal interlayer expansion—from 8.3 Å (10.66°) to 9.2 Å (9.56°) in pure water and 9.0 Å (9.86°) in mixed solvents (Fig. 3b, c, Supplementary Figs. 32–34). This enhanced structural stability results from the formation of strong boron-mediated cross-linkages, where Na2B4O7 reacts with -OH on GO surface and outer walls of SWCNT-OH to form stable -C-O-B- bridges (Fig. 3d). The high hydroxyl content (48.7%) on the SWCNT-OH outer walls provides abundant crosslinking sites (Supplementary Fig. 10), enabling robust covalent bonding between SWCNT-OHs and GO22,40. This is further validated by the strongest COB- and BO- signals in the SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane, as shown in the mass spectra and 3D tomography obtained from Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) (Fig. 3e, f), along with a homogeneous boron (B) distribution revealed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping (Supplementary Fig. 35). The SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane’s stable structure and increased negative charge (Zeta: −40.2 mV, Supplementary Fig. 29) enhance the rejection of negatively charged organics – 99.9% for EB, CBB, CR, and MB (>1.5 nm), and over 90% for MXF, EBT, and MO (<1.5 nm) (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 14).

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) scratch tests further demonstrate that SWCNT-OH incorporation significantly improves mechanical robustness in both aqueous and organic environments. Among the membranes tested, the SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane exhibits the most effective crosslinking, as evidenced by its highest destructive load (3.6-4.5 μN) compared to the GO membrane (0.9 μN) and the SWCNT-OH/GO membrane (1:1) (1.8 μN) (Fig. 3g, h). The critical role of -OH groups in Na2B4O7-mediated crosslinking is further highlighted by comparing the destructive load of SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane with that of SWCNT-NH2/GO (0.1:1) (2-2.5 μN) and SWCNT-COOH/GO (0.1:1) (1-1.5 μN) membranes (Supplementary Fig. 36). Moreover, benefiting from the robust -C-O-B- crosslinking structure, the SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane exhibits enhanced tensile strength, chemical durability and antioxidant properties, as evidenced by its high tensile strength (37.98 Mpa), stable water permeability (30.1 l m−2 h−1 bar−1) and consistent rejection rate (92.8%) for PEG3350 even after destructive cleaning (NaOH/SDS) treatment or exposure to an oxidative environment (H2O2) (Supplementary Figs. 37–39).

However, excessive SWCNT-OH loading in GO membrane (1:1 ratio) disrupts crosslinking efficiency due to SWCNT-OH aggregation, as revealed by the weakened -C-O-B- peak intensity in XPS spectra (Fig. 3d), the reduced COB- and BO- signals in the mass spectrum and 3D TOF-SIMS tomography (Fig. 3e, f), and the increased interlayer spacing (12.4 Å in pure water and 11.6 Å in mixed solvents; Fig. 3b-c, Supplementary Figs. 32–34). Consequently, the rejection rate for neutral PEG3350 decreased from 92.8% in SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane to 74.4% in SWCNT-OH/GO (1:1) membrane (Fig. 2b; detailed results in Supplementary Text 3). Nevertheless, the SWCNT-OH/GO (1:1) membrane still achieved > 99% rejection of negatively charged dyes (ARS, CR and MB) due to enhanced Donnan exclusion, supported by its more negative zeta potential (−48.6 mV, Supplementary Figs. 28, 29).

Further investigations with hydroxylated double-walled (DWCNT-OH, inner diameter ~2 nm) and multilayer-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNT-OH, inner diameter ~3 nm) (Supplementary Fig. 40) reveal that their larger inner diameters and average membrane pore size (2.09 and 2.44 nm) (Supplementary Fig. 41) allow the passage of organic molecules, thereby reducing membrane selectivity. In contrast, SWCNT-OH with a 1.5 nm inner diameter—well aligned with the calculated average membrane pore size (1.53 nm)—provides the optimal 1D channel geometry and interlayer spacing for high organic/inorganic separation performance (Supplementary Fig. 42).

Molecular dynamics for mechanism clarification

To investigate the transport dynamics of interlayer H2O and inorganic salts, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed at pressures of 1, 5, and 10 bar on SWCNT-OH nanochannel, GO nanochannel, SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) nanochannel, and SWCNT-OH/GO (1:1) nanochannel (see Methods for details and Supplementary Fig. 43 for the snapshots of the representative atomistic model used in the simulations).

The simulated H2O slip velocities within the inner channels of the SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) nanochannel (0.07, 1.48, and 2.76 m s−1 at 1, 5, and 10 bar, respectively) closely reaches those of SWCNT-OH nanochannel (0.09, 1.64, and 3.07 m s−1 at 1, 5, and 10 bar, respectively), while surpassing those of the GO nanochannel by 7-10 times (0.01, 0.21, and 0.30 m s−1 at 1, 5, and 10 bar, respectively) (Fig. 4a). This discrepancy is attributed to the higher mass transfer resistance in GO nanochannel, arising from its oxygen-containing functional groups15,41. In contrast, the incorporation of SWCNT-OH facilitates the construction of frictionless 1D rapid mass transfer channels, mitigating the adverse effect of oxygen-containing functional groups on the slip velocity of H2O molecules across the GO nanochannel. Notably, the SWCNT-OH/GO (1:1) nanochannel exhibits markedly reduced velocities (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 44), likely due to pore blockages creating “dead zones” and reduced effective transport areas, as illustrated in the proposed mechanism (Supplementary Fig. 45). These findings are consistent with the time-averaged H2O-velocity maps (Fig. 4b), H2O flux simulations (Fig. 4c), and hydrophobicity analyses (Supplementary Fig. 46).

For inorganic salts, the SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane—prepared at the optimal SWCNT-OH ratio demonstrates the lowest rejection rates (Fig. 2b), due to the enhanced transport of hydrated ions through the 1D nanochannel of SWCNT-OH. To clarify this phenomenon, the transport behavior of Na2SO4, MgSO4, MgCl2, and NaCl in channel I (GO nanochannel) and II (SWCNT-OH nanochannel) was analyzed under a pressure of 5 bar (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 47; hydrated ion radius in Supplementary Table 5). All hydrated ions (Na+, Mg2+, Cl−, SO42−) exhibit nearly double slip rates in channel II compared to channel I42, demonstrating that the SWCNT-OHs incorporation creates fast ions transport channels, consistent with the lower salt rejection observed (Fig. 2b). Moreover, monovalent ions permeate faster than divalent ions in both channel I and channel II (Na+ > Mg2+, Cl- > SO42-), indicating size exclusion dominated ion transport in these channels43. Furthermore, Cl- ions (0.165 m s−1 for NaCl and 0.167 m s−1 for MgCl2) show similar velocities in channel I compared with hydrated Mg2+ (0.156 m s−1 for MgCl2 and 0.149 m s−1 for MgSO4), whereas hydrated SO42- ions exhibit much slower transport (0.038 m s−1 for Na2SO4 and 0.043 m s−1 for MgSO4). This indicates that divalent anion permeability in this negatively charged channel is strongly influenced by the Donnan effect. Due to the stronger repulsion of SO42- in channels I and II, a larger number of cations is required on the opposite side to generate a positive potential that facilitates SO42- transport43. However, the potential difference generated by hydrated Na+ is relatively small; even with many Na+ ions passing through, it is insufficient to drive SO42- transport effectively. As a result, SO42- permeability is lower for Na2SO4 (0.038 m s−1 in channel I and 0.093 m s−1 in channel II) than for MgSO4 (0.043 m s−1 in channel I and 0.100 m s−1 in channel II), leading to higher membrane rejection of Na2SO4 (19.6%) than MgSO4 (6.2%).

Scalable membrane fabrication and separation system for efficient MXF recovery

The SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane was selected for scalable fabrication and real MXF recovery due to its structural stability and excellent organics/inorganics separation performance. A modified pressure-driven self-assembly technique (Supplementary Fig. 48) was employed to integrate the SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) onto the inner surface of multi-channel tubular ceramic (TC) membrane modules, thus achieving an effective membrane area of 11 m2 per equipment set (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 49). This technique offers an exciting opportunity for the large-scale fabrication of 2D membranes.

The fabricated SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) TC membrane modules were applied for the recovery of MXF from real MXF crystallization mother liquor using a scaled-up separation system (Fig. 1b). Specifically, the system was applied to treat high-salinity organic wastewater generated from MXF hydrochloride crystallization (Fig. 5b). Pre-treatment involved adjusting the pH to 9 and increasing the temperature to 30 °C to enhance the solubility of residual MXF and prevent crystal residues from blocking membrane channels, thereby improving both separation efficiency and operational stability. After recovery, the permeate undergoes a simple pre-treatment process before being routed to a biochemical methane production system. The concentrated MXF solution is subsequently acidified to pH 4.5 to induce controlled precipitation, yielding a crude product with an MXF purity of approximately 22.8%, which can be further increased to 99.9% through subsequent purification and recrystallization steps in the MXF production process (Supplementary Fig. 50). By contrast, owing to the high concentration of organic solvents in the MXF crystallization mother liquor, the conventional polyamide organic membrane UF-NF process is unsuitable. The Evaporation-incineration process yields a nearly negligible MXF purity (~0.1%), preventing further purification and recovery of MXF. The SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) TC membrane system (includes three sets of equipment) is capable of processing 2 metric tons of mother liquor per day (2 t day−1), with operational stability exceeding 7 days and a consistent rejection efficiency of 90.6% (Supplementary Fig. 51), resulting in the daily recovery of 10 kg MXF (10 kg day−1) (Fig. 5c).

Technological and economic analysis

To assess the economic feasibility of our scalable membrane separation system for MXF recovery, we compared its capital expenditure with other existing technologies. For the SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) TC membrane process, the cost composition includes chemical reagents (5.0%), electricity (56.9%), membrane costs (16.1%), and maintenance (22.0%). Our evaluation shows that the operating cost using the SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) TC membrane process (6.2$ ton−1) is 73.8% lower than that of evaporative crystallization (23.7$ ton−1) and 68.2% lower than that of the UF-NF process (19.5$ ton−1) (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Tables 6-8). Note that the UF-NF process is hindered by poor solvent resistance and severe membrane fouling. The input cost—including capital, utilities, materials, and fixed costs—is $17.9 kg−1 MXF, while the market price of recycled MXF is $138.8 kg−1, yielding a net profit of $120.9 kg−1 MXF (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Table 9). Considering a daily recovery of 10 kg of MXF, the system generates a daily profit of $1,209. Additionally, by reducing 2 t day−1 of waste liquid and hazardous, the system saves $167 t−1 in disposal fees, resulting in an extra $334 in daily savings. In total, the system delivers a daily economic benefit of $1,543, enabling a rapid payback period of just 0.4 years from an initial capital investment of $200,000, highlighting the strong economic feasibility of the SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane separation process for MXF recovery.

Discussion

This study introduces a SWCNT-OH/GO membrane (0.1:1) that successfully breaks the conventional trade-off between water permeability and separation selectivity, providing a viable strategy for sustainable treatment and resource recovery from high-salinity organic wastewater. The integration of SWCNT-OHs creates rapid 1D transport pathways, enhances structural stability through borate crosslinking, and improves antifouling properties. The membrane demonstrates high water permeability (30.1 l m−2 h−1 bar−1) and efficient separation of MXF/NaCl (separation factor 22.2). When scaled up on ceramic substrates to 11 m² and implemented in a pilot-scale separation system, it consistently recovered 10 kg of MXF per day from real wastewater over 7 days, confirming its strong engineering feasibility. Economic assessment further highlights the system’s benefits compared to conventional technologies, including superior cost-effectiveness and lower energy demand. Overall, this work establishes a scalable, high-performance, and sustainable membrane platform that integrates nanomaterial design, system scale-up, and process optimization - advancing high-salinity wastewater treatment and resource recovery technologies.

Methods

Materials

GO nanosheets were synthesized using the Hummers’ method. SWCNT-OH, SWCNT-NH2, SWCNT-COOH, DWCNT-OH, and MWCNT-OH were purchased from Nanjing XFNANO Materials Tech (The specific information about the specifications, such as the length and purity of the CNTs, were listed in Supplementary Table 1). Dopamine hydrochloride (≥98 wt%), tris(hydroxymethyl) amino-methane (Tris) (≥99.9 wt%), PEG3350, MXF, MB, CBB, EBT, a-CD, MO, CR, EB, MLB, and RB were procured from Sigma. MgSO4, NaCl, Na2SO4, MgCl2, ARS, and CAD were purchased from Aladdin. All chemicals were used without further purification. Additionally, PVDF microfiltration (MF) membranes with a mean pore diameter of 0.45 μm. NF270 and UE001 were obtained from RisingSun Membrane Tech.

Preparation of SWCNT-OH/GO membranes

The preparation process involved a modified SEPA CF II Membrane Comprehensive Performance Test System (Sterlitech Corporation, USA) and a modified self-assembly filtration method using PVDF MF membranes as support. To prevent damage to the SWCNT-OH/GO layer caused by the sealing ring of the membrane tank during filtration, the seal contacts of the supporting membrane were coated with a layer of epoxy resin (3 M Scotch Weld DP420) and treated with a dopamine solution (2 g L−1) for approximately 5 minutes. The membrane surface was then rinsed with pure water five times to ensure the removal of any residual dopamine. SWCNT-OH/GO membranes were prepared using pressure self-assembly under cross-flow conditions. The supporting membrane was first filtered with Na2B4O7 (2.5 mM) for approximately 2 hours. Next, a certain amount of GO nanosheets and SWCNT-OH after 10 min ultrasound time were filtered onto the supporting membrane surface in three stages, with each filtrate being collected and returned to the water tank. During the production process (under optimal conditions), the amount of GO remains unchanged (4.32 μg cm−2, the surface density of monolayer GO is 0.144 μg cm−2), and the amount of CNTs is gradually increased (0-4.32 μg cm−2). Na2B4O7 with a concentration of 2.5 mM was added throughout this process. One-third of the total GO and SWCNT-OH dispersions were added and filtered through the membrane in each step, and repeated three times. The cross-flow velocity and transmembrane pressure during the preparation of the SWCNT-OH/GO membrane were set to 0.11 m s−1 and 0.1 MPa. In the fourth filtration process, the cross-flow velocity and system pressure were elevated to 0.44 m s−1 and 1.0 MPa, and the pH of the filtrate was adjusted to 2 using H2SO4. After filtration, the membrane was dried in an oven at 90 °C for approximately 120 minutes. SWCNT-NH2/GO, SWCNT-COOH/GO, DWCNT-OH/GO, and MWCNT-OH/GO membranes were prepared by the same method.

Analytical methods

Morphology characterization

SEM images were obtained using a Hitachi S-5500 SEM operating at 30 kV. All samples were thoroughly dried in an oven at 45 °C, followed by vacuum treatment and carbon coating for 10 s prior to SEM observation. Cross-sectional TEM samples were prepared using the Zeiss Auriga Focused Ion Beam (FIB). HRTEM images were acquired by a JEM-2100 TEM with an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. The surface roughness of the membranes was observed using an AFM (Dimension Icon, Bruker). The microscopic morphology of the SWCNT-OH/GO (0.1:1) membrane was characterized by SEM (Hitachi S-4800) at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV, and its elemental distribution was further analyzed by EDS.

Structure characterization

T2 values were measured at 20 MHz using an NMI20 NMR analyzer (Suzhou NIUMAG Analysis Instruments Corporation). The magnet temperature was maintained at 32 °C to ensure constant molecular motion for system probing. XRD patterns were obtained using Cu Kα radiation (15 mA and 40 kV) on a D8 ADVANCE (Bruker Analytical X-Ray Solutions) at a scan rate of 2° min−1 and a step size of 0.02°.

Surface properties characterization

The dynamic contact angle was measured using the sessile drop method with a KRUSS DSA100 contact angle measuring instrument. Droplets of 5 μL were extruded onto the flat surface of the sample using a micro syringe, and the wetting process was recorded using a high-speed camera. Zeta potentials of the membrane surface were determined by streaming potential measurements using an electrokinetic analyzer (Anton Paar, SurPASS). A 1 mM KCl solution was passed over the membrane surface at a pressure of 0.03 MPa.

Other characterizations

The surface chemistry of the membranes were observed using a Nicolet iN10MX Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Thermo) and an ESCALAB 250XI XPS (Thermo Scientific). Scratch tests were performed using a NanoScope IIIa AFM with a harder probe (RTESP, MPP-11100-10, coefficient of elasticity k: 40 N m−1, Bruker Nano Inc.) to ensure the membranes could be scratched. The deflection sensitivity of the AFM was 45 nm V−1. TOF-SIMS (TOF.SIMS 5-100, ION-TOF GmbH, Germany) was employed to analyze the surface composition and depth-resolved distribution of chemical species in the membranes. The analysis was performed using a Bi3+ primary ion beam at 30 keV, directed at a 45° incident angle over a 200 μm × 200 μm scan area. An O2+ sputtering ion beam at 1 keV was applied, corresponding to a sputtering rate of 0.316 nm s−1 on SiO2.

Membrane performance evaluation

The pure water permeation and rejection performance of the membranes were measured using a modified SEPA CF II membrane cell system (Sterlitech Corporation, USA) at a trans-membrane pressure (TMP) of 0.50 MPa.The effective membrane area of the lab-scale membrane was about 150 cm2. The cross-flow velocity was set at 0.11 m s−1 for flux measurement and 0.44 m s−1 for rejection efficiency measurement. The temperature of the feed solution was maintained at 25 ± 1 °C. For pure water flux measurement, the collected water volume was monitored, and real-time measurement was used with an electric balance (CP4102, OHAUS corporation). The rejection of various salt and organic molecules were measured using PEG3350 solution (0.1 g L−1), MXF solution (0.1 g L−1), MgSO4 solution (25 mM), NaCl solution (25 mM), Na2SO4 solution (25 mM), MgCl2 solution (25 mM), and other organic solutions (10 mg L−1). All separation performance and water flux data, except for dynamic tests, were recorded once the fabricated membranes reached a steady state. The time to reach a steady state varied depending on the membrane architecture and applied pressure, ranging from several hours to several days. Each membrane was analyzed three times under the same conditions, and control experimental membranes were prepared using the same method. The concentrations of PEG were tested following the Hach TOC (COD) method. Salt concentrations were determined using a conductivity meter (WTW), and the concentrations of dye molecules were measured using a spectrophotometric method with a T6 spectrophotometer.
