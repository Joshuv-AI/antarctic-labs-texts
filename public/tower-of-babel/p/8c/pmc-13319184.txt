Strategic selection of multi-parametric laser settings and clinical endpoints in dermatology: an engineering-to-clinical review
Authors: Soobeen Park, Jihee Hyeon, Hongyun So
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSelective photothermolysis, which enables precise targeting of melanin, hemoglobin, and water, is the foundation for modern dermatologic laser therapy. This review bridges laser physics with clinical practice by organizing systems on the basis of spectral domain and indication: Q-switched and picosecond lasers for tattoos and lentigines; pulsed-dye and long-pulsed neodymium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet (Nd:YAG) lasers for vascular lesions; alexandrite and diode lasers for hair removal; and fractional ablative (CO₂, erbium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet [Er:YAG]) and non-ablative platforms for scar revision and rejuvenation. Unlike conventional textbook reviews that merely list device specifications, we specifically define how to dynamically adjust comprehensive parameters not just wavelength, but also pulse duration, fluence, and spot size—in direct response to immediate clinical endpoints observed during treatment. We delineated the selection of parameters—wavelength, pulse duration, fluence, and spot size—in relation to the Fitzpatrick skin type, focusing on cooling, treatment endpoints, and recovery. Current evidence regarding efficacy, recurrence, and complications has been synthesized, and the device parameters have been linked to thermal relaxation and Arrhenius injury kinetics to inform safer evidence-based algorithms and multimodal treatment strategies.Keywords: Laser treatment, Selective photothermolysis, Laser–tissue interaction, Skin optics, Wavelength-based classification

Introduction

For many years, dermatological treatments were dependent on invasive methods such as surgical excision, chemical peeling, and electrocauterization, which often resulted in adverse effects such as bacterial infections, severe pain, and scar formation [1–3]. Subsequently, the advent of non-invasive methodologies in the early 1960s, including tattoo removal and the utilization of ruby lasers for the treatment of vascular lesions, signaled a pivotal paradigm shift within the domain of dermatology [4, 5]. From the mid-1980s, the establishment of laser treatment guidelines by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) spurred diverse clinical studies, solidifying lasers as a core technology that either replaced or complemented existing treatments [6, 7].

The theoretical foundation for the advancement of therapeutic laser technologies is “selective photothermolysis,” a concept proposed in 1983 [8]. This theory posits that precise modulation of wavelength, pulse duration, and damage-threshold fluence can selectively destroy the target tissue while minimizing damage to the surrounding tissues. The interactions between light and tissues are generally categorized into four mechanisms: photothermal, photomechanical, photochemical, and photoacoustic [9, 10]. Selective photothermolysis, that is based on the photothermal mechanism, is the most widely utilized in clinical dermatology. Photothermal interactions induce a variety of effects depending on the tissue temperature. At temperatures ranging from 40 °C to 50 °C, cellular dysfunction and partial, reversible changes occur. At temperatures between 60 °C and 80 °C, protein denaturation and coagulation occur. Finally, above 100 °C, water vaporization and tissue carbonization occur [11, 12].

The skin is composed of three layers: the epidermis, dermis, and hypodermis. The major chromophores identified in this study include melanin, hemoglobin, and water. Melanin exhibits a broad absorption spectrum, and its absorption capacity gradually decreases with increasing wavelength. The absorption spectrum of hemoglobin exhibits distinct peaks at 418, 542, and 577 nm [13]. Optimization of the fluence, pulse duration, and spot size is contingent on the distinct absorption characteristics and laser wavelength range of the target tissue [14, 15]. This optimization can help maximize selectivity and efficiency depending on the type and depth of the lesion [16, 17].

While the fundamental principles of laser–tissue interactions and their clinical implications have been well summarized in prior authoritative reviews, the present work shifts the focus from static theory to the dynamic, real‑time adjustment of comprehensive treatment parameters in response to immediate clinical endpoints across diverse patient populations. Specifically, the range of 650–1350 nm is referred to as the “optical window,” where tissue absorption is minimal, thereby maximizing light penetration depth [18, 19]. Lasers operating in this wavelength range, including the ruby laser (694 nm), alexandrite laser (755 nm), and diode laser (810 nm), have been extensively utilized in the visible and near-infrared spectra [20]. The lower-wavelength ruby laser has been demonstrated to be highly effective for the removal of pigmented lesions and tattoos owing to its capacity for strong melanin absorption [21, 22]. However, the use of this laser is associated with an elevated risk of burns in patients with darker skin tones. The efficacy of near-infrared neodymium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet (Nd:YAG; 1064 nm) and mid-infrared erbium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet (Er:YAG; 2940 nm) lasers for skin rejuvenation has also been demonstrated.

As shown in Fig. 1, laser technology has been used in various fields, including wrinkle treatment, hair removal, and drug delivery [23, 24].

Additionally, the presence of both research and industrial momentum is noteworthy. The past two decades have seen a steady increase in the number of academic publications related to dermatological laser therapy (Fig. 2a). Furthermore, the field of dermatology is expected to demonstrate sustained growth, as indicated by current market projections (Fig. 2b). Despite this wealth of literature, many existing review articles remain heavily theoretical. In light of these considerations, the objective of this review is: first, to facilitate the prediction of thermal distribution and penetration depth on the basis of the absorption spectrum of the target chromophore, and second, to undertake systematic parameter optimization. Additionally, this review seeks to provide an engineering interpretation of the multi-chromophore responses observed at various wavelengths. Our objective is to directly translate these engineering principles into actionable clinical algorithms. Specifically, we delineate how practitioners should dynamically adjust treatment parameters in direct response to immediate clinical endpoints observed during treatment. This is realized by systematically comparing and organizing laser systems, moving away from existing disease-centered classifications [4]. To this end, we first provide a synopsis of the physical principles of selective photothermolysis along with a discussion of the key parameters. We then systematically examine the laser–tissue interactions and conclude by organizing clinical applications by wavelength band, supplemented with comprehensive clinical guidelines and management protocols for complications. Through this study, we aim to enhance the understanding of laser treatment technology and establish a basic framework for follow-up research and clinical development.

Optical properties of the human skin

The human skin consists of three primary layers: the epidermis, dermis, and hypodermis (Fig. 3a). Each layer has distinct optical properties [27]. The thickness of the epidermis varies substantially, ranging from tens to hundreds of micrometers, depending on the anatomical region. The epidermis is devoid of blood vessels and is subdivided into the stratum corneum, stratum lucidum, stratum granulosum, stratum spinosum, and stratum basale. The stratum corneum consists of approximately 15–30 cell layers and possesses a thickness of 10–30 μm; however, it can grow to a thickness of several hundred micrometers in areas such as the palms and soles [28, 29]. Melanocytes, specialized cells responsible for melanin production, reside in the basal layer of the epidermis. Melanosomes, the melanin chromophores produced by these cells, serve as primary targets for laser treatment of pigmentary skin disorders. The average dermal thickness is approximately 1–2 mm [30]. Superficial vascular plexuses are distributed in the upper portion (0.3–0.6 mm depth) of the dermis, while deep vascular plexuses are found in the lower portion (1.0–1.5 mm depth) [31, 32]. The dermis is primarily composed of collagen fibers, which account for approximately 70%–80% of its dry weight, and elastin fibers, which constitute 2%–4% of the dermis [33, 34]. These fibers exhibit strong scattering properties. Subcutaneous tissue is predominantly composed of adipocytes, with thicknesses ranging from several millimeters to several centimeters, contingent on an individual’s body composition and specific anatomical region. Consequently, the anatomical composition of the tissue dictates the optical properties of the skin, with the refractive index and absorption and scattering coefficients exhibiting distinct characteristics in each layer [35].

The refractive index () of skin tissue varies with wavelength, generally ranging between 1.34 and 1.44 in the 400–1600 nm range [35]. Furthermore, the material displays dispersion characteristics, which manifest as a modest decline in intensity with increasing wavelength. The epidermis and dermis exhibit complex refractive indices, where the real and imaginary parts are related to light refraction and absorption, respectively [36]. Notably, the optical constants are subject to variations in accordance with the wavelength and skin type, as illustrated in Fig. 3c. The behavior of light within the skin can be classified into absorption, scattering, transmission, and reflection. Each of these paths varies depending on the layer and wavelength. The epidermis exhibits a high absorption coefficient () in the ultraviolet (UV) and visible light regions (400–600 nm), which decreases with increasing wavelength to approximately 0.01–0.1 mm-1 at 1064 nm. The scattering coefficient, denoted by (), typically ranges from 1 to 10 and demonstrates a tendency to decrease with increasing wavelength. The absorption coefficient of the dermis is reported to be approximately 0.02–0.06 mm-1 at 633 nm, with a scattering coefficient of 2–4 mm-1. The anisotropy factor () in this layer ranges from 0.7 to 0.9, which indicates markedly strong forward scattering characteristics [37]. Subcutaneous tissue exhibits lower absorption and scattering coefficients than the epidermis and dermis, resulting in relatively high light transmittance. These variations in optical constants are closely associated with major chromophore distribution within the skin.

The skin is composed of various chromophores, including melanin, hemoglobin, water, and lipids. Oxyhemoglobin demonstrates discernible absorption peaks within the visible spectrum at wavelengths of 418, 542, and 577 nm. Clinically, its high vascular selectivity at wavelengths of 577–600 nm enables efficient treatment [38]. Melanin demonstrates robust absorption in the UV and visible regions of the electromagnetic spectrum [39, 40]. However, its absorption coefficient decreases with increasing wavelength, thereby reducing the therapeutic efficacy at higher wavelengths [41]. This is shown in Fig. 3b, which presents wavelength-specific absorption curves of major chromophores and representative lasers. This visual confirmation provides a tangible basis for wavelength selection. The quantity and distribution of melanin among these groups varies according to race and skin type, a phenomenon that can be explained by the Fitzpatrick skin type classification [42].

The optical properties of the skin are influenced by various factors in addition to its anatomical composition. The Fitzpatrick skin types, which were first proposed in 1975, categorize skin into six types (Type I to VI) on the basis of responses to UV radiation [45, 46]. Although the melanin volume fraction per skin type varies across reports, the findings for non-sun-exposed areas indicate that the melanin volume fraction in Type I skin is approximately 3%–5%, while that in Type VI skin is 15%–20% or higher [46]. Furthermore, light skin characteristically displays melanosomes in an aggregate form, whereas dark skin exhibits larger, dispersed melanosomes [47]. These disparities are closely associated with the UV response. Individuals with Type I and II skin typically possess fair skin with low melanin concentrations. This biological trait predisposes these individuals to sunburn when exposed to prolonged UV radiation, resulting in minimal tanning. Freckles are also prevalent in these types, and they exhibit the lowest absorption coefficients in the visible light spectrum. Individuals with Type III and IV skin exhibit skin tones that range from light brown to medium brown. These individuals experience mild sunburns with prolonged UV exposure and achieve average tanning. For the 500–600 nm wavelength range, they generally show higher absorption coefficients than individuals with Type I and II skin. Whereas, individuals with Type V and VI skin exhibit skin tones ranging from dark brown to very dark brown, indicating the highest melanin concentration [48, 49].

Furthermore, epidermal thickness tends to gradually decrease with age, although the rate of reduction varies by body region and age group [50–52]. This forms the core rationale for selecting the laser treatment wavelengths.

Fundamentals of laser–tissue interactions (selective photothermolysis)

The efficacy of laser treatment is contingent on meticulous configuration of the pertinent parameters. Consequently, a comprehensive understanding of the physical mechanisms by which the light source interacts with the tissue is essential. The principle of selective photothermolysis, which is primarily based on photothermal mechanisms, has been applied in the field of dermatology. Anderson and Parrish proposed a triad of pivotal conditions for effective execution of selective photothermolysis [8]: First, a laser wavelength commensurate with the absorption characteristics of the target must be used to ensure selective absorption. Second, the pulse duration must be equal to or shorter than the target thermal relaxation time. Third, the fluence within the target must reach the damage-threshold fluence while remaining below the threshold in the surrounding tissues. These principles of selective photothermolysis are illustrated in Fig. 4. These conditions are determined using mathematical models that predict the spatial distribution of light within tissues.

Clinical and functional applications of laser systems

The practical translation of laser–tissue interaction principles into dermatological procedures has expanded the therapeutic scope of laser technology beyond traditional resurfacing and lesion ablation. Advances in device control, wavelength selectivity, and pulse engineering have allowed clinicians to achieve selective tissue remodeling and targeted drug delivery with improved precision and safety. Among these, laser-assisted drug delivery (LADD) has emerged as a key application that combines photothermal modulation with pharmacological therapy, facilitating enhanced transdermal transport of active compounds through controlled disruption of the stratum corneum barrier.

In addition to the role of lasers in drug delivery, understanding the fundamental mechanisms of laser–tissue interactions, including photothermal, photomechanical, photochemical, and photoacoustic effects, is essential for optimizing clinical outcomes. Each mechanism produces distinct biological and structural responses within the skin, influencing the treatment efficacy, downtime, and adverse event profiles. These physical interactions form the scientific basis for various dermatological applications, ranging from pigmentation and vascular therapies to scar remodeling and rejuvenation.

This section provides an overview of these mechanisms, focusing on their clinical relevance, followed by a detailed discussion on LADD as a representative example of how fundamental laser principles can be harnessed for controlled, minimally invasive therapeutic interventions.

Laser-assisted drug delivery

Human skin is composed of three layers: epidermis, dermis, and hypodermis [44]. For effective drug delivery, the stratum corneum barrier must be removed from the epidermis. The drug penetration patterns in relation to different laser-assisted treatment modalities are shown in Fig. 6. The intact skin has a stratum corneum that can reduce drug penetration. Conventional laser-treated skin shows superior drug penetration but is vulnerable to external stimuli. In addition, recovery requires a long time. Therefore, fractional lasers are commonly used in therapy. The partial removal of the stratum corneum creates microchannels that extend into the dermis, thereby enabling drug delivery. Although the penetration effect is lower than that of conventional laser-treated skin, various drugs can be used to accurately treat the target [84].

LADD can be divided into invasive and non-invasive methods. The invasive method creates microchannels using a laser, and injects the drug directly into the dermis. Because of its high drug permeability, this method can be used for deep scarring or polymer drug treatment. However, this approach can cause erythema or scabbing [85, 86]. The non-invasive method involves applying the drug to the skin surface by increasing the permeability of the stratum corneum with a laser. With this method, the risk of infection is low and the recovery speed is high; however, the depth of penetration is limited. This method is suitable for whitening or treating shallow pigmentation diseases [87].

Laser–tissue interactions: four main mechanisms

The biological response of skin tissue to laser irradiation depends primarily on the duration of exposure and power density. Based on these parameters, laser–tissue interactions are categorized into four principal mechanisms: photothermal, photomechanical, photochemical, and photoacoustic effects [88, 89]. As illustrated in Fig. 7, each mechanism reflects a distinct physical pathway through which optical energy is converted to biological or structural change within the skin. These mechanisms collectively determine the therapeutic outcome, ranging from tissue ablation and remodeling to selective pigment destruction and photochemical modulation. When the laser is irradiated, light energy is first absorbed by specific chromophores and subsequently converted into thermal or mechanical energy, leading to phenomena such as protein denaturation, vaporization, carbonization, and dissolution.

Photothermal effect

In the photothermal effect, chromophores absorb light energy and convert it into thermal energy. This heat results in phenomena such as coagulation, vaporization, carbonization, and dissolution within cells. This mechanism forms the basis for the effects of alexandrite lasers, which are absorbed by melanin, on pigmented lesions; diode lasers in hair removal; and Er:YAG and CO2 lasers, which are absorbed by water, in skin peeling and wrinkles [90–92].

Gold nanoparticle-mediated photothermal therapy (Gold PTT) is a treatment modality that uses the photothermal effect. In this approach, gold nanoparticles are used to maximize the effect of the lasers. When gold nanoparticles are applied to the lesion area and irradiated, they absorb light and generate heat. This approach is mainly used for acne treatment, but its use remains limited, necessitating more research in the future [93, 94].

Photomechanical effect

In the photomechanical effect, chromophores exposed to high-power energy in a short time instantaneously heat and expand, generating a shockwave. This shockwave ends before the heat spreads to the surroundings, thereby causing minimal heat damage and limiting the destruction to the target [95]. Under stress confinement, the pressure accumulates and shock waves are amplified, thereby strengthening the photomechanical effect. The photomechanical effect can appear as plasma formation, shock waves, cavitation, or jet formation [96–98]. It has been applied to the Nd:YAG laser, which is mainly used for tattoo removal, and the picosecond laser shows higher removal rates for black, red, and green pigments than the nanosecond laser. The Er:YAG laser also shows a high absorption coefficient with a short irradiation time, and can also elicit photomechanical effects [99].

Photochemical effect

The photochemical effect occurs under prolonged irradiation with a low-intensity laser, which causes chemical reactions in skin cells. It can act selectively on specific cells and simultaneously cause cell death and immune responses. A representative treatment that employs this effect is photodynamic therapy (PDT), which is widely used to treat acne [100, 101]. PDT is performed using a photosensitizer (ps), light source, and oxygen. The ps accumulates in the target tissue and transitions to an excited state when irradiated with a specific wavelength of light. These radicals react with oxygen to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), which damages the target tissue to induce therapeutic effects. 5-Aminolevulinic acid (ALA) and methyl aminolevulinate (MAL) are ps precursors that are converted into protoporphyrin IX (PpIX), an active ps, and the accumulated PpIX is converted to ROS on exposure to light [102, 103]. In acne therapy, blue light (410 nm) is used to suppress acne-causing bacteria, whereas red light (630 nm) penetrates deeper and helps suppress inflammation; therefore, the two are often used in combination [104, 105].

Photoacoustic effect

The photoacoustic effect does not simply involve thermal burning. When high-intensity energy is irradiated onto a chromophore for a short period, the temperature of the chromophore that has absorbed this energy rises rapidly to create a pressure wave. This pressure wave spreads and destroys the target pigment or ink. This approach is widely used to remove tattoos. Similar to the photomechanical effect, the picosecond laser is a better alternative for generating photoacoustic pressure waves. It is also effective for pigmented lesions such as seborrheic keratosis or the regeneration of skin elasticity because it can remove pigments while minimizing skin damage [106–108].

In addition to laser treatment, the photoacoustic effect is also used for diagnosis using photoacoustic imaging (PAI). Ultrasound signals generated by laser absorption in chromophores are reconstructed into images, enabling the high-resolution visualization of deeper skin structures. It can also be used to diagnose melanoma [109].

Lasers by wavelength

Wavelengths used in dermatology can be divided into five categories: UV (200–400 nm) [15], visible light (400–760 nm) [110], near-infrared (760–1400 nm), mid-infrared (1400–3000 nm), and far-infrared (> 3000 nm) [111]. The main chromophores are hemoglobin at short wavelengths, melanin at medium wavelengths, and moisture at long wavelengths. The wavelength used for tattoo removal differs by ink color, and the wavelength used for hair removal differs by the skin type or hair thickness; therefore, laser wavelength should be selected carefully [37]. Ultraviolet and far-infrared lasers are not included in the following sections because their dermatologic use is limited; UV systems are primarily applied for phototherapy rather than selective photothermolysis, while far-infrared sources exceed typical clinical ranges for cutaneous treatment.

Visible light

Visible light penetrates the skin depending on its wavelength and interacts with the major chromophores from the epidermis to the dermis [110]. Blue light (400–470 nm) is absorbed by melanin in the epidermis and is mainly used for the treatment of acne and reduction of inflammation. Yellow light (570–590 nm) is absorbed by hemoglobin in the upper dermis and is effective against vascular lesions and erythema. Red light (630–700 nm) penetrates most deeply. It is absorbed by the mitochondria, and facilitates skin soothing, regeneration, and wrinkle improvement [112].

Lasers that use visible light include the potassium titanyl phosphate (KTP) and ruby lasers. The KTP laser employs the selective photothermal principle with a wavelength of 532 nm. Its penetration depth is relatively low; however, it exhibits high absorption by both hemoglobin and melanin. Absorption by hemoglobin enables the treatment of vascular lesions such as telangiectasia and hemangiomas, while absorption by melanin, especially in the upper layer of the epidermis, is effective in treating shallow pigmentation lesions such as freckles and blemishes [113, 114]. The ruby laser, with a 694 nm red-light wavelength, is strongly absorbed by melanin and is therefore highly effective in pigment-targeted therapy. It is effective in removing black, blue, and green tattoos by selectively destroying the pigment using a very short high-power pulse. However, it shows relatively low absorption by hemoglobin. Therefore, treatment of vascular lesions with this laser is difficult and requires considerations based on the skin type [115].

Near-infrared rays

Although near-infrared (NIR) rays have lower energy than visible light, they penetrate deeper and can reach layers from the dermis to the hypodermis. The main chromophores targeted by NIR lasers are the mitochondria and water. NIR lasers are widely used for hair removal, pigmentation treatment, and scar management.

Diode lasers with a wavelength of 810 nm are the most widely used lasers in hair-removal equipment worldwide. In comparison with the alexandrite laser, diode lasers penetrate deeper, providing excellent effects on thick and deeply rooted hairs. These lasers can be applied to various skin types because of the low absorption rate by epidermal melanin [121]. The effectiveness of hair removal using a diode laser is presented in Fig. 9 (a-b) and (c-d). This treatment resulted in a significant reduction in the average hair count without clinically meaningful side effects [122]. During laser hair removal procedures utilizing diode lasers (810 nm, 940 nm, or 1060 nm), perifollicular erythema and edema were observed immediately after treatment and served as clinical endpoints. These transient effects were mild to moderate in all treated subjects and disappeared within 24 to 48 h. The presence of perifollicular erythema and edema was more pronounced with triple-wavelength diode lasers compared to single 810 nm diode lasers [123]. Owing to their high absorption by water, diode lasers have also been used for scar and wrinkle treatments [124].

The alexandrite laser has a wavelength of 755 nm and a high melanin-absorption rate; therefore, it is specifically utilized for pigmented lesions. This laser uses a picosecond laser source with a pulse shorter than 1000 times that of nanoseconds; therefore, it uses instantaneous pressure waves to achieve high pigment-destructive power while causing minimal damage to the surrounding tissues [125, 126]. For tattoo removal, the target chromophores are not endogenous pigments but rather the ink particles themselves. Because tattoo pigments are located in the dermis (Fig. 11), one study [128] used NIR rays that can penetrate deeply into the dermis. The laser mainly used for tattoo removal is the Nd:YAG laser, which has a wavelength of 1064 nm and is safe even on dark skin owing to its low melanin-absorption rate. It is most effective for black, blue, and navy pigments, whereas other wavelengths are combined to remove pigments with bright colors. In tattoo removal with Q-switched or picosecond lasers, immediate ash-white frosting is a recognized clinical endpoint [107]. It is associated with photomechanical and photoacoustic disruption of tattoo pigment, with transient whitening caused by intradermal vacuole formation and light scattering. However, laser and light-based devices can also cause complications such as blistering, especially when treatment settings are excessive or in higher-risk skin types [130].

Mid-infrared rays

The main chromophore in the mid-infrared (MIR) ray wavelength range is water; thus, radiation in the MIR range is absorbed in the upper epidermis and dermis because approximately 70% of the skin is composed of water. This wavelength range is more effective for resection or vaporization than deep penetration. It is widely used for the treatment of fine wrinkles and for improving skin texture. The lasers mainly used are the Er:YAG and CO2 lasers.

The Er:YAG laser has a wavelength of 2940 nm and a high water-absorption rate; therefore, treatment with this laser allows instantaneous water vaporization with minimal damage due to heat. These effects can improve fine wrinkles and skin texture. The CO2 laser has a wavelength of 10,600 nm. Since the heat generated by this laser spreads more widely, it can be transferred to the deeper regions of the skin, which is effective in treating deep wrinkles and improving skin elasticity [131].

In clinical practice, ablative lasers such as CO₂ and Er:YAG are guided by visual endpoints during skin resurfacing. Pinpoint bleeding is commonly used as an indicator that adequate ablation has reached the papillary dermis, while excessive thermal injury should be avoided to reduce the risk of delayed healing and scarring. Clinical applications of dermatologic laser and light-based therapies

Parameter adjustments and clinical management of complications

To ensure the practical utility of this review, it is imperative to outline the specific parameter adjustments required for different Fitzpatrick skin types to maximize efficacy while minimizing adverse events. When treating darker skin tones (IV–VI), the increased melanin concentration in the epidermis acts as a prominent competing chromophore, significantly raising the risk of epidermal thermal injury and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation. Consequently, a highly tailored parameter strategy is required: fluences should be conservatively lowered, and pulse durations must be extended beyond the thermal relaxation time of the epidermis (to allow sufficient epidermal cooling) while still targeting deeper dermal structures [135, 136]. Furthermore, integrating aggressive epidermal cooling mechanisms and utilizing lower pulse repetition rates are mandatory to prevent cumulative bulk heating. In laser surgery, immediate skin responses can serve as clinical endpoints that reflect either therapeutic efficacy or impending injury, and therefore provide a practical basis for real-time parameter adjustment. Accordingly, visible responses such as erythema, whitening, or purpura should be interpreted in the context of the target lesion and skin type when optimizing treatment settings [137, 138]. Clinicians must strictly monitor immediate clinical endpoints; for instance, any immediate epidermal whitening (graying) when targeting deep dermal vessels or hair follicles in skin tones V and VI patients indicates excessive epidermal absorption, necessitating an immediate cessation and parameter recalibration.

While dermatologic lasers are generally safe when correctly parameterized using the aforementioned FST guidelines, adverse events can still occur, necessitating prompt and appropriate clinical management. As noted, the most frequently encountered complication is PIH. PIH can be effectively minimized by strict pre- and post-operative ultraviolet (UV) protection and is actively managed with topical bleaching agents (e.g., 4% hydroquinone) combined with mild topical corticosteroids [139]. Thermal burns and epidermal blistering may result from excessive fluence, overlapping pulses, or inadequate epidermal cooling. Immediate management of thermal injuries includes the application of cold compresses, sterile emollients, and topical antibiotics (e.g., mupirocin) to prevent secondary bacterial infections [140]. In severe cases of thermal damage that pose a high risk of hypertrophic scarring, early intervention with intralesional triamcinolone acetonide injections and the application of silicone gel sheeting is highly recommended to optimize cosmetic outcomes and restore the skin barrier [141].

Conclusions

Selective photothermolysis remains the fundamental cornerstone for the advancement of dermatologic laser technologies. This review has organized the strategic selection of multi-parametric settings—wavelength, fluence, pulse duration, and spot size—not as isolated specifications, but as a synergistic triad tailored to specific biological targets. We have delineated how engineering principles, such as the Arrhenius injury model and thermal relaxation time, can be translated into actionable clinical algorithms. By monitoring immediate clinical endpoints—such as purpura, ash-white frosting, or pinpoint bleeding—practitioners can establish a dynamic feedback loop to adjust these parameters in real-time, enhancing both efficacy and safety.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. While the physical models and guidelines presented provide a robust framework, they are often based on idealized tissue properties and steady-state approximations. In actual clinical practice, significant inter-individual biological variability—including differences in epidermal thickness, chromophore density, and tissue hydration—can lead to responses that deviate from theoretical predictions. Furthermore, while fractional systems and LADD offer promising results, the incidence of adverse reactions like post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH) in darker skin tones (FST IV–VI) necessitates long-term monitoring and further validation of safety protocols.

Looking ahead, the evolution of dermatologic lasers is shifting toward ultra-short pulse durations, specifically femtosecond lasers. These platforms enable “cold ablation” through non-linear multiphoton absorption, potentially allowing for precise tissue vaporization with minimal collateral thermal damage. While attosecond lasers currently remain within the realm of fundamental physics research, they represent a theoretical frontier for future molecular dermatology. Ultimately, the future of the field lies in integrating these advanced pulse technologies with personalized, data-driven prediction systems to further refine the precision of laser-tissue interactions.
