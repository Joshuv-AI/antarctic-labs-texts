Resuscitation of a patient with progressive AIDS-related opportunistic infections complicated by MODS through multidisciplinary collaboration: case report
Authors: Shaowei Huang, Xiaofeng Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobally, AIDS-related opportunistic infections complicated by multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) are associated with high mortality and are difficult to treat. This article summarizes the resuscitation experience of a patient with AIDS-related opportunistic infections-induced multiple MODS and explores the multidisciplinary collaborative treatment strategies.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on a 37-year-old female patient withImmune Deficiency Syndrome (AIDS) who was admitted to the hospital due to “oliguria for 2 months and fever accompanied by dyspnea for 4 days”. She had a 10-year history of HIV infection without regular antiretroviral therapy. Complications such as renal failure, Co-infection of the lungs, respiratory failure, heart failure, liver injury, and cardiac arrest occurred. Multidisciplinary collaboration was carried out to promptly initiate comprehensive treatments including advanced life support and targeted anti-infection therapy. Rapid initiation of Antiretroviral Therapy (Rapid ART) was started after the patient’s condition stabilized.ResultsAfter 2 months of comprehensive treatment, the patient regained consciousness, was weaned off the ventilator, and showed improvement in liver and kidney functions and inflammatory markers, achieving clinical cure.ConclusionEarly recognition, strong advanced life support, targeted anti-infection therapy, and timely initiation of Rapid ART are the keys to the successful treatment of MODS caused by AIDS-related opportunistic infections. It suggests that for patients with AIDS-related opportunistic infections complicated by multiple organ failure, active comprehensive treatment can still lead to a good prognosis.

Background

Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection, which leads to Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome (AIDS), is a worldwide pandemic [1]. Patients infected with HIV/AIDS are susceptible to numerous opportunistic infections (OIs) due to significantly compromised cellular immune function. These infections can disseminate and are often life-threatening, frequently advancing rapidly to severe complications such as septic shock and MODS, which are associated with extremely high mortality rates. OIs are defined as infections produced more frequently or with significant severity due to severe immunosuppression [2]. Multiple Organ Dysfunction Syndrome (MODS) is a leading cause of mortality among critically ill patients, multidisciplinary collaboration plays a crucial role in diagnosing and treating these complex cases. Early diagnosis, effective advanced life support, targeted anti-infection therapy, and rapid initiation of Antiretroviral Therapy (Rapid ART) are the core elements for improving the prognosis. This article examines crtical aspects of a multidisciplinary collaborative approach in treating a case of MODS resulting from opportunistic infection in AIDS patients, aiming to offer insights for improving the management of patients with concurrent opportunistic infections and MODS.

Case report

A 37-year-old, female, patient weighing 52 kg, was admitted to the hospital on April 28, 2025, due to “oliguria for 2 months and fever accompanied by dyspnea for 4 days”. Two months prior to admission, her urine output gradually decreased to 400–500 mL per day, accompanied by low back pain and fatigue. Four days before admission, she experienced irregular fever with a maximum body temperature of 38 °C, along with palpitations, chest tightness, dyspnea, cough, and expectoration of a small amount of white sticky sputum. She had a 10-year history of HIV infection without regular ART or monitoring of CD4 + T lymphocyte count and viral load. Upon examination, her body temperature was recorded at 37.8 °C, her pulse rate was 91 beat per minute, respiratory rate was 18 breaths per minute, blood pressure was 144/105 mmHg, and her SpO2 was 95%. Oral examination revealed thick, white, curd-like plaques distributed on the tongue dorsum, buccal and palatal mucosa. (Fig. 1). The breath sounds in both lungs were slightly rough, accompanied by a small amount of moist rales. Mild pitting edema observed in both lower extremities. Blood tests were conducted (Figs. 2 and 3), revealing an absolute CD4 + T lymphocyte count of 26 cells/µL. Sputum smear analysis identified yeast-like spores and Gram-positive cocci, while blood culture confirmed the presence of Staphylococcus epidermidis, Metagenomic Next-Generation Sequencing (mNGS) of Bronchoalveolar Lavage Fluid (BALF) detected Pneumocystis jirovecii (30 sequences), Human herpesvirus 1 (60 sequences), Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus. Her creatinine (Cr) level was 1080 µmol/L, and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) measured 26.7 mmol/L. Chest CT (Fig. 4) revealed diffuse exudation and interstitial changes in both lungs. Arterial blood gas analysis indicated hypoxemia. Pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (Pr-BNP) was significantly elevated to 4976 pg/mL, and the electrocardiogram displayed T-wave changes in several leads. The echocardiogram showed enlargement of the atria and ventricles and pulmonary hypertension. D-dimer levels were significantly elevated to 2.99 mg/L, and CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) showed demonstrated thrombus formation in the sub-segmental pulmonary artery of the right lower lobe. The comprehensive diagnosis was: (1) AIDS with multiple infections (Pneumocystis jirovecii, Yeast-like fungi, Staphylococcus epidermidis, Epstein-Barr virus and Cytomegalovirus infections); (2) MODS; (3) Acute sub-segmental pulmonary embolism. The patient’s condition deteriorated rapidly, with progressive dyspnea and expectoration of pink frothy sputum. SpO2 remained below 90% despite high-flow oxygen inhalation. Non-invasive ventilation was initiated; however, respiratory failure worsened, leading to impaired consciousness. Consequently, endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation were performed. Subsequently, heart rate, blood pressure, and SpO2 could not be detected. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation, including external chest compression and epinephrine, etc. was conducted for 15 min, resulting in the restoration of spontaneous circulation. Patients received oral sulfamethoxazole–trimethoprim (0.48 g per tablet, two tablets three times daily) for Pneumocystis jirovecii infection, intravenous voriconazole (6 mg/kg every 12 h) for yeast-like fungal infection, intravenous meropenem (1 g every 8 h) for Staphylococcus epidermidis infection, and intravenous ganciclovir sodium (5 mg/kg every 12 h) for Epstein–Barr virus and cytomegalovirus infections. There was a significant increase in alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and total bilirubin (TBIL) and direct bilirubin (DBIL), was observed, with peak values of 2619 U/L for ALT, 9816 U/L for AST, and 114.4 µmol/L for TBIL, consistent with diagnosis of acute severe drug-induced liver injury. Throughout the course of the disease, sequential organ failure and decompensation occurred in the respiratory, circulatory, hepatic, renal, coagulation (with a tendency toward disseminated intravascular coagulation, DIC), and central nervous systems. Active treatments including mechanical ventilation, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, vasoactive drugs, and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT, specifically in continuous veno-venous hemodiafiltration, mode is CVVHD) were implemented to effectively address electrolyte and acid-base disorders, manage fluid overload, eliminate inflammatory mediators (PCT, CRP, and IL-6, etc.), maintain homeostasis, and administer mild hypothermia therapy. The initial empirical anti-infection therapy proved ineffective. The treatment plan was modilfied based on the drug sensitivity results of from BALF mNGS. MZ–TMP and Voriconazole was discontinued due to liver injury and replaced with Liposomal amphotericin B (L-AMB, 10 mg/kg). Intravenous clindamycin was administered at a dose of 600 mg every 8 h for Pneumocystis jirovecii infection. Continue intravenous meropenem treatment (1 g every 8 h) for Staphylococcus epidermidis infection. The patient received intravenous ganciclovir sodium 0.625 mg/kg three times weekly following dialysis for the treatment of Epstein–Barr virus and cytomegalovirus infections. Anticoagulation for pulmonary embolism was achieved with subcutaneous low-molecular-weight heparin (calcium heparin, 100 U/kg daily) plus oral rivaroxaban 20 mg once daily. Acute heart failure was treated with diuresis, vasodilation and inotropic therapy. Glutathione (1.2 g daily) and ademetionine (1000 mg daily) were used for liver protection and jaundice reduction in the treatment of drug-induced liver injury. Enteral and parenteral step-by-step nutrition was provided, and anemia and hypoproteinemia were addressed. Once severe complications were controlled, ART was initiated, antiretroviral therapy included tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF 300 mg once weekly), lamivudine (3TC 25 mg once daily), dolutegravir (DTG 50 mg once daily). Figures 2 and 3 depict the dynamic evolution of key indicators including inflammation and organ function. Following multidisciplinary collaborative treatment, the patient regained consciousness on May 18, the endotracheal tube was removed on May 20, and all indicators gradually improved. Chest CT (Fig. 5) revealed significant absorption of pulmonary exudates. The patient was clinically cured, discharged from the hospital and continued ART along with regular hemodialysis.

Discussion

According to United Nations AIDS (UNAIDS) [3], there are approximately 40.8 million HIV-infected individuals worldwide as of 2024, and about 44.1 million deaths due to AIDS-related diseases. Worldwide, one in three people seek health care for HIV/AIDS-related OIs [1]. Without treatment, HIV will gradually destroy the immune system and AIDS [4, 5]. From the perspectives of critical care medicine, infectious disease, and advanced life support, an analysis of the time-series data from blood tests presented in Figs. 2 and 3 reveals that the patient experienced a fatal Co-infection. This infection triggered a severe cytokine storm, resulting in sequential MODS, the disease was successfully reversed. From D1 to D15, the patient exhibited profound immune deficiency. A Cr level of 1080 µmol/L, BUN level of 26.7 mmol/L, elevated PCT, CRP, and IL-6, along with a (1–3)-β-D-glucan level as high as 695.17 pg/mL indicate renal failure complicated by deep-seated fungal infection. Etiological examination confirmed the presence of Pneumocystis and Candida. From D18 to D33, the core inflammatory mediator IL-6 increased to 509.4 pg/mL. ALT surged to 2619 U/L, AST rose to 9816 U/L, and TBIL reached 114.4 µmol/L, likely due to the combined effects of medications such as voriconazole and sulfonamides, ischemia-hypoxia, and direct damage from inflammatory factors. cTnI increased from 30.97 µg/L to 152.50 µg/L, while MYO also reached 1501.5 ng/mL, correlating with the occurrence of cardiac arrest. On D18, D-Dimer was elevated to 65.09 mg/L, and INR rose to 2.52, indicating a propensity for DIC and potential events such as pulmonary embolism. From D33 to D46, intensive advanced life support broke the vicious cycle. Between D51 to D59, inflammatory markers and organ functions gradually improved. The critical aspects of treatment involve precise anti-infection strategies and the effective disruption of the inflammatory cascade and organ damage through advanced life support.

OIs play a crucial role in the morbidity and mortality of HIV-infected individuals, responsible for 94.1% of deaths related to HIV [6–8]. The patient a perilous progression from immune collapse to infection outbreak, then to followed by an inflammatory storm, multiple organ dysfunction, and ultimately, the challenging reversal of the disease was accomplished through critical care medicine and multidisciplinary teamwork. The core essence of treatment involves multi-dimensional advanced life support and targeted interventions for organ function. The patient exhibited dysfunction in multiple organs including the kidneys, lungs, heart, liver, and brain. The prompt initiation of CRRT not only substitutes renal function but also establishes a stable internal environment for that facilitates the recovery of other organ functions including the heart and lungs, by enabling continuous and gradual fluid removal and the adsorption of inflammatory mediators. When initiating empirical anti-infection treatment, it is essential to promptly identify the spectrum of infectious pathogens using BALF-mNGS. Despite major advances in ART, opportunistic infections remain a major cause of morbidity and mortality, especially in low-and middle-income countries where ART access and adherence are limited [8]. Over the past 20 years, ART has proven a critical component in fighting against the HIV pandemic, which causes the AIDS [9]. Current standards for HIV treatment advise starting ART as soon as possible following an HIV-positive test result [10, 11]. The timing of ART initiation for the patient needs to be carefully weighed. Initiating ART too early may increase the risk of immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) and drug interactions/toxicity, while initiating it too late may not be able to repair the immune system. These symptoms typically correlate with the degree of immunosuppression, with patients having CD4 T cell counts < 100 cells/µl being at the highest risk for severe outcomes [12–14]. The patient ART after experiencing a critical phase organ dysfunction, including acute liver injury and while maintaining relatively stable vital signs. The patient tolerated the treatment well. Rapid ART is helpful in inhibiting viral replication, promoting the recovery of CD4 + T lymphocytes, and is crucial for preventing subsequent infections and improving the prognosis. Aptly called “Rapid Start”, this linkage intervention has an evidence base with numerous studies demonstrating its effectiveness in decreasing the time required to initiate treatment and to achieve viral suppression [11, 15, 16]. Early diagnosis and treatment are therefore critical in preventing severe complications and improving patient outcomes [14, 17, 18].

Notably, the single-case design limits definitive causal inferences regarding opportunistic infections, pulmonary embolism, and comorbidities. It is essential to reinforce standardized multidisciplinary care, and large-scale studies are necessary to elucidate these interactions and enhance clinical management. The intensive care unit is responsible for diagnosis, decision-making and management, while other specialties provide collaborative support. Multidisciplinary collaboration is carried out to address complex related clinical issues. Meanwhile, Successful clinical trial execution relies on a well-structured workflow and seamless collaboration among multidisciplinary teams [19]. Teamwork is considered a dynamic process that involves two or more healthcare professionals who share a common background and skill set, pursue the same healthcare goals, and engage in joint physical and mental efforts to assess, plan, or evaluate patient care [20].

The successful resuscitation in this case results from the close collaboration among multiple disciplines, including critical care medicine and infectious diseases. Even in patients with AIDS-related opportunistic infections, who exhibit severely compromised immunity and multiple organ dysfunction, a favorable prognosis can still be attained through comprehensive treatments. These treatments incorporate modern critical care practices and multidisciplinary collaborative technologies, such as CRRT and advanced respiratory and circulatory life support. The treatment of patients with AIDS-related opportunistic infections complicated by MODS, necessitates additional high-quality clinical studies. Such research is essential to establish evidence regarding the optimal timing and modality of CRRT, targeted pathogen treatment, the ideal timing for Rapid ART initiation, prevention of IRIS, and the effectiveness of multidisciplinary collaborative management.
