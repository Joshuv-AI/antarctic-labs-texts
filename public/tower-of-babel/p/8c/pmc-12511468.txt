Acidifying agents impact erlotinib and gefitinib pharmacokinetic parameters and elevate liver enzymes in Wistar rats
Authors: Amsha S. Alsegiani, Aliyah Almomen, Maria Arafah, Nourah Z. Alzoman, Abdullah K.Alshememry
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Erlotinib (ERL) and Gefitinib (GEF) are weakly basic drugs with pH-dependent solubility profiles mainly dependent on stomach pH. We proposed possible drug and/or food-drug interactions with acidifying agents. Citric acid (CA) and phosphoric acid (PPA) are the most common acidifying agents used in food and medication to treat certain conditions. This study evaluated the impact of concomitant consumption of CA and PA on ERL and GEF pharmacokinetic parameters (PKs). The PKs of ERL and GEF were investigated in rats after four weeks of CA and PPA consumption in low (175 mg/kg) and high (100 mg/kg) doses using UPLC-MS/MS. Data indicated that acidifying agents altered PKs of ERL and GEF dose-dependently. High doses of CA and PPA significantly increased the Cmax of ERL by 103% and 218%, the AUC0–72 by 35% and 78%, respectively, while reducing CL/F by 44% with CA and 74% with PPA. For GEF, Low and high doses of PPA increased Cmax and Tmax with a reduction of CL/F. A low dose of CA did not impact Cmax, significantly decreased AUC0–72 (28%), and increased CL/F (16%). The high dose of CA increased Cmax (13%) with no impact on AUC0–72 and decreased CL/F (23%). Furthermore, both doses of acidifying agents significantly increased liver enzyme levels and reduced body weight within two weeks. The results demonstrated that the PKs of both drugs were changed, and caution and close monitoring should be taken with ERL and GEF when co-administered with an acidifying agent.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the second most common cancer, and non-small cell lung carcinoma (NSCLC) is the most prevalent type of lung cancer (Thandra et al. 2021). NSCLC is commonly diagnosed at young ages, between 20 and 46 years old, and 25% of cases are not attributable to smoking, indicating it is more associated with heritable mutations (Arnold et al. 2016; Sun et al. 2007). The estimated worldwide incidence of NSCLC is approximately 2.2 million per year, and there were 1.8 million deaths reported in 2020. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) are effective target chemotherapies for advanced NSCLC treatment with mutations in epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), with a high response rate (Alanazi et al. 2020; Ladanyi & Pao 2008; Masood et al. 2019). Erlotinib (ERL) and Gefitinib (GEF) are first-generation oral reversible TKIs approved as the first-line treatment for patients with known EGFR-activating mutations, with a 65–90% response rate and progression-free survival rates (Ladanyi & Pao 2008). These TKIs bind reversibly to the adenosine triphosphate (ATP) binding pockets on the EGFR receptors, inhibiting cell activation, angiogenesis, downstream signaling, and reducing metastatic potential (Modi & Andey 2024).

The ERL and GEF are weak base drugs with a pH-dependent solubility profile that mainly depends on the stomach pH. Their optimal drug absorption was detected at a physiologically low stomach pH (pH = 2–3) and decreased with increasing gastric/intestinal pH due to disease or concomitant medication. Various antiacid drugs, such as ranitidine and omeprazole, increased stomach pH, leading to a 40–50% decrease in their absorption (Kletzl et al. 2015; Kumarakulasinghe et al. 2016). In addition, ERL pharmacokinetic parameters (PKs)and bioavailability have been reported to increase by nearly 100% when ingested with food and decrease by 40% when taken on an empty stomach (Ling et al. 2008). Similarly, GEF drug exposure was increased by 32% with food and reduced by 34% on an empty stomach (H. Swaisland et al. 2001; H. C. Swaisland et al. 2005). Furthermore, recent findings from our group showed that the co-administration of flavored water or aspartame, an artificial non-saccharide sweetener, can alter the PKs of ERL and GEF (Almomen et al. 2020; AlRasheed et al. 2022). Therefore, ERL and GEF are candidates for drug-drug and food-drug interactions that might affect their exposure and induce drug-related toxicities.

As a healthy lifestyle is recommended during cancer treatment (Kizub et al. 2025), the general population has recently widely consumed acidic food. It is found abundantly in many foods as natural acids or acid additives that are reported to induce liver injury and affect liver metabolism (Chen et al. 2014; Chun et al. 2016). Acid additives, also known as acidifying agents, are compounds classified as organic, such as citric acid (CA), or inorganic compounds, such as phosphoric acid (PPA). CA and PPA are the most commonly used acidifying agents and are widely consumed as part of a regular diet as antioxidants and preservatives (Blekas 2016). In medication, CA is combined with a stimulant laxative to help resolve constipation or empty the bowel; constipation is a common problem in cancer patients (Larkin et al. 2018; Song et al. 2014). and PPA is also used as an ingredient in electrolyte replenishment and total parenteral nutrition (TPN) therapy that commonly describe in cancer patients with malnutrition (Cotogni et al. 2020; Li et al. 2019; Yu et al. 2020). Therefore, these acidifying agents may impact the expression or activity of transporter proteins and cytochrome P450 enzymes and affect ERL and GEF metabolized in the liver (Li et al. 2007).

Analytical methods for determining ERL and GEF with other drugs or food in biological samples are available (Almomen et al. 2020; AlRasheed et al. 2022; Kletzl et al. 2015; Kumarakulasinghe et al. 2016; Ling et al. 2008; R. W. van Leeuwen et al. 2016); however, a method for their simultaneous determination with acidifying agents, such as PPA and CA, does not exist. Therefore, this study aims to investigate whether drug-food and/or drug-drug interactions exist during the use of ERL or GEF with long-term intake of CA and PPA as the most common acidic source in drug and acid-additive food. The availability of such a method could offer valuable insights into ERL and GEF's potential drug-drug and/or food-drug interactions, ultimately improving care for NSCLC patients.

Materials and methods

Materials

ERL and GEF were obtained from Haoyuan Chemexpress Co., Ltd., Shanghai, PR China. Domperidone was used as the internal standard (IS) and was purchased from Sigma Aldrich (Chemie GmbH, Steinheim, Germany). Phosphoric acid and citric acid were obtained from Merck. Methanol of HPLC grade was obtained from Panreac, E.U. The liver enzyme kit (ALT, AST, and GGT) was purchased through the United Diagnostic Industry, Dammam, KSA. ERL and GEF were obtained from Pfizer Inc. (New York, USA).

Experimental animals

Fifty ten-week-old male Wistar rats weighing 250 ± 30 g were obtained from the animal house of the College of Pharmacy at King Saud University, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Rats were randomly assigned to nine cages (n = 5). Cages were housed at a room temperature of 25 ◦C and an average humidity of 50%. Animals had free access to commercial standard food and water. All rats were observed daily to ensure that all animals were healthy. The animal experiments followed the ethical guidelines of the Ethical Committee for Performing Studies on Animals at King Saud University, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, protocol number KSU-SE-19–13.

PK study design

To test the effect of CA and PPA, fifty male Wistar rats were divided into 5 groups, two cages for each group, cage A and cage B. Animal groups were as follows: Group I received water, group II received a low dose of CA (175 mg/kg), group III received a high dose of CA (1000 mg/kg), group IV received a low dose of PPA (175 mg/kg) and group V received a high dose of PPA (1000 mg/kg). All CA and PPA doses were given once a day for four weeks. After 4 weeks of CA or PPA administration (day 28th), one dose of 20 mg/kg of ERL suspended in 0.5% carboxymethyl cellulose was given to cage A of groups I, II, III, VI, and V. Also, a single dose of 20 mg/kg of GEF suspended in 0.5% carboxymethyl cellulose was given to cages B in groups II, III, VI, and V. Drugs were administered to rats between 7:00 and 8:00 am. Blood samples were collected via tail vein in heparin tubes at predetermined intervals post-drug administration at 0.5, 1, 2,4,6,8, 24,48, and 72 h., then immediately centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min at 4 ◦C, and plasma samples were kept frozen at − 20 ◦C until the day of analysis. Animals'body weights were measured once every three days over four weeks during the experiment. Statistical differences between animals in different treatment groups were calculated three times during the study: on day one before the administration of the acidifying agent, on day 12 (2 weeks), and at animal euthanasia on day 28 (4 weeks).

Sample preparation and analysis

Our previous study described sample preparation and methods of validation in detail (Almomen et al. 2020). Each sample was prepared as 25µl of plasma and 30µl of (internal standard) IS; the volume was completed to 100µl with methanol. Analytes were extracted from the plasma matrix using the solid phase extraction method (SPE). UPLC-MS/MS system analysis was carried out using a Waters Xevo TQ-S triple quadrupole mass spectrometer detector. The chromatography separations were achieved using Waters Xevo TQ-S (Waters, Singapore), C18 column (100 × 1.0 mm, 1.7 µm particle size) (Waters, Dublin, Ireland). Chromatographic analysis was performed at a 0.2 mL/min flow rate using a mixture of ACT water (80: 20 (v/v)) with 0.1% formic acid. Suitable dilution of the plasma sample was made before the actual analysis. The quantitative analysis was performed using the MRM mode of transitions from protonated precursor [M + H] + to product ions at m/z 447.25 > 128.08 (GEF), m/z 394.20 > 278.04 (ERL), and m/z 426.26 > 175.07 for the internal standard domperidone.

Liver enzyme analysis

Another set of animals was used to evaluate the effect of acidifying agents on rat Liver enzymes. Sixty rats were divided into 12 cages (n = 5); six were used to measure Liver enzymes after two weeks of acidifier administration, and the other 6 were used to measure Liver enzymes after 4 weeks of acidifier administration. Groups at each time point were as follows: regarding CA, group 1 received water, group II received a low dose (175 mg/kg) of CA, and group III received a high dose (1000 mg/kg) of CA. Regarding PA, group 1 received water, group II received a low dose (175 mg/kg) of PPA, and Group III received a high dose (1000 mg/kg) of PPA. All doses were given once a day through oral gavage. Blood samples were obtained before CA and PPA administration and after two or four weeks of CA and PPA administration. Liver enzyme levels were measured using liver enzyme kits, Rat CYP450 kit (MM0247R2 Jiangsu Baolai Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China), following the manufacturer's protocol.

Liver histology

Toward the end of the Liver enzymes study, animals underwent euthanasia, and Liver tissues were harvested and preserved in 10% formalin for 48 h. Subsequently, the tissues underwent dehydration using 70% ethanol before being forwarded to the Department of Pathology, College of Medicine, King Saud University, for staining with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E). Pathologists conducted histological analysis of liver tissues from both treated and untreated animals using light microscopy.

PKs analyses

Sample preparation and UPLC-MS/MS analysis were performed as previously described in our earlier study(Almomen et al. 2020; AlRasheed et al. 2022; Maher et al. 2016). PK analyses of ERL and GEF were conducted in all treated rats using non-compartmental analysis (NCA) with PKSolver, an Excel 2010 Add-in. The maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) and the time to reach (Tmax) were determined directly from the plasma concentration–time profiles (Fig. 1a-b). Graphs were plotted with GraphPad Prism 8.0.2.Fig. 1Plasma concentration–time curve of a) ERL and b) GEF after oral administration in rats receiving low and high citric and phosphoric acid doses

Plasma concentration–time curve of a) ERL and b) GEF after oral administration in rats receiving low and high citric and phosphoric acid doses

To estimate the terminal elimination rate constant (λz), linear regression was applied to the terminal log-linear phase of the concentration–time curves, using three time points for ERL and four for GEF. The elimination half-life (T½) was calculated using the equation: T1/2 = 0693/λz. However, T½ is indirectly influenced by clearance and volume of distribution, both normalized by bioavailability.The area under the plasma concentration–time curve from time zero to the last measurable concentration at 72 h (AUC0-72) was calculated using the linear trapezoidal rule. Total AUC from time zero to infinity (AUC0-∞) was obtained by summing up AUC0-t and the extrapolated area from the last measurable time point to infinity, which was calculated as (Clast) divided by λz. The apparent oral clearance (CL/F) was determined using the equation: CL/F = Dose/AUC0-∞, where the dose was 20 mg/kg for ERL and GEF, CL represents drug clearance, and F is the bioavailability. However, Formulation-dependent bioavailability influenced Cl/F values.

Statistical analysis

After 4 weeks of consuming acidifying agents, the PKs of each treatment group were compared to the control group using two-way ANOVA, followed by the Bonferroni multiple comparisons test. Statistically significant values were represented as ≤ 0.05 (*), P ≤ 0.01 (**), P ≤ 0.001 (***), and P ≤ 0.0001 (****) comparison between each treatment group and control. All results were expressed as mean ± SD standard deviation (SD).

Discussion

Performing the possibility of drug-drug and/or food-drug interaction is essential for studying the factors that impact the efficacy and safety of the drug during treatment and is critical for determining drug bioavailability and PKs (Butler & Riley 2022; Choi & Ko 2017; Palleria et al. 2013). This study was conducted as an extension of our previous work to examine the bioavailability of ERL and GEF and the possibility of drug-drug and/or drug-food interaction (Almomen et al. 2020; AlRasheed et al. 2022). For the first time, we investigated the concomitant effects of the prolonged consumption of two doses of typical acidifying agents, CA or PPA, on the bioavailability of ERL or GEF in rodents. We also examined their impact on liver enzymes and rat body weight. Then, we investigated the effect of repeated doses of these acidifying agents on hepatic inflammation at a histological level.

Rats were selected for medical research because of their complex genomic, anatomical, and physiological resemblance to humans, making them a suitable model for researching human diseases and conditions (Blais et al. 2017; Gibbs et al. 2004). The study was performed by quantitatively analyzing plasma samples of Wistar rats at different time points (0.5, 1, 2,4,6,8, 24,48, and 72 h.) following a single dose of 20 mg/kg oral administration of both ERL and GEF. The lower dose (175 mg/kg) of acidifying agents was selected to reflect a physiologically relevant level approximating high-end human dietary exposure when adjusted for body surface area using standard allometric scaling (Reagan-Shaw et al. 2008). The higher dose (1000 mg/kg) was chosen to explore potential dose-dependent effects and to model excessive or chronic intake scenarios that could occur through concentrated dietary supplements or high consumption of medications containing acidifying agents.

It was confirmed that the solubility of ERL and GEF is pH-dependent and decreases with increasing gastric and intestinal pH due to disease or concomitant medication (Kumarakulasinghe et al. 2016). Consequently, our results showed that both low and high doses of the two acidifying agents tested increased the mean exposure of ERL by increasing all PKs, except for the AUC0-72, in a low dose of CA (175 mg/kg). They also decreased the CL/F in all groups. Differences in ERL PKs were more pronounced at the high dose (1000 mg/kg), indicating that they affect the PKs of ERL in a dose-dependent manner. In addition, the increase in Cmax, Tmax, and T1/2 suggests that acidifying agents might impact the mean exposure of the drug and require attention regarding dose adjustment. The exposure of these drugs could have increased because of the interference of citrates and phosphates with GI tight junctions (Lambros et al. 2022; Ritz et al. 2012). However, the low dose of CA had no impact on the GEF Cmax, Tmax, and T1/2 compared to a higher dose, indicating that the low concentration of CA does not alter GEF exposure. In addition, the low CA dose showed increased drug apparent oral clearance and AUC0-∞ with a decrease in AUC0-72, indicating an impact on the dose regimen; therefore, dose adjustment is needed. On the other hand, a high dose of CA and both doses of PPA showed an increased GEF exposure, increasing Cmax and Tmax, and subsequently decreasing the apparent oral clearance.

In parallel with our results, it has been reported that acid-inhibitory drugs, such as histamine-2 receptor antagonists (H2 Blockers) and proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), increase gastric and intestinal pH, which may decrease TKI solubility, bioavailability, and therapeutic efficacy (Patel et al. 2020; R. W. F. van Leeuwen et al. 2017). Therefore, our finding suggests that acidifying agents have the opposite effect on TKIs. Similar to our results suggestion, earlier clinical studies showed that using acidic beverages, such as cola, significantly increases the mean exposure of ERL in patients treated with proton pump inhibitor (PPI) because of increased solubility and absorption rate (Peters et al. 2014; R. W. van Leeuwen et al. 2016).

There is strong evidence that the impact of acidifying agents was more pronounced on body weight. It has been shown that a high phosphorus diet significantly reduces body weight in humans and animals (Ayoub et al. 2015; Chun et al. 2016). In addition, high citric acid diet consumption was associated with body weight loss and toxicity (Roy et al. 2004; Saleem et al. 2004). In parallel with these reports, both doses of all acidifying agent groups experienced a significant reduction in body weight compared to the control groups. However, body weight reduction is an important variable that may independently influence PKs and liver function (Walter-Sack & Klotz 1996). A decrease in body weight can affect drug distribution, metabolism, and clearance through several mechanisms, including altered hepatic enzyme activity, changes in plasma protein binding, and reduced adipose or lean tissue compartments that influence drug volume of distribution. In our study, all animals were monitored for weight changes throughout the treatment period. While weight loss was observed in treatment groups, we attempted to minimize this confounding factor by standardizing drug dosing based on body weight (mg/kg).

Previous preclinical studies have shown that specific concentrations of the food additive CA can cause liver damage by decreasing antioxidative enzyme activity, such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT), and aspartate transaminase (AST) (Chen et al. 2014). Another study has shown that liver injury in high-phosphorus diets may be caused by reducing protein synthesis and increasing lipid, fatty acid, and glucose metabolism(Abuduli et al. 2016; Chun et al. 2016; Ohdachi et al. 1999). Similarly, our results showed that low and high doses of both acidifying agents have significantly increased all tested Liver enzymes used in the study after 2 and 4 weeks (ALT, ALP, GGT, and AST).

Furthermore, it was recognized that most TKIs induced hepatotoxicity (Shah et al. 2013). This hepatic injury potential might be related to a particular chemical structure or related to drug metabolism by liver enzymes (Kijima et al. 2011; Shah et al. 2013). At the histological level, our results showed that liver sections from control rats that received one dose of ERL or GEF showed no significant pathological changes in the lymphocytic lobules. Furthermore, we observed that consuming acidifying agents produced mild portal lymphocytic inflammation with GEF and mild portal lymphocytic inflammation with ERL. Moreover, we observed that GEF produced mild portal lymphocytic inflammation after consuming acidifying agents and moderate portal lymphocytic inflammation with ERL. Liver damage and elevated liver enzymes induced by acidifying agents can affect drug metabolism, particularly CYP450 enzymes. Since ERL and GEF are extensively metabolized by CYP3A4 (Ionescu and Caira 2010; Josephs et al. 2013; Kijima et al. 2011; Xu & Li 2019)CYP1A1 significantly influences ERL metabolism, and CYP2D6 plays a significant role in GEF metabolism; their concentration and exposure can be altered by concurrent use with other drugs or foods containing CA or PPA agents. All this evidence points to acidifying agents increasing and accelerating the liver inflammatory crisis of TKIs. Overall, ERL and GEF induced acute hepatocellular injury and hepatobiliary cholestasis with repeated doses of acidifying agents. To prevent hepatotoxicity, monitoring liver function should be considered during ERL and GEF treatment with protracted consumption of acidifying agents (PPA and CA).

There is a potential for chronic exposure to CA and PPA, which are common in certain foods and drugs containing CA or PPA agents, to modulate drug metabolism enzymes and transporters over time. These findings may be particularly relevant for patients with high intake of medicines or foods containing CA or PPA agents, which could alter therapeutic efficacy or toxicity. It is essential to consider such interactions in clinical settings, especially for drugs with narrow therapeutic indices or those extensively metabolized in the liver or gut.

Conclusions and future direction

Prolonged consumption of low and high doses of CA and PPA significantly alters ERL and GEF PKs in a dose-dependent manner, and drug bioavailability requires careful monitoring. These findings can be used to optimize the management of drug-drug and/or food-drug interactions between these acidifying agents and patients taking ERL and GEF. In addition, acidifying agents significantly increased liver enzyme levels and reduced body weight within two weeks. Furthermore, acidifying agents increase and accelerate the liver inflammatory crisis when co-administered with ERL and GEF. However, our study has several limitations, such as a single dose of ERL and GEF being investigated after four weeks of CA and PPA administration. Monitoring multiple doses of ERL and GEF warrants further investigation. Further clinical studies should be conducted to understand and explore the impact of acidifying agents and TKI drug combinations in human populations. Finally, it would be desirable if the effect of acidifying agents on ERL and GEF were identified on human liver enzymes.
