Sustainable Ball Milling‐Assisted Synthesis of Bread Waste‐Derived Highly Porous Carbons for Adsorption‐Based Applications
Authors: Barbara Szczęśniak, Adam Kapusta, Jerzy Choma, Mietek Jaroniec
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A facile synthesis was used for the preparation of highly porous carbons from bread waste. This synthesis relied on the fast ball milling of only two reagents, dry bread, and a mild activation agent, followed by direct thermal treatment at 800°C, afforded carbons with high specific surface areas up to 2400 m2·g−1, and a large pore volume up to 1.56 cm3·g−1. This seems to be the first attempt to employ ball milling for the synthesis of sustainable bread‐derived highly porous carbons. Effective activation was achieved thanks to homogeneous distribution of the activating agent throughout the precursor, ensured by mechanochemical treatment. These carbons adsorbed a high amount of carbon dioxide at 0°C and 1 bar, up to 6.7 mmol·g−1. Moreover, cyclic stability, CO2/N2 selectivity at 0°C, and benzene adsorption–desorption isotherms at 20°C were determined for the selected samples. High porosity in carbons is essential for adsorption and catalysis‐related applications. Additionally, the proposed strategy provides a solution for cost‐effective and feasible food waste management.

Introduction

Food waste represents a major global challenge, contributing to resource inefficiency, substantial economic losses, and escalating environmental pollution. According to the 2024 report by the Food and Agriculture Organization, the financial losses resulting from food waste amount to about 1 billion USD annually [1]. In light of growing environmental concerns and the need for sustainable global economic development, there is an increasing interest in innovative solutions for resource recovery and waste management. One promising approach involves the conversion of food waste into functional materials with desirable properties, e.g., carbon‐based materials for catalysis or adsorption‐related applications.

The annual global bread production exceeds 100 million tons. Europe leads the global bread market with a 53.6% share, followed by the United States at 28.6%, the Asia‐Pacific region at 10.9%, and the Middle East and Africa at 6.9% [2]. Bread waste represents a major global concern, roughly accounting for about 10% of total bread production. Due to its organic and biogenic composition, bread waste poses significant environmental and public health risks, contributing to the degradation of natural ecosystems. Notably, bread waste is rich in glucose‐based polymers, primarily starch, which comprise up to 70% of its content and can serve as a valuable feedstock to produce carbon‐based materials [3, 4].

Carbon materials are essential to modern science and technology, with broad industrial and everyday life applications [5]. Converting food waste into carbon materials has emerged as a promising area of research. Several recent studies have been devoted to carbons derived from bread. Yuan et al. [6] prepared multifunctional carbon foams using bread as the primary raw material via a simple carbonization process. The resulting foams exhibited a high compressive strength of 3.6 MPa and a density of 0.29 g·cm−3. Similarly, Yu et al. [7] converted bread into carbon foam attractive for microextraction applications, e.g., as an adsorbent of polybrominated diphenyl ethers. The obtained mesoporous carbon foam had a high specific surface area (SSA) of 872 m2·g−1. Tian et al. [8] developed a straightforward pyrolysis‐based method utilizing inexpensive and available precursors such as wheat flour, sodium bicarbonate, cysteine, and cobalt nitrate to produce hierarchical porous carbons embedded with core–shell nanoparticles and nitrogen and sulphur heteroatoms. These composite carbon‐based materials exhibited high SSAs of up to 734 m2·g−1. Elsewhere [9], carbon composites containing TiO2 were synthesized via in situ pyrolysis using bread as the carbon precursor and tetrabutyl orthotitanate as TiO2 source. In the synthesis, the naturally porous, sponge‐like structure of bread that retains moisture was used to facilitate the hydrolysis of tetrabutyl orthotitanate. The resulting C‐TiO2 composite, containing 38.8 wt.% carbon, was evaluated as an anode material for lithium‐ion batteries, delivering a reversible capacity of 231 mAh·g−1 at a current density of 100 mA·g−1 after 200 cycles. Cao et al. [10] demonstrated that direct pyrolysis and CO2 activation (at 850°C for 90 min) of waste bread resulted in activated carbon foam (with a yield of 16.8%) exhibiting a high SSA of 1575 m2·g−1 and a large total pore volume of 0.88 cm3·g−1. Taslim et al. [11] described a similar method yielding a 3D hierarchical porous carbon with a SSA of up to 610 m2·g−1. The sample was used as electrode material, displaying a high specific capacitance of 202 F·g−1 at 1 A·g−1 in 1 M H2SO4. Deng et al. [12] developed a strategy for producing three‐dimensionally hierarchical porous carbon composed of macro‐, meso‐, and micropores by mixing biomass sources (including α‐cellulose, xylose, glucose, starch, chitin, sucrose, and bamboo) with KHCO3, followed by high‐temperature treatment. The resulting porous carbons had high surface areas of up to 1893 m2·g−1 and a total pore volume of up to 1.37 cm3·g−1. Wood et al. [13] proposed utilizing waste bread‐derived carbon electrodes for water desalination. These electrodes featured hierarchical pore structures with high salt adsorption capacities. Duan et al. [14] reported the recycling of bread waste to synthesize a conductive carbon composite decorated with silver nanoparticles for chemical sensor applications. Elsewhere [3], a sample of food waste (selected food such as apples, bread, green beans, cabbage, cheese, and canned chicken mixed together in a food processor) containing about 70% of moisture was first subjected to hydrothermal treatment (220°C, 30 min), followed by KOH activation at 800°C, gave highly porous carbons with a high surface area of 2885 m2·g−1 and a large total pore volume of 1.93 cm3·g−1. The sample exhibited an exceptional hydrogen storage capacity of 6.15 wt.% at ‐197°C and under 23 bar. The main limitation of the synthesis method is its low yield; the resulting hydrocarbon yield was about 23%, and it decreased further during the activation process due to the release of H2O, CO2, and CO. Pietrzyk‐Thel [15] investigated the conversion of stale bread into sponge‐like carbon materials for dye adsorption and supercapacitors. Activated carbon obtained at 900°C showed a well‐balanced microporous and mesoporous structure with a high SSA (~1583 m2·g−1), making it a cost‐effective adsorbent for removing crystal violet dye from aqueous solutions. Davidraj et al. [16] provided a comprehensive review of recent advancements in nanoporous carbons derived from food waste, including bread, for energy storage. The review covers strategies for converting food waste into high‐performance nanoporous carbon, highlighting how intrinsic (e.g., elemental composition) and extrinsic (e.g., processing conditions) factors influence their electrochemical efficiency. These materials are attractive due to their abundance, low cost, easy processing, and low environmental impact. Overall, porous carbons can be prepared from bread waste, e.g., by using direct CO2 activation methods, typically yielding materials with high SSAs of about 1600 m2·g−1 [10, 11]. However, the synthesis yield of physically activated bread‐derived carbons is rather low. Chemical activation methods are primarily based on KOH activation [17], and it is worth exploring other, more sustainable alternatives that could improve the porosity and efficiency of the resulting carbon materials while maintaining an operational simplicity, analogous as those in physical activation processes.

In this work, a simple and efficient method was developed to produce highly porous carbons from bread waste using a strategy based on ball milling and chemical activation. This approach not only offers a sustainable route for creating functional porous carbons but also provides a practical solution for food waste management.

Experimental

Synthesis of Chemically Activated Bread‐Derived Carbons

In brief, 1 g of powdered, dried bread and 4 g of an activating agent were milled for 30 min with a rotation speed of 500 rpm in the Planetary Mono Mill PULVERISETTE 6 classic line, ball milling machine (Fritsch). Next, a solid mixture was directly transferred to a tube furnace and carbonized at 800°C for 1 h under flowing nitrogen with a heating rate of 5°C·min−1. After cooling, the obtained carbons were purified in 2 M HCl and stirred with a magnetic stirrer overnight to remove residues. Finally, the resulting carbons were rinsed with water and dried at 80°C. The as‐prepared carbons were denoted as 1B:4x, where x represents the activator used: C ‐ potassium carbonate; O ‐ potassium oxalate; Cit ‐ potassium citrate. A reference sample prepared without the addition of an activating agent was denoted as BN8. Additionally, the sample labeled as 1B:4C‐mortar was obtained the same way as 1B:4C but using a mortar and pestle instead of the milling machine. Similarly, 1B:1KOH‐mortar sample was obtained by KOH activation using a 1:1 precursor to the activator molar ratio and mortar and pestle to mix the reagents together.

Synthesis of Physically Activated Bread‐Derived Carbons

In brief, about 5 g of powdered, dried bread was subjected to physical activation at 800°C for 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5 h under flowing CO2. Next, the samples were purified and dried in the same way as in the case of 1B:4x series. The as‐obtained carbons were labeled as BC8‐xh, where x refers to the activation time in hours. Additionally, the sample denoted as BC7‐3h was activated at 700°C for 3 h.

Measurements and Calculations

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM), X‐ray diffraction (XRD) analysis, and nitrogen adsorption data were used to characterize the synthesized carbons. SEM images were obtained using the scanning electron microscope LEO 1530 manufactured by Zeiss operated at 2 kV acceleration voltage. The instrument was equipped with energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) detector to perform elemental composition analysis. The XRD patterns were recorded using a Bruker D2 PHASER diffractometer equipped with Cu radiation, operating at 30 kV and 10 mA, over the 2θ range of 10°‐70° at room temperature. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms were measured at ‐196°C using the volumetric analyzer ASAP 2020 (Micromeritics). Experimental errors associated with measurements performed on commercial adsorption analyzers have been addressed in previous studies [18, 19]. All samples were outgassed at 200°C under vacuum for 2 h prior to adsorption measurements. Carbon dioxide adsorption isotherms were measured at 0°C and 25°C. The SSA was determined using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method based on low‐temperature nitrogen adsorption isotherms measured in the relative pressure (p/p0) range of 0.05‐0.25. The selected linear region of the relative pressure range, limited to 0.25, minimizes the influence of capillary condensation in mesopores in the SSA calculations [20]. Note that the BET surface area calculated for microporous materials may be overestimated because the model cannot distinguish between monolayer and multilayer adsorption resulting from pore filling in small micropores. Additional uncertainty may arise from using a fixed value of nitrogen cross‐sectional area (0.162 nm2) in the BET method. More recent studies have shown that this value varies slightly depending on the adsorbent type, the presence of active sites, the pore type, and adsorption temperature [21, 22]. The total pore volume (Vt) was calculated using the volume of adsorbed nitrogen at a relative pressure ≈0.99. Volumes of micropores were calculated using the nonlocal density functional theory (DFT) method for carbon slit‐shaped pores under the assumption of energetic heterogeneity and geometrical corrugation of the pore walls. Pore size distribution (PSD) and micropore volume (V micro) were calculated from low‐temperature nitrogen adsorption data using 2D‐NLDFT method for carbons with slit‐shaped pores by considering the energetic heterogeneity and geometrical corrugation of the surface. The mesopore volume (V meso) was calculated as the difference between V t and V micro.

Benzene adsorption–desorption isotherms were measured on a volumetric gas sorption analyzer, Autosorb 6300 (Anton Paar) outfitted with the vapor option. All samples were degassed in a vacuum at 200°C for 2 h prior to each adsorption experiment. The sample temperature was maintained by an external recirculating bath at 20.0°C for duration of each measurement. The purity of benzene used for analysis was >99.7%.

Results and Discussion

Morphological Characterization

SEM images of the selected activated samples 1B:4C, 1B:4O, 1B:4Cit, and BC8‐3h are presented in Figure 1. The high‐speed ball milling of bread precursor with an activating agent (potassium carbonate, potassium oxalate, or potassium citrate) affords carbons with highly developed porosity after carbonization, as observed in their SEM images (1B:4C, 1B:4O, 1B:4Cit, respectively). An interconnected carbon framework with large macropores dominates in the chemically activated samples, which is characteristic of carbon foams. In contrast, physical activation yielded carbon with different morphology (BC8‐3h), exhibiting a high contribution of quite uniformly distributed smaller pores (mesopores) in its structure.

The composition and purity of the selected activated samples 1B:4C, 1B:4O, 1B:4Cit, and BC8‐3h were examined by EDS analysis, see Table 1. These samples contain small amounts of silicon and/or sulphur that originated from quartz furnace accessories. Powder XRD patterns of all samples studied are shown in Figure 2. The patterns are characteristic of nanoporous amorphous carbons. Residual peaks observed at around 17.5° and 31.0° (2θ) for some samples, such as 1B:1KOH‐mortar, 1B:4O, or BN8, indicate the presence of residual SiO2.

Nitrogen adsorption measurements at −196°C were carried out to evaluate porosity of the resulting carbons. Figure 3 shows nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms measured at ‐196°C for all samples studied, and Figure 4 presents the corresponding PSD curves determined using the DFT method. The facile and fast milling of the dried bread with various activating agents, followed by the direct one‐step carbonization and activation process, afforded highly porous carbons with SSA in the range of 1118 – 2400 m2·g−1, Table 2. Among different activating agents used (such as K2CO3, K2C2O4, C6H5K3O7, KOH), the most effective agent for developing porosity of the bread‐derived carbons was K2C2O4. The obtained carbon, denoted as 1B:4O, exhibited a very high SSA of 2400 m2·g−1 and a large total pore volume of 1.56 cm3·g−1. Its micro‐mesoporous structure featured a large volume of micropores of about 0.97 cm3·g−1, including a high contribution of the smallest pores up to 0.7 nm (ultramicropores) of 0.24 cm3·g−1. Other salts K2CO3 and C6H5K3O7 were also efficient in the milling‐assisted synthesis of bread‐derived porous carbons, especially K2CO3, which afforded high SSA of 2178 m2·g−1 and Vt of 1.31 cm3·g−1 in the resulting carbon 1B:4C. The reference sample 1B:4C‐mortar prepared analogously, but using mixing of the precursor with the activating agent with mortar and pestle instead of ball milling, exhibited about two times lower SSA of 1038 m2·g−1 and total pore volume of 0.64 cm3·g−1, indicating the importance and high effectiveness of mechanochemical mixing of the reactants in the synthesis. Some studies [23, 24] indicate that during high‐speed milling, intense impact, compression, and shear forces break bread particles into progressively smaller fragments, producing micrometer‐sized particles. These mechanical forces induce significant structural changes in the milled material, including starch‐chain scission, restructuring, amorphization, and the formation of numerous structural defects. As a result, the carbon precursor can be uniformly mixed with the activating agent at the micrometer scale. Consequently, the subsequent activation process is more efficient than the process involving mixing with a conventional mortar and pestle [25, 26]. Activation with K2C2O4 proceeds through its thermal decomposition above 700°C, producing K2CO3, which then follows the reduction reaction, mainly according to (Equation 2) [26, 28]. During this process, carbon atoms are selectively removed from the carbon matrix, generating porosity within the framework. The released CO and CO2 gases act as secondary activating agents, further optimizing the activation process. The relatively low yield of the chemically activated carbons (4–25%) indicates that a relatively large quantity of gases is released during this process. Carbons 1B:4O and 1B:4C showed comparable or higher porosity than KOH‐activated carbon (1B:1KOH‐mortar). Despite the relatively low ratio of KOH to the precursor in the synthesis, the yield was very low, about 4%. The yield of K2CO3‐ and K2C2O4‐activated carbons is also rather low 9–12%. Higher yields are obtained while using C6H5K3O7 as an activating agent because of the incorporation of carbon atoms from the C‐rich salt into the final carbon structure (1B:4Cit). However, the 1B:4Cit sample featured a lower value of SSA as compared to those of 1B:4O, 1B:4C, or 1B:1KOH‐mortar, which can be linked to the different activation mechanisms during the thermal treatment process. The facile mechanochemical strategy using potassium oxalates, carbonates, and citrates as activating agents presented in this paper resulted in the formation of a vast number of small pores, usually in the range of micropores and tiny mesopores (24 nm), in bread‐derived activated carbons (Figure 4). Activation with organic salts (oxalates, carbonates, and citrates) as well as with CO2, leads to the selective consumption of carbon atoms and generation of very narrow pores within the carbon framework, resulting in large volumes of ultramicropores in these carbons ranging from 0.21 to 0.24 cm3·g−1. In contrast, KOH activation produces carbon (1B:1KOH‐mortar) with a broader PSD and a lower ultramicropore volume (V ultra = 0.17 cm3·g−1) [13]. The sample exhibits a substantial amount of pores in the range of 0.7–2 nm, along with a high contribution of small mesopores (Figure 4). According to the reported KOH activation mechanism [29], at 300–600°C KOH begins reacting with carbon atoms at the edges of aromatic structures, forming K2CO3, K2O, and gaseous H2. At 700°C, reactions between K2O and carbon generate abundant small pores in the carbon framework (Equation (5) ). At 800°C, K2CO3 further reacts with carbon atoms, leading to the enlargement of the existing pores. Overall, KOH is an efficient activating agent; however, its hygroscopic nature limits its reaction with the bread‐waste precursor. Increasing the KOH‐to‐bread ratio could potentially improve the structural parameters of the resulting carbon. Nevertheless, such an approach would lead to a very low carbon yield (less than 4%). In addition, higher KOH loadings pose safety concerns and can cause severe corrosion and damage to quartz furnace accessories and potentially stainless‐steel milling components. The high hygroscopicity of KOH also makes it unsuitable for solvent‐free mechanochemical milling, as it readily absorbs moisture, leading to clumping and adhesion to the grinding balls, which severely limit effective mixing. Activation with organic potassium salts appears to be a more sustainable alternative to traditional KOH activation.

A series of CO2‐activated carbons was also synthesized for comparison, see Table S1. The optimal conditions for physical activation were determined to be 3 h of CO2 treatment at 800°C (carbon BC8‐3h). The as‐obtained carbon exhibited SSA of 1841 m2·g−1 and total pore volume of 1.49 cm3·g−1. Isotherms measured for CO2‐activated carbons are type IV according to the IUPAC classification characteristic for mesoporous materials [30, 31]. The presented synthesis method for preparing bread‐derived, highly porous carbons is a relatively straightforward and cost‐effective. Note that physical activation can be carried out using standard equipment, whereas chemical activation requires a milling tool to effectively mix the activating agent with the precursor. Nevertheless, the latter approach typically yields carbon materials with a higher degree of porosity, namely a larger volume of micropores (samples 1B:4O and 1B:4C) than those obtained via CO2 activation. The energy consumption associated with both activations is similar; CO2 activation requires a longer thermal treatment time (3 h), whereas chemical activation involves the use of the potassium salt, ball milling for 30 min, 1 h of thermal treatment, and washing with a diluted HCl solution. Overall, this method offers a simple and relatively sustainable route for converting bakery food waste into porous carbons with predominantly microporous structures, which are advantageous for a wide range of applications.

Carbon Dioxide Adsorption

Carbon dioxide adsorption isotherms were measured for the selected samples, including all chemically activated carbons at 0°C and 25°C (Figure 5a and b, respectively). The highest amount of CO2 of 6.7 mmol·g−1 at 0°C and 1 bar was adsorbed by 1B:4O carbon because of its well‐developed porosity with large volume of ultramicropores. The adsorption capacity was determined as the average value obtained from five adsorption–desorption cycles (Figure S2a). Carbons 1B:4C and BC8‐3h exhibit similar pore structures and adsorption properties, capturing nearly 6.0 mmol·g−1 at 0°C and 1 bar. Carbon dioxide adsorption isotherms measured for CO2‐activated carbon BC8‐3h are presented in Figure S1. Moreover, the CO2 adsorption capacity for the best adsorbent studied (1B:4O) was measured at higher temperature, 50°C and 1 bar and it was 2.2 mmol·g−1 (Figure S2b). Apparently, the carbon studied is more suitable for pressure swing adsorption (PSA) and low temperature adsorption than for realistic flue‐gas conditions (e.g., 40–60°C and low CO2 partial pressures). Additionally, the CO2/N2 selectivity at 0°C was determined for the selected samples 1B:4O and BC8‐3h by fitting the corresponding adsorption isotherms using a virial‐type equation (Figure S3). The calculations showed that the BC8‐3h sample exhibits higher CO2/N2 selectivity (~38) than 1B:4O (~17), indicating stronger interactions between CO2 molecules and the carbon surface than N2 molecules and the carbon surface (Table S2). The enhanced selectivity of BC8‐3h may be associated with the presence of smaller ultramicropores compared to those in 1B:4O, which promote overlapping adsorption potentials and facilitate micropore filling [32]. The calculated CO2/N2 selectivity is comparable to that of other carbonaceous adsorbents [33, 34]. Table 3 shows a comparison of carbon dioxide adsorption properties of the recently reported various biomass‐derived porous carbons. The synthesized carbons in this study are among the best CO2 adsorbents synthesized in recent years. One should note that the synthesis involves nontoxic reagents and is carried out in a simple and fast manner, which makes the method especially practical for broader applications.

Adsorption of Volatile Organic Compounds

Benzene was used as a model compound to evaluate the adsorption behavior of the selected samples 1B:4O and BC8‐3h toward volatile organic compounds and acquire additional structural information about the sorbents studied. Importantly, these samples exhibit similar composition and surface chemistry, with about 8.1–8.2 at.% of oxygen, as determined by EDS analysis (Table 1). Therefore, the measured benzene isotherms confirm differences in their pore structures (Figure 6). Chemical activation with K2C2O4 leads to micro‐mesoporous carbons with a notable contribution of large mesopores. Whereas CO2 activation generates smaller pores, predominantly in the range of micropores and small mesopores. Therefore, a higher benzene adsorption capacity of 13.74 mmol·g−1 is observed at 20°C and a relative pressure 0.95 for 1B:4O sample, in comparison to BC8‐3h sample, which adsorbed 12.04 mmol·g−1 of benzene at the same measurement conditions. This is caused by vapor condensation in mesopores at a relative vapor pressure close to unity [42]. The shapes of benzene adsorption–desorption isotherms measured for both samples correlate well with nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms measured at low temperatures, confirming a similar adsorption mechanism based on physisorption [43]. The determined SSA values using the BET method from benzene adsorption isotherms for the selected samples 1B:4O (2187 m2·g−1) and BC8‐3h (1467 m2·g−1) are lower than those calculated from nitrogen adsorption. This is associated with the larger kinetic diameter of benzene molecules (~0.59 nm) compared with that of nitrogen molecules (~0.36 nm). As a result, benzene molecules cannot penetrate small ultramicropores (Table S3). The differences are more pronounced for BC8‐3h (about 20.3%) than for 1B:4O (9.7%), indicating a high contribution of smaller ultramicropores in BC8‐3h carbon.

Conclusions

In this study, we present an environmentally friendly method for producing porous carbon materials via a facile mechanochemical approach. This method involves 30 min of direct ball milling of bread with a chemical activator, which after the activation process, yields highly porous carbon materials with SSAs up to 2400 m2·g−1, and total pore volume up to 1.56 cm3·g−1. This method seems to be facile and suitable for the large‐scale production of activated carbons with a developed porous structure from bread waste. The versatile and simplified mechanochemical route facilitates the preparation of precursors for carbonization and activation. Given the nontoxic nature of the reagents used, the resulting porous carbons can be safely employed as adsorbents in diverse purification and separation applications, e.g., for carbon dioxide capture (reaching 6.7 mmol/g at 0°C and 1 bar).
