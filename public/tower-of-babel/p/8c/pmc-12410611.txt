Nationwide data-driven quality improvement in ultrasound medicine: insights from an indicator-based initiative in China
Authors: Aiping Zeng, Rui Zhang, Li Ma, Xixi Tao, Luying Gao, Jianchu Li, Hongyan Wang, Yuxin Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere are only a limited number of countries systematically tracking the quality of ultrasound at the national and institutional levels. Our study presents an example of a national, data-driven approach to ultrasound medicine quality measurement and improvement through indicators.AimThis study aims to establish a nationwide, indicator-based framework for measuring and improving the quality of ultrasound medicine in China. By developing and applying quality indicators, we seek to systematically evaluate performance, identify areas for improvement and guide data-driven quality enhancement efforts across institutions.MethodsTo enhance ultrasound quality and improve quality management, a leadership team was established. Pertinent quality indicators were developed as evaluation metrics. Quality improvement programmes were designed following the identification of key causes of suboptimal performance. A Plan-Do-Study-Act method was applied. Nationwide sample surveys were conducted annually to collect and document the relevant quality data. 10 ultrasound quality indicators were selected and comparisons of data from 2020 to 2023 were made. χ2 tests were used to evaluate statistical differences.ResultsThe ultrasound instruments quality inspection rate, the completion rate of notification of ultrasound critical findings within 10 min, the qualification rate of ultrasound reports remained and the completion rate of inpatient ultrasound examinations within 48 hours remained consistently high. The Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System utilisation rate of breast lesions in ultrasound reports, the concordance rate of ultrasound diagnoses, the accuracy rate of ultrasound diagnosis of breast lesions and the concordance rate of ultrasound diagnosis of ≥50% carotid stenosis all improved and showed an upward trend.ConclusionsIndicator-based data can drive nationwide measurement, improvement and monitoring of ultrasound medicine quality. The use of indicators supports sustained efforts to improve quality and safety in ultrasound services.Keywords: Quality improvement, Quality measurement, Diagnostic Imaging, Diagnostic Services

Introduction

Ultrasound is widely accepted in clinical practice, and the quality of imaging services is being addressed in most countries.1 2 Incentives for ultrasound departments to continually improve the quality they provide are multifactorial.3 The achievement of optimal interpretation of ultrasound examinations depends on solid knowledge, skilled operation, accurate lesion identification and minimal cognitive biases.4 Variability in the interpretation quality of ultrasound images is an increasingly recognised problem that negatively impacts patient care.5 Incorrect diagnosis may lead to treatment delays or failure to follow recommended therapeutic plans, decreased patient and employee satisfaction scores, and even threaten the patient life safety or cause disability.6 Effective quality management can lead to improvements in medical outcomes, patient satisfaction and service accessibility.7,9

“You can’t improve what you can’t measure” was often quoted when it comes to quality management.10 Quality indicators play an important role in quantitatively measuring the quality of medical services.11 Scientific and reasonable quality indicators can not only effectively evaluate and monitor medical quality, but also help managers clarify areas that require improvement, focus on key issues and implement targeted interventions.12,14 In the epoch of value-oriented medicine, data-driven quality improvement has become increasingly paramount to guarantee secure and efficacious imaging services.15

While the improvement of healthcare quality is a global goal, national-level initiatives remain limited in many areas. Several countries have established national quality improvement systems built around indicator-based performance evaluation, which provide comprehensive insights into the quality of healthcare services.16,18 However, the systematic evaluation of ultrasound medicine quality remains underexplored worldwide. To date, very few studies have focused specifically on nationwide quality measurement and monitoring in ultrasound medicine, leaving a notable gap in the literature. This study aims to address that gap.

In contrast to many countries where ultrasound services are integrated into radiology departments, China operates independent ultrasound departments.19 This structural difference creates unique challenges for standardising quality control. To address these challenges, a set of evidence-based ultrasound quality indicators was developed through a comprehensive literature review and a three-round modified Delphi process conducted by 36 national experts appointed by the China National Ultrasound Quality Control Centre (NUQCC). These indicators were then validated through pilot studies in selected hospitals across the country, confirming the feasibility of their collection and evaluation.20 These indicators were designed to reflect both operational efficiency and diagnostic accuracy. This indicator framework is expected to help institutions implement quality improvement programmes more effectively, realise continuous quality advancement and enhance quality management in ultrasound medicine at the institutional level. With these indicators, the national landscape of ultrasound medicine can be systematically mapped and its development continuously monitored.

Existing research endeavours concerning the enhancement of medical imaging service quality predominantly concentrate on the hospital level or department level,21,25 without addressing how national-level data can be used to drive systematic improvement. This study presents an example of how ultrasound quality indicators can be used to document institutional performance nationwide and to drive quality improvement based on national data. In this study, the programme was further expanded—both in scope and scale—with more participating hospitals and an updated set of quality indicators. The aim of this study is to demonstrate that ultrasound quality indicators are effective not only for measurement, but also for improvement and monitoring of ultrasound service quality. The use of indicator-based data provided a powerful approach to evaluating quality improvement programmes and supporting the advancement of ultrasound service delivery.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective, observational and descriptive study was based on hospital-level data voluntarily submitted through a nationwide quality assessment questionnaire via a reporting platform supervised by the NUQCC. This initiative involved regions and municipalities across China (including the Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps, but excluding Hong Kong, Macao and Taiwan), constituting the largest institutional-level dataset in the field of ultrasound medicine in China to date. The survey focused on institutional performance related to ultrasound service quality and safety. The survey was administered annually as part of a national ultrasound quality initiative, and data were collected from 2020 to 2023.

Leadership of quality improvement initiative

To enhance quality management, improve monitoring of medical services and evaluate their performance, China established the NUQCC. As a national authority in ultrasound quality, the NUQCC plays a pivotal role in ensuring the quality and safety of ultrasound services. Its responsibilities include:

Root cause analysis

To identify the underlying causes of suboptimal ultrasound quality, multiple brainstorming sessions were conducted. A fishbone diagram was created to illustrate multiple factors that contribute to variable accuracy of ultrasound diagnosis (figure 1). Through the cause-and-effect diagram, three major barriers (lack of awareness of quality control, lack of quality management standards, lack of adherence to ultrasound examination protocols or guidelines) were pinpointed and provided reference for targeted interventions.

Improvement programme

A quality programme was devised to use institutional data to facilitate incremental, progressive changes by applying Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) methodology28,30 (figure 2).

Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle diagram.

Plan: A quality control team was constructed in each ultrasound department of participating hospitals. The quality control team was led by senior sonographers, and they were responsible for monitoring quality performance by regularly collecting data of quality indicators.

Do: Education about the definition and meaning of quality indicators, quality control management standards, practical approaches to quality improvement, standardised ultrasound examination protocols and international guidelines. It included face-to-face instruction and web-based tutorials.

Study: Submission of randomly selected ultrasound examinations to the NUQCC is voluntary for each participating hospital. Randomly selected examinations would be audited by experts, and criteria included: (1) image quality and storage completeness, (2) report quality and completeness, (3) adherence to protocols, (4) accuracy of diagnosis (compared with the result of pathology or clinical diagnosis).

Act: After conducting a review of each image and report, feedback would be sent back and adjustments were required to be made. Corresponding hospitals need to resubmit randomly selected ultrasound examinations, and these would be reviewed again to track the changes. And then enter the next PDSA cycle.

The detailed definitions of these indicators are provided in online supplemental table S1. All indicators reflect data aggregated annually from each institution.

Using data from 2020 to 2022, we calculated the 3-year average level of each indicator, providing a reference for evaluating the development trend of the indicators. This information was used as a benchmark for recognising strengths and identifying areas that need improvement. Data in 2023 were used as the post-programme result. A comparison of preprogramme and postprogramme results was made to validate the ability of those quality indicators to detect variations. The SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound) goal was set as: (1) within 1 year, indicators with good performance should be higher than or equal to their 3-year average; and (2) indicators with suboptimal performance should have improved compared with their 3-year average.

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS V.27 (IBM). χ2 tests were performed to compare differences between groups for categorical variables. The significance level was set to α=0.05.

There are geographical variations of quality indicators in 2023, as presented in online supplemental figure S3. Zhejiang, Beijing, Jiangsu and Shanghai exhibited high average monthly workload, while Tibet reported the lowest average monthly workload. Across the majority of regions, the ultrasound instruments quality inspection rate, the completion rate of inpatient ultrasound examinations within 48 hours, the completion rate of notification of ultrasound critical findings within 10 min, and the qualification rate of ultrasound reports surpassed 90%, signifying minimal geographical heterogeneities. Regarding the BI-RADS utilisation rate of breast lesions in ultrasound reports, Shanghai reported the highest rate of 98.60%, whereas Gansu recorded the lowest at 65.59%. The concordance rate of ultrasound diagnoses peaked in Chongqing at 92.42%, while Guizhou showed the lowest concordance rate at 83.17%. When it comes to the accuracy rate of ultrasound diagnosis of breast lesions, Beijing led with 90.46%, whereas Tibet lagged behind with 33.33%. In terms of the concordance rate of ultrasound diagnosis of ≥50% carotid stenosis, Heilongjiang achieved the highest at 95.45%, contrasting with Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps, which reported the lowest rate at 70.76%. As for the incidence of major complications associated with ultrasound-guided interventions, Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps reported the highest at 1.66%, whereas Tibet reported the lowest incidence at 0%.

Discussion

Our study has provided valuable insights into the efficacy of indicator-based, data-driven ultrasound medicine quality measurement and improvement. Our findings revealed a notable enhancement in different aspects of ultrasound quality despite increased average workload, thereby underscoring the beneficial effects of this quality improvement initiative. This study has assessed the quality of ultrasound medicine at the institutional level rather than at the individual patient level. Furthermore, this study provides documentation for quality of ultrasound at a national level, while limited countries are able to record the quality of their imaging services delivered to all their citizens.

Sustained quality measurement and improvement

The continuous collection and analysis of data from 2020 to 2023 demonstrate the long-term viability of indicator-based ultrasound quality monitoring in routine practice. The use of standardised and validated indicators allowed for consistent year-over-year comparisons, thereby enabling the detection of meaningful trends and quality gaps. A noteworthy example is the upward trend in the concordance rate of ultrasound diagnoses. Although a slight, statistically non-significant decline was observed in 2023 compared with 2022, the indicator remained above the established multi-year benchmark. This marginal fluctuation may be attributable to factors such as increased case complexity, the participation of less experienced sonographers and sustained high workloads—all of which are recognised challenges to maintaining consistently high diagnostic performance. Nevertheless, the overall upward trend supports the value of sustained quality interventions and ongoing data-driven feedback.

Moreover, the completion rate of notification of ultrasound critical findings within 10 min was consistently maintained at a high level and showed an increasing trend. Indeed, such programmes aimed at improving quality have demonstrated efficacy in diminishing errors, legal complaints, clinician burnout and augmenting the satisfaction of various stakeholders, encompassing both patients and medical practitioners.31 Under institutional guidance and expert coaching, targeted intervention strategies could significantly improve the quality of care.32 It can be considered that these quality improvement programmes also helped raise awareness about the importance of fostering a culture of quality and safety, which is helpful to avoid undesirable consequences.33

Key factors enabling successful implementation

The successful implementation of quality improvement cannot be achieved without two factors. First, it is well-chosen, pertinent, measurable and feasible indicators that guided our quality improvement effort, which aligned with the overall quality vision and management strategy.34 Quality data can not only reveal less-favourable areas requiring enhancement, but also facilitate the prioritisation and selection of essential quality indicators for tracking.35 Second is that support from local governments and health committees was received to ensure smooth implementation. The unequivocal and steadfast endorsement from leadership plays a pivotal role in fortifying quality enhancement initiatives.36 37 Authentic leadership spearheaded the concerted endeavour to attain consistent excellence in medical ultrasound practices nationwide.38 39

Workforce burden and regional disparities

It is also worth noting that sonographers have a heavy workload. The increasing demand for ultrasound examinations and the sonographer shortage is becoming a common phenomenon.40 41 Sustained workload may potentially compromise the quality of ultrasound examinations, contribute to operator fatigue and increase the risk of diagnostic errors.40 The high average monthly workload per sonographer persisted from 2020 to 2023 without significant alleviation. Addressing the heavy workload of sonographers requires a multifaceted approach, including expanding the ultrasound workforce, implementing targeted training programmes to improve efficiency, and promoting task delegation or technological innovations to help alleviate this burden.

Moreover, our study revealed that significant variations in quality of ultrasound medicine existed across different regions. Many countries documented the temporal and geographical variations of the diagnostic imaging.42 The initiatives aimed at enhancing quality may exert differential impacts on various sociodemographic groups.43 The significant regional disparities observed in our findings may be attributed to multiple factors, including differences in healthcare infrastructure, resource availability, training and certification standards, economic development and patient demographics.

Provinces such as Zhejiang, Beijing, Jiangsu and Shanghai reported higher workloads, likely reflecting stronger healthcare systems, whereas Tibet showed the lowest workload, possibly due to limited access and lower population density. While some indicators—such as equipment inspection, timely examinations and report qualification—exceeded 90% in most regions, more specialised metrics showed notable variation. The BI-RADS utilisation rate, for example, ranged from 98.60% in Shanghai to 65.59% in Gansu, indicating potential gaps in training or guideline adherence. Similarly, diagnostic accuracy for breast lesions varied widely, from 90.46% in Beijing to 33.33% in Tibet. Differences were also observed in the concordance rate for carotid stenosis diagnosis and the incidence of major complications from ultrasound-guided procedures. These findings highlight the need for region-specific strategies—such as workforce training, resource allocation and quality assurance—to address disparities and support consistent, high-quality ultrasound care nationwide.

Further regional-level trend analysis over multiple years may provide deeper insights and will be explored in future studies.

Towards a more comprehensive quality indicator system

Improvement and application of the indicator system will inevitably be a continuous process of evolution and development.44 We should consistently identify more potential quality indicators and expand the scope of quality indicators, especially explore more disease-specific quality indicators to give a more comprehensive understanding of quality.45 This sampling survey only collected two disease-specific quality indicators, and the limited number of indicators related to diagnostic accuracy may lead to a limited scope of quality assessment. We hope that the richness of the well-chosen quality indicators will lead to more personalised and detailed quality measurement and improvement plans.46 We believe that it will be key to continuous improvement in not only patient care but also sonographer practices being delivered.

Policy implications and future direction

These findings have important implications for health policy and clinical practice. First, the use of national ultrasound quality indicators provides a scalable framework for standardising quality assessment across institutions. Second, the data-driven approach enables policy-makers to identify regional disparities and allocate resources more effectively. Third, routine implementation of such indicators may foster a culture of continuous quality improvement, encouraging institutions to benchmark their performance and make evidence-based changes.

While this study provides a comprehensive understanding of the efficacy of ultrasound quality indicators, it also has some limitations. First, the generalisability of the results may be limited because the data collection primarily occurred from secondary and tertiary hospitals. Including primary care centres in future research may reveal different patterns of ultrasound quality, as these institutions often have varying resource levels, personnel training and clinical demands, which might potentially influence both the feasibility and effectiveness of quality improvement efforts. Future studies could aim to include primary care centres for a more comprehensive analysis. Second, the reliance on self-reported data may introduce bias, as it is subject to variability in reporting practices across institutions. This highlights the importance of developing more timely and objective data collection mechanisms in the future. Third, since the sample survey for data collection was conducted once a year, there is a delay of assessment and feedback on quality. This limits the responsiveness of quality improvement interventions, as problems may go unaddressed for extended periods. It is crucial to find ways to collect quality data in a timely manner without placing excessive demands on already heavy workloads, so that institutions can respond more rapidly when suboptimal data is detected.

Conclusions

This paper demonstrates the feasibility of using quality indicators to enable nationwide measurement, improvement and monitoring of ultrasound services, supported by comparative data and firm leadership. Quality indicator-based data not only assess performance but also play a pivotal role in improving ultrasound practices across individual institutions. The monitoring of ultrasound quality has been greatly enhanced through the use of relevant quantitative indicators. The use of standardised quantitative indicators has significantly enhanced quality monitoring efforts. Taken together, these findings suggest that quality indicators can make a meaningful difference. Future research may consider expanding the scope of indicators to include more disease-specific or patient-centred metrics, as well as exploring how emerging technologies—such as artificial intelligence or advanced imaging techniques—may further impact and enhance the quality of ultrasound services. It is hoped that this work will inspire further research and wider adoption of similar quality frameworks in other clinical areas.
