COBRA N2 NA vaccines induce protective immune responses against influenza viral infection
Authors: Xiaojian Zhang, Amanda L. Skarlupka, Hua Shi, Ted M. Ross
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInfluenza neuraminidase (NA) is a promising target for a broadly protective vaccine. In this study, the Computationally Optimized Broadly Reactive Antigen (COBRA) methodology was used to develop N2 NA vaccine candidates. The unique wild type (WT) N2 sequences of human and swine influenza strains isolated between 1957 and 2019 were used to design the COBRA N2-A NA vaccine, while the unique WT N2 sequences of human influenza strains isolated between 2000 and 2019 were used to design the COBRA N2-B NA vaccine. Sera collected from COBRA N2 NA vaccinated mice showed more broadly reactive antibody responses against a broad panel of H×N2 influenza virus strains than sera collected from mice vaccinated with WT N2 NA vaccines. Antibodies elicited by COBRA or WT N2 NA antigens cross react with recent human H3N2 influenza viruses from different clades, while the antibodies elicited by A/Switzerland/9715293/2013 hemagglutinin (HA) reacted with viruses from the same clade. Furthermore, mice vaccinated with COBRA N2-B NA vaccine had lower viral lung titers compared to mock vaccinated mice when challenged with human H3N2 influenza viruses. Thus, the COBRA N2 NA vaccines elicit broadly protective murine anti-NA antibodies against multiple strains across subtypes and the viral loads were significantly decreased in the lungs of the mice in the COBRA N2 NA vaccine groups, compared to the mice in the mock vaccinated group, indicating that the COBRA-based N2 subtype NA vaccines have a potential to be a component in a universal influenza vaccine.

Introduction

Influenza viruses are common respiratory pathogens that cause seasonal influenza in humans. Each season, influenza viruses contribute to 290,000–650,000 deaths worldwide.1,2 Current seasonal influenza vaccines are strain dependent and were designed to direct the immune responses mainly to the hemagglutinin (HA), one of the major protective antigens on the surface of virion. Due to antigenic drift, the current commercial influenza virus vaccines require reformulation annually to match circulating influenza strains. Even though, the effectiveness of the seasonal influenza vaccine ranges between 10% and 60% according to Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). The annual influenza virus vaccine is less effective against pre-pandemic strains, such as H5N1, H7N9, and H2N2, due to a lack of preexisting immunity in most of the human population to these pre-pandemic strains.3,4

Compared to HA, neuraminidase (NA) is the second-most abundant surface glycoprotein and often undergoes less antigenic drift than HA for both H1N1 and H3N2 influenza subtypes.5 And multiple subtypes of HA share the same subtype of NA, such as H1 and H5 HA are both compatible with N1 and H2, H3, and H9 HA are all compatible with N2. Therefore, by targeting only two antigenic subtypes of NA (N1 and N2), the protective breadth is widened to cover human seasonal epidemics strains (H1N1 and H3N2) and potential pandemic strains (H5N1, H2N2, H5N2, H7N2, and H9N2)6,7 indicating the potential of NA to be a complementary component in a multivalent universal influenza vaccine with broad immune reactivity and longer-term protection. The broadly protective human monoclonal antibodies isolated from a H3N2-infected donor targeted the active site of the influenza virus neuraminidase.8 In addition, a universal anti-neuraminidase antibody targeting highly conserved epitopes on influenza neuraminidase inhibited influenza B and all influenza A subtypes,9,10 indicating that the universal epitopes exist among NA of different types and subtypes. Therefore, adding more evidence for using NA as a potential target in a broadly protective influenza virus vaccine.

Anti-NA immunity can be induced by either influenza virus infection or vaccination.11 The isolated NA protein from influenza virions is equally immunogenic as HA proteins that induces robust antibody responses.12 There is no antigenic competition between N1 and N2 when they are administered simultaneously.13 NA proteins purified from intact influenza viruses, baculovirus systems, or yeast systems are immunogenic and safe in humans and pre-clinical animal trials.14–18 Immune responses directed against NA provide protection against influenza viral infection by reducing the pulmonary viral titers,11,12,19 and there is a correlation between the NA-inhibiting (NAI) antibodies and the protection against disease caused by natural or experimentally administered influenza A virus infection.20,21 Furthermore, the preexisting anti-NA antibodies are associated with shortened duration of influenza A(H1N1)pdm virus shedding and illness in naturally infected adults.22 Therefore, the presence of anti-NA antibodies seems to be critical when the HAI antibody titers against more recent circulating virus strains are close to or below the limit of detection (1:10). Unfortunately, the NA potency is not standardized in current influenza vaccines, thus the anti-NA immunity induced by these influenza vaccines varies due to various amount and stability of the NA content in the vaccine.23 Thus, a universal influenza virus vaccine that targets the immune responses toward to both HA and NA is urgently needed.

High doses of influenza virus vaccines induce significantly more anti-NA antibodies than the standard vaccines among elderly subjects.24 And a more balanced and broadened immune response can be achieved through supplementing the conventional influenza virus vaccines with purified viral neuraminidase25,26 or using the disassociated HA and NA protein from intact virions or recombinant HA and NA protein produced in baculovirus as the vaccine.27–29 A comparison of the relative immunogenicity and protection against homotypic and heterotypic H3N2 viral challenge induced by a variety of vaccine platforms that include both HA and NA elicit an increased level of protection against influenza viruses.30 In addition, HA and NA elicit distinctive types of protection against pandemic A(H1N1) influenza virus infection in ferrets,31 indicating that optimal protection was achieved by combining both antigens in the vaccine.

In this study, COBRA NA vaccine candidates elicited broadly reactive immune responses as part of a next generation, universal influenza virus vaccine. Previously, an N1 NA vaccine candidate was engineered using the COBRA methodology32–34 that successfully protected mice and ferrets challenged with differing subtypes of influenza viruses (H1N1 and H5N1).35,36 This method was applied to the development of N2 NA vaccine candidates: COBRA N2-A and N2-B NAs by using N2 NA protein sequences. The COBRA N2 NA vaccines were tested for the ability of eliciting broadly protective murine anti-NA antibodies against multiple strains across subtypes and the protection against challenge of recent H3N2 influenza viruses in both naive and pre-immune mouse models.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animals were cared for under the USDA guidelines for laboratory animals, and all procedures were conducted under Biosafety Level 2 conditions, in compliance with the Animal Use Protocol (no. A2021 06–016) approved by the University of Georgia Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC).

Viruses

The N2 subtype viruses used in this study were propagated once in either embryonated chicken eggs or Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells. The seeds of the viruses were from either the Influenza Reagents Resource (IRR), BEI Resources, the Centers for Disease Control (CDC), or a generous gift from the Mark Tompkins laboratory at the University of Georgia. The aliquots of virus lots were stored at −80°C for single-use applications. The hemagglutination titer of the frozen aliquots was determined with either 0.8% turkey red blood cells (TRBCs) or 0.75% guinea pig red blood cells (GPRBCs) (Lampire Biologicals, Pipersville, PA, USA) in the presence of 20 nM Oseltamivir. The historical influenza virus, Pan/99 (H3N2), was used for establishing pre-immunity in mice, while influenza viruses, SW/NC/15 (H1N2), and Kan/17 (H3N2), were used for challenge infections. In addition to Pan/99, SW/NC/15 and Kan/17, the following viruses were used in the hemagglutinin inhibition assay (HAI) and enzyme-linked lectin assay (ELLA): A/Swine/Missouri/A01444614/2013 (SW/MO/13; H1N2), A/Chicken/Potsdam/4705/1984 (CK/Pot/84; H2N2), A/Chicken/Pennsylvania/29810-4/2004 (CK/PA/04; H2N2), A/Hong Kong/8/1968 (HK/68; H3N2), A/Texas/1/1977 PR8 reassortant (TX/77 × PR8; H3N2; 6:2 reassortant virus with A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 internal genes and TX/77 HA and NA gene segments), A/Sichuan/2/1987 (SC/87; H3N2), A/Wisconsin/67/2005 (WI/05; H3N2), A/Perth/16/2009 (Per/09; H3N2), TX/12 (H3N2), Switz/13 (H3N2), HK/19 (H3N2), and Tas/20 (H3N2). The swine-origin H1N2 influenza viruses (SW/MO/13 and SW/NC/15) were cultured in MDCK cells; all other viruses were grown in egg-culture. MDCK cells were maintained with Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) with 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S) (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) at 37°C with 5% CO2.

Vaccine design and preparation

Full-length WT N2 NA amino acid sequences of human and swine origins were downloaded from the GISAID database (www.gisaid.org) and used to generate N2 subtype NA vaccine antigens through the COBRA methodology.35–38 Briefly, the full-length sequences were aligned (Geneious v11.1.5), the most common amino acid at each position was determined and resulted in primary consensus sequences. The resulting primary sequences from each clade were then realigned to generate a secondary consensus. This process was continued until a single final consensus was obtained. Finally, the ultimate amino acid sequence was reverse translated and optimized for expression in mammalian cells, including codon usage and RNA optimization (Genewiz, Washington, DC, USA), before inserting into the pTR600 vector for VLP production. The NA only VLPs were made by transfecting the human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293-T cells with two individual plasmids expressing either the HIV p55 Gag sequence, to serve as the structure of the particle, and one of the various influenza A WT N2 NA or COBRA N2 NA expressing plasmids in previously described mammalian expression vectors.32 Recombinant soluble proteins were used for vaccination included: Switz/13 NA, Switz/13 HA, COBRA N2-A NA, and COBRA N2-B NA. The optimized coding sequence for wildtype and COBRA proteins in a pcDNA3.3 vector was expressed into soluble protein using a HEK-293T cell expression line as described previously.35,39 Protein was extracted using HisTrapExcel columns with the AKTA Pure System (GE Healthcare Bio-Sciences AB, Uppsala, Sweden). Purified proteins were concentrated with phosphate-buffered saline + 0.1% w/v sodium azide (PBSA). Protein samples were mixed with 4× Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad) and subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) to check the purity (Figure S1). Protein concentration was determined using Micro BCA Protein Assay Reagent kits (Pierce Biotechnology, Rockford, IL, USA) and stored at −80°C until used for vaccination.

Mice experiment

BALB/c and DBA/2J mice (females, 6–8 weeks old) were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) and housed in microisolator units with the free access to food and water. The influenza naive BALB/c or DBA/2J mice (9–12/group) were vaccinated with a 1:1 mixture of WT or COBRA NA VLP vaccines (1 μg of NA content per mouse) in PBS and AddaVax squalene-based oil-in-water adjuvant (InvivoGen, San Diego, CA, USA) in a total volume of 50 μL. Mice were vaccinated via intramuscular injection at week 0 and boosted with the same vaccine formulation at the same dose at weeks 4 and 8. PBS mixed 1:1 by volume with adjuvant served as a mock vaccination.

The influenza naive DBA/2J mice (12/group) were made pre-immune to H3N2 influenza by intranasally administering the Pan/99 influenza virus at a final concentration of 5 × 105 PFU/50 μL in PBS to each mouse at week 0. Then the pre-immune mice were vaccinated via intramuscular injection with a 1:1 mixture of WT or COBRA soluble recombinant NA protein (3 μg per mouse) in PBS and AddaVax squalene-based oil-in-water adjuvant in a total volume of 50 μL at week 4 and boosted with the same vaccine formulation at the same dose at week 8. PBS mixed 1:1 by volume with adjuvant served as a mock vaccination.

Two weeks after final vaccination, blood samples were collected from mice for serum isolation. Serum samples were stored at −20°C until further analysis. At 4 weeks after final vaccination, a H1N2 influenza virus SW/NC/15 was used to challenge vaccinated BALB/c mice intranasally at a dose of 106 PFU in a total volume of 50 μL, while a H3N2 influenza virus Kan/17 was used to challenge vaccinated DBA/2J mice intranasally at a dose of 1.925 × 107 PFU in a total volume of 50 μL. After challenge, the bodyweight changes as well as clinical symptoms of mice were monitored daily for 14 days. Lung samples were harvested on day 3 and/or 6 post-infection. Any animal who lost 20% of their original bodyweight or reached a humane endpoint score 3 was humanely euthanized.

Hemagglutination-inhibition (HAI) assay

The HAI assay was used to characterize the antibody response in mice sera against HA with 0.75% of GPRBCs according to the WHO laboratory influenza surveillance manual.40 Briefly, the receptor-destroying enzyme (RDE) (Denka Seiken, Co., Japan) treated mice sera were diluted in a series of two-fold serial dilutions in v-bottom microtiter plates. The sera dilutions were mixed with 4 hemagglutination units (HAU)/25 μL of each A(H3N2) virus at an equal volume. Oseltamivir carboxylate was used to as a neuraminidase inhibitor at a final concentration of 20 nM. The sera-virus mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min before the addition of 0.75% GPRBCs. The plates were incubated at room temperature for 1 h. The reciprocal dilution of the last well that contained non-agglutinated GRBCs was reported as HAI titer.

Influenza viral plaque assay

For viral titration, the plaque-forming units (PFU) of each mouse lung sample were determined on MDCK cells. Briefly, MDCK cells were seeded in six-well plates at 1 × 106 cells/well in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. MDCK cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 18–20 h before virus inoculation. Lung tissues were homogenized at 1:10 (w/v) in DMEM supplemented with 1% P/S. The supernatant of homogenate was 10-fold diluted serially in DMEM supplemented with 1% P/S. MDCK were washed 2 times with DMEM supplemented with 1% P/S, and then infected with 100uL of each dilution of homogenate supernatant. After 1 h of incubation at room temperature, the supernatant was removed and cells were washed twice with DMEM supplemented with 1% P/S. An overlay of 0.8% agarose (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) in 1×MEM supplemented with 1 μg/mL of TPCK trypsin (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) was added onto cells after second wash. Cells were then incubated at 37°C + 5% CO2 for 48–72 h. The cells were fixed with 10% buffered formalin for 15 mins at the end of incubation and stained with 1% crystal violet (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) for 15 mins at room temperature. The excess crystal violet was removed by rinsing plates in tap water. The lung viral titers were calculated and presented as plaque forming-units (PFU)/g of lung tissue.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

COBRA or wildtype rNA was coated with 100 μL of 1 μg/mL of in carbonate buffer (50 mM carbonate buffer [pH 9.5]) in 96-well Immulon 4HBX ELISA plates (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA), and the plate was incubated at 4°C for 18–20 h. The nonspecific epitopes on the plates were blocked with 1% BSA in PBS with 0.05% Tween 20 (PBST+BSA) solution (blocking buffer) for 1 h at room temperature. The raw sera were threefold serially diluted in blocking with an initial dilution of 1:500. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 90 min with the addition of sera dilutions after blocking. After washing, goat anti-mouse IgG-HRP (horseradish peroxidase) (cat. no. 1030–05, Southern Biotech, Birmingham, AL, USA) secondary antibody was added at 1:4,000 in blocking buffer. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 90 min. After washing, plates were developed with 2,29-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) substrate in McIlvain’s buffer (pH = 5) at 37°C for 10 min. The colorimetric reaction was stopped with the addition of 1% SDS in dH2O, and the absorbance was measured at 414 nm using a spectrophotometer (PowerWave XS; BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).

NA content in VLPs was determined by ELISA with N2 NA-specific reference mouse sera. Briefly, NA VLP supernatant was harvest and purified with sucrose gradient centrifugation. The serially diluted resuspension of N2 NA VLP was coated in 96-well Immulon 4HBX flat-bottom plates with carbonate coating buffer. On the same plates, N2 NA proteins of known concentration were coated to generate the standard curve. ELISA was performed with N2 NA-specific reference mouse sera as primary antibody and goat anti-mouse IgG as secondary antibody. Plates were developed with substrates of ABTS and read at 414 nm. The concentration of NA VLPs was calculated based on the standard curve of N2 NA proteins.

Enzyme-linked lectin assay (ELLA) for na-inhibition assay (NAI)

Sera was treated with RDE, and then heat-inactivated at 56°C for 8 hours to completely deactivate the NA activity of the Vibrio cholerae neuraminidase. The NA activity of the virus was determined as previously described and diluted to a concentration providing 90–95% NA activity.41,42 From an initial dilution of 1:100, the pooled sera samples were diluted two-fold in Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.133 g/L CaCl2 and 0.1 g/L MgCl2 (DPBS), 1% BSA, 0.5% Tween-20 (DPBS-BT). The sera were added to a PBS+Tween-20 (PBS-T) washed fetuin plated coated previously overnight with 100 μL of 25 μg/mL fetuin. The serial dilutions were added in 50 μL in duplicate per pooled mice sample. In the control wells, 50 μL of DPBS-BT was added in substitution of sera. The control wells included at least six wells with no sera and no virus for the subtraction of the background absorbance and another minimum of six wells with no sera and only virus to serve as the 100% NA activity threshold. The diluted virus was added in 50 μL, and the plate was rocked to mix. Plates were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 16–18 hours. After which, they were washed 6X with PBS-T, and 100 μL of peanut agglutinin-HRPO (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was added at a dilution of 1:1000 in DPBS + 1% BSA (DPBS-B). Plates were incubated in the dark for 2 hours at RT. After washing 3× with PBS-T, 100 μL of o-phenylenediamine dihydrochloride (OPD; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) in 0.05 M phosphate-citrate buffer with 0.03% sodium perborate pH 5.0 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was added to the plates. They were incubated in the dark at RT for 10 min and stopped with 100 μL of 1 N sulfuric acid. The absorbance was read at 490 nm using a spectrophotometer (PowerWave XS; BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). The background absorbance was subtracted, and the serum-containing wells were normalized with the average of the virus-only wells defining 100% NA activity. Non-linear regression was conducted in Prism 9 using the duplicates to provide the average estimated log10 50% NAI titer for each pooled mice sera sample.

Statistical analysis

The statistical differences between groups was analyzed by using “Mann–Whitney test” in GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). Data is presented as absolute mean values ± standard error of the mean (SEM). A “p” value less than 0.05 was defined as statistically significant (*, p < .05; **, p < .01; ***, p < .001; ****, p < .0001).

Results

COBRA N2 NA vaccines induced more broadly reactive antibody responses against multiple H×N2of influenza virus strains than WT N2 NA vaccines

NA antigens were designed from sequences obtained from Genbank and GISAID databases37 using the COBRA methodology.32–34 The COBRA N2-A and N2-B NA antigens were designed using human and swine origin influenza virus NA sequences isolated from 1957 to 2019. Both COBRA N2-A and N2-B NAs belong to the human/swine NA clade N2.2 (Figure 1a). The phylogenetic analysis showed that the COBRA N2-A NA sequence was similar to A/Panama/2007/1999 (Pan/99; H3N2) NA sequence, with 10 amino acids differences, while the COBRA N2-B NA sequence was similar to A/Texas/50/2012 (TX/12; H3N2) or A/Switzerland/9715293/2013 (Switz/13; H3N2) NA sequences, with 11 different amino acids (Figure 1b). To investigate the breadth of antibody response after NA vaccination, virus-like particles (VLPs) expressing wild-type (WT) or COBRA N2 NA antigens were used to vaccinate mice (Figure 1c). Sera collected at week 10 and 12 from mice vaccinated with the COBRA N2-A NA had NAI activity against the panel of swine H1N2, avian H2N2, and human H3N2 viruses isolated from 1968 to 2013 (11 out of 14, 78%) (Figure 2). In contrast, mice vaccinated with the COBRA N2-B NA had NAI activity against the same swine and avian strains, but in contrast, detected recent human H3N2 viruses isolated primarily between 2009 and 2019 (10 out of 14, 71%) (Figure 2). For comparison, sera collected from mice vaccinated with A/Swine/North Carolina/152702/2015 (SW/NC/15; H1N2) NA had NAI activity against swine H1N2 and the human H3N2 virus, A/Panama/2007/1999 (3 out of 14, 21%). Sera collected from mice vaccinated with the Pan/99 NA had similar NAI activity sera from SW/NC/15 (4 out of 14, 28%). In contrast, mice vaccinated with A/Kansas/14/2017 (Kan/17; H3N2) NA had sera with NAI activity against swine H1N2 and the 2017 and 2019 human H3N2 viruses (4 out of 14, 28%). Overall, COBRA N2 NA vaccines induced antibodies with a broader NAI reactivity against multiple H×N2 of influenza virus strains than WT N2 NA vaccines. COBRA N2-B NA vaccine contributes to decreased viral lung titers following challenge with seasonal human H3N2 influenza virus

Serum samples were collected from each mouse after final boost and then used in ELLA to evaluate the NAI antibody response after NA vaccination (Figure S2). Both COBRA N2-A and N2-B NA vaccines elicited NAI activity in sera against all four H3N2 influenza viruses representing clades N2.2d (H3N2 subtype human IAVs circulating in the 1990s) and N2.2e (H3N2 subtype human IAVs circulating after 2000). Mice vaccinated with the Pan/99 NA vaccine elicited NAI activity against the homologous Pan/99 and heterologous Switz/13 virus (Figure S2a). Mice vaccinated with the Switz/13 or Kan/17 NA vaccine also elicited NAI antibodies against all four viruses tested. However, the titers against heterologous viruses were lower than the NAI antibodies were elicited by COBRA N2 NA vaccines. Following challenge with Kan/17 (clade N2.2e), all mice lost body weight by day 3 pi (Figure 3a). The Switz/13 and Kan/17 NA vaccinated mice had similar weight loss patterns. The COBRA N2 NA vaccinated mice lost more weight than the mice vaccinated either with Switz/13 or Kan/17 NA vaccines, but had similar weight loss to the mice in Pan/99 NA or mock groups. Similar to the mice in Switz/13 and Kan/17 NA vaccinated groups, the COBRA N2-B NA vaccinated mice had significantly lower viral lung titers compared to Pan/99 NA, COBRA N2-A, or mock vaccinated mice (Figure 3c). H3N2 pre-immune mice vaccinated with COBRA N2-B NA vaccine had lower lung viral titers following H3N2 influenza virus challenge

Mice were infected with historical human H3N2 influenza virus, Pan/99, to generate a pre-immune state prior to vaccination (Figure 4). Following the booster vaccination, all vaccines elicited robust antibody responses against their respective homologous NA antigens (Figure S3a–d). However, the pre-immune mice that were mock vaccinated had low IgG binding titers against all four NA antigens tested (Figure S3e–h). The vaccine elicited sera was assessed for cross-reactive IgG binding to NA protein antigens (Figure 4a–c). Sera collected from pre-immune mice only (mock vaccinated) had little or no detectable titers against either COBRA or Switz/13 NA. Mice vaccinated with either COBRA NA vaccines had cross-reactive IgG binding antibodies against COBRA and Switz/13 NA antigens (Figure 4a–c). Mice vaccinated with Switz/13 HA had antibodies that bound specifically to Switz/13 HA and not NA (Figure 4d). Sera was collected from mice vaccinated with the Switz/13 HA against both the homologous Switz/13 and the clade 3C.3a1 Kan/17 virus (Figure 4e,f), but not the more contemporary H3N2 influenza viruses (Figure 4g,h) A/Hong Kong/2671/2019 (HK/19, clade 3C.2a1b.1b) and A/Tasmania/503/2020 (Tas/20, clade 3C.2a1b.2a.1). COBRA N2 NA vaccines protect mice against swine H1N2 influenza virus challenge

All mice were challenged with a swine H1N2 virus (SW/NC/15) to evaluate the protection after NA vaccination (Figure 6). Mock vaccinated mice lost weight as early as 2 days post-infection (pi) with an average of 82% of their original weight by day 6 pi (Figure 6a) with 60% of the mice surviving challenge (Figure 6b). Mice vaccinated with the COBRA N2-A NA vaccine had minimal weight loss at day 3 pi with 100% of the mice surviving challenge, while mice vaccinated with the COBRA N2-B NA vaccine had a peak loss of weight on day 5 pi and only 20% of the mice survived. Mice vaccinated with any of the three wild-type NA proteins had similar weight loss trends with the average peak loss of weight on day 4 pi. Furthermore, mice vaccinated with either the COBRA N2-A NA or Pan/99 NA had significantly lower viral lung loads compared to the mock vaccinated mice (Figure 6c). The simple linear regression analysis (Figure 6d) showed that the viral lung titer is highly correlated with the NAI antibody titer (R2 = 0.9089). Therefore, COBRA N2-A NA vaccine contributes to reduced mortality caused by influenza viral infection and the NAI antibodies elicited by COBRA N2-A NA vaccine decreased viral titers in the lungs of mice compared to mock vaccinated animals. Discussion

Current influenza virus vaccines mainly focus on developing humoral immune responses by inducing neutralizing antibodies against HA. However, the immunity induced against neuraminidase (NA) enhances the protection against influenza in humans21,22 and in animals.25 To develop next-generation influenza vaccine, COBRA-based N2 subtype NA vaccines were designed and characterized for the breadth of antibody response and protection against influenza virus challenge. The COBRA N2 NA vaccines elicited more broadly reactive antibody responses against multiple NA antigens from H×N2 subtypes of influenza viruses and protected mice against influenza virus challenge by reducing the pulmonary viral titers. The COBRA N2 NA vaccines have a potential as a vaccine component in the next generation of influenza vaccines, as they enhance the protective immune responses against influenza virus infection. The phylogenetic analysis showed that the HA and NA of influenza viruses undergo independent evolution with NA evolving slower than HA. As a result, the antigenicity of NA is not as diverse as HA. Even though, the WT NA vaccines still have narrow breadth of antibody response, the antibodies elicited against Pan/99 NA do not inhibit the enzyme activity of Kan/17 or HK/19 NA, indicating that the NA antigens from historical influenza virus isolates do not provide protection against the infection caused by recent circulating influenza virus strains. Compared to wild-type NA vaccines, the antibodies elicited by COBRA N2 NA vaccines cross-react with a wide range of N2 NA antigens from H×N2 subtypes of influenza viruses in ELLA, which means the antibodies elicited by COBRA N2 NA vaccines still can be used to fight the infection caused by current circulating influenza virus strains. The antibody recognition pattern is also different between two COBRA N2 NA vaccine candidates, with the antibodies elicited by COBRA N2-A NA vaccine recognizing more historical human H3N2 influenza isolates, while the antibodies elicited by COBRA N2-B NA vaccine recognize more recent human H3N2 influenza isolates. The phylogenetic analysis showed that the COBRA N2-A NA sequence was close to Pan/99 NA sequence, while the COBRA N2-B NA sequence was close to TX/12 or Switz/13 NA sequences (Figure 1b), indicating that COBRA N2-A NA retains the antigenic epitopes from historical H3N2 isolates, while COBRA N2-B NA retains antigenic epitopes from recent H3N2 isolates. Therefore, the different antibody recognition patterns reflect the antigenic differences between COBRA N2-A and N2-B NA vaccine candidates.

The NA catalytic site residues, as well as the framework residues, are highly conserved (Figure S4) even under immune pressure.43 NA antigenic drift is mainly driven by the accumulation of amino acid changes around the enzymatic active site that is located on the top of the NA head,44 as well as those at the lateral surface of the NA head.45 Overall, the antigenicity of vaccine antigen determines the breadth of antibody responses. And key residues in certain epitopes can largely influence the antigenicity of vaccine antigen. Our serological analysis indicated that either of the two COBRA N2 NA vaccines elicited more broadly reactive antibodies against diverse N2 NA antigens compared to any WT N2 NA vaccines used in this study. The loss of a glycosylation site at amino acid 329 and the E344K mutation in N2 NA contribute to the antigenic drift of the human H3N2 virus, with a significant decrease in the inhibiting activity of the antisera to the mutated viruses.46 The COBRA N2-B NA maintained the glycosylation at residue 329, the same as WT N2 NA proteins from earlier H3N2 viruses in 2014, but do possess the E344K mutation, as WT N2 NA molecules from later H3N2 viruses (Figure 1b). Besides these, the acquisition of a glycosylation site at residue 245 and the P468H mutation also may impact NA-antibody-based protection against A(H3N2) virus infections.47 The COBRA N2-B NA remained non-glycosylated at residue 245 which is the same as the NA from the A/Hong Kong/4801/2014 (H3N2) vaccine strain, but also has the P468H mutation which is also located in the NA from A/Kansas/14/2017 (H3N2) vaccine strain (Figure 1b). The balanced gain or loss of the key residues at multiple epitopes, achieved through the COBRA methodology, might contribute to the broadly reactivity of the antibodies elicited by COBRA N2-B NA vaccine.

Mechanisms of NA immunity include inhibiting NA enzymatic activity and activation of Fcγ receptor (FcγR) or C3 complement receptor (C3R)-mediated signaling pathways in effector cells.45,48 Anti-NA antibodies can reduce pulmonary virus titers in mice after infection and can shorten the duration of influenza virus shedding and illness in naturally or experimentally infected people.11,12,21,22 The antibodies targeting to NA usually do not take effect as efficiently as the antibodies targeting HA because they are infection permissive as previously reported.12,19 As observed in the naive mice (Figure 3a,b), only the wildtype Kan/17 NA vaccination partially mitigated Kan/17 virus induced disease, while COBRA NA, as well as other wildtype NA vaccines, failed to mitigate the disease. In the pre-immune mice (Figure 5a,b), HA or NA vaccinations elicited distinct immunity against influenza challenge as the HA vaccination completely mitigated the disease, while the NA vaccination only partially mitigated the disease. These data suggested that the high levels of anti-NA antibodies determined in ELLA (Figure S2) or enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Figure 4) are relatively inefficient in mitigating the disease upon influenza virus infection, but they are associated with reduced pulmonary viral titers in both naive and pre-immune mice that are challenged with A (H3N2) influenza viruses (Figures 3c and 5c).

People have influenza-binding antibodies due to “immune imprinting”49 elicited by infections from previously circulating influenza viruses and wide use of the influenza vaccine. This influenza immune imprinting has long-lasting effects on the immune responses to subsequent influenza virus infections and vaccination, usually by boosting the antibody responses to the cross-reactive epitopes on HA and NA proteins from memory B cells.50,51 We also investigated the effects of preexisting antibodies on the immune responses to the NA vaccination. The elicitation of immune responses by infection with historical human H3N2 influenza viruses results in low levels of anti-NA and anti-HA antibodies. These antibodies were boosted to high levels following two NA or HA vaccinations. Only HA-based vaccines mitigate the disease in the mice challenged with human H3N2 influenza viruses and all NA-based vaccines failed to mitigate the disease in the mice challenged with human H3N2 influenza viruses. The preexisting influenza virus immunity established by challenging mice with a historical human H3N2 influenza virus does not seem to have large effects on the elicited immune responses by either NA or HA vaccination. The limitation of the current pre-immune mouse models is that they do not exactly mimic the complex immune background of humans that could be exposed to several influenza viruses or vaccination at different points during a lifetime. Therefore, it is difficult to mimic the influenza immune background of humans in any animal models.

Since HA and NA induced different types of immunity against influenza, the optimal protection could be achieved by the combination of the two antigens.12,31 HA-based vaccines induced robust homotypic anti-HA antibody response, but with narrow breadth. In contrast, NA-based vaccines elicit virus infection permissive antibodies with broader breadth.52 As a potential future study, we hypothesized that lower dose of HA in combination with NA vaccination would provide better protection in reducing the severity of disease and viral shedding. HA is highly immunogenic and this protein induces neutralizing antibodies even at minimal doses.12 In addition, there is no HA-dominant antigen competition when the recombinant HA and NA antigens are co-administrated.27 As previously shown, COBRA-based HA vaccines elicit broadly reactive antibody response.33,34,53 The COBRA-based HA vaccines in combination of COBRA-based NA vaccines could maximum the breadth of antibody response by reducing the severity of disease and viral shedding resulting in a true universal influenza vaccine. The addition of either HA- or NA-based vaccine reduces the transmission of pandemic H1N1 virus from vaccinated ferrets to naïve ferrets or naïve infected ferrets to vaccinated ferrets.36 However, both types of vaccine can provide the protection from severe disease, especially against a newly emerged strain, a benefit elicited by both HA and NA immunity.

Overall, the COBRA-based N2 subtype NA vaccines elicited antibodies that cross-reactive with a broad range of N2 NA antigens of different origins, while the breadth of antibodies elicited against the WT N2 subtype NA vaccines are narrow. And COBRA NA vaccines induced protective immune responses in mice against influenza viral infection by reducing the pulmonary viral titers. Our results suggest that the COBRA-based N2 subtype NA vaccines have a potential to be a component in a universal influenza vaccine.
