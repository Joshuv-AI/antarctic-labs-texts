Textual quantitative research and implications of China’s medical education policies—analysis based on the “tool-development-intensity” three-dimensional framework
Authors: Li Yang, Yulin Chai, Ai-min Tang, Xue-jing Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo analyze China’s "medical education" policies issued in the past three decades using the "tool-development-intensity" framework, explore the focus and deficiencies of policy texts, and propose recommendations.MethodsBased on content analysis and quantitative analysis, construct the three-dimensional framework of "tool-development-intensity," classify and conduct cross-comparative analysis on national-level medical education policy provisions.ResultsEnvironmental policy instruments dominate medical education policies (57.76%, intensity 0.585), primarily comprising “coordination mechanisms” (intensity 0.248) and “enhanced safeguards” (intensity 0.181), reflecting the government's governance approach centered on top-level design and institutional safeguards. Supply-oriented (20.69%, intensity 0.200) and demand-oriented tools (21.55%, intensity 0.215) are notably underutilized. Across medical education dimensions, “guarantee mechanisms” exhibit the highest policy intensity (0.312), followed by “training models” (0.228), while “schooling formats” receive the least attention (0.136). Three-dimensional cross-analysis reveals structural imbalances in policy tools across different developmental dimensions, such as the “guarantee mechanism” heavily relying on environmental tools.ConclusionChina's medical education policies exhibit a characteristic “environment-dominated, safeguarding-priority” pattern, reflecting strong state mobilization and institutional regulation. However, significant shortcomings remain in stimulating grassroots innovation, promoting diversified institutional models, and supporting international research collaboration. Future efforts should optimize the policy tool structure, increase investment in supply–demand policies for critical weak links, and advance the coordinated, high-quality development of the medical education system.Keywords: Medical education, Policy tools, Textual analysis, Educational development

Introduction

Global medical education has undergone a scientific transformation marked by the Flexner Report, the widespread adoption of PBL teaching models, and a humanistic reorientation centered on professional competence [1, 2]. While China has adopted international practices such as student-centered teaching, it faces practical challenges in localizing policies [3]. Policy transplantation must adapt to domestic institutional and cultural contexts,otherwise, implementation risks failure [4]. As the core means for governments to advance educational objectives, policy instruments must be selected and combined to meet the unique demands of medical education—its high clinical practicality and social service orientation. A single policy dimension struggles to address complex realities.

Against this backdrop, this study aims to systematically analyze the tool structure, configuration effectiveness, and underlying logic of China's medical education policies. It explicitly poses the following research questions: What configuration characteristics do policy instruments exhibit across different dimensions of medical education development? Does the current policy structure exhibit typological imbalances? How does the distribution of policy intensity reflect institutional preferences?

To address these questions, this paper constructs a three-dimensional analytical framework integrating “policy instruments—medical education development—policy intensity.” For the first time, it applies policy instrument theory, educational development dimensions, and intensity quantification methods to analyze Chinese medical education policy texts. This framework overcomes the limitations of traditional unidimensional analysis by systematically identifying the distribution, intensity, and combined effects of policy instruments across different educational development dimensions, thereby revealing the underlying governance logic and structural tensions within policy texts. Through empirical coding and multidimensional measurement, this study aims to provide evidence-based support and theoretical innovation for optimizing China's medical education governance system and promoting the rational transformation of policy instruments.

Review of the literature

The composition and instrumental nature of educational policy

As a branch of public policy, educational policy is defined as “action plans or guidelines formulated by public authorities in specific contexts to achieve certain objectives” [5]. A comprehensive policy framework typically encompasses core elements such as policy subjects, objects, environment, process, and content [6, 7]. The realization of policy effectiveness hinges on deconstructing the synergistic mechanism among “policy instruments-development context-implementation outcomes.”

Classification of policy instruments and theoretical framework

Policy instruments represent specific types of government intervention in educational development [8], with diverse classification approaches. Academic literature has proposed frameworks such as Howlett and Lamis' tripartite classification (voluntary, coercive, hybrid) and McDonald and Elmore's quadripartite classification (command, incentive, functional expansion, authority restructuring) [9]. Among these, Rosswell and Segfield's classification of environmental, supply-side, and demand-side policy instruments [7] provides the direct theoretical foundation for analyzing China's medical education policies in this study.

Contextual adaptation challenges in policy transfer and borrowing

Policy transfer and borrowing globally often face challenges of localization failure. Research indicates that the successful transplantation of policy instruments heavily depends on their adaptability to the target region's institutional environment, cultural traditions, and implementation capacity. For instance, Georgia's vertical management system overlooked local participation [10], while Kazakhstan and Turkey were constrained by historical institutional legacies [11, 12].

East Asian cases further reveal the profound filtering effect of cultural cognition. Fundamental differences exist between Eastern and Western pedagogical philosophies [4], and directly transplanting East Asian accountability systems to the UK yielded limited results due to differing institutional soils [13, 14]. China's own curriculum reforms and preschool education practices also demonstrate that borrowed policy tools are prone to being distorted by local paradigms (e.g., exam-oriented education) [13, 15]. Successful cases—such as Germany's export of education through “diplomatization” tools [16], Qatar Education City's phased localization strategy [17], and China's restructuring of student loan policies through local pilot programs [18]—demonstrate the critical importance of dynamic “tool-context” adaptation.

Research gaps and theoretical advancement of this study

While existing research offers rich perspectives on policy migration, significant gaps remain: First, most studies focus on basic education or macro-level policies, lacking attention to the specialized domain of higher medical education. Second, analytical approaches often remain confined to unidimensional discussions of either tools or contexts, failing to integrate policy tool types, educational development dimensions, and policy intensity quantification into a three-dimensional analysis. Although emerging methods like AI text mining show promise in capturing “instrument configuration preferences” [19] and advocacy coalition frameworks reveal the political logic behind policy intensity generation [20], no systematic framework has yet been developed for evaluating China's medical education policies.

Therefore, this study aims to fill this gap by constructing a three-dimensional framework of “policy instruments—medical education development—policy intensity.” This framework systematically analyzes the internal structure and configuration effectiveness of China's medical education policies, providing new analytical perspectives and empirical evidence for understanding the localization logic of policy transplantation.

Data sources and research methodology

Data sources

The study searched “medical education” and “medical personnel training” from official policy search websites, such as The State Council of the People’s Republic of China and the laws and regulations database of Peking University's Legal Information Network, up to January 2023. Two administrative regulations, No. 34 of the State Administration (2020) and No. 63 of the State Administration (2017), 83 departmental regulations, including 26 departmental normative documents (1 expired), 54 departmental working documents (3 expired), and three administrative approvals of licences were searched. Of the total documents, 69 were related to education, including 21 comprehensive regulations on education, 7 on education reform, 1 on institutions and teachers, 1 on academic degrees and academic registration, 17 on higher education, 2 on adult education, 19 on vocational and personnel education, and 1 on special education.

In order to ensure the accuracy and representativeness of the selection of policy texts, the following selection principles have been established: first, national-level reform policies are selected, that is, those issued by the State Council and other ministries and commissions either individually or jointly; second, the content of policy texts is closely related to medical education and directly or indirectly reflects the relevant contents and regulations of medical education; third, the main selection is the complete content of regulations, standards, plans, opinions, notices, methods, and other documents that can fully explain government policies [21]. By excluding documents with key words but no substantive content, and excluding informal official documents such as news reports and policy interpretations, the documents in this study mainly highlight the relevance of “education” and distinguish “medical industry policies.” A total of 29 documents were selected after rigorous review by two researchers from our research group, as shown in Table 1 [22]. These 29 documents were used as the main targets of the study, and the core elements and key terms in the documents were extracted. To ensure the validity of the coding, two people were introduced to perform coding simultaneously, and the agreement and coding reliability of the coders were calculated. The agreement of this study was 0.878 and the reliability was 0.932, which was higher than 0.8 and met the established criteria [21].

Summary of medical education policy documents

Research methods

Employ content analysis to conduct systematic and objective quantitative analysis of textual content. By examining the substance of policy documents, establishing a policy analysis framework through literature review and theoretical analysis, coding texts using Nvivo 11.0 Plus software, creating case node classifications, assigning values to cases to build matrix codes, and utilizing the software's query function to generate character code matrix tables, we analyze medical education reform policies based on content analysis methodology and propose recommendations. Following the “Policy Document Name Number-Chapter Number-Clause Number” format, for example, the clause “Implementing the Fundamental Task of Fostering Morality and Cultivating Talent” in the core document “Guiding Opinions of the General Office of the State Council on Accelerating the Innovative Development of Medical Education” (State Council General Office Document [2020] No. 34) is coded as “Ⅰ−1–1-1”. where the policy number I indicates the first clause within the first section of the first chapter of the document. If a clause spans multiple paragraphs, the paragraph number is appended. This sequential coding process enables the selection of corresponding policy instruments and dimensions of educational development. To operationalize the definitions of coding categories.

This study established detailed coding rules and semantic criteria for each policy instrument subcategory based on the established three-dimensional analytical framework. The coding process involves comprehensive judgment based on the substantive content and policy intent of each clause. Specific examples are as follows: Environmental Tools—Strengthening Safeguards: Operationally defined as clauses in policy texts that propose development directions, principles, guiding ideologies, or set macro-level objectives. For instance, the statement in the Guiding Opinions, “Guided by Xi Jinping Thought on Socialism with Chinese Characteristics for a New Era… placing medical education in a position of priority for the development of education and health,” aims to clarify strategic direction and is thus coded under this category. NVivo 11 serves as the analytical platform, undertaking three core functions: constructing the classification framework, coding and retrieving policy provisions, and generating foundational visualizations. It provides end-to-end technical support for cross-analysis across policy instruments, development dimensions, and intensity dimensions.

Construction of a three-dimensional analysis framework

This paper takes policy documents as its research subject, systematically analyzing the issues present in medical education reform policies. It conducts a quantitative analysis from three dimensions: policy tools, medical education development, and policy intensity, establishing a three-dimensional analytical framework for medical education reform policies. The X-axis represents policy tool types, the Y-axis represents medical education development, and the Z-axis represents policy intensity. By observing the proportions of the X, Y, and Z dimensions, as well as the combined X–Y-Z dimensions, this study analyzes the policy trends in China's medical education reform.

Dimension X: policy instruments

This study adopts Roswell and Sagefield's classification of policy instruments, which is divided into environment-based, supply based, and demand-based policy instruments. Environment-based policy tools are the environmental guarantees for medical education reform, specifically divided into five aspects: strengthening guarantees, system construction, coordination mechanisms, regional development, and international exchange;Supply based policy tools are the driving force in promoting the reform and development of medical education, which are divided into four areas: discipline research, faculty development, teaching materials, and institutional development;Demand-oriented policies are the driving force behind the reform of medical education and are divided into four areas: talent training, competence training, academic education, and moral education construction [23]. Policy tool connotation

Y-dimension: medical education development

The ultimate goal of medical education policy is to develop medical education and cultivate qualified healthcare professionals for the nation. Based on relevant literature, this study identifies five dimensions of medical education development: ideological stance, system construction, institutional forms, training models, and protection mechanisms [23, 24]. The ideological stance defines the direction of educational development and is manifested in the guiding principles and educational objectives outlined in policies. System construction refers to the governance framework of medical education and includes admissions, program design, faculty development, employment, and professional certification systems. Institutional forms represent the landscape of medical education development and are reflected in policies related to regional coordination, institutional models, social sector involvement in education, and vocational training. Educational models pertain to aspects such as classroom instruction, practical training, and institutional resources outlined in policies. Support mechanisms are reflected in funding allocation, rights protection and support, employment guarantees, and quality assurance in education.

Z-dimension: policy strength

This paper combines two indicators—the frequency of policy tool usage and the policy-issuing entity (local or central) — to quantitatively analyze policy tool intensity based on policy text content. Using policy tool type as the classification criterion, the paper statistically analyzes the frequency with which different policy-issuing entities use policy tools, compiles a frequency table of policy tool usage across all levels, and calculates an intensity index of the application of various policy tools by local and central entities based on the proportion of frequency of usage relative to the total frequency [25, 26]. The paper uses policy intensity as the Z dimension, with higher scores indicating stronger policy intensity. This dimension quantifies the results of strategic interactions among different advocacy alliances within the medical education policy realm.

Research results

Dimension X: analysis of policy instruments

As shown in Fig. 1, the government started to use supply based, demand-based, and environment-based policy instruments, with 232 policy codes. Supply based policy instruments have 48 reference points, accounting for 20.69% of the total; demand-based policy instruments have 50 reference points, accounting for 21.55%; and environmental policy instruments have 134 reference points, accounting for 57.76%. It can be seen that the current medical education reform policy in China favours environmental policy tools.

Distribution of secondary nodes of policy tools

Within the internal structure of various policy tools, environmental tools primarily consist of “coordination mechanisms” (42%) and “enhanced safeguards” (32%), reflecting a strong government-led approach and emphasis on resource allocation. However, “international exchange” accounts for only 1%, indicating a significant underemphasis on the international dimension in current policies. Among supply-oriented tools, “curriculum and teaching materials” (46%) and “faculty development” (25%) are more frequently employed, indicating policy focus on foundational teaching resources and teacher workforce development. Conversely, “disciplinary research” accounts for only 10%, suggesting weak impetus for research innovation. Among demand-oriented tools, “talent cultivation” (36%) and “capacity training” (34%) were relatively frequent, indicating policy emphasis on talent export and capability enhancement. However, “moral education development” (12%) and “academic degree education” (18%) still have room for improvement.

Dimension Y: analysis of the development of medical education

The medical education dimensions generated 216 political text codes. As shown in Fig. 2, the ideological stance has 38 reference points, accounting for 17.59%; the system construction has 33 reference points, accounting for 15.28%; the form of schooling has 30 reference points, accounting for 13.89%; the mode of training has 50 reference points, accounting for 23.15%; and the guarantee mechanism has 65 reference points, accounting for 30.09%. It can be seen that in the development dimension of medical education, the highest proportion is in the guarantee mechanism, while the lowest proportion is in the form of education.

From the perspective of policy tool types, environmental policy tools hold an absolute dominant position, with an intensity index as high as 0.585. Among these, “coordination mechanisms” (intensity 0.248) and “enhanced safeguards” (intensity 0.181) contribute the most, reflecting strong government-led and top-down design characteristics. From the perspective of medical education development, the “guarantee mechanism” has the highest intensity (0.312), followed by the “training model” (0.228), indicating that these two are the core focus of current policies. Meanwhile, the “schooling format” has the lowest intensity (0.136), reflecting its relatively insufficient emphasis.

Discussion

Insights

This study finds that the distribution of policy instruments within China's medical education policy texts exhibits significant imbalance, with pronounced differences in their internal structures. Overall, supply-oriented, demand-oriented, and environment-oriented policy instruments account for 20.69%, 21.55%, and 57.76% respectively, with environment-oriented instruments exceeding half of the total. Furthermore, the distribution of secondary sub-instruments under each category is also uneven. This finding aligns with policy instrument theory, which posits that instrument selection is strongly influenced by political structures and institutional pathways [7]. The policy tool imbalance revealed in this study fundamentally reflects power structure disparities among different alliances within China's medical education policy subsystem. The Administrative Authority Alliance, leveraging its efficient resource integration and policy penetration capabilities, dominates the supply of environmental tools such as accreditation standards. Conversely, the Clinical Practice Alliance, due to its relatively loose organizational structure, struggles to effectively implement demand-driven tools. This unilateral dominance pattern validates the alliance framework's assessment of the relationship between alliance resources and policy influence [20], while also exposing the policy risks inherent in single-alliance monopolization [27]. Therefore, future policy optimization should focus on fostering belief integration and collaboration between the two alliances. Establishing cross-alliance consultation mechanisms can achieve dynamic optimization of policy tool allocation, enhancing the local adaptability and sustainability of policy transplantation [13, 18].

At the operational level, it is recommended that the national level systematically optimize the internal structure of policy instruments, appropriately increasing the proportion of supply-side and demand-side policy tools. The selection of policy instruments should not only emphasize sustainability [28] but also align with the country's political context and developmental stage, reflecting a unity of phased and continuous approaches [29, 30]. When formulating policies, emphasis should be placed on the coordination and integration of different tools [31], particularly strengthening the application of incentive-based mechanisms. Increased resource allocation for research platforms and institutional development is essential to effectively advance medical education reform. Furthermore, deeper, implicit policy objectives should be achieved through enhanced international exchange and systematic development [32, 33].

From the perspective of medical education development dimensions, the distribution of policy nodes across dimensions is generally balanced, with relatively uniform policy intensity allocation. The state places high importance on the safeguarding mechanism for educational development, accounting for 30.09% of policy nodes, followed by training models. The smallest proportion is allocated to institutional forms, at 13.89%. Further analysis indicates that environmental policy tools dominate within the medical education development dimension, particularly within the safeguarding mechanisms dimension. A staggering 91.67% of reference points in this dimension rely on environmental tools, while supply-oriented tools account for only 3%. This severe internal imbalance reveals a path dependency on administrative directives within current governance, reflecting the powerful inertia of the vertical management system. Although central policies transmit responsibility targets through intensive environmental tools, they have failed to fully activate market-demand tools and grassroots supply vitality. To overcome this dilemma, it is recommended to grant universities greater autonomy in curriculum accreditation and establish a quality assessment mechanism centered on peer review by professional communities, replacing the single-channel administrative acceptance [34]. Simultaneously, decentralize resource integration autonomy to permit universities greater flexibility in allocating fiscal funds. Furthermore, embed university governance representatives within policy-making bodies to establish institutionalized co-governance mechanisms between academic communities and government departments [35].

From the distribution of policy intensity, it generally aligns with the frequency of policy nodes. The state exerts the greatest intensity on environmental policy tools, while policy intensity is most pronounced in the safeguarding mechanisms dimension of medical education development. This indicates that the state tends to ensure the order and direction of development by strengthening environmental regulations and solidifying the foundation for policy implementation through increased investment in safeguarding mechanisms. This aligns with the institutional logic of state-led resource allocation and top-down design during the transition period. However, this intensity configuration pattern also exposes certain shortcomings in the current governance system, potentially objectively suppressing the autonomy of grassroots innovation entities and the functioning of market mechanisms. Particularly in areas like institutional forms of education, which should inherently foster greater innovation vitality, the relatively weak policy intensity reveals a deep structural contradiction of “prioritizing normative safeguards over diverse cultivation.” Future policy adjustments must focus on balancing intensity with innovation.

The findings of this study offer clear practical implications for policymakers and medical education institutions. Policymakers should strive to optimize the toolkit structure of medical education policies, moderately reduce the overuse of environmental tools, and enhance the coordination between supply-side and demand-side policies. This includes granting universities greater flexibility and institutional space, particularly in institutional autonomy, resource allocation, and innovative training models [36]. For instance, matching funds could guide universities in piloting curriculum innovation and international collaboration, while establishing evidence-based dynamic policy evaluation mechanisms to enhance adaptability and responsiveness at regional and institutional levels [37]. Educational institutions should proactively transform their roles from policy implementers to collaborative innovators, actively incorporating social resources and international expertise [38]. They should strengthen faculty development, expand cooperative networks, and effectively complement national strategies in safeguarding mechanisms and intrinsic development.

To achieve systemic transformation in medical education, policy tools must be closely integrated with educational development dimensions to fully leverage their guiding and enabling roles [37]. Governments should coordinate policy safeguards and resource allocation at the macro level, further strengthening support in funding, institutional development, and international platform construction [39], while aligning with socio-cultural contexts and global trends [40, 41]. It is recommended that reforms to streamline administration and delegate power be deepened to further expand universities' autonomy in operations and innovation, fostering a new governance model for medical education characterized by multi-stakeholder participation and multidimensional resource integration. Additionally, policy safeguards for international talent mobility and collaborative research should be enhanced to actively integrate into the global medical education ecosystem, thereby achieving high-quality, sustainable, and internationalized development of China's medical education.

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. First, the selection of policy texts focused on the national level, which may not fully reflect local-level specific practices and variations. Furthermore, the document screening criteria may introduce selection bias. Second, despite efforts to ensure objectivity during the coding process, manual interpretation and classification inevitably involve subjective judgment, potentially introducing coder bias. Furthermore, while the three-dimensional analytical framework developed in this study possesses a degree of universality, it originates from China's specific institutional and cultural context. Certain implicit governance logics may not have been fully captured, limiting its explanatory power when directly transplanted to other contexts. Future research could incorporate local policy texts and field survey data, combined with official statistical data, to conduct mixed-methods validation. This approach would further enhance the framework's robustness and applicability.

Conclusion

This study analyzes the national medical education policy system based on a three-dimensional framework comprising policy tools, medical education development, and policy intensity. It finds that the excessive allocation of environmental policy tools has led to an imbalance in the tool structure, weakening the actual effectiveness of supply–demand-oriented tools. While the distribution of policy nodes in the medical education development dimension is balanced, there are internal issues of mismatched adaptation, manifested in the highest number of policy tools in the safeguarding mechanism dimension, coupled with an imbalance in the proportion of the three types of policy tools within the safeguarding mechanism and ideological stance dimensions. Policy intensity results indicate the existence of institutional inertia where administrative dominance replaces grassroots innovation. In the future, it is urgent to optimize the structural composition of policy tools, strengthen the coordination between supply–demand-oriented and demand-oriented tools, and concurrently delegate core institutional autonomy such as curriculum design authority and resource integration authority, enabling universities to become the institutional supply entities within an open medical education ecosystem.
