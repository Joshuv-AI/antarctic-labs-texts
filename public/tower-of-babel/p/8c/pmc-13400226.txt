Dermographometer assessment in children with urticaria☆
Authors: Preawkalaya Suksai, Adithep Sawatchai, Potjanee Kiewngam, Wanlapa Jotikasthira, Watcharoot Kanchongkittiphon, Wiparat Manuyakorn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSymptomatic dermographism (SD) is the most common subtype of chronic inducible urticaria, yet its prevalence and clinical relevance in pediatric urticaria remain incompletely characterized. Objective assessment of SD and its relationship with disease activity in children is limited.MethodsIn this prospective observational study, children aged 1–18 years with acute or chronic urticaria were enrolled at a tertiary care center. Dermographometer testing using FricTest® 4.0 was performed at baseline and repeated after 6 weeks. SD was defined as a wheal diameter ≥3 mm with pruritus at 10 min after provocation. Disease activity and control were assessed using the Urticaria Activity Score over 7 days (UAS7) and the Urticaria Control Test (UCT).ResultsA total of 144 children (median age 9 years; 49.3% male) were included, of whom 79.2% had chronic urticaria. SD was identified in 24.3% of patients, with similar prevalence between acute and chronic urticaria. Children with SD were older and more frequently reported skin stroking or scratching as a trigger (p < 0.001). Dermographometer wheal diameter correlated with UAS7 (r = 0.33, p < 0.001). Lower dermographometer thresholds and more positive FricTest pins were associated with greater disease activity. At 6-week follow-up, children with SD showed improved dermographometer responses but were less likely to achieve well-controlled disease. SD at presentation was more frequent among children who progressed to chronic urticaria.ConclusionSD is common in pediatric urticaria and is associated with higher disease activity and poorer short-term disease control. Dermographometer-based provocation testing may complement patient-reported outcome measures.

Introduction

Urticaria is one of the most common acquired skin disorders, affecting approximately 20% of children during their lifetime.1 Urticarial wheals are characterized by sharply circumscribed, superficial swelling of variable size and shape, typically associated with reflex erythema and pruritus. These lesions are transient, resolving within 30 min to 24 h, and the skin subsequently returns to normal.2 Urticaria may manifest as wheals, angioedema, or both, and is classified as acute or chronic depending on whether symptoms persist beyond 6 weeks. Chronic urticaria is further categorized into chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) and chronic inducible urticaria (CIndU), among which symptomatic dermographism (SD) is the most common, affecting 65–73% of children with CIndU.3,4

Given the high prevalence and the recurrent nature of urticaria, lasting 1–4 years in CSU and even longer in CIndU,5 accurate assessment of disease activity is essential for optimizing management. Although patient-reported measures such as the Urticaria Activity Score (UAS7) and Urticaria Control Test (UCT) are widely used to guide treatment, they provide limited objective information, particularly in pediatric populations. This underscores the need for complementary tools capable of capturing the physiological component of urticaria beyond subjective symptom reporting.

The dermographometer, a validated non-invasive instrument used for provocation testing and threshold assessment in SD, offers such potential. Previous studies have demonstrated a substantial overlap between SD and CSU, with approximately 71% of patients with SD also having CSU. Additionally, 76% of patients with CSU report at least 1 physical trigger, 25% of which are confirmed as SD.6,7 These findings highlight the clinical relevance of SD across urticaria subtypes. However, epidemiological data and objective assessments in children remain limited, leaving a gap in understanding how SD contributes to overall disease burden. To address this gap, this study aimed to determine the occurrence of SD among children with urticaria and to examine the correlations between dermographometer-derived measurements and patient-reported outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This prospective observational study consecutively enrolled children aged 1–18 years who were diagnosed with urticaria by a physician. No formal sample size calculation was performed, and all eligible patients during the study period were included. Both acute and chronic urticaria were consecutively enrolled. Each participant underwent an objective assessment using a dermographometer (FricTest 4.0) and completed subjective evaluations, which included the Urticaria Activity Score (UAS7) and the Urticaria Control Test (UCT). Participants were excluded if they had open wounds at the dermographometer provocation site or had experienced anaphylaxis within the preceding month.

The study was approved by the Ethical Review Board, Mahidol University, and conducted from March 2023 to February 2024. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and their caregivers.

Study protocol

At the initial visit, baseline characteristics, including age, sex, underlying diseases, and current medications, were recorded. Clinical information related to urticaria (onset, duration, frequency, triggers, and prior treatments) was obtained. A dermographometer provocation test was performed to confirm SD and determine the individual threshold. Participants and caregivers completed the UAS7, which reflects symptoms during the preceding 7 days, and the UCT, assessing disease control over the past 4 weeks. For younger children under 5 years of age, UAS7 and UCT were completed by caregivers based on observed symptoms and behavior. Standard medical treatment was provided following the assessment. Participants continued their standard medical treatment, including antihistamines, and no medication washout was performed prior to dermographometer testing. A follow-up visit was scheduled 6 weeks later, during which the dermographometer test and questionnaires were repeated using identical procedures.

Dermographometer assessment and symptomatic dermographism

FricTest® 4.0 (MOXIE GmbH, Germany) is a validated dermographometer featuring a comb-like, four-pronged design that provides simultaneous stimulation at 4 pressure levels (I–IV). The device was placed perpendicularly on the volar forearm or on the upper back for children younger than 5 years and pressed until all pins were nearly invisible. It was then drawn once across approximately 6 cm of skin. A positive response was defined as a palpable wheal ≥3 mm in diameter at 10 min after provocation.8 SD was diagnosed when a positive wheal response was accompanied by pruritus.9

Urticaria activity score (UAS7)

The UAS7 evaluates 2 domains of disease activity: (1) the 24-h wheal count and (2) pruritus intensity, yielding a total score from 0 to 42. Higher scores indicate greater disease severity. The Thai version of UAS7 has been validated and incorporated into clinical practice guidelines.10

Urticaria control test (UCT)

The UCT consists of 4 items assessing disease control and its impact on quality of life over the preceding 4 weeks. The validated Thai version demonstrates good reliability and clinical applicability. Non-commercial use was authorized by MOXIE GmbH.

Study outcomes

Dermographometer results were recorded as the number of positive pins referred to as the Total Fric Score (TFS),11,12 and the wheal diameter at 10 min post-provocation.13 The dermographometer threshold was defined as the lowest stimulation level that produced a wheal response.

For disease activity assessment, UAS7 scores were categorized as follows: 0–6, well-controlled; 7–15, mild; 16–27, moderate; and 28–42, severe disease.14 A UCT score ≥12 (out of 16) indicated well-controlled urticaria.2 Treatment response and disease progression were evaluated by comparing dermographometer results, UAS7, and UCT scores between the first and second visits.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 18.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic data. Non-normally distributed variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was used to assess correlations between dermographometer wheal diameter and UAS7, and between UAS7 and UCT scores. The Kruskal–Wallis and Mann–Whitney U tests were used to evaluate the association between disease severity and FricTest grading. Differences between subgroups, such as symptomatic dermographism (SD) versus non-SD and acute versus chronic urticaria, were analyzed using the Pearson Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Changes in clinical and dermographometer findings between visits were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

Results

Clinical characteristics and subgroup comparisons

A total of 144 children with urticaria were enrolled, of whom 71 (49.3%) were male, with a median age of 9 years. Allergic rhinitis was the most common comorbidity (76, 52.8%), followed by atopic dermatitis (23, 16%), food allergy (17, 11.8%), and both asthma and drug allergy (11, 7.6% each). Autoimmune thyroiditis was uncommon, identified in only 3 patients (2.1%). SD was confirmed by a positive provocation test in 35 children (24.3%). The prevalence of SD did not differ significantly between acute and chronic urticaria (p = 0.273). Children with SD were older, with a median age of 12 years, compared with 8 years in those without SD (p = 0.003). However, sex distribution, urticaria duration, UAS7, and UCT scores showed no significant differences. Consistent with provocation test findings, children with SD more frequently reported skin stroking or scratching as a trigger of wheals than those without SD (74.3% vs. 34.0%; odds ratio (OR) 5.62, 95% confidence interval (CI) 2.37–13.30; p < 0.001). Antihistamine use did not differ between children with and without SD (eTable 1).

When comparing acute and chronic urticaria, children with acute urticaria were younger (median age 6 (3, 10.3) vs. 9 (5, 14) years; p = 0.008). Infection, based on a history of fever, respiratory symptoms, or gastrointestinal symptoms, was the predominant trigger reported in acute urticaria (p < 0.001). Disease activity was higher in acute urticaria, with a median UAS7 of 10.5 (3, 16.3) compared with 4 (0.8, 10) in chronic urticaria (p < 0.001). Conversely, UCT scores were higher among children with chronic urticaria, reflecting better disease control, likely due to ongoing treatment (p < 0.001). Antihistamine use was comparable between acute and chronic urticaria (eTable 2).

Associations between dermographometer reactivity and urticaria disease activity

A moderate positive correlation was observed between the median wheal diameter at 10 min after dermographometer provocation and the UAS7 (r = 0.33, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1a). Consistently, children with SD who demonstrated 3 or 4 positive FricTest pins had significantly higher median UAS7 scores compared with those without SD (p = 0.002 and p = 0.028, respectively), and within the SD subgroup, lower dermographometer thresholds, reflected by 3 or 4 positive pins, were associated with more active disease than having only 1 positive pin (p = 0.001 and p = 0.022) (Fig. 1b). Overall, increasing dermographometer reactivity was associated with higher disease activity. Clinical outcomes and disease progression by dermographism status

Of the 144 patients enrolled at baseline, 35 had SD, and 109 did not. At the 6-week follow-up, 135 patients (93.8%) completed the visit, including 33 with SD and 102 without SD. The remaining 9 patients were lost to follow-up due to inability to contact or failure to return for the scheduled visit. All longitudinal analyses were performed on participants with available follow-up data.

All patients demonstrated significant improvement in urticaria activity and symptom control after receiving standard treatments, with notable reductions observed at the 6-week follow-up (p < 0.001). Objective assessments showed that children with SD exhibited a significant decline in the number of positive FricTest pins (p = 0.009) (Fig. 3a), whereas those without SD displayed only minimal dermographometer changes across visits (p = 0.014) (Fig. 3b). From a medication standpoint, both groups experienced comparable rates of disease control, defined as the ability to discontinue or taper antihistamines or maintain the same regimen with adequate symptom suppression, with success rates of 84.8% and 85.3%, respectively. Similarly, fewer children with SD achieved UCT-defined disease control at the 6-week follow-up compared with those without SD (57.6% vs. 67.6%; OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.29–1.45). Despite comparable medication outcomes, patient-reported and clinician-scored outcomes suggested less apparent clinical improvement among children with SD. In the SD group, the distribution of UAS7 categories (well-controlled, mild–moderate, severe) and UCT-defined control status remained largely unchanged at 6 weeks, indicating that their clinical improvement was less evident despite ongoing treatment (Fig. 3c). In contrast, a significantly greater proportion of children without SD achieved well-controlled urticaria, as indicated by both UAS7 and UCT (p = 0.038 and p < 0.001, respectively) (Fig. 3d). Among the 29 patients who initially presented with acute urticaria and completed follow-up, 11 (37.9%) progressed to chronic urticaria, whereas 18 (62.1%) achieved remission by week 6. SD was identified during the acute episode in nearly half of the children who later developed chronic urticaria (45.5%), while none of the patients who achieved remission had SD (p = 0.004). This finding suggests that the presence of SD may be associated with an increased risk of progression to chronic urticaria.

Discussion

This study explored the prevalence of SD in 144 patients aged 1–18 years with urticaria and found that 24.3% of children had a positive provocation test by dermographometer. SD was more common among older children who reported skin stroking or scratching as one of the triggers. There was no significant difference in the diagnosis of SD in acute and chronic urticaria. A previous prospective study investigating the clinical profiles of children and adults with acute urticaria reported that dermographic urticaria accounted for 5.6% of the pediatric population.15 However, the diagnosis of SD was based on patient's history and general physical examination. In contrast, the use of a standardized provocation test in our study likely allowed for more accurate detection of SD, particularly in children with concomitant acute urticaria.

When provocation testing was applied, SD was identified in up to 16.7% of children presenting with acute urticaria, and nearly half of those who later progressed to chronic urticaria had SD diagnosed at the initial visit. This finding suggests that SD may represent an early marker of disease persistence rather than merely a coincidental finding. A study by Mori F et al. reported that dermatographism was elicited in 50.6% of acute urticaria and 52.2% of chronic urticaria children.16 Another retrospective single-center study analyzed 253 children aged less than 18 years, found dermographism in 50.6% of patients with chronic urticaria; however, data in that study were obtained from medical records review and questionnaires.3 In contrast, SD confirmed by dermographometer testing was present in 26.3% of children with chronic urticaria in our cohort. Moreover, a study focusing on CIndU in children reported that SD was the most prevalent subtype, affecting 65% of patients when provocation testing with a tongue blade was used.4 These findings were consistent with our study that SD was identified in 65.2% of cases with physical urticaria. Taken together, these data highlight the importance of objective provocation testing in defining the true burden of SD in pediatric urticaria.

In addition to its diagnostic value in SD, FricTest allows for quantitative assessment of individual urticarial trigger thresholds. Our study demonstrated meaningful correlations between dermographometer-derived parameters and patient-reported outcomes, supporting the clinical relevance of provocation-based measurements. Specifically, the association between the number of positive pins and the UAS7 suggests that dermographometer reactivity may reflect overall urticaria activity rather than an isolated local skin response. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating correlations between FricTest results and disease activity in SD and other forms of inducible urticaria.12,13,17,18

Evidence from interventional studies further supports this utility, as treatment with omalizumab at both 150 mg and 300 mg doses resulted in rapid improvements in FricTest-measured friction thresholds, accompanied by statistically significant and clinically meaningful reductions in disease activity and disease impact.17 Similarly, dermographometer-based assessments demonstrated a small but statistically significant reduction in cumulative wheal widths following UVB phototherapy (from 9.0 [3.5] mm to 5.7 [2.1] mm; P = 0.038).13 Improvements in provocation testing outcomes and FricTest-assessed disease activity have also been shown to correlate with skin mast cell depletion in barzolvolimab-treated patients,12 while FricTest-derived trigger thresholds measured at baseline and 3 months after benralizumab initiation further highlight the value of standardized skin provocation testing for objectively capturing treatment responses over time.18 Collectively, these data support the use of standardized provocation testing as an objective adjunct to patient-reported measures for assessing disease activity and treatment response in pediatric urticaria.

One of the most clinically relevant findings of our study was that children with SD demonstrated poorer disease control despite having comparable antihistamine use and similar medication control rates to those without SD. This suggests that SD may identify a subgroup of patients with a greater symptomatic burden that is not fully captured by treatment intensity alone. The coexistence of physical urticaria and chronic spontaneous urticaria has been recognized as one of the factors associated with longer disease duration.19 Consistent with this, children with SD in our cohort achieved less disease control, as assessed by both UAS7 and UCT, compared with those without SD at the same follow-up interval. From a clinical perspective, objective identification of SD may therefore help clinicians recognize children who require closer follow-up and potentially more individualized management strategies. However, confidence intervals around the estimated effect size were wide, likely reflecting limited statistical power for subgroup analyses, and the observed associations should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution. Although causality and long-term prognostic implications cannot be established from the current study, the presence of SD may be associated with a more difficult-to-control disease course.

The strengths of this study include the use of a validated, standardized provocation test to diagnose SD and the inclusion of both acute and chronic urticaria in a pediatric population. Importantly, the simultaneous assessment of objective dermographometer measurements and patient-reported outcomes allowed for a comprehensive evaluation of disease activity. Limitations include the single-center design, relatively small sample size, and short follow-up duration, which preclude definitive conclusions regarding long-term prognosis. In additional, as dermographometer testing was performed without antihistamine washout, reflecting real-world clinical practice; however ongoing treatment may have attenuated wheal responses and influenced UAS7 scores, potentially leading to underestimation of disease activity. Notably, antihistamine use was comparable across clinically relevant subgroups (SD vs. without SD and acute vs. chronic urticaria), which may partially mitigate this effect. Furthermore, in children younger than 5 years, UAS7 and UCT were completed by caregivers, which may introduce subjectivity and affect measurement accuracy. Although a previous study has suggested that dermographism responses are comparable across commonly used anatomical sites, such as the back and forearm,20 the use of different sites in our study (upper back in younger children and the volar forearm in older children) may have introduced some variability in skin reactivity. Finally, given the number of statistical comparisons performed, there is a potential risk of type I error; therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution and considered exploratory. In addition, some subgroup analyses included small numbers of patients, particularly those with both acute urticaria and symptomatic dermographism, which may limit the generalizability of these findings. Accordingly, future multicenter studies with extended follow-up are warranted to determine whether early identification of SD can inform risk stratification and personalized management strategies in pediatric urticaria.

Taken together, these findings suggest that SD is a common and clinically relevant feature in pediatric urticaria that can be objectively assessed using standardized provocation testing. The observed associations between dermographometer reactivity, disease activity, and short-term disease control support the role of FricTest as a complementary tool to patient-reported outcome measures in routine clinical practice. Early identification of SD may help identify children who require closer monitoring or more tailored management. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the prognostic implications of SD and to define the optimal role of dermographometer-based assessment in the long-term management of pediatric urticaria.
