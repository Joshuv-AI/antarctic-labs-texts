Intraperitoneal ZP123 improves aged oocyte quality by restoring granulosa cell gap junctions and improving mitochondrial function
Authors: Hui Teng, Qijun Xie, Cheng Zhou, Rujun Ma, Xie Ge, Jun Jing, Zhichuan Zou, Yuming Feng, Li Chen, Bing Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFemale reproductive aging is the most critical determinant of decreased fertility because the quality of oocytes gradually deteriorates with age, leading to poor embryo development, increased miscarriage rates, and reduced live births. Oocyte growth and maturation depend on bidirectional communication with surrounding granulosa cells through gap junctions, which are composed mainly of connexin 43 (CX43). Although intercellular communication mediated by CX43 is essential for folliculogenesis, how it becomes altered in the context of ovarian aging and drives oocyte deterioration remains largely unclear. This study aimed to clarify age-related alterations in gap junction communication and investigate whether augmenting CX43-mediated coupling via the peptide ZP123 might restore oocyte competence and enhance fertility outcomes in older females.ResultsOur data demonstrated that CX43 expression in mouse ovaries and granulosa cells markedly decreased in an age-dependent manner, and this reduction was evident at both the protein and mRNA levels. The level of phosphorylated CX43 at Ser373 decreased markedly, indicating that total CX43 loss was the primary contributor to impaired gap junction communication. In vitro, our study revealed that ZP123 treatment significantly enhanced dye transfer between KGN cells but did not increase CX43 expression, suggesting an improvement in gap junction intercellular communication without increasing CX43 protein abundance. In vivo, chronic ZP123 administration to aged mice restored the normal gap junction between granulosa cells, improved follicle development. Functionally, ZP123 enhanced oocyte maturation (GVBD and MII rates), corrected spindle abnormalities, improved mitochondrial distribution, and increased ATP production while reducing ROS accumulation. Moreover, the mitochondrial membrane potential and Ca²⁺ homeostasis were restored, and excessive opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore was prevented. Consistent with these cellular improvements, ZP123-treated aged mice presented increased ovulation numbers and enhanced preimplantation embryo development, including increased blastocyst formation rates.ConclusionsThis study revealed that disrupted CX43-mediated communication is a key mechanism underlying the decrease in oocyte quality during ovarian aging. Pharmacological enhancement of gap junctions with ZP123 effectively restored ovarian function, oocyte competence, and developmental potential, highlighting gap junction modulation as a novel and promising therapeutic avenue to mitigate age-related fertility decline in women of advanced maternal age.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-026-02139-1.Keywords: Aging, Granulosa cells, Gap junction, CX43, ZP123, Oocyte

Introduction

Infertility has become an increasing public health concern in recent decades, currently affecting 10–15% of couples worldwide [1, 2]. Decreased female fertility is a major contributor to this problem [3]. Age represents a key physiological and nonmodifiable determinant among multiple factors influencing fertility, which decreases gradually but significantly beginning at approximately 32 years of age and decreases more rapidly after 37 years of age [4, 5]. Specifically, the prevalence of infertility increases from < 15% in women under 35 years to ~ 30% in those aged 35–40 years and exceeds 60% after 40 years [6]. The age-related decline in female fertility is attributed primarily to reduced ovarian function [7], impaired endometrial receptivity [8], oocyte quality [9], etc., in which oocyte quality deterioration is the decisive factor [10], which leads to increased incidences of embryo arrest, spontaneous abortion, and birth defects. Nevertheless, the trend of delayed childbearing in modern societies is rapidly increasing [11], and advanced maternal age (AMA) has emerged as a pressing global challenge [12]. Accordingly, improving the fertility of AMA has become a major issue that urgently needs to be solved in the field of reproductive health worldwide [13, 14].

Oocyte growth and maturation occur within the follicular microenvironment and critically depend on surrounding granulosa cells [15, 16]. Communication between oocytes and granulosa cells is bidirectional, and this interaction is a key determinant of oocyte competence [17]. Among the mechanisms mediating this crosstalk, gap junctions play a central role. These specialized intercellular channels, composed of connexin (CX) proteins, enable the transfer of small signaling molecules (< 1 kDa) between adjacent cells, thereby coordinating metabolic and electrical activities [18, 19]. In ovarian follicles, gap junctions establish functional coupling between oocytes and granulosa cells, forming a metabolic syncytium that supports folliculogenesis [20, 21]. Among the connexin family, connexin 43 (CX43, encoded by Gja1) is the predominant gap junction protein expressed in granulosa cells and is essential for follicle development [22]. Genetic studies have demonstrated that loss of CX43 leads to follicular arrest at the primary stage and impaired oocyte growth, highlighting its indispensable role in ovarian function [20].Importantly, CX43 function is not solely determined by its expression level but is also tightly regulated by post-translational phosphorylation throughout its life cycle. CX43 exists in multiple phosphorylation-dependent forms (P0–P3), which are associated with distinct subcellular localization and functional states [23]. For example, the P2 isoform represents the membrane-localized functional pool incorporated into active gap junction plaques, whereas the P3 isoform is associated with channel internalization and degradation [24, 25]. In addition, phosphorylation at specific regulatory sites, such as Ser373, promotes CX43 membrane trafficking, plaque stabilization, and efficient intercellular coupling. Together, these findings suggest that both CX43 abundance and its phosphorylation status are critical determinants of gap junction functionality.

Given the importance of gap junction communication, pharmacological modulation of this pathway has emerged as a potential strategy to enhance intercellular coupling. ZP123 (rotigaptide), a synthetic antiarrhythmic peptide, has been shown to stabilize CX43-mediated gap junction channels and prevent channel uncoupling under pathological conditions [26]. Previous studies have demonstrated that ZP123 increases gap junction conductance and preserves CX43-dependent coupling in cardiac myocytes and other CX43-expressing cell models [27]. However, whether pharmacological enhancement of gap junction communication by ZP123 can improve ovarian function and oocyte quality during reproductive aging remains unknown. Despite the well-established role of gap junctions in folliculogenesis, how gap junction communication is altered during ovarian aging and how these changes contribute to the decline in oocyte quality remain largely unclear. Addressing this knowledge gap is essential for understanding the mechanisms underlying maternal age–related infertility and may provide new therapeutic strategies to improve reproductive outcomes in advanced-aged females.

In this study, 2-month-old and 12-month-old mice were used to model reproductive-aged and advanced-aged females, respectively. We first demonstrated that gap junction communication between granulosa cells is impaired in aged ovaries. To further explore the functional significance of this defect, we employed both in vitro assays and in vivo mouse models to evaluate the effects of ZP123, a pharmacological enhancer of gap junction function. Our results show that ZP123 administration improves CX43-mediated intercellular communication and is associated with enhanced follicle development, oocyte maturation, spindle organization, and mitochondrial function, ultimately leading to improved preimplantation embryo development and reproductive outcomes. Together, these findings highlight the critical role of gap junction communication in maintaining oocyte competence and suggest that ZP123 may represent a potential therapeutic strategy to alleviate maternal age–related reproductive decline.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

ICR female mice of SPF grade were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Nanjing Medical University. The animals were maintained under controlled environmental conditions (20–25 °C, 50–70% relative humidity, and a 12 h light/dark cycle) with free access to standard chow and water. All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Jinling Hospital, Nanjing Medical University (Approval No. 2023JLHGZRDWLS-000133) and conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Previous studies have reported that female mice exhibit a decline in fertility beginning at ~ 6 months of age, with 10–12 months corresponding to women aged ~ 38–40 years, similar to human perimenopause [28]. Accordingly, 8-week-old mice were used to represent young females, and 12-month-old mice were used to model advanced maternal age. For aged-mouse treatment, 11-month-old females received daily intraperitoneal injections of ZP123 (200 nmol/kg) for 1 month [29] until they reached 12 months of age.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR)

Total RNA was extracted via the TRIzol method and reverse-transcribed with HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, R323-01). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed with SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, Q711-02). 18 S rRNA served as the internal control, and relative expression levels were calculated via the 2^−ΔΔCT method [30]. The primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blotting

Protein samples were separated by SDS‒PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in TBST for 2 h at room temperature, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and then washed three times with TBST. After incubation with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature, signals were detected via an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (FDBio, FD8020). Antibody information is listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Phos-tag SDS PAGE analysis

To assess the phosphorylation status of target proteins, a Phos-tag mobility shift assay was performed. Ovarian tissues were lysed in extraction buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, supernatants were collected, and immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted and resuspended in SDS loading buffer. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE with or without Phos-tag reagent (50 µM) and MnCl₂ (100 µM). Phos-tag gels were run at 80 V for 3–4 h under cooling conditions, while regular gels were run at 100 V for 1-1.5 h. Gels were washed with transfer buffer containing EDTA to remove Mn²⁺, followed by washing without EDTA, and proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes at 4 °C. Membranes were incubated with primary and secondary antibodies, and signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence. Phosphorylated and non-phosphorylated forms were distinguished by mobility shifts in the Phos-tag gel.

Granulosa cell isolation from mice

Female mice of different ages were intraperitoneally injected with 5 IU pregnant mare serum gonadotrophin (PMSG; Ningbo Sansheng, Ningbo, China) to stimulate granulosa cell proliferation. After 44 h, the ovaries were collected, and granulosa cells were released by puncturing the follicles with 26-gauge needles under sterile conditions. The cells were washed, harvested by brief centrifugation, and cultured as previously described [31].

KGN human granulosa cell tumor cell culture and treatment

KGN cells, a human granulosa-like tumor cell line, were obtained from EK Bioscience (Shanghai, China). The cells were maintained in DMEM/Ham’s F12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Cat#10099-141), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. For treatment, the cells were exposed to 50 nM ZP123 for 5 h.

Lucifer yellow dye transfer assay

KGN cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per 60-mm dish and cultured in 6-well plates. The cells were washed with PBS, and 10 µL of Lucifer yellow (LY) dye (4 mg/mL) was applied while the monolayer was scratched to perform the scrape-loading assay. The plates were incubated at room temperature for 1 min, washed with PBS, and then incubated in fresh PBS at 37 °C for 10 min to allow dye diffusion into neighboring cells. The cells were subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Images were acquired with an Olympus IX-81 microscope (10× objective, 488 nm excitation), and the dye transfer distance was quantified via ImageJ software (NIH, USA) [32, 33].

Superovulation, in vitro fertilization, and embryo culture

The female mice were superovulated via the intraperitoneal injection of 5 IU PMSG, followed by 5 IU hCG 48 h later. Fourteen to fifteen hours after hCG injection, cumulus-oocyte complexes (COCs) were collected from the oviducts. COCs were placed in M2 medium and treated with hyaluronidase for 2–3 min to remove cumulus cells, and MII oocytes were isolated, counted, and analyzed.

For in vitro fertilization, COCs were collected in human tubal fluid (HTF; Sigma, MR070). Sperm from the cauda epididymis of wild-type males were capacitated in HTF medium and then coincubated with COCs at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 4–6 h. Fertilized embryos were subsequently transferred to KSOM medium and cultured, with developmental progression monitored at E1.5, E2.5, E3.5, and E4.5.

Mouse oocyte collection and culture

For the collection of oocytes, ICR mice were killed by cervical dislocation, and the ovaries were collected in M2 medium. GV intact oocytes were picked from a pipetted tube under a stereoscope. Groups of 25–30 oocytes were placed in 30 µL droplets of M16 culture medium under mineral oil in a cell culture dish and maintained at 37 °C in a 5% humidified CO2 atmosphere for maturation in vitro.

Immunofluorescence staining

For frozen ovarian sections, the ovaries were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, cryosectioned at 8–10 μm, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 1% Triton X-100, and blocked in 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). The sections were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. For oocytes and embryos, samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 1% Triton X-100, and blocked in 5% BSA. The samples were subsequently incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and then with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. For both assays, the nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342, and fluorescence images were acquired via a Zeiss LSM 700 confocal microscope. Antibody information is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Assessment of Oocyte ROS, MMP, GSH and ATP

Reactive oxygen species (ROS), the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), and ATP levels were assessed via specific fluorescent probes. The probes used included dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (Beyotime) for ROS, MitoTracker Red CMXRos (Beyotime) for mitochondria, ATP Red 1 (Beyotime) for ATP, and JC-1 (Beyotime) for the MMP. The kit information is provided in Supplementary Table 3. Oocytes were incubated in M2 medium containing the appropriate probe for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark with 5% CO₂, washed five times with PBS, and immediately analyzed via a laser scanning confocal microscope.

Statistical analysis

All the experiments in this study were performed in triplicate. Fluorescence intensity analysis was conducted via ImageJ software (v1.53, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, US), statistical analyses were carried out via SPSS software (v26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, US), and figures were generated via GraphPad Prism software (v8.0, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, US). Statistical differences between groups were analyzed via independent-samples t tests, and the results are presented as the means ± standard errors of the means (means ± SEMs). Statistical significance was determined as indicated in the figure legends, with P values < 0.05 considered significant (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001).

Results

Age-dependent decline of CX43 expression and phosphorylation in granulosa cells

To determine whether CX43 expression changes with age, ovarian tissues and granulosa cells from mice at 2, 4, 8, and 12 months were collected, and total CX43 levels were analyzed by Western blotting. The results revealed that total CX43 protein levels were highest at 2 months and gradually decreased at 4 and 8 months, reaching the lowest levels at 12 months in both ovarian tissues (Figure S1A, B) and granulosa cells (Fig. 1A, B). Immunofluorescence staining of cumulus–oocyte complex (COCs) further demonstrated that the number of CX43-positive signals in granulosa cells was markedly reduced in 12-month-old ovaries compared with 2-month-old controls, confirming the age-dependent decline in CX43 protein levels (Fig. 1C). qRT‒PCR analysis revealed a progressive decrease in CX43 mRNA expression with age in granulosa cells, with the 12-month group showing only 27% of the level observed at 2 months (Fig. 1D).

Given that CX43 exists in an unphosphorylated form (P0) and multiple phosphorylated forms (P1–P3) [23], a Phos-tag SDS PAGE assay was performed to assess its phosphorylation status in granulosa cells. Multiple CX43 forms (P0–P3) were detectable at 2 months, whereas the intensities of all bands progressively decreased with age, reaching the lowest levels at 12 months (Fig. 1E). Ser373, a key regulatory site located at the C-terminal tail of CX43, promotes channel opening [34]. Western blot analysis showed that p-CX43 (Ser373) levels gradually decreased with age in in both ovarian tissues (Figure S1C, D) and granulosa cells (Fig. 1F, G), with the lowest levels observed in the 12-month group. Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant reduction in aged mice compared with young controls. To assess the functional consequence, gap junction intercellular communication (GJIC) in human ovarian granulosa-like tumor cell line KGN cell was evaluated using a Lucifer yellow (LY) dye transfer assay. In the normal and negative control (NC) groups, LY dye efficiently diffused from the scrape line to surrounding cells, whereas diffusion was markedly restricted in the Cx43 knockdown group (Fig. 1H). Quantification revealed a significant reduction in dye transfer area in the knockdown group compared with the normal and NC groups, with no difference between the normal and NC groups (Fig. 1I). These results indicate that CX43 expression and phosphorylation decrease with ovarian aging and that Cx43 knockdown significantly impairs gap junction intercellular communication in granulosa cells.

ZP123 enhances gap junction communication in KGN without increasing CX43 expression

ZP123 reportedly improves CX43-mediated gap junction function [35]. To further assess its effect on granulosa cell communication, the KGN cell was treated with 50 nM ZP123 for 5 h [26], and then GJIC was evaluated by LY dye transfer through gap junctions. In the NC + Vehicle (solvent control) group, LY dye diffusion was limited to cells adjacent to the scratch line. In contrast, treatment with ZP123 markedly enhanced dye spread to neighboring cells, indicating improved GJIC. However, in Cx43 knockdown (Cx43-KD) cells, LY dye transfer was substantially reduced, and ZP123 treatment failed to restore dye diffusion to levels comparable to those in control cells (Fig. 1K&L). Collectively, these results demonstrate that ZP123 enhances GJIC in KGN cells in a CX43-dependent manner. However, western blot analysis revealed no obvious change in CX43 or phosphorylated CX43 (Ser373) protein expression after ZP123 treatment (Figure S1E&F). In summary, these findings indicate that ZP123 improved GJIC in KGN cells, but this effect was not accompanied by increased CX43 or p-CX43 (Ser373) protein levels.

ZP123 improves ovarian gap junction ultrastructure

To further determine whether ZP123 can improve ovarian GJIC in vivo, 11-month-old mice were divided into two groups, the control and treatment groups, by administering intraperitoneal injections of ZP123 and vehicle daily for one month. To evaluate the potential systemic effects of long-term ZP123 administration, metabolic parameters and serum biochemical indices were assessed in 12-month-old mice treated with vehicle or ZP123 for one month. No significant differences were observed between the two groups in body weight, daily water intake, daily food intake, urine output, or fecal output (Figure S2A–E).Furthermore, serum biochemical analyses revealed no significant changes in liver function markers, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), gamma-glutamyltransferase (γ-GT), total bilirubin (TBIL), direct bilirubin (DBIL), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), albumin (ALB), or total bile acids (TBA) (Figure S2F–M). Similarly, renal function indicators, including blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (Cr), and uric acid (UA), showed no significant differences between groups (Figure S2N–P). In addition, cardiac enzyme parameters, including creatine kinase (CK), creatine kinase-MB isoenzyme (CK-MB), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and LDH-1, were comparable between the two groups (Figure S2Q–T).Collectively, these results indicate that chronic ZP123 administration does not induce detectable systemic metabolic or organ toxicity in aged mice. COCs were collected from 2-month (young control mice), 12-month + vehicle group, and 12-month + ZP123 groups for ultrastructural analysis. Electron microscopy revealed that granulosa cells in 2-month-old mice exhibited well-defined gap junction structures. In contrast, COCs from 12-month-old mice showed loose cell contacts with shorter and less discernible gap junction structures. Notably, ZP123 treatment partially improved gap junction–like ultrastructural features in aged COCs (Fig. 2A and Figure S2U).Taken together, these findings suggest that ZP123 treatment is associated with an improvement in ovarian gap junction ultrastructure in aged mice.

ZP123 improves follicle development in aged mice

Fertility testing was performed to assess the effect of ZP123 on the reproductive phenotype of aged mice. The average litter size of the ZP123-treated 12-month-old mice (3.10 ± 1.91 pups) was greater than that of the untreated 12-month-old mice (1.70 ± 1.82 pups), but the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.11) (Fig. 2B). Consistently, superovulation analysis revealed a marked reduction in ovulation numbers in 12-month-old untreated mice compared with 2-month-old young controls, whereas ZP123 administration significantly increased ovulation numbers (Fig. 2E). In line with these findings, compared with 2-month-old control mice, 12-month-old untreated mice presented markedly fewer primordial (609.0 ± 78.4 vs. 223.3 ± 26.5, P < 0.001), primary (172.0 ± 12.5 vs. 46.0 ± 7.94, P < 0.001), secondary (124.3 ± 28.3 vs. 31.3 ± 10.1, P < 0.001), preantral (21.3 ± 1.53 vs. 9.67 ± 2.10, P < 0.001), and antral (18.0 ± 1.73 vs. 7.67 ± 1.53, P < 0.001) follicle counts. Most notably, ZP123 administration significantly increased the numbers of secondary (54.0 ± 11.36, P < 0.05), preantral (13.3 ± 0.58, P < 0.05), and antral (11.3 ± 1.15, P < 0.05) follicles relative to those in untreated 12-month-old mice (Fig. 2C, D). Collectively, these results indicate that ZP123 promotes the survival and development of recruited follicles, thereby improving functional follicle activity in aged mice.

ZP123 improves oocyte maturation and preimplantation embryo development in aged mice

To further evaluate oocyte quality and developmental competence, in vitro maturation (IVM) assays were performed. Oocytes from 12-month-old mice presented significantly lower GVBD (81.3 ± 10.7% vs. 44.4 ± 2.4%, P < 0.001) and MII rates (66.5 ± 4.3% vs. 25.0 ± 1.5%, P < 0.001) than those from 2-month-old controls. Notably, ZP123 treatment markedly improved these outcomes, with GVBD and MII rates increasing to 74.0 ± 8.0% and 60.8 ± 1.4%, respectively (Fig. 2F&G).

In parallel, IVF experiments were conducted to assess preimplantation embryo development. Compared with 2-month controls, 12-month mice presented significantly lower proportions of 4-cell (92.7 ± 0.5% vs. 67.5 ± 4.5%, P < 0.05), morula (91.9 ± 1.9% vs. 64.9 ± 5.9%, P < 0.05), and blastocyst formation (85.4 ± 1.1% vs. 58.9 ± 3.4%, P < 0.05) embryos. However, ZP123 administration effectively rescued these defects, increasing the rates of 4-cell, morula, and blastocyst formation to 87.7 ± 2.2%, 83.6 ± 1.3%, and 79.5 ± 5.1%, respectively (Fig. 2H&I). Taken together, these findings indicate that intraperitoneal injection of ZP123 improves oocyte maturation and preimplantation embryo development in aged mice.

ZP123 restores spindle integrity and mitochondrial function in aged oocytes

To evaluate the effects of ZP123 on spindle integrity and mitochondrial function in aged oocytes, fluorescence staining was carried out. The results revealed that normal MII oocytes presented a typical barrel-shaped spindle structure with well-aligned chromosomes at the metaphase plate. In contrast, a significantly greater proportion of abnormal spindle morphologies (e.g., irregular, monopolar, or multipolar spindles) and chromosome misalignment (e.g., displacement, scattering, or even migration toward spindle poles) were observed in the 12-month group (13% vs. 43%, P < 0.05). Notably, as shown in Fig. 3A&B, the proportion of abnormal spindle configurations was markedly reduced in the ZP123-treated group (22.4%, P < 0.05).

We subsequently examined mitochondrial function. Immunofluorescence imaging revealed that mitochondria in 2-month control oocytes were predominantly enriched around chromosomes, whereas perichromosomal clustering was markedly reduced in oocytes from the 12-month group. Notably, ZP123 treatment substantially restored the mitochondrial content in aged oocytes compared with that in the 12-month untreated group (Fig. 3C&D). Furthermore, compared with those in 2-month-old oocytes, significantly elevated ROS levels were observed in 12-month-old oocytes (8.0 ± 5.7 vs. 37.3 ± 10.7, P < 0.05), accompanied by a pronounced reduction in ATP content (28.4 ± 4.1 vs. 17.4 ± 2.1, P < 0.05). However, in the ZP123-treated group, ROS accumulation was effectively attenuated (15.8 ± 7.8, P < 0.05) (Fig. 3E&F), whereas ATP production was restored (21.5 ± 2.3, P < 0.05) (Fig. 3G). Overall, our findings indicate that ZP123 treatment can restore spindle integrity and improve metabolic homeostasis in aged oocytes.

Mitochondrial function is restored by ZP123 in aged oocytes

The mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) was first assessed via JC-1 staining. In 2-month-old oocytes, stronger JC-1 fluorescence-positive signals (red color) predominantly clustered into large clusters, indicating a normal MMP, whereas in 12-month-old oocytes, weaker positive signals and loose clustered masses with increased green monomers were observed, which was consistent with mitochondrial depolarization. Importantly, ZP123 treatment restored the red/green ratio toward control levels, indicating the recovery of the MMP (Fig. 4A and B). A similar trend was confirmed by TMRE fluorescence staining (Fig. 4C and D). Mitochondrial calcium flux and permeability were next evaluated via Rhod-2 AM staining and calcein-AM/CoCl₂ assays, respectively. Rhod-2 AM staining revealed that mitochondrial Ca²⁺ levels were significantly decreased in 12-month-old oocytes but were effectively restored following ZP123 treatment (Fig. 4E and F). In parallel, oocytes from 12-month-old mice presented significantly greater opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) than did those from 2-month-old control mice, whereas ZP123 administration markedly reduced pore opening. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that ZP123 treatment alleviates age-associated mitochondrial dysfunction in oocytes by preserving the MMP, limiting mPTP opening, and maintaining mitochondrial Ca²⁺ homeostasis.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that maternal aging is associated with a progressive decline in connexin 43 (CX43) expression and impaired gap junction intercellular communication (GJIC) between granulosa cells, both of which are essential for folliculogenesis and oocyte maturation. Consistent with this, pharmacological enhancement of gap junction function by ZP123 significantly improved intercellular coupling in KGN cells in vitro. Furthermore, in vivo administration of ZP123 in aged mice was associated with partial improvement of gap junction ultrastructure within cumulus–oocyte complexes, accompanied by enhanced follicle development, improved oocyte maturation, and increased preimplantation embryo development. Collectively, these findings support a functional link between CX43-mediated communication and the maintenance of oocyte competence during ovarian aging.

Given the presence of multiple connexin isoforms in the ovary, we focused on CX43 rather than CX37. CX43 is the predominant connexin mediating intercellular communication among granulosa cells and forms the principal gap junction channels within the granulosa cell network. Its essential role in folliculogenesis is well established, as it enables bidirectional transfer of metabolites and signaling molecules between oocytes and granulosa cells, thereby supporting oocyte growth and maturation [36–38]. Consistently, studies in Gja1 knockout mice have demonstrated that loss of CX43 leads to follicular arrest and infertility [39]. In contrast, CX37 is primarily localized at the oocyte–granulosa cell interface and mediates heterologous communication between oocytes and cumulus cells, representing a distinct functional pathway [40]. Therefore, given that our study specifically addressed granulosa cell communication during ovarian aging, CX43 represents the most relevant target. Moreover, ZP123 is a known enhancer of CX43-mediated gap junction activity, further supporting this focus. Importantly, although the role of gap junctions in folliculogenesis is well established, their alteration during ovarian aging remains poorly understood. In this context, our findings provide direct evidence that CX43-mediated intercellular communication declines with age.

Interestingly, an apparent discrepancy was observed between the timing of CX43 decline and the onset of reproductive aging. Fertility decline occurred earlier (approximately 6–8 months of age) than the most pronounced reduction in total CX43 protein observed at 12 months. However, both Western blot and qRT–PCR analyses revealed a gradual decrease in CX43 expression beginning as early as 4–8 months, indicating that CX43 downregulation is a progressive, age-associated process rather than a late-stage event. Importantly, GJIC is not solely determined by total CX43 abundance but is also tightly regulated by post-translational modifications, membrane localization, and channel gating. Therefore, functional impairment of GJIC may precede overt reductions in protein levels. Consistent with this, Phos-tag analysis revealed early reductions in multiple phosphorylated forms of CX43, suggesting that disruption of its functional regulation occurs at an early stage of ovarian aging.

To further elucidate this mechanism, we focused on phosphorylation at Ser373 as a representative regulatory site of CX43. Ser373 is a well-characterized functional site that promotes channel opening and membrane localization, thereby enhancing gap junction communication [41]. Importantly, Phos-tag analysis revealed global reductions in CX43 phosphorylation (P1–P3 forms), supporting a widespread disruption of its regulatory state. Based on these findings, Ser373 was selected as a functionally relevant indicator of CX43 activity. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that CX43 contains multiple phosphorylation sites governing distinct aspects of its lifecycle, and a comprehensive analysis of all regulatory sites was beyond the scope of this study. Future investigations will be required to fully define the phosphorylation-dependent regulatory network of CX43 during ovarian aging.

Importantly, although GJIC in KGN cell was significantly improved following ZP123 administration, neither total CX43 nor phosphorylated CX43 levels were restored. This suggests that ZP123 enhances gap junction function independently of changes in protein abundance, likely by modulating channel gating or stabilizing connexin channels. Consistent with this, previous studies have shown that ZP123 increases connexin channel open probability and stabilizes intercellular coupling [26]. Our findings extend these observations to the reproductive system, demonstrating enhanced granulosa cell communication following ZP123 treatment.

At the same time, the potential systemic effects of long-term ZP123 administration should be considered. Given its intraperitoneal delivery and the widespread distribution of gap junctions across multiple organs, it is important to distinguish ovarian-specific effects from systemic influences. In this study, metabolic and biochemical analyses revealed no significant differences between ZP123- and vehicle-treated groups, suggesting the absence of overt systemic toxicity. However, subtle systemic effects cannot be excluded. Therefore, future studies using ovarian-targeted delivery approaches, ex vivo culture systems, and pharmacokinetic analyses will be necessary to further clarify the tissue-specific actions of ZP123.

In addition to gap junction communication, we further explored downstream cellular processes associated with oocyte quality. Aging is known to impair mitochondrial function, spindle organization, and chromosomal alignment. In this study, ZP123 treatment reduced spindle abnormalities and restored mitochondrial function, as evidenced by decreased ROS levels, increased ATP production, stabilization of mitochondrial membrane potential, reduced mPTP opening, and normalized mitochondrial Ca²⁺ homeostasis. These findings suggest that ZP123 exerts broader protective effects on oocyte quality beyond gap junction regulation.

Notably, impaired GJIC, mitochondrial dysfunction, and spindle abnormalities likely form an interconnected regulatory network during ovarian aging. Although the causal relationships among these processes remain unclear, the multifunctional effects of ZP123 suggest that it may act as a key intermediary linking gap junction communication with mitochondrial function and cytoskeletal stability. This integrated mechanism may underlie its ability to partially restore oocyte competence in aged ovaries.

From a translational perspective, these findings highlight ZP123 as a potential therapeutic strategy for advanced maternal age. Current approaches, such as antioxidant supplementation or mitochondrial replacement, have limited efficacy or feasibility. By targeting the fundamental communication network between oocytes and granulosa cells, ZP123 may provide a novel means of improving ovarian function and reproductive outcomes.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study was conducted in mouse models and a granulosa-like tumor cell line, and its relevance to human ovarian aging requires further validation. Second, the long-term safety and optimal dosing of ZP123 remain unclear. Finally, additional studies are needed to explore potential combinatory therapeutic strategies.

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that ZP123 enhances CX43-mediated gap junction communication and alleviates mitochondrial dysfunction in aged oocytes, thereby improving folliculogenesis, oocyte maturation, and embryo development. These findings underscore the importance of intercellular communication in maintaining oocyte competence and suggest ZP123 as a promising candidate for mitigating age-related reproductive decline.
