Cholesterol modified defense peptide as an EMP2-siRNA delivery system for synergistic immunogene therapy against breast cancer
Authors: Xiaoyun Wang, Siliang Chen, Gu He, Yanghui Zhu, Nan Zhang, Changyang Gong, Xiang Li, Yujuan Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Epithelial membrane protein 2 (EMP2) plays crucial roles in cell proliferation, migration, and adhesion. Despite its importance, conventional EMP2 RNAi therapy shows limited efficacy in vivo. We therefore developed a novel RNA-delivery system utilizing self-assembling defense peptide-cholesterol conjugates for efficient EMP2-siRNA transfection. The engineered HH2-siEMP2 nanoparticles exhibited optimal size and positive surface charge, conferring excellent serum stability and enhanced cellular uptake in breast cancer cell lines. These nanoparticles effectively silenced EMP2 expression, leading to significant suppression of tumor migration and invasion in both in vitro and in vivo models. Beyond direct anti-tumor effects, the HH2-C conjugate demonstrated immunomodulatory properties by promoting Th1 cell expansion, reducing Th2 cells and immunosuppressive Tregs, and restoring Th17/Treg balance. These findings establish EMP2 as a promising therapeutic target in breast cancer and highlight the potential of HH2-C-based nanoparticles as a dual-function platform combining efficient siRNA delivery with immunostimulatory activity.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BRCA) is one of the most common types of female malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1,2]. Despite significant advancements in early diagnosis and treatment, the emergence multidrug resistance and severe side effects have led to low overall survival rates along with poor quality of life [3,4]. Consequently, the development of diverse therapeutic strategies has been pursued to overcome the problems. Notably, gene therapy has garnered substantial attention as a mean to enhance the efficacy of breast cancer treatment [5,6]. Gene therapies can be divided into viral vectors and non-viral vectors, each utilizing different delivery systems [6]. Viral vectors, while effective, carry the potential risk of gene disorders, insertional mutation, and immunogenicity, thus limiting their widespread application [7,8]. Non-viral vectors, on the other hand, present a promising alternative by circumventing the drawbacks associated viral vectors. However, non-viral vectors currently face challenges such as low transfection rates and suboptimal gene expression efficiencies [9].

Epithelial membrane protein 2 (EMP2) is a tetraspan protein associated with aggressive disease, exhibiting elevated expression in advanced breast, ovarian, and endometrial malignancies [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Within these tumors, EMP2 has been identified as an independent prognostic indicator, with its expression inversely correlated with patient survival [[14], [15], [16]]. In normal tissue, EMP2 displays distinct distribution patterns across various cell types, with high expression observed in secretory endometrium [17,18], lung alveolar epithelium [19], and moderate levels found in the eye, heart, thyroid and intestine [20,21]. As a member of the growth arrest-specific gene 3/peripheral myelin protein 22 (GAS3/PMP22) four-transmembrane protein family, EMP2 plays important roles in multiple physiological and biochemical processes [[22], [23], [24]]. It is believed to regulate the trafficking of diverse proteins and glycolipids to their appropriate plasma membrane localizations. Specifically, EMP2 facilitates the transport of several class molecules to specific functional domains on the plasma membrane, including focal adhesion kinase, integrins, major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC class I) molecules, CD54 and glycosylphosphatidylinositol-linked proteins [25,26]. Furthermore, EMP2 is involved in signal transduction of membrane receptors implicated in cell-cell interactions and recognition of the extracellular environment [22,23,26]. Notably, EMP2 promotes tumor growth in vivo and facilitates cell migration and invasion, partially through the upregulation of avβ3 integrin expression and activation of focal adhesion kinase, Src kinase and other signaling pathways [27].

HH2 is a synthetic cationic innate defense regulator (IDR) peptide with potent immunomodulatory properties. While natural IDR peptides exhibit diverse immunomodulatory functions, including chemokine production, immune cell differentiation, and inflammatory regulation-their clinical development faces critical limitations: hemolytic activity, cytotoxicity towards mammalian cells, and unwanted mast cell degranulation. HH2 overcomes these barriers through rational design optimization. Unlike natural IDRs, HH2 demonstrates minimal cytotoxicity while maintaining robust immunomodulatory activity, particularly in suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-10 [[28], [29], [30]]. This synthetic peptide retains beneficial functions including enhanced chemotaxis, dendritic cell and macrophage activation, and selective immune response modulation, while eliminating the adverse effects that plague natural counterparts [[31], [32], [33]]. The superior safety profile and preserved efficacy position HH2 as a promising candidate for clinical immunotherapy applications, extending beyond traditional antibacterial uses to encompass anti-tumor therapeutic strategies. In our previous studies [[34], [35], [36], [37], [38], [39], [40], [41]], we have reported the cholesterol-modified form of the antimicrobial peptide could act not only as a satisfactory nanocarrier but also as an immune-modulator that directly interacts with immune cells. Here, we designed HH2-C as an innovative immunogenic nanocarrier for siEMP2 delivery to regulate the tumor microenvironment. HH2-C (Chol-suc-VQLRIRVAVIRA-NH2) consists of a cationic hydrophilic antimicrobial peptide conjugated with cholesterol at the N-terminus, enabling self-assembly into stable nanoparticles that efficiently load siEMP2 through electrostatic interactions (Scheme 1A). To evaluate therapeutic efficacy, we performed comprehensive assessments spanning from in vitro breast cancer cell line studies to in vivo validation using both zebrafish xenografts and subcutaneous mouse models. In vitro, HH2-siEMP2 nanoparticles demonstrated potent anti-tumor activity, significantly inhibiting cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. These promising results translated effectively to in vivo settings, where treated animals showed substantial tumor volume reduction compared to controls. Materials and methods

Data mining and analyzing

Expression data for EMP2 were downloaded from the TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas) and GEO (Gene Expression Omnibus) databases to retrieve clinical data related to breast cancer. The TCGA database was used to analyze the mRNA expression level of EMP2 in tumor and normal tissues of 18 different tumors. The mRNA expression level of EMP2 in tumor and adjacent normal tissues were further evaluated in 26 types of cancer from TCGA databases. Breast cancer samples were divided into luminal A, luminal B, HER2+ and basal-like subtypes using the PAM50 (Prediction analysis of microarray 50) classifiers (Supervised risk predictor of breast cancer based on intrinsic subtypes), as well as normal, through the Kruskal-Wallis test. Alternatively, they were divided into negative and indeterminate & positive groups based on estrogen (ER) or progesterone (PR) status using Mann-Whitney U tests. The pathway activity between EMP2-high and EMP2-low group were calculated from the Gene Set Cancer Analysis (GSCA, https://guolab.wchscu.cn/GSCA/#/) database. The EMP2 single-cell gene expression level was investigated in the GSE161529 dataset.

Overall survival analysis was performed by the log-rank test based on EMP2 expression. Patients with BRCA were divided into low-expression and high-expression groups based on the median value of EMP2 mRNA expression, where P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Tumor immune microenvironment analysis

Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was implemented to quantify the immune cell infiltration in BRCA samples. Based on the expression level of EMP2, patients were divided into high-expression EMP2 and low-expression EMP2 groups using “GSVA” package in the R programming language (https://www.r-project.org) [42]. Box plots were generated to observe the enrichment scores between EMP2 expression levels and the immune infiltration of various immune cells in the two groups. Furthermore, the correlation between EMP2 expression and immune cells was calculated using the Pearson method. To verify the correlation between immune infiltration and EMP2 expression level, breast cancer samples were selected from the TCGA database and divided into two groups according to the EMP2 expression level. Immune scores, stromal scores, and ESTIMATE scores of EMP2 expression were obtained from ESTIMATE. The abundance of 22 types of immune cell infiltration between EMP2-high and EMP2-low group was calculated using CIBERSORT.

Single cell RNA sequencing analysis

All single-cell RNA-seq analyses were conducted using the integrated and comparative analysis workflows provided by Seurat (v5.0.3). For quality control, we retained cells that expressed more than 200 genes (with genes defined as detected in at least three cells), fewer than 2500 genes, and had mitochondrial gene content below 5 %. Only cells that met these criteria were included in downstream analyses (Fig. S5A–B). The correlation scatter plot showed the scRNA data were qualified (Fig. S5C).

To enable cell clustering, scRNA-seq data of normal breast tissue, triple negative breast cancer (TNBC), ER + breast cancer and HER2+ breast cancer patients from dataset GSE161529 were integrated. Following integration, a shared nearest-neighbor (SNN) graph was constructed, and cell clusters were identified using the Louvain method at a resolution of 0.1. For visualization, unsupervised clustering results were projected using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Differential expression analysis was performed using the FindAllMarkers function, applying Wilcoxon's rank-sum test with default settings for both one-vs-all and pairwise group comparisons. Genes with a false discovery rate (FDR) below 0.05 and absolute log2(fold change) ≥ 0.25 were considered significant. Cell clusters were then manually annotated based on their top five marker genes.

Briefly, 16 sub-populations were reported: (0) Luminal epithelial cells, which highly expressed SCGB2A2, MUCL1, PIP, SCGB1D2, TFF1; (1) T cells, which highly expressed CD3D, CD2, TRBC1, CD7, GZMA; (2) Macrophages, which highly expressed C1QA, C1QC, C1QB, FPR3, OLR1; (3) Cancer-associated fibroblasts, which highly expressed COL1A1, ASPN, COL10A1, COL11A1, WISP2; (4) Basal-like tumor cells (TNBC), which highly expressed KRT6A, LY6D, LEMD1, SPRR2A, PSORS1C2; (5) Inflammatory epithelial cells, which highly expressed PTN, SNORC, SAA2, PIGR, CCL28; (6) Neuroendocrine-like epithelial tumor cells, which highly expressed LINC00052, RNF152, CA2, TRH, C6orf141; (7) Myoepithelial cells, which highly expressed KRT5, ACTG2, LAMA3, TNS4, OXTR; (8) Fibroblasts, which highly expressed MMP3, MMP10, DLK1, MMP12, CXCL6; (9) Plasma cells, which highly expressed IGHG1, IGLC2, IGLC3, IGLV3-1, IGLL5; (10) Endothelial cells, which highly expressed ADGRL4, CLDN5, ACKR1, MYCT1, SELE; (11) Smooth muscle cells, which highly expressed RGS5, MT1A, IL6, MYH11, PPP1R14A; (12) ER+ breast cancer cells, which highly expressed STC2, FASN, TMC5, CLIC6, MAPT; (13) Luminal progenitor-like tumor cells, which highly expressed LINC01238, CST5, KCNJ3, TRPA1, AC123023.1; (14) Mature neuroendocrine tumor cells, which highly expressed CPB1, IGFBP5, GRIA2, ASCL1, ATRNL1; (15) Mast cells, which highly expressed TPSAB1, TPSB2, CPA3, MS4A2, SLC18A2.

Materials

HH2-C were purchased from Ketai Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Specific siRNAs for silencing the EMP2 protein gene were kindly provided by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). The scrambled siRNA, serving as the negative control siRNA (si-NC) and the Cy3-siRNA were also purchased from Shanghai GenePharma. The sequence of siRNA used for our experiments are as follows: siEMP2: sense: 5′-CCUCCAUCAUCC AGCUAAU dTdT-3′, antisense: 5′-AUUAGCUGGAUGAUGGAGG dTdT-3'. si-NC: sense: 5′-UUCUCC GAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′, antisense: 5′-ACGUGACACGUU CGGAGAATT-3'. 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Merck Life Science, Shanghai, China). Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was also bought from Sigma-Aldrich (Merck Life Science, Shanghai, China). Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent kit was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Shanghai, China), and TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit was purchased from Solarbio (Beijing, China).

Preparation of HH2-siRNA nanoparticles

The defense peptide-cholesterol conjugate (HH2-C, 1 mg) was suspended in 10 mL Milli-Q water and stirred overnight to dissolve. For the formulation of HH2-siRNA complexes, the peptide solution was first diluted with RNase-free water to the desired concentration and then, siRNA (20 μM) was added to the peptide solution at room temperature with different N:P ratios (ranging from 1.25 to 160 or 1 to 400). The solution was gently mixed and left at ambient temperature for 20 min. The supernatant was filtered using a 0.22 μm PVDF membrane (Merck Mililipore Ltd., Shanghai, China) and stored at 4 °C for later use.

Characterization of HH2-siRNA NPs

Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) and ζ-Potential The size distribution and zeta potential of HH2 nanoparticles and HH2-siRNA NPs (with determined N/P ratios) were measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS/ZS90 instrument (Malvern Instruments, UK). Samples were freshly prepared by dispersing NPs in Milli-Q water to obtain appropriate viscosities. The data were obtained from three independent repeated measurements.

Transmittance Electron Microscopy (TEM) The Morphology of NPs was characterized using TEM (FEI Company, Helios G4 UX, USA). The NPs were dissolved in Milli-Q water and dropped onto the copper grid. After 3 min, the excess sample solution was tapped off with filter paper, and the grid was air-dried. Each grid was stained with a droplet of 2 % phosphotungstic acid for 2 min, followed by removal of excess and natural drying of the sample.

Agarose gel retardation analysis

For the agarose gel retardation assay, HH2 nanoparticles were mixed with siRNA (20 pmol) at different N/P ratios (molar ratio of HH2-nitrogen atoms to siRNA-phosphate) ranging from 1.25 to 160, and 3.125 to 400. After 20 min of incubation at room temperature, the complexes were mixed with 1 μL of 6 × loading buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and subjected to 1.5 % (w/v) agarose gel (Lonza, Rockland, ME USA) containing SYBR-Safe DNA Gel Stain (Invitrogen, Shanghai, China). Electrophoresis was performed at 120 V for 30 min in TAE buffer (40 mM Tris pH 7.6, 1 % (v/v) acetic acid, 1 mM EDTA). The resulting gel was visualized using a Gel DocTMXR + gel documentation system (Bio-Rad, USA).

Serum stability of siRNA in NPs

HH2-siRNA NPs (with N/P ratios ranging from 12.5 to 50) were mixed with 20 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, China) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After that, excess heparin was added and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Naked siRNA without or with FBS served as the control. All samples were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis for 30 min at 120 V.

Cell culture and transfection

The murine-derived breast cancer cell line 4T1 and human breast cancer cell lines MDA-MB-231(MB231) and BT474 were obtained from our laboratory. 4T1 cells were routinely maintained in 1640 media with 10 % FBS, and 1 % Penicillin/Streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China). MB231 and BT474 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were grown in a humidified 5 % CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C. Cells were seeded at a concentration of 2.5–5 × 104 cells/mL and cultured in complete medium in 6-well plates for 24 h to reach 60 % to 70 % confluence before transfection. For transfection, EMP2 siRNA or negative control siRNA were separately dissolved in RNase-free water. Each RNA solution was then combined with the respective volume of HH2 or Lipo3000 solution, gently mixed, and left at room temperature for 20–30 min to form siRNA complexes. After washing the cells with PBS, 200 μL/well of HH2/HH2-siRNA nanoparticles or siEMP2 Liposome mixture was added, followed by the addition of 800 μL/well serum-free MEM. All cells were incubated with 50 nM siEMP2 and the nanoparticles (N:P = 12.5) or Lipo3000 transfection reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions for 6 h. Subsequently, the media were changed to serum-containing 1640/DMEM. Cells were used for subsequent experiments immediately after transfection.

Cell uptake with flow cytometry and fluorescence microscope

The quantitative evaluation of cellular uptake of siRNA was analyzed by flow cytometry (FACSAria; BD Biosciences). Primary breast cancer cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 500 μL of DMEM medium supplemented with the respective treatments: control, Naked-siRNA-Cy3, HH2-siRNA-Cy3, and Lipo-siRNA-Cy3 transfected groups. After transfection 10 min, 0.5 h, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 6 h, the cells were trypsinized, harvested, rinsed three times with PBS, fixed with 4 % ice-cold paraformaldehyde, and resuspended. The mixture was then incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. A total of 20000 cells per sample were counted, and the experiment was performed in triplicate. Fluorescence intensity was detected, and gates for viable cells were established by side-scatter versus forward-scatter. Images analysis was carried out using FlowJo software.

All breast cancer cells were seeded in confocal dish at 70–90 % confluence and transfected as described above. After 6 h of transfection, the cells were washed 2–3 times with pro-cooled PBS, followed by incubation with DAPI (5 μg/mL, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) at room temperature in the dark for 10–30 min. The staining solution was then removed, and the cells were washed 2–3 times with pro-cooled PBS again. Finally, all the cells were subjected to confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Cells with different treatments, including control, HH2, HH2-siNC, HH2-siEMP2, naked-siEMP2, and Lipo-siEMP2, were the same as those used for Western blot experiments. Total RNAs was extracted from the samples using the TRIzol reagent (Ambion, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China), The concentration and purity of total RNA were assessed using a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China). 2 μg of total RNA was reverse transcribed to synthesize cDNA using PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Beijing, China) with oligo (dT) and random primers according to the manufacturer's protocol. The primer sequences used in this study were as follows: EMP2: (forward primer: 5′- GTGCCCGGTTTGGATTTGTC-3′, reverse primer: 5′-CGTGGACACGATGTGGAAGA-3′) and GAPDH: (forward primer: 5′-AGACAGCCGCATCTTCTTGT-3′ reverse primer: 5′- AATCCGTTCACACCGACCTT-3′). The primers were designed using NCBI and synthesized by TSINGKE Biological Technology (Sichuan, China). Subsequently, real-time PCR was performed using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Tli RNaseH Plus) (TaKaRa, Beijing, China) reagent in a Bio-Rad instrument (Shanghai, China). The Q-PCR profile included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 35 cycles at 95 °C for 5 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 95 °C for 10 s. A melt curve from 65 °C to 95 °C, with an increment of 0.5 °C for 5 s was also included. The relative level of mRNA expression was calculated using the cycle threshold values (Ct) of each PCR product and normalized with GAPDH as the reference gene using the comparative 2−ΔΔCt method. All results were performed in duplicate for each sample.

Western blotting

For western blot analysis, cells were treated as previously described and then scraped and washed with ice-cold PBS. Subsequently, cells were lysed on ice using RIPA buffer (MilLipore Corp., Bedford, MA, USA) supplemented with 1 mM PMSF (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) to obtain whole-cell extracts. The lysates were centrifuged at 10,000 g for 30 min at 4 °C to remove insoluble materials. Tthe protein content was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China), and the samples were denatured with 1 × SDS-PAGE protein loading buffer by boiling for 5 min. Equal amounts of total protein from each treatment group were separated on a 12.5 % sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). The proteins were then electro-transferred onto PVDF membranes (MilLipore Corp., Bedford, MA, USA). After blocking with 5 % skim milk for 1–2 h, the membranes were immunoblotted overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including rabbit anti-EMP2 (1:500, Abcam, USA) and mouse anti-β-actin (1:10000, Proteintech Group, Wuhan, China). The next day, the primary antibodies were washed 3 times with TBST, and the membranes were incubated with goat anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG-HRP secondary antibodies (1:10000, Proteintech Group, Wuhan, China) at room temperature for 1 h, followed by washing with TBST. The immunoreactive target proteins were visualized using Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (MilLipore, Corp., Bedford, MA, USA) and detected using a Chemi-Doc System (Clinx GenoSens, Shanghai, China).

Cell proliferation assay

The effect of HH2 and HH2-siEMP2 on cell viability was analyzed using the MTT assay. Breast cancer cell lines were trypsinized and seeded into flat-bottom 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 to 5 × 103 cells per well, with three replicates per condition. After overnight incubation, the attached cells were treated with various concentrations (1.563, 3.125, 6.25, 12.5, 25, and 50 μg/mL) of HH2-siEMP2. Following an additional incubation for 24 h, 48 h,72 h, 200 μL of fresh medium containing 0.5 mg/mL MTT was added to each well and incubated for an additional 3–4 h at 37 °C. The medium was then aspirated, and 150 μL of DMSO was added to solubilize the formazan crystals. The absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 562 nm using Spectramax® Absorbance Reader CMax Plus.

Wound healing migration assay

For the wound healing migration assay, cells were seeded at a density of approximately 5 × 105 cells/well into a 6-well culture dish until reaching 80 % confluent. The cell monolayers were carefully scratched using a 200-μL sterile plastic pipette tip to create a wound approximately 1 mm wide. The cellular debris was then washed with PBS, and the cells were incubated in FBS-free medium containing HH2, HH2-siNC, HH2-siEMP2, scrambled siEMP2 (mock group), and Lipo-transfected siEMP2 (positive control). The cells were further cultured for 72 h at 37 °C, and the migrated cells were fixed with pre-cooled 4 % paraformaldehyde and washed three times with PBS. Images were captured at 0 and 72 h under the microscope (Olympus, IX73, Tokyo, Japan) to monitor cell migration at the wound edge. Cell imaging was performed by the Image J software.

Invasion assay

Cell invasion ability was carried out using 24-well culture plate and transwell membrane filter inserts, following the manufacturer's protocol (MilLipore Corp., Bedford, MA, USA). The upper surface of the membrane was coated with 100 μL (15 %, v/v) of Matrigel solution (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). All cells were trypsinized, resuspended with 100 μL of serum-free medium containing the respective drugs, and seeded at a density of 2.5 × 105 cells/well onto the upper compartment of the transwell chamber. The bottom chambers were filled with 700 μL of medium containing 10 % FBS. After 72 h of incubation at 37 °C, non-invading cells on the top surface of the membrane were gently removed using cotton swabs. Invaded cells, which migrated to the bottom face of the membrane, were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with crystal violet. The number of invasive cells was counted in ten randomly selected fields per membrane ( × 100 magnification) using a microscope (Olympus, BX53, Tokyo, Japan) for each assay. Three independent assays were performed to ensure the reliability of the results.

3D cell culture

Tumor spheroids were generated in 96-well round-bottomed plates (Corning, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) coated with 0.5 % sterile agar (Sigma-Aldrich, Shanghai, China) by seeding 5000 cells/well. The cells were suspended in a medium containing 2.5 % (v/v) Matrigel gel. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 72 h to allow spheroid formation. Following this, drugs were added to the culture medium and further incubated for an additional 72 h. Subsequently, the spheroids were photographed using a light microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Zebrafish toxicity tests and tumor xenotransplant model

Zebrafish (Danio rerio) used in this study were fed at State Key Laboratory of Biotherapy, Sichuan University. All experimental protocols were approved by and conducted in accordance with the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments, following the recommendations outlined in the Regulation for the Management of Sichuan University.

Adult wild-type zebrafish (AB strain) and the transgenic (fli: EGFP) zebrafish lines were obtained from the China Zebrafish Resource Center (CZRC). The zebrafish were maintained at a temperature of 28 °C with a 14-h light and 10-h dark cycle. They were housed in fish tanks with recycle water supplemented with 10 % deionized water (pH 7.0) and were fed live brine shrimp twice a day. Zebrafish embryos were obtained from individual adult fish through pairwise breeding, as previously described [43]. For zebrafish embryos of the Tg (fli: EGFP) strain, HH2/HH2-siEMP2 NPs were incubated with the embryos for 72 h in 96-well plates to evaluate potential toxic effects. At approximately 6 h post-fertilization (hpf), HH2 or HH2-siEMP2 NPs were added to duplicate plates at various concentrations ranging from 15.625 μg/mL to 250 μg/mL, with each concentration tested on ten embryos in 200 μL medium. The test solution was replaced daily. Throughout the 3-day observation period, mortality, motor activity, heart activity, teratogenesis, and tail detachment from each larva were monitored. Untreated controls were incubated in reconstituted water and consistently showed no teratogenic effects or lethality associated with the 72-h incubation period. Morphological evaluations of the zebrafish embryos were performed at 72 hpf using a Nikon SMZ800 stereoscopic microscope.

For the tumor xenotransplant model, Zebrafish larvae at 48 hpf were anesthetized with 40 μg/mL tricaine (3-aminobenzoic acid, Sigma-Aldrich, Shanghai, China). The anesthetized embryos were immobilized in a 1 % agarose gel (Solarbio life of Science, Beijing, China) for microinjection. Prior to injection, MB-231 cells were labeled with mCherry in vitro and suspended in serum-free DMEM at a concentration of approximately 400 cells in 10 nL. The tumor cells were loaded into the borosilicate glass capillaries needles (1.0 mm in diameter, JiTian Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Beijing, China) and injected into the avascular region of the yolk sac of each embryo using an automated microinjector (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). After injection, the larvae were immediately transferred to water for recovery during 1 h. Subsequently, drugs were diluted in system water and added to the plates at 12 h post-injection (hpi). The injected embryos were kept at 33 °C and examined daily to monitor tumor growth and invasion using a fluorescent microscope. After 72 hpi, the larvae were imaged, and cancer progression, including tumor size and cell migration, was analyzed.

Subcutaneous transplanted tumor and treatment in mice

Female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks age, 18–20 g average body weight) were purchased from Sichuan University Experimental & Research Animal Institute (Sichuan China). The mice were housed at the Animal Center of the State Key Laboratory of Biotherapy, Sichuan University, and acclimated for one week under controlled temperature and aseptic conditions, with a 12-h light/dark cycle. They were provided with food and water ad libitum. The animal experimentation protocols were conducted in accordance with the principles outlined by the Ethics Review Committee for Animal Experimentation of Sichuan University. For the subcutaneous tumor growth study, a total of 1 × 106/100 μL 4T1 cells in serum-free DMEM were subcutaneously inoculated into the left flanks of the mice. Once the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3 (calculated using the formula: V = 0.5 × length × width2), the animals were randomly assigned to one of four treatment groups (n = 5/group) as follows: i) control group (saline solution); ii) HH2; iii) HH2-siNC NPs; iv) HH2-siEMP2 NPs. The drugs were administered via tail intravenous injection, with 10 nmol/100 μL HH2-siNC/siEMP2 NPs (0.5 nmol siRNAs) or saline, for a total of 5 doses administered at 2-day intervals. The tumor size was measured using a caliper, and the mice's weights were recorded every 2 days. One the day following the final administration, the mice were anesthetized and sacrificed. The tumors were then excised, photographed, weighted, and processed for immunohistochemistry (IHC) or immunofluorescence (IF) analysis. Formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tumor specimens were sectioned at approximately 4 μm, mounted onto glass slides, and then subjected to hematoxylin & eosin (H&E), conventional IHC, and multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) staining according to established protocols.

Histopathologic staining

The tumors of mice were staining with IHC or IF. Tumor sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: Ki-67 (IHC, Abcam, 1:1000), CD31 (IF, 1:500), CD8 (IHC, ab217344, 1:500), CD49b (IHC, ab181548, 1:500), FoxP3 (IHC, ab215206, 1:500), CD4(IF, ab288724, 1:50), CD94(IF, ab217362, 1:200), CD11b (IF, ab8878, 1:50), Gr-1(IF, ab25377, 1:100). Subsequently, the sections were washed with PBS 2–3 times and incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody for 1 h at 37 °C. After another round of washing, the specimen slides were stained with DAPI (Biofroxx, Germany) or underwent 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DBA, Sigma-Aldrich, Shanghai, China) staining for 10 min, followed by counterstained with hematoxylin (Biosharp, China) for 30 s. Slides were then rinsed with water.

mIHC

mIHC allows for the simultaneous detection of six or more biomarkers within FFPE tissue samples, making it a valuable investigative tool for immuno-oncology research. This methodology significantly enhances our understanding of complex biological systems, particularly in the context of tumor microenvironments. In brief, FFPE tissue sections were initially subjected to thermal treatment at 60 °C for 4–6 h. Subsequently, sections underwent deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a series of graded ethanol solutions, and rinsed with deionized water. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocking using H2O2 solution, followed by heat-induced antigen retrieval using a microwave oven. The slides were then sequentially incubated with primary antibodies against Foxp3 (HA722835), PD-1 (ab214421), CD68 (ab283654), and α-SMA (ET1607-53) at optimized dilutions. Following primary antibody application, sections were incubated with polymeric HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Signal development was achieved using Opal fluorophore-conjugated tyramide signal amplification (TSA) at 1:100 dilution (Akoya Biosciences). This sequential staining process was repeated for all six markers. Finally, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI at 1:10 dilution before washing, mounting, and multispectral imaging. For statistical robustness, each experimental group contained four biological replicate tissue sections, with five regions of interest (ROIs) demarcated per section for subsequent statistical analysis.

Mice T lymphocytes analysis by flow cytometry

Results and discussion

EMP2 is dysregulated in human breast cancers

To compare EMP2 expression between pan-cancer and normal tissues, we analyzed the TCGA dataset across multiple cancer types (Fig. 1A, Table S1). Our analysis revealed distinct expression patterns: EMP2 was significantly upregulated in breast (BRCA), cervical (CESC), esophageal (ESCA), kidney clear cell (KIRC), thyroid (THCA), and pancreatic (PAAD) carcinomas. In contrast, EMP2 showed consistent downregulation in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), colorectal adenocarcinoma (COADREAD), kidney papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), and kidney chromophobe (KICH). Given the prominent upregulation in breast cancer, we further investigated EMP2 expression across different breast cancer subtypes. When stratified by hormone receptor status (Fig. 1B–C), EMP2 levels were notably higher in both ER-positive and PR-positive tumors compared to their negative counterparts. Analysis of molecular subtypes revealed more complex patterns (Fig. 1D): EMP2 expression progressively decreased from Luminal B (highest) through Luminal A and HER2+ to Basal-like subtypes (lowest), with each comparison showing statistical significance. These subtype-specific expression patterns suggest that EMP2 may play distinct roles in different breast cancer contexts. Overexpressed EMP2 related to infiltrating immune cells in both bulk and single cell RNA-sequencings

To elucidate the clinical significance of EMP2 expression in breast cancer, we investigated its impact on the immune microenvironment and patient outcomes. Analysis of TCGA-BRCA cohort revealed that EMP2 expression levels significantly influenced immune infiltration patterns (Fig. 1E). Patients with low EMP2 expression exhibited higher ImmuneScore and ESTIMATEScore compared to the high-expression group, suggesting enhanced immune activity. This differential immune landscape was reflected in specific cellular compositions (Fig. 1F): the EMP2-high group showed significant depletion of anti-tumor immune cells, including memory B cells, CD8 T cells, follicular helper T cells, and M1 macrophages, while displaying enrichment of immunosuppressive M2 macrophages and resting mast cells. Notably, regulatory T cells and neutrophils were also reduced in the EMP2-high group, indicating complex immune remodeling. These immunosuppressive features translated into clinical outcomes, as Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated that patients with high EMP2 expression experienced significantly worse overall survival (HR = 1.45, P = 0.03) compared to those with low expression (Fig. 1G). Collectively, these findings establish EMP2 as a key modulator of immune evasion and a potential prognostic biomarker in breast cancer.

To investigate EMP2 expression patterns at single-cell resolution, we performed comprehensive scRNA-seq analysis on breast cancer samples. Following principal component analysis (Fig. S5D), we identified 16 distinct cell clusters using UMAP visualization at a resolution of 0.1 (Fig. 1H). Cell type annotation was performed based on established marker genes (Fig. S5E–F), revealing diverse cellular populations within the tumor microenvironment. Analysis of EMP2 expression across these cell populations uncovered notable heterogeneity (Fig. 1I). Specifically, EMP2 showed elevated expression in malignant epithelial cells compared to other cell types, with particularly pronounced upregulation observed in both TNBC and ER-positive breast cancer samples. Intriguingly, while both subtypes exhibited high EMP2 levels, the expression patterns differed across cellular compartments, suggesting subtype-specific regulatory mechanisms. These single-cell findings provide granular insights into EMP2's cell-type-specific expression and further underscore its potential as a therapeutic target across different breast cancer subtypes.

Synthesis and characterization of HH2-siRNA NPs

In this study, we developed a straightforward approach for preparing HH2-siRNA nanoparticles (NPs), as illustrated in Scheme 1A. The synthesis began with the cationic defense regulator peptide HH2, prepared using solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) methodology [44]. Cholesterol conjugation to the N-terminus yielded the amphiphilic HH2-cholesterol (HH2-C), which spontaneously self-assembled into nanoparticles capable of encapsulating siRNA through electrostatic interactions within the core, rather than through surface adsorption alone [45]. The physicochemical properties of the resulting nanoparticles were systematically characterized. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis at an N/P ratio of 12.5 revealed a marked reduction in hydrodynamic diameter from 304.2 ± 107.0 nm for empty HH2 NPs to 88.56 ± 20.59 nm upon siRNA incorporation (Fig. 2A). This substantial size reduction indicates efficient siRNA condensation driven by electrostatic interactions. Zeta potential measurements showed a decrease from 25.7 ± 5.31 mV to 5.83 ± 4.20 mV following siRNA loading (Fig. 2A), suggesting partial charge neutralization while maintaining a net positive surface charge essential for cellular uptake. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed the formation of well-dispersed, spherical nanoparticles with smooth surface morphology, corroborating the size reduction observed by DLS (Fig. 2B). To validate the siRNA binding capacity, gel retardation assays were performed across N/P ratios ranging from 0 to 400. Complete siRNA complexation, evidenced by the absence of siRNA migration on 1.5 % agarose gels, was achieved at N/P ratios ≥12.5 (Fig. 2C and S6). Release and stability analysis of HH2-siRNA NPs

The controlled release and stability of siRNA are critical determinants of nanoparticle performance. We first evaluated siRNA release profiles using heparin competition assays. Heparin, a polyanionic biomolecule, effectively displaces siRNA from cationic complexes through competitive binding [46,47]. Agarose gel electrophoresis revealed complete siRNA release from HH2-siRNA NPs across all tested N/P ratios in the presence of excess heparin (Fig. S7Fig. S8), demonstrating the reversible nature of electrostatic complexation essential for intracellular siRNA delivery. Building on these release characteristics, we assessed the protective capacity of HH2-siRNA NPs against serum nucleases-a prerequisite for successful in vivo applications [48]. Naked siRNA underwent near-complete degradation within 24 h in 20 % FBS, while siRNA encapsulated in HH2 NPs (N/P ratios: 12.5–50) remained intact, exhibiting migration patterns comparable to untreated controls (Fig. 2D). This robust serum stability demonstrates that HH2 nanoparticles effectively shield siRNA from enzymatic degradation, thereby extending circulation time and preserving therapeutic potential for in vivo transfection.

Hemolysis assay of HH2-siRNA NPs

Biocompatibility assessment is crucial for evaluating nanoparticle-blood interactions and clinical translation potential [49]. We conducted hemolysis assays to determine the hemocompatibility of HH2 and HH2-siRNA nanoparticles. Red blood cells (RBCs) were incubated with nanoparticle formulations (1.625–50 μM) for 2 h at 37 °C, using 1 % Triton X-100 and PBS as positive and negative controls, respectively. The hemolysis profiles revealed concentration-dependent effects for both formulations (Fig. S9A–B). HH2 nanoparticles induced substantial hemolysis at high concentrations, reaching approximately 70 % at 50 μM, but demonstrated acceptable biocompatibility at lower concentrations, with only 1.98 % hemolysis at 1.625 μM. Notably, siRNA incorporation significantly enhanced hemocompatibility: HH2-siRNA nanoparticles exhibited a maximum hemolysis of only 6.13 % at 50 μM and less than 1 % at concentrations ≤25 μM. Given that materials causing <5 % hemolysis are considered hemocompatible for biomedical applications [50], HH2-siRNA nanoparticles can be safely administered at concentrations up to 25 μM without hemolytic concerns.

HH2-siRNA NPs suppressed EMP2 expression levels in breast cancer cells

To comprehensively evaluate the effect of HH2-siEMP2 NPs on EMP2 expression, we examined both mRNA and protein levels using qRT-PCR and western blotting. Three breast cancer cell lines (4T1, MB231, and BT474) were treated with HH2, HH2-siNC, HH2-siEMP2, naked-siEMP2, or Lipo-siEMP2 for 24 and 48 h. At the mRNA level, qRT-PCR analysis revealed a consistent pattern across all three cell lines (Fig. 2E). HH2-siEMP2 treatment resulted in significant EMP2 mRNA reduction in 4T1, MB231, and BT474 cells (∗∗P < 0.01), with Lipo-siEMP2 showing similar albeit less pronounced effects (∗P < 0.05). In contrast, cells treated with HH2, HH2-siNC, or naked-siEMP2 showed no appreciable changes in EMP2 mRNA expression compared to controls, demonstrating the specificity of the silencing effect. This consistent suppression pattern was mirrored at the protein level. Western blot analysis (Fig. 2F) confirmed that HH2-siEMP2 and Lipo-siEMP2 effectively diminished EMP2 protein expression across all three cell lines, while HH2, HH2-siNC, and naked-siEMP2 treatments left EMP2 protein levels unchanged. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that HH2-siEMP2 consistently downregulates EMP2 expression at both transcriptional and translational levels across multiple breast cancer cell lines. The data underscore the essential role of the HH2 carrier in facilitating efficient intracellular delivery of siEMP2 for functional gene silencing.

Anti-cancer capacities of HH2-siEMP2 NPs in subcutaneous mouse tumor models

To assess the safety and therapeutic potential of HH2 and HH2-siEMP2 nanoparticles (NPs), we conducted in vivo studies using murine tumor models. Initially, healthy mice received tail vein injections of HH2 and HH2-siEMP2 NP formulations. Throughout the experimental period, all animals survived without significant weight changes among treatment groups (Fig. S16). Histopathological examination using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed normal tissue architecture in all major organs, with nuclei stained blue and extracellular matrix/cytoplasm stained red. No pathological alterations were detected in heart, liver, spleen, lung, or kidney tissues (Fig. S17). Subsequently, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of HH2-siEMP2 NPs using a 4T1 breast cancer xenograft model in BALB/c mice (n = 5 mice per group). When tumor volumes reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were randomly allocated to four treatment groups: control (saline), HH2-siNC NPs, HH2, and HH2-siEMP2 NPs, all administered via tail vein injection (The dose volumen, and treatment schedule are provided in Method 2.18.).

As shown in Fig. 5D, both HH2 and HH2-siEMP2 NPs significantly inhibited tumor growth compared to control groups. By day 11 post-treatment initiation, the HH2-siEMP2 NPs group demonstrated significant tumor suppression relative to control mice (P < 0.05; Fig. 5E) (consider providing exact P-value if available). Final tumor weights were 0.793 ± 0.379 g for HH2-siEMP2 NPs and 0.886 ± 0.477 g for HH2, compared to 1.431 ± 0.387 g for control and 1.338 ± 0.602 g for HH2-siNC NPs groups. The HH2-siEMP2 NPs treatment resulted in a 44.6 % reduction in tumor weight compared to the control group (calculated as: (1.431–0.793)/1.431 × 100 %; P < 0.05) (Fig. 5F-H) (consider specifying the statistical test used, e.g., one-way ANOVA with post-hoc analysis). Notably, no adverse effects were observed throughout the treatment period, confirming the safety profile of the formulations [55,56]. To elucidate the mechanisms underlying the anti-tumor effects of HH2-siEMP2 NPs, we performed immunohistochemical analysis of tumor sections. Tissues were stained for Ki-67 (proliferation marker), TUNEL (apoptosis marker), CD31 (angiogenesis marker), and DAPI (nuclear stain) (Fig. 5I and J). Quantitative analysis revealed significantly decreased Ki-67-positive cells in tumors treated with HH2 (63.25%), HH2-siNC (44.25%), and HH2-siEMP2 NPs (12.12%) compared to control (92.07%) (% positive cells). TUNEL-positive signals (green fluorescence) were increased in all treatment groups, with the most pronounced effect observed in the HH2-siEMP2 group, indicating enhanced apoptosis. CD31 immunostaining (red fluorescence) demonstrated marked reduction in tumor vasculature following HH2 and HH2-siEMP2 treatment, suggesting anti-angiogenic effects ( CD31-positive area percentage, Con: 30.50%, HH2: 8.1%, HH2-siNC: 12.71%, HH2-siEMP2: 1.93%). These results collectively demonstrate that both HH2 and HH2-siEMP2 effectively inhibit tumor proliferation, suppress angiogenesis, and induce apoptosis in vivo, with HH2-siEMP2 showing superior efficacy.

Regulation mechanism of HH2-siEMP2 NPs on tumor microenvironment

To investigate the impact of HH2/HH2-siEMP2 NPs on tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and the tumor microenvironment (TME) in breast cancer, we performed comprehensive immunological analyses. We first examined key immune cell populations within tumors using immunohistochemical staining for CD8 (cytotoxic T cells), CD49b (NK cells), and FoxP3 (regulatory T cells). CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) directly kill tumor cells through MHC class I-restricted mechanisms, while CD49b + NK cells enhance antitumor immunity through cytotoxic activity. In contrast, FoxP3+ Tregs suppress antitumor immune responses within the TME. As shown in Fig. 6A, treatment with HH2 and HH2-siEMP2 NPs resulted in substantial infiltration of CD8+ T cells and CD49b + NK cells into tumors. Importantly, we observed a marked decrease in immunosuppressive FoxP3+ Tregs in treated tumors. These findings indicate that HH2 and HH2-siEMP2 NPs effectively promote antitumor immunity while reducing immunosuppressive cell populations [57,58]. We next evaluated Th1 helper cells (CD4+CD94+) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs; CD11b + Gr-1+) within the TME. Th1 cells mediate antitumor immunity through IFN-γ production, whereas MDSCs suppress T cell function and promote tumor progression. Given the known ability of immunomodulatory peptides to activate dendritic cells and subsequently influence T cell differentiation [72], we examined the Th1/Th2/Th17/Treg axis using flow cytometry (Fig. S18). Treatment with HH2 significantly increased Th1 and Th17 cell proportions while decreasing Th2 cells compared to controls (P < 0.01; Fig. 8A and B). Most strikingly, Treg frequency was dramatically reduced in HH2-treated groups (P < 0.001; Fig. 8C and 8D, and Fig. 19). This shift from a Th2/Treg-dominated immunosuppressive environment to a Th1/Th17-mediated antitumor response represents a key mechanism of HH2 action. The increased Th1 response enhances tumor immune surveillance through IFN-γ production, while reduced Th2 and Treg populations limit immune suppression and tumor escape [[73], [74], [75], [76]]. Finally, we characterized T cell memory populations based on CD45RA and CD62L expression patterns (Fig. S20). In our study, we observed an increase in IL-17A levels following HH2 treatment, although the detailed underlying mechanism requires further studied. Treg cells have been reported to suppress tumor antigen-specific T cell responses within the TME [[81], [82], [83], [84], [85]]. Host defense peptides (HDPs), also known as antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), are small, biologically active molecules widely distributed across diverse organisms. These peptides exhibit a broad spectrum of activities, including antimicrobial, antitumor, antiviral, and immunomodulatory functions. Compared to conventional small-molecule drugs and biologic agents, HDPs typically demonstrate lower toxicity, enhanced penetration into tumor tissues, and more efficient target binding, while carrying a reduced risk of inducing allergic reactions or immunosuppression [86]. These properties position HDPs as highly promising therapeutic candidates in cancer treatment, particularly in the context of tumor immunotherapy, as they can recruit and activate immune cells, modulate immune responses, and enhance tumor cell immunogenicity [87,88]. HDPs are renowned for their multifaceted anticancer effects, which include inhibiting tumor cell proliferation, reducing invasiveness, inducing apoptosis, and disrupting tumor cell membranes [81]. These antitumor mechanisms are generally classified as either membrane-disruptive or non-membranolytic. The membrane-disruptive effects capitalize on the amphiphilic nature of HDPs, which are often cationic and amphipathic. Through electrostatic interactions with negatively charged phosphatidylserine and sulfated glycosaminoglycans on cancer cell membranes, HDPs selectively bind and destabilize these membranes, ultimately inducing cell death. The increased membrane fluidity of cancer cells further facilitates HDP internalization, triggering downstream apoptotic pathways and inhibiting autophagy. Additionally, HDPs can target intracellular processes by disrupting the cytoskeleton, inhibiting DNA and protein synthesis, reducing enzyme activity, and inducing cell death via apoptosis or necrosis [89,90]. Within the tumor microenvironment (TME), HDPs exert not only direct cytotoxicity against tumor cells but also serve as immune modulators. They attract and activate immune cells, stimulate the release of chemokines and cytokines, and bridge innate and adaptive immunity, thereby enhancing the antitumor immune response. This immunomodulatory capacity highlights the potential of HDPs as adjuncts in tumor immunotherapy [91].

A pivotal aspect of immune regulation in the TME involves specific molecular targets such as epithelial membrane protein 2 (EMP2). EMP2 is known to promote tumor metastasis and is overexpressed in aggressive forms of breast cancer. It is implicated in oncogenic processes including tumor-driven angiogenesis and the enrichment of cancer stem-like cells [92,93]. Our data indicate that EMP2 expression is markedly elevated in breast cancer (BRCA), with higher levels correlating with poorer patient survival. EMP2 is also associated with drug resistance, particularly in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), underscoring its potential as a novel therapeutic target. Although EMP2's functions in the TME and its influence on immune responses in breast cancer remain incompletely understood, our analysis reveals that EMP2 expression positively correlates with mast cells, Th2 cells, immature dendritic cells (iDCs), and Th17 cells, but negatively with Th1 cells, macrophages, and regulatory T cells (Tregs). These findings suggest that EMP2 expressions may reflect the degree of lymphocyte infiltration in breast cancer, providing a rationale for targeting EMP2 in immuno-oncological interventions [94].

Despite their therapeutic promise, individual HDPs are susceptible to degradation by peptidases and encounter substantial biological barriers during delivery to tumor tissues. Accordingly, the self-assembly and functionalization of HDPs into nanocarriers have emerged as effective strategies in tumor nano-immunotherapy [95]. A representative example is HH2, a synthetically engineered HDP comprising 12 arginine-rich amino acids and exhibiting amphiphilic properties. In our study, we developed a cholesterol-conjugated HDP that self-assembles into nanoparticles capable of loading siRNA to suppress tumor growth. Intriguingly, siRNA loading resulted in a reduced nanoparticle size, potentially due to the binding of negatively charged siRNA, which decreases electrostatic repulsion within the complex.

Given their high specificity, bioactivity, and low toxicity, HDPs are attractive candidates for cancer therapy, acting on both innate and adaptive immunity. The innate immune response-mediated by neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells (DCs), and natural killer cells (NKs)-responds rapidly to pathogens via pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) [96,97]. In contrast, adaptive immunity, characterized by specificity and memory, involves T cells with cytotoxic capabilities and B cells that produce antibodies and cytokines [98]. Notably, certain T cell-activating peptides can inhibit Tregs and, when combined with adjuvants, serve as antigens in vaccines that enhance T cell-mediated immunity [95,99]. Building on prior research, we established that HH2 exerts immunomodulatory activity and that EMP2 expression is linked to immune cell infiltration in breast cancer. Consequently, we loaded siEMP2 into HH2 nanoparticles to investigate their impact on the TME. Our results demonstrate that peptide-based nanovesicles, effectively promote DC maturation and enhance T cell-mediated cytotoxic responses, leading to robust antitumor immunity [100].

The TME is a complex, dynamic network comprising immune cells, stromal cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), endothelial cells, and extracellular matrix. Immune suppression within the TME is largely mediated by the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and CAFs [[101], [102], [103], [104], [105], [106]]. PD-1, expressed on B cells, T cells, and NK cells, interacts with PD-L1, which is upregulated in tumors and various immune cells, leading to T cell exhaustion and reduced antitumor activity. TAMs, particularly the M2 phenotype, and CAFs contribute to immune evasion, angiogenesis, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance [107]. In our study, multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) revealed that HH2 and HH2-siEMP2 treatments significantly decreased the expression of immunosuppressive markers FoxP3, PD-1, CD68, and α-SMA in tumor tissues, indicating effective modulation of the immunosuppressive TME and activation of antitumor immunity.

The balance between Th1 and Th2 responses is critical for maintaining immune homeostasis and effective tumor surveillance [108]. Th1 cells mediate cellular immunity and antitumor responses, whereas Th2 cells facilitate humoral immunity and may protect tumors from immune rejection. Our findings show that HH2 treatment shifts the Th2-dominant response toward a Th1 phenotype, enhancing antitumor immunity. Th17 cells, which play a dual role in tumor progression and immune regulation, were also increased, while Treg cells were significantly reduced following HH2 treatment, resulting in an elevated Th17/Treg ratio and further strengthening antitumor immunity. After pathogen clearance, memory T cells (central memory T cells, Tcm; effector memory T cells, Tem; and TemRA) maintain immune surveillance [[109], [110], [111], [112], [113], [114]]. Notably, HH2 treatment promoted Tcm expansion over Tem, increasing the Tcm/Tem ratio and enhancing tumor immune memory. Collectively, these results demonstrate that HH2 modulates the TME by reprogramming immune cell populations, breaking immune suppression, and fostering durable antitumor responses [[115], [116], [117], [118], [119]].

Conclusion

In brief, current study elucidates the multi-level therapeutic effects of HH2-siEMP2 nanoparticles (NPs) against breast cancer, progressing from molecular mechanisms to systemic responses. At the molecular level, we identified EMP2 overexpression in breast cancer tissues through TCGA analysis, revealing its association with poor overall and disease-specific survival. The synthesized HH2-siRNA NPs demonstrated optimal physicochemical properties-appropriate size distribution, positive surface charge, and serum stability-enabling effective gene delivery and EMP2 silencing. At the cellular level, HH2-siEMP2 NPs exhibited potent anti-cancer effects by inhibiting proliferation, migration, and invasion of breast cancer cells. These NPs were efficiently internalized across multiple cell types while maintaining excellent biocompatibility. Moving to the tissue level, the treatment disrupted three-dimensional tumor spheroids and significantly suppressed tumor growth in both zebrafish and mouse models without inducing toxicity. Most notably, at the systemic level, HH2 demonstrated remarkable immunomodulatory properties that reshaped the tumor microenvironment. The treatment promoted anti-tumor immunity by increasing Th1 cells while decreasing immunosuppressive Th2 and Treg populations. Additionally, HH2 expanded central memory T cells (Tcm) and modulated IL-17A levels, collectively enhancing immune surveillance and preventing tumor immune escape. These findings reveal how HH2-siEMP2 NPs orchestrate a comprehensive anti-cancer response across multiple biological scales, from gene silencing to immune system activation, establishing a promising foundation for integrated cancer immunotherapy approaches.
