Sing-a-Lung 2.0: protocol for a multicentre randomised controlled trial to investigate the effects and mechanisms of online-delivered singing training versus usual care 6–18 months after surgical resection for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)
Authors: Mette Kaasgaard, Morten Borg, Morten Hostrup, Christian Borbjerg Laursen, Lotte Holm Land, Gitte Alstrup, Anne Orholm Nielsen, Julie Kissow Kristensen, Mikkel Bjørck Naver, Ole Hilberg, Uffe Bodtger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPatients who have undergone curative surgery for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) often experience long-term reduced quality of life (QoL), high symptom burden, risk of physical deconditioning and comorbidity. Current Danish rehabilitation offers are heterogeneous and not specifically tailored to the disease-specific needs and no long-term targeted support is currently available. Previously, singing—delivered as a structured training modality—has conferred both physiological and psychological improvements in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, which may likely be transferable to NSCLC, as the two conditions share overlapping symptoms and characteristics. We aim to explore whether a singing-based intervention improves physical function, QoL and symptom burden 6–18 months postsurgery in NSCLC. Moreover, we aim to explore the underpinning physiological mechanisms of singing.Methods and analysisWe will conduct a multicentre randomised controlled trial, comparing 10 weeks’ online-delivered structured singing training to usual care. Trial outcomes include primary outcome, physical capacity (measure: Six-Minute Walking Test), and secondary outcomes, QoL and symptoms (measures: St. George’s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ); the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Questionnaire (QLQ-C30; QLQ-LC13); Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) and airway physiology (measure: forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC)). Explorative outcomes include aerobic fitness/maximal oxygen uptake (VO2-max); exercise-induced adaptations; respiratory muscle strength and control; inflammatory biomarkers. Analysis: Descriptive statistics, stratified analyses, regression models and association and independence between objective and subjective outcomes.Ethics and disseminationThe trial was approved by the Committee on Health Research Ethics (SJ-1027) and the Danish Act on Processing of Personal Data (p-2024-19634). Results will be reported in peer-reviewed scientific journals.Trial registration numberNCT07460999.Keywords: Lung Physiology, Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer, Patient Outcome Assessment, Surveys and Questionnaires, Respiratory Muscles, Respiratory Function Test, Pulmonary Rehabilitation

Background

In Denmark, lung cancer is the second most common cancer (incidence rate=5000 new cases/year).1 2 At the same time, 5-year survival and the prevalence of long-term survivors is increasing.3–7 The preferred treatment for low-stage (stages I and II) non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is surgical resection, yet stereotactic radiation therapy (localised disease) or concomitant radio-chemotherapy (locally advanced disease) also have curative potential.4 8 The current surveillance procedure after NSCLC surgery consists of follow-up CT scan every 3 months for the first 2 years and every 6 months during the subsequent 3 years, or—for stage I disease—CT scan every 6 months for 2 years and then one annually for 3 years.8–11

Postsurgical rehabilitation is recommended by the European Respiratory Society and the American Thoracic Society to reduce symptoms, increase quality of life (QoL) and to optimise functional capacity.4 6 12 13 Indeed, lung cancer often has long-term reduced QoL compared with age-matched controls,12 13 besides risk of reduced physical activity and deconditioning, symptom clusters (eg, dyspnoea, cough, pain, fatigue, dysphonia), and comorbidities (some, but not all, tobacco-related).4–6 10 14 15 Particularly, there is a frequent overlap between lung cancer and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).4 6 16 For stage I–II lung cancer, the rehabilitation programmes specifically should include physical exercise training6 16–20 either as combined strength and endurance training alone or in combination with respiratory training (grade A-evidence).17 Moreover, the following critical outcomes have recently been defined in a Delphi study on a Core Outcome Set in lung cancer rehabilitation:20 ‘Breathlessness, activities of daily living, physical function, health-related quality of life, emotional and mental well-being and pain’.20

However, the Danish rehabilitation offer after NSCLC surgery is heterogeneous and disease-unspecific and without follow-up to support the long-term impaired physical capacity, QoL or symptom burden.6 14 21–23 Our recent observational study22 on current rehabilitation programmes following NSCLC surgery found that only 25% had received a disease-specific offer, which, nevertheless, was related to improved QoL when compared with non-disease-specific offers.22 Moreover, a large proportion reported disease-specific symptoms such as fatigue (78%), dyspnoea (65%) and pain (47%).22 A subsequent cross-sectional study 6–12 months after NSCLC surgery confirmed impaired QoL and physical functioning in 51% and dyspnoea in 70%.23 These findings are in line with previous Danish studies reporting impaired QoL and persistent symptoms long-term after NSCLC surgery10 15 and with a systematic review reporting impaired QoL in a significant proportion of patients after NSCLC surgery.24

Against this background, there is an identified need to address the unmet needs of the overlooked group of patients with long-term impaired QoL and symptoms long-term after NSCLC surgery. Specifically, physical functioning and QoL are prognostic factors for survival in NSCLC, but are potentially modifiable, and therefore essential to address.25 26 Therefore, in Sing-a-Lung 2.0 we aim to investigate whether structured singing training can be one such relevant activity. Indeed, in recent decades, singing has become increasingly popular for people with respiratory diseases27–30 as an activity comprising both elements of both physiological training and psychosocial aspects.30–35 Overall, previous studies have been small and heterogeneous, but have reported that singing is associated with improved breathing and dyspnoea control30 31 33 35–38 and may confer physiological training effects31 with a workload equivalent to brisk walk pace.31 39 Moreover, singing has been reported to improve QoL28 35 38 40–43 and is proposed to be an enjoyable and motivating supplement or alternative to participation and maintenance of conventional physical exercise training within and after rehabilitation programmes.44

There are, however, different approaches to singing.27 30 45 The currently most well-documented, best-practice approach is Singing for Lung Health (SLH) which has been developed within a multidisciplinary healthcare setting in the UK since 200830 31 as a structured, disease-specific approach. Specifically, SLH aims to target the specific challenges in respiratory diseases through the use of exercises and songs as tools for purpose and, moreover, includes simple movement and dancing while singing.29–31 34 46 In our previous large-scale randomised controlled trial (RCT)—Sing-a-Lung 1.0—we demonstrated that SLH as part of community-based pulmonary rehabilitation for COPD improved physical capacity and QoL in a dose-response manner32 47 and a posthoc analysis suggested that SLH was related to improved respiratory muscle control, dyspnoea control and heart rate response.33 A follow-up study in this cohort48 indicated that singing conferred long-term breathing control and did not limit long-term physical activity,48 in line with initial follow-up studies29 46 48 49 and reports,50 51 describing singing as beneficial and safe activity.

However, further research is warranted to conclude on efficacy, effectiveness, underlying mechanisms, optimal delivery of singing and on the impact of singing on other lung diseases.31 33 Although singing has not yet been investigated in patients after NSCLC surgery, we expect that results from Sing-a-Lung 1.0 are transferable, especially as several epidemiological and common pathogenetic factors are shared between COPD and NSCLC patients52 and as the incidence of lung cancer and COPD is often overlapping.4 6 16

The singing training intervention of Sing-a-Lung 2.0 will be conducted online, as neither a homogeneous disease-specific rehabilitation programme nor a long-term follow-up offer following NSCLC surgery exists, as the number of lung cancer patients in each municipality is limited, and as transportation issues constitute a well-known barrier to attendance and adherence.22 Moreover, previous studies have reported online-delivered singing as feasible and well-accepted.53–57

Aims and hypotheses

In the main study, we aim to investigate whether a 10-week course of online singing training (delivered as SLH) improves key physiological and psychosocial outcomes and reduces symptom burden compared with usual care in patients who display persistent symptoms 6–18 months after curative intended NSCLC surgery. We hypothesise that SLH provides relevant and beneficial improvements in physiological and psychological parameters, relieves symptom burden and is superior to usual care.

Additionally, we aim to investigate the underpinning mechanisms of singing training compared with usual care. Specifically, we aim to explore physiological adaptations and to explore the correlation between objective and subjective improvements. We hypothesise that singing training improves respiratory muscle strength, physical fitness, oxygenation and inflammation and is superior to usual care.

Methods

Trial design

We will conduct an RCT—Sing-a-Lung 2.0—within 10 weeks’ online-delivered singing training or 10 weeks’ usual care. To enhance incentive to attend, the usual care group will be offered 10 weeks’ of singing training after the usual care period.

The trial was registered 04 March 2026 (NCT07460999). Onset for participant recruitment was 20 March 2026, and anticipated enrolment completion is October 2027. The trial protocol adheres to the Standard Protocol Items as described in the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) 2025 statement: updated guideline for protocols of randomised trials58 and the intervention will be described using the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) checklist.59 For trial overview, specifically depicting patient flow, see figure 1.

Randomisation and allocation procedure

After completion of baseline assessment, included study participants will be individually randomised in a 2:1 allocation ratio to either Group 1, receiving 10 weeks’ online singing training (SLH) (90 min, twice per week) or group 2, receiving 10 weeks’ usual care (followed by—to enhance willingness to participate in the trial—10 weeks’ online SLH (90 min, twice per week)). Participants can mark their preferred times for participating in singing training (six optional courses). Subsequently, the randomisation procedure will be performed using REDCap and the participant will be informed about the randomisation by a staff person at each site. The randomisation procedure is entirely computer-generated and with no influence from any research group member or staff person.

When approximately eight participants are collected for a specific course time, the head study nurse (GA) will note the singing teachers who will then send out information to the collected participants with a short presentation of themselves and a link to the first session. Each site will recruit patients ongoingly during the trial period or until the number needed to treat is reached.

Blinding

The nature of the trial precludes blinding of participants or singing teachers. Assessors are blinded at V0 (preintervention) and pseudo-blinded at follow-up visits (V1, V2). We consider that full assessor blinding is unachievable since V2 visits are only performed after a singing intervention. To reduce the impact of incomplete assessor blinding at V1 and V2, we have taken the following actions:

Intervention: singing training (Singing for Lung Health, SLH)

The study intervention is singing training, which will be conducted according to an intervention protocol based on the SLH-approach (see table 2).30–32 34 Sessions will include physical, vocal and breathing exercises and musical repertoire, specifically designed to improve strength, endurance and flexibility of the respiratory muscles. Besides, the intervention will include movement, dancing and artefacts for playful games along with singing.

The singing training will be delivered by three professional singing teachers specifically trained and experienced within the SLH approach. Specifically, the intervention will be delivered via a video conferencing application (Zoom Video Communications, ‘Zoom’) throughout the 10 weeks’ intervention phase (90 min twice a week, ie, a total of 20 sessions), and the participants will, this, attend from their homes. Control: usual care

The control group (group 2) will receive usual care, that is, no specific additional intervention above those which the person usually engages with. Before assessment 2, the participants will provide self-reported information about their activities during the 10 weeks’ usual care.

Given that there is no structured, active ingredient in the usual care component, and thus, no potential carry-over effect, a wash-out period when crossing from usual care to the singing training intervention is not needed.

Ahead of study onset, all sites will be introduced to and agree on conforming to all data collection and assessment procedures to ensure homogeneity across sites.

All PROMs and researcher-developed questionnaires (specially developed for this trial) will be completed online by the participants via REDCap prior to the time for assessment at T0, T1 or T2. In case that a patient has not completed PROMs and questionnaires at the time for assessment, the patient will be given time to complete them immediately ahead of the assessments. Objective data will be collected by experienced study nurses within respiratory medicine at each site. In all patients from site Bispebjerg and in a subgroup from site Zealand University Hospital, both basic assessments and advanced assessments will, however, be performed by experienced research assistants within exercise physiology at August Krogh Section for Molecular and Human Physiology, Department of Nutrition, Exercise and Sports, Faculty of Health and Medical Sciences, University of Copenhagen.

A closed profile in REDCap (data access group) for data entry and data collection will be configured for the assessors at each site. After completion of data collection, each site ensures anonymisation (pseudonymisation) of site-specific data and deletes all patients’ personal information. The head nurse, the principal investigator, or any research group member will not be able to access data directly in REDCap until data is fully anonymised. The anonymised data will then be sent via encrypted email and accessed by the principal investigator for combined analysis, reporting and publication of results.

Reasons for exclusion during the screening and recruitment procedures will be systematically recorded. Moreover, reasons for non-adherence (eg, lack of interest, illness) and non-retention (withdrawal of consent, lost to follow-up) will be systematically recorded.

Outcomes and measures

Primary study outcome is between-group change in physical capacity, assessed with the Six-Minute Walking Test (6MWT), and with supplementary reporting of within-group change. Secondary outcomes include between-group and within-group changes in both objective outcomes and subjective PROMs, for example, QoL symptom burden, symptoms of anxiety and depression, airway physiology, adherence and drop-out rate. Additional outcomes include aerobic fitness/maximal oxygen uptake (VO2-max), exercise-induced adaptations and respiratory muscle strength and control. Analysis

For the main analysis, an a priori statistical power analysis calculation (performed in www.clincalc.com/stats) showed that the needed sample size is n=78 study participants (ie, with a 2:1 allocation ratio: group 1: 52; group 2: 26). Including an expected drop-out rate of 20%, an overall total sample of n=100 study participants is needed (Test H0: n1=n2, where n1 is the mean in population one and n2 is the mean in population 2; power: 80%; β=0.2; two-tailed α=0.05; and effect size: group 1: 15 m±15; group 2: 5 m±15). In the power analysis, we considered data from previous studies on the primary study outcome 6MWT distance (MID: ≥30 m),32 65–67 with the final calculation based on findings from our previous RCT, demonstrating a pre–post difference of mean 13.1 m±36.3 after 10 weeks of singing in pulmonary rehabilitation for COPD.32

For a supplementary, observational study of all observations who received the singing intervention (group 1 (change from V0 to V1), n=52; group 2 (change from V1 to V2), n=26; total n=78) (see online supplemental file 2 for power calculation).

Data will be described with number and percentage (categorical data) and either mean±SD (normally distributed continuous data) or median and range (not normally distributed continuous data). Differences in within-groups and between-groups effects will be investigated with relevant tests for continuous (independent samples, paired-samples, related-samples) and categorical data (χ² or Fisher’s exact test) and differences quantified and reported with p values and 95% CIs.

Group and time (preintervention vs postintervention) are considered fixed factors and a random intercept for subjects for a full factorial design. Key outcomes of the analysis: (1) difference in slopes (ie, difference in effect size) between the groups over time (group by time interaction) and (2) respective effects of SLH and usual care. Moreover, output from primary and secondary outcomes will be stratified in reaching of the Minimal Important Differnce (MID)/Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) or not (reported as number and percentage). Association and independence between objective and subjective outcomes will be explored using Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test for 2×2 tables.

Multivariable mixed-effects regression models for both numerical and categorical outcomes will be included to elucidate any relevant correlations and associations.

The primary analysis will follow the intention-to-treat principle. Missing data will be handled using multiple imputation to maintain statistical power and minimise bias, and sensitivity analyses will be performed using the principles of last observation carried forward or next observation carried backward.68

Explorative subgroup analyses will include baseline factors such as comorbidities, type of surgery, body mass index, lung function, rehabilitation attendance, self-reported daily activity, previous experiences with singing and expectations towards benefits of singing.

Primary analyses will be performed by the principal investigator and advanced analyses of physiological parameters will be performed in collaboration with members of the research group. Statistical analyses will be performed using the most updated version of statistical software STATA (StataCorp LLC, Texas, USA) and R free software environment for statistical computing and graphics (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance will be reported as p<0.05.

Results will be reported according to Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT 2025 statement: updated guideline for reporting randomised trials).69

Patient and public involvement

Patients have implicitly been involved in defining relevant outcomes for our trial via the updated Core Outcomes Set for pulmonary rehabilitation for patients with lung cancer.20 Moreover, for the present trial, four patients have been involved in the development and qualification for the recruitment procedure, providing feedback on the written information materials and the assessment procedure ahead of study onset to ensure semantic understanding and feasibility of data collection. In the development of the intervention programme (content and approach), five patients were involved in testing the programme and providing feedback on comprehension of instructions, exercises and repertoire via the online format. Finally, a qualitative-based documentation—in the form of a visual production—will be made to document the project, intervention, data collection and perceptions of participating.

Possible risks

The research group is not familiar with any side effects related to participation in the intervention. Previous studies have described singing as a safe and well-accepted activity,30 32 35 40 and we expect that the participants will benefit positively from the intervention. Any adverse events that do arise will be addressed in the regular research group meetings, with strategies for the future mitigation of these events strategised in collaboration with the local ethics committee, where necessary.

Contingency strategy and mitigation plan

The research group will have monthly investigator meetings to ensure feasibility and to address challenges that may arise. (1) Low trial inclusion rate (3 months with <5 per month): The study will be expanded to recruit patients over a longer period and inclusion of further sites will be considered. (2) Non-attendance: If a patient misses 2 weeks in a row during the intervention, motivational strategies will be intensified ad hoc, depending on reason. (3) High dropout rate after the inclusion: If dropout of the first 10 patients exceeds 30%, reasons for dropout will be analysed and strategies will be settled ad hoc until the needed sample size is reached.

Dissemination

All results, positive, negative or inconclusive, will be reported in accordance with recommendations from the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors and made publicly available in peer-reviewed journals (article 1: Primary outcome and main secondary outcomes; article 2: Subgroup analyses and explorative physiological outcomes). Authorship will be awarded according to Vancouver regulations (‘Uniform Requirements’). Results will, moreover, be shared in social media channels and news media, and at conferences, scientific meetings, seminars, etc.

Expected results and perspectives

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the effects and mechanisms of singing training for NSCLC. Moreover, it is the first study to suggest singing training specifically targeted the overlooked group of patients with persistent symptoms 6–18 months after NSCLC surgery.

Overall, we expect that singing training improves relevant physiological and psychological parameters, relieves symptom burden, and, moreover, may break the vicious circle of physical deconditioning, psychological distress, and social isolation which many people with lung diseases—including patients with lung cancer—experience.

Moreover, the trial will provide useful knowledge for health professionals, facilitators, politicians, health authorities, media and may generate hypotheses and form the basis for further research, including implementation research.
