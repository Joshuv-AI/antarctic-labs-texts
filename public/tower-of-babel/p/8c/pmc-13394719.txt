Experiences of COVID-19 survivors in Gauteng province, South Africa: socioecological implications for South Africa’s preparedness for future pandemics
Authors: Doudou K Nzaumvila, Joel M Francis, Indiran Govender, Olufemi B Omole
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGauteng province was at the epicentre of the COVID-19 pandemic in South Africa. An understanding of the experiences of survivors in this setting has the potential to inform South Africa’s preparedness and response in future pandemics. We aimed to explore the experiences of survivors via a qualitative study conducted in Tshwane and Sedibeng districts, Gauteng. One-on-one interviews were conducted on 20 purposefully sampled survivors and carers. Analysis was guided by the framework methods. An explanatory model was developed using the social ecological model. As a result, eight major themes emerged from the study and included participants’ awareness and diagnosis of their illness, personal attitudes, quarantine and home care, in-hospital care, psychosocial support, illness outcomes, COVID-19 aftermath, and returning to normal life. The study findings revealed experiences that were intense and filled with morbid thoughts, fraught with physical and emotional challenges, highlighted the need for psychosocial support, and exposed the vulnerabilities and inadequacies of the current South African health system. These experiences implicate several individual, family/communal, health systems, and policy/government level gaps that warrant strengthening for better preparedness for future pandemics.Keywords: Covid-19, preparedness, survivors, Gauteng

Introduction

The outbreak of severe acute respiratory syndrome corona virus (SARS-CoV-2) started in December 2019 and originated from Wuhan, China (Chan et al. 2020, World Health Organization (WHO) 2020). Soon it spread rapidly globally and prompted the WHO to declare a global pandemic (WHO 2020). An estimated 5.9 million COVID-19 deaths were reported globally, with excess mortality estimates of up to 18.2 million deaths (Wang et al. 2022). In South Africa, about 91 061 deaths were reported, with excess mortality estimates of two to three times the reported deaths (Bradshaw et al. 2022).

The rapid spread of SARS-CoV-2 was life-threatening and caused psychosocial distress (Chen et al. 2020, Tumbo et al. 2023). With no cure or treatment, public health measures such as social distancing and self/facility quarantine were implemented to limit contact, but both were linked to short- and long-term psychological distress (Ho et al. 2020, Lu 2020). A 2020 rapid review found younger age, no children, low education, mental health disorders, healthcare work, and stressors before/after quarantine were linked to negative psychological effects (Rothan and Byrareddy 2020). These effects persist post-pandemic, impacting health, quality of life and economic productivity of survivors, carers, and families (Nxumalo et al. 2023). We used the term ‘survivor’ to reflect the lived experiences of individuals who recovered from COVID-19 during the early phase of the pandemic, when uncertainty, and/or unavailable or limited treatment options, and absence of vaccines contributed to heightened fear and mortality risk. The term captures both the biomedical and psychosocial dimensions of having endured a potentially life-threatening illness. We have clarified this in the introduction.

Most studies on COVID-19 have focused on the epidemiology, clinical presentations, and outcomes (Chen et al. 2020). Others have explored the psychological effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on healthcare workers (Sharma et al. 2020, Nxumalo et al. 2023) and the end-of-life care of bereaved families and patients (Bronfenbrenner 1993). Yet, these experiences have implications for South Africa’s preparedness for future pandemics, particularly for Gauteng province, which was at the epicentre of the pandemic and where 19 958 deaths and an estimated 58 254 excess mortality from COVID-19 were reported (Bradshaw et al. 2022). Moreover, the experiences of survivors may inform tasks and activities needed at different societal levels for future pandemic preparedness. Such multi-layered segmentation of how people respond to their physical and social environment is encapsulated in the social ecological model (SEM) of human behaviour (Bronfenbrenner 1993). While several studies have examined the clinical consequences of COVID-19, there is a paucity of research investigating the lived experiences of survivors, especially in South African contexts. To our knowledge, no research has examined the experiences of COVID-19 survivors in Gauteng province about the disease, recovery and its extensive psychosocial ramifications. Comprehending these experiences is essential for guiding patient-centred care, post-recovery assistance and future pandemic readiness.

The research question of this study was: what were the lived experiences of individuals who survived the initial waves of COVID-19–19 in Gauteng province, South Africa? Consequently, this study sought to investigate the lived experiences of persons who survived COVID-19 in two health districts in Gauteng province, South Africa, and to discern insights that may guide methods for addressing future public health emergencies.

Methods

Approach

A phenomenological approach was used to explore and understand the lived experiences of COVID-19 survivors in Gauteng province, for socioecological implications for South Africa’s preparedness for future pandemics, and interpretations related to the phenomenon under investigation. The approach allowed the researchers to gain deeper insight into how COVID-19 survivors made meaning of their experiences within their specific context. This choice of approach was motivated by the fact that it is particularly useful, as we aimed to interpret individuals’ experiences of a particular phenomenon as they are lived and perceived by participants themselves (Creswell and Poth 2018). The authors were part of academic teams with qualitative research backgrounds. They were fully aware of their professional engagement with the study context, which provided insight into the phenomenon under investigation, while also requiring ongoing reflexivity throughout the research process. To support reflexive practice, the research team engaged in regular analytic discussions and maintained reflexive notes during data collection and analysis. This process enabled the researchers to remain aware of how their perspectives, assumptions and experiences could influence data interpretation, while ensuring that the findings remained grounded in participants’ narratives and experiences.

This qualitative study utilized one-on-one, in-depth interviews to investigate the lived experiences of COVID-19 survivors.

Setting

The research was executed in the Tshwane and Sedibeng health districts of Gauteng province, South Africa. The study cohort comprised all people who had survived COVID-19 infection and were recorded in the National Institute for Communicable Diseases (NICD) database within the two districts. Researchers employed purposive sampling to telephonically reach out to adult COVID-19 survivors aged 18 and above who consented to participate. A total of 20 participants were consecutively included in the study, comprising 9 from Tshwane and 11 from Sedibeng Districts. These districts are two of the five health districts in Gauteng, and typify the urban, peri-urban and remote populations of the province. Gauteng province was at the epicentre of the COVID-19 pandemic in South Africa with ∼34% of confirmed cases (National Institute for Communicable Disease 2020). Tshwane health district is located in the northernmost part of Gauteng province and has a land area of 570 380 km2 and a population of 3 606 241. Healthcare services are delivered by one academic, one regional and five district hospitals, as well as eight community health centres with 68 fixed clinics service units (National Institute for Communicable Disease 2020, Sundararaman et al. 2021). Similarly, Sedibeng health district is located in the southernmost part of the Gauteng province and covers a land area of 4173 km2 and a population of 1 002 082. Healthcare services are delivered by 1 regional and 2 district hospitals, 4 community health centres, and 34 fixed clinics (Sundararaman et al. 2021).

Data collection

Recruitment was conducted using the existing register. Potential participants were contacted telephonically, informed about the objectives of the study and invited to participate. An experienced qualitative researcher who is a retired psychologist and fluent in English and other local languages conducted one-on-one interviews, with an interview guide (Annexure 1). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation in the study. After the objectives and purpose of the study had been explained, potential participants were invited to take part in the study. Those who agreed to participate were scheduled for an interview. On the day of the interview, participants provided written informed consent before the commencement of data collection.

The interview began with an exploratory statement: Tell me in as much as detail as possible about your experience with COVID-19. Interviews were audiotaped and lasted between 45 and 60 minutes. Filed notes were also kept. The sample size was determined by the concept of information power (Malterud et al. 2016). Twenty participants were interviewed, and the data collected provided sufficient depth and relevance to address the research questions and develop a comprehensive understanding of the lived experiences of COVID-19 survivors. Data sufficiency was deemed attained after 20 interviews, at which time the researchers agreed that the data were sufficient to answer the primary research question. Participants were debriefed after each interview. Light refreshments were served and each participant received R100 (US$5.5) reimbursement for their transport fare.

Analysis

Data analysis followed the framework analysis that involved several stages, including familiarization with the data, development of an analytical framework, indexing of transcripts, charting data into a matrix and interpretation of themes (Goldsmith 2021). Recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim before the analysis and included translation of vernacular terms into English by a professional linguist. Post-transcription, the 20 transcripts were read independently by D.K.N. and O.B.O. to familiarize themselves with the data. Initial data patterns and codes were identified manually. These were discussed between the two researchers, and informed a framework developed to guide further analysis. The framework was to focus on ‘Participants and carers’ experience of the onset of the symptoms of COVID and diagnosis’; ‘The experience of care’; ‘Perceptions of psychosocial support’; ‘The illness outcome’; and ‘The recovery journey and returning back to normal life’. This structure was revised iteratively to allow for the inclusion of any new theme and/or delete an existing one. Consensus between the two researchers was sought for all revisions. Afterwards, D.K.N. and O.B.O. independently analysed the data using available qualitative software analysis tool to them (NVivo 14; Lumivero 2023) to D.K.N. and MAXQDA 24 (VERBI Software 2023) to O.B.O., respectively, to generate codes. These were compared and merged in an iterative process of discussion and consensus, and emergent themes and sub-themes identified. Indexing was performed by applying the framework to the data with codes linked to emergent themes and sub-themes. These linkages were reviewed manually and themes charted, ensuring best fit and inclusion of quotes that reflected the diverse participants’ experiences. Inter-relatedness among major themes was explored by the researchers in iterative processes of data engagement, review and consensus. These results were subjected to peer review by the third author, I.G. The initial eight major themes (and 32 sub-themes) were merged into the final five major themes. Interpretation of the study findings took place in iterative discussions between D.K.N. and O.B.O. and subjected to validation by I.G. These processes enhanced the credibility and trustworthiness of the findings through investigator triangulation. Member checking was also performed by taking the study findings and interpretations back to three of the study participants (P15 and P18) who independently confirmed that the results reflected their experiences. In all of the steps of analysis, researchers ensured reflexivity through self-awareness of their own preconceptions and prejudices, avoiding imposing these on the study findings, but rather interpreting and grounding the findings in the context from which they have emerged. For this article, we employed the SEM (Bronfenbrenner 1993) to explore the implications of the study findings for South Africa’s future pandemic preparedness.

Ethical considerations

The research was conducted according to the Helsinki Declaration. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Human Research and Ethics Committees (Medical) of the University of the Witwatersrand (Clearance number: M201130) and the Sefako Makgatho University of Health Sciences (SMUREC/M/71/2021:IR).

Healthcare services receivedParticipants reported that they received homecare, in-hospital care and COVID vaccination. The experience and attitudes to homecare were mixed. While participants appreciated the efforts of their loved ones who cared for them at home, the experience of quarantine was intensely negative. Quarantine/isolation was likened by a participant to ‘Mptjanyana’ (a room where people infected by leprosy were kept): ‘Isolation to me was like I was not welcome in the society. It made me feel bad because even people who were visiting our home, they knew that I was there, but they were told that I was in isolation and could not come out because I have COVID’ (P17, 55 years, male, married). Another reported: ‘Uhm, it was not a nice experience at all … My physical and my psychological experiences … Another one is feeling lonely at home … I don’t have anyone to talk to …’ (P5, 31 years, female, single). Despite this negative perception, most participants complied with the COVID-19 regulation because of the fear of transmitting the infection to their loved ones at home: ‘Yes, he is taking pills. So, I was very scared for him, but he didn’t get it … Yes, I went to another bedroom for his safety’ (P2, 50 years, female, married). Nonetheless, quarantine regulations were commonly breeched due to difficulties in maintaining physical and emotional detachment from their loved ones and myths that COVID-19 patients die when alone. This is reflected by a participant who said: ‘But then there was a time when I decided that I am moving back to the bedroom. It’s either I die as well because I had to take care of him. Not knowing who will take care of me after …’ (P3, 37 years, married).In addition to fear, secrecy and guilt feelings, participants also experienced stigma with home quarantine: ‘So, I felt like they were not treating me well … Because now I was thinking that before I knew I had COVID, I would just sit with them and cough and talk …’ (P17, 55 years, male, married). Despite the loneliness of quarantine, participants reported good social support from family and co-workers.With regard to treatment, participants reported receiving treatments from their loved ones, community pharmacies, general medical practitioners (GPs) and home visits by Department of Health tracing teams. Regardless of professional or educational standing, participants reported using homeopathic remedies that included home-brewed herbal concoctions, vitamins and supplements. The prevalent use of these remedies was exemplified by the statement of one participant who, although a professional nurse, engaged in the use of herbal concoction during the COVID-19 pandemic as treatment. She said, ‘And when I passed, I was like, here are these trees (tusk leaves). And that side, it is a lot of them. This medication is galore here … laughing … I looked, I saw many cars, women. And I said oh, I am also joining the queue. I want this. I went and put it … laughing … into the plastic. I am going to keep it in my house in the fridge. Each time I need, I will take, put in a pot and boil, sieve, and now it is my medication, and drink … laughing …’ (P15, 50 years, female, married).Although not reported by all the participants, COVID-19-infected persons were not left in limbo. The health system ensured that patients were kept in care through follow-up telephone calls and home visits by the Outbreak response teams: ‘People from Department of Health used to call me to check up on me. They would ask if anyone in the house is infected and there was no one’ (P2, 50 years, female, married).Most participants perceived that the COVID-19 vaccination was useful, accessible, and accepted. However, a sizeable minority thought it made their health status worse. Those that were accepting of the vaccine felt that while they could not impose the vaccine on anybody, it was unfair to others for some people to refuse to be vaccinated as this behaviour negates the development of herd immunity that is desperately needed. In the words of a participant, ‘… vaccination does not only provide protection for oneself as a patient receiving the vaccine but also your immediate loved ones …’ (P5, 31 years, female, single). Spirituality also played a role in vaccine hesitancy as some viewed the COVID-19 vaccine and whether to accept it as a spiritual issue, as exemplified by a participant who said ‘I did not want to (vaccinate), deep down in my spirit. I need to disclose this. My spirit did not want me to take any vaccine. Every time I wanted to go, then my soul would say where are you going? I never went’ (P11, 51 years, female, divorced).Only a few participants reported being admitted to the hospital. The experience of seeking in-facility treatment was characterized by perceptions of poor access when they or their loved ones deteriorated clinically and needed hospital admission for high care or intensive care. A participant reported that ‘When she (mother) arrived at the hospital … it was cold, and people were being triaged in the tent and the tent is cold. So, mama was in the tent from the morning until around 23h00 in the evening, being old. We waited and waited while they were triaging them and looking for beds for admission’ (P14, 56 years, female, widowed). This poor access was compounded by people’s morbid fear of death. Hence, not only do people not have access to in-patient care but they do not want to be admitted for fear of not coming out alive. A carer reported: ‘I brought him home and he said maybe he needs to be admitted. I told him that I don’t want him to be admitted. I was scared because I felt like if he goes to the hospital he is going to die’ (P3, 37 years, female, married). However, when COVID-19 victims’ clinical conditions deteriorated, they needed persuasions from family and friends (against their fear of dying in hospital) to get them admitted to the hospital: ‘She (participant’s cousin) would say you guys are going to the hospital. I won’t have sick people around me. Then we would say but the hospital is where we are going to die. And she would say ‘No’; it is where you are going to get help’ (P10, 38 years, female, single).As occurred during homecare, loneliness was also a key problem during in-hospital care, as friends and families were not allowed to visit. This was most profound during the first wave of the pandemic when there were many deaths among hospitalized patients. The propensity for deaths among hospitalized victims in the first wave invoked intense fear and resulted in stigma, such that even healthcare professionals were scared and unsure of their own survival despite the use of PPE: ‘… and (the) worst part is the first wave. Even the physician cannot get inside of your ward. He is on the window. You can feel when they are afraid of you. The nurse and everyone else is afraid of you. I don’t remember being auscultated by that physician … you would see the way they are wearing PPE; it was like you are a danger…’ (P6, 48 years, male, married).

Psychosocial supportPsychosocial support was more available during home care than during in-facility care. Even when a COVID victim died, the family support often continued, as reported by a participant who said: ‘But he (father-in-law) made sure that he was taking care of everything for his late wife. He was like a father when we needed him most. He was there. He made sure that his wife had a dignified funeral’ (P3, 37 years, female, married). The available psychosocial support was provided by family, friends, faith-based organizations, and not by government. The faith-based support included prayers and was reported to help victims pull through the ordeals of COVID infection. This was exemplified by a participant who said ‘The only thing that sustained me was prayer … I thought it was a spirit. I am attacked by a spirit. It is a force that wanted to overpower me. I did not think of any other thing, but I said ‘Lord, I am going to survive this. I am going to pull through’’ (P11, 51 years, female, divorced). Another said: ‘She (wife) organised a prayer group online; pastors and you know. Some pastor even asked for money from her … They asked for R10 000. She gave one of the pastors $1000, which is R15000 … She spent more time with God, and she went to church’ (P6, 48 years, male, married). Despite providing support to victims, there was no support for carers. This resulted in physical and emotional exhaustion that sometimes warranted them to lie in order to escape and take some break: ‘We were scared to tell him that we went home. We are tired. You can’t tell a sick person that you are tired. So, we just said we were in the car sleeping …’ (P3, 37 years, female, married). Similarly, healthcare workers who became infected with the COVID-19 virus reported that their employers did not provide them with psychosocial support: ‘So, I also felt that my employer is not doing anything for me … I caught this thing (COVID-19) because of the hard work I was doing. By the time I was in need of it (support), nothing was happening …’ (P14, 56 years, female, widow).Social media, cell phones, and television were used as tools for psychosocial support, particularly during home quarantine for communicating with others, public health education, as a sources of information on COVID-19, and helping survivors cope with loneliness: ‘But people will text … and you see, you will be on WhatsApp, Facebook and you are forever on social media. It will keep you busy so that you don’t think about this …’ (P20, 30 years, female, single). However, there was a downside, in that, aside from too much unscientific information, other people were insensitive to the type of information to share. A participant captured this and said ‘…when you are sick, other people are not sensitive. They (colleagues) would be telling you that … They would be sending messages and telling you that so and so has passed away. So and so is hospitalised, so and so is in ICU. Those messages were the ones which were causing more harm than anything. I ended up switching off my phone’ (P18, 58 years, female, widowed).

Returning to normal lifeParticipants reported that the COVID-19 pandemic came as a storm and disrupted society at all levels: ‘I mean COVID actually came as a storm, and it showed no discrimination. It came for everyone. Whether you have money, whether you live in the township, you live in the suburbs, it came for everyone …’ (P5, 31 years, female, single). A participant reported: ‘It hurts because sometimes I don’t even know what is happening to my body. I don’t know how to deal with it. Some days it shuts down and you find that some days my legs are swollen … So, now, the symptoms that I have, I am on chronic medication for blood clots. Another reported on financial strain, saying: ‘When I came out of the hospital, the medical aid (insurance) bill was R300 000 (+/− USD 16 200), just for being in the hospital. Then my medical aid (insurance) was exhausted …. When I went to the pharmacy for medication, they told me that the medical aid was exhausted …’ (P12, 59 years, female, married). While most participants were not coping well with returning to normal life, only a few had access to a psychologist. Because so many deaths had occurred …’ (P16, 57 years, female, widowed).Looking into the future, participants highlighted the need for more research on COVID-19, particularly on developing treatment, vaccine effectiveness and hesitancy, the long-term effects of unmeasured intake of herbal and homeopathic remedies, and the economic and psychosocial implications for survivors: ‘You must do more research on COVID. Discussion

This study found that the experiences of COVID-19 survivors transcended their awareness of COVID-19 illness, the healthcare services received, psychosocial support, illness outcomes, and sequelae and their returning back to normal life. These experiences have implications for the survivors’ coping abilities and resilience, and the South African health system’s preparedness for future pandemics. This is so because pandemics are realities of life and are likely to become more frequent in the increasingly volatile climate, mobile, and interconnected global village (Sundararaman et al. 2021).

Although participants were promptly aware of their COVID-19 illness and had access to COVID testing, social circumstances, fears of death, loneliness, stigma, guilt, and secrecy all influenced the survivors’ illness experiences and their attitudes to COVID-19 vaccination. While these issues have previously been reported (Ho et al. 2020, Sharma et al. 2020, Nxumalo et al. 2023), they are amplified by poor knowledge and myths about COVID-19. Improving people’s health literacy may therefore be an intervention that may moderate the negative emotional experiences and improve adherence to quarantine regulations, the uptake of COVID-19 vaccines and the post-COVID-19 physical and mental health aftermath (Kathirvel 2020).

The mental health effects of the COVID-19 pandemic have been previously described and include anxiety disorders, depression, burn-out and post-traumatic stress disorders (Kim 2024). All of these have been reported as triggers for long-term mental health disorders (Brooks et al. 2020) and explain the high burden of mental health disorders that have characterized the post-COVID era (Kathirvel 2020). To attenuate these negative emotions, there is a need for psychosocial support for victims and carers during pandemics and this signifies a current policy gap that must be filled for better preparedness for future pandemics (Tumbo et al. 2023, Nasiri et al. 2024). In addition, healthcare workers need to be upskilled to identify the mental health effects of the illness experience and support patients and carers to enhance their coping capacity and resilience, as these have been reported to lower the risks of morbidity and mortality among survivors (Moeti et al. 2023). However, a South African study reported that the risk of incident or exacerbated adult mental health disorders was not attenuated by social support during COVID, suggesting that some mental health problems such as depression and anxiety may alter the perception of social support received (Brooks et al. 2020, Ho et al. 2020, Nxumalo et al. 2023). Hence, more research is needed to fully elucidate this relationship.

The constrained access to healthcare services experienced by participants reflects the incessant poor access that continues to characterize the public healthcare services in South Africa. This mostly explains the locking out of many patients from in-patient care, the high excess out-of-facility COVID-19 deaths and the negative emotions and attitudes reported by participants (Moeti et al. 2023). To avert this in future pandemics, there is a need for a steady investment into health infrastructure to ensure a responsive and elastic health system that is able to absorb surges in demands that will come with the next inevitable pandemic (Ho et al. 2020, Sundararaman et al. 2021, Nasiri et al. 2024). Such a resilient health system must employ a design that increases infrastructural capacities across all levels of care and interphase with health technologies (including the use of artificial intelligence) to increase human efficiencies and productivity, and the effectiveness of clinical processes. In addition, human settlement policies must aspire to homes and community designs that facilitate safe quarantine conditions to relieve pressures on healthcare facilities, particularly from severely ill individuals.

The positive general reception of the COVID-19 vaccine by participants must be applauded and leveraged on by the South African health system to improve vaccine uptake to rapidly build population immunity. This is important as public health containment measures are often not as effective as they should be, as seen by the breaches in our study (Engelbrecht et al. 2022, Moeti et al. 2024). The few participants that expressed vaccine hesitancy based on myths and unproven associations with adverse reactions call for intensification of vaccine education to enhance vaccine literacy and build trust between health authorities and the population. These have been found to increase vaccine uptakes (Moeti et al. 2024).

That private GPs and community pharmacies augmented access to healthcare services and provided relief to the overstretched public healthcare services during the COVID pandemic aligns with the principles of community-oriented primary care (COPC). COPC promotes the harnessing of communal resources to respond to the population health needs (Hedima et al. 2021, Phanudulkitti et al. 2023). Ensuring partnerships between public and private healthcare facilities and community-based non-governmental organizations is therefore a policy and pragmatic imperative for future pandemics.

The poor sensitivity of healthcare workers to the fears and emotional upsets of victims during in-patient care and when breaking the news of the deaths of loved ones reiterates previous reports that healthcare workers in South Africa are ill-equipped for palliative care, including the management of end-of-life issues (Bongongo et al. 2022). Healthcare workers’ training in palliative care is therefore a policy imperative for optimal preparedness for future pandemics. Ideally, palliative care should be integrated into routine services, pre-pandemic. In addition, policies that find balance between preventing the spread of the biologic agents such as the COVID virus and sensitivity to the cultures and beliefs of the people are needed to ensure a culturally dignifying disposal of human remains. This will address concerns and participants’ perceptions that the remains of their loved ones were not respectfully handled.

The aftermath of COVID-19 experienced by participants in this study aligns with the physical, neurocognitive, psychosocial and quality of life issues previously reported and mimic long COVID (Kathirvel 2020, Perumal et al. 2023). Long COVID is a syndrome that affects people infected with COVID-19 and manifests with multiple and lingering health problems that can be debilitating, negatively affecting personal and workplace productivity, social relationships, financial wellbeing and quality of life, well beyond the acute phase of COVID infection (Kathirvel 2020, Perumal et al. 2023). In addition to the lingering physical and mental health problems, the financial burdens reported by participants were not limited to debts from treatments not covered by medical insurance and the financial demands of ongoing care but include economic losses from ongoing decreased productivity. Such a burden can be enormous at population level, considering that long COVID may affect up to 1 in 4 survivors and underscores the call for universal healthcare coverage that is free at the point of service. In addition, long COVID needs to be considered as an occupational health and workplace safety priority. As such, healthcare and workplace pandemic preparedness policies need to ensure that post-pandemic, physical and mental health assessments are conducted on workers prior to full return to work. It should not be assumed that workers would automatically be fit for previously normal duties. Workers with severe long COVID impairments may need to be paced to work demands, and a rehabilitation and reintegration or medical boarding plan as deemed fit, implemented by a multidisciplinary team. In addition, debriefing and counselling programmes must be offered to give a ‘listening ear’ to workers as participants felt that employers did not care about their post-COVID wellbeing.

At a personal level, the study findings suggest participants’ awareness of their COVID-19 illness was prompt and associated with appropriate help-seeking behaviour and reasonable access to testing. However, the intense fear of death and uncertainty with survival require that citizens be empowered pre-pandemic or at the beginning to improve their general health literacy to dispel myths and information lacking evidence. Citizens should also be empowered to take responsibility for assessing their own health risks, exposures and vulnerabilities, and respond with good help-seeking behaviours during pandemics. To this end, healthy lifestyles, including vaccination uptake, need to be promoted and community participation enabled to build trust with health authorities. Trust combined with improved health literacy has the potential to promote evidence-based decision-making, good health, or help-seeking behaviours and may reduce vaccine hesitancy at the individual level (Engelbrecht et al. 2022, Goje and Kapoor 2024).

In this study, participants reported little or no community engagement, possibly because of the COVID-19 regulations that limited social interactions. However, psychosocial support was provided by family and friends, as previously reported elsewhere (Brooks et al. 2020, Nxumalo et al. 2023). Yet, among the disenfranchised and outlying populations, such as found in many settings in South Africa, community participation is a key for uptake of public health initiatives such as the isolation/quarantine regulation policy and COVID-19 vaccination. The poor community participation might have influenced the perceived authoritarian approach of the health authorities and the culturally insensitive COVID funeral regulations reported by participants.

At the health system level, the COVID-19 pandemic exposed pervasive social disparities in healthcare coverage and access, and poor preparedness of the Gauteng public health services, similar to reports in other settings. The health system was challenged in its capacity to accommodate increases in demand for in-facility care. While several reports have alluded to this (Bongongo et al. 2022), all public and private healthcare facilities and community-based non-governmental organizations need to be considered as commonwealth, and additional investments made need to be made on health infrastructures to ensure unhindered access to healthcare services across public and private facilities during pandemic.

The study findings indicate that at the policy level, there is a need to promote universal healthcare coverage as demanded by the WHO policy paper (Ranabhat et al. 2023). Health policies must focus on improving access to in-facility care, advocate for vaccination mandate, advocate for rights and patents to manufacture vaccines locally, promote investment in and improve the use of health technologies for communication, health education, patient tracing and follow-up, and support treatment adherence. Furthermore, funding for research needs to be increased to explore how best public health containment of outbreaks of biological agents, vaccine development, understanding, and mitigating undesirable behaviours that have adverse psychosocial impact on the course and outcomes of the pandemic should be implemented.

This study has potential for some limitations: This was a qualitative study and, as such, the findings cannot be generalized. The purposive sampling makes the study prone to sampling bias, but this was limited using independent district representatives for recruitment in the two districts. Oversight was also provided by the first and senior author to ensure that the research procedures were standardized across the two districts. The interviews in the two districts were conducted by the same experienced interviewer. The independent generation of codes by the first and senior authors, and the subsequent scrutiny for agreement by a third author added to the trustworthiness and credibility of the findings.

In conclusion, the experiences of COVID-19 survivors in two districts of Gauteng, South Africa, were intense in nature and occurrence, and physically and emotionally challenging. They transcended participants’ awareness of their COVID-19 illnesses, the healthcare services received (home and in-patient care), social support, illness outcomes and sequelae, and their return to normal life. These experiences implicate several individual, family/communal, health system and policy/government level gaps in South Africa and underscore the need to strengthen the health system for a better preparedness for future pandemics.
