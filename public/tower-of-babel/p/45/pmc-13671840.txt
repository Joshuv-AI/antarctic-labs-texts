Effect of social jetlag on metabolic syndrome varies by body mass index: evidence from Taiwan Biobank
Authors: Shih-Kai Kao, Kai-Hsuan Cheng, Jerry Cheng-Yen Lai, Shaw-Ji Chen, Meng-Ying Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Social jetlag (SJL), the misalignment between biological and social time, has been linked to adverse metabolic outcomes. However, the effect of body mass index (BMI) on this association remains unclear, particularly in Asian populations. This cross-sectional study utilized data from the Taiwan Biobank, including 65,832 adults with complete information on sleep timing and metabolic parameters. SJL was calculated as the difference in mid-sleep time between workdays and free days and categorized as <2 or ≥2 h. Metabolic syndrome (MetS) was defined using standard clinical criteria. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to examine the association between SJL and MetS, with stepwise adjustment for sociodemographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors. Additional analyses stratified participants into BMI categories (<24, 24–26.9, ≥27 kg/m2). After full adjustment including BMI, SJL ≥2 h was not significantly associated with MetS (adjusted odds ratio = 1.05, 95% confidence interval: 0.98–1.12; p = 0.180). In BMI-stratified analyses, a significant association was observed only in the overweight group (BMI 24–26.9 kg/m2; adjusted odds ratio = 1.12, 95% confidence interval: 1.01–1.24; p = 0.035), but not in normal-weight or obese groups. An association between SJL and MetS was observed only in the overweight group, suggesting a BMI-dependent association between SJL and MetS. These findings highlight the importance of considering BMI when evaluating the association between SJL and MetS and indicate that the relationship between circadian misalignment and metabolic risk may vary across BMI categories.

Introduction

The effect of social jetlag (SJL), a form of circadian rhythm misalignment resulting from discrepancies between biological and social clocks, on lifestyle-related health outcomes has garnered increasing research attention [1, 2]. SJL is defined based on the absolute difference in midsleep points between workdays and weekends; it commonly occurs because the high demands of modern lifestyles can lead to irregular sleep patterns [3]. Studies have shown that SJL is associated with adverse health outcomes, including obesity, cardiovascular diseases, and components of metabolic syndrome (MetS), suggesting that it may represent a modifiable public health risk factor [4-7].

MetS, a cluster of interconnected metabolic abnormalities, including central obesity, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and insulin resistance, is a major predictor of cardiovascular diseases and type 2 diabetes [8-12]. Identifying and addressing modifiable risk factors for MetS is crucial in mitigating its long-term health consequences [7, 13-15]. However, despite extensive research, findings on the association between SJL and MetS remain inconsistent, with studies reporting positive, null, or inverse association under certain conditions [5, 16-19]. These discrepancies indicate that further research is required to understand the influence of SJL on MetS in diverse populations. Moreover, demographic factors (e.g., age and sex) and lifestyle characteristics as well as body mass index (BMI), may influence any association between SJL and MetS, and therefore, should be considered.

This study utilized a large, population-based cohort from the Taiwan Biobank (TWB) to examine the association between SJL and MetS in Taiwanese adults. Given the extensive biochemical, lifestyle, and sociodemographic information available in the TWB, we evaluated whether SJL was independently associated with MetS and further investigated whether this relationship varied across levels of adiposity. In particular, we examined the role of BMI in this association to determine whether susceptibility to circadian misalignment varies by weight status. By integrating detailed population data with stratified analyses, this study aimed to clarify the metabolic relevance of SJL and identify subgroups of individuals who may be most vulnerable to its effects, thereby informing more targeted strategies for MetS prevention in populations experiencing circadian disruption.

Methods

Taiwan Biobank

We conducted a population-based cross-sectional study using pooled data from 65,832 individuals included in the TWB during 2015–2024. The TWB is a government-supported initiative established to collect comprehensive lifestyle and genetic data from the Taiwanese population [20, 21]. To ensure confidentiality, the TWB deidentifies data, without alteration. Individuals whose data are included in the TWB provide written informed consent, detailed information regarding their daily lives, and blood samples. In addition, they undergo physical examination as well as complete a structured questionnaire covering personal information and lifestyle factors in a face-to-face interview with a TWB researcher.

Definition of MetS

In individuals included on the TWB, waist and hip circumference are measured through physical examination. Fasting blood sugar, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and triglyceride are determined through biochemical examinations. MetS was defined using the modified National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III criteria [22]. In accordance with these criteria, MetS was diagnosed based on the presence of at least three of the following five metabolic abnormalities: (1) waist circumference ≥90 cm in men and ≥80 cm in women, (2) triglyceride levels ≥150 mg/dL or pharmacotherapy for hyperlipidemia, (3) HDL-C levels <40 mg/dL in men and <50 mg/dL in women, (4) blood pressure ≥130/85 mmHg or pharmacotherapy for hypertension, (5) fasting blood glucose level ≥100 mg/dL or use of glucose-lowering medication.

Definition of SJL

The current study analyzed SJL, defined as the absolute difference between the midsleep point on weekends and the midsleep point on work (or school) days, as described by Wittmann et al. and Roenneberg et al. [1, 23]. Individuals with SJL <2 h and SJL ≥2 h were divided into non-SJL and SJL groups, respectively. The 2-hour threshold has been widely used in previous epidemiological studies as an indicator of clinically relevant circadian misalignment [16, 24-27].

Other covariates

During the interview with a TWB researcher, various demographic data were collected, including sex (male vs. female); age; BMI; regular exercise habits (yes vs. no); education level (college or graduate school vs. high school, elementary school, or none); residential urbanicity (rural, sub-urban, or urban); tobacco smoking habit, alcohol consumption habit, areca nut chewing, coffee consumption, and tea consumption. BMI categories were defined according to Taiwanese criteria: normal weight (<24 kg/m2), overweight (24–26.9 kg/m2), and obesity (≥27 kg/m2), which are commonly used for Asian populations because metabolic risk tends to occur at lower BMI levels compared with Western populations [28, 29].

Statistical analysis

The characteristics of the individuals in the SJL and non-SJL groups are presented as means ± standard deviations for continuous variables and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t test, and categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Univariable analyses were performed to describe the associations between participant characteristics and MetS. Covariates included in the multivariable logistic regression models were prespecified based on clinical relevance and prior literature. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to evaluate the association between SJL and MetS, and adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS for Windows (version 25; Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The design of the TWB was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Biomedical Science Research (IRB-BM) at Academia Sinica, Taipei, Taiwan, and the Ethics and Governance Council of the Taiwan Biobank, Taipei, Taiwan. This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of MacKay Memorial Hospital (21MMHIS351e).

Results

Baseline characteristics stratified by the presence of SJL

Among the 65,832 participants, 14.0% had SJL ≥2 h. Individuals with SJL ≥2 h were younger (mean age 47.4 vs. 56.0 years, p < 0.001) and slightly less likely to be male (34.9% vs. 36.4%, p = 0.006) (Table 1). A higher proportion of participants with SJL ≥2 h had a college-level education or above (71.4% vs. 56.1%, p < 0.001) and were currently employed (86.3% vs. 55.9%, p < 0.001). The distribution of residential areas also differed between groups (p < 0.001). Regarding behavioral factors, individuals with SJL ≥2 h were less likely to report regular exercise (24.7% vs. 47.7%, p < 0.001) and had a higher prevalence of tobacco smoking, alcohol consumption, and coffee consumption (all p < 0.001). Areca nut chewing did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.736). The average SJL difference in sleep timing was substantially larger in the ≥2-h group (2.51 ± 1.36 h vs. 0.15 ± 0.40 h, p < 0.001). Regarding the laboratory parameters, participants with SJL ≥2 h had lower HbA1c levels (5.77% vs. 5.87%, p < 0.001), lower fasting glucose (p < 0.001), lower HDL-C (p < 0.001), and higher LDL-C (p < 0.001), although triglyceride levels showed no significant differences. In terms of vital signs and anthropometrics, systolic blood pressure was lower and BMI was higher in the SJL ≥2-h group (both p < 0.001), whereas waist circumference was similar in both groups. The prevalence of MetS was lower among those with SJL ≥2 h (22.7% vs. 25.9%, p < 0.001). The distribution of individual components varied across groups: participants with SJL ≥2 h had a lower prevalence of high blood glucose and high blood pressure (both p < 0.001), a slightly higher prevalence of low HDL-C (p = 0.010), and similar rates of large waist circumference and high triglycerides.

Association between SJL and MetS

Table 2 presents the unadjusted ORs for the associations between SJL and general characteristics in MetS. Compared with individuals with SJL <2 h, those with SJL ≥2 h had significantly lower odds of MetS (OR 0.84, 95% CI 0.80–0.88, p < 0.001). Increasing age was associated with higher odds of MetS (OR 1.04 per year, 95% CI 1.03–1.04, p < 0.001), whereas women had lower odds compared with men (OR 0.71, 95% CI 0.69–0.74, p < 0.001). Higher BMI was strongly associated with MetS (OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.34–1.36, p < 0.001). Participants with a college or graduate education had lower odds compared with those with high school education or below (OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.60–0.64, p < 0.001). Married individuals also showed slightly reduced odds compared with unmarried, separated, or widowed participants (OR 0.95, 95% CI 0.91–0.99, p = 0.007). Currently employed individuals had significantly lower odds of MetS (OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.68–0.73, p < 0.001). Regarding place of residence, suburban areas (OR 1.08, 95% CI 1.04–1.12, p < 0.001) and rural areas (OR 1.10, 95% CI 1.02–1.19, p = 0.012) were associated with higher odds of MetS compared with urban areas. Several behavioral factors were also associated with MetS: regular exercise was associated with lower odds (OR 0.93, 95% CI 0.90–0.97, p < 0.001), whereas tobacco smoking (OR 1.65, p < 0.001), alcohol consumption (OR 1.40, p < 0.001), and areca nut chewing (OR 2.28, p < 0.001) were associated with higher odds. Coffee consumption was associated with reduced odds (OR 0.84, p < 0.001), whereas tea consumption was associated with increased odds of MetS (OR 1.22, p < 0.001).

Table 3 presents the adjusted associations between SJL and MetS. In the fully adjusted logistic regression model without BMI (Model 1), participants with SJL ≥2 h had significantly higher odds of MetS compared with those with SJL <2 h (AOR 1.14, 95% CI 1.07–1.21, p < 0.001). However, after additional adjustment for BMI in Model 2, the association between SJL and MetS was no longer statistically significant (AOR 1.05, 95% CI 0.98–1.12, p = 0.180). These findings indicate that BMI appears to attenuate—or potentially mediate—the relationship between SJL and MetS when included in the regression model.

BMI-stratified associations between SJL and MetS

Formal interaction testing between SJL and BMI categories was performed by including interaction terms in the multivariable logistic regression model. The interaction terms for both the overweight and obesity BMI categories were statistically significant (both p = 0.003), indicating that BMI significantly modifies the association between SJL and MetS. Table 4 shows the stratified analysis of SJL and MetS across different BMI categories. Among participants with BMI <24, SJL ≥2 h was not associated with MetS (AOR 1.07, 95% CI 0.93–1.23, p = 0.359). In the BMI 24–26.9 kg/m2 group, however, SJL ≥2 h was significantly associated with higher odds of MetS (AOR 1.12, 95% CI 1.01–1.24, p = 0.035). For participants with BMI ≥27 kg/m2, no significant association was observed (AOR 1.00, 95% CI 0.90–1.09, p = 0.907). These results indicate that the association between SJL and MetS is present only among individuals in the intermediate BMI range.

Discussion

This study examined the association between SJL and MetS. Although SJL ≥2 h showed a significant association with MetS in the model without BMI adjustment, this relationship became non-significant after BMI was included. Further analyses revealed that the SJL–MetS association varied across BMI strata. After adjusting for multiple demographic and lifestyle covariates, individuals classified as overweight (BMI 24–26.9 kg/m2) showed an association between SJL ≥2 h and MetS. In contrast, no significant association was observed among participants with normal BMI (<24 kg/m2) or those in the obese group (≥27 kg/m2). These findings highlight the potential modifying role of BMI in the association between SJL and MetS. Formal interaction testing further indicated that BMI significantly modifies the association between SJL and MetS. However, given the modest effect size observed in the overweight subgroup, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

The stratified findings of this study—showing that the association between SJL and MetS varies across BMI strata—partially align with the existing literature, which generally supports an association between SJL and adverse metabolic outcomes [1]. Several studies have reported positive associations between SJL and MetS. Parsons et al. identified significant associations between SJL and markers of adiposity, including BMI, fat mass, obesity, and MetS in their primary analysis [4]. Similarly, in a population-based cohort, Koopman et al. observed that individuals younger than 61 years with SJL >2 h had a 2.13-fold higher prevalence of MetS compared with those with SJL <1 h [16]. In occupational settings, Islam et al. reported that Japanese working adults with SJL ≥2 h had nearly double the odds of MetS, and this association remained robust even after adjustment for multiple confounders [24].

In contrast, the present finding that the overall association between SJL and MetS became non-significant after adjustment for BMI is consistent with the mixed findings reported in recent meta-analyses. Notably, Zhang et al. found that while large SJL was associated with waist circumference among non-shift workers, its associations with overall BMI or fasting glucose levels were inconsistent [30]. These discrepancies likely reflect substantial heterogeneity across study populations and methodologies. Several modifying factors have been proposed: (1) age, with stronger associations observed in younger adults (<61 years) and attenuated effects in older individuals [6, 16, 17]; (2) underlying health status, considering that associations differ between general populations and clinical cohorts such as individuals with type 2 diabetes or established MetS [31]; and (3) chronotype variability, given evidence that higher SJL may be associated with increased waist circumference among morning-type individuals but not among evening-type individuals [32, 33]. Collectively, these observations support the notion that the metabolic associations of SJL are context-dependent and influenced by demographic, behavioral, and circadian phenotypic factors.

SJL represents a continuous spectrum of circadian misalignment, and the degree of misalignment may vary widely depending on lifestyle factors and social schedules [1]. In epidemiological studies, a threshold of ≥2 hours has frequently been used to indicate greater circadian misalignment [16, 24-27]. Although larger discrepancies in sleep timing may theoretically be associated with stronger metabolic associations, the present study adopted the 2-hour cutoff to ensure comparability with previous population-based studies. Future studies using SJL as a continuous variable or alternative thresholds may provide further insights into dose–response patterns.

The observation that an association between SJL and MetS was observed only among individuals in the overweight range (BMI 24–26.9 kg/m2) suggests that this subgroup may be positioned at a transitional metabolic state. SJL has been associated with alterations in metabolic pathways, including the regulation of insulin sensitivity [34] and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis signaling [35, 36]. Individuals with normal BMI may have fewer underlying metabolic abnormalities, which could potentially attenuate the observed association between SJL and MetS. In contrast, those in the overweight category (24–26.9 kg/m2) often exhibit substantial accumulation of adiposity—particularly visceral fat, which has been linked to adverse metabolic profiles [37, 38]. BMI may act as both a confounder and a mediator in the relationship between circadian misalignment and metabolic outcomes. Therefore, the BMI-stratified findings in the present study should be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory rather than definitive evidence of BMI-specific effect modification.

Circadian misalignment reflected by SJL may be related to metabolic alterations in the overweight subgroup through several plausible pathways. One possibility is that SJL is more closely related to central adiposity than to overall BMI, because larger SJL has been consistently associated with greater waist circumference in prior studies [16, 39], despite mixed associations with BMI [17, 30]. Emerging evidence also suggests that circadian misalignment could interact with visceral adipose tissue, potentially interacting with visceral adipose tissue-related metabolic processes [40]. Another pathway that potentially contributes to a higher prevalence of MetS in the overweight subgroup is related to glycemic regulation. Larger SJL has been associated with higher HbA1c independent of BMI in some populations [4, 41]. Among individuals in the overweight range, who may already exhibit early metabolic abnormalities, these combined alterations in adiposity distribution and glucose regulation may help explain the higher prevalence of MetS observed in this subgroup. In contrast, among individuals with obesity (BMI ≥27 kg/m2), the dominant metabolic burden of excessive adiposity, such as chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and dysregulated lipid metabolism, may overshadow any potential association between SJL and MetS [8, 37]. This masking effect of obesity may partly explain why the association between SJL and MetS was observed only in the overweight subgroup in the present study.

This study provides several strengths that support the interpretation of the findings of SJL and metabolic outcomes. First, the use of a large population-based biobank sample (N ≈ 65,000) provides substantial statistical power to detect modest associations between SJL and MetS, exceeding that of smaller cohort studies reported in the literature [16, 24]. Second, the definition and assessment of MetS were based on objective clinical and laboratory measurements, including fasting glucose, lipid profiles, blood pressure, and anthropometric indices, thereby enhancing the accuracy and validity of outcome classification compared with studies relying primarily on self-reported data. Third, the analyses incorporated extensive adjustment for potential confounding factors, such as age, sex, indicators of socioeconomic status, smoking, and alcohol consumption [4, 16]. This comprehensive control supports a more precise estimation of the association between SJL and MetS. Finally, an important methodological strength lies in the stratified BMI analysis, which examined BMI as a moderating factor rather than solely treating it as a covariate. This approach indicated that the association between SJL and MetS was concentrated within the overweight subgroup (BMI 24–26.9 kg/m2), offering a more nuanced understanding of BMI-dependent differences than studies employing broader obesity classifications [16, 31]. These strengths enhance the robustness of the present findings.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and prevents determination of the temporal relationship between SJL and MetS. Second, SJL was calculated from self-reported sleep schedules rather than objective measurements, introducing potential recall bias. Third, despite adjusting for numerous covariates, residual confounding cannot be ruled out, particularly from unmeasured factors such as chronotype, shift work, dietary patterns, psychological stress, and sleep disorders. In addition, although the definition of MetS incorporated treatment status for hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and hyperglycemia, detailed information on specific medication classes, treatment duration, dosage, adherence, and anti-obesity pharmacotherapy was not available. Therefore, the potential influence of pharmacotherapy on the association between SJL and MetS could not be fully assessed. Fourth, although BMI was used as a covariate and stratification factor, more precise indicators of adiposity, such as visceral fat or body fat percentage, were not available, limiting interpretation of weight-dependent effects. Finally, the study population consisted solely of Taiwanese adults, which may restrict generalizability to populations with different genetic, lifestyle, or occupational characteristics. Future longitudinal studies incorporating objective sleep assessments and detailed metabolic profiling are warranted to clarify these associations.

In conclusion, in this large population-based analysis, SJL of ≥2 h was associated with MetS in the preliminary adjusted models, but this association was fully attenuated after adjustment for BMI. Stratified analyses revealed that an association between SJL and MetS was observed only among individuals with BMI between 24–26.9 kg/m2. These findings suggest that BMI may modify the association between SJL and MetS. Further longitudinal and mechanistic studies are warranted to clarify potential pathways and better understand BMI-dependent differences in the relationship between circadian misalignment and metabolic health.
