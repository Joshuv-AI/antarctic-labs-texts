Investigating the impact of viscosifier concentrations on hydraulic horsepower at the bit
Authors: Chukwudi Ohaegbulam, Bright Wecheonwu, Kevin Igwilo, Chinyere Ezekannagha, Izuwa Nkemakolam, Ifeanyichukwu Onyejekwe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study focuses on optimizing the rheological properties of drilling mud by incorporating Xanthan gum at two concentrations (6 g and 12 g) to improve flow behavior, cutting transport, and hydraulic horsepower efficiency. The mud samples were analyzed using the Power Law and Herschel-Bulkley models to determine the most accurate representation of their rheological behavior. The Herschel-Bulkley model provided a better fit for both concentrations, with higher R² values and lower sum of square errors (SSE) compared to the Power Law model. For the 6 g Xanthan gum sample, the fluid behavior index (n) was 0.476, indicating shear-thinning properties and the yield strength (τy) was 2.112 lbf/100 ft². In contrast, the 12 g sample exhibited a higher yield strength of 14.142 lbf/100 ft² and a fluid behavior index closer to 1 (n = 0.965), suggesting shear-thickening behavior. The hydraulic horsepower analysis indicated that the 6 g mud was more efficient at higher flow rates, maintaining a consistent increase in horsepower up to 800 gpm. The 12 g mud, on the other hand, exhibited higher viscosity and increased pressure losses, resulting in a decrease in efficiency at flow rates exceeding 400 gpm. Both mud samples showed shear-thinning characteristics, but the 6 g formulation was better for transporting cuttings under dynamic conditions, while the 12 g formulation excelled in suspending cuttings during stagnant periods, due to its higher yield strength. Further optimization revealed that the 6 g mud was suitable for larger nozzle sizes and higher flow rates, with an optimum flow rate of 800 gpm, while the 12 g mud, with its increased viscosity, performed better with smaller nozzle sizes and flow rates around 400 gpm. This study highlights that the 6 g Xanthan gum mud is ideal for high-flow-rate operations, offering better cutting transport capabilities, while the 12 g mud is more effective in ensuring cuttings suspension when circulation ceases. These findings provide crucial insights for selecting optimal drilling mud formulations, considering both cutting transport and suspension requirements based on specific operational needs.Keywords: Drilling fluids, Non-Newtonian flow models, Hydraulic optimization, Drilling efficiency, Statistical modeling, SimulationSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

The role of hydraulic horsepower (HP) at the drill bit is crucial in enhancing drilling performance by optimizing cuttings transport, reducing bit wear, and improving penetration rates Hydraulic horsepower refers to the amount of energy required to circulate drilling fluid through the system and deliver it to the drill bit at a sufficient pressure and flow rate to effectively remove cuttings and maintain efficient drilling operations. The effectiveness of hydraulic horsepower is highly dependent on various factors, including fluid properties, pump performance, and wellbore conditions1. A key factor influencing hydraulic horsepower is the concentration of viscosifiers in the drilling fluid. Viscosifiers, typically made from polymers or bentonite clays, alter the rheological properties of the fluid, affecting both the pressure and flow characteristics of the drilling system2. These additives are commonly used in drilling fluids to improve their ability to lift and suspend cuttings from the wellbore, contributing significantly to the overall efficiency of the drilling operation3. The interaction between fluid rheology and hydraulic horsepower at the bit remains an important area of study in the ongoing effort to optimize drilling performance. Several studies have investigated how viscosifier concentrations impact hydraulic horsepower. According to Mike & Kasiena4, increasing the concentration of viscosifiers leads to higher fluid viscosity, which in turn requires greater pump pressure to maintain the same flow rate, consequently affecting the hydraulic horsepower at the bit. The increased viscosity aids in the lifting of cuttings from the wellbore but may cause an increase in frictional losses and energy consumption. The increased friction due to higher viscosity results in a higher pressure drop across the system, which demands more power to overcome these losses and maintain the desired flow rate. As a result, the total hydraulic horsepower required increases, which can lead to inefficiencies in the drilling operation and higher operational costs5.

However, excessive viscosifier concentrations can lead to diminishing returns, where the hydraulic horsepower required for effective drilling becomes disproportionately high, leading to inefficiencies6. In cases where viscosifiers are overused, the fluid may become excessively thick, creating higher friction losses within the system, which can negatively impact the overall efficiency of the drilling operation. Studies have shown that while higher viscosifier concentrations can improve cuttings transport by increasing the fluid’s ability to suspend and carry cuttings to the surface, this comes at the cost of increased energy consumption, leading to higher hydraulic horsepower demand7. The resulting increase in pump pressure can cause wear and tear on the equipment, which may lead to more frequent maintenance and potential downtime. To mitigate these issues, it is essential to identify the optimal viscosifier concentration that balances the need for effective cuttings transport with the goal of minimizing excessive pressure losses that increase hydraulic horsepower demand8. Studies by Gowida et al.9 suggest that optimal viscosifier concentrations must balance the need for efficient cuttings transport with the goal of minimizing excessive pressure losses that increase hydraulic horsepower demand. In their model, they demonstrate that viscosifier concentration plays a pivotal role in optimizing the relationship between flow rate and required hydraulic horsepower. They argue that an optimal concentration of viscosifiers can be determined by analyzing the balance between the increased lifting capability and the additional energy requirements. Their model incorporates both experimental data and theoretical calculations to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between viscosifier concentration and hydraulic horsepower, helping operators to optimize fluid formulations. The trade-off between fluid rheology and power requirements has led to further research into alternative solutions, including the use of low-shear-rate viscosifiers and rheological modifiers that maintain adequate viscosity without significantly raising hydraulic horsepower requirements10,11. Low-shear-rate viscosifiers are designed to maintain fluid viscosity under low flow conditions while reducing viscosity under higher shear rates. This can reduce the frictional losses and energy consumption associated with high-viscosity fluids in high-flow-rate conditions. Rheological modifiers, such as associative thickeners, can provide similar benefits by adjusting the rheological properties of the fluid to enhance cuttings transport without excessively increasing the hydraulic horsepower required. These solutions have shown promise in improving the efficiency of drilling operations, particularly in deep-water drilling and other high-pressure, high-temperature environments where maintaining fluid efficiency is critical12. Additionally, experimental investigations by Kerunwa et al.13 highlight the importance of fluid rheology and well bore eccentricity in determining the impact of viscosifier concentrations on hydraulic horsepower, with deeper wells requiring more efficient fluid formulations to handle the increased pressure and temperature conditions. As drilling depths increase, the pressure and temperature conditions within the wellbore become more extreme, which can significantly affect fluid behavior. In deep wells, the required hydraulic horsepower is higher due to the increased friction losses and the need to overcome greater pressure differentials. In such cases, the selection of an appropriate viscosifier concentration becomes even more critical to maintaining drilling efficiency while preventing excessive energy consumption14. One of the primary research gaps in the existing body of knowledge is the limited understanding of the interplay between viscosifier concentration and hydraulic efficiency at the bit. While numerous studies have investigated the rheological behavior of drilling fluids, there remains a paucity of research focused on the optimization of viscosifier concentrations to balance cuttings transport efficiency and suspension capabilities under varying operational conditions. Much of the existing literature has focused on general rheological properties without delving into the specifics of how these properties translate into hydraulic horsepower performance at the drill bit15,16. Additionally, most prior studies have either generalized shear-thinning behavior without detailed quantitative comparisons or overlooked the implications of specific nozzle sizes and flow rates on hydraulic horsepower efficiency17,18. Moreover, the comparative evaluation of Power Law and Herschel-Bulkley models in predicting fluid behavior has often been limited to empirical observations rather than a rigorous statistical approach19. The Power Law and Herschel-Bulkley models are commonly used to describe the rheological properties of drilling fluids, particularly in the context of shear-thinning behavior, where the fluid’s viscosity decreases with increasing shear rate20,21. While these models have been widely used, there remains a need for more rigorous statistical analysis to determine which model provides the best fit for specific fluid formulations under different operational conditions. The current study bridges this gap by employing R² values and sum of square errors (SSE) to substantiate the selection of an appropriate rheological model. By incorporating more robust statistical methods, the study aims to provide a more accurate representation of fluid behavior and hydraulic horsepower demands in real-world drilling operations. Another notable gap is the lack of optimization strategies tailored to different operational conditions, particularly in balancing high-flow-rate efficiency with adequate cuttings suspension when circulation ceases. When circulation stops, as in situations of tripping or when drilling is paused, the ability of the drilling fluid to suspend cuttings and prevent them from settling is critical. If the fluid’s suspension capability is insufficient, cuttings can accumulate in the wellbore, potentially leading to stuck pipe incidents or other drilling challenges. Therefore, it is essential to develop optimization strategies that account for both the fluid’s ability to transport cuttings during high-flow-rate conditions and its ability to suspend cuttings when flow rates decrease. This dual optimization approach is particularly important in deep-water and high-temperature wells where both flow conditions and wellbore geometry vary significantly throughout the drilling process22,23. The article addresses these gaps by systematically examining the effect of Xanthan gum concentration on drilling mud properties and their subsequent impact on hydraulic horsepower. Xanthan gum is a widely used biopolymer that acts as a viscosifier in drilling fluids, and its concentration can have significant effects on fluid behavior. By varying the concentration of Xanthan gum, the study investigates how changes in fluid rheology impact both cuttings transport and hydraulic horsepower requirements. The research highlights the importance of understanding the specific effects of different viscosifiers on hydraulic horsepower at the drill bit and provides insights into the optimization of viscosifier concentrations for different drilling scenarios.

Methodology

Mud preparation procedure

For each sample, 350 mL of water was measured in a graduated cylinder, to which 0.25 g of soda ash was added to reduce water hardness. Next, 10 g of bentonite was introduced to the treated water, and the bentonite slurry was sheared for 15 min before being left to hydrate for 10 h. After the pre-hydration period, the slurry was agitated, and 0.25 g of caustic soda was mixed in for two minutes. Following this, 28 g of sodium chloride was added and mixed for two minutes. Subsequently, xanthan gum (either 6–12 g, depending on the formulation) was incorporated into the mixture and stirred for about three minutes. Finally, 77 g of barite was added, and the mixture was agitated for 20 min to complete the mud preparation. This procedure was repeated with both xanthan gum concentrations (6 g and 12 g) using a Hamilton Beach mixer at medium speed. Xanthan gum was selected as the primary varying agent because it is a widely used, environmentally friendly, and biodegradable viscosifier that strongly influences drilling fluid rheology and hydraulic horsepower. The two concentrations (6 g and 12 g) were chosen to represent practical low and high concentration boundaries, thereby enabling the evaluation of rheological variation and its effect on hydraulic horsepower. This range allowed us to observe both moderate and high concentrations, providing insights into trade-offs between cuttings transport and energy efficiency. Rheology test

Rheological properties of the mud samples were determined using a Fann viscometer. Prior to testing, the viscometer was calibrated, and the mud samples were heated to 180 °F in a thermo-cup to replicate down hole conditions. Measurements were taken at 600, 300, 200, 100, 6, and 3 rpm, with dial readings recorded at each interval. Gel strength was measured every 10 min at 3 rpm. Results and discussion

This study focuses on the optimization of the rheological properties of drilling mud, specifically using Xanthan gum as a viscosity-enhancing additive. The optimization was performed to identify the rheological model that best describes the behavior of the drilling mud at two different concentrations (6 g and 12 g) of Xanthan gum. Two commonly used rheological models were considered: the Power Law model and the Herschel-Bulkley model. The Optimization process was conducted using MATLAB fsolve routine to minimize the Sum of Squared Errors (SSE) between the experimentally measured and model-predicted shear stress values and maximize the coefficient of determination (R²) to ensure strong correlation and predictive accuracy. This will evaluate the models’ ability to describe the mud’s rheological properties and to determine the hydraulic horsepower expended at the bit.

Evaluation of cutting transport capability

Both drilling mud samples exhibit shear-thinning behavior, as indicated by the fluid behavior index (n) values. Shear thinning is desirable because it reduces the viscosity of the mud at higher shear rates, improving the transport of cuttings during drilling operations.

For the 6 g sample (n = 0.476), the fluid exhibits more shear thinning than the 12 g sample (n = 0.965). This suggests that the 6 g mud has a better cutting-carrying capacity under dynamic conditions, as its viscosity will decrease more readily when subjected to the shear stresses in the annular flow.

For the 6 g sample (n = 0.476), the fluid exhibits more shear thinning than the 12 g sample (n = 0.965). This suggests that the 6 g mud has a better cutting-carrying capacity under dynamic conditions, as its viscosity will decrease more readily when subjected to the shear stresses in the annular flow.

The 12 g sample, while exhibiting a more pronounced shear-thickening behavior, has a significantly higher yield strength (τy = 14.142 lbf/100 ft² compared to 2.112 lbf/100 ft² for the 6 g sample). This makes the 12 g sample more effective in suspending cuttings when circulation is halted, as the mud is more resistant to settling under static conditions.

This makes the 12 g sample more effective in suspending cuttings when circulation is halted, as the mud is more resistant to settling under static conditions.

The consistency index (k) is also a key factor in evaluating the mud’s ability to suspend cuttings. The 6 g sample has a higher consistency index (k = 1.931 lbf sn/100 ft2) than the 12 g sample (k = 0.079 lbf sn/100 ft2), indicating that the 6 g mud is thicker and can suspend cuttings more effectively when circulation stops.

This is an important consideration when evaluating the mud’s overall performance in drilling operations, as cuttings suspension is critical during non-circulating periods.

Conclusion and recommendation

This study evaluated the effect of Xanthan gum concentrations (6 g and 12 g per 350 mL barrel-equivalent) on the rheological and hydraulic performance of water-based drilling muds. The analysis involved rheological modeling using the Power Law and Herschel–Bulkley models, alongside optimization of hydraulic horsepower (HHP) using MATLAB. The results confirmed that the Herschel–Bulkley model provided a superior fit for both mud samples, as evidenced by higher coefficients of determination (R²) and lower sum of squared errors (SSE), capturing yield stress behavior more accurately than the Power Law model. The 6 g sample demonstrated stronger shear-thinning behavior (fluid index n = 0.476) with moderate yield strength (2.112 lbf/100 ft²), making it efficient for cutting transport during circulation. In contrast, the 12 g sample exhibited higher yield strength (14.142 lbf/100 ft²) and more Newtonian-like behavior (n = 0.965), favoring cuttings suspension during static periods. Optimization of hydraulic horsepower revealed that the 6 g mud supported higher flow rates (up to 800 gpm) with lower pressure losses, resulting in higher energy efficiency. In contrast, the 12 g mud displayed diminishing returns at flow rates beyond 400 gpm due to elevated viscous resistance and increased pressure losses. Nozzle configuration further confirmed these trends, with larger optimal nozzle areas for the 6 g sample (0.65 in²) compared to 0.3 in² for the 12 g sample. Overall, the 6 g Xanthan gum formulation is recommended for drilling operations that require high flow rates and efficient energy use, particularly in formations where continuous circulation is prioritized. On the other hand, the 12 g formulation is more appropriate for scenarios where maintaining cuttings in suspension during pump shutdowns is critical, such as in extended bottom-hole assemblies or while tripping pipe. Future work may explore hybrid mud systems incorporating nanoparticles or other viscosifiers to balance suspension and flow efficiency. Moreover, operational monitoring of pressure losses should be enforced, especially with higher viscosifier concentrations, to avoid energy inefficiencies and ensure safe wellbore cleaning during drilling operations.
