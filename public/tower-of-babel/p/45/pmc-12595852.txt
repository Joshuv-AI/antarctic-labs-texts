Methionine induced placental angiogenesis through activating WNT3A/CTNNB1-PIGF-VEGFR1 pathway
Authors: Yijin Zou, Jiayu Tu, Ziqi Wang, Shuang Quan, Linlu Zhao, Yiwen Yang, Xiangzhou Zeng, Shiyan Qiao, Xiangfang Zeng, Shuang Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlacenta is highly susceptible to oxidative stress during pregnancy, which is a major cause of abnormal vascular development, fetal growth restriction and preterm birth. Methionine exhibits remarkable efficacy in promoting embryonic development and pregnancy outcomes, yet the role of methionine in placental antioxidant capacity and angiogenesis remains unclear.MethodsPregnant rats and porcine iliac artery endothelial cells (PIECs) were used in our study. Pregnant rats were fed with methionine supplementation or methionine free diet. PIECs were treated with methionine, ROS inducer, VEGFR1 inhibitor, CTNNB1 knockdown or overexpressing.ResultsOur findings revealed that dietary methionine supplementation significantly increased the levels of glutathione, while reducing the levels of malondialdehyde in rat placentae. Moreover, experiments from PIECs treatment with ROS inducer, VEGFR1 inhibitor, knockdown or overexpressing of CTNNB1 revealed that methionine regulated angiogenesis in the placenta by modulating ROS levels and the CTNNB1 signaling pathway. Mechanistically, methionine enhanced the transsulfuration metabolism in placental vascular cells, leading to the production of the antioxidant glutathione and a reduction in ROS levels, followed by activating the WNT3A/CTNNB1 signaling pathway. CTNNB1 bind to PIGF, which promoted the phosphorylation of VEGFR1, thereby enhancing angiogenesis.ConclusionsThis study elucidated that methionine promoted placental angiogenesis through the ROS-WNT3A/CTNNB1-PIGF-VEGFR1 axis, providing new therapeutic targets for pregnancy complications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02488-2.

Background

Placental angiogenesis, which begins in early pregnancy and continues throughout pregnancy, is crucial to placental development [1]. It forms a complex vascular network to ensure that the fetus receives an adequate supply of nutrients and oxygen while facilitating the removal of metabolic waste [2]. In addition, placenta-derived mesenchymal stem cells have attracted significant attention in regenerative medicine research [3]. These cells secrete various bioactive molecules, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [4], making them a promising therapeutic tool for diabetic foot ulcers [5, 6] and myocardial infarction [7, 8]. Placental metabolism generates a large amount of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [9]. Excessive ROS induces oxidative stress, leading to cell damage, apoptosis, and disrupts the balance between pro-angiogenic and anti-angiogenic factors [10], resulting in abnormal placental vascular development [11]. Abnormal placental angiogenesis will lead to placental insufficiency, which in turn can cause a series of pregnancy complications [12], such as preeclampsia [13] and fetal growth restriction [14, 15]. How to safely and effectively help women and mammals to improve placental angiogenesis is a hot and challenging issue for both human beings and animal husbandry.

Angiogenesis is regulated by various growth factors [16], cytokines, and signaling pathways [17]. VEGF binds to endothelial growth factor receptors (VEGFRs) on endothelial cells [18], triggering downstream signaling cascades that promote endothelial cell survival, proliferation, and migration [19]. Cytokines, such as Interleukin-8 (IL-8) [20], can promote angiogenesis by recruiting endothelial cells [21]. Notch signaling pathway regulates endothelial cell fate decisions [22], controlling the specification of tip and stalk cells during angiogenesis and influencing vessel branching [23]. Recent studies have found that maternal dietary supplementation with n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids can promote placental angiogenesis [24] and nutrient transport through the GPR120 signaling pathway [25]. Adenosine activates adenosine A2a receptor to promote the phosphorylation of Stat3 (signal transducer and activator of transcription 3) [24] and Akt (protein kinase B) [26], increasing angiogenin-dependent angiogenesis [27], thereby reducing the incidence of intrauterine growth restriction in piglets [28]. These findings suggest that targeted nutritional interventions could serve as effective approaches to enhance placental angiogenesis and improve reproductive outcomes.

Methionine is an essential amino acid that cannot be synthesized in the body and must be obtained from the diet [29]. In addition to its role in protein synthesis, methionine has several important biological functions [30]. Its metabolite, S-adenosylmethionine, provides methyl groups for the methylation of DNA, RNA, and proteins [31]. Methionine metabolism also generates antioxidants such as glutathione (GSH) and taurine [32]. Studies have shown that gilts dietary methionine supplementation significantly increases the birth weight of piglets and the litter weight [33]. In pregnant sows, dietary methionine supplementation increases placental stromal vascular density [34]. Adding an appropriate level of methionine to the culture medium can significantly enhance the migration and tube formation ability of porcine vascular endothelial cells [35]. In mice, intraperitoneal injection of methionine can significantly increase the mRNA expression of placental amino acid transporters [36]. In cows, methionine supplementation during late gestation promotes the transport of nutrients between the uterus and placenta, thereby increasing feed intake and the birth weight of newborns [37]. Moreover, this intervention is associated with significant changes in placental metabolism and DNA methylation levels [38]. These findings indicate that methionine plays an important role in placental development and reproductive outcomes. Our previous studies have demonstrated that dietary methionine supplementation significantly increases the number of live offspring and litter weight in rats and pigs, while reducing variation in birth weight within litters [39]. In addition, methionine supplementation activates the SAMTOR/mTORC1/CAD pathway, improves maternal-fetal immune tolerance, and promotes embryonic implantation [40, 41]. However, the mechanisms by which methionine alleviates placental oxidative stress and regulates placental vascular development remain poorly understood.

The purpose of this study was to explore the effects of methionine on placental development and its underlying mechanisms. Through rat feeding, porcine iliac artery endothelial cells (PIECs) culture, gene interference and over-expression, combined with transcriptomics, protein interaction prediction and ChIP-seq, we demonstrated that methionine could improve placental antioxidant capacity and placental vascular development. Mechanically, methionine enhanced the expression of cystathionine β-synthase (CBS, the critical enzyme involved in transsulfuration pathway), increasing the level of GSH, while reducing the level of ROS in placentae and PIECs. Furthermore, methionine activated the WNT3A/CTNNB1 signaling pathway. CTNNB1 interacted with placental growth factor (PIGF), thereby enhancing the phosphorylation of VEGFR1, ultimately alleviated ROS-induced inhibition of cell migration and angiogenesis. Our findings not only enrich the theoretical framework of angiogenesis regulation but also provide new insights in nutritional interventions during pregnancy.

Materials and methods

Animals

Eight-week-old female Sprague Dawley rats were purchased from Huafukang Bioscience Co., Inc. (Beijing, China) and caged in a specific pathogen-free animal room under a controlled environment (temperature set at 23 °C and a lighting schedule of 12 h light/12 h dark). Pregnancy was induced by co-caging overnight, and the presence of spermatozoa in the vaginal smear was defined as day 1 of pregnancy. A total of 48 pregnant rats were randomly assigned to two dietary groups: (1) methionine-free purified rodent diet (containing no methionine); (2) 1.0% methionine-supplemented purified rodent diet (containing 1.0% methionine). The dose of methionine was determined according to our previous study [40]. On days 12, 14 and 18 of pregnancy, 8 rats from each group were chosen randomly to be sacrificed. The rats were anesthetized via an intraperitoneal injection of 2.0% sodium pentobarbital (30 mg/kg). All placentae and fetuses from each rat were isolated and weighed. For downstream analyses, the single placenta whose weight was closest to the rat-specific mean was chosen. All procedures were approved by the China Agricultural University Animal Care and Use Committee (AW21804202-1–2).

Cell culture and chemicals

PIECs were obtained from Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. Mycoplasma was tested negative via a MycoBlue mycoplasma detection kit (D101, Vazyme). The following chemical reagents were used: methionine (M9625, Sigma-Aldrich), aminooxyacetic acid hemihydrochloride (AOAA, HY-107994, MCE), ethionine (HY-W017200, MCE), and axitinib (HY-10065, MCE).

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) and adenovirus vector transfection

For the knockdown assay, PIECs were transfected with Lipofectamine™ 3000 transfection reagent (L3000150, Thermo Fisher Scientific) as instructed by the manufacturer. Briefly, cells were grown to 50% confluence in 6-well plates, followed by transfection with 20 nM control siRNA or siRNA targeting CTNNB1 for 6 h. Then, the medium was replaced with RPMI 1640 containing 10% fetal bovine serum, and the cells were incubated for 48 h. Quantitative real-time polymerase reaction (RT-PCR) and western blot were conducted to assess the expression of CTNNB1.

For adenovirus transfection, PIECs were plated in 6-well plates, and at 70%−80% confluence, the cells were infected with adenovirus at a multiplicity of infection of 50. After infection for 6 h, the medium was replaced with RPMI 1640 containing 10% fetal bovine serum, and the mixture was incubated for 48 h. RT-PCR and western blot were conducted to assess the expression of CTNNB1.

The siRNA and Ad-CTNNB1 were purchased from GenePharma Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The siRNA oligo sequences (Table S1), primers for RT-PCR (Table S2) and antibodies (Table S3) used were listed Supplementary Tables.

RNA extraction and RT-PCR

Six rat placentae per group were selected for gene-expression analysis. Total RNA was extracted from placenta or PIECs via TRIzol reagent (9109, Takara), and single-strand cDNA was reverse transcribed via the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (RR047A, Takara). RT-PCR was performed via a One Step TB Green® PrimeScript™ RT-PCR Kit II (RR086A, Takara), and the plates were read with a Roche RT-PCR PCR System. The primers used for RT-PCR analysis are listed in Table S2.

Oxidative system parameters

The ROS levels in rat placenta and cells were measured according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China).

MDA and GSH levels in placentae and cells treated with methionine, AOAA or Rosup were determined via commercial kits (Jiangsu Meibiao Biotech Co., Jiangsu, China; Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China).

The total protein content of placentae and cells treated with methionine, AOAA or Rosup was measured via a BCA protein quantification kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The placental and cellular MDA and GSH levels were normalized to total protein.

Tube formation assay

PIECs were plated in 96-well plates precoated with 50 µL of Matrigel (Corning, USA) and incubated with medium supplemented with methionine, AOAA or Rosup at 37 °C. The images were captured using an Etaluma LS850 automatic live-cell monitoring system (Bio-Beach Co., Ltd.) at 2, 4, 6 and 8 h. The results of tube formation were analyzed via ImageJ software.

Transwell assay

PIECs were collected and suspended in serum-free medium. The cells were subsequently seeded into the upper chamber, followed by the addition of medium supplemented methionine, AOAA or Rosup to the lower chamber. Next, the PIECs were incubated for 24 h and then stained with crystal violet solution. Images were captured via an Olympus inverted microscope and analyzed via ImageJ software.

Western blot

Proteins from placentae and cells were obtained by using lysis buffer. The protein concentrations were determined with a BCA protein quantification kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Approximately 30 µg of protein per sample was transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA) after loading. The samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody. The membranes were then thoroughly washed with TBST and incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. After the proteins were incubated with ECL reagents, the blots were visualized with a ChemiScope system. Quantitative analysis of protein expression levels was conducted via ImageJ software. The membranes were not reused for multiple antibodies. The antibodies used are listed in Table S3.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

ChIP analysis was carried out via the BeyoChIP™ ChIP Assay Kit (Beijing, Beyotime). Briefly, PIECs treated with methionine were digested and crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at 37 °C. The reaction was stopped by adding glycine solution for 5 min. The cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and resuspended in SDS lysis buffer for 10 min. Crosslinked DNA was sheared by sonication into 200–1000 bp fragments, as evaluated by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. A volume of 1.8 mL of ChIP dilution buffer was added to 0.2 mL of chromatin fragments. Then, 0.2 mL of the chromatin fragments was saved for later use as no precipitated total chromatin (input). Fifty microlitres of protein A + G magnetic beads/salmon sperm DNA was added to the remaining chromatin fragments and incubated slowly at 4 °C for 30 min. The supernatants were incubated with 1 µg of anti-CTNNB1 antibody overnight at 4 °C, and normal rabbit IgG was used as a negative control for nonspecific immunoprecipitation. The chromatin-antibody complexes were incubated with 80 µL of Protein A + G Magnetic Beads/Salmon Sperm DNA for 1 h at 4 °C. The antibody/DNA complexes on the beads were collected by centrifugation, and the beads were washed in a series of cold buffers in the following order: low-salt immune complex wash buffer, high-salt immune complex wash buffer, LiCl immune complex wash buffer and TE buffer. The beads were suspended in elution buffer (1% SDS, 0.1 M NaHCO3), and the precipitated protein/DNA complexes were eluted from the antibodies/beads. The crosslinking of protein/DNA complexes was reversed to free the DNA by the addition of 5 M NaCl and incubation of the mixture at 65 °C for 4 h, followed by incubation with 0.5 M EDTA, 1 M Tris-HCl and 20 mg/mL proteinase K at 45 °C for 1 h. Finally, the DNA was purified via a PCR Clean Up Kit/DNA Purification Kit with Magnetic Beads (Beijing, Beyotime) and sequenced at Novogene Co., Ltd.

RNA sequencing

The quality and concentration of RNA were determined via an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer. Libraries were prepared according to the NEBNext R Ultra TM RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (#E7530L, NEB). To guarantee data quality, a useful Perl script was used to filter the original data. The reference genomes and the annotation files were downloaded from the ENSEMBL database. Bowtie2 v2.2.3 was used to construct the genome index, and the clean data were then aligned to the reference genome via HISAT2 v2.1.0. The read count for each gene in each sample was determined via HTSeq v0.6.0, and fragments per kilobase million mapped reads (FPKM) were then calculated to estimate the expression level of the genes in each sample. DEGseq was used for differential gene expression analysis between two samples with nonbiological replicates. A P value was assigned to each gene and adjusted by Benjamini and Hochberg’s approach to control for the false discovery rate.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were expressed as the mean ± S.E.M. of at least three independent experiments. All error bars shown in the figures represent biological variation. Statistical significance and charts were created via GraphPad Prism 9.5. Comparisons between two datasets were performed via Student’s t test. Some datasets were analyzed via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc multiple comparisons test. The level of statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Methionine enhances the transsulfuration pathway and reduces oxidative stress in the placenta during pregnancy

To investigate the effects of methionine on placental development, pregnant rats were fed a methionine-free or 1.0% methionine-supplemented diet and placentae were collected on days 12, 14, and 18 of pregnancy (Fig. 1A). Compared with that of the control group, placental weight was significantly greater on days 12, 14, and 18 of pregnancy (Fig. 1B), whereas the coefficient of variation of placental weight was lower on day 18 of pregnancy in the methionine-supplemented group (Supplemental Fig. S1A). In addition, the offspring birth weights were higher on day 18 of pregnancy in the methionine-supplemented group (Supplemental Fig. S1B). IGF1 and IGF2 are key signals for communication between the fetus and the placenta, as well as for placental vascular development and nutrient transport. The expressions of Igf2 in placentae were significantly increased in methionine-supplemented group on days 12, 14 and 18 of pregnancy (Fig. 1C and Supplemental Fig. S1C-1D). In addition, placental efficiency tended to increase on day 18 of pregnancy in the methionine-supplemented group (Fig. 1D). These results indicated that methionine supplementation during pregnancy improved placental development.

To further explore methionine metabolism in the placentae, we detected the expression of critical enzymes involved in the methionine cycle and transsulfuration pathway. Interestingly, cystathionine β-synthase (CBS), the initial and rate-limiting enzyme in the transsulfuration pathway, was highly expressed in the methionine-supplemented group compared with the control group on days 12, 14, and 18 of pregnancy (Fig. 1E and Supplemental Fig. S1E-1F). The product of the transsulfuration pathway is cysteine, which is an important component of the antioxidant glutathione (GSH). To examine the role of methionine in oxidative stress, we assessed the levels of ROS, GSH and MDA in placentae on days 12, 14, and 18 of pregnancy. Compared with that in the control group, the GSH concentration was greater, whereas the ROS and MDA concentration was lower in the methionine-supplemented group on days 12, 14, and 18 of pregnancy (Fig. 1F and H). These results suggest that methionine enhances the transsulfuration pathway and reduces oxidative stress in the placenta during pregnancy.

Methionine enhances placental antioxidant capacity and promotes angiogenesis

To explore how methionine improves placental development, we conducted RNA-seq on PIECs treated with methionine. The results revealed that 311 (181 increased and 130 decreased) genes were differentially expressed in the methionine supplementation group compared with the methionine-free group. Functional analysis of all of the differentially expressed genes was performed via gene ontology (GO) terms, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, reactome gene sets, canonical pathways and CORUM complexes. The results revealed that methionine supplementation influenced mainly organelle fission, the response to oxidative stress, vascular development, the regulation of the cellular response to stress, sprouting angiogenesis, the regulation of endothelial cell proliferation, the regulation of protein processing, the sulfur compound biosynthetic process and the cellular amino metabolic process (Fig. 2A). Notably, GSEA revealed that compared with those in the control group, the regulation of the response to reactive oxygen species (Fig. 2B) and blood vessel endothelial cell migration were significantly greater in the methionine supplementation group (Fig. 2C). These results indicate that methionine enhances anti-oxidative capacity and vessel development in placentae.

Ethionine is a structural analog of methionine, which competitively inhibits many biochemical reactions involving methionine [40]. To verify the effects of methionine on placental antioxidant capacity, PIECs were treated with methionine and ethionine. Compared with the control group, methionine supplementation increased the cellular GSH concentration but decreased the concentrations of ROS and MDA, which was consistent with the results in the rat placenta (Fig. 2D and F). However, when supplemented with methionine and ethionine, the concentrations of ROS, MDA and GSH did not differ from those in the control group (Fig. 2D and F). In addition, methionine supplementation increased the number of migration cells, which was significantly dampened by the concurrent addition of ethionine (Fig. 2G). To further identify the effect of methionine on placental angiogenesis, PIECs were cultured in matrigel and treated with methionine and ethionine. The results showed that methionine supplementation promoted angiogenesis with increased number of tubes, master junctions, branches and total length (Fig. 2H and L and Supplemental Fig. S2A, S2D-2R). Consistently, immunohistochemical analysis of the placental vasculature revealed that dietary methionine supplementation significantly enhanced the placental vascular density (Fig. S2B-2 C). Taken together, these results suggest that methionine enhances placental antioxidant capacity and promotes angiogenesis.

Methionine enhances angiogenesis by reducing ROS level in placental vascular cells

Rosup is a mixture of compounds commonly used to induce the production of ROS in cells. PIECs were treated with methionine and Rosup, and the concentrations of ROS, MDA and GSH were detected. Compared with those in the methionine-free group, the concentrations of ROS (Fig. 3A) and MDA (Fig. 3B) were significantly lower, whereas the GSH concentration was greater in the methionine supplementation group (Fig. 3C), suggesting that methionine alleviated the oxidative stress induced by ROS.

To determine the specific role of methionine and ROS in placental vascular development, PIECs were treated with Rosup and AOAA (the inhibitor of transsulfuration pathway) and the expression of critical proteins related to vascular development were examined. The expression of VEGFA, VEGFR1, VEGFR2 and p-VEGFR2 showed no difference among treatment groups (Fig. 3D and K). Notably, compared with the control group, methionine supplementation significantly increased the expression of PIGF and p-VEGFR1 (Fig. 3D and K). Conversely, the addition of AOAA attenuated the methionine-induced upregulation of PIGF and p-VEGFR1 (Fig. 3D and K), suggesting that transsulfuration metabolism mediates methionine-regulated vascular development. Transsulfuration is a critical pathway for the synthesis of GSH. In the Rosup-treated group, methionine supplementation markedly enhanced the expression of PIGF and p-VEGFR1 (Fig. 3D and K). Consistently, methionine supplementation increased the PIGF and p-VEGFR1 expression in rat placentae on days 12, 14 and 18 of pregnancy (Supplemental Fig. S3). Taken together, these findings indicate that methionine could reduce ROS levels in vascular cells via the transsulfuration pathway, thereby promoting vascular development.

Methionine enhances angiogenesis and migration through WNT3A/CTNNB1 signaling pathway

The WNT3A/CTNNB1 signaling pathway is the critical signaling pathway for the maintenance of successful pregnancy and plays an important role in the regulation of cell invasion, migration and placenta formation. As a result, we measured the expression of WNT3A and CTNNB1 in placentae and PIECs treated with AOAA and Rosup. Compared with the control group, methionine supplementation significantly increased the mRNA and protein expression of WNT3A and CTNNB1 (Figs. 3E and 4A and B). However, the addition of AOAA eliminated the methionine-induced upregulation of WNT3A and CTNNB1 (Figs. 3E and 4A and B). In the Rosup-treated group, methionine supplementation markedly enhanced the expression of WNT3A and CTNNB1 (Figs. 3E and 4A and B). In addition, methionine supplementation increased the WNT3A and CTNNB1 expression in rat placentae on days 12, 14 and 18 of pregnancy (Fig. 4C and D and Supplemental Fig. S3). These results indicate that WNT3A/CTNNB1 plays the critical role in methionine regulation of placental vascular development.

To further identify the function of CTNNB1 in angiogenesis and migration, stable siRNA-CTNNB1 (si-CTNNB1) and CTNNB1-overexpressing (OE-CTNNB1) PIECs were established (Fig. 4E). When the CTNNB1 was knocked down, the number of migration cells (Fig. 4F), tubes, branches, and master junctions (Fig. 4G and J and Supplemental Fig. S4) in vascular cells were not significantly affected by the addition or deficiency of methionine, as both conditions led to a significant decrease. However, when CTNNB1 gene was over-expressed, the number of migration cells, tubes, master junctions, and branches in vascular cells significantly increased (Fig. 4G and J and Supplemental Fig. S4). In the absence of methionine, the number of branches and number of master junctions were similar between the si-CTNNB1 and OE-CTNNB1 groups, while the number of tubes was increased in OE-CTNNB1 group compared with si-CTNNB1 group (Fig. 4G and J), indicating that CTNNB1 promotes angiogenesis in PIECs. Collectively, these results suggest that WNT3A/CTNNB1 mediates methionine regulation of angiogenesis and migration.

Methionine enhances angiogenesis and migration through the PIGF-VEGFR1 activation

VEGFR1 is a transmembrane receptor tyrosine kinase that regulates angiogenesis, cell migration, and vascular permeability through its interaction with members of the VEGF family, including VEGFA and PIGF. To explore whether the methionine-regulated placental angiogenesis mediated by the WNT3A/CTNNB1 pathway is associated with the PIGF expression and VEGFR1 phosphorylation, we performed CTNNB1 knockdown and overexpression in PIECs cells and detected the levels of PIGF and p-VEGFR1. Compared with the control group, CTNNB1 knockdown markedly reduced the protein levels of PIGF and p-VEGFR1, whereas CTNNB1 over-expression significantly elevated the expression of both PIGF and p-VEGFR1 (Fig. 5A and D, Supplemental Fig. S5A-S5D), suggesting that the methionine might improve angiogenesis and migration through the activation of PIGF-VEGFR1.

Axitinib is a multitargeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor that can inhibit the activity of VEGFR1. To test the roles of PIGF and VEGFR1 in methionine regulation of angiogenesis and migration, PIECs were treated with methionine and axitinib. Compared with the control group, methionine significantly increased the number of migration cells (Fig. 5E), as well as the number of tubes, master junctions, and branches (Fig. 5F H). However, upon the addition of axitinib, the number of migration cells, and the number of tubes, master junctions, and branches were significantly reduced, and these effects could not be rescued by methionine supplementation (Fig. 5E H). In addition, compared to the methionine-deficient group, methionine supplementation significantly upregulated the expression of WNT3A, CTNNB1, and PIGF, while VEGFR expression remained unchanged in the presence of axitinib (Fig. 5I and M, Supplemental Fig. S5E-S5H). Collectively, these results demonstrate that methionine promotes angiogenesis and cell migration primarily through activation of the WNT3A/CTNNB1-PIGF-VEGFR1 signaling axis.

CTNNB1-mediates PIGF transcription and translation

To further explore the underlying molecular mechanism of CTNNB1-regulated PIGF expression, we performed ChIP sequencing to examine the binding site of CTNNB1 responsive elements in PIECs and identified direct CTNNB1 target genes in PIECs. We found significant enrichment of ChIP-seq peaks distributed across the genome, corresponding to 67,632 peaks and 84,665 peaks in the control and methionine supplementation group, respectively (Fig. 6A). The majority of peaks fell into promoter regions (23.17% in control and 22.40% in methionine treatment), suggesting a regulatory role of CTNNB1 in gene transcription (Fig. 6A). Then we examined alterations in CTNNB1 binding sites upon methionine treatment. Although 267 peaks displayed decreased CTNNB1 occupancy, 470 peaks exhibited increased CTNNB1 signals. Interestingly, analysis of genes with either increased or decreased CTNNB1 binding exhibited enrichment in protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum, adherens junction and VEGF signaling pathway (Fig. 6B). Motif analysis with Homer was used to investigate genome-wide CTNNB1-enriched sites (Fig. 6C). Among the direct CTNNB1 targets, we noticed that PIGF was enriched in methionine supplemented group.

In addition, AlphaFold 3, a model that combines physical modeling with deep learning technology, was used to predict the interaction mode between CTNNB1 and PIGF. Colors are used to represent the confidence level of predictions, with blue indicating a very high confidence level. As shown in the Fig. 6D, the combination of CTNNB1 and PIGF was mostly blue and light blue, indicating that the prediction confidence of these regions is relatively high. iPTM (0.34) and pTM (0.57) indicated the reliability of the overall model (Fig. 6D). The predicted aligned error matrix showed the prediction position error. The green area indicated the high reliability of the prediction, this result further supported the prediction reliability of the combined area (Fig. 6E). Collectively, these results suggest that there may be a binding between CTNNB1 and PIGF, and the prediction confidence of the binding region is relatively high.

To further confirm the binding of CTNNB1 and PIGF, we conducted ChIP-polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and dual luciferase experiments. Specifically, ChIP-PCR analysis confirmed a enrichment of PIGF (Fig. 6F). In dual luciferase experiments, transfection of CTNNB1 enhanced the PIGF promoter-driven luciferase activity, suggesting that CTNNB1 increased PIGF gene transcription (Fig. 6G). Taken together, these results demonstrated that methionine modulates the expression of CTNNB1, which enhanced PIGF synthesis by binding to its promoter.

Discussion

In the current study, we revealed that methionine could reduce ROS levels in the placenta and enhance its antioxidant capacity through the transsulfuration pathway. Methionine activated the WNT3A/CTNNB1 signaling pathway in placental vascular endothelial cells and interacted with the downstream target protein PIGF to promote VEGFR1 phosphorylation, thereby facilitating placental vascular migration and angiogenesis. Based on these findings, methionine may serve as a novel nutritional strategy to improve placental development and pregnancy outcomes.

Methionine is not only a key substrate for protein synthesis but also a central hub molecule in the metabolic network. The impact of methionine on vascular development is closely related to its multiple functions in cellular metabolism and signaling regulation. Studies have shown that the methionine metabolite S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) can act as the primary methyl donor [42], regulating gene expression through the catalysis of DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) and histone methyltransferases (HMTs) to form an epigenetic regulatory network [43]. SAM-mediated H3K4me3 [44] can activate angiogenesis-related genes (such as VEGFA and FGF2 [45]), enhancing the proliferation and migration of endothelial cells [46]. Meanwhile, SAM regulates cell cycle-related genes (such as CCND1 and CDK6) through DNA methylation [47], promoting the G1/S phase transition, accelerating cell entry into mitosis, and thereby increasing the proliferation rate [48]. In addition, methionine generates cysteine through the transsulfuration pathway [49], which is then used to synthesize GSH, the most important antioxidant molecule in cells [50]. GSH maintains endothelial cell function by scavenging reactive ROS [51] (such as H2O2 and superoxide anions), preventing oxidative stress-induced DNA damage and mitochondrial dysfunction [52]. In this study, we found that methionine supplementation can enhance the expression of the key enzyme CBS in placental transsulfuration metabolism, promoting GSH production and thereby regulating ROS levels in the placenta.

Placental ROS primarily originate from the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC), NADPH oxidase (NOX), and endoplasmic reticulum stress signaling pathways [53]. In early gestation, NOX-derived superoxide dominates, peaking at 7–9 weeks via p47phox membrane translocation under physiologic hypoxia, whereas mitochondrial ETC contributions remain stable across the first trimester [54]. At ~ 12 weeks, the abrupt rise in intervillous oxygen tension triggers up-regulation of antioxidant enzymes (SOD1, CAT, GPX1), shifting the redox balance from NOX-dominated signaling to ETC-driven ROS buffered by enhanced antioxidant capacity [54]. Moderate levels of ROS are crucial for placental development and functional maintenance [55]. Studies have shown that low concentrations of ROS can act as second messengers to activate the PI3K/Akt and ERK1/2 signaling pathways [56], enhancing VEGFR2-mediated angiogenesis signals [57]. Moreover, ROS signals in the placenta may trigger compensatory antioxidant responses and mitochondrial adaptation [58], thereby maintaining normal tissue function in the face of organelle dysfunction [59, 60]. However, excessive ROS can lead to oxidative stress [61], which is associated with various pregnancy complications, such as preeclampsia, fetal growth restriction, and gestational diabetes [10]. In patients with preeclampsia, placental ROS levels are elevated, and the antioxidant system is impaired, leading to increased trophoblast apoptosis and altered placental vascular reactivity [62]. Although antioxidant therapy is theoretically capable of reducing oxidative stress, clinical trial results have not been ideal [63]. In some studies, over-inhibition of ROS can interfere with normal placental physiological functions and even exacerbate pregnancy complications [9]. Our findings revealed that methionine supplementation enhanced placental vascular endothelial cell migration and angiogenesis through ROS regulation, highlighting its potential therapeutic application for pregnancy complications such as preeclampsia and fetal growth restriction, pending further investigation in pathological models.

The Wnt signaling pathway is significantly expressed in porcine placental angiogenesis and trophoblast cell differentiation. The Wnt signaling pathway has a dual role in embryonic development and organ vascularization, including CTNNB1-dependent (canonical) and CTNNB1-independent (non-canonical) signaling, both of which can regulate angiogenesis [64]. CTNNB1 can enter the nucleus and bind to TCF/LEF transcription factors to form a transcriptional activation complex [65]. On one hand, CTNNB1 can directly regulate the expression of VE-cadherin [66] and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [67] to promote endothelial cell migration, proliferation, and lumen formation [68]. On the other hand, CTNNB1 can activate the expression of MMP2 and MMP9, degrading the extracellular matrix (ECM) to provide a necessary microenvironment for endothelial cell migration and vascular remodeling [69]. In this study, we found that methionine supplementation significantly enhanced the expression of WNT3A and CTNNB1, and that CTNNB1 interference abolished the methionine-induced promotion of vascular cell migration and angiogenesis, confirming that CTNNB1 mediates methionine-regulated placental angiogenesis. Methionine serves as the principal methyl donor for cellular one-carbon metabolism. Short-term methionine deprivation or folate-cycle inhibition by methotrexate has been shown to blunt WNT/CTNNB1 signalling and WNT-driven endo-lysosomal flux, whereas exogenous SAM fully restores these responses [70]. Consistently, genome-wide promoter methylation profiling in cholangiocarcinoma has revealed methylation-mediated expression of multiple WNT pathway genes and specifically flagged WNT3A as a methylation-sensitive locus [71]. Thus, methionine may govern WNT3A transcription in placental vascular cells via SAM-dependent DNA or histone methylation.

A large amount of clinical data has confirmed that PIGF is associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes such as fetal growth restriction [72]. In patients with early-onset severe preeclampsia, serum PIGF levels are significantly lower than those in normal pregnant women [73], and PIGF expression in placental tissues is also significantly reduced [74], accompanied by the risk of placental dysfunction and low birth weight in newborns [75]. In this study, the expression of PIGF in the placentae of methionine-supplemented rats was significantly increased. The expression of PIGF in placental vascular endothelial cells of the methionine-supplemented group was also significantly higher. Notably, PIGF expression in placental vascular endothelial cells was significantly reduced in the CTNNB1 interference group but significantly increased in the CTNNB1 overexpression group, indicating that PIGF expression may be regulated by CTNNB1. Therefore, this study further explored the new mechanism by which CTNNB1 regulates placental angiogenesis. Through alphaFold3 protein interaction prediction, we found that CTNNB1 may interact with the key angiogenesis factor PIGF. Further experiments using ChIP-PCR confirmed that CTNNB1 can bind to PIGF to promote placental angiogenesis.

VEGFR1 is essential in the process of angiogenesis [76]. It activates downstream signaling pathways through binding with PIGF or VEGFA, promoting the proliferation [77], migration, and formation of vascular networks in endothelial cells [78]. In this study, we found that in methionine-regulated placental angiogenesis, PIGF promotes VEGFR1 phosphorylation by binding with VEGFR1, thereby promoting placental angiogenesis. When the VEGFR1 inhibitor axitinib was added, methionine could still enhance the expression of WNT3A, CTNNB1, and PIGF, but there was no significant change in vascular migration and angiogenesis, indicating that VEGFR1 mediates methionine-regulated placental angiogenesis. Studies have shown that PIGF can also promote angiogenesis by binding with VEGFR1 to displace VEGFA, thereby allowing more VEGFA to activate VEGFR2, or by forming PIGF/VEGFA or receptor heterodimers. However, in this study, methionine supplementation had no significant effect on the activation of VEGFA and VEGFR2.

In the present, there are still some limitations in our current findings. First, while our rat pregnancy model and PIECs provide complementary mechanistic insights, direct extrapolation to human pregnancy is constrained by fundamental species differences in placental architecture and function. Human placenta is characterized by deep extravillous trophoblast invasion and extensive remodeling of spiral arteries, events that are only partially recapitulated in the hemochorial labyrinthine placenta of rats and are essentially absent in the epitheliochorial, non-deciduate placenta of pigs [79]. Although PIECs provide a valuable large-animal model for studying vascular growth, nutrient transport and epigenetic regulation, they cannot recapitulate the maternal-fetal interface dynamics unique to humans. Consequently, the extent to which methionine-regulated angiogenesis translates to human pregnancy remains uncertain and requires validation. Second, although this study proposed the key role of ROS in methionine-regulated angiogenesis, the specific sources of ROS, their dynamic changes, and their interactions with other signaling molecules still need further exploration.

Conclusions

Collectively, we revealed the molecular mechanism by which methionine regulates placental angiogenesis through the ROS-CTNNB1-PIGF axis, clarifying that WNT/CTNNB1 is a direct upstream regulator of PIGF and verifying the physiological relevance of this signaling pathway in placental angiogenesis. This study systematically validated the role of methionine in angiogenesis, deepening the understanding of the relationship between amino acid metabolism and angiogenesis regulation, and providing a new theoretical basis for nutritional intervention during pregnancy and vascular function regulation.
