Personalized Video‐Based Educational Platform to Improve Stroke Knowledge: A Randomized Clinical Trial
Authors: Christopher G Favilla, Navpreet Reehal, Stephanie R Cummings, Rebecca Burdett, Laura A Stein, Banafsheh Shakibajahromi, Kristy Yuan, Kelly L Sloane, Scott E Kasner
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStroke knowledge is critical to treatment adherence and poststroke outcomes. Here, we aimed to quantify the impact of a personalized video‐based educational platform to test the hypothesis that it improves patient satisfaction and stroke knowledge.Methods and ResultsIn a single‐center pilot randomized trial, all patients with stroke and caregivers received standard stroke education during the hospitalization, but half were randomized to receive access to MyStroke, a personalized educational platform that provided brief videos about their stroke, risk factors, medications, and poststroke lifestyle. Satisfaction, stroke knowledge, and quality of life were assessed 7, 30, and 90 days after discharge. A total of 120 subjects (96 patients and 24 caregivers) were randomized to standard education (n=59) or MyStroke. At 90 days post‐stroke, those who received MyStroke were more likely to be satisfied with the stroke education the received (90% versus 73%, P=0.05) and more likely to correctly identify their stroke cause (67% versus 32%, P=0.003). However, MyStroke was not associated with a difference in self‐reported quality of life (EuroQol Visual Analogue Scale: 80 versus 75, P=0.06) or general stroke knowledge (total Stroke Patient Education Retention: 5 versus 5, P=0.47). With respect to secondary end points, MyStroke increased risk factor awareness 7 and 30 days poststroke, but this difference was not significant at 90 days.ConclusionsThe MyStroke personalized video‐based education platform improved patient and caregiver satisfaction while improving some aspects of personalized stroke knowledge without improving general stroke knowledge. A multicenter trial is needed to confirm these results, clarify generalizability, and target clinically relevant metrics such as stroke recurrence or adherence.RegistrationURL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique identifier: NCT05118503.Keywords: education of patients, health education, ischemic stroke, patient satisfactionSubject Categories: Ischemic Stroke, Digital Health

Stroke education represents an opportunity to empower survivors of stroke and optimize poststroke care. A patient's level of understanding regarding their stroke cause, risk factors, and an individualized treatment plan has implications for medication adherence 1 and proper management of future stroke symptoms. 2 Stroke education has therefore been a focus of stroke quality improvement efforts 3 and is a standard health system performance measure. 4 Still, stroke education retention remains poor. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Less than a third of survivors of stroke are aware of their stroke cause, and only half can correctly identify a single risk factor. 6 , 7 Less than half of survivors of stroke are aware they are at an increased risk of stroke in the future. 9 Patients and caregivers perceive stroke education to be inadequate and a source of dissatisfaction. 10 , 11 , 12

Stroke education is often delivered verbally by the clinical team during hospitalization and supplemented with printed materials. 13 Unfortunately, standard educational booklets may not be sufficiently engaging, may be easily lost, and the material is often not revisited after discharge. Printed materials may also be suboptimal for certain learning styles (visual or auditory learners). Intensive bedside education, customized printed materials, and video‐based discharge education may improve patient satisfaction and short‐term stroke knowledge, but the long‐term benefit is less clear. 6 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Ongoing engagement after discharge, for example with disease‐based group meetings, has proven effective in a range of diseases 20 including stroke 21 , 22 but is resource and time intensive. In recent years, smartphone applications have targeted stroke awareness 23 vascular risk factor management, 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 and stroke rehabilitation, 29 , 30 , 31 but these applications have not directly addressed the inadequacy of patient and caregiver education regarding the stroke cause, relevant risk factors, and medications. There is an opportunity for a low‐cost personalized educational platform to focus on durable stroke education by leveraging ongoing engagement. Improving patient education regarding the specifics of their stroke and treatment plan may in fact have a synergistic effect with other interventions aimed at risk factor modification or medication compliance. 32 , 33 , 34

Here, we evaluate the impact of MyStroke, a URL‐based personalized educational platform, on patient and caregiver knowledge and satisfaction. MyStroke provides patients and caregivers with a curated set of brief educational videos to address each patient's stroke cause, risk factors, prevention medications, and relevant lifestyle issues. We hypothesized that a customized video‐based platform would improve stroke knowledge and satisfaction with stroke education.

Methods

Anonymized data not published within this article will be made available by request from any qualified investigator.

Study Design

This was a randomized, single‐center, single‐blinded, pilot study conducted at the Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania. Subjects were randomized in a 1:1 fashion to received standard of care stroke education or the MyStroke personalized stroke educational platform (in addition to standard education). Some elements of the institutional standard education were integrated into bedside care that took place before randomization. Further, the clinical team was blinded to the study assignment, so patients who received MyStroke could not reasonably be deprived of standard education. Thus, this trial compares standard education versus MyStroke + standard education.

The study protocol and statistical analysis are available in Data S1. All study procedures were approved by the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Review Board. The study was conducted in accordance with appropriate local laws and regulations, the international standards of good clinical practice, and the Declaration of Helsinki. All study participants provided written informed consent before any study procedures. The study was registered with clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05118503), and research reporting followed Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials guidelines.

Randomization and Blinding

All subjects received standard of care stroke education during the hospitalization and discharge process (see “Stroke education”). By 1:1 simple (unrestricted) randomization, half of the subjects also received the MyStroke personalized education platform on the day of discharge. Study arm assignment was determined by the web‐based tool Research Randomizer. Given the nature of the intervention, subjects could not be blinded to their study arm assignment. After randomization but before discharge, a single study coordinator informed participants of their assignment. The same coordinator entered the study assignment into a Research Electronic Data Captureinstrument (data management platform) that in turn triggered an automated welcome message to the participant. The welcome message contained the link to the subject's MyStroke page only if they were assigned to that arm of the study. The Research Electronic Data Captureinstrument that contained the study assignment was not accessible by any other study team members. Randomization may have occurred before or after discharge education was provided by the bedside nurse. With the exception of a single study coordinator responsible for inputting patient data in MyStroke, all clinical and study team personnel, including the bedside nurse responsible for discharge education, were blinded to the assignment. Study personnel were also blinded when assessing end points.

Participants

Eligible subjects were ≥ 18 years of age and admitted at the Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania between October 2021 and December 2022 with a diagnosis of acute ischemic stroke. Subjects were eligible if there was a plan to discharge from the hospital to either an acute rehabilitation hospital or home, and if they had access to an internet‐enabled smartphone, tablet, or computer. Patients were excluded if they had severe aphasia (indicated by a score of ≥2 on National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale item 9). English fluency was required (not necessarily primary language) because the video content was available only in English. In the case that a patient was dependent on a caregiver, the patient was not eligible for enrollment, but the primary caregiver was eligible if they were responsible for the patient's medication management and participated in medical decision making on behalf of the patient. Eligible caregivers do not necessarily need to support all activities of daily living.

Stroke Education

Standard of Care Education

As per institutional standard of care and The Joint Commission quality measures, every patient with a diagnosis of ischemic stroke received bedside stroke education from the clinical team during the hospitalization. Educational topics included stroke awareness and symptom recognition in addition to personal stroke risk factors and medications. This education was provided by the bedside nurse and also incorporated in the discharge education process. Bedside education was supplemented with a standardized stroke education booklet and standardized printed materials that were included in each patient's discharge instructions. Nonstandardized bedside verbal education is also provided throughout the hospitalization by the clinical team (ie, discussion during bedside rounds). The timing of standard stroke education was graphically summarized in Figure S1. Throughout the study period, 8 vascular neurologists were responsible for the clinical care delivered.

MyStroke

MyStroke is a URL‐based application that provides patients and caregivers with brief educational videos via any internet enabled device. At the time of the study, MyStroke was not commercially available. The MyStroke content and platform were developed by the study team based on input from stroke physicians, nurse practitioners, nurses, patients, and caregivers. The library of videos address a range of topics, addressed later. The narrated scripts targeted a fifth‐grade reading level. After study enrollment, the treating vascular neurologist selected the patient's stroke cause, stroke risk factors, and prevention medications via the MyStroke administrative page (Figure S2). Each selection populated an applicable educational video on the patient's MyStroke page. Though each individual video is standardized, personalization is facilitated through the curation of the unique combination of videos. Each subject's MyStroke page consisted of 4 sections:

Stroke cause: Under this heading, patients received 1 video that briefly defines an ischemic stroke, describes the cause of their stroke and how a treatment plan can address their unique stroke cause. The video consisted of narrated anatomic animation. Video length was between 60 and 120 seconds, depending on the cause. An example video (atrial fibrillation) was included in Video S1.

A sample MyStroke page can be reviewed at the following URL: https://www.mystrokeapp.com/patients/jaha_mystrokedemo/.

At discharge, subjects received their unique MyStroke link via email or text message, as per their preference. To encourage content review, a text/email‐based nudge was sent on days 1, 3, 5, and 30 after discharge with a link to their MyStroke page. Participants were given the option to opt out of further texts at any time.

Clinical Data Collection

At the time of enrollment, patient demographics and baseline characteristics were abstracted from the electronic health record. Stroke cause, risk factors, and prescribed stroke prevention medications were also abstracted from the electronic health record and confirmed by the treating vascular neurologist. Stroke cause was categorized according to TOAST (Trial of ORG 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment) criteria and subcategorized by a more detailed set of stroke mechanisms that correspond with tailored educational content within MyStroke. For example, a stroke attributable to atrial fibrillation would be categorized as cardioembolic due to atrial fibrillation. With respect to stroke prevention medication, antithrombotic medications were specifically recorded. Antihypertensive and diabetes medications were generically recorded as such, rather than abstracting the names and doses because these agents are commonly titrated in the days and weeks following discharge.

Outcome Assessment

Outcomes were assessed by electronic survey on days 7, 30, and 90 postdischarge. Patients were compensated $10, $15, and $25 for completing these surveys, respectively. Patients received a link to the day 7 and 30 surveys via email or text message, as per patient preference. If not completed within 48‐hours, an additional email or text message encouraged them to complete the survey. The 90‐day survey was completed during a telehealth appointment with a study coordinator (blinded to study assignment). Prespecified co‐primary outcome measures were assessed during the 90‐day evaluation, and included (1) subject‐reported satisfaction with stroke education, (2) correct identification of stroke cause by the patient/caregiver, (3) total score on Stroke Patient Education Retention (SPER) survey, 35 and (4) quality of life as measured by the EuroQol Visual Analog Scale. 36 Of note, satisfaction with education was quantified by a 5‐point Likert‐scale. Secondary outcome measures included the aforementioned metrics at day 7 and day 90, in addition to correct identification of at least 1 of their stroke risk factors and stroke prevention medications at each survey. Tertiary outcome measures included self‐reported medication adherence and readmission within 30 days and 90 days. To collect readmission data, study personnel reviewed the electronic health record and collected information directly from the participants during the 90‐day assessment.

Statistical Analysis

Summary statistics were presented using mean±SDs for continuous variables, medians and interquartile ranges for ordinal or nonparametric variables, and proportions for categorical variables. Because of the relatively small sample size, treatment arms (MyStroke versus standard education) were compared with respect to demographics, stroke characteristics, and discharge status using t tests for continuous variables, Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney tests for ordinal variables, and chi‐square tests for categorical variables. Patient satisfaction with education was quantified by a 5‐point scale, and scores were compared using a Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test. The 5‐point scale was also consolidated into a binary response (satisfied versus unsatisfied) and compared by a chi‐square test. In this case, patients were categorized as satisfied if they indicated that they “agreed” or “strongly agreed” that they received adequate stroke education (ie, the top 2 categories from the 5‐point Likert‐scale). For the educational outcomes, binary variables were compared using chi‐square tests, and ordinal variables were compared using Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney tests. In the primary intention‐to‐treat analysis, all available data were analyzed without imputing missing data or limiting to a complete case analysis. In a secondary analysis of the primary outcome measures, worst case imputation was applied to all missing data. In considering each of the 4 co‐primary outcome measures, a test for interaction was performed across 4 subgroups: (1) age (<60 versus ≥60 years), (2) subject type (patient versus caregiver), (3) stroke severity (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale <4 versus ≥4), and (4) level of formal education (college degree versus less than a college degree). A sample of 120 subjects was selected based on estimated survey completion rate of at least 40%, which would provide 70% power (setting alpha to 0.05) to detect an improvement in the proportion of subjects who correctly recognize their stroke cause (from 33% to 66%). The 70% power benchmark is uncommon but was selected because this trial was designed as a pilot to yield preliminary data that will aid in the design of a future multicenter trial. A more robustly powered study to detect a smaller difference would have been prohibitively large and was thought to be unnecessary at this stage (ie, for a pilot). Further, the estimated survey completion rate was conservatively based on institutional experience, but the use of text/email reminders and a 90‐day telehealth visit was expected to augment retention. Survey completion rate in excess of 40% would provide greater power. All statistical analyses were performed in STATA/SE version 15.1 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX). For all comparisons (and sample size determination), alpha was evaluated by 2‐sided tests.

Results

A total of 120 subjects (96 patients and 24 caregivers) were randomized to receive standard stroke education (n=59) or MyStroke (n=61) in addition to standard education (Figure 1). Patient demographics, baseline stroke characteristics, and discharge disposition were well balanced between the 2 arms of the trial, despite the small size of this pilot (Table 1). After enrollment, 1 subject in the control arm was discharged to a skilled nursing facility rather than acute rehabilitation as planned. When patients and caregivers were analyzed as subgroups, baseline characteristics remained well balanced (Table S1). When a caregiver was enrolled on behalf of a dependent patient, the patients were older (67 versus 61 years; P = 0.03) and had more severe strokes (median admission National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale 6 versus 3; P < 0.001), as compared with the patients who were enrolled without a caregiver.

The 90‐day survey was completed by 74% of the MyStroke group and 73% of the control group. Of those who received access to MyStroke, 84% accessed the content within the first 7 days of discharge, and 88% accessed the content at any point during the 90‐day study. At the time of the 90‐day evaluation, 70% of patients who visited MyStroke did so more than once, and 34% reviewed the educational content at least once in the interval between 60 and 90 days postdischarge.

The co‐primary outcome measures are reported in Table 2. In this primary intention to treat analysis, missing data were permitted and not imputed (nor carried forward). Missing data for the co‐primary outcome measures were well balanced between the 2 groups (26% versus 27%, P=0.91). Participants who received MyStroke were more likely to correctly identify their stroke cause and be satisfied with their stroke education 90 days after discharge. There was no significant difference in the total SPER score or self‐reported quality of life. The analysis of the primary outcome measures was not affected by secondarily limiting the analysis to the 88% who accessed the educational content. There were also no differences in the co‐primary outcome measures when comparing those who did (versus did not) revisit the educational content during the final month of the study (ie, between days 60 and 90). For each of the co‐primary outcome measures, there was no significant interaction of age (dichotomized <60 versus ≥60 years), stroke severity (dichotomized <4 versus ≥4), or subject type (patient versus caregiver), Figure S3. However, among those with less formal education (ie, without a college degree), MyStroke was of greater benefit with respect to self‐reported satisfaction with stroke education (odds ratio [OR], 11.42 versus 0.42; P for interaction 0.003; Figure S3). In a secondary analysis of the co‐primary outcomes measures, worst case imputation was applied to missing data (Table S2), and the results of this analysis did not significantly differ from that of the primary analysis.

Discussion

In this pilot randomized trial, personalized video‐based education via the MyStroke platform improved satisfaction with some aspects of stroke education, most notably awareness of one's stroke cause. In contrast to many postdischarge educational interventions, these benefits were durable, perhaps attributable to its continued accessibility after discharge. Importantly, general stroke knowledge (total SPER score) and quality of life were not affected by MyStroke. However, subjects who received MyStroke were more likely to recognize their stroke risk factors 7 and 30 days after discharge, but this difference was nonsignificant at 90 days, which raises the possibility that a higher degree of ongoing engagement may be necessary. A patient's baseline formal education may mediate the effect of MyStroke, with greater satisfaction reported among those without a college education, but such a subgroup analysis should be interpretated with caution and requires validation. To address the potential generalizability of these results, additional study is warranted in a diverse multicenter population, with an emphasis not just on patient knowledge and patient‐reported outcomes, but also medication compliance, stroke recurrence, and postdischarge health care use.

A range of educational approaches have been studied with goal of improving stroke knowledge after hospital discharge. Generic print‐based materials are commonly used despite being largely perceived to be inadequate and unengaging. Personalized written material is more patient centered but still limited by the nature of the medium and prone to be lost or discarded during transitions in care. Video‐based content may be more effective than text‐based education for a wide range of patients, including patients with stroke. 15 , 37 , 38 Intensive face‐to‐face education is a more active process and well received by patients, but it is resource intensive and of limited long‐term value. 3 Ongoing access and engagement with educational materials is critical to sustaining any potential educational benefit. 22 , 39 MyStroke is longitudinally accessible and leverages automated nudges to encourage ongoing engagement. Patients can share their unique URL with family members or friends, which may enhance stroke education throughout a patient's support network. MyStroke is easily scalable as a patient's page can be customized in less than a minute via any internet‐connected device. The vast majority of MyStroke patients used the content after discharge, most of whom reviewed material more than once. About one‐third revisited content more than 2 months after discharge, despite no nudges beyond 30‐days postdischarge. This significant minority reveals willingness on behalf of survivors of stroke and might be improved upon by augmenting accessibility or providing additional nudges. However, within this study, revisiting educational content more than 2 months postdischarge was not associated with a difference in stroke knowledge at 90 days. The optimal timing of engagement requires clarification, and it is possible that the type of content (or level of detail) may need to evolve over time to mirror a patient's evolving experience.

Several smartphone applications have been studied in poststroke care but largely focused on generic stroke awareness, 23 risk factor management, 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 and rehabilitation. 29 , 30 , 31 The focus of the current study was instead on personalized stroke education to addressed inadequate knowledge regarding one's stroke cause, relevant risk factors, and medications. In prior work, a better understanding of one's stroke risk factors and the basis of the treatment plan was associated with favorable lifestyle modifications 32 and medication adherence. 33 , 34 Although the goal of MyStroke was to target personalized elements of stroke education, the platform did not include material to bolster general stroke awareness or stroke symptom recognition. One of the co‐primary outcome measures, the total SPER score, is heavily dependent on general stroke awareness, so perhaps not unexpectedly MyStroke failed to affect this measure. There is an opportunity to develop or adapt content to address this gap in the current MyStroke educational platform as awareness (symptom recognition and awareness of the time sensitive nature of stroke) may affect how patients respond to future stroke symptoms.

As noted, MyStroke was of greater benefit with respect to satisfaction among subjects with less formal education (ie, without a college degree). Although the interaction terms were statistically nonsignificant, the point estimates raise the possibility that those with less formal education derive greater benefit with respect to stroke cause awareness and self‐reported quality of life (P for interaction 0.06 and 0.08, respectively). Given the limited sample size, subgroup results should be interpreted cautiously. Strong conclusions should not be drawn from any subgroup, but these observations may encourage future work to validate these findings or perhaps tailor the educational intervention to a subgroup of interest. Quality of life may not initially seem to be a relevant end point given the nature of the intervention, but prior work has demonstrated an association between level of stroke knowledge and quality of life. 40 , 41 , 42 Further, less formal education has previously been associated with lower health‐related quality of life after a stroke, 43 , 44 highlighting this subgroup as a vulnerable population that stands to derive greater benefit from an educational intervention.

Despite the growing recognition of the importance of patient‐centered outcome measures, medication compliance, readmissions, and stroke recurrence represent key quality metrics that stand to not only improve long‐term patient outcomes but also reduce health care costs. Still, satisfaction may be of independent value to many health care systems as the patient experience may affect value‐based reimbursement. The current study lacked power to detect potentially meaningful differences in readmissions and stroke recurrence. A future trial may target these end points and clarify the cause of stroke recurrence/readmission to understand the mechanism by which patient knowledge might contribute to this clinically relevant outcome. If future work intends to reduce readmissions or stroke recurrence, additional educational content may be needed to improve risk factor management, optimize health system use, or target medication adherence more aggressively (self‐reported medication adherence was not altered by the current iteration of MyStroke). Importantly, readmission may bias the assessments of knowledge if additional education is administered during the hospitalization. For nonstroke readmissions this may be most relevant to education regarding vascular risk factors and medications. This should not meaningfully detract from overarching findings because this would bias toward the null, and differences in stroke knowledge were also noted at day 7 and 30 (favoring MyStroke) despite the fact that the 30‐day readmission rate was well balanced.

There are several opportunities to improve upon the MyStroke platform. First, video content could be expanded upon. Videos were designed to be very brief to first and foremost target awareness and therefore emphasize limited teaching points. Expanded video content could be developed, and links to supplementary content could be provided at the end of each video. A typical case could be used as a model to support a wider range of learning styles. Improving patient self‐management may require more than education, but improving patient knowledge and awareness is an important step toward improving self‐management. In fact, stroke education has previously been shown improve self‐efficacy and quality of life, 45 whereas improving medication knowledge and awareness may support adherence. 46 Still, leveraging interventions that go beyond education may more clearly benefit long‐term risk factor management and medication adherence. For example, blood pressure and glucose tracking can be embedded within the relevant sections and may be critical to bridge the gap between knowledge/awareness and improved risk factor management. Additional tools could provide medication reminders or otherwise support adherence and provide a mechanism by which to notify care teams if medication concerns arise. The potential utility of artificial intelligence‐based chatbots is rapidly evolving in health care, and future work may consider such a tool to address a range of patient and caregiver follow‐up questions. Feedback from key stakeholders, most notably patients and caregivers, will be critical to developing platform improvements.

When expanding content or integrating additional modules, it may be important to consider relevant theories of disease self‐management. 47 For example, the Individual and Family Self‐Management Theory provides a framework the considers 3 dimensions: (1) context, (2) process, and (3) outcomes. 48 Context was extensively considered in the development of MyStroke, in which the content was highly applicable to the individual patient, accessible, and simplistic. In fact, personalization can be of great benefit with self‐management interventions. 49 However, the current platform does not account for the manner in which context evolves over time (ie, relevant issues and needs may be different 1 week versus 2 months postdischarge). A key element of process is knowledge, which is of course the primary focus of MyStroke, but future improvements could better support self‐regulation (goal setting, self‐monitoring/evaluation). Finally, outcomes could be structured in a way that allows proximal outcomes, such as engagement or medication adherence, in order to facilitate more distal outcomes, such as blood pressure control, stroke prevention, and quality of life.

Interpreting the study results requires careful consideration of the standard education given to all patients. As noted, this study reports the benefit of MyStroke as a supplement to standard education (rather than in lieu of standard education). This design was justified because some of the standard education was administered before randomization, so it was infeasible to entirely withhold standard education from those assigned to received MyStroke (high likelihood of contamination bias). Although some elements of the standard education at this single center were standardized (nursing educational topics, discharge printed materials, and stroke booklet), the bedside education provided by treating physicians was not standardized. Over the course of the study period, 8 different vascular neurologists participated in the inpatient clinical care provided, so its possible the quality and quantity of education provided varied across providers. This may affect stroke knowledge, but randomization should neutralize potential differences in the 2 study arms. Participants were informed of their study arm assignment on the day of discharge, which may have taken place before standard discharge education. If a patient was informed of their assignment to the MyStroke arm of the study, this may have negatively influenced their level of engagement with standard discharge education or made them less likely to read the standard stroke booklet. However, this limitation, if relevant, would have biased the results toward the null.

This study has several additional limitations. The digital divide may limit not just broad use of MyStroke but affected study eligibility. The relevance of this limitation may vary regionally. The small sample size is underpowered to detect potentially meaningful differences in medication awareness and risk factor knowledge. In fact, the sample size was selected based on a 70% power benchmark, which was selected because of the pilot nature of the study, with the goal of leveraging these observations to design a larger multicenter trial. Thus, even though conclusions should be limited, even nonsignificant difference may be preliminarily informative. Noncompliance was self‐reported, which although validated, may be less reliable than pharmacy record review or other objective approaches. In fact, noncompliance rates were very low, raising the possibility of inaccurate reporting or a highly engaged and thus compliant population. The single‐center design limits generalizability, particularly to stroke centers with different patient demographics and different approaches to education of patients with stroke and caregivers. Content was available only in English, so translated content is needed to study the intervention in a more diverse population. Study participants had relatively mild strokes, so it is unclear if the educational intervention is as accessible or effective in patients with more severe deficits. About two thirds of patients completed all follow‐up surveys, which far exceeded expectations, but one might hypothesize that those who responded were more engaged and therefore more likely to benefit from the intervention. Encouragingly, retention was similar in both arms of the trial. One cannot draw any clear conclusions regarding the potential benefit of MyStroke to caregivers, but given the frequent reliance on caregivers, this should be a focus of future work. MyStroke accessibility and engagement was self‐reported, but future use of analytics may more precisely characterize platform use. Future work should quantify health literacy among participants to determine if formal education status as described in this study is a marker of health literacy. The MyStroke educational content largely focuses on cause, risk factors, and meds, so there is an opportunity to refine the educational content to improve stroke symptom recognition. Baseline knowledge was not assessed at the time of enrollment because there was concern that it could bias the 7‐day assessment. Further, the use of randomization should have minimized any potential differences in baseline knowledge between the 2 arms of the trial. Still, assessing baseline knowledge in future work could help to more thoroughly characterize the trajectory of stroke knowledge and clarify the temporal impact of the intervention.

Conclusions

The MyStroke personalized, video‐based education platform improved satisfaction with stroke education while improving participants' knowledge of the stroke cause 90 days postdischarge. At earlier timepoints, MyStroke was associated with improved vascular risk factor awareness, but this was not durable at 90 days. Despite some benefit with respect to personalized stroke knowledge, MyStroke did not improve general stroke knowledge, so there are opportunities to refine the educational content to target stroke awareness or other meaningful education metrics. The benefit of MyStroke was particularly evident in the subgroup of participants with less formal education. Subgroup findings should be interpreted cautiously in the context of a relatively small pilot trial, but this raises the possibility that the intervention may be of particular value to vulnerable and underserved population. A larger multicenter trial is warranted to confirm the observations reported here, provide greater generalizability, and target clinically relevant metrics such as stroke recurrence and medication compliance.
