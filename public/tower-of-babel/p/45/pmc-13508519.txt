Ultrabroadband Multiplex CARS Imaging Reveals Distinct Protein- and Lipid-Rich Domains in Arbuscules
Authors: Amu Sofue, Naoya Takeda, Shinsuke Shigeto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi form mutualistic symbioses with a wide range of terrestrial plants, in which they exchange inorganic nutrients taken up from the soil for plant-derived photosynthates such as sugars and lipids. This nutrient exchange occurs exclusively at arbusculeshighly branched hyphal structures that develop in the plant root cortex. Although the molecular mechanisms underlying arbuscule development and function have been extensively studied using targeted approaches based on transgenic plants and fluorescence microscopy, directly visualizing the various molecular components crucial for arbuscule function using nontargeted approaches remains a major challenge. Here, we demonstrate that ultrabroadband multiplex coherent anti-Stokes Raman scattering (CARS) microspectroscopy enables two- and three-dimensional imaging of arbuscule-containing regions in plant roots. Using genetically transformed Lotus japonicus roots colonized by the AM fungus Rhizophagus irregularis, we identify Raman spectroscopic signatures of arbuscules indicative of protein-rich domains. These domains are associated with periarbuscular membrane-embedded transporters that are involved in AM nutrient exchange, including phosphate transporters, as well as other proteins present in fungal and plant cells adjacent to the arbuscules. Additionally, we detect adjacent lipid-rich regions corresponding to arbuscule trunks that mainly contain unsaturated triacylglycerols transferred from the host plant. Our findings highlight the potential of ultrabroadband multiplex CARS imaging as a label-free, in situ imaging tool for studying arbuscules, providing deeper chemical insights into AM symbiosis.

Introduction

Most land plants engage in symbiotic associations known as arbuscular mycorrhiza (AM) with obligate biotrophic fungi of the phylum Glomeromycota, a relationship estimated to have originated approximately 460 million years ago. − This ancient mutualism played a pivotal role in the initial colonization of terrestrial habitats by plants and continues to underpin current strategies for environmental conservation and sustainable agriculture. , At the heart of the AM symbiosis is a tightly coordinated bidirectional nutrient exchange: AM fungi facilitate the uptake of essential soil minerals such as nitrogen and phosphorus, while host plants reciprocate by supplying photosynthetically fixed carbon in the form of sugars and lipids. ,

This nutrient exchange occurs at specialized, highly branched fungal structures called arbuscules, which form within the cortical cells of plant roots. Enclosed by a plant-derived periarbuscular membrane − (PAM), arbuscules increase the contact surface area between the two symbiotic partners, thus enabling cross-kingdom molecular transport. Despite their critical role in AM symbiosis, the molecular architecture and functional dynamics of arbuscules remain incompletely understood. Recent advances in plant genetics and molecular biology have illuminated key regulatory pathways governing arbuscule development and degeneration. , Fluorescence microscopy using transgenic plants that express genetically encoded markers has proven particularly instrumental in studying arbuscules. However, this targeted approach is limited in its ability to simultaneously analyze diverse classes of biomolecules and is inapplicable to AM fungi due to the lack of a robust genetic transformation system.

To address these limitations, there is a pressing need for nontargeted analytical techniques capable of capturing the chemical complexity of AM symbiosis at the cellular level in both plant and fungi. Raman spectroscopy has emerged as a promising imaging modality in this regard, yielding molecular fingerprints of complex biological systems with high chemical specificity. This technique, which relies on inelastic scattering of light by molecular vibrations inherent to virtually all biomolecules, can detect a wide range of endogenous biomolecules such as proteins, lipids, DNA/RNA, polysaccharides, and other metabolites, without disrupting the native physiological state of the sample. For example, we have demonstrated its exceptional applicability to various microorganisms, including yeasts, filamentous fungi, , and bacterial biofilms. ,

Raman spectroscopy has also been used to study plant cells and tissues. − In these investigations, coherent Raman scattering techniques, such as coherent anti-Stokes Raman scattering (CARS) and stimulated Raman scattering (SRS), have been employed, as well as spontaneous Raman scattering. While CARS and SRS provide essentially the same molecular vibrational information as spontaneous Raman scattering, they are superior in imaging speed owing to their high signal generation efficiency, making them especially suitable for rapid, large-area imaging of plant leaves and roots. Previous studies using these techniques have predominantly focused on visualizing and characterizing plant cell wall components, such as lignin and cellulose. − More recently, label-free multimodal imaging (CARS, two-photon excitation fluorescence, second harmonic generation, and other optical techniques) has been performed to visualize the AM fungus Rhizophagus irregularis in Panicum hallii roots. However, that study presented only a C–H stretching CARS image of a vesicle, a nutrient storage structure formed by AM fungi in roots. Thus, the application of Raman-based techniques to AM symbiosis is still at a nascent stage, and Raman imaging of arbuscules has yet to be achieved.

Here, we report ultrabroadband multiplex CARS imaging of arbuscules in root cortical cells. Unlike previous SRS and CARS studies, − ,, which look at relatively narrow spectral windows (e.g., the C–H stretching region spanning a few hundred wavenumbers), our multiplex CARS technique utilizes a supercontinuum (SC) light source to cover a broad spectral range exceeding 1000 cm–1. This enables us to measure the entire fingerprint region of Raman spectra sufficient for detailed chemical analysis. Using the model legume Lotus japonicus colonized by R. irregularis and transformed with a pPT4:GFP construct for arbuscule localization, we successfully identified distinct Raman signatures associated with protein- and lipid-rich domains within arbuscules, allowing us to generate chemically specific images of arbuscules. Our findings not only reveal high expression of AM-specific nutrient transporters in the PAM and the periarbuscular space (PAS) and accumulation of transferred lipids, but they also lay a solid foundation for truly label-free, in situ imaging of arbuscules and other AM symbiotic structures.

Results

Identification of Arbuscule-Containing Root Cells Using GFP Fluorescence

As shown in Figure A, we prepared thin sections of R. irregularis-infected transgenic L. japonicus roots for multiplex CARS imaging experiments (see Materials and Methods for details). Although our ultimate goal is to achieve label-free vibrational imaging of arbuscules in intact root samples, this remains challenging because the precise localization of arbuscules is difficult through bright-field observation alone. Without the use of a reliable method to locate arbuscules, it would be impracticable to determine regions of interest (ROIs) for CARS imaging, given that arbuscule’s vibrational spectroscopic signatures are entirely unknown at the moment. Therefore, in this initial attempt to visualize arbuscules using CARS microspectroscopy, we leveraged green fluorescent protein (GFP) labeling to identify arbuscule-containing regions in the roots at the expense of the label-free nature of the technique. We employed a transgenic L. japonicus that contains GFP gene under the control of AM induced gene Phosphate transporter 4 (PT4) promoter. We sectioned roots exhibiting GFP fluorescence (Figure B) and sandwiched each thin section in water between two coverslips, one of which was gridded. Subsequently, we repeated GFP fluorescence observation in the thin section sample to locate arbuscules using the grid marks (Figure C). With this biological validation, we were able to perform unambiguous CARS imaging of arbuscules.

Multiplex CARS Imaging of Arbuscules Reveals Protein and Lipid Raman Signatures

First, we imaged an arbuscule-containing region in the root cortex using our ultrabroadband multiplex CARS microspectrometer, , which is schematically depicted in Figure D. In addition to its remarkable spectral coverage mentioned above, this apparatus offers a large penetration depth due to the use of near-IR pump (ω1) and Stokes (ω2) beams, lending itself to imaging root samples. Further details of the apparatus can be found in Materials and Methods.

Figure A shows a bright-field optical image of the arbuscule-containing region in the root cortex. Within the scanned area (indicated by a white box in Figure A), only an intraradical hypha is discernible, indicating the difficulty of identifying arbuscules without prior knowledge obtained through staining or fluorescence labeling. Figure B displays the imaginary part of the CARS susceptibility spectra, Im­[χR (3)], acquired at three distinct positions (a–c) within the ROI. Positions a–c were selected from the imaging data to represent areas likely corresponding to a fungal hypha, an arbuscule, and no distinct structure, respectively. These Im­[χR (3)] spectra were retrieved from the raw CARS data using the maximum entropy method − (MEM) and are equivalent to spontaneous Raman spectra. The spectra were further processed to reduce noise using singular value decomposition (SVD) and finite impulse response (FIR) filtering (see Materials and Methods for details).

The spectrum at position a is in excellent agreement with well-established Raman spectra of unsaturated lipids. Prominent Raman bands are observed at 1265, 1302, 1440, 1656, and 1746 cm–1, assignable to the CC–H bending, CH2 twisting, CH2 scissoring, cis-CC stretching, and ester CO stretching modes, respectively (Table S1). − The presence of the 1746 cm–1 band indicates that the detected lipids mainly occur as triacylglycerols (TAGs; see below).

In contrast, the spectrum at position b exhibits a sharp Raman band at 1003 cm–1, which is attributed to the ring-breathing vibration of phenylalanine residues and hence serves as an unequivocal marker of proteins. This assignment is further supported by the CH3 degenerate deformation band at 1454 cm–1, which is slightly upshifted relative to the lipid-associated CH2 scissoring band at 1440 cm–1, as well as by the amide I band, primarily associated with the CO stretching of the amide bond, observed at 1659 cm–1 (refs ,, ). At position c, no appreciable Raman bands are observed, as expected.

Having identified the marker Raman bands, we visualized the distributions of the corresponding molecular components within the ROI using their integrated intensities (univariate Raman images; Figure C). The resulting Raman (Im­[χR (3)]) images at 1746, 1656, and 1440 cm–1 delineate a ∼5 μm-thick fungal hypha exhibiting the onset of branching, i.e., an arbuscule trunk. − Note that although there is overlap with the protein Raman bands at 1454 and 1659 cm–1, the bands at 1440 and 1656 cm–1 show distributions similar to the 1746 cm–1 band, characteristic solely of esterified lipids, due to their significantly higher intensities. The high intensities and spatial distributions of the lipid Raman bands support the assignment that they are associated with intracellular storage materials, namely, TAGs, rather than membrane lipids (e.g., phospholipids). Other neutral lipids such as sterol esters may also contribute to the lipid signals, but their abundance has been reported to be minor in AM fungi compared to the predominant TAGs. − The three images reveal that this structure was filled with lipids transferred from the host plant − and accumulated there before being delivered to the fungus through its mycelium. AM fungi are known to form spherical or oval vesicles within roots as lipid storage organs, typically at a later stage of AM development. , However, based on its morphology, the lipid-rich domain observed in the present study is unlikely to be a vesicle.

The Raman image at 1003 cm–1 differs considerably from these lipid images and exhibits an inhomogeneous distribution across the cortical cell, reflecting the finely branched, tree-like structure of an arbuscule. As shown in the merged image of the 1003 and 1656 cm–1 signals, our CARS imaging visualizes how the fungal hypha develops into the arbuscule surrounded by the PAM. Similar results were obtained from other arbuscule-containing regions (see Figure S1 for an example): in all cases, protein- and lipid-rich domains corresponding to an arbuscule and a fungal hypha, respectively, were observed.

Multivariate Analysis Confirms Protein- and Lipid-Rich Domains

Although the univariate approach presented above did a fairly good job of visualizing the distinct structure of the arbuscule, it does not fully exploit the wide spectral coverage afforded by the current multiplex CARS technique and cannot inherently eliminate contributions from overlapping Raman bands of different molecular species. Therefore, we employed a multivariate approach, multivariate curve resolution–alternating least-squares (MCR–ALS), ,,, to extract the spectra and images of pure components from the Im­[χR (3)] hyperspectral data. Figure shows the results of the MCR–ALS analysis assuming three components (denoted 1–3), applied to the same data as in Figure but without FIR filtering. We deliberately used the imaging data prior to the FIR filtering step because of concerns that the spectroscopic signature of the arbuscule observed above might have arisen from artifacts introduced during the filtering process.

(B) Raman images of components 1–3 (top to bottom). The image size is 25 × 25 μm2.

The spectra of components 1 and 2 (Figure A, top and middle) are nearly identical to those at positions a and b in Figure B, indicating that these components are associated with lipids and proteins, respectively. Notably, the protein marker band at 1003 cm–1 is clearly identified despite the substantially higher noise level in the spectrum of component 2 compared with its FIR-filtered counterpart. Accordingly, the Raman image of component 1 (Figure B, top) closely resembles the univariate images at 1746 (Figure C, cyan), 1656 (Figure C, green), and 1440 cm–1 (Figure C, red). Likewise, the image of component 2 (Figure B, middle) is similar to the 1003 cm–1 image (Figure C, magenta). Component 3 exhibits a flat, featureless spectral profile (Figure A, bottom), which can be attributed to background signals remaining after baseline correction. The corresponding Raman image of component 3 (Figure B, bottom), showing a relatively homogeneous distribution surrounding the arbuscule trunk, further corroborates this interpretation. Taken together, these results validate and reinforce the findings obtained with the univariate approach.

Comparison with a Non-Arbuscule-Containing Region

As a control experiment, we next performed multiplex CARS imaging on a region lacking arbuscules, where GFP fluorescence was not detected. A bright-field image of this region (Figure A) reveals cortical cells and an intraradical hypha, as well as a vascular bundle-like structure. In contrast to the case of the arbuscule trunk (Figure C), the lipid Raman images at 1656 and 1746 cm–1 are localized to the intraradical hypha on the left side of the ROI (Figure C, green and cyan). These images show a line pattern characteristic of lipid droplets trapped and dragged by the focused laser beams during sample scanning. Similar line patterns appear in the Raman image of the lignin band at 1605 cm–1 (Figure C, yellow), which coincide with dark vertical lines visible in the bright-field image (Figure A). We attribute these structures to Casparian strips, band-like formations in the root endodermis of vascular plants that act as physical barriers to apoplastic transport, crucial for pathogen exclusion, selective nutrient uptake, and other physiological processes. Casparian strips are primarily composed of lignin and suberin, with the former accounting for the vertical line patterns in the 1605 cm–1 image.

Cellulose was detected only at the right edge of the ROI (Figure C, blue). This region may correspond to the xylem, which is especially abundant in cellulose. Although lignin and cellulose, as primary components of the plant cell wall, are ubiquitous in roots, they tend to accumulate in specific regions such as the Casparian strips and xylem. Consequently, their Raman images display highly localized distributions. Consistent with the fact that mature plant cells are largely occupied by vacuoles, no characteristic Raman spectra were observed in this ROI, apart from those of lipids in the hypha and cell wall components.

To assess differences in protein abundance associated with the presence and absence of arbuscules, we also generated a Raman image at 1003 cm–1 for the non-arbuscule-containing region (Figure C, magenta). Except for some part within the intraradical hypha, the region lacking arbuscules did not exhibit a pronounced protein distribution, in contrast to the Raman image at 1003 cm–1 in Figure C. As shown in Figure D, the intensity of the 1003 cm–1 band normalized to that of the 1440 cm–1 band was significantly higher (P < 0.001) in the arbuscule-containing region than in the non-arbuscule-containing region. This result provides quantitative evidence that arbuscules are indeed much richer in proteins.

3D Chemical Mapping of the Arbuscule

Owing to its nonlinear nature, CARS possesses intrinsic three-dimensional (3D) sectioning capability. We therefore performed 3D multicomponent imaging of an arbuscule-containing region. Figure shows the spatial distributions of lipids (1746 cm–1, cyan), lignin (1605 cm–1, yellow), and proteins (1003 cm–1, magenta) at five different depths, which visualize the 3D structures of an intraradical hypha, plant cell walls, and an arbuscule, respectively. Based on transmission electron microscopy observations of R. irregularis arbuscules in Medicago truncatula cortical cells, Ivanov and co-workers classified intraradical hyphae into three types: arbuscule trunks (3–5 μm), thick branches (1–2 μm), and fine branches (0.6–0.9 μm). According to this classification, the hyphae observed in Figure appear to be an arbuscule trunk (similar to that seen in Figure C) and thick branches. At each Z-position, the protein signal at 1003 cm–1 occupies more than half of the ROI, which measures approximately 15–30 μm in length and width. Along the Z-axis, the protein signal intensity varies gradually over a depth of 8 μm, peaking at Z = 2 μm. These dimensions derived from our preliminary 3D imaging are in line with the size distribution of arbuscules reported in the literature. −

Discussion

Using ultrabroadband multiplex CARS imaging, here we have chemically visualized arbuscules, the workhorse of AM symbiosis, in both 2D and 3D, along with adjacent intraradical hyphae and host plant structures such as Casparian strips. Despite previous studies on Raman-based visualization of plant cell walls and other components − , and vesicles in AM, this is the first report, to our knowledge, of vibrational spectroscopic imaging of arbuscules. It should be noted that our observations are limited to a single plant–AMF system under specific growth conditions, and whether these findings are widely generalizable across other systems or conditions remains to be investigated. Nevertheless, the Raman signatures of arbuscules unraveled in our work provide a valuable basis for future Raman imaging studies of AM symbiosis.

The most important finding is that arbuscules are rich in proteins, as indicated by the 1003 cm–1 band. This observation was unexpected, as proteins are hardly detectable in R. irregularis hyphae and plant cortical cells under typical experimental conditions. One might argue that the use of GFP labeling to identify AM-colonized cells in L. japonicus roots resulted in the strong protein Raman signals observed, but we consider this unlikely. Prior Raman studies employing GFP labeling have shown no appreciable differences in the protein-dominating Raman spectra between GFP-labeled and unlabeled cells, nor any significant impact of GFP excitation on cellular Raman spectra. , Furthermore, expression of the GFP under the control of PT4 promoter leads to at most 2-fold increase in protein concentration, which would be insufficient to account for the markedly high signal intensity of proteins observed here. It is thus highly plausible that the protein-rich domains in the arbuscules we observed resulted from a plethora of transporters embedded specifically in the PAM. ,, These membrane proteins include AM-specific inorganic phosphate (Pi) transporters ,− as well as lipid transporters mediating lipid transfer from plant to fungus. − For example, MtPT4, a M. truncatula Pi transporter localized to the branching region of the PAM, plays an essential role not only in Pi transport but also in arbuscule development. Likewise, ATP-binding cassette half-transporters STR and STR2, also localized to the PAM, have been shown to be involved in the transport of plant-derived lipids. , Because the PAM is only a few nanometers thick, far below the spatial resolution of our method (∼0.4 μm), the observed protein-rich domain should be interpreted as encompassing not only the PAM itself but also the adjacent fungal and plant cells including the PAS. In addition to abundant transporters, other symbiosis-associated proteins and ribosomes responsible for their synthesis are also likely to contribute to the detected signal.

Another noteworthy finding is the lipid-rich domains within arbuscule trunks. Our data demonstrate that the fungal hyphae connected to the arbuscule were filled with lipids (see Figure C), rather than containing few lipid droplets as observed in the intraradical hypha shown in Figure C (cyan and green). The lipids accumulated in the arbuscule trunk are considered to be TAGs (evidenced by the ester CO stretching band at 1746 cm–1) made from unsaturated fatty acids (evidenced by the CC stretching band at 1656 cm–1). This finding is consistent with existing models of lipid biosynthesis in AM. ,, Further analysis will provide more detailed chemical information on these lipids, such as their composition. The lipid Raman signals were only detected in arbuscule trunks, and the reason for their absence in the fine branches remains unclear. One possible interpretation is that lipids transferred from the host plant are converted into TAGs in the arbuscule trunks and subsequently accumulate there.

Despite the successful visualization of as many as four molecular components (proteins, lipids, lignin, and cellulose) in plant roots infected with the AM fungus, Pi eluded detection. Pi absorbed by AM fungi from the soil is converted into polyphosphate (polyP), a linear polymer of phosphate residues linked by phosphoanhydride bonds. It is then translocated through extra- and intraradical hyphae and eventually delivered to arbuscules. PolyP exhibits a relatively simple Raman spectral profile with two predominant bands at ∼700 and ∼1160 cm–1 (refs – ), and should therefore be readily detectable if present. The absence of polyP signals in our CARS imaging data may indicate that polyP was depolymerized in the arbuscule and released into the PAS as shorter-chain polyP and/or Pi, , potentially at concentrations below the detection limit of our method. To enable phosphate detection, time-course CARS imaging will be needed to examine arbuscule-containing cells at different time points postinoculation. Furthermore, the use of plant samples grown under P-limiting conditions may increase the likelihood of detecting polyP.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings highlight the potential of Raman imaging for the chemical analysis of plant–fungal interactions. In this work, we employed a GFP-labeled transgenic plant to precisely identify arbuscule-containing cells for Raman spectral acquisition. Now that the vibrational signatures of arbuscules have been elucidated, multiplex CARS imaging of AM symbiosis without GFP labeling can be pursued using these signatures as a reference. Furthermore, physical sectioning of root samples may not be necessary except in cases of very thick roots. Our study paves the way for the utilization of multiplex CARS microspectroscopy as a truly label-free, in situ imaging platform capable of probing multiple molecular components involved in AM symbiosis, including exchanged nutrients, thereby advancing our understanding of this intricate mutualistic interaction.

Materials and Methods

Plant Growth and Fungal Infection

Seeds of L. japonicus MG-20 pPT4:GFP transgenic lines were scarified with sandpaper and surface-sterilized with 1% sodium hypochlorite for 5 min. After washing five times with sterilized water, the seeds were germinated on 0.8% agar plates in a growth chamber (16 h light/8 h dark, 23 °C). Spores of R. irregularis (DAOM197198, Premier Tech) were inoculated into pots containing a sterile 1:1 (v/v) mixture of sand and vermiculite, supplemented with half-strength Hoagland solution and 0.1 mM KNO3. The concentrations of phosphate in the Hoagland solution and of KNO3 were adjusted to promote optimal plant growth. The inoculated pots were incubated for 3 days in a growth room (16 h light/8 h dark, 24 °C) to allow for hyphal extension. The seedlings of L. japonicus were then transferred to the inoculated pots and grown for 3 weeks under the same environmental conditions prior to CARS measurements.

Vector Construction and Plant Transformation

The PT4 promoter sequence (1500 bp) was amplified by PCR from L. japonicus genomic DNA. SphI and NcoI sites were introduced at the 5′ and 3′ end of the PT4 promoter, respectively, using the primers with an adaptor for each enzyme site. The 35S promoter/GFP:Nos terminator sequence in pUC18 plasmid was inserted into the EcoRI and HindIII sites of the pCAMBIA2300 plasmid (GenBank accession AF234315.1). The 35S promoter region of this plasmid was then replaced with the PT4 promoter sequence at the SphI and NcoI sites to create the PT4 promoter/GFP (pPT4:GFP) construct. This construct was introduced into L. japonicus MG-20 by Agrobacterium tumefaciens AGL1, as the previously described method. The transgenic plants (T2) were verified by PCR amplification of the GFP gene and by the GFP fluorescence in roots infected with AM fungi. T3 and T4 seeds from the transgenic plant were used in this study.

Sample Preparation and Fluorescence Observation

The 3 week-old AM fungus-infected plants were uprooted from the pots and washed with ultrapure water to remove the soil (Figure A). Root segments exhibiting GFP fluorescence indicative of AM colonization were selected under a fluorescence microscope (TE2000-U, Nikon; Figure B) and longitudinally sectioned along the root axis using a scalpel. Each section was mounted by placing it in 30 μL of ultrapure water between a round coverslip imprinted with horizontal and vertical grids at 150 μm intervals (GC1300, Matsunami Glass) and a rectangular coverslip. The assembly was sealed with Vaseline to prevent desiccation. The mounted sections were then observed under the fluorescence microscope, and AM-colonized regions exhibiting GFP fluorescence were identified using the grid as a guide (Figure C). Immediately after the preparation, the sample was transferred to the multiplex CARS microspectrometer for imaging analysis.

Ultrabroadband Multiplex CARS Microspectroscopy

Multiplex CARS imaging experiments were performed with a laboratory-built CARS microspectrometer (Figure D), which has been described in detail previously. , In brief, a subnanosecond microchip laser (OPERA, Leukos) was used as the main laser source. The laser output at 1064 nm (∼800 ps pulse duration and 33 kHz repetition rate) was split into two portions: one served as the pump (ω1) beam, whereas the other was directed into a photonic crystal fiber (PCF) to generate SC light (typically ranging from 600 to 1800 nm). The near-IR portion (1100–1600 nm) of the collimated SC output was selected with long-pass filters and used as the ultrabroadband Stokes (ω2) beam, covering a Raman shift range of 300–3150 cm–1. The ω1 and ω2 beams were then introduced into a customized Nikon ECLIPSE Ti2 microscope and focused onto the sample placed on a three-axis piezoelectric stage (Nano-LP300, Mad City Laboratories) with a 60×, NA = 0.95 objective (CFI Plan Apo Lambda, Nikon). Imaging was performed on regions well below the section plane that appeared undisturbed by the cutting process described above. The sample was scanned with a 1.0 μm step in the X and Y directions and with a 2.0 μm step in the Z direction using the piezoelectric stage. Unless otherwise noted, the laser power at the sample point was 9.0 mW for the ω1 beam and 1.7 mW for the ω2 beam. The CARS signal at ωCARS = 2ω1–ω2 generated in the forward direction was collected with a 40×, NA = 0.6 objective (CFI S Plan Fluor ELWD, Nikon). After passing through notch and short-pass filters, the CARS signal was analyzed with a spectrograph (SP2300, Teledyne Princeton Instruments) and detected with a CCD detector (BLAZE-100HRX, Teledyne Princeton Instruments). The spectral resolution with a 600 mm–1 grating was 4.4 cm–1. The lateral (XY) and axial (Z) spatial resolutions were estimated to be 0.44 and 3.4 μm, respectively.

Spectral Analysis

As in our previous studies, , the recorded multiplex CARS spectra were subjected to the following spectral pretreatment pipeline: phase retrieval to extract spontaneous Raman-equivalent spectra, baseline correction, and noise reduction. Raw CARS spectra inherently contain the interference from the vibrationally nonresonant background, typically manifesting as dispersive spectral patterns. These distorted spectra hinder downstream analyses such as SVD and band image construction. Therefore, MEM was employed to extract the imaginary part of the vibrationally resonant contribution of the CARS susceptibility, Im­[χR (3)], from the raw CARS spectra. − Initially, cosmic-ray-removed CARS spectra were divided by the water spectrum measured at the same focal plane within the same sample, as a reference for the nonresonant background. The Im­[χR (3)] spectra, which are equivalent to spontaneous Raman spectra and hence linear to molecular concentration, were then deduced by estimating the phase function using MEM. During the phase retrieval, baseline correction was carried out using the asymmetrically reweighted penalized least-squares (arPLS) method. The smoothness parameter λ was set to either 106.7 or 107.1, depending on imaging data set. The resulting Im­[χR (3)] spectra were denoised using SVD, while retaining either 15 or 16 singular components.

Since the SVD treatment alone was insufficient to fully remove high-frequency noise presumably arising from optical interference in the setup, additional noise reduction was performed using FIR filtering. Similar to SVD-based denoising, FIR filtering can avoid distortion of spectral shapes, particularly sharp features such as the protein Raman band at 1003 cm–1, which is often problematic when using the Savitzky–Golay method. FIR filtering has the added advantage of selectively attenuating specific frequency ranges of noise, which was critical in this study. A low-pass FIR filter with a filter order of 101, a cutoff frequency of ∼0.11 relative to the sampling frequency, and a Hamming window was designed and applied to the SVD-treated Im­[χR (3)] spectra. An example is shown in Figure S2 that illustrates the effectiveness of the FIR filter in attenuating high-frequency noise while keeping the spectral shape unchanged.

To generate a Raman (Im­[χR (3)]) image that represents the two-dimensional spatial distribution of a molecular species, the intensity of its Raman band was determined by calculating the area intensity between the band contour and a linear baseline that connects the two ends of the interval defining the band. , No image filtering was applied to the generated Raman images.

MCR–ALS analysis was also performed on the Im­[χR (3)] hyperspectral imaging data, following our previous work ,,, and that of others. , In brief, MCR–ALS seeks a low-rank factorization of a non-negative data matrix M∈Rm×n in the form M ≈ WH, where W∈Rm×k consists of columns representing the Raman spectra of k components and H∈Rk×n consists of rows representing their spatial concentration profiles. Both matrices W and H are constrained to be non-negative, , ensuring their physical and chemical interpretability. The parameter k denotes the number of components to be assumed by the analyst. In this study, k was set to 3 based on the magnitudes of the singular values obtained from SVD. Starting from an initial estimate of W generated from the SVD vectors, optimal solutions for the non-negative matrix factorization were obtained by minimizing the objective function ∥M−WH∥F 2, where ∥·∥F denotes the Frobenius norm. The optimization was carried out using the ALS algorithm (non-negative LS). L1-norm penalties (lasso regularization) were applied to both W and H during the ALS optimization, with a regularization parameter of 0.001. The ALS fitting was iterated 5000 times until convergence was achieved.

The SVD noise reduction, FIR filtering, band area calculation were performed on Igor Pro 9 (WaveMetrics), while the arPLS, MEM, and MCR–ALS were computed using in-house Python code.

Statistical Analysis

A Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare the intensities at 1003 cm–1 between arbuscule-containing and noncontaining regions of the root. A P-value < 0.001 was considered statistically significant. The Wilcoxon test was performed with Igor Pro 9.
