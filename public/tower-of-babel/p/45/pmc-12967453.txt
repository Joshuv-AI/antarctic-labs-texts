Unveiling the association between low-density lipoprotein cholesterol lowering and fracture risk
Authors: Zeqin Wen, Xiaoxiao Li, Yilun Wang, Yuqing Zhang, Junqing Xie, Houchen Lyu, Changjun Li, Jie Wei, Guanghua Lei, Chao Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsHigh serum low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels are associated with increased risk of fracture; however, it remains unclear whether lowering LDL-C reduces fracture risk, and, if so, whether the magnitude of LDL-C reduction is linearly associated with fracture risk. This study aimed to evaluate the relation between the degree of LDL-C reduction and fracture risk.MethodsThis population-based cohort study included individuals aged 40 to 90 years who initiated statins for hyperlipidaemia from IQVIA Medical Research Data primary care database in the UK (2000 to 2022). The primary outcome was hip fracture. Secondary outcomes included composite fracture and major osteoporotic fracture. A hypothetical target trial was emulated to assess the effect of achieving a LDL-C level of 1.8 to 2.6 mmol/L or < 1.8 mmol/L versus > 2.6 mmol/L induced by statin initiation within one year on the risk of incident and recurrent fractures over five years.ResultsAmong 165,242 people with hyperlipidaemia initiating statins (mean age 62.6 years, 51.1% female), the five-year risk of incident hip fracture was lower in the 1.8 to 2.6 mmol/L arm (0.53%) and in the < 1.8 mmol/L arm (0.52%) than the > 2.6 mmol/L LDL-C arm (0.65%). The corresponding hazard ratios (HRs) were 0.77 (95% CI 0.65 to 0.91) and 0.68 (95% CI 0.54 to 0.86), respectively. A similar decreased risk of recurrent hip fracture was observed for the 1.8 to 2.6 mmol/L arm (HR = 0.79, 95% CI 0.39 to 1.58) and < 1.8 mmol/L arm (HR = 0.32, 95% CI 0.15 to 0.66), respectively. Additionally, lowering LDL-C levels reduced the risks of composite fracture and major osteoporotic fracture.ConclusionIn this population-based cohort study, LDL-C lowering was associated with a decreased risk of fracture in individuals with hyperlipidaemia. This decreased risk appeared to be associated with the extent of LDL-C lowering, suggesting that the therapeutic paradigm of ‘lower is better’ could be advantageous for fracture prevention in individuals with hyperlipidaemia.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2026;15(3):227–237.Keywords: Target trial emulation, Real-world evidence, Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, Statins, Fracture, low-density lipoprotein, hip fractures, Hyperlipidaemia, composite, osteoporotic fractures, cohort studies, serum, randomized controlled trial, blood pressure, haemoglobin A1c

Introduction

Fracture is a leading contributor to morbidity and mortality in adults, particularly among the elderly.1,2 It also imposes substantial social and economic burdens, owing to prolonged absenteeism and extensive healthcare use.3,4 Given the trend of an ageing population, the number of individuals worldwide at a high risk of fractures is projected to exceed 300 million by 2040.5 Consequently, the World Health Organization designated fracture prevention as a global public health priority.6

Hyperlipidaemia is one of the most prevalent conditions among adults living in the general community, with a global prevalence ranging from 16% to 48%.7,8 However, 21.8% of patients with hyperlipidaemia do not receive any pharmaceutical therapy.9 Previous studies have illuminated the detrimental effect of cholesterol accumulation on bone at both cellular and animal levels.10,11 One Mendelian randomization study and one cross-sectional study in humans found a positive association between serum low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels and fracture risk.12,13 Statins are the first-line LDL-C-lowering drugs recommended by professional clinical guidelines.14,15 Several cohort studies comparing fracture risk among users undergoing LDL-C lowering therapy with statins and non-users have produced contradictory findings, frequently confounded by indication bias.16-20

In addition, considering that extremely low levels of serum LDL-C can further decrease cardiovascular events, the most recent guidelines endorse the therapeutic paradigm of ‘lower is better’ for individuals with hyperlipidaemia.21,22 However, in a randomized controlled trial (RCT), the intensive therapy group (targets: LDL-C < 2.0 mmol/L, haemoglobin A1c < 6.2%, blood pressure < 120/75 mm Hg) exhibited a higher, albeit non-statistically significant, fracture risk compared with the conventional therapy group (targets: LDL-C < 3.1 mmol/L, haemoglobin A1c < 6.9%, blood pressure < 130/80 mmHg) (hazard ratio (HR) 1.15, 95% CI 0.91 to 1.47; p = 0.241).23 Therefore, further exploration of the association between LDL-C lowering and fracture risk, particularly concerning the extent of LDL-C lowering, holds considerable clinical significance.

To fill this knowledge gap, we conducted a population-based cohort study to emulate a RCT using an electronic health records database. We compared the five-year risks of the incident and recurrent fractures among hyperlipidaemia individuals whose LDL-C level reached the target levels of 1.8 to 2.6 mmol/L or < 1.8 mmol/L with > 2.6 mmol/L within one year after the initiation of statin therapy, respectively.24,25

Methods

Data source

These Methods have previously been reported in a study by Wei et al.26 We used data from the IQVIA Medical Research Database (IMRD), which incorporates information from The Health Improvement Network (THIN, a Cegedim database). IMRD is an electronic health records database containing data from general practitioners (GPs) across the UK. The database provides detailed information on sociodemographic characteristics, anthropometric measures, lifestyle factors, and clinical encounters with GPs, including prescriptions, specialist referrals, hospitalizations, and laboratory test results. Diagnoses are coded using the READ classification system, while medications are coded according to the Multilex system. The validity of IMRD for clinical and epidemiological research has been confirmed in earlier studies.27,28 Approval for this study was granted by the IMRD scientific review committee and the institutional review board, with informed consent waived. Reporting of the study adhered to the STROBE guidelines (Supplementary Material).

Study design and cohort definition

We included participants who were aged between 40 and 90 years, had hyperlipidaemia between 1 January 2000 and 31 March 2022, and had at least one year of continuous enrolment with GPs before entering the study. We used Read codes to define hyperlipidaemia according to previous studies using IMRD.29,30 Of them, we identified statin initiators based on the first record of statin prescription after the diagnosis of hyperlipidaemia. The date of the first statin prescription was assigned as the index date for each participant. Individuals were excluded if they had no LDL-C level measurement before the index date. We emulated analyses of a hypothetical target trial using a ‘cloning, censoring, and weighting‘ (Figure 1) approach to assess the effect of LDL-C lowering, particularly the extent of LDL-C lowering, on the risk of fracture within one year after initiation of statin.31,32 Supplementary Table i provides details on the specification of the target trial and its emulation using observational data.

a) Study design of a hypothetical randomized controlled trial (“target trial”), and b) a diagram of the cloning and censoring process in six hypothetical patients. During the grace period, if an individual experienced a fracture, was lost to follow-up, or died before achieving the therapeutic target (e.g. patient B or D), that person was considered to be adhering to his/her assignment in all arms, and the event was counted in each of the assigned arms. If an individual experienced these events after achieving the therapeutic target (e.g. patient F), only the copy in the “LDL < 1.8 mmol/L” arm was considered to be adhering to his/her assignment, and the event was counted in this arm. The other two copies were censored, and the event did not count in those arms. LDL-C, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol.

Assessment of outcomes

The primary outcome was hip fracture.26 Secondary outcomes included composite fracture (fractures at any sites)33 and major osteoporotic fractures (hip, vertebral, wrist, and humerus fractures).34 We defined fracture outcomes using Read codes from previously published studies.26,33 A previous study has validated the definition with a positive predictive value of 91.0% for hip fractures.35

Assessment of covariates

Covariates included sociodemographic factors (age, sex, and socioeconomic deprivation index score), BMI, lifestyle factors (smoking and drinking status), LDL-C level, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, liver disease, transient ischaemic attack, congestive heart failure, myocardial infarction, pneumonia or infection, dementia, depression, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, fall, rheumatoid arthritis, gastrointestinal bleeding, stroke, venous thromboembolism, varicose veins, osteoporosis, atrial fibrillation, osteoarthritis, chronic kidney disease, anxiety, and cancer) prior to the index date, medication use (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), opioids, antihypertensive, antidiabetic, nitrates, oestrogen, aspirin, thiazide diuretics, loop diuretics, potassium-sparing diuretics, proton pump inhibitors, corticosteroid, anticoagulants, and antiosteoporosis drugs), and healthcare use (number of hospitalizations, GP visits, and referrals from specialists) during one year before the index date. Missing values for BMI category, smoking status, and drinking status were addressed by creating a distinct category for missing data.

Statistical analysis

Participants were followed from the initiation of statin therapy until the first occurrence of the following events: fracture, death, disenrollment from a GP practice participating in IMRD, five years of follow-up, or the end of the study (31 March 2022). We divided the follow-up time into five one-year time blocks starting from statin initiation. We compared the risk of fracture between three weighted comparison groups using a pooled logistic regression model including an indicator for ‘LDL-C lowering‘ and adjusting for the year of follow-up (linear and quadratic term) and baseline confounders in the weighted population.36 The odds ratio generated from this model approximated the hazard ratio (HR) because the outcome is rare.36 We used a robust standard error to compute 95% CI for HR estimates. We estimated the absolute risk difference of fracture over five years by fitting the pooled logistic models with product terms between the ‘LDL-C lowering‘ indicator and the year of follow-up variables. We used a nonparametric bootstrap analysis with 20 samples to compute the 95% CI for absolute estimates. Linear trends were assessed by a pooled logistic model, using the median value of LDL-C in each arm. We performed analyses to assess the relationship between LDL-C lowering and the risk of both incident and recurrent hip fractures.

In addition, we conducted three sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of our study findings. First, we employed a sequential regression method to impute missing data values of the covariates. To minimize random error, we imputed five datasets and averaged these measures using Rubin’s rules.37 Second, we exclusively analyzed participants initiating simvastatin, comprising the largest proportion at 65.8%. Third, we evaluated the association between LDL-C lowering and the risk of both incident and recurrent composite and major osteoporotic fractures, respectively.

All analyses were conducted using SAS software, v. 9.4 (SAS Institute, USA), and a two-sided p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

Among the 889 participants with hyperlipidaemia and a history of hip fracture assigned to the LDL-C > 2.6 mmol/L, 1.8 mmol/L ≤ LDL C ≤ 2.6 mmol/L, and LDL-C < 1.8 mmol/L arms, 500, 332, and 199 adhered to their assigned treatment within one year after initiating statin therapy, respectively. Discussion

In this large population-based cohort study using the GP electronic health records from the UK, LDL-C lowering was associated with a decreased risk of both incident and recurrent hip fracture in individuals with hyperlipidaemia. The risk reduction appeared dose-dependent with LDL-C levels. Similar associations were observed for composite and major osteoporotic fractures. These findings underscore the potential benefits of LDL-C lowering, suggesting that the therapeutic paradigm of ‘lower is better‘ may be advantageous for fracture prevention in individuals with hyperlipidaemia.

Previous studies investigating the association between LDL-C lowering and fracture risk have yielded inconsistent findings. Two cohort studies reported a protective association between statin use and fracture risk,18,19 whereas three other cohort studies found no significant association.16,17,20 These studies used individuals not receiving statins as the comparison group, potentially introducing confounding by indication bias. Furthermore, a RCT reported a non-statistically significant difference in fracture risk between the intensive therapy group (targets: LDL-C < 2.0 mmol/L, haemoglobin A1c < 6.2%, blood pressure < 120/75 mm Hg) and the conventional therapy group (targets: LDL-C < 3.1 mmol/L, haemoglobin A1c < 6.9%, blood pressure < 130/80 mm Hg).23 This trial was conducted in patients with type 2 diabetes, who may not necessarily have high LDL-C levels. Additionally, the control group aimed for a relatively low LDL-C level (< 3.1 mmol/L) and met the other two treatment targets of HbA1c and blood pressure. Considering that the fracture was rare, the relatively small sample size (n = 268) might lack the power to detect statistically significant differences in the risk of fracture between the two comparison groups. In the current study, we found that LDL-C lowering was associated with a decreased risk of fracture in individuals with hyperlipidaemia, and this reduced risk appeared to correspond with the extent of LDL-C lowering.

Several biological mechanisms have been proposed to understand the association between LDL-C lowering and fracture risk. First, cholesterol has been reported to inhibit osteoblast proliferation and differentiation, while increasing oxidative damage in osteoblasts,11 collectively elevating fracture risk. Second, a high-cholesterol diet in rodent models enhanced osteoclastogenesis and decreased bone mineral density.10 As a result, LDL-C lowering with statins is associated with a decreased risk of fracture, and this reduced risk appears to correspond with the extent of LDL-C lowering. However, statins may directly improve bone mineral density by modulating osteoblast-induced bone formation and osteoclast-induced bone absorption.38 For instance, lovastatin has been shown to increase the expression of bone morphogenetic protein 2, thereby promoting osteoblast differentiation.39 Although statins may enhance bone mineral density directly, our findings suggest that the primary mechanism by which statins lower the risk of fracture among patients with hyperlipidaemia is through LDL-C lowering.

Fractures are a major global health concern, leading to significant clinical consequences and substantial economic burden.1-4 Hyperlipidaemia is one of the most prevalent diseases in the general population, with nearly half of patients not receiving pharmaceutical treatment.7,8,40 However, previous clinical guidelines did not address the role of LDL-C lowering in preventing fractures.41,42 Our study found that lowering LDL-C could decrease the risk of incident and recurrent fractures in patients with hyperlipidaemia, with the degree of risk reduction correlating with the extent of LDL-C lowering. Given the high prevalence of hyperlipidaemia,7,8 the large number of untreated patients,40 and the substantial burden of fractures on morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs,2-4 our findings suggest that LDL-C lowering may warrant consideration in clinical practice and treatment guidelines for fracture prevention, especially among patients with hyperlipidaemia who are at high risk of fracture.

Several strengths of our study merit comment. Using a real-world population-based electronic database, we emulated a RCT to evaluate the effect of LDL-C lowering, particularly the extent of LDL-C lowering, on fracture risk among patients with hyperlipidaemia. This approach allowed us to assess the role of LDL-C lowering on the fracture risk while minimizing confounding by indication bias, as all individuals received statins. Compared with previous studies, ours had a considerably larger number of subjects in each LDL-C level category, enhancing the statistical power to detect differences even in a relatively lower rate of incident fracture. Furthermore, the consistent associations of LDL-C lowering with different outcomes indicate the robustness of our study findings.

However, our study also has some limitations. Although we employed target trial emulation with observational data to minimize biases inherent to the study design, such as ensuring new user cohorts, correctly defining the start of follow-up time, and applying rigorous approaches to control for confounding by mimicking a RCT, this approach cannot fully substitute RCTs. Certain covariates, including disease severity, bone density, and frailty, may not have been adequately captured by the variables available in IMRD. Therefore, residual confounding cannot be completely ruled out, as would be possible in a well-conducted trial. Furthermore, medications prescribed by GPs may not equate to what patients take. Additionally, statin initiators who achieved lower LDL-C levels may have received better overall healthcare and taken statins longer than their comparators; thus, we cannot separate the effect of healthcare quality from that of LDL-C lowering. Finally, excluding individuals without LDL-C level measurement before the index date may limit the generalizability of our findings to a population that might receive less healthcare, or experience less severe disease.

In summary, in this population-based study, LDL-C lowering was associated with a decreased risk of incident and recurrent fracture in individuals with hyperlipidaemia, and this decreased risk appeared to be associated with the extent of LDL-C lowering, suggesting that the therapeutic paradigm of ‘lower is better‘ could be advantageous for fracture prevention in individuals with hyperlipidaemia. Additional studies, including RCTs, will be needed to corroborate our findings and explore the therapeutic potential of statins for decreasing fracture risk among patients with hyperlipidaemia.
