The Role of Gut Microbiota in Postmenopausal Women: Implications for Lipid Metabolism and Targeted Nutritional Interventions
Authors: Claudia Reytor-González, Ludovica Verde, Giuseppe Annunziata, Náthaly Mercedes Román-Galeano, Raquel Horowitz, Martina Galasso, Giovanna Muscogiuri, Evelyn Frias-Toral, Daniel Simancas-Racines, Luigi Barrea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose of ReviewThis review explores the complex interplay between menopause, estrogen decline, lipid metabolism, and gut microbiota alterations. It highlights the physiological and metabolic changes that predispose postmenopausal women to dyslipidemia and increased cardiovascular disease risk, with particular emphasis on the emerging role of the gut microbiota in modulating lipid homeostasis and inflammatory pathways. In addition, it examines the therapeutic potential of microbiota-targeted nutritional strategies to restore metabolic balance and improve cardiometabolic outcomes in postmenopausal women.Recent FindingsRecent clinical and experimental evidence indicates that menopause-related hormonal changes and aging are associated with gut microbiota dysbiosis, which may contribute to adverse lipid profiles through mechanisms involving bile acid metabolism, short-chain fatty acid production, and low-grade systemic inflammation. Associations between specific microbial taxa and lipid metabolic patterns have been reported; however, findings remain heterogeneous and causal relationships are difficult to establish due to confounding factors such as diet, lifestyle, and medication use. Nutritional interventions aimed at modulating the gut microbiota—including Mediterranean, plant-based, and DASH dietary patterns, increased dietary fiber intake, and supplementation with prebiotics, probiotics, polyphenols, phytoestrogens, and omega-3 fatty acids—have shown potential to improve lipid profiles and cardiometabolic risk markers.SummaryThe gut microbiota emerges as a relevant contributor to menopause-associated dyslipidemia and cardiovascular risk. While microbiota-targeted nutritional strategies are promising, further longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to clarify causal pathways and identify clinically actionable microbial signatures. Integrating microbiome-informed nutritional approaches into clinical practice may represent a future strategy to improve cardiometabolic health in postmenopausal women.

Introduction

Menopause represents a critical transition in a woman’s life, marked not only by the end of reproductive capacity but also by profound metabolic shifts. The decline in ovarian estrogen production is associated with significant changes in energy balance, fat distribution, and lipid metabolism, which may increase susceptibility to metabolic disorders such as dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and cardiovascular disease (CVD) [1].

This increased vulnerability appears to result from complex, multifactorial mechanisms involving unfavorable lipid profile changes, endothelial dysfunction, and central adiposity. Postmenopausal women commonly display elevated levels of total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and triglycerides, alongside decreased high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), which may promote the development of atherosclerosis and associated cardiovascular events. Concurrently, increased visceral fat has been associated with systemic inflammation and insulin resistance, further compounding the cardiometabolic burden [2].

Beyond reproductive cessation, menopause is accompanied by widespread physiological alterations. Estrogen deficiency has been linked to a shift in fat distribution toward central obesity, impaired endothelial function through diminished nitric oxide availability, and increased vascular stiffness, collectively contributing to a higher risk of hypertension and CVD. Moreover, the gut microbiota—comprising trillions of microorganisms integral to digestion, immune modulation, and metabolic regulation—has emerged as a potential contributor to host lipid metabolism and energy homeostasis. Recent studies have reported associations between microbiota dysbiosis and obesity, type 2 diabetes (T2D), and dyslipidemia, suggesting a possible intermediary role in postmenopausal metabolic disturbances [3, 4].

Estrogen plays a central role in lipid regulation. It enhances hepatic LDL receptor expression, facilitating LDL-C clearance. It also promotes HDL-C synthesis through apolipoprotein A-I regulation and inhibits hepatic lipase activity, thereby preserving HDL particles. In addition, estrogen suppresses lipoprotein lipase activity in adipose tissue, limiting triglyceride accumulation [5]. The decline in estrogen during menopause may disrupt these mechanisms, contributing to an atherogenic lipid profile characterized by elevated LDL-C and triglycerides and reduced HDL-C levels. This lipid imbalance has been associated with the formation of small, dense LDL particles, impaired reverse cholesterol transport, and remnant lipoprotein accumulation, all of which are recognized contributors to atherogenesis [5].

The dyslipidemic profile observed in postmenopausal women is considered an important contributor to their elevated cardiovascular risk. Hormonal alterations, central adiposity, insulin resistance, hypertension, and systemic inflammation may act synergistically in the development and progression of coronary artery disease, stroke, and peripheral vascular disease [5, 6]. Consequently, early identification and intervention targeting these modifiable risk factors may be important for reducing cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in this population.

This review explores the complex interplay between menopause, estrogen decline, lipid metabolism, and gut microbiota alterations. It underscores the physiological changes that may predispose postmenopausal women to dyslipidemia and CVD, highlighting the gut microbiota’s emerging role in modulating metabolic processes. Furthermore, it examines the therapeutic potential of microbiota-targeted nutritional strategies to restore metabolic balance and improve cardiometabolic outcomes in postmenopausal women, while critically appraising the strength and limitations of the available evidence.

Gut Microbiota and Host Lipid Metabolism

Overview of Gut Microbiota Composition and Function

The human gastrointestinal tract harbors a complex and dynamic community of microorganisms, collectively known as the gut microbiota. This diverse ecosystem includes bacteria, archaea, viruses, and fungi, with bacteria representing the most extensively studied component. The predominant bacterial phyla in the gut are Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes, followed by Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria, Fusobacteria, and Verrucomicrobia [3].​

Some of the main functions that the gut microbiota performs include the fermentation of indigestible dietary fibers into short-chain fatty acids (SCFA), the synthesis of vitamins (e.g., vitamin K and certain B vitamins), modulation of the immune system, protection against pathogenic microorganisms, maintenance of intestinal barrier integrity, and the metabolism of bile acids (BA), lipids, and other nutrients. Through these mechanisms, the gut microbiota may influence host energy homeostasis and lipid profiles [7].​

The composition and functionality of the gut microbiota are influenced by various factors, including genetics, diet, age, medication use (notably antibiotics), and environmental exposures. Disruptions in the gut microbial balance, referred to as dysbiosis, have been associated with metabolic disorders such as obesity, T2D, and CVD. However, most of the available evidence derives from observational human studies and experimental models, and direct causality remains to be firmly established [6].​

Mechanisms of Microbiota Influence on Lipid Metabolism

The gut microbiota may modulate lipid metabolism through several interconnected pathways. Key microbial metabolites, such as SCFA, have been implicated in the regulation of lipid synthesis, storage, and oxidation through effects on host signaling pathways and gene expression [8]. In addition, the gut microbiota participates in BA metabolism, thereby influencing lipid absorption and cholesterol homeostasis.

Dysbiosis may interfere with these regulatory processes and has been linked to dyslipidemia and systemic inflammation. Microbial components such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS) have been proposed to contribute to chronic low-grade inflammation. This inflammatory state may disrupt lipid transport and storage and may increase free fatty acid release. These mechanisms are regarded as potential contributors to atherogenesis, although much of the mechanistic evidence originates from preclinical research rather than large-scale clinical trials [9].

Overall, the gut microbiota is increasingly recognized as a potential intermediary between diet, host metabolism, and lipid-related health outcomes [10]. Nevertheless, the strength of evidence varies across mechanisms, and translation into clinically meaningful endpoints remains an area of active investigation.

BA Metabolism

Primary BA are synthesized from cholesterol in the liver and secreted into the intestine to facilitate the emulsification and absorption of dietary fats. Within the intestinal lumen, these primary BA are converted by resident microorganisms into secondary BA through deconjugation and 7α-dehydroxylation reactions. These microbial transformations modify the hydrophobicity and signaling properties of the BA pool [11, 12].

Secondary BA can activate host receptors such as the farnesoid X receptor (FXR) and the G-protein coupled BA receptor 1 (TGR5), both of which are involved in lipid and glucose regulation. Activation of intestinal FXR suppresses BA synthesis and modulates the expression of genes related to lipid transport and lipogenesis. TGR5 signaling enhances energy expenditure and stimulates glucagon-like peptide-1 secretion.

Alterations in the gut microbial community that influence BA composition may therefore affect lipid homeostasis. However, evidence directly linking specific BA alterations to clinically relevant dyslipidemia in postmenopausal women remains limited and largely indirect [11, 12].

SCFA

SCFA—notably acetate, propionate, and butyrate—are some of the main microbial metabolites produced through the fermentation of dietary fibers and resistant starches. SCFA support gut health by maintaining epithelial barrier integrity and exert systemic effects on metabolism. They serve as substrates for hepatic gluconeogenesis and lipogenesis and modulate lipid metabolism by activating G protein-coupled receptors such as GPR41 and GPR43. Propionate inhibits hepatic cholesterol synthesis, acetate can contribute to lipogenesis, and butyrate promotes intestinal health and has anti-inflammatory properties. SCFA also regulate gene expression through histone deacetylase inhibition, affecting genes involved in lipid metabolism and fatty acid oxidation. Their levels are closely linked to diet and microbial composition: fiber-rich diets enhance SCFA production, which correlates with improved lipid profiles and reduced inflammation, while dysbiosis-associated reductions in SCFA are linked to adverse cardiometabolic outcomes [13, 14].

LPS and Inflammation

LPS are components of the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria and are potent activators of the host immune response. Under conditions of increased intestinal permeability, often associated with dysbiosis, LPS can translocate into the systemic circulation in a process known as metabolic endotoxemia [15]. Even low circulating concentrations of LPS have been associated with chronic low-grade inflammation. This inflammatory milieu may interfere with insulin signaling and lipid metabolism. LPS-mediated inflammation has been proposed to impair lipoprotein function, alter the regulation of enzymes involved in lipogenesis and lipid oxidation, and disrupt lipid transport and storage. In adipose tissue, inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor alpha and interleukin-6 have been linked to adipocyte dysfunction, increased free fatty acid release, ectopic fat accumulation, and hepatic steatosis. These processes are recognized as contributors to dyslipidemia and atherogenesis. However, much of this mechanistic insight is derived from experimental and translational studies rather than large prospective human trials [16].

Specific Microbial Species Linked to Lipid Regulation

The specific composition of the gut microbiota may shape its overall impact on lipid metabolism. Certain microbial taxa have been identified as potentially relevant in this context. For example, Akkermansia muciniphila has attracted considerable attention due to its mucin-degrading properties and its reported associations with improved metabolic parameters, enhanced gut barrier function, reduced systemic inflammation, and favorable lipid profiles [17]. Species within the genus Bacteroides, which efficiently ferment dietary polysaccharides, contribute to SCFA production and have been associated with healthier lipid patterns. Probiotic genera such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium are also frequently linked to lipid-lowering effects. These bacteria may assimilate cholesterol, deconjugate BA, and modulate host gene expression in ways that influence lipid metabolism [18]. Some strains have demonstrated efficacy in clinical trials in reducing serum cholesterol and triglyceride levels; however, results remain heterogeneous and are often strain-specific.

Additionally, certain Clostridium species, particularly those within clusters IV and XIVa, are involved in butyrate production and are considered important for maintaining gut and metabolic homeostasis [19]. Overall, while several microbial taxa show potential relevance, their precise clinical role in postmenopausal dyslipidemia requires confirmation in well-designed longitudinal and randomized interventional studies.

Alterations in Gut Microbiota After Menopause

Evidence of Microbiota Dysbiosis in Postmenopausal Women

Menopause has been consistently associated with alterations in gut microbial composition, although the magnitude and direction of these changes vary across studies. Several investigations report that postmenopausal women exhibit lower gut microbiome diversity than premenopausal women, frequently accompanied by an increased Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio [20]. Beyond diversity indices, compositional analyses suggest enrichment of specific taxa, including Bacteroides sp. strain Ga6A1, Prevotella marshii, and Sutterella wadsworthensis, in postmenopausal cohorts, whereas Escherichia coli–Shigella spp. and Oscillibacter sp. appear more prevalent in premenopausal individuals [21].​ These findings collectively support the concept of menopause-related microbial remodeling; however, the term “dysbiosis” should be applied cautiously, given the heterogeneity of reported patterns and methodological variability among studies.

The decline in circulating estrogen levels during menopause is thought to contribute to these microbial shifts, although direct mechanistic confirmation in humans remains limited. Estrogen deficiency has been associated with reduced microbial diversity and increased intestinal permeability, factors that may favor low-grade systemic inflammation. In parallel, decreased abundance of genera such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, which contribute to barrier integrity and immune regulation, has been reported in some but not all populations [22]. Differences in sequencing platforms, geographic background, dietary intake, body composition, and medication exposure likely account for part of the inconsistency observed across cohorts, underscoring the need for carefully controlled longitudinal studies.

Hormonal-microbiome Interactions

The relationship between sex hormones and the gut microbiota is increasingly recognized as bidirectional. Estrogens, particularly estradiol, have been shown to influence microbial composition and functional activity in both experimental models and human studies. Conversely, the gut microbiota may regulate estrogen metabolism through bacterial enzymes such as β-glucuronidase, which deconjugate estrogens and facilitate their reabsorption into the systemic circulation [23].​ This reciprocal interaction has led to the concept of the “estrobolome,” referring to microbial genes capable of modulating circulating estrogen levels.

During menopause, declining estrogen concentrations may alter this equilibrium, potentially reshaping microbial communities. Some studies describe a postmenopausal microbial profile that resembles that observed in men, characterized by reduced diversity and shifts in relative abundances of specific taxa [24].​ These changes may influence estrogen recirculation and downstream metabolic pathways; however, most available data are cross-sectional, limiting causal inference.

Importantly, a 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis including three case–control studies (n = 156 women) reported more nuanced findings than initially suggested by smaller cohorts. At the phylum level, no significant differences were detected before and after menopause. At the genus level, Odoribacter and Bilophila were more abundant in postmenopausal women, while Shannon diversity showed a non-significant trend toward increase rather than decline. Associations between estradiol levels and specific taxa, including Gammaproteobacteria, Myxococcales, and Prevotellaceae, were modest and inconsistent. These results highlight that menopause-related microbiota changes may be subtle rather than uniformly dysbiotic and emphasize the need for larger, well-powered studies [25].

The Role of Estrogen and Androgen Balance in Shaping Microbiota

Beyond absolute estrogen decline, the relative balance between estrogens and androgens may also influence microbial composition. Elevated serum testosterone and estradiol levels have been associated with greater gut microbial diversity in certain cohorts. Genera such as Acinetobacter, Dorea, Ruminococcus, and Megamonas have been correlated with testosterone concentrations, whereas Slackia and Butyricimonas have been linked to estradiol levels [26].​ These associations suggest that sex hormone profiles may shape microbial ecology, although directionality remains uncertain.

In postmenopausal women, reduced estrogen levels combined with relative androgen predominance may be associated with compositional shifts in the gut microbiota. Such changes could influence metabolic pathways and disease susceptibility, as alterations in microbial composition have been linked to osteoporosis and CVD in this population [27].​ Nevertheless, most studies evaluating hormone–microbiota associations are observational and often insufficiently adjusted for confounding variables, including diet, adiposity, and pharmacologic therapy.

Distinction between Age-related and Menopause-specific Microbiota Changes

Disentangling the effects of chronological aging from those of menopause represents a critical conceptual challenge. Aging independently influences microbial diversity and community structure; however, the menopausal transition introduces abrupt endocrine changes that may exert additional modulatory effects [28]. Evidence suggests that some microbial alterations observed in postmenopausal women cannot be fully explained by age alone. Reports of reduced diversity and a microbial composition more closely resembling that of men support a hormone-dependent component [24].

At the same time, many studies lack age-matched control groups or fail to adjust adequately for metabolic status and lifestyle factors. Consequently, attributing observed changes exclusively to menopause may oversimplify a multifactorial process. Future research should prioritize longitudinal designs that track women across the menopausal transition while carefully controlling for aging-related and environmental influences.

Finally, the ability of the gut microbiota to metabolize estrogens through enzymes such as β-glucuronidase reinforces the bidirectional nature of host–microbiome interactions. Hormonal changes may shape microbial communities, and microbial activity may, in turn, influence systemic estrogen levels (Fig. 1) [29]. Microbiota-Driven Dyslipidemia: Evidence in Postmenopausal Women

Clinical and Preclinical Studies Linking Microbiota Composition to Lipid Profile

Emerging research has highlighted the complex relationship between the gut microbiota composition and lipid metabolism, particularly in postmenopausal women. The decline in estrogen levels during menopause has been associated with shifts in the gut microbiota, which may in turn influence circulating lipid parameters. Clinical studies have reported alterations in microbial diversity and relative abundance in postmenopausal women, some of which correlate with lipid profile variations. For example, a study using 16S rRNA sequencing found that gut microbial distribution in postmenopausal women differed according to follicle-stimulating hormone levels, suggesting that hormonal fluctuations may be linked to microbial remodeling even before the full manifestation of menopausal symptoms [30].

Preclinical models have provided additional mechanistic insight. In ovariectomized mice, widely used as an experimental model of menopause, changes in the gut microbiota composition have been associated with alterations in plasma lipid metabolites. These alterations include higher levels of LDL-C and triglycerides, together with reduced HDL-C, partially mirroring the lipid patterns observed in postmenopausal women. While these findings support a biologically plausible connection between microbial shifts and lipid dysregulation, translation to human populations should be interpreted cautiously, as experimental conditions may not fully replicate the complexity of the menopausal transition [2].

Associations with Obesity, Insulin Resistance, and Systemic Inflammation

The interaction between the gut microbiota, obesity, insulin resistance, chronotype, sleep quality, and systemic inflammation has emerged as a relevant area of investigation in postmenopausal populations [31]. The menopausal transition is frequently accompanied by weight gain and increased visceral adiposity, both of which are independently associated with microbial compositional changes. Studies indicate that greater visceral adipose tissue in postmenopausal women is associated with higher relative abundance of Proteobacteria and elevated markers of endotoxemia.

This state, often described as metabolic endotoxemia, involves the translocation of LPS from the intestinal lumen into the systemic circulation. Circulating LPS has been associated with chronic low-grade inflammation, which may interfere with insulin signaling and lipid metabolism. Such inflammatory pathways are considered important contributors to insulin resistance, a common metabolic disturbance during the postmenopausal period [32]. Nevertheless, most available data are associative, and disentangling the independent effects of adiposity, diet, hormonal decline, and microbial alterations remains methodologically challenging.

Identification of Potential Microbiota-based Biomarkers for Dyslipidemia

The identification of microbiota-based biomarkers has been proposed as a potential strategy for the early detection and management of dyslipidemia in postmenopausal women. Specific microbial taxa have been associated with distinct lipid patterns, raising the possibility that microbial signatures may reflect cardiometabolic risk. Reduced alpha diversity in the gut microbiota has been linked to atherogenic lipid profiles characterized by elevated triglycerides and reduced HDL-C.

In addition, certain bacterial species have shown correlations with lipid metabolism markers, suggesting that microbial composition may provide complementary information beyond traditional biochemical measures. However, current evidence is largely derived from cross-sectional analyses with limited sample sizes, and no standardized microbial biomarkers for clinical use have yet been validated [33].​

Nutritional Strategies to Modulate Microbiota and Improve Lipid Profile

Dietary patterns profoundly influence the gut microbiota and lipid metabolism, particularly in the context of estrogen decline and increased cardiometabolic risk after menopause [34, 35]. Among available lifestyle interventions, diet represents one of the most accessible and modifiable strategies with potential clinical relevance in postmenopausal women [36]. Nevertheless, the strength of evidence differs substantially across dietary approaches, and the extent to which microbiota changes directly mediate lipid improvements remains incompletely established.

Barrea et al. report that diverse dietary strategies—including short-term ketogenic approaches and selected nutraceuticals—may attenuate systemic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction, mechanisms closely related to lipid homeostasis [37]. However, most available data derive from short-term studies or heterogeneous populations, and long-term trials specifically evaluating microbiota–lipid interactions in postmenopausal cohorts remain limited.

Dietary Patterns and Cardiometabolic Effects

Mediterranean Diet (MD)

The MD emphasizes the consumption of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, nuts, olive oil, and moderate intake of fish and poultry. This dietary pattern is rich in monounsaturated fats, dietary fiber, and polyphenols, nutritional components that have been associated with beneficial effects on lipid metabolism, inflammation, immune response, blood pressure regulation, and gut microbiota composition [38–47].

Adherence to the MD has been linked to increased abundance of beneficial gut bacteria such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, together with reductions in potentially pro-inflammatory taxa. However, most evidence supporting microbiota modulation derives from observational cohorts or secondary analyses rather than randomized trials specifically designed to evaluate microbial endpoints. These microbial patterns may contribute to improvements in lipid metabolism, including reductions in LDL-C and triglycerides and increases in HDL-C [48], although microbiota-mediated mechanisms remain inferential rather than definitively demonstrated in postmenopausal women [49–54].​

Importantly, high-level randomized clinical evidence supports the cardiometabolic benefits of the Mediterranean dietary pattern. The PREDIMED randomized controlled trial demonstrated that a MD supplemented with extra-virgin olive oil or nuts reduced the incidence of major cardiovascular events by approximately 30% compared with a control diet in high-risk adults, establishing robust cardiovascular benefit at the population level [55]. Although this landmark trial did not specifically target postmenopausal women, subgroup analyses and age-stratified data suggest relevance for older female populations.

In postmenopausal women specifically, a randomized controlled trial comparing an energy-restricted MD with a Central European diet in postmenopausal women with central obesity demonstrated significant improvements in weight, metabolic risk factors, and cardiometabolic parameters after 16 weeks [56]. Nevertheless, microbiota profiling was not systematically integrated with lipid endpoints in these trials, limiting mechanistic interpretation. Taken together, the MD currently represents the dietary pattern with the strongest clinical evidence for improving lipid parameters and cardiovascular risk in postmenopausal women, although the contribution of microbiota-mediated pathways remains to be clarified in integrative randomized studies.

Plant-based Diets

Plant-based dietary patterns, including vegetarian and vegan diets, emphasize plant-derived foods and limit or exclude animal products. These diets are typically rich in dietary fiber, antioxidants, and phytochemicals—nutritional components that may promote microbial diversity and metabolic regulation [57, 58]. Higher intake of fermentable fiber has been associated with increased abundance of SCFA–producing taxa, which participate in anti-inflammatory signaling pathways and lipid metabolism. However, most evidence linking these microbial changes with lipid improvements in postmenopausal women remains associative rather than causal.

In postmenopausal women specifically, low-fat vegan dietary interventions enriched with soy have been associated with improvements in lipid profiles, including reductions in total cholesterol and LDL-C concentrations [59, 60]. These findings likely reflect multiple mechanisms, including reduced saturated fat intake, higher soluble fiber consumption, and the presence of phytoestrogens.

Evidence from a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials included over 30 RCTs involving approximately 2,300 adult participants, primarily from North America and Europe, with intervention durations ranging from 3 to 24 months. Vegetarian and vegan diets were compared with omnivorous control diets. The analysis demonstrated significant reductions in total cholesterol and LDL-C, while changes in HDL-C were modest and triglyceride responses were heterogeneous. Importantly, most participants were middle-aged adults; sex-stratified analyses were limited, and few trials focused exclusively on postmenopausal women [61]. However, most participants were middle-aged adults, and trials specifically focusing on postmenopausal women remain scarce.

Similarly, a meta-analysis evaluating soy and isoflavone supplementation in postmenopausal women included randomized controlled trials with intervention durations typically between 6 weeks and 12 months. The pooled data indicated modest but statistically significant reductions in LDL-C, with variable effects on HDL-C and triglycerides [59]. Most studies were conducted in Asian and Western populations, and variability in baseline lipid status, isoflavone dose, and equol-producer status may partly explain heterogeneity in outcomes.

Dietary quality also appears critical. Whole-food plant-based diets differ substantially from processed vegetarian alternatives in terms of fiber density, glycemic load, and fat composition. Some evidence suggests that strict low-fat vegan regimens may reduce LDL-C but may not consistently increase HDL-C and may occasionally lower HDL levels [62].

Overall, plant-based dietary patterns show consistent evidence for improving atherogenic lipid parameters, although mechanistic contributions from microbiota modulation remain insufficiently characterized.

Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) Diet

The DASH diet emphasizes fruits, vegetables, whole grains, lean protein sources, and low-fat dairy, while limiting sodium and saturated fat. In postmenopausal women, this dietary pattern is relevant because it targets hypertension and dyslipidemia—two major cardiovascular risk drivers after menopause [63].

In randomized and controlled feeding studies involving adults, the DASH diet has demonstrated reductions in LDL-C (mean difference − 0.10 mmol/L) and total cholesterol (mean difference − 0.20 mmol/L) compared with control diets and has consistent blood pressure benefits [64].

Although high-quality trials specifically restricted to postmenopausal women are sparse, narrative reviews and clinical guidance suggest that the DASH diet is a feasible and potentially cardioprotective dietary approach for women in the menopausal transition, particularly given the increased risk of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and CVD at this life stage [65].

With respect to gut microbiota, direct evidence linking DASH diet interventions to microbiota changes in postmenopausal women with concurrent lipid outcomes is currently limited. However, broader diet quality indices that include components characteristic of the DASH pattern (e.g., high fiber, plant foods) have been associated with microbiome profiles linked to lower cardiometabolic risk in observational research, suggesting a plausible indirect link [66].

In summary, while the DASH diet has moderate evidence for improving cardiometabolic risk factors and lipid profiles in general [67], further well-designed randomized trials are needed to establish its effects on gut microbiota composition and lipid metabolism specifically in postmenopausal women.

Low-carb or Ketogenic Diets

Low-carbohydrate and ketogenic diets are characterized by marked carbohydrate restriction and increased fat intake, leading to nutritional ketosis [68, 69]. These dietary approaches have been proposed as strategies to address weight gain, visceral adiposity, and insulin resistance—metabolic alterations commonly observed after menopause [70–72].

Clinical evidence in postmenopausal women suggests heterogeneous lipid responses. A 2025 prospective open-label clinical study involving women with essential hypertension and obesity reported that a very-low-calorie ketogenic diet reduced central blood pressure and improved several cardiometabolic markers [73]. However, the study was single-center, non-blinded, and of limited duration, which restricts interpretation of long-term cardiovascular outcomes.

Although reductions in triglyceride levels are frequently reported with low-carbohydrate interventions, some studies indicate that ketogenic diets may increase LDL-C concentrations in certain individuals [74]. These responses appear influenced by dietary fat quality, baseline metabolic phenotype, and possible genetic variability.

Importantly, robust randomized controlled trials specifically examining ketogenic diets, gut microbiota composition, and lipid endpoints concurrently in postmenopausal women remain limited [75, 76]. Most available studies are short-term (typically ≤ 24 weeks), include relatively small sample sizes, and rarely stratify outcomes by menopausal stage or hormonal status. Microbiota-Targeted Nutritional Interventions

Prebiotics and Probiotics

Prebiotics are non-digestible food components that selectively stimulate beneficial microbial taxa, whereas probiotics are live microorganisms that may confer health benefits when administered in adequate amounts. Both strategies have been investigated for their potential to modulate the gut microbiota and influence lipid metabolism in postmenopausal women [80, 81]. In this population, the evidence base is expanding beyond lipid endpoints to include menopause-related outcomes, although study quality and consistency remain variable.

Several probiotic strains, including Lactobacillus acidophilus and Bifidobacterium longum, have been evaluated in randomized clinical settings and have been associated with modest reductions in total cholesterol and LDL-C [80, 82].​ Proposed mechanisms include BA deconjugation and SCFA production, although these pathways have largely been inferred from mechanistic studies rather than conclusively demonstrated in long-term clinical trials in postmenopausal populations.

Synbiotic formulations combining fermentable fibers such as inulin with selected probiotic strains have also been explored. In postmenopausal women, some randomized trials have reported reductions in total cholesterol and LDL-C concentrations following short-term supplementation [83–85]. Nonetheless, most interventions have included relatively small sample sizes and follow-up periods typically not exceeding 12–16 weeks. In addition, microbiota sequencing has not consistently been integrated with lipid endpoints, limiting the ability to establish mechanistic mediation.

Evidence regarding probiotic modulation of estrogen metabolism remains preliminary. A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial reported that supplementation with a formulation containing strains with β-glucuronidase activity, particularly Levilactobacillus brevis KABP052, was associated with higher circulating estradiol and estrone levels after 12 weeks compared with placebo [86].

In another randomized controlled trial including 56 postmenopausal women with obesity, multispecies probiotic supplementation appeared to modify functional microbial activity without significantly altering overall taxonomic composition or alpha diversity [87]. These findings highlight the complexity of host–microbiota interactions and the variability of responses across individuals.

Additionally, a 2024 meta-analysis of women with overweight and obesity reported that probiotic supplementation was associated with improvements in body weight, glycemic control, and lipid-related outcomes, although results varied by intervention duration and lifestyle co-interventions [82]. Taken together, these data suggest potential metabolic benefits, but the magnitude of effect and the most responsive subgroups remain uncertain.

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of 39 studies including 3,187 women assessed the effects of probiotics during the menopause transition, finding that probiotics can improve menopausal, vasomotor, psychological, and urogenital symptoms, enhance vaginal microbiome health, support bone health, and potentially increase the efficacy and safety of estriol and isoflavones. Meta-analysis of seven studies showed significant improvements in overall menopausal symptoms, hot flashes, psychological symptoms, vaginal dryness, and Nugent scores, although effects on somatic and sexual symptoms were not significant. Despite these promising results, most studies had a high risk of bias, highlighting the need for more rigorous trials, but probiotics may be considered as a supportive strategy in managing menopause-related symptoms [88]. Importantly, many trials do not concurrently evaluate microbiome composition/function alongside clinical endpoints, limiting mechanistic attribution.

Dietary Fiber and SCFA Production

Gut microbiota ferment dietary fiber and generate SCFA, which participate in metabolic regulation. Experimental studies associate propionate with reduced hepatic cholesterol synthesis, while butyrate supports intestinal barrier integrity and modulates inflammatory signaling. However, much of this mechanistic evidence originates from preclinical models rather than long-term clinical trials in postmenopausal women [89].

Higher dietary fiber intake correlates with more favorable lipid profiles, including lower total cholesterol and LDL-C concentrations. Fiber-rich diets also increase SCFA-producing bacteria, although many clinical studies do not simultaneously assess microbiota composition, SCFA production, and lipid outcomes. Consequently, microbiota-mediated lipid effects remain plausible but incompletely demonstrated in this population [89]. Future studies that jointly quantify SCFA (or other functional metabolites) and lipid changes would strengthen causal inference.

Polyphenols and Phytoestrogens

Polyphenols and phytoestrogens are plant-derived compounds that have attracted attention because of their potential health-promoting and anticancer properties [77–79], particularly in the context of cardiometabolic health after menopause. These molecules exhibit antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity and interact with estrogen signaling pathways. They also interact with gut microbiota, influencing microbial composition and metabolic activity [90].

Polyphenols comprise several classes, including flavonoids, phenolic acids, stilbenes, and lignans. Phytoestrogens—such as isoflavones, chromenes, and lignans—share structural similarity with estradiol and can bind to estrogen receptors, with variable affinity and tissue-specific effects [91]. Their biological activity partly depends on microbial biotransformation, which contributes to interindividual variability in metabolic responses.

Isoflavones and lignans require microbial conversion to become biologically active. Gut bacteria convert daidzein into equol and lignans into enterolactone, metabolites with higher bioavailability and receptor affinity. However, only a subset of individuals harbor bacterial species capable of these transformations, including Slackia isoflavoniconvertens and Eggerthella spp., which contributes to variability in cardiometabolic responses [92].

Dietary intervention studies provide additional insights. A controlled dietary trial involving a low-fat vegan diet enriched with soybeans reported improvements in vasomotor symptoms alongside microbiome changes [93]. Similarly, a 2024 meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials found that consumption of unfermented soy products was associated with reductions in total cholesterol and triglycerides and increases in HDL-C in postmenopausal women [60]. These findings support clinical relevance for soy-based strategies, although the contribution of microbiota-mediated biotransformation likely differs across individuals.

Phytoestrogen intake has been associated with modest improvements in lipid parameters, including reductions in total cholesterol and LDL-C and moderate increases in HDL-C [94]. Nevertheless, study results remain heterogeneous and depend on dietary context, dose, and individual metabolic characteristics.

5.3 Omega-3 Fatty Acids and Microbiota Interactions

Omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (omega-3 PUFAs), particularly eicosapentaenoic acid and docosahexaenoic acid, receive considerable attention for their cardiometabolic relevance. Fatty fish such as salmon, mackerel, and sardines provide major dietary sources. In postmenopausal women, clinical studies most consistently report triglyceride lowering, whereas effects on other lipid fractions and vascular outcomes vary across trials [95]. Because study designs, doses, and baseline risk profiles differ, the overall evidence supports benefit for triglycerides but remains mixed for broader cardiometabolic endpoints.

Omega-3 PUFAs may modulate inflammatory signaling pathways, including nuclear factor kappa B, and support the production of specialized pro-resolving mediators such as resolvins and protectins [96]. These pathways offer biological plausibility for anti-inflammatory effects, but clinical studies often report heterogeneous changes in inflammatory biomarkers, particularly when baseline inflammation is low.

Recent research also suggests potential interactions with gut microbiota composition and activity, although mechanisms remain under investigation. Some studies report increased abundance of Bifidobacterium, Akkermansia muciniphila, and Lactobacillus following omega-3 PUFA intake, alongside reductions in potentially pro-inflammatory taxa such as Desulfovibrio and members of the Enterobacteriaceae family [97]. Most studies evaluate relative abundance rather than functional outputs, and only a subset integrates microbiota sequencing with lipid endpoints, which limits mechanistic interpretation.

Randomized trials further suggest that omega-3 PUFA supplementation may improve several cardiometabolic markers, including triglycerides, arterial stiffness, and inflammatory parameters such as C-reactive protein and interleukin-6. However, results remain inconsistent across postmenopausal cohorts, and few trials directly test whether microbiota changes mediate lipid responses [3, 33]. Some reports also describe increased abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila, a microbial pattern associated with improved glucose and lipid homeostasis [3, 33]. Dietary strategies combining omega-3 PUFAs with fiber-rich or polyphenol-rich foods may yield complementary effects on microbiota profiles and lipid parameters, but the current evidence base does not yet allow firm conclusions regarding synergy in postmenopausal women.

A randomized controlled trial reported that multispecies probiotic supplementation in postmenopausal women with obesity altered microbiota functional activity without significantly modifying taxonomic diversity, while several cardiometabolic markers improved modestly [87]. Other clinical investigations have examined probiotic formulations with β-glucuronidase activity, which may influence estrogen metabolism through microbial deconjugation processes in the gut [86]. Overall, dietary interventions represent a central component of cardiometabolic risk management after menopause. Among dietary patterns, the MD currently shows the strongest clinical evidence for improving lipid parameters and cardiovascular outcomes, whereas plant-based and DASH dietary approaches demonstrate moderate but consistent benefits. Microbiota-targeted interventions—including probiotics, prebiotics, dietary fiber, polyphenols, and omega-3 PUFAs—also show biologically plausible mechanisms and emerging clinical evidence. However, heterogeneity in study design, limited integration of microbiome profiling with lipid endpoints, and relatively short intervention durations continue to limit mechanistic interpretation. Future randomized trials integrating lipidomics, microbiome sequencing, and stratification by menopausal status will be essential to clarify causal pathways and support precision nutritional strategies for postmenopausal women.

Clinical Implications and Personalized Nutrition

The integration of gut microbiome research into clinical practice marks a transformative shift toward personalized nutrition. Understanding the intricate relationship between the gut microbiota and individual health profiles offers the potential to tailor dietary interventions, thereby enhancing efficacy and promoting optimal health outcomes.

Potential of Microbiome-based Risk Stratification

Microbial signatures have emerged as potential predictive biomarkers for various diseases, including metabolic disorders, CVD, and gastrointestinal conditions. For instance, specific microbial compositions have been associated with obesity, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia. By analyzing these microbial patterns, clinicians may be able to identify individuals at higher risk, which could facilitate earlier interventions and more individualized management strategies [28].​ Moreover, combining microbiome data with clinical parameters and genetic information may improve the performance of risk stratification models. This integrative approach could provide a more comprehensive representation of an individual's health status, supporting more targeted interventions. For example, integrating microbiome profiles with genetic predispositions has been proposed to improve the prediction of T2D risk, potentially enabling proactive lifestyle modifications [98].

Personalized Dietary Interventions Guided by Microbial Profiling

Personalized nutrition, tailored to an individual's unique microbiome composition, offers a novel approach to dietary interventions. By understanding how specific microbial communities interact with dietary components, clinicians may design targeted nutrition plans intended to optimize health outcomes. Studies have reported that individuals respond differently to the same dietary interventions, with variability partly linked to differences in gut microbiota features [89]. For instance, the effect of high-fiber diets on glycemic control may vary across individuals, depending on microbial capacity to ferment fiber into SCFA. By assessing microbial profiles, clinicians may be able to identify potential responders and non-responders to specific diets, which could improve intervention targeting.

Integrating microbial profiling into clinical practice typically involves collecting stool samples for microbiome analysis, interpreting the data to identify microbial imbalances or deficiencies, and designing dietary plans aimed at modulating the microbiota in a potentially beneficial direction. For example, increasing the intake of prebiotic-rich foods may promote the growth of beneficial bacteria and has been associated with improvements in metabolic health. Additionally, personalized probiotic supplementation, aligned with individual microbial features, has been explored as a strategy that may support microbiome balance and gut health [98, 99].

Limitations of Current Evidence and Practical Challenges in Clinical Application

Although microbiome-based personalized nutrition holds promise, several limitations hinder its clinical application. The relationships between gut microbiota features and health outcomes are not yet fully understood, making causal inference challenging. Significant inter-individual variability in microbiome composition and the absence of standardized analysis protocols further complicate the development of broadly applicable recommendations. Ethical and legal concerns, particularly regarding data privacy and informed consent, also pose challenges. Additionally, the high cost of microbiome analyses may restrict access, especially in low-resource settings. Most microbiome-guided nutritional strategies lack strong clinical validation, underscoring the need for large, randomized trials to confirm their efficacy and safety (Box 1) [100].

Conclusions

Menopause is characterized by a decline in estrogen and accompanying metabolic changes that are consistently associated with less favorable lipid profiles and higher cardiometabolic risk. In parallel, menopause-related shifts in the gut microbiota—often reported as reduced diversity and altered taxa—have been linked to visceral adiposity, dyslipidemia, and insulin resistance.

Current evidence supports a plausible role for the gut microbiota in lipid regulation through BA transformation, SCFA–related pathways, and immune–inflammatory signaling. However, most human studies remain associative, and mechanistic mediation is not consistently demonstrated in postmenopausal cohorts.

Dietary patterns and bioactive compounds that shape the microbiome (e.g., fiber-rich diets, polyphenols/phytoestrogens, and omega-3 PUFAs) have been associated with cardiometabolic benefits, yet findings vary by baseline risk, intervention dose, duration, and interindividual microbial capacity (e.g., equol/enterolignan production). As a result, “one-size-fits-all” microbiota-targeted recommendations are premature, and personalized approaches based on microbial profiling should be viewed as promising but still developing.

Future research should prioritize adequately powered, longer-duration randomized trials that integrate standardized microbiome methods with lipidomics/metabolomics, BA profiling, and clinical lipid endpoints. Stratification by menopausal stage, adiposity, diet quality, medication use (including hormone therapy), and relevant sociodemographic factors will be essential to interpret heterogeneity and improve generalizability. Such designs will better clarify which microbiota-linked pathways are most actionable and how they may inform precision nutritional strategies for cardiometabolic care after menopause.
