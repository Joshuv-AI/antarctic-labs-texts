The SIRT5-SUCLG2 desuccinylation axis delays ovarian aging via a mitochondrial-epigenetic regulatory mechanism
Authors: Dejun Xu, Shuaifei Song, Qingmiao Shi, Zhiling Zhao, Wenhao Zhao, Lei Wang, Guangxin E, Yanguo Han, Yongju Zhao, Zhongquan Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondrial dysfunction and epigenetic alterations play critical roles in aging-related diseases, yet the molecular mechanisms linking mito-nuclear crosstalk to ovarian aging remain poorly understood. Here, single-cell transcriptome analysis of aging ovaries revealed senescence-associated hallmark alterations, including abnormally elevated mitochondrial metabolism, disrupted histone modification patterns, and enrichment of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). We demonstrated that impaired SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation constitutes a key driver of ovarian aging. Mechanistically, we identified succinyl-coenzyme A (CoA) synthetase GDP-forming subunit β (SUCLG2) in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle as the main target of SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation. SUCLG2 desuccinylation at lysine residues K93 and K101 enhanced its protein stability and activity, thereby improving mitochondrial function upon cellular senescence. However, SUCLG2 hypersuccinylation specifically increased H4K8ac through acetyl-CoA accumulation in nucleus, leading to the overexpression of metabolism-related genes to compensate for the energy demand deficiency caused by decreased mitochondrial function during cellular senescence. In vivo functional studies demonstrated that acetyl-CoA oversupply accelerated ovarian aging, whereas ovarian gene therapy employing a SUCLG2 desuccinylation mutant ameliorated this condition. This study illuminates the molecular mechanisms underlying ovarian aging and identifies the SIRT5-SUCLG2 axis as a promising therapeutic target for age-related ovarian dysfunction.Subject terms: Senescence, Infertility, Chemical modification

Introduction

As an important reproductive organ in female animals, the ovary is one of the organs that undergoes functional decline earlier with age, which ultimately leads to a decrease in reproductive ability and even infertility in female mammals1. Ovarian follicles, being the fundamental structural and functional units, directly determine reproductive performance through their developmental maturation. Cellular cycle arrest resulting from granulosa cell (GC) senescence is one of the key factors leading to follicular developmental disorders2. In particular, senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors released by senescent GCs exert profound impacts on the surrounding ovarian tissue microenvironment, compromising follicular structural integrity3. Aging is usually accompanied by a series of changes in biological processes, such as mitochondrial dysfunction, epigenetic alterations, and loss of protein homeostasis4. Previous studies have shown that ovarian aging is closely related to mitochondrial dysfunction and epigenetic alterations in GCs5–7. For example, increased mitochondrial membrane potential and autophagy in GCs can rescue the symptoms of ovarian aging8. Emerging research reveals that mitochondrial dysfunction and epigenetic alterations are closely interrelated and mutually regulated, and that such changes are also two hallmarks of aging4,9–11. Notably, almost all histone-modifying enzymes use important intermediate products of mitochondrial metabolism as substrates for epigenetic modification. For example, the transfer of acetyl-CoA to the nucleus determines the acetylation of histones, which regulates gene expression through chromatin remodeling12–14. This evidence suggests that mitochondrial-to-nuclear communication may be involved in ovarian aging. However, the specific mechanism by which mitochondrial metabolism interacts with epigenetic modifications during ovarian aging remains poorly understood.

Post-translational protein modifications (PTMs) are critical cellular mechanisms responsible for modulating age-related diseases15,16. In recent years, researchers have identified a series of acylation modifications via high-resolution mass spectrometry analysis, including succinylation, butyrylation, crotonylation, and malonylation17–20. However, the exact functions of these acylation modifications in aging remain largely unknown. A recently identified yet conserved lysine succinylation (K-Suc) plays a key role in regulating protein function and cell metabolism17,21. Recent studies have shown that lysine succinylation is associated with ovarian aging and is highly localized to GCs. Specifically, high levels of protein succinylation in the ovaries of aged mice will inhibit the reproductive and endocrine functions of the ovaries, induce GC senescence, and lead to an increase in atretic follicles22. However, the molecular pathway by which lysine succinylation contributes to ovarian aging remains unclear.

Mitochondria are the central site of metabolism, and the intermediate products in this metabolic process, such as succinyl-CoA, are substrates of both succinylation and mitochondria, which participate in the PTMs and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. Notably, the succinyl-CoA synthetase GDP-forming subunit β (SUCLG2) is one of the key enzymes in the TCA cycle. Recent studies have reported that the succinylation modification of SUCLG2 has a profound impact on its function and is involved in cancer23. As a member of the NAD+-dependent deacetylase sirtuins (SIRT) family, sirtuin 5 (SIRT5) has been identified as a key regulatory enzyme that catalyzes lysine desuccinylation21. Importantly, most of its targets are enzymes in mitochondrial metabolic pathways, such as the TCA cycle. Thus, SIRT5 may play a role in regulating mitochondrial metabolic processes by removing succinyl groups, thereby influencing physiological cellular activities24–26. Previous findings position SIRT5 as a critical rheostat linking metabolic stress sensing with reproductive aging trajectories27. The level of SIRT5 and its desuccinylation activity in follicular cells decreases with increasing maternal age and declining ovarian reserve28. However, the specific effects of SIRT5 on succinylation in ovarian aging, particularly the main targets and outcomes of desuccinylation, necessitate further in-depth investigation.

In this study, our findings demonstrate that ‌both metabolic impairment and epigenetic alterations‌ constitute critical hallmarks of ovarian aging. ‌Furthermore‌, hypersuccinylation is associated with the downregulation of SIRT5 during this process. This global succinylation is highly enriched in mitochondria and exhibits obvious cell specificity. Specifically, we confirmed that SIRT5 catalyzed the desuccinylation of SUCLG2 in the TCA cycle pathway. The desuccinylation of SUCLG2 at the two conserved sites, K93 and K101, enhances its protein stability and activity, thereby improving mitochondrial function, while hypersuccinylation mimics at these two sites characteristically leads to H4K8 hyperacetylation via acetyl-CoA accumulation, which subsequently regulates chromatin remodeling. Our findings offer mechanistic insights into how succinylation governs ovarian aging via mito-nuclear crosstalk.

Results

Single-cell transcriptomics reveals senescence-associated metabolic and epigenetic alterations during ovarian aging

For evaluation of aging-related alterations in the ovary, we analyzed the single-cell transcriptomes of young (3-month-old) and aging (9-month-old) ovaries (n = 4 per group). A total of 5974 young and 4928 aging ovarian cells were used to create t-SNE plots (Fig. 1a). Through systematic categorization utilizing rigorously validated cell type-specific biomarkers29, ten distinct cellular populations were identified: granulosa cells (GCs), luteal cells, Theca cells, oocytes, endothelium cells, epithelium cells, stroma cells, phagocyte cells, B cells, and T cells (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). Compared with young ovaries, aging ovaries exhibited a precipitous decline in follicular GC populations, while experiencing a marked surge in immune cell infiltration, particularly phagocytes, B cells, and T cells, indicating that maternal aging alters ovarian cell distribution (Fig. 1b). To investigate aging-related alterations in ovarian aging, we quantified the activity scores of 36 representative pathways associated with 11 established aging hallmarks4. Gene expression profiles linked to mitochondrial dysfunction, including “Oxidative phosphorylation”, “Reactive oxygen species metabolic process”, “Mitochondrial ATP synthesis coupled electron transport”, and “Reactive oxygen species pathway”, showed marked upregulation in GCs from aged ovarian tissues (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Signaling pathways involving “Histone modification”, “Chromatin assembly or disassembly”, and “Chromatin remodeling” were significantly upregulated in GCs of aged ovaries, indicating profound epigenetic dysregulation during ovarian aging (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Similarly, pathway activity analysis revealed that GCs in aged ovaries displayed pronounced activation of multiple regulatory pathways, particularly those governing “Cell aging”, “Autophagy”, “Insulin signaling pathway”, “Ubiquitin dependent protein catabolic process”, “DNA repair”, and “Chronic inflammatory response” (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Interestingly, single-cell metabolism (scMetabolism) analysis revealed significantly enhanced metabolic activity in GCs from aged ovaries, particularly in oxidative phosphorylation, citrate cycle, and pyruvate metabolism pathways (Fig. 1c). These alterations reflect a compensatory adaptation of senescent ovarian GCs to energy deprivation and survival stress.

AMH signaling, which serves as a cornerstone of ovarian reserve, was abundant in GCs, however, this critical communication between GCs, and Theca cells markedly diminished in aging ovaries (Supplementary Fig. 1d). Conversely, immune-granulosa cell interactions were amplified via VISTA and TNF signaling pathways (Supplementary Fig. 1e, f), which are key mediators of senescence-associated phenotypes (SASP). These results show that ovarian aging involves significant changes in cellular communication, potentially contributing to age-related loss of follicular reserve. Subsequently, we conducted differential gene expression analysis in GCs. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed significant upregulation of pathways including “ATP metabolic process”, “Regulation of protein modification”, “Cell aging”, “Chronic inflammatory response”, and “Histone acetyltransferase binding activity” in aged ovarian GCs (Fig. 1d). Conversely, signaling pathways such as “Reproduction”, “Chromatin binding”, “Negative regulation of histone modification”, and “Histone deacetylase activity” were downregulated (Fig. 1d). The data indicate a coordinated dysregulation in aged GCs characterized by metabolic hyperactivity, inflammation-driven senescence, and epigenetic destabilization. Given our discovery of abnormally hyperactive energy metabolism in aging ovaries, we further investigated the differential expression of metabolic genes in GCs. A heatmap analysis demonstrated significant downregulation of genes involved in mitochondrial respiratory chain complex assembly (e.g., Ndufs1, Ndufs3, Ndufa5, Ndufv2) and gluconeogenesis (e.g., Pgam1, Fbp1, Pck2) in GCs from aging ovaries (Fig. 1e). Conversely, genes associated with mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (e.g., Cox17, Atp6v0d1, Atp6v0c) showed marked upregulation in these aging cells (Fig. 1e). Reciprocal expression patterns between mitochondrial complex assembly genes and oxidative phosphorylation constituents may underlie compensatory mechanisms during ovarian aging. We observed that the levels of ‌ATP-citrate lyase (Acly)‌ and ‌acetyl-CoA carboxylase (Acaca)‌ were significantly decreased in aged ovarian GCs, reflecting a ‌profound dysregulation of the acetyl-CoA metabolic network‌ in these senescent cells (Fig. 1e). Interestingly, we found that succinate-CoA ligase subunit beta (Suclg2), a key enzyme catalyzing the interconversion of succinyl-CoA and succinate in the tricarboxylic acid cycle, was significantly downregulated in aging ovarian GCs (Fig. 1e). We further compared the expression differences of genes involved in succinate metabolism (Sucla2, Suclg1, and Suclg2) between young and aged ovarian cells. The results demonstrated specific downregulation of these genes in maternally aged GCs and stromal cells, with Suclg2 exhibiting the most pronounced reduction in GCs (Fig. 1f). Quantitative analysis demonstrated significant accumulation of acetyl-CoA in aged ovarian GCs (Fig. 1g), with this metabolite acting as a donor substrate to influence epigenetic alterations. Although both succinate and succinyl-CoA were upregulated in ovarian GCs from aged mouse (Fig. 1h, i), the succinate/succinyl-CoA ratio was markedly decreased (Fig. 1j). This observation suggests that the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle is compensatorily activated in senescent GCs, yet the metabolic conversion of succinyl-CoA is likely impaired, leading to a disproportionately greater accumulation of succinyl-CoA relative to succinate. Similarly, aged ovarian GCs displayed a markedly reduced NAD⁺/NADH ratio (Fig. 1k), indicating concomitant dysregulation of mitochondrial metabolism and compromised oxidative phosphorylation capacity.

To further elucidate the fate of GCs during ovarian aging, GCs were segregated into four distinct clusters (Fig. 1l), including preantral30,31, antral32,33, mitotic34,35 and atretic GCs36,37, with each cluster defined by its cell subtype-specific molecular markers. Single-cell trajectory analysis using Monocle 3 revealed significant alterations in the differentiation dynamics of ovarian GCs during aging. We found that a striking reduction in preantral GCs and antral GCs populations, with a substantial proportion of preantral GCs aberrantly differentiating into atretic GCs (Fig. 1m). This phenomenon suggests that preantral follicles in aging ovaries tend to undergo atresia program activation, rather than proceeding to follicular developmental stages. Furthermore, the expression of cellular senescence markers such as Cdkn1a and Casp8 was upregulated in atretic GCs of aged ovaries, while Glb1 levels were increased in antral GCs, and Cdkn1a (p21) was elevated in mitotic GCs (Fig. 1n). The heterogeneous expression of senescence markers suggests that follicular atresia during ovarian aging is closely linked to the senescence regulation of GCs. To validate these findings, we conducted a comparative assessment of senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) activity in young (3-month-old) and aging (9-month-old) ovaries. Strikingly, SA-β-gal signals were specifically localized to GCs, with aged ovaries exhibiting a marked increase in SA-β-gal-positive cell populations compared to young ovaries (Fig. 1o). Together, these results suggest that GCs undergo a striking reprogramming of cell trajectories and cellular senescence with advancing age, which may be mechanistically linked to excessive accumulation of acetyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA, thereby triggering concurrent metabolic dysfunction and epigenetic alterations.

SIRT5-dependent desuccinylation deficiency is a key driver of ovarian aging

Given the high conservation of aging-related molecular pathways between bovine and human ovaries38, we adopted bovine ovarian models to elucidate the core molecular mechanisms underlying ovarian aging. In accordance with previous methods39, we divided bovine ovaries into young (3 ~ 5-year-old) and aged (≥ 10-year-old) groups according to the follicular reserve capacity and age and confirmed increased expression of the aging markers p53 and p21 in the aged ovaries (Fig. 2a). We also detected a decrease in SIRT5 protein expression in aged ovaries (Fig. 2b). We subsequently examined the levels of posttranslational modifications (PTMs) that may be regulated by SIRT5. We found that the global levels of protein acylation modifications, including acetylation, malonylation and glutarylation, were elevated in aged ovarian tissues (Fig. 2c–f). Notably, succinylation, the key modification regulating mitochondrial metabolism, displayed the most prominent increase (Fig. 2c) in aged ovarian tissues. To further clarify the regulatory specificity of succinylation, we inhibited the enzymatic activities of other Sirtuin isoforms and HDACs with specific inhibitors. The results showed that suppressing the activity of mitochondrial-localized SIRT3 had no obvious impact on the global abundance of protein succinylation (Supplementary Fig. 2a). By contrast, blocking the activities of nuclear-localized SIRT7 and HDACs significantly increased the level of succinylation modification (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). Correspondingly, immunofluorescence (IF) analysis of ovarian tissues also revealed a decrease in SIRT5 expression and an increase in succinylation in aged ovaries, which were reflected mainly in follicular GCs, suggesting that this succinylation is specifically present in GCs (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Notably, the results confirmed that low levels of SIRT5 are associated with ovarian aging and are accompanied by hypersuccinylation of PTMs in vivo. Therefore, we separately isolated GCs from young and aged ovaries to assess the SIRT5 protein level, and the results were similar to the trend of changes in ovarian tissue. Compared with that in young granulosa cells (YGCs), the expression of SIRT5 in aged granulosa cells (AGCs) was decreased (Fig. 2g), but the level of succinylation was increased (Fig. 2h), indicating that hypersuccinylation in GCs is closely related to ovarian aging. We further induced GC senescence with etoposide (Supplementary Fig. 3a–d) to assess changes in SIRT5 and succinylation levels upon senescence. As expected, SIRT5 was downregulated, whereas succinylation was increased in etoposide-treated cells compared with controls (Fig. 2i, j).

Immunoblotting analysis demonstrated that SIRT5 overexpression inhibited the level of succinylation in GCs (Fig. 2k, l), whereas SIRT5 knockdown led to a significant increase in succinylation compared with that in the control group (Fig. 2m). Given the low expression of SIRT5 in senescent GCs, we hypothesized that SIRT5 is involved in cellular senescence. To examine this further, we analyzed the changes in cell proliferation and senescence following treatment with Etoposide alone or SIRT5 overexpression. As expected, compared with the control, Etoposide treatment alone significantly inhibited granulosa cell proliferation, as indicated by a decrease in the percentage of EdU-positive cells, which was subsequently reversed by SIRT5 overexpression (Supplementary Fig. 2e). In addition, Etoposide promoted the secretion of senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) in GCs, while the overexpression of SIRT5 inhibited its secretion (Supplementary Fig. 2f). Further flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that, relative to control cells, treatment with Etoposide induced a robust G2-phase cell cycle arrest. Notably, this etoposide-induced G2-phase arrest was ameliorated when cells were co-treated with SIRT5 (Supplementary Fig. 3e). We performed proteomic analysis in SIRT5-overexpressing GCs. The results showed that SIRT5 overexpression caused the upregulation of 217 proteins and the downregulation of 198 proteins in GCs (Supplementary Fig. 4a,and Supplementary Data 1). These differential proteins were distributed mainly in the nucleus, cytoplasm, and plasma membrane (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Among them, the most significant change was observed for P4HA1 (Supplementary Fig. 4c), which contributes to succinylation40. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that differentially expressed proteins affected by SIRT5 overexpression were involved in the process of cellular senescence (Supplementary Fig. 4d). Together, these data indicate that hypersuccinylation caused by SIRT5 downregulation is a key driver of ovarian aging.

We quantified the relative abundance ratios of mitochondrial Sirtuin isoforms to COXⅣ. SIRT3 remained unchanged, while the SIRT5/COXⅣ ratio declined significantly in senescent GCs (Fig. 2n). This excludes mitochondrial loss as a cause and demonstrates that SIRT5 is specifically downregulated during ovarian aging. To further clarify whether SIRT5 downregulation is a causal driver or a downstream consequence of ovarian aging, we used a SIRT5 knockout mouse model to verify this in vivo. Periodic Acid-Schiff and hematoxylin (PAS-H) staining demonstrated that genetic ablation of SIRT5 resulted in a significant decline in follicle number reserve (Fig. 2o–q). In SIRT5-deficient mice, serum AMH was markedly reduced with a concurrent increase in FSH, which confirms the deterioration of ovarian reserve function (Fig. 2r, s). We further observed a marked reduction in litter size of SIRT5 knockout mice (Fig. 2t). Collectively, these in vivo and in vitro phenotypic findings demonstrate that SIRT5 downregulation acts as a key regulatory determinant underlying ovarian aging.

Emerging evidence highlights non-histone lysine succinylation as a critical post-translational modification that regulates metabolic homeostasis. Given its established links to age-related metabolic dysregulation, we hypothesized that this succinylation modification may drive ovarian functional decline. To test this, we administered sodium succinate to female mice for 30 days in vivo (Fig. 2u). We found that although there was no significant change in ovarian weight (Supplementary Fig. 2g, h), the ovarian index was significantly decreased in the mice fed sodium succinate (Supplementary Fig. 2i). Immunohistochemistry and immunoblotting revealed that succinylation was significantly increased by supplementation with sodium succinate in ovarian cells (Fig. 2v and Supplementary Fig. 2j). We then assessed the effects of succinylation modification on the ovarian reserve in mice fed sodium succinate, a succinylation modifier. Notably, the number of primordial (Pm), primary (Pr), and secondary-antral (Sec-Ant) follicles decreased significantly, whereas the number of activated primordial follicles (APF) increased (Supplementary Fig. 2k,l). As expected, increased expression of the aging marker p21 was observed in hypersuccinylated ovaries (Fig. 2w). Notably, hypersuccinylation decreased the average litter size (Fig. 2x). Collectively, these findings indicate that elevated lysine succinylation is potentially linked to ovarian aging.

SIRT5-mediated mitochondrial protein desuccinylation and its association with metabolism

Cellular senescence is closely associated with metabolic dysfunction4. Given SIRT5’s mitochondrial localization and its role in metabolic regulation through protein desuccinylation21, we hypothesize that SIRT5 modulates GC senescence via mitochondrial metabolic control. We generated a targeted energy metabolome in control and SIRT5-overexpressing GCs, and a total of 59 metabolites were detected (Fig. 3a). Compared with control cells, SIRT5-overexpressing cells exhibited significantly different levels of 21 metabolites, including 9 upregulated and 12 downregulated (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Data 2). SIRT5 overexpression primarily regulated the mitochondrial metabolic profile (Fig. 3b). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant alterations in mitochondrial metabolic pathways, particularly the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle (Fig. 3c). Within the SIRT5 overexpression group, key TCA cycle intermediates, including citric acid, isocitric acid, and succinic acid, were upregulated, whereas acetyl-CoA, succinyl-CoA, and fumaric acid showed significant downregulation (Fig. 3d). This metabolic shift confirms that SIRT5 participates in the reprogramming of mitochondrial metabolism. To identify the downstream protein targets of SIRT5-related lysine succinylation, we performed 4D-FastDIA quantitative succinyl proteomic analysis in GCs overexpressing SIRT5 or a control vector to obtain a global view of the SIRT5 regulation of K-Suc. Proteomic analysis identified a total of 1546 succinylated lysine sites, spanning 632 proteins (Supplementary Data 3). Notably, 10 sites and proteins were upregulated, and 568 sites and 250 proteins were downregulated. These data indicate that SIRT5 is broadly involved in protein lysine desuccinylation (Fig. 3e). Further quantitative analysis revealed that 578 lysine residues distributed on 260 proteins were significantly different (Fig. 3f). These differential modification sites and proteins accounted for 37.39% and 41.14%, respectively, of the total protein content (Fig. 3f), indicating that SIRT5 is a key factor regulating protein succinylation in GCs.

We subsequently used Motif-x and iceLogo analyses to reveal the specific motifs upstream and downstream of the adjacent sides of the succinylated lysine residue. Alanine (A), isoleucine (I), and glutamic acid (E) appeared in upstream, while valine (V), leucine (L), and phenylalanine (F) appeared in downstream of the K-Suc site, which provided a reference for predicting the potential desuccinylation sites regulated by SIRT5 (Fig. 3g, h). Moreover, secondary structure prediction analysis revealed that, compared with unmodified lysine, succinylated lysine had no significant effect on the distribution of alpha-helices, beta-strands, or coil but decreased the surface accessibility (Fig. 3i). Subcellular structure annotation demonstrated that the succinylation sites regulated by SIRT5 were distributed throughout the cell but were concentrated mainly in the mitochondria (51.54%), followed by the cytoplasm (25.38%) and nucleus (8.08%) (Fig. 3j). Consistent with this finding, immunoblotting results revealed that the differential changes in SIRT5 and succinylation in both young and aged ovarian GCs were also reflected mainly in mitochondrial proteins rather than in cytoplasmic proteins (Fig. 3k, l). In addition, the overexpression of SIRT5 led to a general decrease in lysine succinylation in these subcellular compartments (Fig. 3m). Overall, these findings suggest that SIRT5 mainly desuccinylates mitochondrial proteins in GCs.

To further understand the potential functional involvement of succinylated proteins in GCs, we first searched for the top 30 differentially modified sites located in the mitochondria (Fig. 3n). The downregulated sites included K118, K93, and K111 of the succinyl-CoA ligase β subunit (SUCLG2), K307 of malate dehydrogenase 2 (MDH2), and K166 of NipSnap homolog 3 A (NIPSNAP3A) (Fig. 3n). In contrast, there was also an upregulated site, K272 of solute carrier family 25 member 4 (SLC25A4). These proteins are closely related to cellular metabolism (Fig. 3n). After that, we performed KEGG functional annotation on all identified succinylated proteins. These proteins are related to the metabolism, organismal systems, and cellular processes of GCs, especially metabolic processes, and most of them are involved in “carbohydrate metabolism”, “amino acid metabolism” and “energy metabolism” (Fig. 3o). In addition, functional module analysis of the differential protein interaction network further revealed the regulatory effects of succinylated proteins on metabolism, such as the “tricarboxylic acid cycle”, “oxidative phosphorylation”, and “fatty acid degradation” (Fig. 3p), supporting the importance of SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation in regulating the energy metabolism of GCs.

We further constructed a global succinylation map covering energy metabolism, clarifying the regulatory effects of SIRT5-dependent succinylated proteins at distinct metabolic nodes (Supplementary Fig. 5). Overall, succinylation omics showed that SIRT5 weakened the succinylation of most enzymes in each pathway, further indicating that SIRT5 plays a vital role in regulating mitochondrial metabolism. For example, consistent with previous results from the metabolome, several key enzymes in the TCA cycle have succinylation targets that bind to SIRT5, including K93 of citrate synthase (CS), K587 of aconitase 2 (ACO2), and K413 of isocitrate dehydrogenase 2 (IDH2), which may be related to changes in the content of metabolites in the TCA caused by the overexpression of SIRT5 (Supplementary Fig. 5). Moreover, the rate-limiting enzymes of glycolysis, K841 of hexokinase (HK) and K115 of pyruvate kinase (PKM), were also significantly downregulated (Supplementary Fig. 5). Furthermore, several catalytic enzymes involved in fatty acid β-oxidation, oxidative phosphorylation, ATP metabolism, and leucine and isoleucine degradation pathways were also desuccinylated. Interestingly, K193 of cytochrome P450 family 11 subfamily A member 1 (CYP11A1), which is related to steroid hormone synthesis in GCs, was also found to be a target of SIRT5 and could potentially affect the development status of subsequent ovarian follicles (Supplementary Fig. 5). Together, our results reveal that SIRT5-driven desuccinylation is extensively engaged in the metabolic regulation of GCs and intimately linked to TCA cycle activity.

SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation of SUCLG2 at lysine residues K93 and K101

To screen for SIRT5 target proteins in the TCA cycle, we identified the top 30 differentially abundant sites and proteins. As shown in the radar plot, multiple lysine residues in the SUCLG2 protein were highly desuccinylated by SIRT5 (Fig. 4a). In addition, our succinylation omics data demonstrated that K108 of SUCLA2 and K45 of SUCLG1, which are homologous to SUCLG2, were also desuccinylated by SIRT5. However, the number of sites and the degree of variation were far less significant than those of SUCLG2 (Fig. 4b,c), further confirming that SUCLG2 is a key target of SIRT5 in the TCA cycle and plays a unique role in this reaction. To further characterize the spatial distribution of SIRT5 and SUCLG2 in ovarian tissues, we performed immunofluorescence analysis and confirmed the colocalization of SIRT5 and SUCLG2 in ovarian follicular cells (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Notably, we identified eight desuccinylated lysine residues throughout the SUCLG2 amino acid sequence. Of these candidate sites, merely K93 and K101 exhibited high evolutionary conservation across different species (Supplementary Fig. 6b), and the desuccinylation modification at these two sites was unambiguously confirmed via mass spectrometry (Supplementary Fig. 6c). Subsequently, immunoprecipitation (IP) assays revealed that etoposide-induced cellular senescence markedly elevated SUCLG2 succinylation compared with the control (Fig. 4d). In contrast, SIRT5 overexpression significantly reduced SUCLG2 succinylation (Fig. 4e). These findings suggest that SUCLG2 succinylation is involved in the regulatory modulation of granulosa cell senescence. By immunoblotting analysis, we further confirmed that SUCLG2 protein expression was significantly downregulated in aged ovaries and senescent GCs (Fig. 4f, g). Therefore, we constructed SUCLG2 interference and overexpression models in GCs (Supplementary Fig. 6d, e) and detected the effect of SUCLG2 on the viability of GCs. The results revealed that a decrease in SUCLG2 expression inhibited cellular activity, whereas the overexpression of SUCLG2 restored cellular viability (Supplementary Fig. 6f). Moreover, SUCLG2 knockdown increased the secretion of SA-β-Gal and accelerated cellular senescence (Fig. 4h). Co-IP analysis verified that SIRT5 directly binds to SUCLG2 (Fig. 4i). To investigate whether SIRT5 directly desuccinylates SUCLG2 at the K93 and K101 lysine residues, we performed in vitro desuccinylation assays using synthetic succinylated peptides containing K93suc or K101suc as substrates. Using LC-MS/MS, we identified the succinylated SUCLG2 peptides GRGK(Succinyl)GVFNSGLKGGV or GRGKGVFNSGLK(Succinyl)GGV in the in vitro desuccinylation reaction system (Fig. 4j). Quantitative mass spectrometry confirmed that SIRT5 strongly catalyzes the removal of succinyl groups at the K93 and K101 residues (Fig. 4k, l). Subsequently, we further analyzed potential aging-related succinyltransferases. The results revealed that the expression of the succinyltransferase KAT2A was markedly upregulated in aged ovarian tissues, whereas CPT1A showed an opposite downward trend (Fig. 4m). When cellular senescence was induced, the protein abundance of KAT2A also increased significantly, while no obvious change was observed in CPT1A (Fig. 4n). Knockdown of KAT2A significantly decreased the global protein succinylation level (Fig. 4o, p). IP assays further demonstrated that downregulating KAT2A markedly diminished SUCLG2 succinylation in GCs (Fig. 4q). Overall, these results demonstrate that SUCLG2 at K93suc and K101suc is a direct desuccinylation target of SIRT5.

SUCLG2 desuccinylation enhances protein stability and activity

The inverse changes in SUCLG2 succinylation and protein abundance during ovarian aging led us to hypothesize that succinylation governs SUCLG2 stability. Structural analysis revealed that lysine residues K93 and K101 of SUCLG2 are co-localized within the ATP-grasp domain, with close spatial proximity in the tertiary structure (Fig. 5a), suggesting that their succinylation status critically regulates enzymatic function through spatial positioning. Furthermore, we performed site-directed mutagenesis of SUCLG2 at lysine residues K93 and K101, converting them to glutamic acid (K → E) to mimic hypersuccinylation or to arginine (K → R) to mimic desuccinylation (Fig. 5b). Single-site SUCLG2 mutants K93E and K101E increased pan-K-Suc signals of SUCLG2 in GCs (Fig. 5c), indicating that K93 and K101 represent the major succinylation sites of SUCLG2. Ubiquitination-mediated protein degradation is an important mechanism for regulating intracellular protein levels41. We further examined the polyubiquitination pattern of SUCLG2. Cellular senescence significantly increased K48-linked (Fig. 5d) and K63-linked (Fig. 5e) ubiquitin modification of SUCLG2, whereas SIRT5 overexpression obviously reduced its polyubiquitination (Fig. 5f, g). Consistently, senescence strengthened the interaction between SUCLG2 and ubiquitin monomers (Supplementary Fig. 6g), whereas SIRT5 overexpression suppressed their binding (Supplementary Fig. 6h). Subsequent observations showed that hypersuccinylation at K93/K101 of SUCLG2 promoted its K48‑ and K63‑linked polyubiquitination (Fig. 5h, i), while desuccinylation at these two residues inhibited such polyubiquitination. Similarly, SUCLG2 succinylation strengthened its interaction with ubiquitin monomers (Supplementary Fig. 6i). Cycloheximide (CHX) inhibition of general protein synthesis was applied to evaluate succinylation-dependent turnover of SUCLG2. Relative to wild-type SUCLG2 and K93R/K101R desuccinylation mimics, the K93E/K101E hypersuccinylation mutants exhibited faster SUCLG2 degradation (Fig. 5j–l). These results indicate that the low protein level of SUCLG2 in senescent GCs is due to its increased succinylation, which leads to a decrease in protein stability. These findings demonstrate that SUCLG2 succinylation modulates its protein stability via the ubiquitin-proteasome degradation pathway.

Given that SUCLG2 is localized to mitochondria involved in the TCA cycle, we next examined the effect of SUCLG2 succinylation on mitochondrial distribution. The results revealed that the distribution ratio of SUCLG2 protein in the mitochondria (Mito)/cytoplasm (Cyto) was decreased in aging-induced GCs (Fig. 5m). Moreover, the succinylation-mimetic mutant at K93 and K101 also disrupted the relative Mito/Cyto distribution of SUCLG2 and reduced the mitochondrial location of SUCLG2 protein (Fig. 5n). Using IP assays, we also found that the increase in SUCLG2 succinylation weakened its binding to TOM20, a member of the mitochondrial transport complex (Fig. 5o). These data suggest that SUCLG2 succinylation at K93 and K101 regulates its mitochondrial location.

SUCLG2 catalyzes succinyl-CoA, free phosphate (Pi) and GDP to produce GTP, CoA, and succinate (Fig. 5p)42. To elucidate the effects of K93/K101 succinylation on SUCLG2 catalytic activity, we conducted structural succinylation simulation of SUCLG2 via PyMOL, followed by AlphaFold3 to predict its binding mode with the ligand GDP (Fig. 5q). The dynamic fluctuations of the ligand GDP in the WT model greatly increased during the molecular dynamics simulations, whereas the SUCLG2-GDP dimers in the succinylation model were more stable and less prone to dynamic reactions (Fig. 5r), suggesting that SUCLG2 succinylation at the K93 and K101 sites inhibited its molecular dynamics. Consistent with the RMSD results, analysis of the radius of gyration (Rg) revealed that SUCLG2 succinylation caused greater conformational changes (Supplementary Fig. 6j). However, succinylation at the K93 and K101 sites only slightly increased the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) during the simulations (Supplementary Fig. 6k). Furthermore, we found that succinylation of K93 and K101 also resulted in a decrease in the temperature factor (b-factor) value of the SUCLG2 protein during MD simulation, resulting in an increase in the high-flexibility region (Supplementary Fig. 6l). Quantitative metabolic assays confirmed that SUCLG2 hypersuccinylation at K93/K101 reduced succinate and accumulated succinyl-CoA relative to wild-type and desuccinylation-mimetic mutants, thereby suppressing its catalytic function in succinyl-CoA metabolism (Fig. 5s, t). Collectively, K93/K101 hypersuccinylation drives ubiquitin-mediated degradation of SUCLG2, compromises its mitochondrial targeting efficiency, and disturbs conformational dynamics, thereby suppressing its protein stability and enzymatic function.

SUCLG2 succinylation modulates mitochondrial function by regulating acetyl-CoA homeostasis

Given its role as a pivotal mitochondrial metabolic enzyme, we propose that SUCLG2 regulates mitochondrial function via TCA cycle flux.‌ To interrogate this, we first assessed ‌alterations in mitochondrial morphological dynamics‌ in GCs upon SUCLG2 overexpression or knockdown. The immunoblotting results revealed that SUCLG2 knockdown resulted in decreased expression of mitochondrial fusion (OPA1, MFN1, and MFN2) and fission (DRP1 and FIS1) proteins, whereas SUCLG2 overexpression increased the expression levels of these proteins (Fig. 6a, b). Additionally, compared with the transfected K93E/K101E mutants of SUCLG2, the K93R and K101R mutants also increased the expression of mitochondrial dynamics-related proteins in GCs (Fig. 6c), suggesting that SUCLG2 desuccinylation promotes mitochondrial fusion-fission. Given that mitophagy acts as a core mechanism governing mitochondrial quality control43, we further validated the regulatory effect of SUCLG2 succinylation on mitophagy. The expression of canonical mitophagy markers (PINK1, Parkin, p62, and the LC3Ⅱ/I ratio) was markedly altered following SUCLG2 knockdown or overexpression (Fig. 6d, e), indicating that SUCLG2 modulates mitophagic flux in GCs. Following mutant plasmid transfection, K93E/K101E hypersuccinylation mimics showed a decreased Parkin, PINK1, and LC3Ⅱ/I ratio, along with an increased p62 level, compared with K93R/K101R desuccinylation variants, indicative of suppressed mitophagic flux (Fig. 6f). Additionally, COXⅣ expression, a key subunit of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex IV, was suppressed by SUCLG2 knockdown‌ but elevated by SUCLG2 and its desuccinylation-mimicking mutations (K93R/K101R) (Fig. 6g–i). Next, we used MitoTracker to label mitochondria in GCs to detect the effect of SUCLG2 succinylation on mitochondrial morphology. Compared with WT SUCLG2 and K93R/K101R variants featuring elongated filamentous mitochondria, K93E/K101E mutations triggered pronounced mitochondrial fragmentation (Fig. 6j, k). These findings indicate that hypersuccinylation at K93/K101 of SUCLG2 disrupts normal mitochondrial morphological homeostasis. Furthermore, co-staining for mitophagy markers and lysosomes revealed that compared with WT SUCLG2 and the K93E/K101E hypersuccinylation mimics, the K93R/K101R desuccinylation mimics exhibited robust mitophagic activity and enhanced colocalization with lysosomes (Fig. 6l–n). Consistently, we observed similar trends in mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm). ΔΨm was reduced by SUCLG2 K93E and K101E mutants, whereas it was markedly enhanced by K93R and K101R mutants (Fig. 6o, p). These results indicate that desuccinylation at lysine residues K93 and K101 of SUCLG2 enhances mitochondrial quality control, whereas hypersuccinylation compromises this essential homeostatic pathway.

We further performed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to investigate the effects of SUCLG2 succinylation on mitochondrial ultrastructure during granulosa cell senescence. Compared with normal GCs, etoposide-induced senescent cells exhibited mitochondrial swelling with cristae fragmentation. Mitochondrial structural damage was effectively alleviated by SUCLG2 K93R/K101R desuccinylation mimics, while K93E/K101E hypersuccinylation mimics exhibited no protective improvement (Fig. 6q, r). Collectively, these data demonstrate that site-specific desuccinylation at K93/K101 of SUCLG2 is critical for preserving mitochondrial function and quality control during cellular senescence. To elucidate the regulatory mechanism of SUCLG2 succinylation on mitochondrial function, we conducted targeted energy metabolomics analysis in GCs transfected with either hypersuccinylation mutants (K93E/K101E) or desuccinylation mutants (K93R/K101R) (Supplementary Data 4). KEGG pathway analysis of differentially abundant metabolites between the two groups identified significant perturbations in the TCA cycle (Fig. 6s). Notably, quantitative assays demonstrated that acetyl-CoA was significantly enriched in K93E/K101E hypersuccinylation mutants, whereas desuccinylation-mimetic K93R/K101R mutations effectively relieved acetyl-CoA overaccumulation (Fig. 6t). These findings provide additional experimental validation that SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation suppresses acetyl-CoA levels, corroborating prior mechanistic studies on this regulatory pathway.

To determine the mechanistic link between SUCLG2 succinylation and mitochondrial bioenergetics, we examined the functional consequences of acetyl-CoA metabolic flux alterations induced by this post-translational modification. Given that acetyl-CoA cannot enter cells directly, we used its metabolic precursor, sodium acetate trihydrate (SAT), to increase intracellular acetyl-CoA levels. As expected, acetyl-CoA levels in cells increased in a dose-dependent manner with SAT treatment (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Mitochondrial staining revealed that excessive SAT supplementation exacerbated mitochondrial fragmentation (Supplementary Fig. 7b, c). Notably, exogenous SAT treatment further aggravated this fragmentation under SUCLG2 hypersuccinylation and partially abrogated the beneficial mitochondrial morphology conferred by SUCLG2 desuccinylation (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Consistent with these morphological alterations, the protective effect of SUCLG2 desuccinylation on ΔΨm was also abolished by SAT overexposure (Supplementary Fig. 7d, e). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that site-specific desuccinylation at K93/K101 of SUCLG2 enhances mitochondrial function and quality control by maintaining cellular acetyl-CoA homeostasis.

SUCLG2 hypersuccinylation drives H4K8ac accumulation and chromatin remodeling

Acetyl-CoA serves as the exclusive donor for lysine histone acetylation and functions as a signaling molecule to modulate chromatin remodeling44. We hypothesize that SUCLG2 hypersuccinylation drives acetyl-CoA accumulation, which in turn remodels histone acetylation levels and ultimately modulates mitochondrial function. To further confirm whether acetyl-CoA oversupply mediated by SUCLG2 succinylation in the nucleus, we isolated nuclei for acetyl-CoA quantification. Our data demonstrated that SUCLG2 hypersuccinylation mimics at K93 and K101 significantly elevated nuclear acetyl-CoA levels, while desuccinylation mimics reduced acetyl-CoA content (Fig. 7a). Subsequent analysis of histone H4 acetylation sites (H4K5, H4K8, H4K12, and H4K16) revealed that hypersuccinylation at K93/K101 of SUCLG2 elevated H4 acetylation levels in GCs (Fig. 7b). In contrast, desuccinylation at these two sites of SUCLG2 reduced histone acetylation modification (Fig. 7b). Furthermore, SUCLG2 knockdown significantly enhanced H4K8ac accumulation, while SUCLG2 overexpression conversely reduced H4K8ac levels (Fig. 7c, d). We generated hypersuccinylated and desuccinylated mimic mutations simultaneously at the K93 and K101 residues of SUCLG2. Consistently, hypersuccinylation increased H4K8 acetylation levels, whereas desuccinylation suppressed this epigenetic modification (Fig. 7e). These findings establish that H4K8ac serves as a crucial regulatory target of SUCLG2 succinylation via nuclear acetyl-CoA metabolic flux, revealing an epigenetic regulatory axis in ovarian function (Fig. 7f).

Notably, alterations in histone acetylation directly modulate the transcriptional activation of target genes. To explore the potential function of H4K8ac, we performed CUT&Tag analysis to determine the downstream pathway of H4K8ac regulation in GCs. CUT&Tag data analysis demonstrated significant enrichment of H4K8ac peaks in GCs overexpressing SUCLG2 desuccinylation-mimicking mutations (K93R/K101R) (Fig. 7g). The distributions of the differential binding peaks obtained from the two groups in the whole-genome functional region were subsequently annotated. Consistently, 21.70% of differential binding peaks in the SUCLG2 WT vs. K93E/K101E comparison, and 19.47% in the WT vs. K93R/K101R comparison, were localized to the promoter-TSS region (Fig. 7h). However, only 6.80% of the peaks were located in the promoter-TSS region, and most of them were located in the intron region between the K93E/K101E and K93R/K101R group (Fig. 7h). Subsequently, through a de novo motif search with differential binding peaks between the two groups, we found that upregulated and downregulated genes with H4K8ac had different enriched DNA sequences (Fig. 7i). Differential peak analysis identified 104,310 upregulated peaks and 40,933 downregulated peaks in genes associated with SUCLG2 hypersuccinylation at K93 and K101, compared with those in the WT (Fig. 7j). The upregulated peaks were enriched in Gene Ontology (GO) terms related to “histone modification”, “cellular response to oxidative stress” and “regulation of chromosome organization”, and the downregulated peaks were enriched in “positive regulation of protein targeting to mitochondrion”, “mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid cycle enzyme complex” and “mitochondrial transport”, which enriched in mitochondrial function in SUCLG2 hypersuccinylation group (Fig. 7j). In contrast, the upregulated peaks were enriched in GO terms related to “regulation of autophagy”, “histone deacetylation”, “ovarian follicle development” and “mitochondrial fission-fusion” in SUCLG2 desuccinylation compared with the WT (Supplementary Fig. 8a). We subsequently compared differential peaks between the SUCLG2 hypersuccinylation group and desuccinylation group. As shown in the volcano plot, there were 153,358 differential peaks between K93E/K101E and K93R/K101R. Among these, 104,310 peaks were downregulated, and 49,048 peaks were upregulated in the desuccinylation group compared with the hypersuccinylation group (Supplementary Fig. 8b), suggesting that SUCLG2 succinylation at K93 and K101 promotes DNA binding to Histone H4K8ac. In SUCLG2 hypersuccinylation group, the upregulated differential peaks were enriched in “RNA polymerase II-specific DNA-binding transcription factor binding” and “chromatin remodeling”, whereas the downregulated peaks were associated with “cell fate commitment”, “positive regulation of cellular component biogenesis”, and “Mitochondrial respiratory chain complex assembly” (Supplementary Fig. 8b). CUT&Tag analysis revealed that mitochondrial function modulated by SUCLG2 succinylation is tightly coupled with epigenetic remodeling, thereby exerting a critical role in regulating cell fate. To further complement these findings, we performed RNA-seq analysis using the same experimental groups. The results revealed differentially expressed gene sets in SUCLG2 hypersuccinylation and desuccinylation groups when compared with the wild type (Supplementary Fig. 9a–c and Supplementary Data 5). Clusters of Orthologous Groups of Proteins (COG) analysis was used to classify the differentially expressed genes between groups. The results showed that these genes were significantly enriched in biological processes such as translation, posttranslational modification, and signal transduction mechanisms (Supplementary Fig. 9d–f). Therefore, we subsequently screened the specific demethylases (Phf8 and Kdm4b) involved in regulating histone methylation to assess whether H4K8ac regulates GC gene expression at a deeper level. Specifically, the binding peaks of Phf8 and Kdm4b to H4K8ac were decreased in the SUCLG2K93R/K101R mutation group (mimicking desuccinylation), whereas the SUCLG2K93E/K101E mutation (mimicking hypersuccinylation) enhanced their binding to H4K8ac (Fig. 7k,l). RT-qPCR validation confirmed that Phf8 and Kdm4b were markedly suppressed in SUCLG2K93R/K101R desuccinylation mutants compared with hypersuccinylation mimic (K93E/K101E) and WT controls. Conversely, the two genes were robustly upregulated in the K93E/K101E group (Fig. 7m,n). We subsequently detected the levels of histone methylation (H3K4me3, H3K9me3, and H3K27me2) in the SUCLG2 WT, K93E/K101E, and K93R/K101R mutant groups. These histone methylation sites are specific targets of demethylases such as Phf8 and Kdm4b45,46. The results revealed that these histone methylations were especially increased in the desuccinylation group (Fig. 7o). Notably, trimethylation at H3K9 is linked to transcriptional repression47,48. We further silenced Phf8 and Kdm4b with targeted specific siRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 8c, d), followed by detection of histone methylation profiles (H3K4me3, H3K9me3, H3K27me2). The results demonstrated that knockdown of either Phf8 or Kdm4b significantly elevated these histone methylation levels (Fig. 7p, q). Furthermore, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that the cellular senescence pathway was upregulated in the SUCLG2 hypersuccinylation group compared with the WT and desuccinylation groups (Fig. 7r). This global shift in the transcriptome explains the phenotype of cellular senescence accelerated by SUCLG2 hypersuccinylation. Transcriptomic analysis showed that SUCLG2 hypersuccinylation at K93/K101 activated the transcription of glycolipid metabolism genes (HLCS, GPD1, ACOT8, AGPAT4) and mitochondrial functional genes (SDHAF1, D2HGDH, CYP1B1; Fig. 7s). In sharp contrast, desuccinylation of these two sites markedly elevated the levels of key OXPHOS genes in GCs (ATP8B4, ATP5MD, ATP5ME, ATP5F1E, COX7A1, NDUFB1; Fig. 7t). Overall, hypersuccinylation at K93/K101 of SUCLG2 lead to nuclear acetyl-CoA accumulation to promote H4K8 acetylation. Combined with subsequent histone H3 demethylation, these epigenetic signals induce chromatin remodeling, reprogram metabolism, and thereby govern cellular senescence progression (Fig. 7u).

Acetyl-CoA oversupply accelerates ovarian aging in vivo

We next evaluated the effects of SUCLG2 overexpression or its succinylation mutants on etoposide-induced cellular senescence. Flow cytometry analysis showed that compared with the control group, etoposide increased the accumulation of cells in the G2 phase (Fig. 8a). However, in cells overexpressing SUCLG2 or carrying the K93R/K101R mutation, the G2-phase cell cycle arrest was reversed, while the K93E/K101E mutation had no significant effect (Fig. 8a). Consistently, SA-β-Gal staining revealed significant promotion of cellular senescence in etoposide-treated cells compared with controls, which was alleviated by K93R/K101R mutation (Fig. 8b). These data suggest that desuccinylation-mimicking mutation of SUCLG2 at the K93 and K101 sites relieves cellular senescence.

Given that SUCLG2 hypersuccinylation promotes acetyl-CoA accumulation and its role in cellular phenotypes, we administered sodium acetate trihydrate (SAT) to female mice as a metabolic precursor to elevate acetyl-CoA levels in vivo (Fig. 8c). Our results revealed that the ovarian weight and ovarian index (ovarian weight/body weight) of the mice administered SAT decreased compared with those of the control female mice (Fig. 8d–f). To further verify the metabolic mechanism underlying acetyl-CoA-dependent ovarian aging, we quantified the levels of acetyl-CoA, succinyl-CoA, and succinate in ovarian tissues following SAT treatment. Our results demonstrated that SAT markedly elevated the contents of acetyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA, whereas it significantly reduced intracellular succinate abundance (Fig. 8g–i). These findings further corroborate that elevated succinyl-CoA abundance may promote excessive succinylation of SUCLG2, thereby inhibiting its catalytic activity and repressing the conversion of succinyl-CoA to succinate. Immunoblotting results showed that the levels of global lysine acetylation and H4K8ac were increased in the ovaries of the SAT-treated mice compared with the control mice (Fig. 8j, k). Furthermore, the expression of the aging marker p21 was also elevated in the ovaries of SAT-treated mice, particularly in the GCs (Fig. 8l), suggesting that an excessive increase in acetyl-CoA levels raises the level of H4K8 acetylation, thereby accelerating ovarian aging. We subsequently examined the effects of excessive acetyl-CoA on ovarian morphology and follicular development in mice. The immunoblotting results revealed that the expression of FSHR and AMH in the ovaries of the SAT-treated mice was lower than that in the ovaries of the control mice (Fig. 8m). In addition, HE staining results revealed that the number of follicles in SAT-treated mouse ovaries decreased (Fig. 8n, o), particularly primordial (Pm), primary (Pr), and secondary-antral (Sec-Ant) follicles, with a significant reduction in their numbers (Fig. 8p). In contrast, activated primordial follicles (APF) showed a significant increase (Fig. 8p). These findings indicate that the ovarian reserve of SAT-treated mice was reduced. We subsequently mated the two groups of mice with young wild-type male mice and calculated the number of pups per pregnancy to assess the effect of acetyl-CoA accumulation on female fertility. The average litter size of the SAT-treated mice was significantly lower than that of the control mice (Fig. 8q). Taken together, these results suggest that SUCLG2 hypersuccinylation promotes excessive acetyl-CoA accumulation, thereby accelerating ovarian aging and impairing female fertility.

SUCLG2 desuccinylation ameliorates ovarian aging in vivo

To further investigate the effects of SUCLG2 desuccinylation on ovarian aging in vivo, we conducted intraovarian site-directed injections of adeno-associated virus 9 (AAV9) carrying SUCLG2 desuccinylation mutants at lysine residues K94 and K102 in 6-month-old mice (Fig. 9a). Three months following AAV9 transduction in mice, periodic acid-schiff staining with hematoxylin (PAS-H) demonstrated that the SUCLG2 desuccinylation group had a significantly higher number of follicular GCs surrounding oocytes when compared with the wild-type (WT) AAV9 group. (Fig. 9b). During ovarian aging, growing follicles harboring 20–30 μm oocytes displayed a sparse GC monolayer. Strikingly, SUCLG2 desuccinylation rescued follicular growth by reestablishing multilayered GC architecture, thereby restoring morphological integrity (Fig. 9b). Quantitative analysis revealed that desuccinylation of SUCLG2 significantly thickened the GC layer (Fig. 9c). Furthermore, immunostaining revealed that SUCLG2 desuccinylation significantly increased AMH expression in GCs, thereby indicating potential amelioration of ovarian reserve decline (Fig. 9d, e). Considering that SUCLG2 succinylation is closely associated with H4K8 acetylation. To investigate this, immunostaining showed that H4K8ac is predominantly localized in the GCs of aging ovaries (9-month-old mice, Fig. 9d). As expected, H4K8ac levels were significantly reduced in SUCLG2K94R/K102R mutants, in sharp contrast to the WT group (Fig. 9d, f), indicating that SUCLG2 desuccinylation effectively mitigates H4K8 hyperacetylation in ovaries. To further clarify the acetyl-CoA-linked metabolic regulation of ovarian aging, we measured acetyl-CoA, succinyl-CoA, and succinate in the ovaries of SUCLG2K94R/K102R mutant mice. Relative to wild-type ovaries, the mutant group showed decreased acetyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA abundance, alongside marked increases in succinate and the NAD⁺/NADH ratio (Fig. 9g–j). Such metabolic alterations indicate that K94/K102 desuccinylation of SUCLG2 improves its catalytic performance, alleviates acetyl-CoA overaccumulation, and ultimately suppresses histone acetylation.

We previously uncovered compelling evidence that SUCLG2 succinylation governs cellular senescence in vitro. To rigorously validate the translational relevance of this mechanism in vivo, we conducted comparative analyses of senescence levels in WT ovaries versus SUCLG2K94R/K102R mutant ovaries through SA-β-Gal staining assays. We observed that the SA-β-gal signal was specifically detected in the GCs of aged ovaries (Fig. 9k). By contrast, SUCLG2K94R/K102R mutant ovaries showed a significant decrease in SA-β-gal signaling compared with WT controls (Fig. 9k, l), demonstrating that SUCLG2 desuccinylation effectively attenuates age-related GC senescence. To elucidate GC function and the endocrine system in SUCLG2K94R/K102R mutant mice, we subsequently analyzed GC-derived hormones and gonadotropins. Serum concentrations of GC-derived hormones AMH, inhibin A, and estradiol were markedly elevated in SUCLG2K94R/K102R mutant female mice compared to WT mice at 9 months of age (Fig. 9m–o), suggesting that GC function is markedly improved by SUCLG2 desuccinylation. Conversely, the serum concentrations of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH) were significantly elevated in WT mice compared to SUCLG2K94R/K102R mutant mice (Fig. 9p, q). Notably, diminished serum AMH levels and elevated FSH concentrations serve as established diagnostic biomarkers for primary ovarian insufficiency in women49,50. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that desuccinylation at K94/K102 of SUCLG2 in mice counteracts age-related ovarian functional decline by restoring granulosa cell hormone secretory capacity and reestablishing hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis homeostasis. Interrogation of SUCLG2 desuccinylation has revealed its critical role in modulating the ovarian follicle reserve, as evidenced by the number of primordial follicles in SUCLG2K94R/K102R mutant mice being higher than that of WT mice (Fig. 9r). The ovaries of SUCLG2K94R/K102R mutant mice exhibited a significant increase in the development of follicles (preantral/secondary follicles), coupled with a marked decrease in activated primordial follicles (APF; oocyte diameter > 20 μm lacking cuboidal GCs), suggesting enhanced ovarian function. (Fig. 9r). Further fertility assessment showed that SUCLG2K94R/K102R mutant mice exhibited a significantly higher litter size compared to WT mice (Fig. 9s). Collectively, these pieces of evidence reveal that desuccinylation at K94/K102 of SUCLG2 significantly ameliorates ovarian aging and effectively mitigates age-associated follicular depletion by inhibiting granulosa cell senescence.

To distinguish whether ovarian aging is driven by impaired SIRT5/SUCLG2 signaling or widespread protein hyperacylation induced by cellular NAD⁺ exhaustion, we analyzed ovarian aging phenotypes in SUCLG2 wild-type and K94/K102 succinylation‑mutant mice with or without NMN (nicotinamide mononucleotide) supplementation (Supplementary Fig. 10a). PAS-H staining of ovarian tissues demonstrated that NMN supplementation increased follicle abundance in aged WT mice, yet failed to rescue follicle loss in mutant mice with constitutive succinylation at the K94 and K102 sites of SUCLG2 (Fig. 9t). Similarly, while NMN supplementation increased AMH and decreased FSH in wild-type mice, it failed to alleviate ovarian aging phenotypes in succinylation-mutant mice (Fig. 9u, v). Consistently, NAD⁺ replenishment could not rescue the reduced litter size in mice harboring succinylation at K94/K102 of SUCLG2 (Fig. 9w), further confirming that persistent SUCLG2 succinylation-mediated impairment of ovarian reserve is unresponsive to NMN intervention. Immunofluorescence staining of ovarian tissues showed that NMN supplementation reduced the levels of pan-succinylation and pan-acetylation in follicles of wild-type mice, while failing to decrease global acylation levels in follicles from mice with constitutive SUCLG2 K94/K102 succinylation (Supplementary Fig. 10b–d). Consistent with our observations, these data further confirm that SUCLG2 hypersuccinylation induces intracellular accumulation of acetyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA, thereby triggering global protein hyperacylation. Consistently, we found NMN markedly attenuated global succinylation in ovarian tissues from wild-type mice (Fig. 9x). Further IP assays confirmed that NMN reduced the succinylation levels of SUCLG2 (Supplementary Fig. 10e). Quantitative IP-MS analysis demonstrated that NMN decreased the intensity of succinylation at lysine residues K94 and K102 in wild-type mice (Fig. 9y). Collectively, these results demonstrate a key role of NAD+ in ovarian aging that is mediated by the SIRT5-SUCLG2 axis.

Discussion

Ovarian aging is a major contributor to the universal decline in fertility, endocrine signaling dysfunction, and the rising burden of chronic diseases in female mammals51. Some PTMs have been identified as important regulatory models closely related to ovarian function52. Among them, lysine succinylation (K-Suc) is a conserved PTM that is ubiquitous in prokaryotes and eukaryotes and is widely involved in metabolic-related diseases53,54. Compared with other chemical modifications, the succinylation of lysine residues has a profound effect on the structure and function of proteins due to the introduction of two negative charges and larger groups17. However, its potential role in ovarian function is poorly understood. Notably, SIRT5 is the only known desuccinylase responsible for “erasing” (desuccinylating) succinylation modifications located in mitochondria21,55–57. Here, we define a core succinylation regulatory axis, SIRT5 (desuccinylation eraser)-SUCLG2 (functional substrate), that modulates the key molecular events driving ovarian aging. Unlike cytoplasmic malonylation and glutarylation modifications, succinylation is more specific in mitochondria, where the modified substrate succinyl-CoA, which in turn is a metabolic substrate for the TCA cycle, controls mitochondrial metabolism25. This study reveals that protein hypersuccinylation is mechanistically linked to ovarian aging and is associated with suppressed SIRT5 expression. Specifically, we demonstrate that SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation at K93/K101 of SUCLG2 enhances its structural stability and catalytic activity. This posttranslational modification orchestrates acetyl-CoA homeostasis to maintain epigenetic fidelity, ultimately attenuating ovarian aging. Our study uncovers a pivotal NAD⁺‑SIRT5‑SUCLG2 signaling axis that orchestrates mitochondrial metabolism and epigenetic remodeling in the ovary. During ovarian aging, impaired NAD⁺/SIRT5 signaling induces excessive succinylation at K93/K101 of SUCLG2, perturbing TCA cycle homeostasis and driving massive acetyl‑CoA accumulation. These alterations are further corroborated by our observations that succinylation at K93/K101 of SUCLG2 causes reduced mitochondrial membrane potential and overt mitochondrial structural damage. Aberrant nuclear acetyl‑CoA buildup, resulting from compromised mitochondrial membrane integrity, further initiates extensive chromatin remodeling, which drives granulosa cell senescence and progressive ovarian functional decline. Physiologically, global hypersuccinylation accelerates SIRT5‑dependent NAD⁺ catabolism, thus forming a self‑reinforcing metabolic vicious cycle to fuel ovarian aging. Under sustained acylation stress, NAD⁺ acts as a critical rate‑limiting metabolite. Our data showed that NMN‑mediated NAD⁺ replenishment efficiently enhances mitochondrial sirtuin activity (particularly SIRT5) to alleviate pervasive acylation dysfunction. Such metabolic remodeling restores balanced mito‑nuclear crosstalk and ultimately attenuates age‑associated ovarian deterioration.

Current findings reveal that SIRT5 overexpression elevates intracellular NAD⁺ levels via desuccinylation of mitochondrial proteins. In vivo assays further confirmed that site‑specific desuccinylation at K94/K102 of SUCLG2 mitigates ovarian NAD⁺ overconsumption. As reported by Richard et al.58, succinylation stress triggers severe NAD⁺ depletion and restricts the activity of NAD⁺-dependent deacylases, highlighting NAD⁺ as a vital rate-limiting metabolite for balancing protein acylation and mitochondrial function. Notably, because both SIRT3 and SIRT5 are strictly NAD⁺-dependent deacylases, the NAD⁺ decline under succinylation stress not only impairs SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation but also restricts intrinsic SIRT3 activity. Consistently, our results confirmed that NMN supplementation replenishes cellular NAD⁺ pools, reduces global protein acetylation and succinylation. It restores the deacylation activity of SIRT5 and SIRT3, as evidenced by reduced succinylation at K93/K101 of SUCLG2. This metabolic correction ameliorates acylation dysregulation, stabilizes mitochondrial homeostasis, and relieves reproductive impairment caused by excessive succinylation. Consistent with recent studies59,60, maintaining NAD⁺ homeostasis is indispensable for balancing protein acylation patterns and preserving mitochondrial integrity under succinylation/acylation overload. NMN-mediated NAD⁺ restoration, therefore, represents a promising strategy to treat disorders associated with aberrant acylation.

Cellular senescence is a complex biological process often accompanied by the emergence of several markers, such as mitochondrial dysfunction, loss of protein homeostasis, and metabolic disorders61–63. Interestingly, our single-cell transcriptome analysis revealed that ovarian GCs exhibit metabolic hyperactivity, likely a compensatory response to mitochondrial dysfunction or insufficiency caused by cellular senescence, to meet the cells' survival demands. Importantly, SIRT5 plays a crucial role in regulating these events. In recent years, an increasing number of studies have shown that SIRT5 ameliorates ovarian aging64,65. However, the mechanistic underpinnings of SIRT5 in the regulation of ovarian function remain poorly characterized, warranting targeted investigations to elucidate its role in ovarian aging. In this study, we found that SIRT5 is closely associated with succinylation and ovarian aging, both in vitro and in vivo, and is involved in cellular metabolism. Specifically, we found that SIRT5 can affect the TCA cycle, resulting in changes in the contents of multiple metabolites. This depends on SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation of related metabolic enzymes in the TCA cycle, which in turn affects their activity. We found that SIRT5 expression is not fully dependent on mitochondrial mass and is specifically downregulated in the aging ovary. Accumulating evidence indicates that diminished SIRT5 expression is closely correlated with aging-related diseases61,66. In the present study, we observe that SIRT5 exhibits a selective downregulation during ovarian aging, a pattern not detected for other sirtuin family members or mitochondrial electron transport chain proteins. Our succinylome profiling further confirms that SIRT5 mediates protein desuccinylation across multiple subcellular compartments. The precise molecular mechanism underlying this specific age-related decline of SIRT5 in ovarian tissue warrants further exploration in future work.

Using succinylome proteomics, we mapped the global landscape of SIRT5-mediated protein succinylation, identifying 1,546 succinylated lysine sites across 632 proteins. These targets are primarily enriched in key metabolic pathways, including the TCA cycle, oxidative phosphorylation, ATP metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, β-oxidation, and amino acid catabolism. Consistent with our findings, researchers reported that SIRT5 predominantly targets metabolic proteins associated with ketogenesis (100%), β-oxidation (93%), amino acid catabolism (58%), TCA cycle (43%), and ATP synthase (43%)25,67. It is worth noting that the majority of mitochondrial proteins are encoded by the nucleus and are subsequently transported to the mitochondria to sustain mitochondrial metabolism. These proteins precisely regulate metabolic pathways within the mitochondria, such as the TCA cycle68, oxidative phosphorylation69, and fatty acid β oxidation70, through reversible chemical modifications. Their roles in aging-related diseases have garnered significant attention. Interestingly, our findings revealed that SIRT5 directly interacts with SUCLG2 and desuccinylates it at the K93 and K101 sites. A recent study has reported that SUCLG2 knockout leads to mitochondrial dysfunction in tumor cells and inhibits the function of other key metabolic enzymes23. Notably, Hu et al.23 demonstrated that K93 succinylation enhances SUCLG2 stability, whereas we observed the opposing regulatory effect. This divergence arises from distinct SUCLG2 succinylation landscapes between tumor cells and normal somatic cells. Only three succinylation sites were mapped in human lung cancer cells, while eight endogenous modification sites were identified in bovine ovarian GCs. Variations in the number of modified residues and cellular microenvironments drive opposing functional outcomes at the same lysine site, highlighting that SUCLG2 succinylation exhibits profound context-dependent functional plasticity. While aged ovaries undergo global hypersuccinylation, most modified lysine sites are functionally inert. SUCLG2 acts as a core enzyme governing mitochondrial TCA cycle and acyl-CoA metabolism. We found that the K93 and K101 residues of SUCLG2 are located within the conserved ATP-grasp domain and are highly conserved across diverse species. Both our in vivo and in vitro evidence demonstrate that SIRT5 exhibits potent desuccinylation activity at these two key lysine sites. Integrating molecular dynamics simulations and biochemical assays, we verified that succinylation at these two sites profoundly represses SUCLG2 catalytic activity in succinate production and accelerates its ubiquitin-dependent degradation. This dual regulatory mechanism compromises SUCLG2 function by impairing enzymatic activity and promoting protein breakdown, ultimately perturbing TCA cycle flux and mitochondrial metabolic homeostasis. Thus, K93 and K101 represent core functional sites of modification for TCA cycle modulation.

Compared with other studies, the present study revealed that hypersuccinylation of SUCLG2 promotes the excessive accumulation of acetyl-CoA. Similarly, Wu et al. reported that a deficiency in SUCLG2 leads to the accumulation of acetyl-CoA in T cells, thereby causing rheumatoid synovitis71. Furthermore, blood metabolomics has revealed that carbon dioxide produced by the excess oxidation of acetyl-CoA in the TCA cycle is one of the causes of mitochondrial dysfunction72. In this study, we discovered that the desuccinylation at 93 K/K101 of SUCLG2 suppressed its proteolytic activity and improved mitochondrial function, thereby inhibiting cellular senescence. This evidence indicates that hypersuccinylation of SUCLG2 impairs mitochondrial quality and function by elevating acetyl-CoA levels in response to cellular senescence. Notably, acetyl-CoA is the sole donor for histone acetylation in eukaryotic cells73,74. Numerous studies have shown that epigenetic alterations contribute to aging, including abnormal post-translational modifications of histones and altered genomic methylation patterns4. These histone modifications are also closely associated with tissue-specific changes in aging. Increased acetylation of H4K16 or H3K4 and decreased trimethylation of H3K9 or H3K27 have been observed in fibroblasts from aged individuals and those with progeroid syndrome75. Our study further demonstrated that hypersuccinylation of SUCLG2 increased histone H4K8 acetylation and decreased H3K9 trimethylation, thereby promoting mitochondrial metabolism gene expression and metabolic hyperactivity. The molecular mechanisms mediated by SUCLG2 corroborate our single-cell transcriptomic findings, revealing significant upregulation of metabolic pathways such as glycolipid metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation in aging ovarian GCs.

It is widely recognized that acetyl-CoA is a central metabolite; however, because of its instability, few studies have examined how alterations in acetyl-CoA levels affect the aging process. A recent study indicated a reduction in acetyl-CoA levels in the brains of aging mice. Conversely, the administration of small molecule compounds to elevate acetyl-CoA levels in the brain was associated with increased histone H3-specific locus acetylation and memory improvement76. However, studies on Drosophila surprisingly indicated that acetyl-CoA and citric acid levels increased in middle-aged individuals. Moreover, the knockdown of ACLY led to decreased acetyl-CoA levels, which resulted in metabolic changes and an extended lifespan77. In yeast, the overactivation of acetyl-CoA synthetase Acs2p promotes histone acetylation, which results in a shortened lifespan78. In this study, the in vivo administration of sodium acetate indicated that a high content of acetyl-CoA led to ovarian reserve loss and ovarian aging. Studies have shown that caloric restriction (CR) stimulates autophagy to delay aging in heart, skeletal muscle, and adipose tissue through decreasing acetyl-CoA levels79. This finding appears to corroborate our single-cell transcriptomic analysis, which revealed abnormally heightened metabolic activity in aged ovaries. Specifically, we observed that hypersuccinylation of SUCLG2, mediated by abnormal elevation of acetyl-CoA flux, accelerates ovarian aging. The role of acetyl-CoA in aging regulation is characterized by dual functionality and environmental dependency. Current evidence suggests that its biological effects are influenced by metabolic sources, subcellular localization, and regulatory pathways. Notably, the pro-senescence effects of acetyl-CoA seem to be mediated via chromatin remodeling mechanisms. In line with our findings, Etoh et al.80 demonstrated that ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY)-mediated acetyl-CoA production promotes the transcriptional activation of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) genes through H3K27 histone acetylation in senescent cells, thereby driving cellular senescence.

We consistently observed elevated succinyl-CoA and acetyl-CoA levels in aged ovaries, and these two key substrates of protein modification can exert pleiotropic regulatory effects on mitochondrial function and chromatin remodeling. For mitochondrial function, succinyl-CoA, as a key mitochondrial modification substrate, mediates mitochondrial protein succinylation to regulate metabolic enzyme activity and mitophagy. Nengroo et al.81 reported succinate-induced succinyl-CoA elevation causes TCA cycle protein succinylation and impairs purine synthesis, while Lancaster et al.82 linked succinyl-CoA accumulation to cerebral hypersuccinylation and mitochondrial dysfunction. Wang et al.83 showed SIRT5 deletion leads to excessive mitochondrial UCP1 succinylation and disrupted respiration. Acetyl-CoA acts as a signaling metabolite connecting metabolism to mitophagy via NLRX1, supporting mitochondrial quality control84. For chromatin remodeling, nuclear succinyl-CoA and acetyl-CoA serve as histone modification substrates. Wang et al.13 demonstrated that succinyl-CoA enables KAT2A-mediated H3K79 succinylation, and Gao et al.85 revealed that SUCLG1 depletion impairs BRD4-chromatin interaction via histone succinylation. Our identified SUCLG2 regulates acetyl-CoA flux and H4K8 acetylation. Citrate produced by the TCA cycle can be cleaved and converted into acetyl-CoA by ATP-citrate lyase, and then participates in histone acetylation86. Li et al.87 further demonstrated that SREBP1-mediated elevation of the acetyl-CoA reservoir enhances histone acetylation and induces vascular smooth muscle cell senescence via epigenetic reprogramming. These mechanisms reveal crosstalk between mitochondrial dysfunction and epigenetic alteration in ovarian aging.

Taken together, we establish a conserved NAD⁺/SIRT5/SUCLG2 regulatory axis that governs ovarian aging. Reduced SIRT5 expression induces excessive SUCLG2 succinylation at K93/K101, which disrupts TCA cycle homeostasis and mitochondrial integrity, thereby triggering nuclear acetyl‑CoA leakage. Excess nuclear acetyl‑CoA further initiates chromatin remodeling and ultimately accelerates ovarian aging progression (Supplementary Fig. 11). Nevertheless, the upstream mechanisms driving ovarian NAD⁺ depletion and the deacylation functions of other sirtuins remain uncharacterized in our work. Unraveling the intricate crosstalk between mitochondrial metabolism and epigenetic modulation is critical for combating reproductive aging. Targeted supplementation with NAD⁺ precursors such as NMN offers a viable translational avenue to activate this signaling cascade and attenuate ovarian aging.

Methods

Animals

All experimental animals were used in accordance with the regulations of the ethics committee of Southwest University, and the approval number is IACUC-20220803-03. The bovine ovarian tissue samples used in this study were collected from Holstein cows at Chongqing Jinhang Halal Cattle Products Co., Ltd. Individuals were divided into a young group (3–5 years old) and an aged group (≥10 years old). KM mice were purchased from Ensiweier (Chengdu, China), and SIRT5-KO mice were provided by Shanghai Model Organisms Center (SMOC, Shanghai, China). In vivo feeding experiments were conducted using 4-month-old female mice. All experimental mice were housed in the same room and were aged together, and the room temperature was maintained at 18 °C–24 °C and the humidity was between 40 and 70%. Mice were maintained on a 12-hour light/dark cycle with access to food and water ad libitum. The 4-month-old mice were randomly assigned into three groups and fed with sodium succinate (100 mM), sodium acetate trihydrate (200 mM) and water, respectively. Similarly, the 6-month-old mice received ovarian in situ injections of AAV9 virus carrying either the SUCLG2K94R/K102R mutant or the SUCLG2 plasmid. The 8-month-old mice with or without persistent hypersuccinylation at K94/K102 of SUCLG2 received intraperitoneal injections of NMN (200 mg/kg) or PBS daily for 30 consecutive days.

Preparation and culture of primary ovarian granulosa cells

The ovaries were collected from the slaughterhouse and immediately placed in sterile saline at 37 °C. Wash the ovaries in the laboratory sequentially with sterile saline (three times), 75% alcohol (30 s), and sterile saline (three times). Use a 5 mL sterile syringe to aspirate the follicular fluid from the ovarian follicles, ensuring it is clean, transparent, and free from blood contamination. The follicular fluid was filtered through a cell filter (70 μm) and transferred to a new 15 mL centrifuge tube. After centrifugation at 200 × g for 10 min, the cell pellet was treated with red blood cell lysis buffer (Solarbio) for 30 s and then washed twice with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Gibco) to collect the cell pellet. The cell pellet was resuspended in DMEM/F-12 (Gibco) complete medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cytiva), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 mg/mL streptomycin (Solarbio). Finally, the cell suspension was inoculated into a 25 cm2 cell culture flask and cultured in a cell incubator (37 °C, 5%CO2). In this study, Etoposide (Beyotime) was used to induce cellular senescence in vitro88,89. GCs were treated with specific inhibitors (100 µM 3-TYP, 1 µM TSA and 20 µM YZL-51 N; all from Selleck) for 24 h to inhibit the respective deacylation activities of SIRT3, HDACs and SIRT7.

Microinjection into ovaries

For ovarian injections, two ventrolateral incisions were made approximately 2 mm caudal to the last rib in 6-month-old anesthetized mice. The ovary was exteriorized using fine forceps, with care taken to avoid damaging the vasculature and ovarian capsule. The fat pad surrounding the oviduct and ovarian bursa was gently stabilized, and a glass micropipette was advanced into the sub-tunica albuginea space using a micromanipulator. Mice were injected with adeno-associated virus 9 (AAV9) at a dose of 1 × 1011 vector genomes (vg) per mouse. The commercially available AAV9 vectors were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences, including the control construct AAV9-EGFP-SUCLG2 (catalog No. 20240305AAVH6-S), the desuccinylation-mimetic SUCLG2 mutant vector AAV9-EGFP-SUCLG2-K94R/K102R (catalog No. 20250102AAVH5), and the hypersuccinylation-mimetic SUCLG2 mutant vector AAV9-EGFP-SUCLG2-K94E/K102E (catalog No. 20250102AAVH6).

Overexpression plasmid and siRNA transfection

The overexpression plasmids of SIRT5 (catalog No. GOSA0337089), SUCLG2 wild-type (catalog No. GOSE5001849), SUCLG2K93E (catalog No. GOSE0383394) mutants, SUCLG2K93R (catalog No. GOSE0383395), SUCLG2K101E (catalog No. GOSE0383396), SUCLG2K101R (catalog No. GOSE0383397), SUCLG2K93E/K101E (catalog No. GCPE0386754), and SUCLG2K93R/K101R (catalog No. GCPE0440466) mutants were constructed and provided by GeneChem. According to the instructions, the plasmids were transfected into GCs using LipofectamineTM LTX reagent and PLUSTM reagent (Thermo, 15338). After transfection for 8-12 h, the cells were replaced with fresh complete medium and continued to be cultured for 48 h.

Negative control (NC) siRNA and siRNAs against SIRT5, SUCLG2, PHF8, KDM4B, and KAT2A were synthesized by Sangon Biotech. The full sequences of all siRNAs are provided in Supplementary Data 6. Then, GCs were transfected with RNATransMate (Sangon Biotech). siRNA and RNATransMate were mixed in DMEM, respectively, at room temperature for 10 min, and then mixed and incubated for 15 min, so that the final concentration of siRNA was 20 nM. The mixture was added to the cultured cells, changed to a complete medium after 8 h, and continued to be cultured for 48 h.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

Ovarian tissue was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. The tissue sections of the ovaries were performed by the Servicebio company. The sections were stained with anti-Succinyllysine (PTM-419) and anti-p21 (10355-1-AP) antibodies. Micrographs were obtained using a microscope.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Ovarian tissue sections were prepared as previously described, or a total of 5 × 104 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate with a cell slide. The cell slides were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. Subsequently, the cells were washed three times with PBS, then sealed with a blocking buffer (3% BSA + 0.2% Trition-X-100 in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature. The samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C using anti-FSHR (1:100, Proteintech, 22665-1-AP), anti-SIRT5 (1:100, Proteintech, 15122-1-AP), anti-Succinyllysine (1:100, PTM, PTM-419), anti-DDX4 (1:100, ABclonal, A27291), anti-H4K8ac, anti-AMH (1:100, ABclonal, A8538) and anti-SUCLG2 (1:100, 14240-1-AP) antibodies. After being washed with PBS, fluorescent secondary antibodies (1:500) were added with 1 h of incubation in the dark. Then DAPI (Beyotime) was used for nuclear staining. Finally, the fluorescence microscope was used for photographing.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8, MedChem Express) was used to examine cell viability. Briefly, the treated cells were seeded in a 96-well plate and incubated with 10 μL of CCK-8 solution at 37 °C for 1 h. The optical density (OD) value of each well was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo) at a wavelength of 450 nm, and the relative viability of the cells was calculated. The proliferation ability of cells was evaluated using the BeyoClickTM EdU Cell Proliferation Kit with Alexa Fluor 555 (Beyotime). The procedure was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After EdU staining, cells were visualized using a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Axio observer 3), and the percentage of Edu-positive cells was calculated.

SA-β-gal staining

Cellular senescence was detected following the experimental protocol of the cell senescence β-galactosidase staining kit (Beyotime). For the senescence staining of GCs, the cultured cells were seeded in 24-well plates, washed with PBS, and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min. After fixation, the cells were washed three times with PBS, and subsequently, 300 μL of the prepared staining working solution was added. Finally, the 24-well plates were sealed with a preservative film to prevent evaporation and incubated in an incubator at 37 °C overnight. The stained cells were observed and photographed under an optical microscope (Leica). For the senescence staining of ovaries, the ovarian tissue samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 24 h, followed by cryoprotection through sucrose gradient dehydration (30% concentration) for 60 min. Specimens were subsequently embedded in OCT compound and cryosectioned into serial 8-μm thick paraffin sections. Following PBS buffer rinses to rehydrate the frozen sections, tissues were subjected to senescence-associated SA-β-Gal enzymatic staining through overnight incubation at 37 °C. After washing, nuclear counterstaining was performed using Fast Red solution. Subsequently, histological preparations were then visualized and documented through bright-field microscopy imaging.

Cell cycle distribution assay

After the cells were cultured and treated in 6-well plates, they were collected by trypsin digestion. Then, the cells were fixed in precooled 70% ethanol at 4 °C overnight. After washing, propidium (PI, Beyotime) staining was added and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Cells were filtered through 70-μm cell strainers to obtain single cells prior to detection. Finally, the cell cycle distribution was analyzed by flow cytometry (BD Biosciences). The data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Protein extraction and western blot analysis

After the ovarian tissue had been ground or the treated cells collected, an appropriate amount of RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and deacetylase inhibitors was added to extract total protein. In some experiments, the cytoplasmic proteins and the mitochondrial proteins were obtained using a Cell Mitochondrial Separation Kit. All the operation steps are carried out at 4 °C. The BCA protein concentration assay kit was used to measure the protein concentration, and the protein sample was mixed with the 5× protein loading buffer in a certain proportion and then denatured. An equal amount of protein (20 μg) was added to a 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide (SDS-PAGE) gel for electrophoretic separation and then transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, Millipore) membrane for blocking at room temperature for 90 min. The PVDF membrane was incubated with the primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The membrane was washed three times with TBST the next day, 10 min each time, and then the membrane was incubated with the horseradish peroxidase HRP-conjugated mouse or rabbit secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. Finally, the ECL chemiluminescence kit (Vazyme) was used to visualize the bands on the Bio-Rad imaging system, and band intensities were quantified using Image Lab software.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from GCs using TRIzol reagent (Takara, Japan) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. First-strand cDNA was synthesized as previously reported. Following reverse transcription, quantitative analysis of target transcripts was performed on a CFX96™ Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA) with the SYBR Premix Ex Taq Ⅱ (2×) Kit (Takara, Japan). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method, with β-actin serving as the internal reference for normalization. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Data 7.

Immunoprecipitation

The target antibody with a final concentration of 20 μg/ml was incubated with protein A + G magnetic beads for 1 h at room temperature. Then, the treated cells were washed with PBS, added with Lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors, and lysed at 4 °C for 30 min. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 2500 × g for 2 min and incubated with magnetic beads bound to specific antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The magnetic beads were washed three times with Lysis buffer, added with an appropriate volume of SDS loading buffer, and boiled at 95 °C for 10 min for elution. Finally, the immunoprecipitation complex was analyzed by Western blot.

Mito-tracker red

The treated GCs were seeded in 20-mm-diameter confocal petri dishes at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well. To assess mitochondrial mass changes, according to the manufacturer’s descriptions, the mitochondria of live cells were stained using Mito-Tracker Red (Beyotime, C1035). Finally, cells were observed and filmed on a laser confocal scanning microscope system (Olympus, Japan).

Mitophagy assay

The treated GCs were seeded in 20-mm-diameter confocal petri dishes at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well. Mitophagy in live cells was monitored using the Mitophagy detection kit (DOJINDO, MD01). Briefly, the cells were washed three times with serum-free medium, triple-stained with Mtphagy Dye (100 nM), Lyso Dye (1 μM), and Mito-Tracker Deep Red (200 nM), and incubated in an incubator at 37 °C for 30 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the cells were washed three times with serum-free medium and observed on a laser confocal microscope. The level of mitophagy was defined by the area of Mtphagy dye per cell. The levels of colocalization of both Mitophagy dye and Lysosome dye were also analyzed. Quantification analysis was performed using Image J.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (△Ψm) assay

The treated GCs were seeded in 20-mm-diameter confocal petri dishes at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well. The cells were stained with JC-1 fluorescent probes (Beyotime, C2003S) and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. Quantification for the △Ψm assay was carried out using Image J to measure the fluorescence intensity ratio of JC-1 aggregate/JC-1 monomer.

Nuclear isolation

The nuclei were isolated using a nuclei extraction kit (Solarbio, SN0020). Briefly, a total of 5 × 107 cells per group were collected and extracted with precooled lysis buffer. After adding Reagent A, the cell suspension was ground 30 times using a small-volume glass homogenizer. The nuclei were then harvested by centrifugation at 700 × g for 5 min. After resuspending, the nuclei were purified with Medium buffer. Finally, purer nuclei were preserved at store buffer for acetyl-CoA assay.

Measurement of acetyl-CoA, succinyl-CoA, succinate and NAD+/NADH ratio

For the analysis of acetyl-CoA production, the cellular or nuclear levels of acetyl-CoA were quantified using an acetyl-CoA assay kit (Solarbio, BC0980). Succinyl-CoA levels were measured using an ELISA kit (Mlbio, China). In brief, the total cells (5 × 106 cells) or purified nuclei (5 × 107 cells) per group were lysed with extraction buffer. Following centrifugation at 200 × g for 10 min, the supernatants were incubated with assay buffer at 37 °C. The absorbance values at 340 nm were then measured at 20 s (A20s) and 80 s (A80s) using a microplate reader. The level of acetyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA was represented by the value of A80s minus A20s. To quantify succinate, the cellular levels of succinate were determined using an amplex red succinate assay kit (Beyotime, S0529S). Specifically, the total cells (5 × 106 cells) per group were lysed with BeyoLysis™ Buffer. The lysed samples were then incubated with amplex red working solution for 30 min at 37°C. Succinate levels were measured with an excitation wavelength of 560 nm and an emission wavelength of 590 nm. The intracellular NAD+/NADH ratio was quantified using a NAD/NADH-Glo™ assay (Promega, G9071). Lysed cells (5 × 106 cells) were incubated in 0.4 nM HCl for 15 min at 60 °C. After acid treatment, Trizma® base was added to neutralize the cellular environment. The samples were then incubated with NAD/NADH-Glo™ for 30 min at 37 °C, and detected using a fluorescence microplate reader.

Transmission electronic microscopy (TEM) imaging

GCs were collected and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde. Then, 1% OsO4 in PBS was used to fix for 2 h at room temperature, followed by dehydration with gradient alcohol. Subsequently, the sections were embedded by baking in an oven at 60 °C for 48 h and cut into ultrathin sections (60 nm) with an ultramicrotome. Finally, a transmission electron microscope (HITACHI, HT7700) was used for observation.

Histological analysis and follicle counting

For quantitative analysis of follicle numbers, ovaries were collected and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight. After dehydration, tissues were embedded in paraffin and cut into sections (5 μm), followed by DDX4 immunostaining. Raw follicle counts were multiplied by five to correct for unanalyzed sections, yielding estimated total follicle numbers per ovary90. Follicles were classified into primordial (Pm, oocyte diameter smaller than 20 μm with a layer flat pre-GCs), activated primordial (APF: oocyte diameter greater than 20 μm without cuboidal GCs), primary (Pr, oocyte diameter greater than 20 μm with a single layer of cuboidal GCs), or secondary-antral (Sec-Ant, oocyte with two or more cuboidal GCs) stages. Six ovaries for each group were analyzed.

Measurement of hormones

Mice at random estrous cycle stages were anesthetized with tribromoethanol prior to cardiac puncture for blood collection, followed by euthanasia via cervical dislocation. The collected blood was coagulated at room temperature and subsequently centrifuged at 10,000 g for 5 min to extract serum. The levels of AMH, inhibin A, β-estradiol, FSH and LH in the serum of mice were measured by a competitive-based enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using an AMH ELISA kit (Abcam, ab320790), an inhibin A ELISA kit (Biomatik, EKF59207), an β-estradiol ELISA kit (Cayman, 501890), an FSH ELISA kit (Invitrogen, EEL097), and an LH ELISA Kit (Invitrogen, EEL121).Absorbance readings were acquired at 450 nm wavelength employing an automated microplate reader. Results were expressed in ng/mL for AMH and FSH, and pg/mL for inhibin A, β-estradiol, and LH.

Fertility test

Sexually mature male mice (15-week-old) were used to continuously mate with female mice in the sodium acetate group, the sodium succinate group, the SUCLG2 desuccinylation mutant group, and the corresponding control group (n = 6 for each group). The litter size for each group was recorded.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

According to the SUCLG2 protein (Q3MHX5) sequence obtained from the Uniport database, AlphaFold3 was used to predict the interaction model between protein and GDP model, and PyMOL was used for succinylation. The molecular dynamics simulation was performed using the Gromacs (2023.2) program. The established system first was performed energy minimization and then performed NVT and NPT equilibrium dynamics at 200 ps and 100 ps, respectively, and molecular dynamics sampling was performed for 100 ns. The root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), and radius of gyration (Rg) were calculated by the Gromacs module.

SIRT5 desuccinylation assay in vitro

The in vitro desuccinylation assay at the K93 and K101 sites of SUCLG2 was carried out as previously reported91. Briefly, 1 mg SIRT5 and 5 µg succinylated peptides (GRGK(Suc)GVFNSGLKGGV or GRGKGVFNSGLK(Suc)GGV) were incubated in reaction buffer comprising 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1 mM DTT, and 1 mM NAD⁺. The mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Reactions were then quenched using 50 mL methanol supplemented with 200 mM HCl and 320 mM acetic acid. The peptides were analyzed by HPLC-MS/MS, and target products were quantified based on their peak areas.

Analysis of scRNA-seq data of ovaries

We utilized scRNA-seq data to investigate age-associated molecular alterations during ovarian aging. The publicly available single-cell RNA-seq datasets GSM7325156 (3-month-old), GSM7325157 (3-month-old), GSM7325158 (3-month-old), GSM7325159 (3-month-old), GSM7325160 (9-month-old), GSM7325161 (9-month-old), GSM7325162 (9-month-old), and GSM7325163 (9-month-old) were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). For further analysis of scRNA-seq data, this integrated computational pipeline employed Seurat v4.3.0.1 and Harmony v0.1.1. The process began with stringent quality control (features > 200, mitochondrial genes <20%), followed by SCTransform normalization and the identification of 2000 highly variable genes. After merging samples and performing principal component analysis (PCA), Harmony was applied to correct batch effects. Cell clusters were identified using the top ten principal components, visualized via t-SNE, and ultimately annotated into ten distinct subpopulations based on marker gene expression from CellMarker 2.0. Final annotations encompassed GCs, luteal cells, Theca cells, oocytes, endothelium cells, epithelium cells, stroma cells, phagocyte cells, B cells, and T cells. To identify age-related transcriptional changes, we conducted differential gene expression (DGE) analysis between young and aging groups using Seurat’s FindMarkers function. DGE analysis was carried out to compare gene expression profiles between young and aged samples, with significance defined as P < 0.05 and |log2FC | > 1.25. Gene ontology (GO) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) analyses were performed to explore the functional enrichment of these aging-related genes. For pathway activity analysis, we employed the AddModuleScore and scMetabolism algorithms within the Seurat framework to quantify pathway activation levels at single-cell resolution. Pathway scores were subsequently standardized through z-score normalization, with the resultant values representing relative pathway activity magnitudes. Statistical significance was determined using a Bonferroni correction threshold (adjusted P < 0.05) for multiple hypothesis testing. CellPhoneDB Python package (version 2.1.7) was employed to systematically analyze cell-cell communication networks among ovarian cell subtypes, comparing signaling pathway activity between young and aged mice. To assess granulosa cell fate trajectories during ovarian aging, GC marker genes were partitioned into four distinct clusters: preantral, antral, mitotic, and atretic GCs30–37, with preantral GCs designated as the developmental origin. Subsequently, we reconstructed pseudotime trajectories of GCs in young and aged ovarian samples using Monocle 3.

Energy metabolomics

Energy metabolomics was performed and analyzed by MetWare (Wuhan) based on the AB Sciex QTRAP 6500 LC-MS/MS platform. Differential metabolites between groups were determined by VIP (variable importance in projection) >1 and |Log2FC | > 0.26. Two-group comparisons of metabolite abundances were conducted via two-sided Welch’s t-test.

4D quantitative succinylation proteomics

4D quantitative succinylation proteomics was performed and analyzed by PTM Bio (Hangzhou). After transfection of the SIRT5 overexpression plasmid in GCs for 48 h, the cells were collected and sonicated three times on ice using a high-intensity ultrasonic processor (Scientz) in lysis buffer (8 M urea and 1% protease inhibitor cocktail). The treated cells per group of six biologically replication were mixed into a pool. Then, it was centrifuged at 12,000 g at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration was determined with a BCA kit. After being reduced with 5 mM dithiothreitol for 30 min at 56 °C and being alkylated with 11 mM iodoacetamide for 15 min at room temperature in the dark, the protein sample was diluted to a concentration less than 2 M by adding 100 mM TEAB to the mixture. Finally, trypsin was added at 1:50 and 1:100 trypsin-to-protein mass ratio, respectively, for the first and second digestion.

To enrich modified peptides, tryptic peptides dissolved in NETN buffer (100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 50 mM Tris-HCl, 0.5% NP-40) were incubated with prewashed antibody beads (anti-succinyllysine antibody) at 4 °C overnight. Then the beads were washed four times with NETN buffer, twice with H2O, and three times with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid to elute the bound peptides. Finally, the eluted peptides were combined and vacuum-dried.

The tryptic peptides were dissolved in solvent A (0.1% formic acid, 2% acetonitrile in water) and directly loaded onto a home-made reversed-phase analytical column (25 cm length, 75 μm i.d.). Peptides were separated with a gradient consisting of solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile) with increasing concentrations from 6 to 24% over 70 min, 24 to 35% in 14 min, and reaching 80% in 3 min, then holding at 80% for the last 3 min, all at a constant flow rate of 450 nL/min on a nanoElute UHPLC system. The peptides were subjected to a capillary source followed by the timsTOF Pro (Bruker Daltonics) mass spectrometry. The electrospray voltage applied was 1.60 kV. Precursors and fragments were analyzed at the TOF detector, with a MS/MS scan range from 100 to 1700 m/z. The timsTOF Pro was operated in parallel accumulation serial fragmentation (PASEF) mode. Precursors with charge states 0 to 5 were selected for fragmentation, and 10 PASEF-MS/MS scans were acquired per cycle, with 30 s dynamic exclusion.

The Maxquant search engine (v.1.6.15.0) was used for analysis of the MS/MS data. Tandem mass spectra were searched against the human SwissProt database concatenated with the reverse decoy database. Trypsin/P was specified as a cleavage enzyme, allowing up to two missing cleavages. The mass tolerance was set to 20 ppm and 5 ppm, respectively, in the first search and main search for precursor ions, and 0.02 Da for fragment ions. Carbamidomethyl on Cys was specified as a fixed modification, and acetylation on protein N-terminal and oxidation on Met were specified as variable modifications. The quantitative approach for succinylation lysine site identification and quantitation in this study was performed following the method reported by Meier et al.92, which employed a label-free 4D modified proteomics and proteomics quantification strategy. Briefly, after enrichment of succinylated peptides, the samples were subjected to mass spectrometry analysis using the data-independent acquisition (DIA) full-scan acquisition mode. During tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) detection, comprehensive information of all fragment ions was collected. Finally, the quantitative comparison between experimental groups was conducted based on the intensity of mass spectral peaks. Based on the protocol reported by Meier et al.92, the FDR was adjusted to <1%, which was set for modified peptides. Further normalization and quantitative analysis took the change of differential expression > 1.5 as the threshold of significant up-regulation and <1/1.5 as the threshold of significant down-regulation, respectively.

CUT & tag

The library was constructed using the Hyperactive Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit for Illumina Pro (Vazyme, TD904) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the treated GCs were collected and incubated with concanavalin A-binding magnetic beads, followed by incubation with anti-H4K8ac (ABclonal, A7258) and secondary antibodies, and then with pA-Tn5 transposase. After transposon activation and labeling, DNA was extracted and amplified, then purified using VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, N411) to construct a library. Finally, sequencing was performed on an Illumina NoveSeq 150PE platform by 10× Genomics (Shanghai). Differential peak detection was based on MACS2 callpeak.

RNA sequencing

GCs were seeded in 6-well plates, and after transfection with overexpression plasmids for 48 h, the total RNA samples were isolated using Trizol Reagent (Invitrogen, USA), and RNA concentration and quality were detected. Qualified samples were subjected to library construction and quality control. RNA sequencing was performed by Biomarker Technology (Beijing) based on Illumina. Differential transcripts were analyzed by two-sided Wald test in DESeq2, with P values adjusted by Benjamini-Hochberg FDR. Only the Fold Change ≥ 1.5 and P value < 0.05 were used as criteria for screening differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 or Image J software. Statistical details of experiments can be found in the figure legends and the respective methods section. Datasets with only two independent groups were analyzed using an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test. A final fold change was calculated by averaging the two experiments together, and the P-values of the two separate experiments were combined using Fisher’s method. Unless otherwise specified, for normally distributed data, data are represented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was reported follows, P value < 0.05 was considered significant.
