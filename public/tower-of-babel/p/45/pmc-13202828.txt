Patient partnership in primary health care systems of the future: a structured priority setting exercise
Authors: Rebecca Ganann, Maggie MacNeil, Aya Tagami, Jennifer Boyle, Clare Cruickshank, Aref Alshaikhahmed, Joan Barker, JoAnn Stans, Ashley Chisholm, Gulafroz Akbari, Ron Beleno, Mary Huang, Caron Leid, Vivian R Ramsden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrimary health care (PHC) provides holistic care to patients across the lifespan and offers a whole-society approach to health and wellbeing. PHC services are accessed by more people than any other type of healthcare in Ontario. While patient engagement in research is increasingly common, it is less common in PHC to engage patients in decision-making beyond point-of-care. Roles for patient partners in learning health systems, including PHC, can be unclear and yet patient partners desire opportunities to meaningfully contribute to these rapid cycles of learning and improvement. Facilitating patient engagement in multiple PHC domains (design, delivery, research, and education) may improve how PHC is organized at local, regional, and provincial levels, and how responsive it is to patient needs.MethodsTo explore ways to integrate patient partners into future PHC systems, the study activities were structured into six stages of group concept mapping (which integrates qualitative individual and group processes with multivariate statistics to describe a topic). Our objective was to engage Canadian PHC interest holders to answer the following research question: “In what ways can patient partners support PHC design, delivery, research, and education?”. Ideas were generated by academics/researchers/health professionals [n = 40; 43%], patients/caregivers[n = 19; 20%], trainees [n = 17; 18%], ‘other’ interest holders [n = 11; 18%] and policy/decision makers [n = 7; 7%]. Eleven patients/caregivers were engaged as partners in study planning, implementation and knowledge mobilization; eight also acted as participants in concept mapping activities. Data were collected through an in-person workshop in Ontario, CA and online.ResultsEighty-four discrete ideas were identified, rated and sorted into a nine-cluster concept map. The most important and feasible clusters identified roles in advancing equity, informing research funding/priorities, and supporting patient partnerships. A cluster of ideas about integrating patient partners in PHC policy and governance roles was deemed important but more challenging to enact.ConclusionPHC reform presents opportunities to integrate patient partners’ lived and living experiences and their expertise into health systems. Doing so will contribute to a PHC system informed by patients’ priorities, maximizing real-world impact.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40900-026-00905-5.

Background

Patients can actively contribute to health systems beyond being recipients of care. Canada’s Strategy for Patient Oriented Research (SPOR) sees patients as active partners in health research to improve health outcomes and enhance the health care system [1]. The Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR)’s definition of patient engagement includes individuals with personal experience of living with an illness or other health condition, and family/friend caregivers [2]. Patients, caregivers, and families can be meaningfully engaged as partners across all stages of the research process (governance, priority setting, research question development, collection of research data, and sharing research results and outcomes) [2].

Patient-engaged research can be more relevant, credible, and fundable with stronger societal benefits than research conducted without patient engagement [3, 4]. Patient partners have also reported benefits including new learning, building relationships, facilitating communication, and breaking down barriers, as well as psychological and social benefits from their involvement in research [5]. At a system level, patient engagement contributes to better care outcomes and experiences for patients and caregivers, more efficient use of health system resources, and improved provider experiences [6, 7]. As such, there is increased recognition of the value of patient-oriented approaches in health and health services research, locally, provincially, nationally and internationally.

More specifically, patient engagement is becoming increasingly common in primary health care (PHC) research [8–12]. PHC is a whole-society approach to health and well-being, providing the first point of contact for accessing long-term, person-focused, comprehensive, and coordinated care for most health needs [13, 14]. Patients receive PHC services throughout their lifespan, encompassing health promotion, prevention, treatment, rehabilitation, and palliative care [15]. Administrative billing data shows that in Ontario, more people access primary care services than any other type of healthcare [16]. Consensus has formed around the need for better integration of patient expertise in both PHC research and health systems transformation [17–21]; therefore, it is essential to identify tangible strategies to support integrating this expertise.

A PHC system informed by patient-oriented research (POR) would be more responsive to the needs of thousands of Canadians who receive primary care every day [22]. Yet, relatively few studies exist examining patient-oriented PHC and its impacts compared to POR in other health sectors [8]. Furthermore, although PHC clinicians are experts at engaging patients at the point-of-care, patient integration is less common at other levels of PHC (e.g., clinic-level decisions about care delivery, health systems, professional education, and policy making) [23]. Integrating patient lived experience and expertise at levels beyond point of care can be challenging given the resource-intensity of meaningful engagement work and capacity challenges in the PHC sector, as a whole (e.g., fewer family physicians providing comprehensive primary care, limited support for PHC clinician researchers) [19, 20, 24, 25]. Yet, our work assumes that there is inherent value in having patients’/people’s lived and living (PWLLE) experiences inform PHC at various levels (i.e., design, delivery, education, and research). Adopting a multi-level conceptualization of PHC recognizes a complex system that operates at various levels and extends beyond the patient-provider relationship. We conceived these levels of PHC by drawing on Carman et al.’s [26] framework for engaging patients and Vanstone et al.’s [9] systematic review which categorized health professions education as one of six domains where patients act as partners in health systems.

Previous PHC research has focused on identifying patient priorities for organizing care, without specific attention to identifying roles for patient partners to actively contribute to addressing the priorities [27]. Furthermore, existing research has focused on how to enhance POR more broadly, as opposed to patient-oriented PHC research [28]. A more purposeful approach is required to understand how to leverage patients’ lived and living into PHC systems of the future. To address this gap, we led a priority setting exercise with interested parties from the Canadian PHC community, including patients, care partners, interdisciplinary health care providers, trainees, policymakers, and researchers. We aimed to answer the question, “In what ways can patient partners support PHC design, delivery, research, and education?”

Aim

We aimed to optimize patient engagement in PHC by identifying the priorities of the provincial PHC community to integrate patient partners in PHC design, delivery, research, and education.

Patient Expertise in Research Collaboration (PERC)

Since 2016, PERC has aimed to promote and support the meaningful and appropriate engagement of patients in PHC research and to support research that addresses patients’ needs, preferences, values, and priorities for health and health care in Ontario and beyond. PERC is affiliated with Innovations Strengthening Primary Health Care Through Research (INSPIRE-PHC), which is a provincial research and policy partnership to support accessible, integrated PHC [29] and funded by the Ontario SPOR (Strategy for Patient Oriented Research) Support Unit (OSSU). Funding for this project is provided by OSSU.

PERC’s Advisory Board includes adult, English-speaking Ontarians who self-identify as having lived/living experience managing a chronic and life-limiting condition (as a patient or caregiver) in Ontario’s primary health care system and were recruited by PHC researchers from PERC, INSPIRE-PHC and OSSU. PERC’s patient and caregiver partners have experience with a broad spectrum of conditions including arthritis, cancer, chronic pain, dementia, diabetes, kidney disease, lupus, and mental health challenges. PERC patient advisors are engaged in all stages of PERC’s research and capacity development activities [30, 31]. Aligned with OSSU’s Fairness is Excellence Framework [32], PERC patient partners are diverse across age, attachment to PHC, education, ethnicity, gender, geography, immigration status, and language.

Methods

Study design

The PERC team planned and led a multi-stage priority-setting exercise, guided by Kane and Trochim’s [33] approach to group concept mapping, which includes preparation, brainstorming, sorting/rating brainstormed ideas, constructing/interpreting concept maps, eliciting reasoning, and utilizing data. Group concept mapping is a participatory research methodology that integrates qualitative processes, such as brainstorming, sorting ideas, and interpreting results, with multivariate statistical analyses (multidimensional scaling and hierarchical cluster analysis) to describe a topic [33–35]. Group concept mapping is more participatory than focus groups or other qualitative interviews (since concept mapping participants provide and analyze results) and serves to structure qualitative data [36]. The outputs of group concept mapping produce a visual depiction of the group’s thinking, which is useful for identifying properties of complex systems in policy contexts - maps can align vision with action [37]. Group concept mapping has been identified as an important methodology for accommodating the complexity of healthcare systems, and its participatory, structured nature can bridge different perspectives by enabling multiple types of community members to articulate their thoughts on a topic [38]. This approach was well-suited to the PHC community that includes diverse interests (policy/decision makers; government, not-for-profit, and advocacy groups; researchers; clinicians; patients; caregivers and trainees) operating at different levels (individual, family, community, regional, provincial, national). Participants

In the fall of 2023, PERC hosted a session on POR to launch the priority-setting exercise for group concept mapping at Trillium PHC Research Day - a hybrid online/in-person conference that draws interdisciplinary PHC-related researchers and trainees along with Family Health Team and Community Health Centre Managers, regional and provincial policymakers, clinicians, and patients.

Recruitment for the conference and the priority-setting exercise were advertised through INSPIRE-PHC, OSSU, and PERC’s distribution lists, social media feeds, and websites. At registration, attendees indicated their interest in attending a POR seminar and the initial brainstorming workshop. Any Canadian PHC researcher, policymaker, patient (public), clinician, or trainee was eligible to participate in the brainstorming, sorting, rating, and discussion around how to action highly rated priorities.

The various aspects of Concept Mapping are outlined below:

Concept mapping stage 1: Preparation

In a PERC advisory board meeting, the following question was co-developed by researchers (n = 4), Trainees (n = 2) and PERC patient partners (n = 8) to frame the brainstorming stage:

In what ways can patient partners support primary health care design, delivery, research and education?

Concept mapping stage 2: Generating ideas/brainstorming

During the workshop, nine PERC team members (i.e., researchers, patient partners, and trainees) co-facilitated brainstorming in several small groups of eight to ten people. Engaging the entire Canadian PHC community provided an opportunity to learn from other provincial PHC innovations and consider their applicability for Ontario. The 75-minute workshop was offered to both virtual and in-person conference attendees. Zoom breakout groups were used to facilitate brainstorming discussions for virtual attendees. Participants were encouraged to generate an unlimited number of responses to the question, “In what ways can patient partners support PHC design, delivery, research, and education?”. Facilitators took written notes during the session to capture the brainstormed ideas. Two weeks later, the ideas captured by these notes were entered into the Group Concept Systems web-based software platform [39] by PERC team members.

Three weeks after the session, workshop participants were invited to visit the project website to add to the brainstormed ideas captured by facilitators. Others who were interested, but not available to attend the conference workshop could visit the project’s concept mapping website asynchronously over the following two weeks to suggest their ideas about how patient partners can support PHC. After brainstorming was completed, PERC researchers used an idea synthesis process [33] to ensure that: each statement only represented one idea; each statement was relevant to the prompt; each statement was clear and understandable for participants; and the number of statements were manageable (e.g., some similar ideas were integrated to avoid duplication and reduce participant burden in subsequent stages). The concept mapping methodology recommends retaining fewer than 100 answers to the brainstorming prompt [33].

Concept mapping stage 3: Structuring statements

After idea synthesis, participants were invited to sort the statements into piles using the project’s concept mapping website – this helped to identify different PHC community members’ views on the interrelationship of ideas [33]. See Appendix A for the instructions given to participants about completing this activity. We pilot tested the instructions and sorting process during a PERC Advisory Board meeting with six patient partners, one trainee and four research team members. The results of each concept mapping stage were considered valid within the Group Concept Systems web-based software platform if they were completed by a minimum of 20–30 participants [33]. Participants were sent reminder emails via the project’s concept mapping website to encourage their continued participation across brainstorming, sorting, and rating activities. Participants could engage in any or all steps of the exercise. Participants in the online concept mapping activities were asked four optional demographic questions (age, gender, PHC community member role/group they best identify with, and any traditionally marginalized groups they self-identify with. These included: 2SLGBTQIA+ [Two-Spirit, lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, intersex, and people who identify as part of sexual and gender diverse communities], people with disabilities, racialized people, women).

Concept mapping stage 4: Analysis

Data analysis was completed using Concept Systems by Group Wisdom (web-based statistical software) [39]. This software uses a similarity matrix to show the number of participants who sorted each pair of statements together, and multidimensional scaling of the matrix places points on a two-dimensional map (called a point map), resulting in a set of coordinates for each statement [33]. Hierarchical cluster analysis of the multidimensional scaling results groups the statements into non-overlapping clusters [33]. Stress tests were used to determine the validity of the sorting stage with lower stress test values indicating a better fit of the data within the concept maps. Higher stress test values mean the data were more complex or that they were not often sorted similarly by participants; most group concept mapping projects report stress test values between 0.205 - 0.365 [33].

Concept mapping stages 5: Interpreting maps and 6: Utilizing ideas

Bridging values indicated how cohesive the points inside a cluster were (on a scale of 0–1) and guided discussions with PERC patient partners about how many clusters to include in the resulting concept maps. A smaller bridging value indicated higher cohesion among the points (these points/clusters are also known as anchors, as they were frequently sorted in the same way, indicating participants felt these ideas were related). A larger bridging value indicated less cohesion, or that these clusters were frequently sorted in different ways, indicating these ideas/clusters bridge between different parts of the concept map [40].

Following Kane & Trochim’s [33] emphasis on strategic involvement of project partners in analysis, PERC researchers and patient partners decided the appropriate number of clusters and their labels together. In a PERC Advisory Board meeting, survey responses were supplemented with internal discussions (tracked via meetings notes) to make these decisions. Aggregated results from these analyses (concept maps) informed group discussions about how to utilize brainstormed ideas (the final stage of group concept mapping).

Ethical approval for this project was provided by the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board under project #13199. All participants (in-person or virtual participants) provided consent to participate prior to engaging in the priority-setting exercise in accordance with Canada’s Tri-Council Policy Statement on Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans [41], which aligns with the Declaration of Helsinki [42]. Eleven patients/caregivers from PERC’s Advisory Board served as research partners to co-develop this concept mapping project throughout study planning, implementation and knowledge mobilization and were provided with an honorarium of $25 per hour to acknowledge their contributions. Those who participated in the online concept mapping provided their consent prior to completion and were eligible to be randomly selected to win one of four gift cards in recognition of their participation.

Results

PERC engaged 120 unique Canadian interdisciplinary health care providers, researchers, policymakers, patient/caregiver partners, and trainees in a multi-stage priority-setting exercise over a one-year period. Participants were invited to take part in any or all stages of the concept mapping process, which resulted in varying numbers of participants in brainstorming (n = 94), sorting (n = 24), and rating (n = 38) (See Fig. 1). Table 2 shows the breakdown of participant types at the synchronous brainstorming workshop and asynchronous brainstorming that followed, the majority of which (43%, n = 40) were academics/researchers/health professionals. As many conference attendees represent both researcher and health professional roles, they were combined in the registration form which asked for their primary role. Around 20% (n = 19) of workshop participants were patient/caregiver representatives. Eight of the 11 patients/caregivers, who served as research partners in study planning, implementation and knowledge mobilization, also acted as participants in concept mapping activities. Policymaker representation was limited in all stages but highest in the in-person brainstorming workshop. Most participants resided in Ontario, with some residing in British Columbia, New Brunswick, and Saskatchewan. Answers from participants who chose to answer demographic questions suggest that participants in the online component of this project represented diverse groups, including those traditionally excluded from research (See Fig. 2). Most online brainstorming participants reported having ‘some’ or ‘extensive’ experience engaging patient partners in research. Of the 94 people who participated in the in-person workshop, 29 went on to complete at least one asynchronous phase of concept mapping; 27 people who participated asynchronously had not been part of the brainstorming workshop.

Results and outcomes of several aspects of the concept mapping are outlined below:

Concept mapping stage 2: Generating ideas/brainstorming

Seventy-three percent of workshop participants (73%) participated in-person, while 27% participated in virtual break-out groups. Following the conference, twenty-three people logged into the project’s concept mapping website to brainstorm ways patient partners can support PHC design, delivery, research, and education. More than 75 ideas were generated at the brainstorming workshop, and 86 additional ideas were added to the project’s concept mapping website.

Three PERC researchers (RG, MM, AT) with expertise in qualitative, mixed methods and concept mapping synthesized these ideas to ensure each statement only contained a single idea and that there was no duplication across statements, resulting in 84 discrete ideas about how patient partners can support PHC design, delivery, research and education. The brainstormed ideas are listed in Appendix B.

Concept mapping stage 3: Structuring statements/sorting

Twenty-four participants completed the online sorting activity, dividing the 84 ideas between five and 15 clusters, which each participant labeled to reflect their thinking about how the ideas were related. Given 24 participants completed the sorting activity, the results are considered valid [34]; the stress tests value, 0.293, was in an acceptable range (0.205 - 0.365) [34]. The analyzing software plotted each of the 84 brainstormed statements on a point map, which provided a visual summary of the sorting completed by the participants (n = 24). Statements that were plotted on this map in close proximity represented those that were frequently grouped together by sorters, while more distant statements represented those grouped together less frequently.

In the web-based system, a varying number of clusters could be derived from the point maps. PERC Researchers used bridging values of the points to determine that a nine or ten-cluster solution struck the best balance between too many clusters (which can lead to a fragmented concept map) and too few clusters (which can lead to a concept map that lacks conceptual depth). PERC researchers and patient partners analyzed these two solutions, decided the nine-cluster solution was most cohesive (Fig. 3), and voted on the preferred label for each cluster in an online survey that was circulated after the meeting. The layers of each cluster depict bridging values. Fewer layers indicate smaller bridging values, such as in Cluster 8 (Leading and Co-leading Research Design), Cluster 3 (Primary care practice- Co-design), and Cluster 4 (Equity). This means that the points in each cluster were frequently grouped together by participants. In comparison, Cluster 2 (Supporting Peers, Partnerships & Patients) and Cluster 6 (Being PHC Advisors, Changemakers, and Leaders at the Provincial/Systems Level) have more layers, indicating the points (i.e., ideas) were sorted in varying ways across participants.

Concept mapping stage 4: Analysis/rating

Participants rated each of the 84 ideas on a scale of 0–5 (0 representing low and 5 representing high values) on their importance to patient partners supporting PHC design, delivery, research, and education (n = 34), as well as their feasibility (n = 28) to be implemented in Ontario in the next three years. Importance ratings ranged from 3.36 - 4.63/5, with an average importance rating of 4/5. Feasibility ratings ranged from 3.39–4.5/5 with an average rating of 3.91/5. Figure 4 displays the point ratings mapped into quadrants. The most important and feasible ideas appear in the green upper right quadrant (also known as the ‘Go Zone’); these are the highest priority ideas to act on. Ideas which were rated important, but difficult to carry out appear in the bottom right quadrant (yellow). Ideas that were rated as easy to do, but less important appear in the top left quadrant (orange). Ideas that were rated below average in both importance and feasibility appear in the bottom left quadrant (orange).

Concept mapping stage 5: Interpreting maps

Of the 84 ideas answering the focus prompt, 26 were above average in importance and feasibility, 16 were rated as important, but hard to do, 14 were rated less important but easy to do, and 28 were rated below average on both importance and feasibility. The most important idea in the Go Zone about how patients can contribute to PHC design, delivery, research and education was ‘By being treated as an integral part of the research team (e.g., not just a token added on’ (point 28) averaging 4.63/5 from 34 participants. The most feasible idea in the Go Zone was ‘By reviewing knowledge mobilization products for clarity’ (point 32) averaging 4.5/5 in feasibility from 28 participants.

Go Zone ideas - rated as both important and feasible, included: awareness-raising about the role and value of patient expertise among clinicians (point 18; importance: 4.38/5, feasibility: 4.39/5) and researchers (point 28; importance: 4.63/5, feasibility: 4.29/5), the importance of advocating for a shared health record (point 62; importance: 4.49/5, feasibility: 4.14/5), and the importance of advocating for meaningful partnerships that foster inclusivity (point 15; importance: 4.32/5, feasibility: 4.16/5) and promote diverse voices (point 17; importance: 4.52/5, feasibility: 4.13/5). As seen in Appendix B, these point ratings have some of the smallest standard deviations of the 84 points, meaning these ideas were rated with less variation between participants than other points in the project.

Ideas around integrating patient partners in PHC policy and governance roles were rated as important but challenging to enact, e.g., ‘By informing policies to incentivize the engagement of marginalized populations’ (point 84; importance: 4.48/5, feasibility: 3.86/5). Identified roles patient partners can play to support PHC systems of the future that were perceived to be feasible to carry out, but less important included: ‘By participating in national surveys with representative samples’ (point 71; importance: 3.57/5, feasibility: 4.43/5) and ‘By celebrating achievements of patient impact on health care systems’ (point 83; importance: 3.68/5, feasibility: 4.13/5).

Figure 5 supports group thinking at a higher level, by visualizing discrepancies between importance and feasibility based on average scores for the points in each cluster. Ideas in the cluster labeled ‘Equity’ were seen to be equally important and feasible (among the five points in this cluster, importance ranged from 4.11–4.52/5 and feasibility ranged from 3.91 - 4.39/5. The cluster labeled ‘Leading and co-leading knowledge mobilization’ also had congruence between importance and feasibility ratings but was rated second lowest of the nine clusters. The biggest discrepancy between importance and feasibility was seen for ‘Being PHC advisors, changemakers, and leaders at provincial/systems levels’, which was ranked as the second most important cluster but the second least feasible. As with the Go Zone map, the Ladder Graph (Fig. 5) helps to visualize topic areas of the concept map where action may be warranted but challenging, as well as areas where necessary action may be more achievable. Notably, PERC patient partners felt this concept map was an ‘excellent way to communicate this information and should be incorporated into the paper when it comes time to write about this project’. They also felt it was important to note that even the lowest ranked cluster is still considered important and feasible.

Concept mapping stage 6: Utilizing ideas

A survey (n = 7) was conducted with PERC researchers (n = 2) and patient partners (n = 4), and a trainee (n-=1) to gather perspectives on how to act on the findings of this concept mapping project. Of the 26 Go Zone ideas, survey participants were asked to identify points that they felt PERC already addressed, those that cannot or should not be addressed by PERC, and those that are important for PERC to support in the future. The survey also asked respondents to consider what resources (human, financial, knowledge, evidence) would be required to support points identified as important for PERC in the future and to identify any of the 26 Go Zone ideas they were unsure about.

In a meeting with seven patient partners (including two who had completed the survey), two PERC researchers and one trainee (who had completed the survey), the results were discussed. It was agreed that ideas about advocacy for a single electronic medical record (point 62), and leading continuing education courses (point 22) are outside PERC’s scope and should not be addressed by PERC. PERC patient partners agreed that many of the highest rated ideas on promoting awareness about the value of patient partners (point 18), across all stages of research (point 48) supporting equitable (point 17) inclusive partnerships (point 15) are being meaningfully addressed by PERC and should continue. PERC patient partners felt PERC could play an active role to support the creation of learning environments informed by patients’ lived experience and expertise to foster the value of inclusion among trainees (point 20) and build awareness about what a patient partner does (point 13). PERC patient partners were unsure whether points related to grant review were in scope for PERC as a group, including acting as reviewers (point 33), advocating for grants to include more funding to support patient partnership (point 53), and supporting researchers to develop grant budgets that allocate adequate resources for patient partnership (point 49), yet some had engaged in these activities with other organizations. It was noted by some patient/caregiver partners that some training may be required for those who have not reviewed grants before, if they wish to pursue this.

Group discussion focused on the tension between how the top-rated ideas were enacted within PERC compared to how these ideas apply outside PERC. For example, patient partners pointed out that while PERC was building awareness for what a patient partner is and does within the PHC community in Ontario, they felt there was an opportunity to extend this understanding so that the general public could understand the role of patient partners in health system improvements. PERC patient partners working in health professions education shared their passion for working with trainees and how rewarding it can be to share their expertise with future PHC clinicians while they are in their training programs.

Discussion

In 2025, the Government of Ontario invested $1.8 billion to strengthen and transform primary care, recognizing its essential role in improving health outcomes [43]. The funds will support the creation of new primary care teams and the addition of providers to pre-existing teams to connect two million patients to primary care by 2029 [43]. This expansion of PHC is guided by legislation that emphasizes a system responsive to the needs of the communities it serves [44]. Patient partners can support PHC research, practice, and policy to advance Quintuple Aim outcomes and ensure reformed systems are responsive to community needs. Previous PHC research has consulted patients about their priorities for organizing PHC, without giving specific attention to roles for patients as partners in actioning the identified priorities [27, 45]. To understand how to integrate patient partners to support ongoing PHC reform and to understand how INSPIRE-PHC and PERC can best support this integration, this study engaged 120 unique patients, caregivers, interdisciplinary providers, trainees, policymakers, and researchers from the PHC community to understand specific ways patient partners can support PHC design, delivery, research, and/or education.

We found 84 unique ideas about how patients can support PHC systems of the future; 26 of which were rated as important and feasible to enact in Ontario in the next three years. No idea was rated as less important or less feasible than 3.4/5 by 34 members of the PHC community, most of whom were patients or caregivers (61%). The ideas generated in this study respond to the previously identified challenge of learning what roles patients can play to improve PHC and how they can be supported to enact these roles [46]. The tangible roles for patient partners suggested by our findings, specifically those identified as highly important, support calls for improved processes for patients to engage with PHC policies in Ontario and in Canada [47, 48].

Furthermore, the results of this study are timely, given recent provincial guidance for Ontario health teams to develop primary care networks (PCNs), that leverage patient and clinician expertise to guide primary care reform and codesign system change [49]. PCNs offer the opportunity to integrate regional health system decision-making with clinical knowledge and lived experience from primary care and are similar to Primary Health Networks in Australia – which is rated among the top-performing PHC systems in the world [50, 51]. In England, PCNs, which include patients, have contributed to improved patient and provider experience and reduced costs [52].

The ideas in our study can complement the meaningful (and prevalent) involvement of patients at the patient-clinician interface in decisions about their care [23], by making clear ideas about partnering at a variety of institutional levels. To this end, our group has begun knowledge translation efforts to ensure those developing PCNs are aware of the range of ways patient partners can contribute to PHC and which of those were seen to be the easiest to implement.

We found that the most important and feasible roles patients can play to support PHC are through their treatment as equal partners on research teams, by raising awareness about the role and value of patient partners in multiple PHC domains, and by advocating for meaningful partnerships ‘that go beyond being at the table’, while fostering inclusivity and promoting diverse voices. Patient engagement can exist at different institutional levels (e.g., individual, program, policy/governance) [53], and across a spectrum of participation types (e.g., inform, consult, involve, collaborate, empower) [54]. Our findings’ emphasis on meaningful roles and activities may reflect the desire to move towards more participatory versus consultative engagement in PHC research [55]. Reporting of patient engagement in research is often lean in the level of detail described and as a result, it can be challenging for readers to distinguish between patients who are engaged in a one-time, one-directional way, and more integrated, long-term engagement that influences decision-making [9]. This issue may be particularly salient in PHC research where evidence about how to implement more long-term, collaborative partnerships that result in impactful work is unclear [56].

A recent Cochrane Review of 182 studies on patient partnership and health services design, delivery, and evaluation proposed seven best practice principles for engagement including diversity, equity, and mutual respect [57]; principles which are aligned with our findings about the most important and feasible roles patients can play to support PHC systems. By presenting 84 specific roles or actions that patient partners can take, our work serves to operationalize salient principles of high-quality patient engagement for uptake in the PHC sector and enact the foundational PHC principle of public participation to support policies, strategies, and planning [13, 58].

Certain roles were rated as important but challenging to enact, including integrating patients as partners in PHC practice settings, and in policy and governance roles. While these higher-level roles may be perceived as less feasible, they are necessary to meet the foundational principles of good primary care [59–61] and good patient engagement [57]. More than 10 years have passed since the influential ‘Naylor Report’ on healthcare innovation in Canada called for organizations to provide opportunities for patients to improve care, and for health systems to initiate dialogue with patients and the public to identify types of care that will be required in the future [48]. Though viewed as challenging by participants in our project, examples of patients engaged in PHC clinic roles exist, include integrating a peer support worker in a primary and community care clinic in Quebec for people experiencing homelessness during the COVID-19 pandemic [62] and a peer navigator service embedded in PHC for gender diverse people in two areas of Saskatchewan [63]. Additionally, Haesebaert et al. [56] describe training patients in research practices to act as neutral facilitators on councils composed of clinicians, managers, patients, and caregivers to conduct patient-oriented quality improvement projects. McClean and Trigger [64] described the development and implementation of a Community Advisory Council (CAC) to guide activities in a Primary Health Network. Importantly, patient partners’ involvement in practice improvements or policymaking may occur but not be captured in the existing published literature. More examples of patient partnership in these practice/policy roles would be a welcome addition to the PHC field and support it to move beyond patient engagement in research and at the point-of-care.

Other authors have gone so far as to propose that because team-based care and patient engagement both disrupt traditional practices (e.g., in the patient-clinician relationship, relationships between physicians and other PHC team-members) and redistribute power, these trends can be mutually reinforcing if enacted simultaneously [46, 65–67]. Alidina et al. [46] also propose that patient involvement in team-based primary care delivery can enhance a team identity, a feature of high-performing PHC teams. Galvez-Hernandez and colleagues [68] found that patient and community engagement (including infrastructure to support in/formal patient contributions in safe spaces and feedback loops to utilize input from patients to improve how interprofessional care teams operate) was a key concept in their framework for studying how interprofessional care teams evolve over time. This finding illustrates the value of patient and community engagement to guide interprofessional primary care team activities’ and will support longitudinal study of interprofessional care team implementation in Ontario going forward [68].

Taken together, these findings reinforce the idea that now more than ever, (i.e., during significant PHC reform) is an ideal time to embed opportunities for patients to partner in future PHC systems. Governments can request accountability for patient engagement as a condition of additional PHC funding to organizations, as they have done with Ontario Health Teams [69] which can ensure that patient engagement is not simply a ‘nice to have’, but an essential feature of future PHC systems.

Group concept mapping served as a novel way to engage a variety of diverse members of the PHC community about how patient partners can directly contribute to primary care systems. Our results are strengthened by adhering to recent reporting guidance for Trochim’s concept mapping research from Pantha and colleagues [70]. PERC patient partners could choose the level of engagement in this study; options ranged from time-limited, consultative involvement (e.g., brainstorming ideas) to partnership in planning, implementing, and translating findings over time (i.e., how many PERC patient partners contributed). Working with PERC patient partners to interpret our findings as part of the ‘utilizing ideas’ stage reinforces the credibility of our findings and grounds them in lived experience. We heard that patient partners want to support PHC systems in ways the literature calls for and are looking for meaningful opportunities to do so.

The variety of modalities for engagement of patient partners in this study may be more reflective of the dynamic realities of engaging patients than frameworks suggest [71]. By offering different modalities for patients to participate, we may have contributed to the consistent involvement of patient and family caregivers in different stages of this project over many months. Even so, group concept mapping is a complicated process, made even more by the sheer number of ideas brainstormed in this project [72]. The fact that there were more participants who rated the brainstormed ideas (later activity) than sorted them (earlier activity) differs from most concept mapping projects, which usually experience attrition in the number of participants over time [33]. This discrepancy could indicate the complexity of the sorting task compared to rating the ideas. This project experienced a decline in number and in role diversity from the 94 participants in the brainstorming workshop to the 29 who continued to participate on the project website. An advantage of group concept mapping is the ability to stratify results by role type to see how different groups perceive the issue but requires 15–20 individuals per group [34]. The limited participation (e.g., policymakers’ perspectives were not well-represented in the later stages of this project) meant that we did not have the sample size to compare how different roles rated different ideas, as we had originally hoped. This may have also contributed to the perception that patient engagement in policy was important yet hard to do. A stronger policymaker presence in later stages of the project may have led to patient engagement in policymaking being rated as more feasible than it was, if, for example, they were aware of patient engagement initiatives in policymaking that were conducted internally within their portfolios and not featured in the published literature.

Despite the timeliness in terms of policy relevance surrounding PHC transformation, the results from this work have not yet had extensive uptake. Like Haesebaert et al. [56], patient partners expressed discouragement at the perceived limited impact of the results of this project which led to wider-ranging discussions about the impact of partnered approaches – both within PERC and in general. Nevertheless, the results of this project will guide strategic directions for the PERC Advisory Board, including the decision to host a workshop on patient impact narratives [73]. Our group reflected on how it could supplement the annual workshops about patient partnership offered to trainees affiliated with the Transdisciplinary Understanding and Training in Primary Health Care (TUTOR-PHC) Program to primary care trainees not part of this program and has recently implemented a workshop for PHC nurse practitioner Masters students, other nursing graduate students and faculty.

Other organizations operating in PHC design, delivery, research, or education could utilize the results of this project to prioritize actions on ideas which are outside the scope of PERC’s mandate. Doing so would maximize the utility of group concept mapping as a methodology in complex, interdependent systems such as PHC [74]. The ideas in this project also present areas where action may not be fruitful (e.g., ideas rated below average in importance and feasibility), which may be of equal interest to those working in this space, such as convenors of PCNs in Ontario.

Conclusion

Opportunities exist to integrate patient partners’ important lived experience and valuable expertise for PHC transformation. This work highlights the many ways and realms in which patient partners can contribute their expertise to PHC systems. Integrating patients’ lived and living experiences and expertise in various aspects of PHC design, delivery, education and research will contribute to a PHC system informed by patients’ priorities and maximize real-world impact.
