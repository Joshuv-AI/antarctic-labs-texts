Trend analysis of medical expenses under DRG payment reform in China
Authors: Li Du, Shu Fang, Qilin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe impact and evolving trends of Diagnosis-Related Group (DRG) payment reform on inpatient medical expenses have drawn substantial academic and societal attention. However, there remains a notable gap in research focusing on the cost-control efficacy and its dynamic trends under this reform. Employing Mann–Whitney U testing, structural change analysis, and interrupted time-series methods, this study quantifies pre-post DRG reform shifts in inpatient expenditures, insurance compensation patterns, and expenditure trajectories. The results showed that per capita hospitalization expenses decreased 713.93 yuan (β1 + β3 = − 713.93, p < 0.001) per month, with monthly medical insurance payments declining 0.82% (β1 + β3 = − 0.82, p < 0.01) versus 0.82%, monthly out-of-pocket expenses increases (β1 + β3 = 0.82, p < 0.01). In cost structure, the structural change in hospitalization costs was 4.37%, which pharmaceutical, health material, laboratory and medical service costs declined while examination costs rose significantly (p < 0.01). While DRG payment reform reduces certain hospitalization costs, out-of-pocket expenses for patients have increased, highlighting ongoing financial challenges. Drug and material expenses remain primary cost drivers, whereas medical service fees are undervalued. Future reforms must prioritize clinical expertise recognition, optimize insurance fund efficiency, and lower patient copayments to balance affordable care, hospital viability, and financial sustainability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20902-0.Keywords: DRG payment, Hospitalization expenses, Gray correlation structure variation, Interrupt time series, ChinaSubject terms: Health care, Medical research

Introduction

The aging population, increasingly complex disease patterns, rising healthcare demand, and structural imbalances in health resources have collectively led to sharp increases in medical costs, posing a significant global challenge1. The preliminary estimate of China’s total health expenditure in 2023 reached 9.05758 trillion-yuan, accounting for 7.2% of the country’s gross domestic product (GDP), which increased from 3.0% in 19782,3. The total per capita health expenditure was as high as 6,425.3 yuan, which accounts for more than 20% of total household expenditure, thereby inducing the risk of impoverishment due to illness or falling back into poverty1. The increase in medical expenses and the imbalance in their structure severely jeopardize the stable operation and sustainable development of China’s medical insurance fund, leading to a heavy economic burden, restricting the basic right of healthcare, and presenting significant challenges to the country’s healthcare system.

To control the unreasonable increases in medical expenses and to establish an efficient medical insurance payment system, the National Medical Insurance Administration (NHA) in China implemented a payment model based on diagnosis-related groups (DRG) in 20194. This model is one of the internationally recognized advanced payment methods and represents a new trend in controlling medical costs in current healthcare reform. Accordingly, the DRG model has become a key direction for medical insurance payment system reforms in several countries, including China. DRG payment reform involves categorizing patients into similar case groups on the basis of factors such as their condition, treatment methods, and course of illness; determining the average cost for each group; and establishing medical insurance payment standards on the basis of the case group as a unit5. The purpose of DRG payment is to conserve healthcare funds, optimize the allocation of medical resources, alleviate the burden on patients, and foster a balanced development between medical expenses and medical quality6. It has been reported that the DRG payment system has been introduced in about 40 countries around the world, including the United States7, Germany8, the United Kingdom9, France10 and Japan11, and has achieved good results in controlling costs. The experience of nearly four decades of implementation of the DRG payment system in the United States has shown that the system has been effective in controlling the excessive growth of inpatient hospitalization costs, and the average annual growth rates of hospital costs and per capita hospital costs have both declined. Germany’s DRG system categorizes patients based on their detailed information at the time of discharge from the hospital, and pilot data from 2003 showed that the average cost of care in Germany’s 750 hospitals was reduced by 35%, and the average length of stay was shortened by 30%. In the United Kingdom, since the implementation of DRG until 2015, hospital costs as a percentage of total health expenditure fell to 41.8%, and the efficiency of hospital services was improved. The implementation of Japan’s DPCs system has optimized the allocation of health resources, and the average number of patient hospital days has decreased from 20.4 days to 18.8 days. Switzerland12, Australia13, South Korea14 and other countries have also actively borrowed the DRG model from developed countries and achieved significant results to control medical costs.

Inpatient expenses are crucial indicators for assessing the utilization of medical resources, and these expenses directly reflect the use of medical resources and costs15. Hospitals should focus on the optimization of the medical cost structure, cost control and other aspects of operation management; furthermore, hospitals should provide decision-making and operation support via refined management16. Due to the continuous development of DRG payment reform in China, changes and trends in patient hospitalization costs after the implementation of DRG have attracted considerable attention in academic circles and in societies at home and abroad. The effects of the implementation the DRG payment policy in different countries and regions are heterogeneous, and there is no consensus regarding the changes and trends in hospitalization costs. In terms of inpatient expenses, a study by Hu et al15. revealed that the implementation of the DRG significantly reduced inpatient expenses for lung cancer patients. In addition to lung cancer patients, patients with cervical cancer, hypertension, cardiovascular diseases and other diseases also presented similar results12,13,15–17. However, Gao et al.18 suggested that while DRG reforms can control the growth of medical expenses, the cost-control effect gradually weakens or even disappears over time, which may be related to the mismatch between medical costs and DRG payments, especially the high price of high-value consumables and inspection and other items, the excessive development of high-profit projects for profit purposes, and the lack of power for cost control.

In terms of the inpatient cost structure, after implementing DRG payment, the United States strengthened the management of rational drug use, gave priority to cost-effective drugs, reduced the use of high-priced drugs and adjuvant drugs, leading to a decrease in the proportion of drug costs in inpatient medical expenses9. Lin et al.,4 reported that after the implementation of the DRG reform, the proportion of hospital drugs decreased by 2.63%, and the proportion of medical services increased by 2.98%, making the structure of medical costs more reasonable. Chen et al.,19 reported that drug costs were the factor that most strongly contributed to changes in the inpatient cost structure, and the proportion of medical service fees increased to 26.30%. Following the implementation of the DRG payment system in Germany, examination costs have been effectively controlled and the cost structure has been refined through strengthened clinical pathway management and optimized examination processes20. In general, DRG payment reform made the medical cost structure more reasonable, but further optimization is necessary. Some scholars have also researched the structure of hospital expenses for single diseases with the DRG model and reported that DRG reform has beneficial effects on the control of drug costs and consumable costs among cervical cancer patients; furthermore, the DRG model has shown a synergistic effect on policy to a certain extent, thus indicating its clinical value5. Ge et al., reported that in the context of the DRG model, examination fees and laboratory fees for hypertensive patients are important internal factors of hospitalization costs, but medical service fees reflecting the labor value of medical personnel need to be further improved16.

Numerous studies have examined whether DRG payment reform can reasonably control costs and optimize the structure of inpatient expenses, but the results have not been consistent. However, few studies have analyzed trends in patient hospitalization expense. Therefore, the current study analyzed the structural changes and trends in hospitalization expenses for insured patients under the DRG payment model. This study aimed to further explore the effects of these changes on patient burdens and hospital management, optimize the cost structure, enhance the hospital’s internal refined management, leverage the DRG payment method, and provide theoretical and policy support for the continuous optimization of DRG payment reform policies by medical insurance management departments. Achieving these aims will help guide medical institutions’ service behavior, control irrational increases in medical costs, ensure the stable operation of medical insurance funds, and ultimately achieve the goal of a win‒win situation for the three parties in the medical insurance system.

After the DRG payment policy, the cost structure for inpatients changed significantly. The DRG price itself is a bundled payment that covers drugs, examinations, laboratory tests, health materials, and medical service costs. Within this bundle, the costs for inpatients decreased as follows: drug costs decreased by 13.7%, examination costs increased by 4.17%, laboratory costs decreased by 2.30%, health material costs decreased by 27.18%, and medical service costs decreased by 7.59%. Furthermore, the distribution of payment for the total hospitalization costs shifted. The portion covered by medical insurance decreased by 7.59%, and out-of-pocket expenses decreased by 6.84%. Despite overall hospitalization cost reductions, the proportion of out-of-pocket expenses demonstrated an upward trend, indicating potential financial strain for patients. Moreover, the average length of stay decreased by 16.67%. All of these differences were statistically significant (p < 0.01).

Gray correlation analysis and structural change analysis of hospitalization costs before and after the implementation of the DRG payment policy

Results of gray correlation analysis of hospitalization costs in the two groups

Dimensionless processing was performed via normalization using initial values. The reference sequence for this study was the average total hospitalization costs per admission, and the comparison sequences included five types of expenses: average drug costs, average examination costs, average laboratory costs, and others. When performing gray relational analysis, the distinguishing coefficient was set at 0.5.

After the implementation of the DRG payment policy, medical service costs had the strongest association with total inpatient costs. This relationship was amplified after the implementation of the DRG payment policy. Health materials costs had the second strongest association with total inpatient costs, which drug costs ranked third, indicating that both remained the main factors affecting the total cost. Laboratory costs had the fourth strongest association with total inpatient costs, and this association remained relatively stable over time. Examination costs had the fifth strongest association with total inpatient costs, with association becoming weaker after the implementation of the DRG payment policy, thus suggesting a reduction in the influence of examination fees on total inpatient costs (Table 3).

Analysis results of structural changes in hospitalization costs in the two groups

The change in the structure of inpatient expenses after the implementation of the DRG payment policy was 4.37%. Specifically, the structural change value (VSV) of drug costs decreased by 1.22%, indicating a reduced proportion of drug-related expenses. The structural change contribution rate of drug costs was 27.92%, with an annual average change rate of – 4.99%, thus highlighting the significant role of reduced drug costs in the overall decline in hospitalization expenses. The VSV of health material costs decreased by 0.97%, the contribution rate of structural change was 22.2%, the average annual rate of change was – 3.64%, and the pulling force was – 0.81%, thus highlighting the significant role of reduced health material costs in the overall decline in hospitalization expenses. The VSV of medical service costs was 0.99%, with a structural change contribution rate of 22.65% and an annual average change rate of 3.46%. This increase in medical service costs had a substantial positive impact, and the pulling force was 0.78%, indicating a strong upward pulling on hospitalization costs. The examination costs exhibited a structural change value of 0.68%, with an annual change rate of 7.04% and a pull force of 1.1%, suggesting a positive effect on the cost structure. Laboratory costs exhibited a structural change of just 0.51%, with an annual change rate of 4.86%, and the pull force was negligible at 0.57%, indicating that examination costs had impact on the overall hospitalization cost (Table 4).

Analysis of interruption time series of hospitalization costs in the two groups

This study used monthly data from the observation period starting from month 1 (November 2021) to month t, where 1 = 11/2021, 2 = 12/2021, 3 = 01/2022, and so on. The DRG payment policy was implemented starting from the 6th month. A total of 10 months (5 months before and 5 months after the reform) were included in the analysis. The Cumby–Huizinga test was used to check for autocorrelation in the time series. The p values for both the first-order and second-order autocorrelation tests were > 0.05, suggesting that there was no autocorrelation in the time series. The augmented Dickey‒Fuller (ADF) test was applied to check for the stationarity of the time series data. After first-order differencing, the p values were all < 0.05, indicating that the time series of inpatient expenses before and after the implementation of the DRG payment were stationary, thus making it suitable for interrupted time series analysis.

The changes in inpatient expense indicators before and after the implementation of the DRG payment policy are shown in Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8. These indicators were entered into a piecewise linear regression model, as shown in Table 5: (1) Before the implementation of DRG, per capita hospitalization costs decreased significantly, with an average decrease of 418.86 yuan per month, but the difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). After the implementation of DRG, the β3 values was − 295.07, but the difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). The average monthly decline in hospitalization costs was β1 + β3 =− 713.93(p < 0.001), meaning that monthly hospitalization costs decreased by an average of 713.93 yuan per month after the implementation of the DRG payment policy (Fig. 1). (2) Before the implementation of DRG, medical insurance expenses decreased significantly, with an average decrease of 287.29 yuan per month (p < 0.05). After the implementation of DRG, the proportion of medical insurance payments showed an immediate increase of 3.83% per month (β₂ = 3.83, p < 0.01). The β3 values of the monthly medical insurance expenses was − 93.13 (p < 0.001), meanwhile, the β3 values of the proportion of monthly medical insurance expenses was − 1.11% (p < 0.01), indicating that the policy had a consistent and significant impact on reducing the medical insurance expenses. The proportion of monthly medical insurance expenses was β1 + β3 = − 0.82 (p < 0.01), indicating that after the implementation of the DRG payment policy, the proportion of medical insurance payment decreased by 0.82% per month (Fig. 2). (3) Before the implementation of the DRG payment policy, the monthly out-of-pocket expenses decreased by 365.24 (β1 = − 365.24, p < 0.01). After the DRG policy was implemented, out-of-pocket expenses showed an immediate increase of 1233.99 (β₂ = 1233.99, p < 0.05). However, at the same time, the β2 value for the proportion of out-of-pocket expenses was − 4.41 per month (p < 0.01), indicating that the proportion of out-of-pocket expenses dropped by 4.41% immediately after the reform. After the implementation of the reform, out-of-pocket expenses increased by 326.80 yuan per month (β3 = 326.80, p > 0.05), but this result was not statistically significant. After controlling for the time series effects, the proportion of monthly out-of-pocket expenses increased by 0.82% (β1 + β3 = 0.82, p < 0.01) (Fig. 3). (4) Before the implementation of the DRG payment policy, drug costs decreased by 323.73 yuan per month (β1 = − 323.73, p < 0.001), and the proportion of drug costs decreased by 0.08% per month (β1 = − 0.08, p < 0.05). Discussion

This study analyzed changes in the structure and trends of inpatient costs before and after the implementation of DRG payment reform policies at a tertiary general hospital in Wuhan. The findings revealed that DRG payment reform significantly influenced inpatient cost the structures and dynamics.

The DRG payment policy has a significant effect on controlling hospitalization costs for patients, with a notable reduction in hospitalization expenses. However, the proportion of out-of-pocket expenses have increased. Research has shown that the implementation of the DRG payment policy has led to a significant downward trend in the total hospitalization expenses, which suggested that the DRG payment reform can effectively curb the growth of medical expenses, aligning with previous studies21,22. By establishing payment standards for each disease group, DRG restructures hospitals’ economic incentive mechanisms, driving them to shift from the traditional “fee-for-service” model to a “diagnosis-based payment” system. Hospitals achieved cost reductions by optimizing clinical management pathways, prioritizing cost-effective medication lists, standardizing medical supply protocols, and implementing transparent healthcare quality measures alongside target-based management systems. These measures encouraged medical staff to adopt cost-conscious practices, standardize clinical procedures and select cost-appropriate treatment regimens, thereby reducing patients’ financial burdens23. Additionally, the results found a 16.67% reduction in hospitalization days post DRG payment reform, consistent with Tan et al.24. Length of stay, a key determinant of hospitalization costs, positively correlates with medical expenditures25. As one of the key indicators of DRG, the time consumption index has improved the efficiency and cost awareness of medical staff to a certain extent. By actively carrying out daytime chemotherapy and daytime surgery26,27, medical institutions optimize the diagnosis and treatment process, improve medical efficiency, shorten the hospitalization time of patients, thereby reducing the number of invalid hospitalization days, which effectively reduces the hospitalization expenses of patients.

Despite a decrease in median out-of-pocket expenses for patients after the DRG payment reform, their proportion of total hospitalization costs increased, consistent with Jian et al.28. The increase in the proportion of out-of-pocket expenses has both common causes and differences among different groups (Supplementary Tables S1-2). Specifically, the proportion of elderly patients at their own expense was significantly higher. As the patient’s age increases, the physiological functions decline continuously, and the diseases are characterized by comorbidity and chronicity. It not only increases the complexity and difficulty of treatment, directly leading to the prolongation of hospital stay, but also requires additional expenses for rehabilitation and care. DRG payment may not cover extended costs such as long-term medication and rehabilitation treatment29. Higher surgery complexity also increased costs due to advanced technologies, high-value consumables (e.g., imported equipment, special implants), postoperative special medication, as well as the development and application of new technology and new business30. Occupational groups with different types of medical insurance (e.g., employee medical insurance vs resident medical insurance) face varying reimbursement rates, self-paid upper limit, and disposable income, influencing their willingness to pay for premium treatment and services31, while patients with resident medical insurance are the opposite32. This increase in out-of-pocket expenses partially offset the DRG payment policy in alleviating the problem of “expensive medical treatment”, highlighting the need to consider individual characteristics of patients and diseases in future policy adjustments.

Therefore, dynamically adjusting the DRG disease group catalog, improving the quantity and standards of DRG subgroups, and updating the medical insurance payment catalog by incorporating factors such as surgical complexity level, patient age, and treatment modalities as grouping nodes are necessary to make DRG grouping more scientific and reasonable33. At the same time, a multi-level security system should be built to expand medical insurance coverage, and commercial insurance institutions should be encouraged to develop medical insurance products for elderly patients, such as long-term care insurance, to provide more security options34. These measures can reduce both total and out-of-pocket expenses, effectively addressing “high medical costs”.

DRG payment contributes to a more rational cost structure, but further optimization is needed. The study explored changes in the structure of hospitalization expenses before and after the implementation of the DRG payment policy, and the degree of change was 4.37%, indicating an effect on the structure of hospitalization expenses. The structure of drug costs and health materials costs showed a negative change, with the proportions declining, indicating that reductions in those costs were crucial in lowering overall hospitalization costs. This effectively alleviating the situation of “supporting with drugs” and “supporting medicine by consumption” within the healthcare system and making the structural changes in patients’ hospitalization costs reasonable, which was consistent with previous research4,5. On one hand, since the implementation of the DRG payment policy, hospitals have transformed the ‘status’ of drugs and consumables into operating costs, thus strengthening the management of drug procurement and usage to reduce overall medical costs by optimizing the drug list and limiting the use of high-cost drugs5. On the other hand, the national government has strengthened the management of high-value medical consumables and forced hospitals to strengthen the fine management of high-value medical consumables by using the DRG payment policy. By adjusting the performance distribution and implementing unified coding management for high-value consumables, measures such as information traceability control consumable costs growth35. Moreover, because medical insurance policy controls costs, it has changed clinical use from expensive imported consumables to cost-effective domestic consumables, ensuring the quality of medical care, thereby reducing material waste and reducing the economic burden on patients.

However, this study found that the contribution rate of structural change (CRSV) of drug costs and health material costs were 27.92% and 22.2% respectively, totaling 50.12%, remaining the main factor affecting hospitalization expenses, which was consistent with Ma et al36. Possible reasons include changes in the disease spectrum and new technologies and services development, increasing demand for special drugs and materials. Meanwhile, medical technologies upgrades, increased consumables use during treatment, and growing patients’ health awareness have inclined both patients and doctors to choose expensive drugs or consumables. At the same time, with the competition in the medical industry, unreasonable medical diagnosis and treatment behaviors have also, to a certain extent, have further increased the financial burden on patients23. Therefore, the government should strengthen the supervision and control of the price of drugs and consumables, encourage the normalization of new technologies, and give certain medical insurance policy support. Hospitals should strengthen the supervision and management of drug use and high-value consumables, establish a warning system for abnormal usage, and link usage outcomes to performance evaluations, so as to realize the monitoring of the use of drugs and high-value medical consumables by medical staff.

Medical service costs exhibited the highest correlation with hospitalization expenses (average correlation coefficient: 0.984), with the CRSV of 22.65%, ranking second, and a strong positive pulling on total cost (0.78%), indicating that DRG payment reform encouraged hospitals to prioritize diagnosis and treatment services reflecting technical labor value of medical staff, aligning with the reform expectation of “changing cages for birds”, which was consistent with the research of Zhang et al37. With the development of medical technology, hospitals may introduce more medical service projects, often requiring higher technical standards and more complex procedures. These advanced technologies require higher costs, increasing related medical services. As medical services, a “mixed commodity” with “medical” and “nonmedical” services priced jointly, the labor value of healthcare workers has not yet been fully reflected38. A relevant study39 indicated that medical service item prices in public hospitals in China were relatively low. Appropriately adjusting these prices enhances healthcare institutions’ revenue structures and effectively controls the proportion of unnecessary examinations, tests, and drug expenses. The “Opinions of the General Office of the State Council on Promoting the High-quality Development of Public Hospitals” ([2021] No.18) proposed in 2021 to support public hospitals in optimizing income structures and increasing the proportion of medical service income (excluding drugs, consumables, inspection and laboratory income) in medical income. Therefore, medical insurance institutions should utilize price adjustment as a macro-control tool to advance medical service prices reform. Hospitals should mobilize medical staff enthusiasm, optimize income structure, increase the proportion of medical service income, and reduce expenditures on drugs and consumables40.

In addition, this study also found that the VSV of examination costs and laboratory costs were 0.68% and 0.51% respectively, but their annual average change rates were relatively high, at 7.04% and 4.86% respectively, indicating that in the initial stage of DRG payment implementation, examination costs and laboratory costs showed a phenomenon of “high growth, moderate contribution”, which was also one of the main reasons for the current high medical costs37. On one hand, cost large-scale medical and inspection equipment indirectly increased hospitalization expenses. On the other hand, enhanced medical literacy may lead patients to pursue comprehensive and systematic examinations. Therefore, in the early stage of reform, fine management and control are necessary to prevent these costs from becoming a structural issue. Hospitals should refine clinical pathways through standardized diagnostic panels to eliminate non-indicated testing, while embedding robust cost surveillance mechanisms—particularly targeting high-value or anomalous diagnostic expenditures. Concurrently, incentive structures must incorporate evidence-based testing compliance rates into performance metrics to optimize diagnostic decision-making and rationalize clinical behaviors40. At the same time, efforts should improve medical services homogenization, strengthen information systems interoperability, and promote sharing and mutual recognition of inspection and test results among tertiary hospitals. This will not only improve the utilization efficiency of medical resources and optimize the expense structure, but also further reduce the medical burden on patients.

DRG reform exhibits short-term transitional fluctuations, and then a long-term dynamic adjustment mechanism needs to be established. The impact of DRG payment reform on medical expenses and structure presented stage characteristics, with cost control effects varying over time, highlighting differences between short-term and long-term impact41. Although there was a short jump in the total cost, medical insurance expenses, out-of-pocket expenses, drug cost and health material cost at the moment of the implementation of the reform—reflecting adaptive adjustments during policy transition, it decreased significantly over the long term, confirming the inhibitory effect of DRG payment on resource-consuming costs and the continuous effectiveness of cost control4. Notably, the insurance reimbursement share continued to diminish while the out-of-pocket proportion raised commensurately—signaling potential cost-shifting risks that warrant vigilance. For medical service expenses, there was an instantaneous increase in reform but a long-term downward trend. This contrast may be due to the lag of DRG payment dynamic adjustment or the hospital’s reduction of service input under the pressure of cost control. This typical pattern of “short-term fluctuations followed by long-term declines” profoundly reveals the deep-seated effects of the DRG pre-payment system on hospital cost behaviors. Therefore, during the implementation of the DRG payment reform, first of all, we should comprehensively consider various factors and construct a dynamic adjustment mechanism for DRG grouping and payment standards. Establish a regular evaluation system to periodically assess the rationality of DRG grouping. According to the evaluation results, the DRG grouping was dynamically adjusted, and the new diseases, complications and complications were included in the grouping consideration, so that the grouping was more suitable for clinical practice. At the same time, the technical difficulty (CMI), risk degree, resource consumption intensity, medical quality, efficiency and other performance evaluation results of medical services were used as important adjustment factors to reasonably adjust the price of medical services reflecting technical services, so as to avoid hospitals being forced to cut essential services due to insufficient labor value36. Medical institutions should be encouraged to optimize service processes, improve service efficiency, and increase investment in necessary medical services while controlling costs, such as strengthening chronic disease management and health education, so as to enhance the overall level of medical services. Secondly, an intelligent supervision and feedback system should be established. Combined with the management standards of clinical pathways, this system should focus on cost structures and medical quality. Utilize information systems to strengthen key monitoring of diagnosis and treatment behaviors for diseases with rapidly increasing costs. It was necessary to enhance the prevention, control, and supervision of cost-shifting risks. When the proportion of out-of-pocket expenses of medical institutions exceeds a certain threshold, early warning and special verification (especially high-value consumables, special services, off-formulary drugs, etc.) were initiated to prevent hospitals from passing on expenses to patients by increasing out-of-pocket expenses40. Meanwhile, the medical insurance intelligent auditing system should be improved to conduct real-time monitoring of medical cost composition, focusing on abnormal fluctuations in expenses such as medication costs and medical supply costs, and promptly detecting and punishing illegal charges, cost shifting and other behaviors. Finally, incentive mechanisms should be enhanced to promote effective management and enhance resource utilization efficiency in hospitals by establishing and improving cost accounting systems to strengthen internal cost control. In conclusion, by establishing and continuously optimizing a dynamic adjustment mechanism characterized by scientific grouping and pricing, intelligent and precise supervision, and an incentive-compatible orientation, we can effectively cope with short-term fluctuations in hospitalization expenses, prevent structural risks, consolidate the long-term effectiveness of cost control, and ultimately guide the medical system to shift from scale expansion to an intensive development path centered on quality and value.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations. First, the research analyzed data from only a single tertiary hospital in China, and the data cover only five months before and five months after the implementation of the DRG payment policy. This limited scope of data constrains our capacity to adequately control for potential seasonal fluctuations in hospitalization costs, which may introduce biases into the model estimates. Future research should adopt a multicenter design and a longer time frame to better verify the sustainability of the observed effects and control confounding factors such as seasonality. Second, in recent years, the centralized procurement of drugs and consumables has affected the hospitalization expenses of patients to a certain extent, and evaluating the impact of drug consumption in the short term is impossible. Future studies could adopt a more rigorous multi-group design, with control groups or comparisons of implementation in different regions and hospitals, to more accurately attribute the effects of the DRG policy. Furthermore, while our study demonstrates significant reductions in inpatient drug costs and length of stay, it is limited to the inpatient setting. We did not track subsequent outpatient expenditures. It is possible that these reductions reflect a shift in care from inpatient to outpatient settings rather than a net reduction in total healthcare costs. Future research that should integrate inpatient and outpatient claims data to track patient-level expenditures across care settings, which is crucial for distinguishing between genuine systemic efficiency gains and mere cost-shifting phenomena following DRG implementation.

Conclusion

This study confirmed that the DRG payment policy can effectively control and reduce medical costs by continuously adjusting and optimizing the structure of hospitalization expenses. However, the proportion of out-of-pocket expenses has increased, and the burden of medical treatment has not been alleviated. The structure of hospitalization expenses has been optimized, yet drug expenses and health material expenses remain the main influencing factors, and the labor value of medical staff has not received sufficient attention. Therefore, in accordance with the principle of “total volume control and structural adjustment”, efforts should be made to promote the transformation of the medical system towards quality and efficiency. This can be achieved by scientifically optimizing DRG grouping nodes, establishing an intelligent supervision system to prevent and control cost shifting, and implementing price reforms and a “retained surplus” mechanism to enhance the value of medical services.

Methods

Data sources

In China, hospitals are graded and managed in accordance with the Regulations on the Administration of Medical Institutions and the Measures for the Grading and Management of Hospitals. They are divided into three levels, among which tertiary hospitals are at the highest level. Tertiary hospitals provide comprehensive medical services, undertake certain teaching and scientific research tasks, possess a high level of medical technology and comprehensive service capabilities. They can better reflect the actual changes in medical costs and have a certain degree of national applicability.

Wuhan, as the center city of central China, has rich in medical resources and has a high level of medical services. The hospital selected in this study has significant demonstration value and significance for the DRG policy research in China, which its DRG case-mix index (CMI) is in the leading position in Wuhan and even nationwide, with a high level of medical service capacity and complex case handling capacity. As a pilot health insurance institution in Wuhan, the actual data of the hospital can reflect the changes in patients’ hospitalization costs before and after the implementation of the DRG payment policy in a more comprehensive way, which has certain representative and reference value.

This study utilized data derived from the Wuhan medical insurance management platform and the hospital information system (HIS) of a tertiary hospital in Wuhan. The study population comprised patients covered by Wuhan medical insurance who were hospitalized from November 2021 to August 2022. April 2022 was designated as the breakpoint, dividing the entire observation period into two phases: prior to the implementation of the DRG payment policy in Wuhan (November 2021 to March 2022) and after the implementation of the DRG payment policy (April 2022 to August 2022). The objective of this study was to analyze and evaluate trends in hospitalization expenses for inpatients before and after the introduction of the DRG payment policy. The hospital ranks among the top four hospitals in Wuhan, with its case-portfolio index for DRG being one of the strongest in the region. As a pilot institution for the implementation of Wuhan medical insurance, the hospital’s actual data can reflect the actual changes in hospitalization costs for patients before and after the introduction of this medical insurance program.

Data analysis

We compared each evaluation index before and after the implementation of the DRG payment policy. Data analysis was performed via SPSS version 26. We found that all of the data followed nonnormal distributions. Since inpatient medical expenses often exhibit skewness and heteroscedasticity, the nature of the dataset does not follow a normal distribution. The Mann–Whitney U test was selected as our primary non-parametric test. The Mann–Whitney U test is a robust nonparametric test that effectively compares the differences between two independent sample distributions, independent of the distribution pattern of the data, and has obvious advantages when dealing with this type of data. Consequently, the Mann–Whitney U rank sum test was employed to compare continuous variables, with a significance level set at α = 0.05. A p value of less than 0.05 indicated that the difference between the two groups was statistically significant.

The structural change analysis method was used to analyze the changes in the hospitalization costs of patients before and after the implementation of the DRG payment policy. This analytical method involves four indicators: the VSV, the structural change degree (DSV), the CRSV, and the pulling force indicator36. The VSV is defined as the difference between the component ratio at the end of a specified period and that at its beginning. This method is commonly used for processing dynamic data and provides a comprehensive reflection of both structural characteristics and changes in medical expenses over time. The DSV reflects the comprehensive change in each individual cost within the cost, with higher values indicating a greater the degree of structural fluctuation42. The pull force index of structural variability reflects the extent to which changes in individual cost items within a specific period affect the total cost. The CRSV represents the ratio of the absolute value of structural variation for each component within medical expenses during each period relative to the DSV during that same period. This parameter illustrates how changes in cost composition impact the overall cost structure. The parameters were calculated as follows: (1) VSV: VSV = Xi1-Xi0; (2) DSV: DSV = ∑ | Xi1-Xi0|; (3) CRSV: CRSV =| Xi1-Xi0 |DSV × 100%; and (4) pulling force = contribution rate of cost structure change × average annual growth rate × 100%.

To identify the indicator changes before and after the implementation of the DRG payment system, the study utilized Interrupted Time Series (ITS) analysis, which is a quasi-experimental research design commonly used in evaluating public health interventions. The ITS analysis covered a time series from November 2021 to August 2022, with 5 months before the reform (November 2021 to March 2022) and 5 months after the reform (April 2022 to August 2022). The ITS analysis was used to estimate the instantaneous (change in outcome level) and long-term (change in outcome trend) impact of the DRG policy.
