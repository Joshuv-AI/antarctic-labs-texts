Evaluating Circulating Tumor DNA Clearance as an Early Intermediate End Point Predicting Clinical Benefit in Metastatic Nonsquamous NSCLC After First-Line Chemoimmunotherapy
Authors: Min Yuan, Haolun Ding, Weiwei Zhuang, Yaning Yang, Xu Steven Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn the first-line (1L) NSCLC setting, overall survival (OS) as a trial end point is increasingly challenging due to longer survival and confounding from novel later-line therapies. This study aims to evaluate whether circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) clearance (alone or with radiographic response) is a reliable early surrogate end point for progression-free survival (PFS) and OS in 1L NSCLC studies.MethodsData from a phase III trial (IMpower150) of patients with untreated metastatic nonsquamous NSCLC were used. ctDNA clearance, unconfirmed radiographic response, and confirmed radiographic response at approximately 6 months after treatment were evaluated as surrogates for PFS and OS. The meta-analytic approaches were used. The data were randomly split into four cohorts, treating each as an independent “trial” to simulate a multi-trial meta-analysis. This process was repeated 50 times for statistical robustness.ResultsctDNA decline correlated with longer PFS and OS, with greater declines associated with better outcomes. Patients achieving ctDNA clearance (CL) by week 21 had the most significant PFS and OS improvements (p < 0.001). Radiographic response (Resp) within 6 months posttreatment was also associated with improved survival. Combined ctDNA_CL and Resp further enhanced prognostic discrimination. When comparing atezolizumab plus bevacizumab plus carboplatin plus paclitaxel versus bevacizumab plus carboplatin plus paclitaxel (BCP), the individual-level association between 6-month ctDNA CL plus Resp and survival yielded a global odds ratio of 2.06 (95% CI: 2.02–2.11) for PFS and 6.08 (95% CI: 5.92–6.23) for OS. Similar global odds ratios were observed for atezolizumab plus carboplatin plus paclitaxel versus BCP. For the atezolizumab plus bevacizumab plus carboplatin plus paclitaxel versus BCP comparison, cohort-level associations between 6-month ctDNA CL plus Resp and PFS were R2WLS = 0.41 (95% CI: 0.33–0.50) and R2copula = 0.38 (95% CI: 0.30–0.45), whereas associations with OS were R2WLS = 0.48 (95% CI: 0.40–0.56) and R2copula = 0.51 (95% CI: 0.43–0.60) at 6 months. Slightly lower cohort-level associations were observed for atezolizumab plus carboplatin plus paclitaxel versus BCP (R2 = 0.34–0.41).ConclusionctDNA decline is associated with improved survival outcomes in a clear dose–response manner. ctDNA clearance and radiologic response provide complementary prognostic information, and combining these end points (ctDNA_CL + Resp) can further improve prediction of PFS and OS. This combination also enhanced the correlation between treatment effects on the early end points and PFS/OS at the trial level. However, their overall ability to predict treatment effects at the trial level remained modest for both PFS and OS, likely due to the limited data from a single study. Further validation in larger, multi-trial data sets is needed to establish ctDNA clearance as a reliable early surrogate end point in first-line NSCLC.Keywords: ctDNA clearance, NSCLC, Surrogacy, Meta-analysis

Introduction

NSCLC, with an incidence of more than 2 million cases annually worldwide,1 remains the most common type of lung cancer, characterized by a heterogeneous genetic and clinical profile. The introduction of immune checkpoint inhibitors has significantly transformed the first-line (1L) treatment paradigm, leading to extended overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) compared with traditional chemotherapy.2, 3, 4, 5 Despite these advances, NSCLC continues to have a high mortality rate, with a 5-year relative survival rate of approximately 26.4%,1 highlighting the urgent need for continued development of more effective and less toxic therapeutic approaches.

OS remains the accepted standard for evaluating clinical benefit in oncology, yet using OS as a primary end point in NSCLC trials poses significant challenges.6 The effectiveness of modern therapies necessitates prolonged follow-up periods or larger patient recruitment, making OS-based studies more complex. In addition, the high prevalence of comorbidities among patients with NSCLC introduces competing survival risks, potentially confounding NSCLC-specific treatment effects. Moreover, the availability of effective second- and third-line therapies can obscure the OS advantage of first-line regimens, further complicating trial design and interpretation.7

Consequently, surrogate end points such as PFS and response rates are widely used to assess treatment efficacy in NSCLC trials.8 PFS, in particular, has been extensively used as a regulatory end point due to its ability to capture early treatment benefits and its correlation with OS in certain settings.9 However, the strength of this correlation remains debated, as some trials have found significant PFS improvements without a corresponding OS benefit.10 Given these limitations, there is a critical need to identify and leverage novel intermediate clinical end points that can predict clinical benefits more rapidly than traditional measures of disease progression or mortality, thereby hastening the delivery of effective treatments and enhancing patient outcomes.

Emerging evidence highlights circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) as a promising biomarker for predicting PFS and OS in NSCLC.11,12 ctDNA levels offer a real-time, noninvasive measure of tumor burden, often detecting disease progression, recurrence, or metastases earlier than traditional imaging.13 Studies have revealed that lower posttreatment ctDNA levels correlate with longer PFS and OS.14, 15, 16 Assaf et al.11 recently developed an elastic net-based prognostic machine learning model using ctDNA data from the IMpower150 trial, reinforcing the prognostic value of ctDNA. Given its sensitivity and specificity, ctDNA clearance has the potential to serve as an effective surrogate marker for treatment response in metastatic NSCLC, particularly after first-line chemoimmunotherapy.11,17 Despite these findings, few studies have quantitatively evaluated ctDNA surrogacy in NSCLC—that is, its ability to predict treatment effects and whether the treatment effects on early ctDNA end points reliably predict the treatment effects on survival outcomes—using standardized statistical approaches such as meta-analytic methods.

In this study, the viability of ctDNA decline (alone or in combination with radiographic response) as an early, intermediate clinical end point for patients with metastatic nonsquamous NSCLC receiving first-line chemoimmunotherapy was evaluated. Using the ctDNA data set from the IMpower150 clinical trial,11 a dual-level meta-analytic assessment was conducted. At the individual patient level, the prognostic value of ctDNA clearance was quantified, whereas at the trial level, the correlation between treatment effects on ctDNA-based end points and on long-term survival outcomes was analyzed. These findings contribute to the validation of ctDNA levels as an early intermediate marker, potentially accelerating the development of novel therapies and improving clinical outcomes for patients with metastatic NSCLC.

Method

Study Population

This analysis used data from the IMpower150 trial (NCT02366143), a phase III, open-label, randomized clinical study aimed at assessing the efficacy and safety of various treatment regimens for patients with stage IV or recurrent metastatic nonsquamous NSCLC who had not received prior chemotherapy.18 Patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio to receive atezolizumab plus carboplatin plus paclitaxel (ACP), bevacizumab plus carboplatin plus paclitaxel (BCP), or atezolizumab plus BCP (ABCP) every 3 weeks for four or six 21-day cycles, followed by maintenance therapy with atezolizumab, bevacizumab, or a combination of both. Treatment continued until disease progression, intolerable adverse effects, or patient withdrawal.

Longitudinal ctDNA samples were collected from 466 patients at both baseline and on-treatment time points. Specifically, plasma samples were obtained at cycle 1 day 1 (C1D1; baseline), cycle 2 day 1 (C2D1; week 3), cycle 3 day 1 (C3D1; week 6), cycle 4 day 1 (C4D1; week 9), and cycle 8 day 1 (C8D1; week 21). Genomic analysis was performed using a custom 330-kb hybrid-capture next-generation sequencing (NGS) assay targeting 311 cancer-related genes. The assay followed the FoundationACT protocol, requiring a minimum of 20 ng of circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) per sample. Variant calling excluded known germline variants, noncoding alterations, CHIP-associated genes, and regions with insufficient coverage, thereby focusing on somatic mutations relevant to disease monitoring. Details of ctDNA sample collection, processing, and development of the ctDNA assay were described elsewhere.11 The study protocol received approval from the ethics committees at all participating sites, and adherence to International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki was ensured. Detailed methodology and eligibility criteria for the study have been previously published.

End Points

The primary end point of the clinical trial was OS, defined as the duration from random assignment to the date of death from any cause. Secondary end points included PFS, calculated from random assignment/study inclusion to the date of progressive disease or death of any cause. The response to treatment was assessed using tumor imaging classified according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors system.19 Radiologic tumor evaluations were performed using computed tomography (CT) at baseline, every 6 weeks for the first 48 weeks, and every 9 weeks thereafter. Tumor response was assessed centrally by blinded independent reviewers. Response categories included complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD). Radiologic response (Resp) was defined as achieving either CR or PR. Confirmed response (cResp) is defined as a tumor response (CR or PR) verified through follow-up imaging after the initial observation. In contrast, an unconfirmed response (uResp) denotes an initial CR or PR that lacks subsequent imaging confirmation. For the purposes of this analysis, the intermediate end points evaluated were ctDNA clearance, cResp, uResp, cResp plus ctDNA clearance, and uResp plus ctDNA clearance at approximately 6 months. Supplementary Figure 1 presents some examples to explain the definitions of uResp cResp, ctDNA clearance, uResp plus ctDNA clearance, and cResp plus ctDNA clearance.

Data Analysis

A two-level meta-analytic approach proposed by Burzykowski et al.20 and Buyse et al.21 was applied. This method enables the concurrent assessment of individual-level surrogacy by analyzing the association between ctDNA clearance and clinical outcomes (OS/PFS) within patients and trial-level surrogacy by determining whether a treatment effect on ctDNA end points can reliably predict a treatment effect on OS/PFS outcomes across study arms.

The individual-level association between early intermediate end points and PFS/OS was quantified using the global odds ratio (OR), estimated through a bivariate Plackett copula model. Statistical significance was defined as a 95% confidence interval for the OR that excluded 1.0. In addition, the relationship between intermediate end points at 6 months and PFS/OS was evaluated using a 6-month landmark analysis.

To estimate trial-level correlation between a treatment effect on intermediate end points and a treatment effect on OS/PFS, two different methods were used: (1) a Plackett copula-based random-effects model that estimated R2Copula to accommodate between-trial variability and patient-level correlations and (2) weighted least squares (WLS) regression that produced R2WLS values which described the proportion of variance in treatment effects on OS/PFS explained by treatment effects on intermediate end points. In the WLS method, WLS estimates treatment effects on both outcomes using separate marginal models (i.e., logistic regression for intermediate end points and Cox proportional hazards model for OS/PFS) and weights were assigned based on either the trial sample size or the inverse variance of the log ORs for the treatment effect on intermediate end points within each trial.

Because this analysis relied on data from a single trial (IMpower150), a multi-trial meta-analysis framework was simulated by randomly partitioning the data set into four randomly assigned cohorts, each treated as a separate trial. This methodology, originally proposed by Halabi et al.22 for assessing the surrogacy of PSA decline in prostate cancer studies, allows for a more comprehensive evaluation of surrogate end points within a structured meta-analytic approach. The selection of four cohorts ensured that each treatment group maintained a minimum of 50 patients per fold. Because the partitioning was random, the process was repeated 500 times to enhance reliability. The global OR and R2 were then computed across all replicates and averaged to obtain final estimates.

The analysis assessed treatment effects across two regimen comparisons: ABCP versus BCP and ACP versus BCP, facilitating the cross-validation of intermediate end points across different therapeutic approaches. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1), using the survival and Surrogate packages.23,24

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Of the 466 patients in the IMpower150 trial with on-treatment ctDNA measurements, those with OS/PFS durations of less than 24 weeks were excluded in the landmark analysis. The final analysis included 402 patients for OS and 297 for PFS. The patient selection flowchart is presented in Supplementary Figure 2. Baseline characteristics were well-balanced across treatment arms, with no significant differences in demographic or clinical variables (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

The median PFS for the overall study population was 7 months (interquartile range, 4–13 mo), whereas median OS was 19 months (interquartile range, 9–33 mo). Patients in the ABCP arm demonstrated significantly longer PFS compared with those in the BCP and ACP arms, indicating a stronger treatment benefit with ABCP (Supplementary Table 3). For OS, the treatment effect for ABCP was also more favorable than for BCP. Overall, the trends in treatment effects within this ctDNA subset were consistent with those reported for the overall study population.18

Figure 1 further illustrates the strong correlation between survival outcomes and the magnitude of ctDNA reduction. For both PFS and OS, hazard ratios (HRs) progressively decreased with deeper ctDNA declines, indicating a lower risk of progression or death among patients with greater reductions. Patients achieving a more than or equal to 90% ctDNA decline had significantly longer PFS compared with those with lesser declines (HR: 0.75; 95% CI: 0.58–0.96; p = 0.021) (Fig. 1A). Moreover, those who attained complete ctDNA clearance exhibited the lowest risk of progression (HR: 0.54; 95% CI: 0.41–0.73; p < 0.001), emphasizing the prognostic value of deep molecular responses (Fig. 1A).

A similar trend was observed for OS (Fig. 1B), where greater ctDNA reductions were consistently associated with lower HRs and highly significant p values, suggesting that even modest declines in ctDNA could have clinical relevance. Similarly, complete ctDNA clearance was linked to the lowest mortality risk compared with patients without clearance (HR: 0.35; 95% CI: 0.25–0.50; p < 0.001), underscoring the crucial role of ctDNA elimination in improving survival outcomes (Fig. 1B).

Patient-Level Surrogacy

A meta-analysis was conducted to assess the surrogacy potential of ctDNA clearance (ctDNA CL) during the first 6 months after treatment, unconfirmed response (uResp), and confirmed response (cResp) with 6 months posttreatment. Survival outcomes were evaluated using landmark Kaplan-Meier analysis and log-rank tests to analyze associations between these end points and PFS/OS.

The landmark analysis demonstrated that ctDNA clearance had a significant impact on PFS (Fig. 2), with patients exhibiting detectable ctDNA at approximately 6 months posttreatment experiencing a median PFS of 4.12 months, compared with 9.55 months for those with undetectable ctDNA (HR: 2.06; 95% CI: 1.52–2.78; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2A). Radiographic responders also generally exhibit longer PFS than nonresponders, with confirmed responders (median PFS = 6.79 mo) having a slight advantage than unconfirmed responders (median PFS = 6.16 mo) (Fig. 2A). The global OR analysis (Fig. 4A) also revealed strong individual-level associations between 6-month ctDNA-CL-cResp and both PFS/OS across the two treatment comparisons. In the ABCP versus BCP comparison, the GOR was 2.06 (95% CI: 2.02–2.10) for PFS and 6.06 (95% CI: 5.92–6.23) for OS, indicating a substantial survival advantage for patients achieving ctDNA clearance and a confirmed response. Trial-Level Surrogacy

At the trial level, the strength of surrogacy for treatment effects was assessed using R2copula and R2WLS. At the trial level, combined ctDNA clearance and radiographic response at 6 months generally demonstrated stronger surrogacy for treatment effects than either assessment alone. As expected, ctDNA clearance exhibited better trial-level surrogacy compared with radiographic response, whether confirmed or unconfirmed (Fig. 4B and C).

The correlation between the treatment effect on 6-month cResp plus ctDNA CL and PFS at the trial level was R2WLS = 0.41 (95% CI: 0.33–0.50) for the ABCP versus BCP comparison and 0.41 (95% CI: 0.32–0.50) for the ACP versus BCP comparison. Using the copula model, the corresponding R2copula values were 0.38 (95% CI: 0.30–0.45) and 0.38 (95% CI: 0.29–0.46) for ABCP versus BCP and ACP versus BCP, respectively.

Slightly stronger trial-level surrogacy was observed for OS (Fig. 4B and C), where the correlation between the treatment effect on 6-month cResp plus ctDNA CL and OS was higher, with R2WLS = 0.48 (95% CI: 0.40–0.56) and R2copula = 0.51 (95% CI: 0.43–0.60) for ABCP versus BCP. A slightly lower predictive strength was noted in the ACP versus BCP comparison. The distributions of R2copula and R2WLS for PFS and OS are illustrated in Supplementary Figure 3.

Discussion

To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to apply a meta-analytical approach to evaluate ctDNA clearance as an early intermediate end point across multiple treatment regimens and survival end points in first-line NSCLC. The analysis provides quantitative surrogacy metrics that assess not only the prognostic value of ctDNA clearance at the patient level but also its predictive capability for treatment effects at the trial level. At the patient level, it was assessed whether individuals who achieved an early end point (ctDNA clearance or radiologic response by 6 mo) experienced longer survival, demonstrating their prognostic value. At the trial level, it was evaluated whether treatments that improved early end points also led to greater survival benefits. Treatment effects on PFS/OS were correlated with effects on early surrogate markers across experimental versus control comparisons, deriving two R2 metrics: one from a random-effects copula meta-model (R2Copula) and another from weighted least-squares regression (R2WLS). Clinically, a strong individual-level OR suggests that early response is a favorable prognostic indicator, whereas a high trial-level R2 supports the surrogate’s ability to predict treatment benefit. Together, these analyses can inform the validity of ctDNA clearance and tumor response as early markers of survival.

The accelerated approvals of drugs based on objective response rate and duration of response reflect a move toward more rapidly assessable end points that can hasten drug development processes.25, 26, 27 However, objective response rate and duration of response do not fully capture the complexities of disease biology. The findings reveal that ctDNA CL and radiographic response hold significant prognostic value for both PFS and OS across various treatment regimens (ABCP versus BCP; ACP versus BCP). However, ctDNA CL exhibited stronger correlation with PFS/OS compared with radiographic response and may provide more biologically relevant insights. Moreover, combining ctDNA CL with radiographic response further enhances its prognostic value for both PFS and OS, reinforcing its potential as a complementary biomarker in clinical assessment.

This study also highlights that greater ctDNA declines correlate with more favorable survival outcomes in the 1L NSCLC, with the most pronounced association observed between complete ctDNA clearance and survival outcomes, supporting its potential as a meaningful intermediate end point in clinical trials. The individual patient-level correlation according to global OR between 6-month ctDNA clearance and PFS/OS was strong, reinforcing the prognostic significance of deep molecular responses in this setting.

At the trial level, the correlation between treatment effects on ctDNA clearance end points and PFS or OS was generally modest. For instance, the R2WLS and R2Copula values for cResp plus ctDNA CL were 0.41 and 0.38, respectively, for PFS across the ABCP versus BCP comparison; however, for OS, the values were slightly higher (R2WLS = 0.48; R2Copula = 0.51). The numerical differences between R2Copula and R2WLS likely arose from their distinct derivation methods. R2WLS is derived from a linear regression of marginal treatment effects and does not account for within-trial patient-level correlations. In contrast, R2Copula incorporates both individual-level association and between-trial heterogeneity via a joint modeling approach. Overall, the limited follow-up duration, particularly for long-term survivors, underscores the need for further studies with extended observation periods to fully assess ctDNA clearance as a surrogate end point for OS.

Although the analytic methods used in this study were statistically rigorous, several limitations remain. First, the analysis is based on a single trial with a relatively small data set, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, although ctDNA-based metrics demonstrated prognostic value, the correlations between treatment effects on ctDNA clearance and OS were only modest, and the associations with PFS were relatively weak, underscoring the challenges in fully capturing long-term survival determinants. The variability in surrogacy strength across different treatment regimens further emphasizes the need for validation in larger, more diverse cohorts. Third, differences in ctDNA assay sensitivity, cutoff thresholds, and methodologies across studies may introduce heterogeneity, highlighting the importance of standardizing ctDNA assessments in future trials. Finally, as the treatment landscape for NSCLC continues to evolve with immunotherapy-based regimens and novel targeted agents, the utility of ctDNA clearance as a surrogate end point may vary across different therapeutic approaches, necessitating further investigation to refine its role in clinical decision-making and drug development.

In summary, a greater decline in ctDNA was linked to longer survival in a clear dose–response relationship. In addition, ctDNA clearance and radiologic response each offered distinct and complementary prognostic value, and their combination (ctDNA_CL + Resp) improved the prediction of PFS and OS at the patient level. Nonetheless, the correlation between these early markers and treatment effects at the trial level remained modest for both PFS and OS, which may reflect the constraints of using data from a single study. Broader validation in larger, multi-trial analyses will be essential to confirm ctDNA clearance as a robust early surrogate end point in first-line NSCLC.

In conclusion, the findings of this study demonstrate a strong patient-level correlation between ctDNA decline and survival outcomes in 1L NSCLC, with greater declines corresponding to longer survival in a clear dose–response pattern. Furthermore, ctDNA clearance and radiologic response each offered distinct and complementary prognostic value, and their combination (ctDNA_CL + Resp) improved the prediction of PFS and OS at the patient level. Notably, combining these markers also improved the correlation between treatment effects on the early end points and PFS/OS at the trial level. Nonetheless, the overall trial-level correlation remained modest for both PFS and OS, suggesting that ctDNA clearance may not fully capture treatment effects on PFS/OS. Further validation is necessary to establish ctDNA clearance as a definitive surrogate end point using data from future large-scale studies. Long-term follow-up, harmonization of ctDNA assessment methodologies, and prospective validation in randomized studies will be essential to fully realize the potential of ctDNA clearance as a surrogate biomarker that can predict clinical benefit and allow more rapid drug approval to improve the treatment of NSCLC.
