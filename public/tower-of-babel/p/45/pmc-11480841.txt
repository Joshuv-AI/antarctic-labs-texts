Overcoming drug-resistant tumors with selection gene drives
Authors: Hui Wang, Mingqi Xie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Drug resistance is a major hurdle prohibiting effective treatment of many diseases, including cancer. Using model-guided designs, Leighow et al.1 engineered a dual-switch selection gene drive system custom designed to combat drug-resistant tumors. By demonstrating remarkable killing efficacies in preclinical models using human non-small lung cancer cells in vitro and in mice, this work describes an attractive mindset to develop next-generation anticancer therapies.

Main text

Drug resistance is a major challenge for effective anticancer therapy, with intrinsic genetic heterogeneity within the tumor and the externally applied therapeutic pressure being key drivers of its evolution.1,2 Because drug resistances also evolve at considerably faster timescales than scientists can ever develop effective drugs, combating drug-resistant tumors using sequential (mono)therapies may remain elusive. While combination therapies using multiple drugs to inhibit multiple targets are promising strategies to “outpace” the evolution of drug resistance by intuition, effective therapeutic performances in real practice would require that the different drug targets are strictly orthogonal to each other.3 This would cause enormous challenges not only for drug discovery but also for basic research from a human genetics and cell biology perspective.

Technically speaking, intratumor expression of particular “suicide genes” (also known as gene-directed enzyme prodrug therapy [GDEPT]) may be an effective alternative to overcome the common issues of drug resistance.4 One example is cytosine deaminase, which converts the prodrug 5-fluorocytosine (5-FC) into a potent anticancer agent, 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), and has been tested in clinic.5 In this manner, 5-FC selectively causes cytotoxic effects to tumor tissues that express cytosine deaminase as the therapeutic transgene, not only increasing drug susceptibility of the transduced cancer cells irrespective of the onset of drug resistance but also triggering the killing of the surrounding cell populations through potential bystander effects. At present, expression of such suicide genes in vivo is commonly achieved through delivery strategies adopted from gene or cell-based therapies, which may suffer from inconsistencies in the efficiency of transgene transduction or tumor-tropic migration when treating different types of cancers.6 Therefore, technologies that may enhance or standardize the clinical applicability of GDEPTs may be instrumental to combat drug resistance and develop improved cancer therapies.

Reporting recently in Nature Biotechnology, Justin R. Pritchard and colleagues1 designed a dual-switch selection gene drive with the potential to kill drug-resistant tumors (Figure 1). Gene drives are naturally selected or genetically engineered control devices capable of propagating a particular set of target genes throughout a cell population with super-Mendelian (>50%) probabilities.7,8 Genetically engineered gene drives were traditionally developed to “outcompete” virulent or harmful strains of particular microorganisms or mosquito species, thus offering the prospect of translating gene-editing technologies toward potential applications in environmental sciences.8 Here, by engineering a selection gene drive allowing for external proliferation control of cells stably transgenic for suicide gene expression, the authors elegantly combined GDEPT and synthetic biology to describe an innovative strategy for eradication of drug-resistant tumors. Specifically, the system consists of two gene switches: an evolutionary driver that enables user-defined control of cell proliferation through rapamycin analog (AP20187)-mediated homodimerization of an EGFR-derived kinase domain (S1vEGFR; switch 1) and a cytosine deaminase representing the therapeutic payload enabling 5-FC-mediated cell killing (S2vCyD; switch 2). When cancer cells express both switches, AP20187 exposure triggers the proliferation of cells carrying the therapeutic payload, which is essential to amplify the population size of prodrug-sensitive cells and account for enhanced 5-FC-mediated cytotoxicity. In this manner, the combined action of 5-FU and its bystander effects may be sufficient to eliminate both drug-sensitive and -resistant clones in the tumor as soon as the transgenic cells are stimulated to proliferate into a dominant population size. To support this hypothesis, the authors also developed stochastic models of tumor evolution9 to analyze the evolutionary risks caused by potential mutations during treatment of the gene drive, as well as to identify spatial constraints for achievement of safe and efficacious bystander effects. For experimental validation, the authors first expressed both switches in murine IL-3-dependent BaF3 cells engineered with conditional resistances to erlotinib. AP20187 administration indeed allowed erlotinib-resistant transgenic cells to rapidly proliferate and dominate over the erlotinib-sensitive population, thus confirming the functionality of switch 1. To validate the functionality of switch 2, the authors showed that administration of 5-FC could indeed kill mixed populations of native and transgenic BaF3 cells, confirming the involvement of direct 5-FU-mediated cytotoxicity and its bystander effects. Notably, this bystander effect was comparable to those triggered by cellular adoptive immunotherapies. Specifically, the authors engineered CD19-expressing human non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) PC9 cells as an alternative therapeutic trigger (S2vCD19). Thus, when co-cultured with CD19− cancer cells and primary T cells expressing CD3, T cell-mediated killing of both CD19+ and CD19− PC9 cells was effectively triggered upon administration of the bispecific CD3/CD19 antibody blinatumomab. The bystander effect was confirmed by performing this killing experiment in trans-well plates, where CD19+ and CD19− cells were physically separated.

Next, to test the combined action of switches 1 and 2 in constituting a selection gene drive, the authors pooled three different cell populations of drug-sensitive, drug-resistant, and gene-drive transgenic cells at different predefined ratios. Once AP20187 allowed the transgenic cell population to become dominant, 5-FC was applied. Flow cytometry analysis confirmed that the resistant cell population quickly collapsed upon prodrug exposure, a result that fell in line with the initial study design and the prediction results of the stochastic models. In contrast, no treatment efficacy on the drug-resistant population was observed when only the prodrug was administered without AP20187-mediated stimulation of proliferation. The same phenomena were observed in vivo using xenograft experiments involving corresponding populations of BaF3 and PC9 cells harvested with a similar logic. These results suggest that a selection gene drive therapy may be effective for eradicating heterogeneous populations of cancer cells that would otherwise relapse under conventional monotherapy. Furthermore, the authors show that the kinase domain of S1vEGFR in switch 1 can be flexibly exchanged or mutated to achieve compatibility with different types of drug resistance. For example, when replacing the T790M erlotinib-resistant mutation with a C797S osimertinib-resistant mutation, AP20187 was able to initiate gene-drive-mediated killing of PC9 cells that would have survived from long-term osimertinib treatment. In addition, the gene-drive-mediated killing strategy may also be amenable to cancer cells that acquire off-target mutations. In fact, according to clinical data of NSCLC patients treated with osimertinib, 22%–39% of tumors acquire mutations in genes parallel to or downstream of EGFR.10 Thus, mutants for 94% of all possible amino acid substitutions along the EGFR kinase domain were generated by saturating mutagenesis to describe genetic variants with differential sensitivities to osimertinib. Similarly, using a genome-wide CRISPR knockout library of 76,441 variants, the authors created a diverse population of PC9 cells containing different genetic alterations across the genome. When testing the gene drive system in both libraries in trans, extremely heterogeneous populations were effectively eradicated by the sequential operation of switch 1 (S1vEGFR) and switch 2 (S2vCyD). These results reveal that the gene drive strategy may consistently perform in drug-resistant cancer cells of highly diverse genetic backgrounds (i.e., whether the acquired mutations reside inside or outside of EGFR kinase domains) as long as the gene circuit can be effectively delivered and propagated in a corresponding (sub)population of the tumor.

Taken together, this work describes an interesting alternative to address an important problem of cancer treatments. In particular, moving the gene drive strategy into the arena of cancer therapy was highly innovative. While synthetic gene drive systems were conventionally designed to selectively propagate a “good” genotype alongside the lineages of genetic inheritance, this work was probably unique in pre-installing a synthetic killing switch into a malignant population so that the local concentration of an anticancer agent can be elegantly amplified when the gene drive promotes proliferation of a theoretically “bad” genotype. Furthermore, the gene circuits were compactly designed, modular, and technically compatible with various clinically eligible gene delivery routes. In this work, the authors show that switch 1 can be engineered to comprise various EGFR, RET, or ALK-derived kinase domains to trigger cell proliferation under a diverse set of drug-resistant contexts. Experimentally, the authors showed that switch 2 can also comprise the nitroreductase NfsA as another suicide gene apart from cytosine deaminase, or any surface antigen capable of engaging cytotoxic T cells. Here, the authors employed a standardized experimental design that first uses lentiviral vectors to stably express both gene switches in specific cancer cell lines with a known drug resistance profile, followed by xenograft models involving pre-mixed populations of (1) drug-sensitive cells, (2) drug-resistant cells, and (3) drug-resistant cells carrying the gene drive. While these experimental models suggest that even highly heterogeneous cancer cell populations may be effectively killed by the direct and bystander effects of the selection gene drive, killing efficacies in the ultimate in vivo studies were not complete. In fact, both the experimental and modeling results shown in the paper suggest that optimal killing results may require careful titration of optimal ratios and starting sizes of each cell population and/or simultaneous operation of switches 1 and 2 to some extent. Thus, a key question that remains to be addressed in future work may be whether or how such arguably delicate experimental conditions can translate into a clinically compatible gene delivery or treatment regimen in real practice.
