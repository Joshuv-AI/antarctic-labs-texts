Relationships between intolerance of uncertainty and depression in Chinese high-altitude migrants: the mediating effect of mindfulness
Authors: Xiaoxiao Xu, Ying Wu, Li Sun, Kaixuan Zhou, Lan Li, Liang Zhou, Xie He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe current study aims to explore the path net linking intolerance of uncertainty (IU) and depressive symptoms among migrants at high altitude regions and examine whether mindfulness mediates the associations.Methods1064 Chinese migrants who arrive in Qinghai-Tibet Plateau of China no more than one year were recruited by the cluster random sampling to perform the test with Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale-Short Version-12 (IUS-12), Mindful Attention Awareness Scale (MAAS) and Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). Then, path models were fitted to build the path net linking IU and depressive symptoms, with correlation analysis performed.ResultsThe partial analysis showed that the PHQ-9 was positively correlated with both the prospective IU (r=0.315, P0.05) and inhibitory IU (r=0.404, P<0.05), which were the two dimensions of IUS-12. The MAAS score was negatively correlated with prospective IU (r=-0.196, P<0.05), inhibitory IU (r=-0.385, P<0.05) and PHQ-9 (r=-0.488, P<0.05). Both the prospective IU (β=0.137, 95% CI: 0.079 0.204) and inhibitory IU (β=0.169, 95% CI: 0.097 0.257) could directly affect depressive symptoms. The indirect effect of inhibitory IU on depressive symptoms via mindfulness was found to be significant (β=0.101, 95% CI: 0.068 0.137), accounting for 48.30% of the total effect. On the contrary, the indirect effect of prospective IU on depressive symptoms was found not significant. ConclusionsIU was positively associated with depressive symptoms scores. And it was the inhibitory IU but not the prospective IU directly mediated by mindfulness in depressive symptoms development of migrants enrolled in the study at high-altitude regions of China.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24305-8.Keywords: Intolerance of uncertainty, Depressive symptoms, Mindfulness, Migrants, Path analysis

Introduction

High altitude regions are defined as geographic territories that are 1,500 m above sea level or higher [1]. Acute mountain sickness often occurs in areas above the altitude of 2500 m [2]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis that studied more than 40,000 participates from 4 different countries revealed that the combined prevalence of depressive symptoms was 17.9% at high altitude regions [3]. A higher prevalence of depression (59.4%) was found in residents in high altitude regions in China compared to their peers in the low regions (11.1%), largely attributable to the naturaypobaric hypoxia environment and limited social circumstances lacking accessibility to mental health services [4, 5]. Meanwhile, driven by the investment, tourists, services, and the counterpart support in the anoxic environment, urbanization in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau has become a special mission in the high-altitude areas, including the urban belt along the Qinghai-Tibet Railway construction, town belt building along the border town zone, and so on. It has attracted a large stream young labor concerned in this study, coming from the lower mainland of China, especially since the financial crisis of 2008 and employment problems happening in many countries. The studies focused on the migrant workers in China, which were a special phenomenon in the process of China’s economic transformation, revealed the vulnerable state of this population. The education status and the marital status might be correlated to the major depressive disorder prevalence of the internal migrant work of China [6, 7].

As we know that the high plateau climate and complex uncertainty factors make adaptation tough in the unfavorable environment, especially for immigrants from low altitude regions who prone to have adjustment problems resulting from both physiological and mental factors [4]. Both acute and long-term high-altitude environment exposure could impair numerous brain functions including cognitive performance, emotion adjustment, behavior reaction and other mental function of immigrant population [8]. Impairment symptoms including memory loss, behavior reduction and poor thinking is undeniably within the first one to 2 weeks after exposure to high-altitude [9]. The individuals’ emotional performance was adversely affected in acute exposure to altitude above 3000 m detected by the Mood State Inventory, the Clyde Mood Scale, and the Multiple Affective Adjectives Checklist [10]. Kious et al. (2019) [11] firstly provided evidences from a large cohort suggesting that moving from low altitude (close to sea level) to high altitude (average altitude was ≥ 900 m and the maximum altitude was 1641.7 m) contributes to increase the risk of depression, anxiety and suicide ideation in 3764 graduate medicine student participants immigrating due to their work. Additionally, long-term exposure to high-altitude hypoxia even made immigrant population had pronounced declined in attention and work capacity [12]. Still, former studies have paid close attentions to the effects induced by the alteration in brain bioenergetics, such as serotonin synthesis in the relative hypoxia environment to the psychiatric symptoms [11]. Here, we focus on the mental health of high-altitude migrants to explore more effective depression interventions based on psychological theories and technologies.

Intolerance of uncertainty (IU) is originally defined as a cognitive bias that represents emotional, behavioral and cognitive responses to uncertain to events, regardless of whether these events will occur in the future [13]. Carleton (2016) [14] described IU as the “individual’s dispositional incapacity to endure the aversive response triggered by the perceived absence of salient, key, or sufficient information, and sustained by the associated perception of uncertainty”. Individuals with high IU tend to negatively interpret uncertain situations, perceive themselves as unable to face uncertainty, and experience emotional distress [15]. Models of psychopathology posit that the incapacity to tolerate unknowns appears to be a robust vulnerability factor in a range of psychological disorders [16–19]. Robust positive relationships between IU and depressive disorders diagnoses [15] and symptoms [20] were found. Moreover, IU has been typically conceptualized as a higher order construct and comprised of two lower-order dimensions: the prospective IU and the inhibitory IU. The prospective IU relates to uncertainty regarding future events representing the cognitive dimension. It could refer to the desire for predictability, for knowing what will happen in the future, and engagement in seeking information to reduce uncertainty. And the inhibitory IU represents the behavioral dimension and could refers to avoidance and paralysis in the presence of uncertainty [15, 17, 21]. Some studies indicated that both the prospective IU and inhibitory IU were significantly correlated with depression [22], and the inhibitory IU was more strongly related to depression than the prospective IU [20, 23]. However, Saulnier et al. (2019) [24] found that the inhibitory IU was related to the cognitive factor of depression, not the affective/somatic factor. And prospective IU was not related to cognitive or affective/somatic factors. In order to clarify above issue, we delved into the relationships between IU (especially the prospective IU and the inhibitory IU) and depression in high-altitude migrants.

Exploring protective factors to alleviate the negative psychological impact of IU has important clinical intervention implications. Mindfulness defined as the ability to observe thoughts, bodily sensations or feelings in the present moment with an open and accepting orientation toward one’s experiences is one of these protective factors [25]. Sharma and Kumra (2022) [26] revealed that mindfulness was negatively related to depression. Additionally, mindfulness was negatively associated with IU and its two components, and the mediating role of mindfulness between IU and depression has been confirmed [22, 27]. Specifically, Nekic and Mamic (2019) [22] revealed that mindfulness partially mediates the relationship between the IU and depression in female students. Sbrilli et al. (2021) [27] found a significantly mediating effect of mindfulness in between IU and psychological symptoms (anxiety, depression, somatization) among pregnant individuals and new mothers within 6 months postpartum. The possible explanation for the mediating role of mindfulness between IU and depression is that mindfulness encourages individuals to be aware of their internal experience and change the ways in which they relate to negative thoughts and emotions [28]. Model of mindful emotion regulation also suggests that mindfulness enhances individual’s perception and non-reaction, and cultivates the ability to not automatically evaluate emotional distress [29]. Hence, mindfulness allows individuals to maintain a non-reactive mode, re-evaluate uncertain situations as non-threatening and acceptable, ultimately lead to reductions in depression [30]. However, the mediating role of mindfulness in between the lower-order dimensions of IU and depression in a larger sample of immigrant populations from low-altitude to high-altitude is still unclear.

Therefore, our study leverages path analysis to explore the effect of IU on depression, including the lower-order dimensions of IU (prospective IU and inhibitory IU), and the mediating role of mindfulness among high-altitude migrants. We hypothesize that: (1) both the prospective IU and inhibitory IU were positively related to depression and inversely related to mindfulness; (2) mindfulness could play a buffering role between the prospective IU and depression, the inhibitory IU and depression.

Materials and methods

Participants

A cross-sectional was conducted in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau of China during August and September 2022. 1064 healthy Chinese migrants who arrive in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau no more than one year and mainly engaged in physical work were conducted to participant in this study. Before arriving in these regions, all of them received physical health check and mental health assessment. The assessment of mental health was conducted by completing test questions on the Wen-Juan-Xing (https://www.wjx.cn/login.aspx) platform. A total of 1064 valid questionnaires were returned and the effective response rate was 100%. As shown in Table 1, the age of participants ranged from 19 to 40 years old (median (Interquartile Range, IQR) = 24.00 (4.00)). Among them, 16 participants were junior school degree, accounting for 1.50%, 395 participants were senior school degree, accounting for 37.12%, 479 participants were college degree, accounting for 45.03%, and 174 participants were bachelor degree or above, accounting for 16.35%. 178 were married, accounting for 16.7%, 882 were single, accounting for 82.89%, and 4 were divorced, accounting for 0.38%. This study was approved by the ethics committee of Army Medical University (No.2021-11-02) in Chongqing, China, shown in the files of the ethics proof.

Measures

Intolerance of uncertainty scale-short version-12 (IUS-12)

The IUS-12 was used to measure the degree of individual uncertainty tolerance [31]. This scale is adapted from the 27 items complete version prepared by Freeston et al. (1994) [13] and consists of 12 items. It uses a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (not at all characteristic of me) to 5 (very characteristic of me) to evaluate two sub-scales of IU (prospective IU and inhibitory IU). A greater score in each subscale indicates a greater level of prospective IU and inhibitory IU. The reliability coefficients (Cronbach’s Alpha and Omega) of all variables were showed in Table 2.

Mindful attention awareness scale (MAAS)

The MAAS is a 15-item single-dimension measure of trait mindfulness. This scale measures the frequency of open and receptive attention to and awareness of ongoing events and experience [32]. Response options ranged from 1 (almost never) to 6 (almost always) and a summated score can be computed for the MAAS total score, where a higher score indicates a higher level of mindfulness.

Patient health questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)

The PHQ-9 was administered to assess depressive symptoms over the last two weeks [33]. Respondents indicated, on a 0 (never) 3 (almost always) scale, the frequency with which they experienced the following symptoms: (1) anhedonia, (2) depressed mood, (3) sleep disturbance, (4) fatigue, (5) appetite changes, (6) low self-esteem, (7) concentration problems, (8) psychomotor disturbances, and (9) suicidal ideation. Total scores range from 0 to 27, with scores!≥ 10 representing clinically significant depressive symptoms. Furthermore, the PHQ-9 is validated as a depressive symptom severity measure (total score 1 4: minimal depression, 5 9: mild depression, 10 14: moderate depression, 15 19: moderately severe depression, and 20 27: severe depression).

Quality control

In order to ensure the smooth progress and quality of the entire study, we trained the researchers and strictly monitored and controlled the questionnaire implementation process. Before the start of the research, all researchers should receive unified training to ensure that each researcher can master the survey methods and techniques, unify the concepts that may be involved in the survey process, and provide unified solutions, guidance, and precautions for possible problems that may arise. During the questionnaire filling process, if the research subjects have difficulty understanding the questionnaire, non-guiding neutral explanations can be given.

Statistical analysis

Measurement data was described by the median and IQR, and count data was described by the frequent and percentage. The partial correlation analysis was used to analyze the relationship between migrants'IU, mindful attention awareness and depression, and the P<0.05 was considered statistically significant (the adjustment factors: age, education and marital status). The Maximum Likelihood method was used to fit and analyze the path model and determine whether it met the multivariate normal or not. When the path model didn't conform to the multivariate normal (the c.r. of multivariate>;1.96), the Bollen-Stine Bootstrap (n=2000) correction method was used to refit the model parameters [34]. As the main model fitness indicator, /df is considered to have good model fitness when it is between 1 and 3 [35]. However, when /df = 0, the model is saturated and needs to be simplified by deleting invalid path relationships (P > 0.05) to improve the model fitness level [35]. Other model fitness indicators include RMSEA (Root-Mean-Square Error of Approximation), AGFI (Adjusted Goodness of Fit Index), NFI (Normed Fit Index), RFI (Relative Fit Index), IFI (Incremental Fit Index), CFI (Comparative Fit Index), and GFI (Goodness-of-Fit Index). SPSS 25.0 was used for statistical analysis, and AMOS 26.0 was applied to construct a mediatingmodel.

Discussion

In recent years, there has been an increasing interest in identifying mental disorders among people in high-altitude regions, where are distributed more than 500 million people all around the world [37], especially the migrants from low-altitude to high-altitude. According to this study, 7.43% of the internal migrant appeared depression symptom, and the median and IQR of prospective IU, inhibitory IU, MAAS and PHQ-9 were 17.00 (5.00), 8.00 (5.00), 87.00 (13.00) and 0.00 (1.00) respectively. Populations are more susceptible to suffer from depression in high-altitude regions compared with those who live in low altitude regions [5, 38]. And the migrant population suffered more adaptive problems caused by the unlivable hypoxia environment, who had been proved experienced mental impairment including emotional regulation, impaired executive function, dysmnesia, and more risk of suicide ideation, whether they have exposed in the high-altitude area for an acute period or a long-time term [4, 8, 9, 11]. The migrant population came from the plain cities of China, arrived in Qinghai-Tibet Plateau not more than one year, and mainly engaged in physical work, ranging from 19 to 40 years old. 61.38% of the participants were college degree, even 16.35% of them were bachelor degree. It appeared a well status of education may be related to the age of the participants, the economic decline along with the rising unemployment of globalization since 2008 and the state education improvement of China. And 83.3% of the participants were single or divorced. The high rate of non-marriage might be one of the characteristics of high-migrant, and the more unitary status might make the migrant more isolated, what might induce more severity emotional disturbance such as major depressive disorder [6]. Moreover, the higher education level population might be more easily to accept the interventions based on psychological theory, such as mindfulness relative interventions.

Mindfulness is one of the protective factors for many psychological disorders. Meanwhile, as an important concept explaining individual differences in mental dimension, IU has been paid fewer attention to [39]. In this study we sought to examine the contribution of IU (including prospective IU and inhibitory IU) and mindfulness in explaining the symptoms of depression in a sample of high-altitude migrants. The reason why IU plays an important role in the development and maintenance of depression may include that IU as a cognitive bias in terms that individual develops negative prediction towards future events [40]. Another explanation was that high levels of rumination (which is a center characteristic of depression), magnified the adverse impacts of uncertain, which in turn increase the occurrence of depression [41]. Although some studies have explored the relationship between IU and depression, less attention has been paid to the correlations between the two lower-order dimensions of IU and depression. Furthermore, we explored the relationship between the two lower-order dimensions of IU and depression, proving that both the prospective IU and inhibitory IU were significantly associated with depression in the high-altitude migrants. It was also supported by the researches of Nekic and Mamic (2019) on the females [22].

Meantime, Sbrilli et al. (2021) [27] tested the direct association among mindfulness, IU and psychological symptoms (anxiety, depression and somatization) in pregnant individuals and new mothers during the COVID-19 pandemic, and found that the indirect effect exerted by IU on psychological symptoms via perceived decrease in mindfulness was significant. Those high in IU tend to interpret uncertainty in a negative fashion, and created a vicious cycle of negative perceptions of uncertainty, avoidance, and escalating negative affect [19]. Altan-Atalay et al. (2024) [30] considered that mindfulness allowed individuals to maintain a non-reactive mode, re-evaluate uncertain situations as non-threatening and acceptable, ultimately led to reductions in depression. This viewpoint may be supported by several theoretical models of mindfulness. The re-perceiving model of mindfulness proposed by Shapiro et al. (2006) [42] considered that mindfulness promoted the automated and separated processing of thoughts, emotions and feeling, resulting in re-perception. According to the Mindfulness-to-Meaning Theory, mindfulness generates well-being by positive reappraisal (the positive psychological process through which stressful events or uncertain/negative situations are reconstructed as benign, meaningful or growth-promoting) [43]. Model of mindful emotion regulation also suggests that mindfulness enhances individual’s perception and non-reaction, and cultivates the ability to not automatically evaluate emotional distress [29]. Thus, mindfulness may reduce the control of immediate emotions and automated inertial responses caused by uncertainty, and decrease depression.

Unfortunately, limited researches explored the mediating role and mechanism of mindfulness between the two lower dimensions of IU and depression. Former studies have reported that mindfulness was negatively associated with prospective IU, inhibitory IU and depression. It meant that prospective IU, inhibitory IU and mindfulness emerged as reliable predictors of depression, which was also supported by Nekic and Mamic (2019) [22]. They revealed that mindfulness partially mediated the relationship between IU and depression in female students. More interestingly, we found the significant mediating role of mindfulness between inhibitory IU and depression, but not the prospective IU among migrants at high altitudes. The insignificant mediating role of mindfulness between prospective IU and depression may related with reasons as follow. The prospective IU was much weakly related to mindfulness than the inhibitory IU in this study, which was consistent with previous research [20, 23]. Saulnier et al. (2019) [24] found that prospective IU was not related to depression (cognitive, affective or somatic factors), whereas the inhibitory IU was related to the cognitive factor of depression, not the affective/somatic factor. The weak or no correlation between prospective IU and depression may result in the insignificant mediating effect of mindfulness between them.

Strengths and limitations

Our findings have significant theoretical and practical implications. In term of the theoretical implication, the effects of prospective IU and inhibitory IU on emotional disorders (such as depression) may be different, which haven’t been explored by previous studies, and this current study enriches the research about IU. In term of practical implication, the mediating role of mindfulness in between inhibitory IU and depression provides empirical evidence for future intervention to alleviate depression in high-altitude migrant populations and enhance their ability to tolerate uncertainty.

Despite its strengths, our study had several limitations. First, this is a cross-sectional study and it limits to infer causal relationship of variables. Longitudinal studies with a randomized intervention component are needed to better understand how IU and mindfulness impact metal health over time. Second, the demographic information collected in this study was not enough, such as information about their jobs, socio-economic status, number of children, reason for migrating, where they came from, etc. Additionally, confounding factors which were not reported in the original dataset, such as culture factor of the migration population, may moderate the apparent associations between altitude measures and psychiatric symptoms.

Conclusion

In summary, this study investigated the relationship between intolerance of uncertainty (IU) and depressive symptoms among Chinese high-altitude migrants, as well as the mediating role of mindfulness. Results indicated that both prospective and inhibitory IU were positively correlated with depressive symptoms. Notably, inhibitory IU impacted depressive symptoms both directly and indirectly through mindfulness, which accounting for 48.30% of the total effect. However, the indirect effect of prospective IU via mindfulness was not significant. These findings highlight the unique roles of IU subdimensions in depression and the protective effect of mindfulness on the link between inhibitory IU and depressive symptoms. The study enhances our understanding of IU’s influence on high-altitude migrants’ mental health and backs mindfulness-based interventions for curbing their depression.
