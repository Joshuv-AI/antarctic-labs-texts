Atrial overdrive pacing in a short RP tachycardia. Understanding the mechanism behind the AHHA response
Authors: Javaid Ahmad Dar, Hariharan Narasaiyan, Anand Manickavasagam, John Roshan Jacob
Published: 2025

Clinical scenario

A 30-year-old woman with a structurally normal heart presented with recurrent episodes of palpitations. Her ECG showed a short RP tachycardia which reverted with adenosine. During the EP study, a regular narrow complex tachycardia was induced on atrial extra stimuli with a VA interval of −10 ms. The earliest A was seen in the proximal coronary sinus. There was some wobble in the tachycardia [430–550 ms], which later stabilized at a cycle length of 430 ms.

The differential diagnosis included typical slow fast AV nodal reentrant tachycardia [AVNRT], atrial tachycardia [AT] with long PR interval and non-re-entrant junctional tachycardia. The variations in cycle length prompted us to consider the possibility of an automatic mechanism, particularly non-reentrant junctional tachycardia.

Final treatment/ablation offered

In the baseline EP study, the patient had two distinctive AH jumps suggestive of more than two AV nodal pathways. During the tachycardia and ventricular pacing, the earliest A is in the proximal CS with a short HA interval [orange line in Fig. 3]. Unlike the HA interval during tachycardia, the retrograde atrial activation sequence is not a reliable criterion to classify AVNRT subtypes [2]. The HA interval being less than 70 ms suggests typical AVNRT. Further, the tachycardia was induced by an atrial extra stimulus with an AH jump, with a diagnosis of AVNRT further supported by the response to ventricular overdrive pacing (Fig. 3). The slow pathway – right inferior extension was ablated, after which there was no inducible tachycardia despite vigorous stimulation. The patient is doing well after six months of ablation without any palpitations.

Shows entrainment of the tachycardia from the ventricle with VAHV response. Also to be noted is the long difference between the tachycardia cycle length and the post-pacing interval as in AVNRT.

In retrospect how do we explain the apparent AHHA response depicted in Fig. 1?

Atrial overdrive pacing was done from the proximal coronary sinus which ostensibly reveals an AHHA response on cessation of pacing. This suggests the arrhythmia could be a junctional tachycardia.

Explanations

On a cursory look, Fig. 1 may be interpreted as a pseudo-AHHA response with the last atrial paced beat being conducted to the second His after it. Pseudo AHHA response is the most common response on atrial pacing in AVNRT with short VA interval. Fan et al. [3] in their study found that all their AVNRT patients had a pseudo-AHHA response on entrainment from atrial overdrive pacing. To determine the response to atrial pacing the last entrained His is determined by measuring the last His signal that is driven by the pacing cycle length. In our case, it turns out to be the His that immediately follows the last atrial-paced beat. So, technically the response after entraining from the atrium is a true AHHA response suggestive of junctional tachycardia. However, one thing that is very unusual here is that the AH interval on atrial pacing during entrainment is shorter than that during the tachycardia (Fig. 1). In typical AVNRT the AH interval on atrial pacing while entraining the tachycardia is usually longer than or equal to the AH interval during tachycardia; but not shorter. So as per the atrial overdrive pacing manoeuvre, the diagnosis here would suggest junctional tachycardia.

A shows, a late APC which is septal refractory, advances the next His by 15 ms and resets the tachycardia. This response is very specific for AVNRT as demonstrated by Padalinam et al. thereby confirming AVNRT [1]. Fig. 2B shows an earlier APC terminates the tachycardia which is a non-specific response and can be seen in both AVNRT and JT.

To explain this odd response in a proven AVNRT, it was postulated that while attempting to entrain the tachycardia the paced A started to go down the fast pathway. The last paced A went down both a fast and slow pathway – a 1 for 2 response (Fig. 4) followed by resumption of typical AVNRT [4]. Another possibility is that the tachycardia terminated on pacing and was promptly reinitiated with a junctional ectopic that happened on cessation of pacing. Since this response was consistently reproducible, we postulate that the first mechanism is most likely. This case again highlights the fallacy of the Atrial overdrive pacing maneuver to differentiate AVNRT from JT [5], deemed 100 % sensitive and specific in the original studies that described it.

Showing the 1 for 2 response on cessation of atrial overdrive pacing with a ladder diagram.

Learning Points: • The AHHA response on atrial pacing can't always differentiate junctional tachycardia from AVNRT. • AVNRT can give rise to AHHA response on atrial entrainment due to a one for two antegrade conduction.

Editorial commentary (Yash Lokhandwala)

There are many maneuvers described to weed out the precise mechanism of a short RP tachycardia. Doing all these maneuvers during a case is tedious and time consuming. More importantly, we may get conflicting results, leading to more confusion than clarification. This is because most maneuvers have limited specificity and several pitfalls. Hence most of us rely heavily on more reproducible and easily perfomed maneuvers, the pillars being the response to His-refractory ventricular extrastimulus and ventricular overdrive pacing.

In this case, Roshan and colleagues have provided a nice nugget highlighting one of these traps and how to unravel it. While an ‘A on V’ tachycardia would be typical AVNRT in over 90 % of cases, sometimes an atrial tachycardia (especially perinodal), an automatic junctional tachycardia or a concealed nodoventricular pathway (very rare) could produce this pattern.

A late atrial extrastimulus is conventionally understood to come at least 50 % later than the AA interval. The response to this can be sometimes misleading, especially if there is spontaneous wobble in AH intervals (not uncommon in AVNRT). Careful analysis of the time to the following His, as shown elegantly here, can clinch the diagnosis if reproducible.

2:1 AV conduction is again underrecognised at the initiation of AVNRT by atrial extrastimuli. A similar phenomenon could occur during atrial overdive of AVNRT, though engagement of the fast pathway antegradely would be unusual and expected to lead to tachycardia termination.
