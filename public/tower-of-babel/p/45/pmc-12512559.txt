Mapping the use of, perception, and knowledge about e-cigarettes among dental students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Isabella Macedo Costa e Silva, Juliana Amorim dos Santos, Bruna Bastos Silveira, Larissa Di Carvalho Melo, Vitória Tavares Castro, Elaine Barros Ferreira, Eliete Neves Silva Guerra, Paula Elaine Diniz dos Reis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe consumption of e-cigarettes is increasing steadily among university students. This might be due to its greater social acceptance related to its contemporary look and variety of flavors. In this context, the objective of this study is to map the use and identify the perceptions and knowledge of the effects of e-cigarette use among dental students at the University of Brasília, Brazil.MethodsA questionnaire on use, perceptions, and knowledge about electronic cigarettes was anonymously distributed to 303 dental students at the University of Brasília, Brazil. Of these students, 184 (60.72%) answered the questionnaire. Descriptive and analytical statistics were performed using proportions and mean values, and the chi-square test was applied to identify significant differences between quantitative variables. To investigate factors associated with the use of these devices and participants' confidence in answering questions about e-cigarettes, two binary logistic regression analyses were performed.ResultsMost participants knew about e-cigarettes but had never used them (63.60%), and 46.70% of the students received some information about the effects of e-cigarettes during undergraduate education. Background

Electronic nicotine delivery systems, such as e-cigarettes, are portable and battery-powered devices designed to heat and vaporize e-liquid solutions, emulating the experience of smoking conventional cigarettes [1, 2]. Recently, the use of e-cigarettes has been steadily increasing due to their contemporary appearance, perceived reduced harm, and the greater social satisfaction they provide compared to traditional cigarettes. Additionally, these devices offer a variety of flavors, pleasant odors, and appealing designs, which attract considerable attention and curiosity [2–5].

Regarding epidemiological data on e-cigarettes, analysis of data from the Global Adult Tobacco Survey (GATS) from 14 countries demonstrated a significantly higher prevalence of e-cigarette use among urban populations, with young adult males having the highest rates of use [6]. Further studies examining the sociodemographic characteristics of e-cigarette users in the United States of America (USA), Europe, and globally [7–9] also corroborate this trend, indicating a higher prevalence of e-cigarette use among men. Although limited, existing studies suggest that vaping is common in universities and that the use of electronic devices has been on the rise among university students [9, 10].

The popularity of e-cigarettes has emerged especially among young people. The transition to university represents a period of heightened vulnerability to substance use [10, 11]. For many young individuals, entering a university institution is seen as an achievement of autonomy, accompanied by the pressures of self-care and responsibilities to their future. The academic environment provides unique opportunities, such as learning, socializing, building new principles, and sharing experiences – including the use of e-cigarettes [3, 12]. Moreover, factors such as anxiety, the need for social acceptance, stress, and the exhaustive academic workload are associated with e-cigarette use among university students [13, 14].

For students in health-related fields, who will play a fundamental role in educating patients about e-cigarettes, their education becomes a complex and dual-edged context. Despite this, there is a noticeable gap in the knowledge of dental students regarding the risks and effects associated with electronic devices. Therefore, this study aimed to map the usage patterns and identify the perceptions and knowledge of dental students regarding e-cigarettes.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study was carried out to map the use, perceptions, and knowledge of e-cigarettes among students in the dentistry program at the University of Brasília.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Health Sciences of the University of Brasília, Brazil (CAAE n 65,155,122.9.0000.0030). All procedures were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants; completion and submission of the online questionnaire implied consent to participate.

Sample

The dentistry program at the University of Brasilia consists of 10 semesters, with 30 new students each semester. During the period the data was collected, the program had a total of 303 active students. Thus, the questionnaire was distributed to all students from the first to the tenth semester via email and social media. The participants were strongly encouraged to complete the questionnaire; however, their participation was voluntary.

Questionnaire

The research instrument used was a controlled digital monitoring tool self-administered and developed by a researcher in Portuguese. The questionnaire was generated in the Google Forms application (Google Search, Melon Park, USA). The questions were prepared and revised together with the research team. Although a pilot study was not conducted, the questionnaire underwent several revisions by the research team before being finalized and distributed. The form was disseminated through an electronic address in the classes of the institution’s dentistry program. The data were collected between December 2022 and February 2023.

The questionnaire was developed based on a review conducted by the Brazilian National Cancer Institute (INCA), which aimed to create an instrument capable of measuring the use, knowledge, and types of e-cigarettes [15]. Additionally, the research by Canzan et al. [16] and Kelsh et al. [14] provided a theoretical framework for questions related to students' perceptions of these devices and their potential health risks. To ensure the adequacy of the language and up-to-date information on consumption trends, websites specializing in the sale of e-cigarettes were consulted, allowing the collection of relevant information on terminology, common usage practices, and flavor variety [17–19].

The first stage of the form was used for the participants to obtain knowledge about the research objective and to agree with the terms proposed by the consent term. If the participant did not agree with the consent form, the data were not collected and were therefore automatically deleted. The other steps were structured into four sections. The first section aimed to quantify the use of e-cigarettes among students, including the frequency with which these devices were used. The second section delved into participants’ perceptions, exploring factors such as attractiveness, social influence, and situations that may lead to the use of e-cigarettes, as well as investigating their perceptions of potential adverse health effects. The third section assessed students’ knowledge of risk factors associated with the use of these devices, their potential harmful health effects, and their ability to discuss the impacts of using these devices. At the end of the questionnaire, in the fourth section, participants were asked to provide information about their individual and sociodemographic characteristics, such as age, gender, family income and semester of undergraduation. The questionnaire contained a total of 20 questions (Appendix 1).

Statistical analyses

Descriptive and analytical statistics were performed using proportions and mean values. For statistical analysis, the responses on the Likert scale, marked as strongly agree and agree in questions of agreement or 4 and 5 in questions of frequency, were grouped and analyzed as agreement proportions. As the data presented nonparametric distributions, the chi-square test was applied to identify significant differences between quantitative variables using the software GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, California). The level of significance was set to p < 0.05.

To investigate factors associated with the use of these devices and participants' confidence in answering questions about e-cigarettes, two binary logistic regression analyses using Jamovi 2.3.28 software were performed. The variables tested across both models were use of e-cigarettes (present/absent), sex (male/female), age (in years), presence of information on e-cigarettes during undergraduation (yes/no), academic semester of the participant in dentistry course, family income, and participants' confidence in answering questions about e-cigarettes.

For the initial models, variables with a minimum association (p < 0.2) in the univariate analysis were included to avoid the premature exclusion of potential confounders or relevant predictors, as recommended by Bursac et al. (2008) using the purposeful selection of variables approach [20]. In the final models, only variables that showed statistical significance (p < 0.05) were retained. To assess the factors associated with use of e-cigarettes by the participants, we considered the dependent variable the use of e-cigarettes (categorized as "Present" or "Absent"). In the initial model, the independent variables tested were family income, academic semester, age, and participants' confidence in answering questions about e-cigarettes. To assess the factors associated with participants' confidence in answering questions about e-cigarettes, the dependent variable was confidence (categorized as "Present" or "Absent"). The independent variables tested were the use of e-cigarettes (presence/absent), sex (male/female), presence of information on e-cigarettes during undergraduation (yes/no), and academic semester of the participant in dentistry. For both models, final variable selection was based on statistical significance and absence of multicollinearity between the variables, assessed through the variance inflation factor (VIF).

Use of e-cigarettes

Among the 184 participants, 63.60% (n = 117) knew about e-cigarettes but had never used them, 35.30% (n = 65) knew about e-cigarettes and had already used the device, and 1.10% (n = 2) had never heard about e-cigarettes. Furthermore, 46.70% (n = 86) of the students received some information about the effects of e-cigarettes during undergraduation.

Moreover, 84.78% were nonsmokers (n = 156), 4.34% (n = 8) used conventional cigarettes and e-cigarettes, just one participant consumed only conventional cigarettes, and 10.32% (n = 19) only used e-cigarettes (Fig. 1).

Types of smoking consumption

Regarding the frequency of e-cigarette use, 73.36% (n = 135) never used it, 20.65% (n = 38) used it rarely, 3.26% (n = 6) used it monthly, 2.71% (n = 4) used it weekly, and 0.54% (n = 1) used it daily. Furthermore, 7.60% (n = 14) of the female participants used e-cigarettes, while only 2.71% (n = 5) of the male respondents used the device. Regarding use and income, only two participants who earned less than one minimum wage used e-cigarettes, while 9.23% (n = 17) of participants who earned more than one minimum wage used e-cigarettes. In this context, there were no statistically significant differences in sex (p = 0.9083) according to the use of e-cigarettes.

Among individuals who used or had used e-cigarettes, 4.89% (n = 9) also used or tried to use the device to inhale another substance. Most of those respondents (n = 7) reported the use of marijuana, while only one user declared that they did not remember which substance was used.

Concerning the factors associated with the use of e-cigarettes, the logistic regression analysis for e-cigarettes use was statistically significant (X2 = 6.14, p = 0.013), with Nagelkerke's pseudo-R-squared (RN2 = 0.0572), indicating that 5.7% of the variability in e-cigarettes use was explained by the model. Among the variables tested, only family income showed a significant association with e-cigarettes use (p = 0.009). The Odds Ratio (OR = 4.0; 95% CI = [1.416, 11.30]) suggests that participants with one monthly minimum wage or less family income were 4 times more likely to report electronic cigarette use than those with more than one minimum wage. Multicollinearity analysis indicated a VIF of 1.0 for the included variable, suggesting no collinearity issues (Table 2). The univariate regression results for all covariates, including those that were not statistically significant, as well as the initial multivariable model with the non-significant covariates, are provided in Appendix 2. A 2 × 2 table considering the use of e-cigarettes and family income is provided in Appendix 3.

Perceptions about e-cigarettes

Participants were asked to report factors that may have influenced their use of e-cigarettes. The participants were also asked to report in which situations they used the device, which factors they considered attractive in the e-cigarette and their knowledge about the adverse health effects resulting from the use of e-cigarettes. The participants could have chosen whatever applied to their situation from a list of reasons given to them in the questionnaire.

Among participants who used e-cigarettes (n = 19), 73.38% reported that the curiosity factor influenced them, while 63.15% reported the friend factor. In addition, other factors that influenced the use of e-cigarettes included pleasant odors, variety of flavors, mental health disorders, partners, family members, and different designs (Fig. 2).

Perceptions about e-cigarettes

Furthermore, 74.45% (n = 137) were nonusers, while 25.54% stated that they used the device at the company of friends. Moreover, anxiety and exhaustive academic load had the same response rate (5.43%; n = 10) for both. Other situations of use regarding e-cigarettes, such as in the company of partners (3.80%; n = 7), stress (3.26%; n = 6), and in the company of family members (2.71%; n = 5), have also been reported.

Discussion

This study aims to assess the perceptions and knowledge related to e-cigarette use among dental students from one of Brazil’s top dental schools. Given that dental students in health-related fields will play a key role in educating patients about e-cigarettes, understanding their usage patterns, perceptions, and level of knowledge is essential for informing future interventions in undergraduate dental education.

In this sample, 35.30% (n = 65) of dental students were familiar with e-cigarettes and had previously used them. The most reported reasons for use were curiosity and peer influence. While most participants acknowledged that the components of e-cigarette aerosols are harmful to health and potentially carcinogenic, 23.36% reported not feeling confident in their ability to discuss the effects of e-cigarette use with patients, friends, or family members. Among users, 71.2% were female. In general, the literature indicates a higher prevalence of e-cigarette use among males [6, 16, 21]. However, contrary to our initial hypothesis, no significant association between e-cigarette use and sex was observed in this study. This finding may be influenced by the predominance of female participants in the sample. Previous survey with 3,516 respondents from Australia, Canada, England, and the USA suggest that women tend to be more health-conscious, which may contribute to lower rates of e-cigarette use due to greater awareness of their potential adverse health effects [22].

Among those who reported using e-cigarettes, 10.32% used only e-cigarettes. This rate is approximately 20 times higher than that of exclusive conventional cigarette use (0.54%). Despite the global efforts led by the WHO to reduce smoking-related harms, including policies aimed at prevention, a decrease in the use of nicotine-based alternatives would be expected among the current generation [23]. However, the appealing features of e-cigarettes—such as their pleasant scent, variety of designs, and wide range of flavors—have contributed to their greater social acceptance and, consequently, to increased use among young people [5]. In our study, curiosity and peer influence were the most reported reasons for use. Nevertheless, despite social acceptance, there remains limited knowledge regarding the specific chemical composition of each e-cigarette brand or device generation. This lack of transparency, along with challenges in accurately assessing toxicity, makes it difficult to determine the true long-term health effects of e-cigarette use [24–26].

It is also important to highlight that e-cigarettes can be used to deliver substances other than nicotine. Flavorings in e-liquids are a key factor in attracting youth to e-cigarette use, yet the inhalation of certain flavoring agents poses significant health risks [27, 28]. At least 65 individual flavoring compounds found in e-liquids or e-cigarette aerosols have been shown to cause toxicity in the respiratory, cardiovascular, circulatory, skeletal, and integumentary systems. Among them, cinnamaldehyde is the most frequently reported cytotoxic compound, followed by vanillin, menthol, ethyl maltol, ethyl vanillin, benzaldehyde, and linalool. Moreover, modern e-cigarettes can be modified to aerosolize cannabis, either as dried plant material or in concentrated extract form [27]. In our study, 3.80% of participants reported using e-cigarettes for marijuana consumption. Although recent literature has identified e-cigarettes as a method for cannabis use, there is a notable lack of data regarding the safety and health implications of this mode of inhalation [25, 26]. Consequently, users may be exposed to harmful substances without fully understanding the associated risks, particularly when vaping cannabis [29, 30]. Additionally, studies suggest that e-cigarette use may serve as a gateway to the initiation of other substance use [27].

Although the attractive factors of e-cigarettes are worrisome, the behavioral factors among the young population analyzed also stand out. The need to belong to the environment influences the use of e-cigarettes. In this study, the friend factor (26.63%) was reported as the main influence of the use of e-cigarettes. In this way, social influence, in addition to being able to shape behavior, plays an important complex role in the decisions that people make about e-cigarettes [31]. With this, the identification of perceptions and influencing factors among university students can be informative for the development of targeted interventions [14, 20].

The positive association between knowledge about the development of cancer and oral impairments in users exposed to e-cigarettes may be attributed to advertisements related to nicotine, which are well known by the population. This may also be related to the content of the lectures from the School of Dentistry.

Furthermore, participants with a family income of one minimum wage or less were four times more likely to report e-cigarette use compared to those with higher incomes. This trend mirrors patterns observed in conventional cigarette use. For instance, Jeon et al. [32] identified a growing disparity in conventional smoking rates between low- and high-income groups, with recent cohorts showing more substantial declines in smoking prevalence among individuals with higher incomes. Similarly, Poonawalla et al. [33] found that lower childhood socioeconomic status was associated with increased smoking during adolescence, suggesting that early-life economic disadvantage may contribute to tobacco use later in life. However, contrasting evidence is presented by Perelman et al. [34], who reported that adolescents from European cities with higher personal income were more likely to smoke conventional cigarettes. This divergence may be attributed to geographical and cultural differences. While both Poonawalla et al. and Jeon et al. focused on U.S. populations—where tobacco use is more prevalent among lower-income individuals—a similar pattern was observed in our Brazilian sample for e-cigarette use. In many countries across the Americas, cultural norms and targeted marketing have historically associated tobacco consumption with lower socioeconomic groups, reinforcing higher usage rates in these populations. In contrast, in certain European contexts, smoking may be more socially accepted among wealthier adolescents who possess greater financial autonomy. These regional disparities underscore the complexity of the relationship between income and tobacco or e-cigarette use, highlighting the need for further research to explore how socioeconomic factors influence substance use across different cultural and economic settings.

Although many students are not current users of e-cigarettes, their role as future health professionals' places them in a unique position to influence public awareness and behavior. However, while a portion of students reported receiving some information about the effects of e-cigarettes during their undergraduate education, a significant number still expressed uncertainty or lack of confidence in discussing these effects with patients, friends, or family members. These findings underscore the need to develop and implement targeted educational strategies within health-related undergraduate programs that comprehensively address e-cigarette use. The present findings underscore the importance of strengthening the training of future health professionals to support effective health promotion grounded in evidence-based practices.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, despite the internal validity of the preassessment, the reliability of the questionnaire was not measured by Cronbach's alpha, and it cannot be stated that there is external validity. Additionally, as with many studies relying on specific questionnaires, the subjective interpretations of the participants may have influenced the results, potentially compromising their accuracy and generalizability. Furthermore, the inclusion of questions about complex social subjects, such as smoking and substance use, could have caused discomfort for participants, leading them to alter their responses or avoid disclosing their true behaviors and thoughts.

Moreover, the study's sample homogeneity, which primarily consisted of students from one institution, may limit the generalizability of the findings. The experiences and perceptions of dental students at other universities or in different regions may differ, as may those from various ethnic backgrounds. Additionally, the study achieved a response rate of approximately 60%, meaning that not all members of the target group of dental students participated, potentially leading to a biased representation of the population. Consequently, the findings of this study may not fully represent the broader demographic of dental students in Brazil.

Despite these limitations, this research is innovative and provides information on the use of e-cigarettes among dental students, which has rarely been explored in the literature, regardless of the wide use of the device among the young population.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides valuable insights into the knowledge, perceptions, and behaviors related to e-cigarette use among dental students at a university in Brazil. Psychosocial influences emerged as key determinants of experimentation, while formal education increased awareness of health risks but did not sufficiently cultivate confidence in clinical communication. These gaps in both theoretical knowledge and practical counseling skills highlight vulnerabilities in current dental curricula. In this context, given the uncertain long-term risks and health effects of e-cigarette use, it is necessary to implement educational strategies in health courses to promote students as health educators. To improve the generalizability of these results, more studies are needed to establish whether these findings are similar among larger samples of dental students and other healthcare students from other universities.
