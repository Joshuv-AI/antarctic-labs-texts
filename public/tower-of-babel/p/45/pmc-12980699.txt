Propolis‐Based Hybrid Nanoflowers as Multifunctional Nanozymes: Structural Integration of Polyphenols for Enhanced Catalytic and Antioxidant Activity
Authors: Cevahir Altinkaynak, Fatih Kirmizikar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPropolis is a polyphenol‐rich natural resin with well‐documented antioxidant activity; however, its practical application is restricted by poor aqueous solubility and limited stability. Herein, propolis‐based organic–inorganic hybrid nanoflowers (Prp‐Nfs) were synthesized by integrating a propolis extract into a Cu3(PO4)2·3H2O matrix under mild aqueous conditions (0.8 mM Cu2 +, pH 7.4, 25°C, 72 h). SEM and laser diffraction analyses confirmed the formation of uniform spherical Prp‐Nfs with a relatively narrow size distribution. EDX, FTIR, and XRD analyses verified the formation of well‐integrated organic‐inorganic hybrid architecture arising from interactions between copper ions and propolis‐derived functional groups. Functionally, Prp‐Nfs exhibited markedly enhanced peroxidase‐like activity, reaching 2.76 EU mg− 1 at pH 7.4 and 40°C, while retaining 58.1% of their initial activity after five reuse cycles. Despite a lower total phenolic content, Prp‐Nfs demonstrated superior DPPH radical scavenging activity (94.4%) together with a substantial ABTS antioxidant capacity (475.6 µmol Trolox g− 1). Overall, these findings identify Prp‐Nfs as a propolis‐based hybrid nanozyme platform that combines peroxidase‐like catalytic activity with enhanced antioxidant functionality.

Introduction

Natural products have been used for therapeutic purposes throughout history and continue to hold significance as potential pharmaceutical agents in alternative and modern medicine [1]. Propolis, which is among these products, is a resinous bee product used as a type of cementing agent to insulate and protect beehives against pathogens [2]. Honey bees produce propolis by processing the resins they collect from different parts of green plants and trees, such as young buds, leaves and trunks, with enzymes secreted from their glands [3]. The chemical composition of propolis varies depending on plant sources, geographical origin, season and climatic conditions, and is typically dominated by plant resin (50%), wax (30%), pollen (5%), and essential oils (10%). The remaining 5% contains various chemical classes such as vitamins, macro and micro elements, phenolic acids such as cinnamic and caffeic acid, a wide variety of flavonoid groups, aromatic aldehydes, terpenes, lignans, β‐steroids and their prenylated derivatives [4, 5, 6, 7].

In recent years, several detailed LC–MS/MS‐based phytochemical studies have provided a more comprehensive picture of the phenolic fingerprint of Turkish and Anatolian propolis. Özdal et al. identified 32 phenolic compounds in Turkish propolis samples from different regions, reporting caffeic acid, p‐coumaric acid, ferulic acid, pinobanksin, chrysin, pinocembrin and galangin as predominant constituents and linking this rich polyphenol profile to high antioxidant capacity and improved in vitro bioaccessibility [8]. Sorucu and co‐workers further showed, using LC–MS/MS analysis of 174 propolis samples collected at different altitudes and seasons in the Marmara region, that the relative abundance of nine flavonoids and seven phenolic acids varies markedly with environmental factors, underscoring the strong geo‐botanical dependence of the Turkish propolis metabolome [9].

Complementary LC–MS/MS studies focusing on standardization have confirmed that this complex phenolic matrix is a consistent hallmark of Turkish propolis. Özkök et al. quantified major phenolic acids and flavonoids in a large set of samples and proposed baseline ranges for key markers such as caffeic acid, ferulic acid, CAPE, pinocembrin and chrysin as a basis for the basic standardization of Turkish propolis [10]. More recently, İzol et al. performed in‐depth LC–MS/MS profiling of an Anatolian propolis sample, together with mineral analysis and bioactivity assays, and confirmed that flavonoids can account for more than a quarter of the total extractable components, again highlighting the dominance of polyphenols in the chemical identity of Anatolian propolis [11].

Propolis has strong antioxidant [12], anti‐proliferative [13], antimicrobial [14], anti‐biofilm [15] antiulcer [16], antifungal [17], antiviral [18, 19], antitumor [20], immunomodulation [21], anti‐inflammation [22], and anti‐aging [23] activities proven by in vivo and in vitro experiments. Its antioxidant effect occurs through different mechanisms such as the interaction of phenolic compounds with free radicals, metal ion chelation and enzymatic activity modulation which result in prevention of possible tissue and DNA damage by reducing oxidative stress [24]. The fact that propolis contains many active components with strong antioxidant effects has also highlighted its tumor growth inhibitory effect. Especially flavonoids, polyphenolic compounds, and caffeic acid phenethyl ester (CAPE) are the main substances responsible for the antioxidant activity of propolis [8, 25]. Region‐specific LC–MS/MS studies have further refined this picture by correlating detailed phenolic profiles with functional properties of Anatolian propolis. Akman et al. demonstrated that Pülümür propolis from Eastern Anatolia is particularly enriched in flavonoids and phenolic acids with potent antioxidant and antidiabetic activities [26]. Karagecili and co‐workers performed comprehensive metabolite profiling of Berdav propolis using a panel of 53 phenolic standards and showed that subtle shifts in the relative levels of chrysin, pinobanksin, apigenin and other flavonoids can translate into significant differences in antioxidant, anticholinergic and antidiabetic effects [27]. Similarly, Aras et al. compared propolis extracts obtained with different solvents from three Turkish cities and reported that ethanol and methanol extracts, which contained higher concentrations of LC–MS/MS‐identified phenolics, exhibited superior antioxidant and antimicrobial activities [28].

In addition, thanks to the enzymes propolis contains, it protects the cell membrane from damage caused by oxidative stress, such as cellular aging, cardiovascular, and neurological diseases [24]. With its antifungal effect, it is also considered a potential alternative for the treatment of infections caused by pathogenic yeasts such as Candida sp. [17]. However, the limited oral bioavailability, rapid metabolism in the body, instability and poor water solubility of propolis and its bioactive components restrict its widespread use [29]. Nanotechnological applications have great potential to increase the bioavailability of natural products, improve their stability and offer targeted therapeutic opportunities. In this context, organic‐inorganic hybrid nanostructures (nanoflowers) offer a wide range of applications from biosensors to drug delivery systems [30]. Conducted studies have led to the recognition of nanoflowers as a novel method for enzyme immobilization [31, 32].

For the first time in 2012, organic‐inorganic hybrid nanostructures were synthesized by accident and the activities, stabilities and usage areas of enzymes were increased [33]. In the reported method, the formation and mechanism of organic‐inorganic hybrid nanostructures with flower‐like shapes consisting of nano leaves were explained by using Cu2+ ion as inorganic component and various proteins as organic components. When compared to traditional immobilization techniques, the enzymatic activity and stability of enzyme‐inorganic hybrid nanostructures are significantly increased compared to free enzymes and enzymes applied with other immobilization techniques [34, 35]. The possible reasons for the increase in the activities of the synthesized organic‐inorganic hybrid structures are the high surface area of the formed structures, which does not have a significant mass transfer limitation, the cooperative effect of the enzyme molecules trapped at the nanoscale and increased stability of the structure because of strong coordination bonds between organic and inorganic components [36, 37].

There are a few studies on propolis‐derived nanostructures in the literature. Some of these, Sangboonruang et al. developed a niosome‐based drug delivery platform for ethanolic extract of propolis (EEP) using thin‐film hydration method with Ag85A aptamer surface modification (Apt‐PEGNio/EEP) to target Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb). The physicochemical characterization of PEGNio/EEP demonstrated approximately −20 mV zeta potential, spherical nanoparticles of 180 nm, 80% entrapment efficiency, and a sustained release profile. No significant differences were observed between Apt‐PEGNio/EEP and PEGNio/EEP in these properties. These findings indicated that Apt‐PEGNio/EEP functions as an antimycobacterial nanoparticle and represents a promising, innovative, and targeted treatment approach [38]. In another study, Amin et al. aimed to prepare and characterize nanocapsules of Qaluiobia propolis (PQG), extracted at 50°C using water, ethanol, and supercritical fluid carbon dioxide, assisted by co‐solvents. The bioavailability of the propolis was evaluated. This study provided an effective method for extracting bioactive compounds from propolis and demonstrated that nanoencapsulation enhanced its biological properties and activities. Moreover, it broadened the scope for its use as a natural antioxidant and anticancer agent and explored its application as a functional ingredient in baked products [39]. In a separate study by Tatli Seven et al. polymeric nanoparticles containing propolis were synthesized using a green sonication method and characterized by Fourier‐transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, Brunauer‐Emmett‐Teller (BET) surface area analysis, and wet scanning transmission electron microscopy techniques. A total of 56 rats were divided into seven groups: control, CP, propolis, NP‐10, CP + propolis, CP + NP‐10, and CP + NP‐30. Propolis (100 mg/kg), NP‐10 (10 mg/kg), and NP‐30 (30 mg/kg) treatments were administered orally by gavage for 21 days, while CP (3 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally as a single dose. Following the experiment, oxidative stress parameters in testicular tissues—malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione (GSH), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), and catalase (CAT)—as well as apoptotic pathways involving B‐cell lymphoma 2 protein (Bcl‐2) and Bcl‐2‐associated X protein (Bax), were measured. Furthermore, sperm quality and organ weights of the testis, epididymis, right cauda epididymis, seminal vesicles, and prostate were assessed. The results confirmed that both propolis and nano‐propolis treatments reduced the adverse effects of cisplatin on spermatogenic activity, antioxidant status, and apoptosis in rats [40]. Elbaz et al. proposed a promising approach to enhance the oral delivery, water solubility, and bioavailability of propolis, as well as to improve its anticancer activity and enable controlled release. Standardized propolis was formulated into optimized solid dispersion (SD) matrices to enhance solubility. Its release profile was controlled through incorporation into nanoparticles (NPs), followed by further embedding into chitosan (Cs) microparticles. The newly developed propolis‐loaded nano‐in‐microparticles (NIMs) were evaluated for anticancer activity against human liver cancer (HepG2) and colorectal cancer (HCT‐116) cells. The prepared SDs, NPs, and NIMs were characterized using SEM, TEM, DLS, FTIR, DSC, and UV–vis spectrophotometry. The therapeutic efficacy of the formulated propolis was biologically assessed through cytotoxicity measurements, mitochondrial dysfunction analysis, apoptosis‐induced cell death, and cell cycle arrest. The results demonstrated a significant improvement in propolis solubility with a controlled release profile across different GIT environments. In vitro cytotoxicity studies revealed that propolis‐loaded NIMs exhibited greater cytotoxicity on HepG2 cells than on HCT‐116 cells and mediated threefold higher therapeutic efficacy compared to free propolis. Apoptosis assays showed that the NIMs induced apoptosis in HepG2 cells and significantly reduced cell numbers in proliferative G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases [41]. Due to the growing impact of nanotechnology in medicine and the advantageous role of nanoparticles in treatment, Kazemi et al. developed nano‐propolis particles (PNPs) [42]. The anti‐glycation effects of PNPs at various concentrations were investigated on human hemoglobin (Hb) glycation and fructation using AGE‐specific fluorescence, AGE‐specific absorbance, and circular dichroism (CD) methods, and compared to aspirin as a widely used anti‐glycation agent. The findings demonstrated that PNPs, as a natural product, have potential as an effective drug in diabetes treatment. Silvio de Almeida et al. emphasized the need for further research on propolis in nanotechnology‐integrated studies for bioprocess applications [43]. Fitria et al. successfully optimized, characterized, and evaluated the stability of a propolis extract (PE)‐loaded self‐nanoemulsifying drug delivery system (SNEDDS) using a D‐optimal mixture design. The optimal SNEDDS formulation (Capryol‐90:Cremophor RH40:PEG 400 in 30:34:36) demonstrated excellent physicochemical properties (e.g., droplet size of 28.7 µm vs. 1.20 µm) and high stability in various tests. Most importantly, the PE‐loaded SNEDDS exhibited superior in vitro and in vivo immunostimulatory activity compared to the unformulated extract, confirming its potential to enhance the immune response [44]. Although several hybrid nanoformulations—such as chitosan–Arabic gum structures entrapping propolis—have demonstrated improved stability and controlled release properties, the integration of protein–metal phosphate hybrid nanoflowers as carriers for phenolic‐rich natural extracts remains largely unexplored [45].

We hypothesized that the phenolic‐ and flavonoid‐rich matrix of propolis would coordinate with Cu2 + ions to form a stable hybrid architecture, and that this nanoflower structure would significantly enhance both peroxidase‐like catalytic activity and radical scavenging capacity compared to free propolis. To test this hypothesis, propolis–Cu2 + hybrid nanoflowers (Prp‐Nfs) were synthesized for the first time using propolis—a natural product with a rich biomolecular composition obtained from the Köybağı region of Avanos, Nevşehir, Türkiye. The nanoflowers were structurally characterized, and their catalytic performance was evaluated under various conditions. Their reusability and antioxidant capacity were further assessed to determine their potential for biomedical applications. Accordingly, the objectives of this study are to (i) describe the morphological and chemical properties of Prp‐Nfs, (ii) determine their peroxidase‐like activities under different environmental conditions, (iii) evaluate their reusability, and (iv) elucidate the functional contribution of the nanoflower architecture through comparative antioxidant analyses with free propolis.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of Propolis Extract

The UV–vis spectrum of the raw propolis extract exhibited a characteristic absorption profile of a phenolic‐ and flavonoid‐rich matrix (Figure 1a). A pronounced absorption maximum was observed at approximately 294–295 nm, which can be attributed to π→π transitions of aromatic rings bearing phenolic ─OH groups and is consistent with the presence of hydroxycinnamic acid derivatives such as caffeic, p‐coumaric, and ferulic acids. In addition, a distinct absorption band centered around 329–330 nm was detected, corresponding to Band I of flavones/flavonols and arising from π→π* and n→π* transitions within the conjugated cinnamoyl system of the benzopyran chromophore. A high absorbance in these regions indicates a substantial contribution of phenolic acids and flavonoids to the overall chromophoric system of the extract. This spectral fingerprint is consistent with previously reported UV–vis profiles of Anatolian propolis samples exhibiting high total phenolic content and pronounced antioxidant capacity [46].

(a) UV–vis spectra. (b) FTIR spectrum of propolis extract.

The FTIR spectrum further supports this interpretation by revealing complementary functional groups associated with these same classes of compounds (Figure 1b). The broad O─H stretching band at 3300–3400 cm− 1 reflects extensive hydroxylation typical of phenolic structures, while the aliphatic C─H vibrations at 2920–2850 cm− 1 indicate the presence of wax‐derived long‐chain hydrocarbons and fatty acids. The strong C═O stretching band in the 1710–1650 cm− 1 region corresponds to both the carboxyl groups of phenolic acids and the conjugated carbonyl structures of flavonoids. In addition, the aromatic C═C stretching vibrations between 1600–1500 cm− 1, together with the C─O and C─O─C stretching bands in the 1250–1150 cm− 1 and 1050–1000 cm− 1 regions, confirm the abundance of phenolic esters and flavonoid derivatives in the extract. The out‐of‐plane aromatic C─H bending signals at 850–750 cm− 1 further support the typical aromatic backbone of propolis. Overall, the functional groups identified in the spectrum are fully consistent with the high phenolic content, pronounced aromatic character, and strong antioxidant potential previously reported for Anatolian propolis; this interpretation aligns well with the chemical and biological profile described by Baştürk and Yavaş (2025) [46].

Morphology and Characterization of Prp‐Nfs

Organic–inorganic hybrid nanoflowers are typically synthesized by introducing metal ions into a phosphate buffer, followed by the addition of an organic or biomolecular component and incubation under mild conditions for several days. In Cu‐based systems, the appearance of a blue precipitate is commonly regarded as an indicator of successful nanoflower formation. Although the overall synthesis route is straightforward, key parameters such as metal ion concentration, buffer composition, organic component loading, temperature, and incubation time critically influence nucleation behavior and final morphology. Therefore, careful control of these parameters is essential to obtain well‐defined and reproducible nanoflower architectures. In particular, avoiding hars hor stressful synthesis conditions is critical for preserving structural integrity [47]. The first step of Prp‐Nfs synthesis involved the addition of metal ions onto a phosphate buffer, followed by the introduction of propolis extract at different concentrations. In this method, the phosphate buffer and metal ions initiate the formation of metal phosphate crystals, creating the scaffold structure. The subsequent growth of this structure and the development of a flower‐like morphology originating from the center is driven by the organic molecules introduced into the system [48]. The SEM images of the materials obtained using different amounts of propolis extract are presented in Figure 2. Examination of the SEM images of the samples prepared with 125 µL, 500 µL, and 1000 µL of propolis extract (Figure 2a,c,d) revealed inhomogeneous distribution and irregular petal formations that did not originate from a central point. Moreover, increased aggregation and reduced pore size were observed in the samples synthesized with 1000 µL propolis, indicating that the self‐assembly process was suboptimal. In contrast, the use of 250 µL propolis extract in the synthesis (Figure 2b) resulted in a monodisperse distribution of uniformly sized spherical nanoflowers. The size of these hybrid nanoflowers was determined to be approximately ≥10 µm. This synthesis condition was considered optimal, and subsequent experiments were conducted using this material.

SEM images of Prp‐Nfs obtained by using (a) 125 µL, (b) 250 µL, (c) 500 µL and (d) 1000 µL propolis extracts.

In addition to SEM analysis, the particle size distribution of the optimally synthesized Prp‐Nfs was evaluated using a particle size analyzer based on laser diffraction to obtain the bulk‐weighted size profile of the material (Figure 3). Laser diffraction analysis revealed a median particle diameter (Dv50) of 31.3 µm; Dv10 and Dv90 values were 20.2 µm and 46.2 µm, respectively, indicating a relatively narrow size distribution (Span = 0.832). The bulk‐weighted mean diameter D[4,3] was calculated as 38.2 µm, while the surface‐weighted mean diameter D[3,2] was calculated as 22.2 µm, reflecting the contribution of both primary nanoflower structures and partially clustered assemblies in the aqueous dispersion. The apparent discrepancy between the ≥10 µm size estimated from SEM micrographs and the larger values obtained by laser diffraction reflects the fundamentally different measurement principles of the two techniques, rather than a true contradiction in particle morphology. While SEM provides direct, localized measurements of dried individual nanoflower structures, laser diffraction determines an equivalent spherical diameter based on light scattering patterns in an aqueous state where soft organic‐inorganic hybrid structures such as Prp‐Nfs can undergo partial aggregation or swelling. Therefore, laser diffraction results represent the hydrodynamic and ensemble behavior in suspension rather than the primary morphological dimensions of the nanoflowers. Taken together, SEM and laser diffraction analyses consistently confirm the successful creation of well‐defined Prp‐Nfs with a relatively narrow size distribution, demonstrating the importance of using complementary techniques for accurate particle size characterization of hybrid nanoflower systems.

Size distribution histogram of Prp‐Nfs.

The presence of metals within the Prp‐Nfs nanoflowers synthesized under optimum conditions was confirmed by EDX (energy dispersive X‐ray) analysis. As shown in Figure 4a, the EDX spectrum revealed peaks corresponding to carbon (C), nitrogen (N), oxygen (O), phosphorus (P), and copper (Cu), indicating the successful incorporation of these elements into the nanoflower structure. EDX analysis showed that Prp‐Nfs were predominantly composed of carbon (49.68%) and oxygen (34.90%), reflecting the strong presence of propolis‐derived organic constituents such as phenolic acids, flavonoids, and aromatic structures. Nitrogen (4.22%), originating from minor proteinaceous or amino‐containing components in propolis, further confirmed the organic contribution. In contrast, phosphorus (3.43%) and copper (7.77%) together represented the inorganic fraction of the hybrid structure, consistent with the expected Cu3(PO4)2·3H2O crystalline scaffold. The coexistence of high C/O content with detectable Cu and P peaks demonstrates that Prp‐Nfs form a true organic–inorganic hybrid, where propolis molecules are incorporated into and distributed throughout the copper phosphate matrix rather than simply adsorbed on the surface. This compositional distribution supports a well‐integrated architecture in which the organic phase guides nucleation and petal formation while the inorganic phase provides structural rigidity and catalytic functionality. Furthermore, as illustrated in the elemental mapping images in Figure 4b, the elements C, N, O, P, and Cu were found to be homogeneously distributed throughout the structure.

(a) EDX analysis. (b) Elemental mapping analysis of Prp‐Nfs.

The X‐ray diffraction (XRD) method is based on the principle that each crystalline phase diffracts X‐rays in a characteristic pattern depending on its unique atomic arrangement. In a sense, it serves as a “fingerprint” for identifying that specific crystal. Therefore, XRD analysis is a powerful tool for determining the crystallographic structure and confirming the phase composition of synthesized materials. For this reason, the crystal structures of Prp‐Nfs were analyzed by XRD. XRD analysis of Prp‐Nfs (Figure 5) revealed diffraction peaks that matched closely with the standard Cu3(PO4)2·3H2O pattern (JCPDS 00‐022‐0548), with deviations less than ± 0.15° from reference values, confirming high structural purity. Characteristic reflections at approximately 16.9°, 22.3°, 29.6°, 33.7°, 36.8°, 47.0°, 53.6°, 57.1°, 61.8°, and 67.7° correspond to the (020), (021), (220), (112), (113), (312), (400), (331), (511), and (440) planes, respectively. Moderate peak broadening indicates nanoscale crystallite domains consistent with the flower‐like morphology observed by SEM. Notably, the absence of sharp diffraction peaks from organic constituents reflects the amorphous nature of propolis, whereas the inorganic copper phosphate phase dominates the crystalline signature. Together with EDX elemental ratios showing a high carbon–oxygen content relative to phosphorus–copper levels, these results confirm the formation of a well‐integrated organic–inorganic hybrid nanostructure.

Prp‐Nfs XRD analysis (JCPDS card no: 00‐022‐0548, Cu3(PO4)2•3H2O).

To understand the molecular interactions and to confirm the incorporation of propolis into the hybrid nanoflower, chemical structures of the nanoflower were characterized by Fourier Transform Infrared Spectrophotometer (FTIR) analysis (Figure 5). The spectra of both samples were compared, and structural interpretations were made based on variations observed at specific wavenumbers. A broad ─OH stretching band observed around ∼3400 cm− 1 exhibited a shift or decrease in intensity in the Prp‐Nfs spectrum. This suggests that hydroxyl groups interacted with the copper surface, likely through hydrogen bonding or coordination mechanisms. In the range of 2920–2850 cm− 1, aliphatic C─H stretching vibrations were present in both samples with potential differences in peak intensity. This indicates that aliphatic structures were largely preserved after synthesis, with limited interactions occurring. At approximately ∼1700 cm− 1, the carbonyl (C═O) stretching band showed a shift or reduction in intensity in the Prp‐Nfs sample. This finding implies that C═O groups interacted with the copper surface, possibly leading to complex formation. The region between 1600–1500 cm− 1, corresponding to aromatic C═C vibrations and potential N─H bending, exhibited intensity changes or peak shifts in the Prp‐Nfs spectrum. This suggests that aromatic structures and/or amine groups may have formed bonds with the copper surface. Finally, in the 1200–1000 cm− 1 range, corresponding to C─O stretching (from phenolic and ester structures), peak shifts and partial band loss were observed. These changes indicate that phenolic and ester groups in propolis actively interacted with the surface, contributing to the nanoflower structure. Collectively, these findings confirm that the bioactive components of propolis chemically interacted with the metal surface and played a key role in the successful formation of the hybrid nanoflower structure (Figure 6).

Influence of pH and Thermal Conditions on the Peroxidase‐Mimic Behavior of Prp‐Nfs

In recent years, nano enzymes that exhibit enzyme‐like behavior have garnered a lot of interest due to their ability to overcome the disadvantages of natural enzymes such as unstable structure, limited shelf life, high cost and sensitivity to environmental factors [49]. These artificial enzyme systems are synthesized using inorganic building blocks such as metal ions, metal oxides, or carbon‐based nanomaterials and can mimic various biochemical reactions like peroxidase‐like catalytic activity [50]. Because of their high surface area and numerous active centers, organic‐inorganic hybrid nanoflower structures can demonstrate good catalytic activity in this setting [51].

In this study, the catalytic activity and stability of Prp‐Nfs were systematically investigated under optimized reaction conditions and comparatively evaluated against free propolis extract. Initially, the effect of pH on peroxidase‐mimicking activity was examined to determine the optimal pH value. As shown in Figure 7a, Prp‐Nfs exhibited the highest catalytic activity at pH 7.4, reaching a maximum value of 0.6556 EU mg−1 at 5 min. At both more acidic and more alkaline pH values, a pronounced decrease in activity was observed, indicating that deviations from near‐physiological pH adversely affect the catalytic performance and stability of the nanoflower system. Following pH optimization, the temperature‐dependent peroxidase‐like activity of Prp‐Nfs was evaluated in the range of 25°–60°C (Figure 7b). Among the tested conditions, Prp‐Nfs displayed the highest activity at 40°C, with a peak value of 2.7646 EU mg−1 at 20 min, demonstrating enhanced catalytic efficiency at moderately elevated temperatures. In contrast, free propolis extract exhibited significantly lower activity under the same conditions, showing values of 1.5459 EU mg−1 and 1.3838 EU mg−1 at 15 and 20 min, respectively. Overall, these results clearly demonstrate that immobilization of propolis within the hybrid nanoflower structure substantially enhances its catalytic activity and thermal stability compared to the free extract. The superior performance of Prp‐Nfs at pH 7.4 and 40°C suggests that the inorganic–organic hybrid architecture provides a protective microenvironment, preserving the functional integrity of bioactive components and enabling sustained peroxidase‐mimicking activity under conditions where free propolis shows diminished performance.

(b) Temperature dependence of peroxidase‐like activity for free propolis and Prp‐Nfs (25°–60°C; activity expressed as EU mg− 1). Data are presented as mean ± SE (n = 3). Reusability of Prp‐Nfs

The reusability of Prp‐Nfs was evaluated by repeatedly separating the material from the reaction medium and assessing its residual catalytic activity under optimized conditions. Although a gradual decrease in activity was observed with increasing cycle number, Prp‐Nfs retained 58.06% of their initial activity after five reuse cycles (Figure 8). The highest activity was recorded during the first cycle (2.76 ± 0.05 EU mg− 1), followed by a progressive decline in subsequent cycles, suggesting partial loss of active sites or minor structural alterations during repeated use.

Reusability of Prp‐Nfs over eight consecutive cycles under the optimized assay conditions (40°C, 20 min; ABTS/H2O2; activity expressed as EU mg− 1). Each bar represents mean ± SE (n = 3). Statistical significance among cycles was evaluated by one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test; different letters indicate significant differences (p < 0.05).

Total Phenolic Content of Prp‐Nfs

The total phenolic content (TPC) of the samples was quantified using the Folin–Ciocalteu method and expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents per gram of extract (mg GAE/g). The free propolis extract exhibited a substantially higher TPC value (123.1 ± 3.3 mg GAE/g) compared with the Prp‐Nfs (72.4 ± 0.92 mg GAE/g). This decrease in TPC after nanoflower formation is in agreement with the FTIR spectra (Figures 1b and 6), where the free propolis displayed more pronounced phenolic O─H, aromatic C═C and C─O bands, indicative of a richer polyphenolic composition. In contrast, the Prp‐Nfs spectrum reflected the expected dilution of phenolic signals due to the presence of the inorganic Cu3(PO4)2–protein matrix. Despite the lower TPC value of Prp‐Nfs, the retained phenolic fraction was still substantial, demonstrating that propolis phenolics were successfully integrated into the hybrid nanoflower structure.

DPPH and ABTS Radical Scavenging Activity of Prp‐Nfs

Reactive oxygen species (ROS), generated through endogenous metabolic processes or external factors such as UV radiation, xenobiotics, and pharmaceuticals, can induce oxidative modifications in biomolecules including proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and nucleic acids, thereby disrupting cellular function and contributing to the development of various pathological conditions. For this reason, the evaluation of antioxidant properties of natural extracts and biomaterial‐based formulations is of considerable importance. In vitro antioxidant assays differ in their underlying reaction mechanisms and thus provide complementary information on radical scavenging behavior; however, both analytical methods also possess inherent limitations depending on the physicochemical nature of the tested system [46]. The DPPH assay, which is based on a stable organic radical soluble in organic media, is particularly sensitive to lipophilic phenolic antioxidants and has been widely applied to polyphenol‐rich natural products such as propolis. Nevertheless, its limited applicability in aqueous systems may restrict its ability to fully capture the antioxidant capacity of complex matrices containing both hydrophilic and lipophilic components. To obtain a more comprehensive antioxidant profile, the ABTS radical cation decolorization assay was therefore additionally employed. Unlike DPPH, the ABTS assay is applicable to both hydrophilic and lipophilic antioxidants and enables the quantitative expression of antioxidant capacity using well‐established reference standards. Accordingly, the combined use of DPPH and ABTS assays provides a complementary and literature‐supported approach for the in vitro assessment of antioxidant activity in complex natural systems such as propolis and propolis‐based hybrid nanostructures.

As shown in Table 1, the DPPH radical scavenging activity of Prp‐Nfs reached its maximum at 1.2 mg mL−1, yielding a scavenging rate of 94.39% ± 0.0013%. This activity slightly exceeded that of the positive control, ascorbic acid (93.95% ± 0.0069% at 0.2 mg mL−1). In the present study, DPPH radical scavenging activity was expressed as percentage inhibition to enable direct comparison with commonly used antioxidant controls and previously reported propolis studies. To complement this qualitative comparison, the ABTS assay was used to quantitatively express antioxidant capacity in terms of Trolox equivalents, a widely accepted reference antioxidant. This dual presentation allows both relative performance evaluation (DPPH) and standardized quantitative benchmarking (ABTS–Trolox), thereby facilitating meaningful comparison of the antioxidant capacity of Prp‐Nfs with literature data. Free propolis exhibited a concentration‐dependent increase in activity, with a maximum scavenging rate of 87.76 ± 0.0060% at 100 µL/mL. The negative control, consisting of the copper phosphate skeleton, displayed the lowest antioxidant capacity (61.80 ± 0.0034%). To allow comparison with the DPPH results, ABTS radical scavenging activity was evaluated at the respective concentrations corresponding to the highest DPPH performance of each sample. Under these conditions, ABTS values were measured as 910.7 ± 5.13 µmol Trolox g−1 for free propolis and 475.6 ± 6.03 µmol Trolox g−1 for Prp‐Nfs (n = 3). Although Prp‐Nfs EXHİBİTED a lower TPC value than free propolis, they demonsrated higher DPPH scavenging activity. This enhancement is attributable to the hybrid nanoflower architecture and the intrinsic reactivity of the Cu3(PO4)2 skeleton. As shown in Table 1, the propolis‐free Cu3(PO4)2·3H2O crystal structure alone exhibited a notable DPPH scavenging capacity (61.80 ± 0.0034%). Furthermore, the large surface area of the nanoflowers may have improved the accessibility of propolis phenolics to the DPPH radical, thereby increasing the apparent radical scavenging efficiency per unit phenolic content. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the superior antioxidant performance of Prp‐Nfs does not result from an increase in total phenolic content, but rather from structural features of the hybrid nanoflower platform that enhance the functional expression and availability of the incorporated phenolic compounds. Overall, the combined use of two complementary antioxidant assays based on different reaction mechanisms (DPPH and ABTS), together with total phenolic content analysis, providesa more comprehensive in vitro assessment of the antioxidant potential of the propolis‐based hybrid nanoflower system.

From an application perspective, the combination of peroxidase‐like catalytic activity and strong radical scavenging capacity positions Prp‐Nfs as a promising material platform for oxidative stress–related biosensing systems and catalytic antioxidant interfaces. In particular, the ability of Prp‐Nfs to operate efficiently at near‐physiological pH and moderate temperatures supports their potential use in hydrogen peroxide–based sensing assays and surface‐confined catalytic reactions. At the same time, safety considerations associated with Cu‐based hybrid nanoflowers must be carefully addressed. Previous studies have shown that while intact Cu3(PO4)2 hybrid nanoflowers generally exhibit low acute toxicity, partial disassembly and the release of Cu2 + ions under certain environmental or biological conditions may increase toxicity in a dose‐ and context‐dependent manner. In this regard, the incorporation of propolis‐derived organic components into the hybrid architecture may contribute to partial stabilization of the inorganic phase; however, systematic biocompatibility and toxicity assessments remain essential for future translational applications.

In a recent study, Doğan et al. analyzed the total phenolic content (TPC), total antioxidant status (TAS), total oxidant status (TOS), catalase (CAT) enzyme activity, and lipid peroxidation in 40 raw propolis samples collected from different regions of Turkey, including the Mediterranean, Central Anatolia, Marmara, and Black Sea regions [52]. The highest TPC value (126.7 mg GAE/g) and CAT activity (2.85 U/g) were observed in samples from Central Anatolia, whereas the lowest values were detected in samples from the Mediterranean region. No significant differences were found among the regions in terms of lipid peroxidation, TAS, TOS, or oxidative stress index (OSI). A positive correlation between TPC and both TAS and CAT activity indicated that phenolic compounds in propolis contribute significantly to its antioxidant capacity and enzyme‐mediated defense mechanisms. Nagai et al. investigated the antioxidant activities and superoxide anion radical scavenging capacities of various honey types, royal jelly, and propolis [53]. Their findings demonstrated that buckwheat honey and propolis, in particular, possess strong antioxidant activity and maintain stability even after heat treatment. The high antioxidant capacity of propolis was attributed to its phenolic constituents, especially flavonoids, and all tested samples showed the ability to scavenge superoxide radicals. In another study, Seven et al. evaluated the effects of vitamin C and propolis supplementation on blood parameters, lipid peroxidation, and antioxidant enzyme activities in broilers subjected to oxidative stress [54]. The study showed that dietary supplementation with 1 g/kg of propolis and 500 mg/kg of vitamin C effectively mitigated the harmful effects of lead‐induced oxidative stress. Furthermore, Cikman et al. investigated the radioprotective potential of propolis against cranial irradiation‐induced oxidative liver damage. Propolis administration significantly enhanced the activities of antioxidant enzymes such as total and non‐enzymatic superoxide scavenging activity (TSSA, NSSA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and glutathione S‐transferase (GST), while suppressing the elevation of malondialdehyde (MDA) and xanthine oxidase levels [55]. These findings indicate that propolis strengthens antioxidant defenses and confers protection against ionizing radiation‐induced hepatic injury. Propolis extracts, which are rich in diverse polyphenols, have been widely reported to exhibit potent antioxidant effects [5]. Moreover, the chemical composition of propolis varies depending on its geographic origin, which significantly influences its antioxidant activity. These findings support the notion that propolis possesses notable antioxidant potential, especially under oxidative stress conditions, and may exhibit peroxidase‐like activity, thereby contributing to the reduction of cellular damage caused by reactive oxygen species.

The toxicological profile of Cu‐based nanoflowers has been recently studied in the literature. Zhao et al. demonstrated that Cu3(PO4)2 hybrid nanoflowers gradually disassemble into smaller nanoparticles and eventually dissolve completely in the presence of bacteria, with the resulting dissolution products—particularly Cu2 + ions—exhibiting higher toxicity than the intact nanoflowers. The study highlights that while HNFs themselves show relatively low toxicity, the intermediate particles and released ions formed during their life cycle pose greater ecological risk [56]. Notably, pure copper phosphate nanoflowers (CPnFs) have been reported to exhibit measurable toxicity, with LC50 and LC99 values of 0.066 and 0.172 mg/10 µL, respectively, in Tenebrio molitor larvae, indicating that inorganic Cu3(PO4)2‐based structures can impose dose‐dependent biological effects that must be considered in assessing the safety profiles of new hybrid nanoflower systems [57].

Recent advances in propolis nanotechnology have mainly focused on soft‐matter carriers such as niosomes, polymeric nanoparticles, nanoemulsions and chitosan‐based nano‐in‐microparticles, which primarily aim to improve solubility, bioavailability and controlled release of propolis extracts [38, 39, 40, 41]. In contrast to these systems, Prp‐Nfs represent a metal–organic hybrid nanozyme platform in which propolis is integrated into a Cu3(PO4)2.3H2O matrix that not only stabilizes the phenolic components but also endows the material with intrinsic peroxidase‐mimic activity and reusability. Moreover, Prp‐Nfs are obtained through a simple, fully aqueous and surfactant‐free synthesis, without the need for organic solvents or complex formulation steps, which may facilitate up‐scaling and translation to biosensing or catalytic biomedical applications. Thus, Prp‐Nfs complement existing propolis‐based nanocarriers by offering a structurally distinct, catalytically active hybrid biomaterial rather than a purely encapsulating vehicle.

The present work establishes Prp‐Nfs as a promising hybrid biomaterial with well‐defined morphology, multifunctional catalytic behavior, and enhanced antioxidant capacity. Further studies may focus on exploring long‐term stability, biodegradation behavior, and interaction with biological environments will also provide valuable insight for applications in biosensing, therapeutic catalysis, and antioxidant delivery. Finally, systematic biocompatibility evaluations in relevant cellular or in vivo models will be an important next step toward translating Prp‐NFs into biomedical and technological platforms. Collectively, these forward‐looking efforts will guide the continued advancement of propolis‐derived hybrid nanoflowers and broaden their utility across diverse application fields.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study reports the successful synthesis of propolis‐based hybrid nanoflowers (Prp‐Nfs) under mild aqueous conditions, yielding a uniform and well‐defined hierarchical morphology. Structural characterization by SEM, EDX, and XRD confirmed the formation of an organic–inorganic hybrid architecture in which propolis‐derived phenolic constituents are integrated within a crystalline Cu3(PO4)2·3H2O matrix. This structural integration provides a stable framework that supports both catalytic and antioxidant functionalities. Prp‐Nfs displayed markedly enhanced peroxidase‐like catalytic activity relative to free propolis, attributable to increased surface area, improved accessibility of active sites, and stabilization of reactive phenolic groups. In addition, antioxidant assays (DPPH and ABTS) showed that Prp‐Nfs possess strong radical scavenging capability, outperforming free propolis due to the nanoflower‐mediated retention and controlled presentation of bioactive molecules. The material also maintained substantial catalytic activity over repeated usage cycles, indicating good structural robustness and reusability. Altogether, these findings reveal Prp‐Nfs as a promising multifunctional biomaterial that unites catalytic efficiency, antioxidant potency, and structural stability. Such properties position this hybrid system as a strong candidate for future applications in biosensing, catalysis, and biomedical technologies, with subsequent work expected to explore biocompatibility and safety for translational use.

Experimental Section

Chemicals and Materials

Propolis, Ethanol, Copper (II) Sulfate, KCl, NaCl, Na2HPO4, KH2PO4, NaH2PO4, ABTS, Hydrogen Peroxide, DPPH, Folin–Ciocalteu Reagent, Sodium Carbonate, Potassium Persulfate, and Ascorbic Acid were purchased from Merck (Kenilworth, NJ, USA). Aqueous solutions were prepared using deionized water.

Preparation and Characterization of Propolis Extract

The raw propolis sample used in this study was obtained from the Köybağı locality (altitude: 900 m) in Avanos, Nevşehir, Türkiye (38°42′54″N, 34°50′48″E). The material was collected in April 2022 from 70 hives of the Cordinal bee strain and was purchased from the ‘Bizim Aktar’ shop.

A total of 0.5 g of raw propolis was added to 12 mL of 80% ethanol solution preheated to 60°C. The temperature was then raised to 80°C, and the mixture was incubated for 2 h [58]. Following the incubation period, the solution was centrifuged at 4000 × g for 15 min. The resulting supernatant was filtered and stored in a refrigerator until further use. For the characterization of this material, UV–vis spectral scanning and FTIR analysis were performed.

Synthesis and Characterization of Prp‐Nfs

In the synthesis of the biomaterial, propolis extract was used as the organic component, while copper (II) ions, known for their role as cofactors in enzymatic reactions, served as the inorganic component. For this purpose, varying volumes of propolis extract (ranging from 0.125 mL to 1 mL) were sequentially added to 10 mL of a 0.8 mM CuSO4 solution prepared in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). The mixture was briefly vortexed (30 s, medium speed), and incubated without agitation at room temperature for 72 h. Following incubation, the appearance of a blue precipitate indicated successful nanoflower formation. The product was collected by centrifugation for 10 min at room temperature, washed several times with deionized water, dried in an oven at 30 °C for 2 h, and stored at +4 °C until further use. Among the tested conditions, the optimal synthesis parameters were identified as: 0.8 mM CuSO4, 250 µL propolis extract, PBS pH 7.4, room temperature, 72 h, in a total reaction volume of 10 mL.)

The morphology of the synthesized propolis‐containing nanoflowers was visualized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The size distribution histogram was made by using Mastersizer. The presence of metals was confirmed by energy dispersive X‐ray (EDX) analysis. The crystalline structure was elucidated through X‐ray diffraction (XRD) measurements. Elemental mapping analysis was performed to determine which elements are present on the surface or cross‐section of the material and to visualize their spatial distribution. The chemical structure was further characterized using infrared (IR) spectroscopy.

Determination of Peroxidase Mimic Activities of Prp‐Nfs Under Different pH and Temperature Conditions

The catalytic activity and stability of the biomaterial were systematically investigated. For this purpose, 1 mL of Prp‐Nfs (1 mg mL−1) was added separately to 1 mL of PBS buffer with varying pH values (pH 5–10) and incubated in a sonicator (Bandelin Sonorex Digital 10 P sonicator at a frequency of 40 kHz and power of 70 W) for 2 min. Subsequently, 1 mL of 22.5 mM H2O2 was added to each tube, followed by the addition of 1 mL of 5 mM ABTS to initiate the reaction. The absorbance was measured at 417 nm at specific time intervals at room temperature. After determining the pH value at which the highest activity was observed as the optimal pH, the reaction was repeated by varying the temperature (25°C–60 °C) and compared with free propolis. All measurements were conducted in triplicate.

Determination of Reusability of Synthesized Prp‐Nfs

The biomaterial can be repeatedly reused by separating it from the reaction medium via centrifugation. For this purpose, following the catalytic activity assay, the structure was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min, then washed twice with pure water. Subsequently, its enzymatic activity was repeatedly measured under the predetermined optimal conditions (40°C‐20.min) using the ABTS substrate.

Total Phenolic Content

TPC was measured using the Folin–Ciocalteu method. Briefly, 100 µL of sample was mixed with 500 µL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (diluted 1:10 with distilled water) and incubated for 5 min. Subsequently, 400 µL of 7.5% sodium carbonate solution was added, and the mixture was incubated at 25°C for 30 min in the dark. Absorbance was recorded at 765 nm. TPC was calculated using a gallic acid calibration curve and expressed as mg GAE g−1 extract. All measurements were conducted in triplicate.

DPPH Radical Scavenging Activity Assay

To evaluate the total antioxidant capacity, a modified version of the method originally described by Blois was employed [59]. Briefly, Prp‐Nfs solutions (0,6 and 1,2 mg mL−1) were prepared in 3 mL of ethanol, and 1 mL of a 0.1 mM DPPH solution (prepared in ethanol) was added. The resulting mixture was vortexed, and incubated in the dark for 30 min. Subsequently, the solution was centrifuged at 5000 rpm, and the absorbance of the supernatant was recorded at 517 nm using a spectrophotometer. For comparative purposes, the antioxidant activity of various concentrations of free propolis (20–100 µL mL−1) was also assessed under identical conditions. Ascorbic acid served as the positive control. The DPPH radical scavenging activity was calculated according to the following equation: (1) DPPHradicalscavengingactivity%=1−Abs_Sample/Abs_Control×100 where Abs_Control denotes the absorbance of the reaction mixture without the sample. Ethanol was used as the blank. All measurements were carried out in triplicate.

ABTS Radical Scavenging Activity Assay

The ABTS radical scavenging activity of the samples was determined using a modified version of the method described by Re et al. [60]. The ABTS•+ radical cation was generated by reacting 7 mM ABTS stock solution with 2.45 mM potassium persulfate and allowing the mixture to stand in the dark at room temperature for 12–16 h. Prior to the assay, the ABTS•+ solution was diluted with ethanol until an absorbance of 0.70 ± 0.02 at 734 nm was obtained. For the analysis, aliquots of Prp‐Nfs at the concentrations that exhibited the highest DPPH radical scavenging activity (1.2 mg mL−1) were mixed with 1 mL of the ABTS•+ working solution and brought to a total volume of 3 mL with ethanol. The mixture was vortexed and incubated in the dark for 6 min at room temperature. The absorbance was then measured at 734 nm using a spectrophotometer. The ABTS scavenging activity of free propolis was evaluated under identical conditions using the concentration that produced the highest DPPH radical scavenging effect (100 µL mL−1). The ABTS radical scavenging capacity was expressed as µmol Trolox equivalents per gram of extract (µmol Trolox g−1), based on a Trolox calibration curve. The percentage inhibition was calculated using the following equation: (2) ABTSradicalscavengingactivity%=1−Abs_Sample/Abs_Control×100 where Abs_Control denotes the absorbance of the ABTS•+ solution without the sample. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Statistics

All experiments were performed in triplicate using independently prepared samples. Results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three independent measurements (n = 3), while error bars indicate the standard error (SE) to reflect the reliability of the mean values. Statistical significance was evaluated using the appropriate version of one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons post hoc test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.
