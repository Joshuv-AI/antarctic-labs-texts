Self-assembled meniscus-specific ECM membrane promotes cell migration and vascular ingrowth for meniscus repair and reconstruction
Authors: Mingze Du, Yuwei Wang, Tuo Wu, Huinan Lai, Liya Ai, Jiajun Liu, Ting Zhu, Jingke Du, Jun Yin, Dong Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe knee meniscus has limited intrinsic repair and regenerative capacity. Safe and effective biotherapeutic products remain lacking, and enhancing cell migration and angiogenesis remains a major challenge. This study aimed to develop a novel self-assembled meniscus-specific extracellular matrix membrane (saECM-M) and evaluate its performance in scaffold-free meniscus repair and scaffold-guided whole-meniscus reconstruction, in comparison with chemically processed decellularized ECM (cpd-ECM).MethodsMeniscal fibrochondrocytes (MFCs) were cultured under membrane-inducing conditions to generate saECM-M in vitro. Its bioactivity was evaluated by cell migration and endothelial network formation assays in vitro and by a scaffold-free meniscus defect repair model in vivo. For whole-meniscus reconstruction, saECM-M was integrated with a biomimetic 3D-printed poly(ε-caprolactone) (PCL) scaffold to guide ECM alignment and provide mechanical support. PCL scaffolds seeded with MFCs alone or combined with cpd-ECM served as controls. Meniscus regeneration was assessed in a rabbit meniscectomy model using imaging, histological, and biomechanical analyses, including chondroprotective evaluation. Transcriptomic profiling and proteomics were performed to investigate molecular pathways associated with saECM-M-mediated cell migration and vascular ingrowth.ResultssaECM-M was characterized as a cell-laden membrane enriched with native meniscus-like ECM components. In vitro, saECM-M significantly enhanced cell migration and endothelial network formation compared with cpd-ECM (approximately 1.2-fold and 2-fold, respectively; P < 0.05), accompanied by increased collagen deposition. In vivo, isolated saECM-M promoted superior meniscus defect repair. When integrated with the biomimetic PCL scaffold, saECM-M further promoted aligned collagen organization, vascular ingrowth, and robust meniscus regeneration. Compared with the PCL-cpd-ECM group, the PCL-saECM-M group showed improved host tissue integration on MRI (WORMS score: 10 ± 1 vs 32.3 ± 2.1), enhanced tensile mechanical properties (79 ± 2 MPa vs 64 ± 7.8 MPa), and superior chondroprotective effects (Mankin score: 1.67 ± 0.58 vs 6.67 ± 0.58) (P < 0.05). Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses identified enrichment of ECM-receptor interaction- and PI3K-AKT signaling-related pathways associated with saECM-M-enhanced cell migration and vascular ingrowth.ConclusionsaECM-M functions as an intrinsically bioactive meniscus-derived matrix capable of promoting both scaffold-free repair and scaffold-guided reconstruction. Compared with cpd-ECM, saECM-M more effectively supports cell migration, angiogenesis, and organized collagen remodeling, leading to superior regenerative outcomes.The translational potential of this articlesaECM-M addresses an unmet clinical need as a cell-secreted, additive-free bioactive matrix. It may serve as a simple biologic augmentation for arthroscopic repair, while integration with an FDA-approved PCL scaffold offers a promising strategy for meniscus replacement with improved bioactivity and biocompatibility.

Introduction

The meniscus is a wedge-shaped fibrocartilaginous tissue that plays a critical role in distributing axial stress, bearing mechanical loads, and maintaining knee joint stability[[1], [2], [3]]. Meniscal injury is among the most common knee pathologies, and meniscectomy remains one of the most frequently performed orthopaedic surgeries in the United States (approximately 700,000 cases annually) [4]. Altered joint biomechanics following meniscal injury often disrupt joint biomechanics [5,6] and accelerate the onset and progression of osteoarthritis (OA) [7,8]. Current clinical repair strategies primarily rely on arthroscopic suturing [9], often supplemented with biological augmentation techniques such as PRP [10], fibrin clot implantation [11], or bone marrow stimulation [12]. Tissue-engineered meniscus (TEM) substitutes are considered a highly promising strategy for achieving functional meniscal regeneration, with three-dimensional (3D) printing technology being widely utilized for its ability to precisely control scaffold architecture [13,14], including the fiber orientation and pore size [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. However, several intrinsic challenges continue to constrain the long-term success and clinical translation of both scaffold-free repair and scaffold-based reconstruction [19]. A key issue is that adult meniscal tissue is dense and avascular, with only the outer third being well-vascularized, which severely limits endogenous cell migration, vascular ingrowth, and nutrient diffusion [20]. The promotion of cell recruitment, angiogenesis, and organized matrix remodeling is a central unmet requirement for meniscus repair and regeneration [21,22].

Strategies relying on exogenous cytokines, growth factors, or cell transplantation, while capable of promoting regeneration to some extent, often suffer from low efficiency, short duration of effect, and potential biosafety risks, including off-target effects, immune reactions, and tumorigenesis, thus limiting their clinical translational value [23,24]. To provide endogenous biological cues, decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM) has been explored as an alternative strategy for meniscus repair and tissue-engineered scaffolds[[25], [26], [27]]. However, traditional decellularization and subsequent processing, such as pulverization and dissolution, often disrupt the intricate ECM structure and bioactive components [28,29]. Furthermore, the fabrication of polymer-dECM composites is often complicated and time-consuming, and may involve extensive chemical or enzymatic processing and potential residual reagents, which can disrupt native matrix architecture and bioactivity [30]. Therefore, further improvements are still required to enhance their bioactivity and reparative efficacy to satisfy the clinical need for durable meniscal repair and regeneration.

To address these limitations, the present study proposes a novel self-assembled meniscus specific extracellular matrix membrane (saECM-M). Meniscal fibrochondrocytes (MFCs) were induced to self-assemble an intrinsically bioactive ECM membrane under domestic bio-enhancement culture conditions. This cell-driven ECM membrane preserves native matrix composition and biological activity, which are frequently compromised during conventional decellularization processes. The intrinsic bioactivity of saECM-M enables the promotion of cell migration, endothelial network formation, and enables superior scaffold-free meniscal repair compared to the cpd-ECM. Furthermore, the saECM-M was integrated with a biomimetic 3D-printed scaffold with polycaprolactone (PCL), an FDA-approved polymer and widely used for meniscal reconstruction [31,32].The scaffold's anisotropic microtopography provides spatial guidance for ECM alignment and mechanical reinforcement, thereby supporting organized collagen remodeling and whole-meniscus reconstruction [28]. Importantly, by restoring meniscal structure and promoting functional regeneration, PCL-saECM-M exhibited superior chondroprotective effects compared to PCL-cpd-ECM. Collectively, the novel saECM-M effectively addresses the key biological barriers of meniscus repair and regeneration, including insufficient cell recruitment, limited vascularization, and disorganized matrix remodeling. Materials and methods

Fabrication of saECM-M

Knee joints of rabbits were collected and menisci were harvested with a scalpel. The menisci were immersed in a PBS solution with 1% P/S added. Subsequently, the tissue was minced into small fragments (approximately 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 mm) with a scalpel. The samples were digested with EDTA in an incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 min. After discarding the supernatant, the pellet was resuspended in a 0.2% solution of collagenase I and collagenase II. The resulting mixture was transferred to a Petri dish and digested overnight. The next day, the mixture was centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. The mixture was then resuspended using complete medium and added to a Petri dish for primary MFCs culture. After incubating for three days, MFCs were transferred to six-well plates and cultured for 3 days. Then, the complete medium was removed and the membrane-forming medium was introduced, which consists of DMEM/F12 (Gibco, USA), Glutmax (Sigma Adrich, USA, 0.01 ml/ml), NEAA (Sigma Adrich, USA, 0.01 ml/ml), P/S(Sigma Adrich, USA, 0.01 ml/ml), Dexamethasone(Sigma Adrich, 100 nM, USA), ITS(Sigma Adrich, USA, 0.01 ml/ml), and Proline (Sigma Adrich, USA, 40 μg/ml). The cells were then incubated for 1 week to form the augmented MFCs-derived saECM-M.

After 7 days of membrane-forming culture, saECM-M sheets were carefully detached from the wells and standardized before subsequent experiments. The membrane diameter and area were determined according to the culture surface of the well. Only intact membranes with uniform morphology and without visible tearing, folding, or incomplete formation were used for subsequent characterization and functional assays. Membrane thickness was measured from histological cross-sections at three randomly selected regions per membrane. For each independent experiment, saECM-M was prepared from MFCs isolated from independent donor rabbits. Unless otherwise specified, at least three biological replicates were included for each assay.

Live/dead cell viability assay and cytoskeleton observation

The six-well plates were washed with PBS. LIVE/DEAD staining was performed using ready-to-use dyes (containing AM, PI, and PBS) under light-avoidance conditions for 0.5 h. A portion was added to 4% paraformaldehyde and 0.1% Triton-PBS for fixation and permeabilization. After sealing with PBST-BSA. the cytoskeleton was stained with Phalloidin and the nucleus with DAPI under light-avoidance conditions. Following the above operations, observations were recorded using a confocal microscope.

Immunofluorescence analysis

The six-well plates were washed with PBS. A portion was added to 4% paraformaldehyde and 0.1% Triton-PBS for fixation and permeabilization. After sealing with PBST-BSA, COL-1 and COL-2 were stained using Goat anti-Rabbit primary antibody (Arigo, China) and donkey anti-Goat secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, USA). The final step involved staining the cytoskeleton with Phalloidin and the nucleus with DAPI under light-avoidance conditions. Following the above operations, observations were recorded using a confocal microscope.

Cytotoxicity evaluation

On days 1, 7, and 14 of in vitro co-culture of MFCs under the normal medium or membrane-inducing condition, the cells were incubated with CCK-8 working solution (Invitrogen, USA) in culture medium at 37 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, the optical density (OD) was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using an ultraviolet spectrophotometer (Thermo Electron Corporation, USA).

Gene expression and protein level quantitation

Total RNA was extracted from saECM-M (n = 3 biological replicates) using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), followed by treatment with DNase I. RNA purity (A260/A280: 1.8–2.0) and integrity (RNA Integrity Number, RIN >9.0) were verified by NanoDrop and Bioanalyzer. Reverse transcription was performed with 1 μg RNA using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara). COL1A1 and fibrogenesis-related genes primers (Table S1) and reference gene GAPDH were amplified in triplicate using SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche) on a QuantStudio 6 system (Applied Biosystems). Data were analyzed via the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Total protein was extracted from MFCs or saECM-M using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE on 8% gels and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against COL1A1 (Arigo, China) and GAPDH. Following incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, immunoreactive bands were visualized using an ECL substrate.

Decellularization and digestion of rabbit meniscus ECM

A cpd-ECM group was established to compare the properties of saECM-M and cpd-ECM materials. Meniscus-derived ECM was prepared following a previously described protocol [33]. Briefly, meniscus samples were washed with PBS and minced into small fragments (5-10 mm3). The fragments were swollen in 0.01 M acetic acid solution (pH 2) at 4 °C for 48 h and subjected to three freeze-thaw cycles. The tissue blocks were then decellularized by agitation in 2% SDS and 10 mM Tris at 25 °C, followed by treatment with 0.1% peracetic acid. After oscillating washes, the material was lyophilized for 24 h. The resulting sponge-like ECM was digested at 50 mg/mL concentration in 0.1% pepsin/0.01 M HCl (pH 2) at 25 °C under agitation for 12 h to obtain an ECM digest. Nucleic acids were removed using 200 U/mL DNase and 50 U/mL RNase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37 °C. Prior to use, the pH of the ECM solution was adjusted to neutral using 0.5 mol/L NaOH and stored at 4 °C.

Transwell assay

Prior to the assay, cells were serum-starved in FBS-free medium for 24 h. Then, 5 × 104 cells were seeded into the upper chamber of a Transwell plate in FBS-free medium. For all groups, the lower chamber contained complete medium supplemented with 10% FBS. In the saECM-M group, saECM-M was added to the lower chamber. In the cpd-ECM group, cpd-ECM was added to the lower chamber. In the control group, no additional matrix material was added. The cells were incubated at 37 °C. At both 24 h and 48 h after incubation, the lower surface of the filter membranes was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 10 min. Finally, migrated cells were observed under an optical microscope and counted from images of three random fields using ImageJ.

Cell migration assay

Cells were seeded into a 6-well plate and scratched when reaching a confluency of 90%. After washing with PBS to remove detached cells, the cells were divided into three groups. The culture medium was respectively substituted with complete medium containing saECM-M, complete medium containing cpd-ECM, and complete medium. After 48 h of culture, cell migration was observed under an optical microscope after removing saECM-M and cpd-ECM fragments, and the migration distance was measured and compared across groups using image analysis software.

HUVECs tube formation assay

Each well of a 48-well culture plate was coated with 100 μL of Matrigel and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to allow polymerization. HUVECs were seeded onto the surface of the Matrigel at a density of 3 × 104 cells per well and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. Tubular network structures were observed and photographed using an inverted microscope. Parameters of tube formation were quantified using ImageJ software.

Fabrication of 3D-printed PCL scaffold

A two-thirds wedge-shaped disc meniscal scaffold was fabricated based on the native dimensions of a rabbit meniscus using fused deposition modeling (FDM) 3D printing technology. The scaffold design featured an inner ring height of 0.3 mm, an outer ring height of 1.5 mm, an inner diameter of 4 mm, and an outer diameter of 10 mm. PCL (Mw 150,000, Daigang Bioengineering Co., Ltd., Shandong, China) was melted inside the printing nozzle and deposited along a pre-designed path. The printing system consisted of a 3D communication module, a stepper-motor platform (model gxyz303020vp, Zuo Youxin Intelligent Equipment Co., Ltd., Shandong, China), a pneumatic extrusion system, and a temperature control system. The printing process was coordinated using Repeater Host v2.1.6 software to regulate the two-stage heating block, while 3D-print software controlled the motion of the nozzle and platform. Prior to printing, PCL pellets were pre-melted and thermally stabilized within the high-temperature extrusion printhead cartridge. Scaffolds were then printed layer-by-layer under constant extrusion pressure. A custom C++-written axis control program interpreted G-code to direct the nozzle, depositing PCL strands in a 0°-90° pattern. Printing parameters were optimized to ensure homogeneity of the scaffold microstructure, including feature size, layer thickness, and inter-layer spacing.

RNA sequencing

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1.0 × 105 cells per well and incubated for 48 h. They were then treated with either normal medium or saECM-M-supplemented complete medium for 72 h. The BGISEQ-500 instrument (PRHS Co Ltd) was used for cells RNA sequencing. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on an adjusted P-value (Q-value) of ≤0.05. DEGs were visualized using a volcano plot depicting logarithmic scale (log2) and the Q-value. Functional enrichment analysis of the DEGs was performed through Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses.

Proteomic analysis

MFCs were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1.0 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 48 h. Subsequently, MFCs were cultured for a further 72 h in either standard medium or saECM-M forming medium. Both the MFCs and the saECM-M were then collected. Global protein expression profiling was performed using data independent acquisition (DIA) based proteomic analysis. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs), defined with a significance threshold of adjusted P-value (Q-value) of ≤0.05, were further analyzed through GO enrichment, KEGG pathway mapping, and protein-protein interaction network analysis using DAVID, STRING, CytoHubba, and OmicStudio tools.

Intrameniscal injection of saECM-M in rabbits

The experiments of New Zealand White rabbits were approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) Medical Ethics Committee of Peking University Third Hospital (Approval No. A2022019). All surgical procedures were performed under general anesthesia induced by intramuscular injection of xylazine hydrochloride (2 mg/kg), followed by maintenance with isoflurane (1.5-3%) in oxygen via a face mask. A medial parapatellar approach was used to open the joint capsule. A cylindrical defect, 2 mm in diameter, was created in the anterior portion of the meniscus, with the defect located within the inner two-thirds of the meniscus to minimize involvement of the peripheral vascularized red zone. In the control group, the wound interface was repaired using 5-0 sutures via an inside-out technique. In the cpd-ECM/saECM-M group, after injecting cpd-ECM/saECM-M into the wound interface, the sutures were knotted to close the site. The medial collateral ligament was repaired using 2-0 sutures. The incision was closed with a continuous suture. Postoperatively, animals received penicillin sodium injections for one week to prevent infection and were housed in a comfortable environment with free movement. 8 weeks after implantation, samples were harvested. The procedure was performed bilaterally, and both knees of each rabbit received the same treatment.

Subcutaneous implantation in mice

C57 mice, supplied by the Experimental Animal Center of Peking University, were randomly divided into three groups. The experiments of mice were approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University (No.PUIRB-LA2022629). Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 2% pentobarbital sodium. Two approximately 1 cm longitudinal incisions were made on each side of the dorsal skin to create subcutaneous pockets, into which the sterile materials were implanted. Post-surgery, mice were housed with free access to food and water. Two weeks after implantation, samples were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and processed for immunofluorescence staining. Imaging was performed using Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). Immunofluorescence staining was quantified using ImageJ software.

Knee joint scaffolds implantation

In terms of the PCL-MFCs scaffold, MFCs were seeded onto the PCL scaffolds and was then cultured in vitro in normal medium for 10 days. The process of the fabrication of PCL-cpd-ECM scaffold was as previously reported [34]. PCL scaffold and cpd-ECM solution were freeze-dried together and crosslinked by immersing them in an EDAC (1-ethyl-3-3-dimethylaminopropyl carbodiimide) ethanol solution. For the PCL-saECM-M composite scaffold, MFCs were also seed onto the PCL scaffolds for the first 3 days, and then the composite was incubated under the membrane-forming condition for 7 days to construct the PCL-saECM-M composite scaffold. Healthy New Zealand White rabbits (3 ± 0.2 kg) were employed. All surgical procedures were performed under general anesthesia induced by intramuscular injection of xylazine hydrochloride (2 mg/kg), followed by maintenance with isoflurane (1.5-3%) in oxygen via a face mask. Under aseptic conditions, an incision was made through the skin and subcutaneous tissue along the medial border of the patellar tendon. The medial retinaculum and part of the vastus medialis muscle were incised, the patella was dislocated laterally, and the knee joint was fully exposed. The anterior horn of the meniscus was transected, and the meniscus was separated from the synovial rim towards the posterior horn. Using curved forceps, the anterior horn and body were grasped, and the posterior horn was completely excised, with care taken to preserve the medial collateral ligament to maintain joint stability. The entire meniscus was excised to create a meniscectomy model. The scaffold was sutured to the adjacent synovium using non-resorbable 4-0 sutures (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA). Eighteen rabbits were randomly divided into three groups including PCL-MFCs scaffolds, PCL-cpd-ECM scaffolds, and PCL-saECM-M scaffolds for meniscal reconstruction, respectively. The anterior and posterior horns of the implant were re-attached to the corresponding ligamentous structures. Finally, the joint capsule, subcutaneous tissue, and skin were closed in layers using 3-0 sutures (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA). Postoperatively, the knee was not immobilized, and the animals were permitted free movement Penicillin (400,000 units) was administered intramuscularly daily for 3 consecutive days. Meniscectomy and scaffold implantation were performed bilaterally, and both knees of each rabbit received the same treatment.

Evaluation of neo-meniscus

Animals were euthanized 18 weeks post scaffold implantation. MRI was performed to evaluate meniscal regeneration and joint degeneration in situ using the Whole-Organ Magnetic Resonance Imaging Score (WORMS) system. The neo-meniscus was grossly examined and scored according to the Gross Evaluation of Meniscus Implant Score criteria. Furthermore, histological analyses (H&E staining, immunofluorescence staining for COL-1 and COL-2 staining) were conducted to evaluate cellular phenotype and ECM distribution. The fluorescence intensity values for COL-1 and COL-2 deposition were semi-quantified using ImageJ. Additionally, neo-tissue specimens were subjected to tensile modulus testing. To assess the in vivo angiogenic potential of the scaffolds, immunofluorescence staining for the endothelial cell marker CD31 was also performed.

Evaluation of articular cartilage

At 18 weeks postoperatively, the rabbits were sacrificed and the entire knee joint specimens, including the femoral condyle and tibial plateau, were harvested. Gross observation of the articular surfaces of the femoral condyle and tibial plateau was first performed and photographed. The specimens were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, followed by decalcification in 10% EDTA solution at room temperature with regular replacement of the decalcification solution until complete decalcification. After decalcification, the specimens were routinely dehydrated, cleared, and embedded in paraffin, and sectioned in the coronal plane at a thickness of approximately 5 μm. The sections were stained with H&E to evaluate cartilage tissue structure, cellular arrangement, and matrix morphology. Safranin O/Fast Green (SO&FG) staining was performed to assess proteoglycan content in the cartilage matrix. Cartilage degeneration was semi-quantitatively evaluated using the International Cartilage Repair Society (ICRS) scoring system and the Mankin scoring system. In addition, the knee joint specimens were scanned using micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT) to assess changes in subchondral bone architecture. After scanning, regions of interest (ROI) in the subchondral bone of the femoral condyle and tibial plateau were selected for 3D reconstruction. Bone morphometric parameters, including bone volume fraction (bone volume/total volume, BV/TV) and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), were calculated using the corresponding analysis software to quantify subchondral bone remodeling.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed by SPSS 20.0 (SPSS Inc., USA) and showed as mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a post hoc test was used to detect the intergroup difference. ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, ∗∗∗P < 0.001.

Results

Cell viability and superior ECM deposition of saECM-M

After isolating from the rabbit meniscus tissue and culturing in vitro for one week, the rabbit MFCs formed translucent homogeneous membrane (Fig. 2A–D), with an average thickness of [236.3 ± 11.47] μm (Fig. S1), indicating reproducible formation of a standardized cell-laden ECM membrane. The membrane-forming cultured MFCs containing saECM-M exhibited an extremely high survival rate, with an average intensity approximately 40% compared with ordinary cultured MFCs (Fig. 2E). Cytoskeleton staining revealed densely packed MFCs within the saECM-M along with an interconnected cytoskeletal network (Fig. 2F). Based on CCK-8 assay results, MFCs cultured in saECM-M displayed significantly higher proliferative activity throughout the 7-day culture period (Fig. 2J). Immunofluorescence analysis indicated that MFCs within saECM-M secreted greater amounts of COL-1 (Fig. 2G), which was further supported by relative RNA expression (Fig. 2H) and protein level (Fig. 2I, Fig. S2). saECM-M promotes the migration of MFCs

The meniscus is a fibrocartilage tissue composed of dense ECM [35]. After injury, it is difficult for cells to migrate from the dense ECM to the site of injury, which is one of the crucial factors leading to the difficulty of self-repair after meniscus injury [36]. To investigate the cellular response of MFCs to saECM-M and compare its effects with those of cpd-ECM, a series of in vitro and in vivo experiments were conducted via the cell migration assay (Fig. 3A and B) and wound-healing assay (Fig. 3C and D). saECM-M obviously facilitated the migration of rabbit MFCs. Notably, in the Transwell migration assay, the number of migrated MFCs in the saECM-M group was approximately three times greater than that in the control group after 48 h. To further investigate the molecular mechanism by which saECM-M promotes migration in rabbit MFCs, RNA sequencing was performed on MFCs treated with or without saECM-M. Principal component analysis (PCA) of gene expression profiles revealed clear separation between the two groups, indicating that saECM-M significantly influenced MFCs gene expression (Fig. S3A). DEGs analysis identified a total of 746 significantly dysregulated genes between the groups (|Log2FC| > 1, Q value < 0.05), with 379 upregulated and 367 downregulated in the saECM-M-treated group compared to the control (Fig. 3E, Fig. S3B). Following the broad-spectrum RNA sequencing of the gene expression profiles, KEGG and GO pathway enrichment analyses were conducted to explore the biological functions of the DEGs. GO pathway enrichment analysis identified key pathways associated with the observed differences between the two groups of MFCs. As illustrated in Fig. 3F, terms such as integrin complex, focal adhesion, collagen-containing ECM, and basement membrane were significantly enriched in the saECM-M-treated group. More specifically, the DEGs associated with ECM revealed that saECM-M altered the deposition of ECM components, consistent with the observed changes in characterization of saECM-M. DEGs enriched in integrin complex and focal adhesion showed evidence that saECM-M could facilitate MFC migration by promoting cell-matrix adhesion. Additionally, KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that DEGs were associated with several signaling pathways, including MAPK signaling pathway, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, and ECM-receptor interaction (Fig. 3G), indicating that these pathways may play essential roles in mediating the biological effects of saECM-M on MFCs. GSEA analysis showed that MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 3H) and ECM-receptor interaction (Fig. 3I) were positively correlated with the saECM-M group.

In vitro pro-angiogenic potential of saECM-M

In adulthood, only the outer one-third of the meniscus, which is the red-red zone, has blood supply, so it cannot obtain sufficient nutrition supply after injury, making it difficult to self-repair [37]. Therefore, promoting endothelial cell migration and vascular formation is crucial for meniscus regeneration. To evaluate the potential of saECM-M in enhancing angiogenesis, the response of HUVECs to saECM-M was explored in vitro. According to the result of cell migration assay and wound-healing assay (Fig. 4A and B), saECM-M could obviously facilitate the migration of HUVECs. Specifically, in the Transwell migration assay, the number of migrated HUVECs in the saECM-M group was approximately twice that in both the control and cpd-ECM groups from 24 to 48 h. In contrast, no significant differences were observed between the cpd-ECM and control groups in either assay, exhibiting superior pro-migratory effect of saECM-M on HUVECs. Subsequently, the tube formation assay was performed by incubating HUVECs on Matrigel for 4 h. As shown in Fig. 4C, few tubular networks formed in the control and cpd-ECM groups. In contrast, saECM-M group presented a detailed and well-developed tubular network structure through analytical labeling. This ability to promote angiogenesis is particularly valuable, as it holds the potential to address the avascular challenge in meniscus healing.

Besides, to further investigate the molecular mechanisms through which saECM-M promoted the migration and tube formation in HUVECs, RNA sequencing was also performed on HUVECs treated with or without saECM-M. PCA of gene expression profiles revealed clear separation between the two groups, indicating substantial alterations in gene expression induced by saECM-M (Fig. S5A). MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. S5G) and PI3K-AKT signaling pathway (Fig. S5H) were positively correlated with the saECM-M group according to the GSEA analysis.

Implantation of isolated saECM-M achieved superior meniscus defect repair

To better examine its in situ regenerative ability, animal surgeries were conducted as shown in Fig. 6A and Fig. S6. After the surgeries, all rabbits survived and recovered well, with no evidence of joint infection or effusion observed. According to the macroscopic observation and histological analysis of neo-menisci, the meniscus defects treated with saECM-M showed improved healing effects and better integration with host tissues, compared to the cases treated with cpd-ECM or those without any treatment (Fig. 6B). Immunofluorescence analysis exhibited ample deposition of COL-1 and COL-2 in saECM-M group, fully filling up the torn meniscus area (Fig. 6C), and the semi quantitative data of immunofluorescence intensity of regenerated tissue in the defect area are shown in Fig. S7. Proliferated and migrated MFCs formed a dense ECM rich in COL-1 and COL-2, closely mimicking the composition of native meniscus tissue. The structures of the regenerated tissues were highly similar to that of the natural meniscus, and a seamless integration and excellent continuity was achieved at the interface. In contrast, the cpd-ECM group showed poorer regenerative outcomes. Although some collagen deposition was detected at the defect edge, its integration with the surrounding natural meniscus was not good, and there were still obvious remaining gaps inside the defect area, indicating the incomplete meniscus restoration. The excellent migration response induced by saECM-M was more effective than cpd-ECM, indicating its outstanding biological activity in recruiting endogenous cells, which was a crucial first step in in situ repair. The seamless integration and abundant collagen deposition observed in vivo further confirmed that saECM-M provided directive signals to guide functional tissue repair.

Besides, in order to evaluate the in vivo biocompatibility of saECM-M, major organs and blood samples were collected for analysis. H&E staining of vital organs indicated no significant histological changes between the rabbits with or without saECM-M implantation (Fig. S8A). In addition, complete blood count analysis, including red blood cell count, white blood cell count, lymphocyte count, neutrophil count, and platelet count, showed no significant differences between groups (Fig. S8B). Blood biochemical indicators were also comparable across groups (Fig. S8C). These findings indicate that saECM-M has good biocompatibility and present its clinical translation potential. From the perspective of the session of in vitro and in vivo experiments, the saECM-M group had best performance in meniscal regeneration among three groups, which was evidenced by enhanced facilitation of MFC migration, abundant and dense ECM secretion, well-organized neo-meniscus structure, and excellent integration with host tissue.

Topological microstructure of 3D biomimetic scaffold drove oriented assembly of saECM-M and collagen fiber alignment

Functional reconstruction of the meniscus requires the restoration of both biological and mechanical properties [38]. Therefore, to construct a scaffold capable of supporting saECM-M while mimicking the biomechanical properties and microporous architecture of the native meniscus, we previously fabricated a biomimetic PCL scaffold with a rhombic gradient microporous structure via 3D printing [39]. To leverage the bioactive properties of saECM-M to meet the mechanical requirements of meniscus, the PCL-saECM-M composite scaffold was developed through the biological process of self-secretion and self-assembly of MFCs. For comparative purposes, PCL scaffolds functionalized with cpd-ECM and PCL scaffolds ordinary seeded with MFCs were also prepared. To verify the efficacy of attachment, surface morphology of the three scaffold groups was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The attached cells or cpd-ECM particles were pointed out by red arrowheads (Fig. S9A). Meanwhile, the SEM image of the longitudinal section showed a tight bond between saECM-M and PCL scaffold (white curve), while cpd-ECM was dispersed in particles on the scaffold (white arrow) (Fig. S9B). Due to the decellularization process of cpd-ECM, SEM-EDS results showed a significant decrease in Na content in the cpd-ECM group, while the saECM-M group retained viable cells, indicating a uniform and widespread distribution of Na on the PCL scaffold (Fig. S9C). The PCL-saECM-M group exhibited abundant cells, intercellular junctions, and dense ECM deposition surrounding the PCL fibers. Viability staining revealed widespread cell adhesion, demonstrating excellent cell activity and adhesion (Fig. S9D left) of PCL-saECM-M composite scaffold. Cytoskeleton and COL-1 immunofluorescence staining showed that PCL-saECM-M composite scaffold had significantly better cell adhesion and meniscus specific ECM deposition than PCL-MFCs scaffold (Fig. S9D right). In addition, to verify the effect of 3D biomimetic PCL scaffold on the orientation alignment of saECM-M collagen fibers, saECM-M was formed on biomimetic PCL scaffold and 2D culture respectively. Subsequently, the immunofluorescence staining orientation analysis of COL-1 by Image J showed that the 3D biomimetic PCL scaffold could induce the orientation arrangement of collagen fibers (Fig. S9E). Therefore, the results indicated that the PCL scaffold and saECM-M were not simply composite, but rather exhibited cross-scale responses to each other. This cross-scale interaction suggests that the scaffold is an active guide for ECM organization, which is fundamental for replicating the anisotropic mechanical properties of the native meniscus.

Discussion

Insufficient cell migration, vascular ingrowth, and matrix remodeling remain major barriers to TEM regeneration and clinical translation. Here, we established a meniscus-specific, intrinsically bioactive saECM-M through cell-driven self-assembly under bio-enhancement membrane-inducing conditions. saECM-M acted as a cell-laden ECM membrane with inherent pro-migratory and pro-angiogenic activity, enabling both scaffold-free defect repair and scaffold-guided whole-meniscus reconstruction. When integrated with a biomimetic 3D-printed PCL scaffold, saECM-M supported organized collagen remodeling, vascularization, superior meniscus regeneration, and chondroprotection compared with cpd-ECM. These findings address the unmet need for effective meniscus therapies, offering a safe, cell-derived material with pro-regenerative cues. saECM-M can augment standard arthroscopic suturing without added complexity and provides better bioactivity and biocompatibility than current implants when combined with FDA-approved PCL scaffolds. With this combination of safety, ease of use, and enhanced bioactivity, saECM-M represents a promising candidate for improving clinical outcomes in meniscus therapy.

Impaired cell migration is a fundamental barrier to meniscal repair and regeneration [20,35]. saECM-M effectively promoted cell migration in vitro. Various strategies have been reported to enhance cell recruitment capabilities, including chemokine delivery [40], growth factor integration [41], and synthetic scaffold surface functionalization [42]. However, these approaches typically rely on exogenous additives, which are associated with limited retention, uncontrollable release kinetics, and increased regulatory complexity. In contrast, saECM-M intrinsically encapsulates viable MFCs within a native ECM microenvironment. This cell-laden membrane not only provides an endogenous cell source but also supports cell migration without the need for exogenous biological agents.

The saECM-M developed in this study showed a strong capacity to promote angiogenesis both in vitro and in vivo. Insufficient vascular ingrowth remains a major limitation in meniscus repair, as neovascularization is closely related to nutrient supply, matrix remodeling, and long-term tissue integration [43,44]. Previous approaches have mainly relied on angiogenic growth factor delivery or endothelial cell co-culture, but these strategies often involve transient effects and biosafety concerns associated with exogenous factors [45,46]. In contrast, saECM-M promotes vascular ingrowth through cell-driven ECM self-assembly, and, when combined with a biomimetic PCL scaffold, scaffold microtopology further facilitates vascular infiltration without exogenous growth factor delivery. Nevertheless, angiogenesis in meniscus regeneration is complex and context-dependent [47]. Although vascular supply is important for development and repair, excessive or persistent vascularization may compromise fibrocartilage homeostasis and promote fibrotic remodeling [46,48]. In our study, the increased vascularization in the saECM-M group was confined to the outer meniscal region and was accompanied by better collagen organization, improved integration, and superior regenerative outcomes, suggesting a repair-supportive vascular response rather than obvious fibrotic deterioration.

As discussed above, saECM-M effectively promoted MFC migration and vascularization both in vitro and in vivo. RNA sequencing further provided mechanistic insight into these effects, highlighting enrichment of PI3K-Akt signaling and ECM-receptor interaction pathways. These pathways are biologically relevant to meniscus regeneration. ECM-receptor interaction is closely associated with cell-matrix adhesion, matrix sensing, and directed migration, which are essential for endogenous cell recruitment into the dense meniscal ECM [49,50]. PI3K-Akt signaling is involved in regulating cell survival, motility, and angiogenic responses, and is therefore consistent with the enhanced migration and vascular ingrowth observed in the saECM-M group [51,52]. In addition, related signaling pathways have also been implicated in fibrochondrocyte biological responses during fibrocartilaginous healing [53]. Together, these findings suggest that saECM-M may enhance pro-regenerative signaling in both MFCs and endothelial cells to support cell recruitment, vascularization, and matrix remodeling during meniscus repair, although the specific molecular mediators still require further validation.

In addition to cell recruitment and vascularization, organized collagen deposition is essential for restoring the anisotropic structure and load-bearing function of the meniscus. The scaffold's 3D interconnected porous architecture plays an important role in cell migration, vascular ingrowth, and collagen deposition [54]. Conventional cpd-ECM-based or randomly porous scaffolds often fail to provide spatial cues for collagen organization, resulting in inferior regenerated tissue [55,56]. In this study, the anisotropic microtopography of the PCL scaffold guided ECM deposition and promoted ordered collagen assembly within scaffold-embedded saECM-M, thereby supporting formation of a structurally coherent fibrocartilaginous matrix and better chondroprotection. Interestingly, the PCL-cpd-ECM group showed a higher WORMS score than the PCL-MFCs group, which may reflect the reduced bioactivity and altered matrix properties of cpd-ECM after decellularization, digestion, and crosslinking. In addition, SEM suggested that cpd-ECM was mainly distributed as particulate deposits rather than as a continuous layer covering the scaffold surface, which may have limited its ability to functionally modify the scaffold. However, this interpretation remains speculative and requires further study.

Our results further demonstrate that integrating a 3D-printed biomimetic scaffold with an intrinsically bioactive, cell-driven ECM membrane preserves bioactivity while simplifying fabrication. The complementary strengths of synthetic polymers and cell-derived ECM represent a promising strategy for meniscal and cartilage repair [57,58]. Compared with dECM-based surface modification, which often involves extensive decellularization, reprocessing, and potential bioactivity loss [59,60], our approach generates a native ECM membrane in situ, retains meniscus-specific collagenous components, and forms a seamless scaffold–matrix interface. This self-assembly process avoids decellularization, encapsulation, and harsh chemical or enzymatic treatments, thereby enhancing translational feasibility.

Recent studies indicate that the field is moving beyond single-function biomaterials toward integrated regenerative systems combining biologically active components, mechanically optimized scaffolds, and microenvironmental regulation [[61], [62], [63], [64]]. Similar self-assembled or ECM-rich cell-derived constructs have also been explored using chondrocytes, mesenchymal stem cells, and fibroblast-related cell sheet systems for cartilage, skin, and other tissue repair applications [[65], [66], [67]], supporting the broader regenerative value of self-organized ECM constructs as biologically active microenvironments. In this context, our PCL-saECM-M strategy fits within this emerging trend by integrating a meniscus-specific bioactive matrix with a biomimetic scaffold that provides structural and topological guidance for organized regeneration.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to establish an MFC-derived self-assembled ECM membrane as an intrinsically bioactive matrix for meniscus defect repair and scaffold-guided whole-meniscus reconstruction using a biomimetic 3D-printed PCL scaffold. By preserving native ECM architecture and bioactivity while avoiding complex material modification, this strategy addresses limited cell migration, vascular ingrowth, and disorganized matrix remodeling. Scaffold-free saECM-M enhanced tissue integration and matrix deposition, whereas the anisotropic PCL framework further guided aligned collagen organization and mechanical reinforcement. Overall, this saECM-M-based strategy offers clear advantages over cpd-ECM approaches and represents a practical solution for meniscal repair and replacement. Despite these encouraging results, clinical translation will require scalable and standardized saECM-M manufacturing, including robust production protocols and quality-control standards. In addition, the current scaffold, optimized for the rabbit meniscus, will require further humanization in material, geometry, and mechanical properties. Regarding cell sourcing, an allogeneic meniscal fibrochondrocyte strategy may be more practical than an autologous approach because it is more amenable to standardized large-scale production, especially for patients with substantial meniscal loss. Nevertheless, further studies are needed to directly compare autologous and allogeneic routes in terms of feasibility, safety, and translational applicability.

While this study demonstrates the promising outcomes, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. The mechanistic insights into saECM-M-enhanced MFC migration and vascularization were primarily derived from in vitro omics analyses and their consistency with the observed regenerative outcomes. However, these findings have not yet been sufficiently validated at the in vivo level. Therefore, the mechanistic interpretation of the current study should be considered preliminary, and further in vivo studies are needed to verify the specific roles of the identified pathways during meniscus regeneration. Additionally, although rabbits are a well-established model for meniscal reconstruction, anatomical and biomechanical differences exist between rabbit and human knees. Future work will explore translation in larger animal models and refine the scaffold architecture and cell-culture conditions for human-scale implants.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed a MFCs-derived, self-assembled ECM membrane (saECM-M) as an intrinsically bioactive meniscus-specific matrix capable of promoting cell migration, vascular ingrowth, and collagen deposition. The isolated saECM-M demonstrated superior reparative potential in vivo for meniscus defect scaffold-free repair compared with cpd-ECM. Furthermore, when integrated with a 3D-printed biomimetic PCL scaffold, saECM-M enabled guided collagen alignment and mechanical reinforcement, resulting in robust whole-meniscus reconstruction and improved preservation of articular cartilage. By minimizing harsh chemical processing while preserving native matrix architecture and bioactivity, this cell-driven saECM-M strategy reduces manufacturing complexity and biosafety risks, providing a practical, safe, and clinically translatable approach for both meniscus repair and replacement therapies.
