The Economic Impact of Cirrhosis and Other Chronic Liver Diseases on Global, Regional, and National Economies
Authors: Ji Li, Yifeng Cheng, Yongli Pan, Xue Liu, Xiaoying Gan, Xiaoqing Pan, Yali Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
INTRODUCTION:To understand the macroeconomic impact of cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases globally, regionally, and nationally, facilitating rational resource allocation. The authors investigated the macroeconomic consequences associated with the burden of cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases across 199 countries in 2021.METHODS:Data on disability-adjusted life years for cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases, including subtypes such as chronic hepatitis B, chronic hepatitis C, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (including cirrhosis), cirrhosis due to alcohol, and cirrhosis from other causes, were derived from the 2021 Global Burden of Disease study database. Gross domestic product (GDP) data, adjusted by purchasing power parity, were obtained from the World Bank. The study integrated GDP and disability-adjusted life year data and applied the value of lost welfare approach to quantify macroeconomic losses. All results are reported in 2021 international dollars, adjusted for purchasing power parity.RESULTS:In 2021, global value of lost welfare from cirrhosis and chronic liver diseases reached $365.13 billion (0.38% of GDP), with subtypes varying chronic hepatitis B (0.18%, $168.41 billion), hepatitis C (0.08%, $77.09 billion), alcohol-related cirrhosis (0.06%, $58.01 billion), and others. Regional disparities emerged: High-income regions showed the highest overall (3.60%) and hepatitis B (1.42%) ratios, Central/Eastern Europe/Central Asia led in alcohol (0.19%) and other causes (0.08%), Latin America/Caribbean in fatty liver (0.07%), and North Africa/Middle East in hepatitis C (0.13%).DISCUSSION:These diseases impose significant macroeconomic burdens globally and by subtype, justifying prioritized healthcare resource allocation to improve patient outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

In 2021, cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases were associated with 2.1% of all global deaths. That year, the global incidence of cirrhosis reached 5.84 million, the prevalence was 170 million, and the number of deaths was approximately 143,000. The resulting disease burden—46 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs)—positions cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases among the leading contributors to the global disease burden (1,2). This burden is also associated with significant losses in productivity (3). Data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study indicate that the incidence of noncommunicable liver diseases, such as metabolic-associated liver disease and alcohol-related liver disease, continues to rise (4,5).Combined with the accelerating trend of population aging, the next 2 decades are expected to witness a marked increase in decompensation events, hepatocellular carcinoma incidence, and liver-related mortality associated with cirrhosis (1,6). In this context, integrating epidemiological surveillance data on cirrhosis with macroeconomic indicators is critical for developing a data-driven framework for optimal resource allocation.

The standardized surveillance system established by the GBD study provides a systematic depiction of the global and regional epidemiological landscape of cirrhosis, including dynamic trends in incidence, mortality, and DALYs (7). The 2021 GBD study expanded its analytical scope to include detailed assessments of 5 major disease subtypes: chronic hepatitis B including cirrhosis, chronic hepatitis C including cirrhosis, cirrhosis due to alcohol, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease including cirrhosis, and cirrhosis due to other causes. These evaluations provide critical evidence to inform the development of targeted prevention and intervention strategies (8,9). However, it is noteworthy that current evaluations of the macroeconomic losses caused by cirrhosis are characterized by significant fragmentation. Most existing studies at the regional or national level are limited to the calculation of direct medical costs and lack systematic assessment of productivity losses, household caregiving burdens, and broader socioeconomic repercussions. Moreover, a standardized framework for estimating economic burden across regions and disease subtypes remains undeveloped (10–12). This data gap directly constrains the ability to prioritize cirrhosis prevention and control within health policy, particularly in critical areas such as resource allocation optimization and cross-sectoral intervention, where evidence-based decision-making remains insufficient. Therefore, there is a pressing need to establish a global macroeconomic impact model that encompasses the full disease spectrum, including subtype classifications, to quantify the long-term effects of cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases on gross domestic product (GDP) growth, social security systems, and household economic resilience. Such a model would serve as a standardized tool for cross-country cost-effectiveness analysis, thereby supporting more informed global health governance.

The value of lost welfare (VLW) analysis is an increasingly sophisticated assessment tool that enables the quantification of economic losses caused by disease burden using a standardized methodology (13–15). This method integrates key concepts from DALYs and the value of a statistical life (VSL), where VSL reflects an individual's willingness to pay to reduce a specific risk of death. By combining these 2 metrics, researchers can systematically assess the multidimensional macroeconomic impact of specific diseases. Notably, the inclusion of VSL enables the VLW model to capture not only economic losses within traditional market settings but also the economic value of nonmarket services while reflecting individuals' perceived value of health itself (16). Owing to its demonstrated strengths in comprehensive benefit assessment, the World Health Organization (WHO) explicitly endorses the use of willingness-to-pay frameworks in health economic modeling. Given its integrative nature, WHO advocates for the inclusion of approaches such as the VLW in macroeconomic health modeling to enable a more holistic evaluation of the long-term economic impact of disease (17).

This study uses the DALY (18), an indicator from the GBD study to systematically evaluate the economic losses caused by cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases in 199 countries (including high, middle, and low-income countries) in 2021. Through standardized analytical techniques, the macroeconomic impact of the disease across countries at varying levels of development was quantified, offering essential economic insights to inform the formulation of global liver disease prevention and control strategies.

METHODS

Data sources

Data supporting the results of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Using the GBD study database, DALY data related to cirrhosis for 2021 were obtained because of the unavailability of more recent DALY data. The GBD study adopts a broad definition for cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases, including ICD-10 codes K70.0–K70.4, K70.9 (K70 = alcoholic liver disease), K74.0–K74.6 (K74 = hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis, nonalcoholic), K76.0–K76.2, K76.7 (K76 = other liver diseases, partially incorporated into the GBD chronic liver disease statistics), K71.7 (K71 = toxic liver disease, chronic damage from drugs/chemicals), K72.1, K72.9 (K72 = liver failure, chronic or subacute), K75.4 (K75 = other inflammatory liver diseases), K76.6 (K76 = other liver diseases, partial subcategories), B18.0–B18.2, B18.9 (B18 = chronic viral hepatitis), E83.0, E83.7 (E83 = metabolic disorder-related liver diseases), and E88.01 (E88 = other metabolic disorders) (Supplementary Table 4 [http://links.lww.com/CTG/B499]). DALY data for 199 countries/regions, age-standardized per 100,000 individuals annually, were obtained. GDP data for each country and per capita GDP data (adjusted for purchasing power parity [PPP] to 2021 US dollars) were obtained from the World Bank's World Development Indicators database (19). All results are reported in 2021 international dollars adjusted for PPP.

The GBD study uses a classification system dividing countries into 7 super-regions to enable standardized comparative analysis. These super-regions include (i) Central and Eastern Europe and Central Asia; (ii) high-income countries; (iii) Latin America and the Caribbean; (iv) North Africa and the Middle East; (v) South Asia; (vi) Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Oceania; and (vii) Sub-Saharan Africa. This classification system enables a systematic assessment of disease burden characteristics across different socioeconomic and geographical contexts (18).

Calculation of VLW

The estimation of the VSL was based on empirical evidence reported in previous studies. At the time of this research, the application of VSL was largely confined to specific population subgroups within high-income countries (16,17). This limitation mainly reflected the relatively abundant resources available in these countries, which supported comprehensive data collection, advanced statistical modeling, and economic evaluations related to life valuation. Furthermore, high-income countries demonstrated greater societal and policy-level acceptance of incorporating quantified life values into decision-making processes.

For many countries and regions, achieving internationally comparable estimates of disease-related economic burden using VSL required the establishment of a standardized assessment framework and its adaptation to different economic contexts. In this study, we adopted a widely used approach by anchoring the calculation to benchmark VSL estimates reported by the US Department of Transportation and subsequently extending this framework for global application. This strategy enabled the use of a unified standard to estimate and compare the economic burden of diseases across countries. The calculation was performed using the following equations:VLW=DALYs(0−100)×VLSY(0−100)VLSY=(VSLpeak,i×r)/(1−(1+r)−L)VSLpeak,i=VSLpeak,USA×(GDPi/GDPUSA)IE

Within this framework, the VLW model incorporated the concept of VSL to quantify economic losses attributable to disease burden. VSL reflects that the maximum amount individuals are willing to pay for marginal reductions in mortality risk and serves as a core indicator for evaluating disease-related macroeconomic losses. For the primary analysis, an income elasticity (IE) of 1.0 was applied, and VSL estimates derived from US Department of Transportation data were used for cross-country extrapolation. This approach facilitated the harmonization of VSL estimates across different national settings. To account for uncertainty in income-related willingness to pay, sensitivity analyses were additionally conducted using alternative IE values of 0.55 and 1.5 (Supplementary Tables 2 [http://links.lww.com/CTG/B497] and 3 [http://links.lww.com/CTG/B498]).

The parameter VSL_peak represents the highest willingness to pay for mortality risk reduction within a population, which typically occurs during middle adulthood. To estimate age-specific VSL year values and adjust for variations across the life course, an age-adjustment function f(a) was introduced. This function modifies VSL_peak to obtain VSLa for a given age by incorporating life-stage–specific willingness-to-pay patterns and applying appropriate weighting across different ages.

Previous research examining capital market constraints has shown that younger individuals with lower income levels often lack access to borrowing mechanisms, limiting their ability to smooth labor market risks and resulting in constrained VSL estimates during early adulthood. Conversely, older individuals, who may have insufficient accumulated assets to buffer risk exposure, tend to select lower risk premiums than younger counterparts, leading to reduced VSL values. In the final step, age-specific DALYs were multiplied by the corresponding VSL year values, aggregated across age groups, and adjusted to 2021 US dollars using PPP conversion factors to derive the overall VLW estimates.

All statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio IDE (RStudio, PBC, Boston, MA), and the study adhered to the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards guidelines.

Ethics statement

This study is based on publicly available, nationally aggregated data. Given this data characteristic, the Institutional Review Board made an official determination in accordance with the Belmont Report and established ethical guidelines: Because the study does not involve individual identification or direct human intervention, it qualifies for an exemption under the “nonhuman subjects research” standard. Therefore, it is exempt from the conventional ethical requirements for human subject research, such as informed consent or minimal risk management.

RESULTS

The highest VLW-to-GDP ratio for cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases was recorded in high-income regions (VLW/GDP = 0.360%; VLW = USD 202.25 billion) and the Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Oceania super-region (VLW/GDP = 0.062%; VLW = USD 58.97 billion). The VLW-to-GDP ratio for cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases in the Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and Central Asia super-region was 0.038% (VLW = USD 36.75 billion). DISCUSSION

Based on existing epidemiological data supporting (2,8), this study further confirms the substantial impact of cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases on global health systems. Our findings indicate that in 2021, these diseases resulted in global direct economic losses reaching USD 365 billion. Importantly, this magnitude is broadly consistent with prior global assessments of liver disease burden while providing additional insights from a willingness-to-pay perspective. By quantifying the economic burden using the value of statistical life (VSL) framework, our estimates extend beyond conventional cost-based approaches and offer complementary evidence to inform health resource allocation, optimization of primary prevention strategies, and prioritization of public health interventions. Such evidence is crucial for improving quality of care, enhancing patient outcomes, and advancing global liver disease control efforts.

The highest VLW/GDP ratio for overall cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases was recorded in the following super-regions: (i) high-income regions and (ii) Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Oceania. Notably, the VLW/GDP ratio for overall cirrhosis and other chronic liver disease subtypes in the high-income super-region falls below the global average. This contrast reinforces the systemic challenges faced by middle- and low-income economies in the allocation of liver disease-related healthcare resources (20–22). A detailed analysis identifies several critical gaps in disease management within these regions: from insufficient screening for alcohol dependence in early-risk and high-risk community populations, low coverage of metabolic syndrome health education (23,24), to poor early recognition of severe symptoms (25), and even inadequate rehabilitation support networks (26). This multifaceted healthcare resource gap not only leads to high patient readmission rates but also contributes to a vicious cycle of economic burden through workforce loss and long-term care expenses (27,28).

In the global context, the economic impact of each subtype of cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases is consistent with the findings from previous GBD studies (2,8). Among them, chronic hepatitis B including cirrhosis accounts for the highest economic impact, followed by chronic hepatitis C including cirrhosis, cirrhosis due to alcohol, cirrhosis due to other causes, and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease including cirrhosis. However, when focusing on different super-regions, there exists a considerable variation in the economic consequences caused by cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases. For chronic hepatitis B including cirrhosis, the VLW/GDP in the high-income super-region (0.142%) is the lowest among all super-regions. For chronic hepatitis C including cirrhosis, the VLW/GDP in the high-income super-region (0.0085%) ranks sixth among all super-regions, comparable to middle- and low-income super-regions. For cirrhosis due to alcohol, the VLW/GDP in the high-income super-region (0.0064%) ranks fourth among all super-regions, while in the Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and Central Asia super-region, the VLW/GDP (0.195%) is the highest among all super-regions. For nonalcoholic fatty liver disease including cirrhosis, the VLW/GDP in the high-income super-region (0.0032%) ranks third, while in the Latin America, North Africa, and Middle East super-regions, the VLW/GDP (0.072%) ranks the highest among all super-regions. These patterns are consistent with evidence of increased risks of alcoholic liver disease and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease in middle- and low-income country subgroups, possibly due to the distribution of alcohol consumption and the increasing burden of metabolic syndrome and obesity in the global population (24,29).

Compared with alternative economic evaluation frameworks, such as the human capital approach or cost-of-illness models commonly used in WHO economic burden reports (29), the VSL-based VLW estimates presented in this study tend to yield higher absolute values. This discrepancy reflects fundamental methodological differences rather than inconsistencies in disease burden ranking. The human capital approach primarily captures productivity losses due to morbidity and premature mortality and may underestimate societal losses by excluding unpaid labor, informal care, and individuals outside the formal workforce. By contrast, the VSL framework incorporates individuals' willingness to pay for mortality risk reduction, capturing both market and nonmarket components of health value. As a result, although WHO and cost-based estimates are more conservative and suitable for fiscal planning, VSL-based estimates provide a broader welfare-oriented perspective that may be more informative for long-term public health priority setting (30). Importantly, despite differences in absolute magnitude, the relative ranking of disease subtypes and regional patterns in our study remains consistent with prior economic analyses, supporting the robustness of our findings.

To further improve policy relevance, a more granular classification of cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases is warranted. Taking chronic hepatitis B-related cirrhosis in the United States as an example, this condition has been associated with macroeconomic losses exceeding USD 100 billion (31). Evidence suggests that nearly half of these patients experience decompensated liver disease (32). However, there is currently a lack of accurate nationwide data regarding liver function decompensation. Considering that liver transplantation is a critical treatment for end-stage cirrhosis and that untreated liver decompensation often results in devastating outcomes for patients, more precise assessments of liver decompensation from both the disease burden (e.g., DALY) and macroeconomic perspectives are of paramount importance. Such assessments are essential to design an effective regional liver disease prevention and control framework and optimize the allocation of limited healthcare resources.

Cirrhosis is primarily caused by chronic hepatitis infections (mainly hepatitis B virus [HBV] and hepatitis C virus [HCV]), nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, and alcohol abuse, which accounts for 98.9% of cirrhosis cases (33). The risk factors for cirrhosis are preventable through medical treatment or lifestyle interventions. Simulation models suggest that achieving a 65% reduction in HBV and HCV infection rates, alongside a 10% reduction in alcohol consumption in 2019, could theoretically lead to a reduction of nearly 392.15 million cirrhosis cases globally (34). Notably, the primary causes and high-risk populations for cirrhosis differ by region, and this variation necessitates that countries adopt region-specific intervention strategies (2,35). In hepatitis-endemic regions, emphasis should be placed on increasing HBV vaccination coverage and expanding accessibility to effective HCV treatments (36,37). In regions with growing problems of obesity or alcohol abuse, innovative policies and interventions are urgently needed to tackle these issues (38,39). We recommend that future cost-effectiveness evaluations of interventions for specific cirrhosis subtypes be conducted. This not only aligns with the WHO's “Precision Public Health” strategy but also, through accurate cost-effectiveness assessments, can help policymakers make informed region-specific decisions. It will enable governments and international organizations to allocate resources effectively and promote the development and implementation of cost-effective interventions to more effectively address the economic impacts of cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases, thereby improving cirrhosis prevention and treatment at the global, regional, and national levels (40).

This study seeks to address important topics in the relevant research field. After an in-depth analysis of the research content, we deem it essential to outline its limitations. First, the study faces limitations in data acquisition, mainly relying on modeling rather than empirical data. For instance, VSL estimates for various countries are derived through models based on empirical data from the United States, and such a transformation may not fully capture regional differences. In the willingness-to-pay assumptions after adjusting for economic scale and purchasing power, an IE of 1.0 was ultimately selected, which may pose its own limitations. Moreover, owing to the lack of high-quality epidemiological studies on cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases in most countries, the DALY data provided by the GBD study are largely based on modeling. Notably, although the 2021 GBD study provided epidemiological data for 5 subtypes of cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases for the first time, the authors recommend further segmentation to improve accuracy in reflecting the epidemiology related to cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases. These limitations suggest that future research should focus more on data diversity and accuracy to address the shortcomings of this study.

This study presents the first thorough analysis of the global economic impact of cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases and their subtypes, covering global, regional, and national levels. Given the substantial economic burden imposed by liver disease and the existing cost-effective interventions, we hope that these findings can serve as a robust basis for advocating increased healthcare investments, particularly in middle- and low-income countries where the economic impact of liver disease is most severe. These countries often face a higher disease burden but relatively insufficient resource allocation. Therefore, by presenting the severity of the economic impact through data, we seek to facilitate the reallocation of resources toward these priority areas.

Cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases have become a global economic burden, affecting the global, regional, and national economies in several ways, from healthcare resource consumption to labor force loss and social security pressure. The complexity of diagnosing and treating different disease subtypes, along with prognosis variations, further amplifies both the depth and scope of the economic consequences. Quantitative research confirms that increasing resource investment can effectively improve patient survival rates and reduce complication-related healthcare expenditures, leading to substantial health economic benefits. However, owing to the uneven distribution of pathogenic factors, such as viral hepatitis, alcohol abuse, and metabolic syndrome, across regions, along with marked differences in healthcare accessibility and economic development, strategies for resource allocation must be designed based on the epidemiological characteristics of the disease and the region's economic and healthcare infrastructure. By using early screening, health education, and targeted healthcare interventions, the risk of disease progression can be reduced, achieving a win-win situation in both economic and health benefits.
