Multi-objective optimization of corrugated microperforated panels for broadband sound absorption using adaptive sampling method
Authors: Zhengping Wu, Lu Ean Ooi, Yuanbo Liu, Pengwei Lyu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corrugated micro-perforated panels (CMPPs) under limited-space installation conditions exhibit complex acoustic behavior due to strong multi-parameter coupling, which makes broadband and high-efficiency sound absorption design challenging. To address this issue, this study proposes a Kriging-based DEU adaptive sampling multi-objective optimization strategy for CMPP absorbers. The sound absorption coefficients of the sample points are calculated by finite element simulations and used for surrogate modeling and optimization. Based on a limited initial sample set generated by LHS, the proposed DEU method performs directional enrichment to improve surrogate model accuracy efficiently. Compared with conventional LHS sampling, the DEU method achieves higher surrogate modeling efficiency, better optimization performance, and improved predictive reliability with fewer samples. MOPSO is then employed to obtain the Pareto front of total absorption area and effective absorption area, and the knee-point configuration is selected as the optimal design. Experimental validation further confirms the effectiveness and practical potential of the proposed strategy for broadband and high-efficiency sound absorption design of CMPP absorbers.

Introduction

With the increasing demand for noise reduction in both industrial settings and daily life1, considerable attention has been directed toward the sound-absorbing structures capable of achieving broadband acoustic performance. Micro-perforated panels (MPPs) are widely used due to the simple structure, stable performance, and long service life2–5. However, conventional flat MPP sound absorbers are governed by local resonance, which results in effective sound absorption only within a relatively narrow frequency band6.

In recent years, corrugated micro-perforated panels (CMPPs) have attracted growing interest. The corrugated structure introduces a non-uniform air cavity depth distribution at the panel level, offering a new way to broaden the absorption bandwidth, as compared to traditional modifications of cavity geometries7–14. Wang and Liu15 proposes sinusoidal CMPPs and find that the corrugation-induced coupling between multiple cavity modes leads to better absorption over nearly the entire frequency range than flat MPPs with the same parameters, especially under large or random incidence angles. Yang et al.16,17 further extends the corrugation from sinusoidal to free-form profiles, obtaining additional enhancements. Different from the aforementioned studies, which mainly focused on the sound absorption characteristics of CMPPs under infinitely large or idealized boundary conditions, Wu et al.18 further investigates the sound absorption behavior of sinusoidal CMPPs with different phase-controlled shapes under limited-space and finite-boundary conditions. In that study, two CMPP geometries corresponding to typical phase parameters are selected, and a finite element (FE)-based theoretical model is established to analyze the sound absorption performance of CMPPs in limited spaces. The sound absorption behaviors of the two structures are then systematically compared. The results show that, under finite-boundary conditions, the CMPP with a more pronounced cavity-depth variation along the wavelength direction exhibites better overall sound absorption performance in the mid-to-high frequency range, together with a flatter absorption trough, thereby broadening the effective absorption bandwidth. This difference can be attributed to the fact that variations in the local acoustic field distribution over the panel surface affect the coupling conditions between the perforated panel and the backing cavity, thereby promoting the excitation and participation of more higher-order modes, which in turn leads to differences in the sound absorption characteristics of the two structures. These studies demonstrate the significant potential of CMPPs for broadband absorption.

In practical noise control engineering, absorbers are often required to deliver high performance over specific target frequency ranges. The acoustic behavior of MPPs is influenced by multiple coupled factors, including the geometry of the perforations19,20, panel parameters21, and cavity configuration22,23. As a result, the design process becomes a multi-parameter, multi-objective optimization challenge. For flat MPPs, acoustic performance can be reasonably estimated using Maa’s theory24,25. In contrast, CMPPs introduce additional geometric variables such as corrugation depth, wavelength, and profile shape. These features lead to non-uniform cavity-depth distributions and trigger multiple localized resonances, resulting in far more complex acoustic mechanisms. For limited-space CMPP absorbers in particular, developing accurate analytical models for such configurations is difficult, and their acoustic responses are typically obtained through computationally expensive numerical simulations15–17, which further increases the cost of optimization.

To reduce the computational burden associated with high-dimensional, simulation-based optimization, surrogate modeling has become an effective strategy in recent MPP research. For example, Hashemi et al.26 demonstrated the usefulness of Response Surface Methodology (RSM) in optimizing MPPs. Support Vector Regression (SVR)27, generalized regression neural networks (GRNN)28, and Kriging models29 have also been widely adopted in acoustic metamaterial design. Among these, Kriging is particularly advantageous for black-box optimization because it provides unbiased optimal estimates and quantifies prediction uncertainty.

The distribution of initial sample points is crucial for constructing accurate surrogate models. Static sampling methods such as Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) and Central Composite Design (CCD) determine samples a priori based on engineering experience and remain widely used26,30. To further improve space-filling properties, Wang et al.31 proposed another static method, the OLHS method, which achieves a more uniform distribution of design variables within the finite design domain. However, as engineering problems continue to evolve toward multi-dimensional and high-dimensional settings, the number of samples required to build an accurate surrogate model increases sharply and becomes difficult to predict, revealing the inherent limitations of static sampling strategies. Consequently, adaptive sampling methods32 have attracted increasing attention and have been progressively developed to improve surrogate model accuracy and efficiency. Li et al.33 introduced a local approximation method based on the most-likely point as the sampling center, in which local sampling is performed around the reliability constraint boundary to improve optimization efficiency. Garud et al.34 proposed a smart sampling algorithm that combines the sample crowding metric and the surrogate departure function to determine new sample locations, thereby considering both spatial coverage and surrogate representation quality. Xu et al.35 quantified the sparsity of new samples and the variation of local responses simultaneously based on distance density and local complexity. Although the above methods can improve the efficiency of surrogate model construction from different perspectives, their sampling criteria mainly focus on local constraint boundaries, spatial distribution, or local response variation characteristics. Under limited sample conditions, the surrogate model may still have relatively high prediction uncertainty in some regions of the design space, while existing methods provide relatively limited consideration of the synergistic relationship among spatial coverage, local response characteristics, and model confidence. For multi-parameter, strongly coupled, and computationally expensive optimization problems, relying only on a single or limited type of sampling information may make it difficult to simultaneously ensure sample distribution uniformity, key-region identification, and prediction reliability. Therefore, the proposed adaptive sampling method in this study incorporates distance density, local complexity, and surrogate-model prediction uncertainty into a unified dynamic enrichment criterion, thereby achieving a better balance among global exploration, local refinement, and model uncertainty control.

The absorption performance of the CMPP depends on the nonlinear coupling of the corrugation profile and other based parameters. Traditional parameter sweeping is time-consuming and may fail to find the global optimum. Various intelligent optimization algorithms have therefore been introduced. Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)36,37, Simulated Annealing (SA)38, Genetic Algorithms (GA)24, and Cuckoo Search (CS)39 have been used for single-objective sound absorption structure optimization, although their efficiency may decrease as the dimensionality and simulation cost increase. In recent years, multi-objective evolutionary algorithms have become important tools for complex engineering structural optimization because they can search for Pareto-optimal solutions among multiple competing design objectives. Zhao et al.40 conducted multi-objective structural parameter optimization of a nonlinear X-shaped isolator based on NSGA-II, demonstrating the applicability of this type of algorithm to multi-parameter, strongly coupled, and nonlinear structural optimization problems. Yao et al.41 adopted the Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II) to optimize reinforced structural configurations with various cross-sectional forms, achieving an optimal compromise between sound insulation performance and structural weight within the 400–3150 Hz frequency range. Ma et al.42 employed a Kriging-assisted Multiobjective Evolutionary Algorithm based on Decomposition (MOEA/D) for aerodynamic stability optimization of lifting bodies, and demonstrated the strong robustness of this surrogate-model-assisted multi-objective optimization method through comparison with NSGA-II. Chen et al.43 further applied the Multiobjective Particle Swarm Optimization (MOPSO) algorithm to identify polyurethane composite formulations that simultaneously exhibit high sound absorption and sound insulation capabilities. Zhang et al.44 applied MOPSO to the mixed-variable multi-objective optimization design of MPP acoustic structures, addressing the coupling between discrete variables such as the number of layers and continuous variables such as structural geometric parameters, and obtained multiple non-dominated design solutions. These studies indicate that multi-objective optimization algorithms have clear advantages in handling multi-parameter coupling and trade-offs among multiple performance objectives. Meanwhile, surrogate-model-assisted multi-objective optimization methods provide an effective route for reducing the computational cost of complex optimization problems. However, under limited-space conditions, the corrugated profile of CMPP sound-absorbing structures is strongly coupled with the micro-perforation parameters, leading to more nonlinear acoustic responses. Therefore, a multi-objective optimization method capable of effectively searching the Pareto front is required to identify a reasonable compromise among different sound absorption performance objectives. MOPSO has the advantages of relatively simple parameter settings, high search efficiency for continuous design variables, and good Pareto front exploration capability, making it suitable for the optimization of complex CMPP absorbers involving multiple performance trade-offs.

Compared with conventional flat MPPs, CMPPs exhibit superior broadband sound absorption performance15,16,18. In particular, Wu et al.18 further indicated that, considering the diversity of practical installation spaces and boundary conditions in engineering applications, differences in the panel shape of CMPPs under limited-space and finite-boundary conditions may lead to significantly different sound absorption behaviors. For micro-perforated panel structures with more complex profiles, the influence of shape variation on their sound absorption characteristics remains to be further clarified. Therefore, optimizing the sound absorption performance of CMPPs under limited-space installation conditions is of considerable importance for engineering applications and also requires corresponding quantitative structural design methods. However, due to the significant coupling effects among multiple structural parameters, and because the corrugated configuration further introduces additional geometric variables such as corrugation depth and wavelength, the increased dimensionality of the design variables substantially enhances the complexity of the optimization design space. Under such circumstances, the sample points required for full-parameter optimization must not only be sufficient in number, but also be able to effectively capture significant local variations in the acoustic response, so as to ensure the predictive accuracy of the subsequent surrogate model. In particular, when the influence of boundary shape is taken into account, three-dimensional numerical models are usually required to predict the sound absorption performance18, which further increases the computational cost of sample acquisition during the optimization process. Meanwhile, existing optimization studies on CMPP absorbers remain limited, and the available related works have generally followed the optimization framework of flat MPPs by simplifying the number of design variables considered15,16. However, whether the existing optimization strategies developed for flat MPPs are applicable to CMPP absorber optimization in limited spaces with multiple participating parameters remains insufficiently verified.Therefore, it is necessary to develop a systematic and efficient optimization strategy for CMPP absorber structures with highly nonlinear cavity profiles and a larger number of design variables.

To address the challenges associated with strong multi-parameter coupling, high optimization cost, and the difficulty of simultaneously achieving broadband and high-efficiency sound absorption for CMPP absorber structures under limited-space installation conditions, this study proposes a multi-objective adaptive sampling optimization strategy for the CMPPs. Based on an experimentally validated FE model, the proposed framework combines an improved adaptive dynamic enrichment algorithm, Kriging surrogate models, and the MOPSO algorithm to enable efficient exploration of the complex design space and to obtain a reasonable trade-off between broadband absorption capability and high-efficiency absorption performance. Meanwhile, the proposed adaptive sampling method is further compared with the conventional static LHS method to evaluate its performance in terms of sampling efficiency, surrogate model robustness, and predictive reliability of the optimization results. This study provides a feasible technical route for efficient surrogate modeling with limited samples and multi-objective optimal design of complex CMPP absorber structures in limited spaces.

Construction of the Kriging surrogate model based on the DEU adaptive sampling method

This study employs the Kriging surrogate model combining the FEM to approximate the acoustic behavior of the CMPP. The model is developed using the proposed DEU adaptive sampling method, which automatically adjusts sampling locations according to the current prediction accuracy. The resulting Kriging model can accurately predict the objective values for the total absorption area (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}_{\mathrm{t}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{t}}$$\end{document}) and the effective absorption area (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}_{0.5}$$\end{document}) and improve the optimization efficiency of the CMPP within the five-dimensional design space, providing a reliable response evaluator for the subsequent multi-objective optimization. To show the advantage of the DEU method, the Kriging surrogate model is compared with that constructed based on the conventional static sampling method.

Latin hypercube sampling design

LHS ensures that each design variable is uniformly sampled over its prescribed range by employing a stratified random sampling strategy. Therefore, the five structural parameter variables for the parametric study in Sect. 2.3 are treated as optimization variables to investigate the coupled acoustic response arising from the interaction among CMPP design variables, which is essential for facilitating practical engineering applications. The initial sample points in the multi-dimensional design space are generated using the LHS experimental design method implemented in the math software MATLAB31.

For a design space with dimension M, the minimum number of samples is generally required. However, the proposed DEU adaptive sampling method in this work does not rely on the conventional static sampling method. Instead, it starts from a uniformly distributed design generated by the LHS method and subsequently introduces new samples points through the DEU method. Accordingly, an initial sample set with a relatively small size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{N}}_{0}$$\end{document} is first adopted depending on the nonlinearity of the problem under investigation, satisfying \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:2M\le\:{N}_{0}\le\:5M\:$$\end{document}[35]. In this study, the initial sample set size is chosen as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{N}_{0}=5M=25$$\end{document}, and the acoustic absorption coefficient responses of all the sample points are obtained using the FEM.

Constructing of the Kriging surrogate model

Owing to the ability to accurately approximate complex nonlinear responses and to provide explicit estimates of predictive uncertainty, a Kriging surrogate model is constructed to predict the acoustic responses of the CMPP structure within the design variables spaces46.

Within the Kriging framework, the acoustic response of a point is represented as the superposition of a global trend component and a local stochastic process component:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\boldsymbol{Y}\left(\boldsymbol{X}\right)={f\left(\boldsymbol{X}\right)}^{T}\beta\:+Z\left(\boldsymbol{X}\right),$$\end{document}

where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:f\left(\boldsymbol{X}\right)$$\end{document} denotes the trend basis (taken as a constant term in this work), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document} is the corresponding regression coefficient vector. To account for local variations that cannot be captured by the global trend, the stochastic term is modeled using a Gaussian process. In this work, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Z\left(\boldsymbol{X}\right)$$\end{document} is assumed to be a zero-mean Gaussian process, and the local deviations between different sample points are quantified through an appropriate covariance function. Considering that the five design variables of CMPP (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d,{t}_{0}, H, h, \sigma$$\end{document}) have distinct physical scales, an anisotropic squared-exponential (Gaussian) correlation function is adopted47 to model the covariance structure.

Given the sample set inputs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{X}=(\:{\boldsymbol{X}}_{1}\:,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{2}\:,\:\dots\:,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{{N}_{s}}\:)$$\end{document} and the corresponding acoustic responses \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{Y}=({\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{y}}_{2},\dots\:,{\:\boldsymbol{y}}_{{N}_{s}})$$\end{document}, the predicted response at a new point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}$$\end{document} is obtained as a linear combination of the sampled responses:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{\boldsymbol{Y}}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)=\sum\:_{n=1}^{{N}_{s}}{\theta\:}_{n}{\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)\boldsymbol{y}}_{n},$$\end{document}

where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{n}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document} denotes the weighting coefficient associated with the n-th sample point, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{N}_{s}$$\end{document} is the number of existing samples. These coefficients are determined by enforcing the unbiasedness condition and minimizing the mean-squared prediction error.

Under these assumptions, the prediction variance at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\:$$\end{document}is given by:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Var\left[\widehat{\boldsymbol{Y}}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)\right]={\delta\:}^{2}{\complement\:}^{2}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right),$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\delta\:}^{2}$$\end{document} denotes the intrinsic variance of the stochastic process, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\complement\:}^{2}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document} represents a dimensionless function that depends on the adopted covariance function and the spatial distribution of the sample points.

A high-quality Kriging surrogate model is crucial for accelerating convergence in the CMPP optimization process. The accuracy of the Kriging surrogate model is normally assessed using the indicators, including the coefficient of determination (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}^{2}$$\end{document}), the root mean square error (RMSE), relative average absolute error (RAAE) and relative maximum absolute error (RMAE)31. These indicators mainly assess the goodness of fit to the training sample points. To evaluate the model’s generalization capability, the predictive coefficient of determination (Pred-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}^{2}$$\end{document}) is also employed to quantify its predictive reliability on unseen samples48, which is calculated through the leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) procedure35. Its definition is:

10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{Pred}} - \:R^{2} = 1 - \frac{{\sum {\:_{{k = 1}}^{{N_{s} }} } (y_{k} - \hat{y}_{k}^{{( - k)}} )^{2} }}{{\sum {\:_{{k = 1}}^{{N_{s} }} } (y_{k} - \bar{y})^{2} }},$$\end{document}

where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{\boldsymbol{y}}}_{k}^{(-k)}$$\end{document} denotes the prediction obtained by omitting the k-th sample from the sample points, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\stackrel{-}{\boldsymbol{y}}$$\end{document} is the average of the true responses.

To ensure adequate predictive accuracy, the two Kriging surrogate models for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}_{\mathrm{t}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{t}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}_{0.5}$$\end{document}, respectively, must meet the accuracy criteria \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}^{2}$$\end{document}>0.9 and Pred-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}^{2}$$\end{document}>0.9, which serve as the primary criteria for terminating the adaptive resampling process. Other indicators (RAAE, RMAE) are adopted here as auxiliary evaluation metrics rather than strict terminating criteria. Because lower RAAE and RMAE values correspond to higher predictive accuracy, their acceptable values are specified as RAAE < 0.2, and RMAE < 0.331. When the aforementioned requirements are fulfilled, the surrogate model is considered sufficiently accurate for subsequent optimization.

The DEU adaptive sampling method

To construct a high-quality surrogate model, each newly added sample should contribute effectively to improving the overall predictive accuracy. Therefore, the selection of new sampling points should simultaneously account for global space-filling, local response-surface complexity35, and regions of high predictive uncertainty in the surrogate model. From this perspective, the sampling process can better explore sparsely sampled or previously unexplored regions of the CMPP response space, capture strongly nonlinear behaviors caused by the coupling of multiple design variables, and identify subdomains where the surrogate model has low predictive confidence and potentially large prediction errors.

To select new sample points that simultaneously meet the above three requirements, a composite sampling score is defined as:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:S\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)=D\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right) \cdot \:E\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right) \cdot \:U\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right),$$\end{document}

where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{D}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document} characterizes the distance density with respect to the existing samples, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:E\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document} quantifies the local complexity of the response near the candidate point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:U\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document} measures the prediction uncertainty of the Kriging surrogate model. To eliminate the influence of different physical scales among the design variables, all distance-based calculations in this study are performed in the z-score standardized design space.

Distance density measure \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:D\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document}.

Distance density measure \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:D\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document}.

Distance density measure \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:D\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document}.

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:D\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document} is used to evaluate the uniformity of samples in the design space35. For a candidate point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}$$\end{document}, its nearest 2M neighbors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\{{\boldsymbol{X}}_{q}{\}}_{q=1}^{2M}$$\end{document} in the M-dimensional design space are first identified. In this study, candidate points that lie closer to the existing samples are assigned higher weights when calculating the distance density. The corresponding distance density formulation is expressed as:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:D\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)=\frac{2M}{\sum\:_{q=1}^{2M}\frac{1}{l({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{X}}_{q})}}\:,$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{X}}_{q}$$\end{document} denotes the candidate point’s q-th nearest neighbor, respectively. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:l({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{X}}_{q})$$\end{document} is the normalized Euclidean distance between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{X}}_{q}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:l({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{X}}_{q})\ge\:0$$\end{document}. The reciprocal-distance form gives closer neighboring samples a greater influence on the distance density value. A larger \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:D\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document} indicates that the region around \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}$$\end{document} is more sparsely sampled and therefore more valuable for model enrichment. When the closest distance satisfies \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:l\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{X}}_{1}\right)=0$$\end{document}, the candidate point coincides with an existing sample, implying that it provides no additional information for model refinement. Hence, the value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:D\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document} is regarded as 0.

(2)Local complexity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:E\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document}.

(2)Local complexity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:E\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document}.

Local complexity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:E\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document}.

Local accuracy the surrogate model requires additional sampling in key regions where the response varies rapidly, whereas smoother regions can be approximated accurately with fewer points35. To quantify the degree of local variation around a candidate point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}$$\end{document}, a local complexity indicator \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:E\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document} is introduced in this work.

Since the existing sample points \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{X}$$\end{document} and their responses \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{Y}$$\end{document} are used as inputs to the Kriging surrogate models, the surrogate prediction at these points exactly matches their true responses. For a candidate point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}$$\end{document}, its response is obtained from the surrogate prediction. The difference between the predicted response \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{\boldsymbol{Y}}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document} at the candidate point and the true responses of its 2M nearest neighboring exising samples is therefore used to characterize the rate of variation of the response in the vicinity of the candidate point. By taking the absolute value of each diviation, the local complexity is formulated as:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:E\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)=\sum\:_{q=1}^{2M}|{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\boldsymbol{q}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{Y}}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)|.$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{X}}_{q}$$\end{document} denotes the q-th nearest existing sample point of the candidate point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{y}}_{q}$$\end{document} is the true response of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{X}}_{q}$$\end{document}. A higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:E\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document} suggests that the response surface around the candidate point exhibits stronger nonlinear variation, implying that additional sampling in this region is more beneficial for improving the surrogate accuracy.

Prediction uncertainty \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:U\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document}.

Within the Kriging surrogate modeling framework, the predictive uncertainty at a candidate point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}$$\end{document} can be characterized by the predictive variance of the surrogate model. Since the present study involves a multi-objective optimization problem, the uncertainty indicators of the two objective-specific surrogate models at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}$$\end{document} are respectively defined as:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{U}_{v}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)=\sqrt{Var\left[\widehat{{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{v}}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)\right]},\:v=\mathrm{1,2}\:$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{v}}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document} denotes the predicted value of the v-th objective surrogate model at the candidate point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Var\left[\widehat{{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{v}}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)\right]$$\end{document} is the corresponding predictive variance. Since the two objectives may differ in numerical range and scale, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{U}_{1}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{U}_{2}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document} are first normalized, and then combined to construct a composite uncertainty indicator. In this study, the mean aggregation scheme is adopted, i.e.:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:U\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)=\frac{1}{2}(\stackrel{\sim}{{U}_{1}}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)+\stackrel{\sim}{{U}_{2}}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)),$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\stackrel{\sim}{{U}_{1}}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\stackrel{\sim}{{U}_{2}}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document} are the normalized uncertainty values.

For CMPP absorbers, the acoustic response surface usually exhibits strong nonlinearity due to the significant coupling among multiple design variables and the non-uniform cavity-depth distribution introduced by the corrugated configuration. As a result, pronounced local peaks and dips may appear in certain frequency ranges. Under such circumstances, relying solely on sample sparsity and local response complexity is still insufficient to comprehensively reflect the predictive reliability of the surrogate model in candidate regions. In contrast, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:U\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document} can directly characterize the confidence level of the surrogate model in a given region. A larger \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:U\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)\:$$\end{document} indicates lower predictive reliability of the model in that region, suggesting that additional samples there are more likely to improve local modeling accuracy. In the proposed method, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:U\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document} is incorporated as an uncertainty-regulation term in the enrichment criterion, together with the distance density \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:D\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document} and the local complexity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:E\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document}, to identify regions that are sparsely sampled, highly nonlinear, and insufficiently reliable in prediction, thereby improving the predictive accuracy and enrichment efficiency of the multi-objective surrogate model for CMPPs.

The DEU method based on the adaptive sampling algorithm.

To efficiently construct Kriging surrogate models with high predictive accuracy using a limited number of simulations, the DEU method based the adaptive sampling algorithm is proposed. The algorithm proposed selectively adds high-quality new samples in an informed manner, rather than uniformly expanding the design set. By combining the DEU scoring criterion \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:S\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}\right)$$\end{document} calculated by Eq. Design of multi-objective optimization based on MOPSO algorithm

Due to the introduction of the corrugation depth h, the geometric configuration of the CMPP exhibits a strong correlation with the cavity depth \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{H}$$\end{document}. The five geometric variables ( d, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{t}_{0}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{H}$$\end{document}, h, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document}) lead to highly nonlinear and strongly coupled acoustic responses of the CMPP. In addition, considering the multi-objective of achieving broadband and high-efficiency sound absorption, a surrogate-assisted MOPSO algorithm is employed to search for the optimal CMPP absorber configurations based on the Kriging surrogate models for the two optimization objectives, which have passed the prediction stability verification.

Optimization objectives and parameters restraint

In this study, to realize broadband and high-efficiency sound absorption of the CMPP within the specified frequency range, the total absorption area \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}_{\mathrm{t}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{t}}$$\end{document} within the frequency range of 200–2800 Hz and the effective absorption area \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}_{0.5}$$\end{document} corresponding to the sound absorption coefficients above 0.5 are chosen as the optimization objectives, with intervals of 5 Hz. The aim of this section is to use the MOPSO algorithm to simultaneously maximize both the total absorption area and the effective absorption area, and to identify the knee point CMPP configurations on the Pareto front, which provide the best trade-off between broadband absorption and effective high-absorption performance.

Both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}_{\mathrm{t}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{t}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}_{0.5}$$\end{document} are defined as frequency-domain integrals of the absorption coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:\left(f\right)$$\end{document}:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{{tot}} = \:\int \: _{{200}}^{{2800}} \alpha \:\left( f \right)\:df,\:A_{{0.5}} = \int \: _{{200}}^{{2800}} \max \left( {0,\:\:\alpha \:\left( f \right) - 0.5} \right)df.$$\end{document}

Parameter settings of the MOPSO algorithm

Based on the coupling acoustic characteristics of the CMPP absorber, The MOPSO algorithm is implemented with 140 particles, a maximum of 300 iterations, and an external archive capacity of 9049. Since the optimization object in this study is a CMPP sound-absorbing structure with five design variables and nonlinear constraints, a relatively sufficient swarm size and external archive capacity are beneficial for improving the coverage of the design space and maintaining the diversity of the Pareto solution set. The initial particle positions are generated using the LHS method to improve the spatial uniformity of the initial swarm distribution. The inertia weight is initialized as w = 0.92 and gradually damped by a damping factor of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{damp}$$\end{document} = 0.98. This dynamic inertia-weight strategy can enhance the global search capability of the particles in the early stage of optimization and improve the local convergence stability in the later stage50. The learning coefficients are set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{c}_{1}={c}_{2}=$$\end{document} 1.6 to balance the effects of the personal historical best position and the global guiding solution on the particle search direction, which control the attraction of particles toward their personal best positions and the global guiding solutions, respectively51. An external archive is maintained throughout the optimization process to store the non-dominated solutions and form an approximate Pareto front in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:({A}_{\mathrm{t}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{t}},{A}_{0.5})$$\end{document} objective space49. The maximum velocity is limited to 12% of the corresponding variable range, and the mutation probability is set to 0.15 to maintain population diversity and reduce premature convergence.

The above parameter settings are selected based on commonly used parameter ranges in studies and preliminary convergence tests for the present five-dimensional constrained optimization problem. To further examine the suitability of the selected parameter settings, the distributions of the archived Pareto solutions were recorded at selected iterations, and the convergence behavior was analyzed through the evolution of the Pareto front. The results show that the Pareto front gradually approached a stable distribution as the number of iterations increased, indicating that the current parameter configuration achieves a reasonable balance among convergence stability, solution diversity, and computational efficiency.

Selection of the knee point

After obtaining the Pareto-optimal solution set, the maximum perpendicular distance criterion is adopted to determine the knee point for selecting a representative compromise solution. The knee point generally represents the solution with the most significant trade-off between different optimization objectives, where a further improvement in one objective may lead to a noticeable deterioration in another objective. Therefore, knee points are commonly used for compromise-solution selection in multi-objective optimization problems52. Owing to the intuitive geometrical interpretation of distance-based knee point identification methods, the knee point in this study was determined by calculating the perpendicular distance from each Pareto solution to a reference line53. Considering that the two optimization objectives, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}_{\mathrm{t}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{t}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}_{0.5}$$\end{document}, have different numerical scales, the objective values of the Pareto solutions were normalized before the distance calculation. Subsequently, a reference line was constructed using the two extreme Pareto solutions, which can be expressed as:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:L:\:\hat{a}{G}_{1}+{\hat{b}G}_{2}+\hat{c}=0,$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{G}_{1}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{G}_{2}$$\end{document} denote the normalized objective values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}_{\mathrm{t}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{t}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}_{0.5}$$\end{document}, respectively, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\hat{a}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\hat{b}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\hat{c}$$\end{document} are the coefficients of the reference line determined by the two extreme Pareto solutions. For the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\zeta{\mathcal{}}$$\end{document}-th Pareto solution, the perpendicular distance to the reference line can be calculated as:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{\zeta\:}=\frac{\left|\hat{a}{G}_{1,\zeta\:}+\hat{b}{G}_{2,\zeta\:}+\hat{c}\right|}{\sqrt{{\hat{a}}^{2}+{\hat{b}}^{2}}}.$$\end{document}

The knee point solution is then determined by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{max}=\mathrm{max\:}\left({D}_{\zeta\:}\right)$$\end{document}, and the corresponding Pareto solution is selected as the knee point.

Experimental validation

To experimentally validate the acoustic performance of the optimized CMPP design, the CMPP configuration corresponding to the identified knee point design obtained from the multi-objective adaptive resampling strategy is fabricated using 316 L stainless steel via 3D printing, as illustrated in Fig. 4(a). Its acoustic performance is subsequently evaluated using the impedance tube, and comparison between experimental and numerical results further confirms the reliability of the proposed optimization strategy.

The normal-incidence absorption coefficients are measured in an impedance tube with an inner diameter of 34.8 mm based on the two-microphone transfer-function method45. The corresponding upper measurement frequency is 5284 Hz. The measurement system shown in Fig. 4(b) comprises a loudspeaker sound source, two GRAS 46AD pressure microphones, an LMS SCADAS data acquisition system, and LMS Test Lab software for signal processing. The specimens are positioned at the termination of the tube, and the cavity depth is adjusted by the rigid termination.

Results and discussion

Acoustic characteristics of the CMPP with parameter study

Figure 5 (a)-(e) illustrate the effects of the perforation ratio \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document}, perforation diameter d, panel thickness \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{t}_{0}$$\end{document}, air cavity depth H, and corrugation depth h on the absorption characteristics of the CMPP absorber. To verify the numerical stability of the parametric analysis results, a representative case with a distinct resonance peak in Fig. 5(a) is selected for further calculation, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:X=[\:d，\:{t}_{0}，\mathrm{H}，h，\sigma\:]$$\end{document} = [1, 1, 30, 30, 0.4%]. In the original numerical setting, the frequency step is 5 Hz, and the mesh contains at least 14 finite elements per shortest acoustic wavelength within the investigated frequency range. In the refined setting, the mesh size is reduced by half, so that the mesh contains at least 28 finite elements per shortest acoustic wavelength, and the frequency step is reduced to 2 Hz. As shown in Fig. 5(f), the absorption curves obtained under the two numerical settings almost overlap. The peak frequency changes only from 645 Hz to 644 Hz, and the peak absorption coefficient changes from 0.8946 to 0.8975. These results indicate that the model is insensitive to variations in mesh size and frequency step near the resonance peak, confirming its good numerical stability.

As shown in Fig. 5(a), as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document} decreases, the number of flow channels per unit surface area is reduced, leading to a higher proportion of viscous dissipation within the micro-perforations and a pronounced increase in the effective acoustic resistance. Consequently, near the low-frequency resonance, the surface impedance more readily satisfies the impedance-matching condition, with the real part approaching unity and the imaginary part approaching zero, thereby resulting in a sharp and high absorption peak. Similarly, reducing d simultaneously increases the acoustic resistance and the effective acoustic mass of the micro-perforations, modifying both the real and imaginary components of the surface impedance. Under the baseline conditions considered in this study, Fig. 5(b) shows that a smaller d improves impedance matching, enhances the absorption peak, and broadens the effective bandwidth. This behavior differs from that of conventional flat MPP absorbers dominated by a single resonance, primarily due to the coupling of multiple cavity modes introduced by the corrugated cavity geometry15,16. Moreover, an increase in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{t}_{0}$$\end{document} effectively lengthens the micro-perforation channels, which enhances viscous energy dissipation through increased acoustic resistance. Meanwhile, the associated increase in effective acoustic mass shifts the resonance frequency toward lower frequencies as shown in Fig. 5(c). In contrast to the panel thickness parameter, the cavity-related parameters exert a different influence on the sound absorption performance as shown in Fig. 5(d). Increasing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{H}$$\end{document} deepens the backing cavity and reduces the equivalent acoustic stiffness of the enclosed air, thereby shifting the resonance and impedance-matching condition to lower frequencies. Consequently, the absorption peak moves progressively toward the low-frequency range, accompanied by a slight decrease in the peak absorption coefficient. However, the overall width of the main absorption region remains broadly comparable, indicating that the effect of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{H}$$\end{document} is primarily reflected in the low-frequency shift of the absorption response rather than in a substantial bandwidth expansion.

Furthermore, Fig. 5 (e) shows that increasing h results in stronger surface undulation and more pronounced local cavity-depth variations, making uniform impedance matching at low frequencies more difficult and thus significantly weakening low-frequency absorption. In contrast, a larger h strengthens local resonant effects in the corrugated cavity and introduces more participating modes for energy dissipation, facilitating the emergence of secondary or multiple absorption peaks in the mid-to-high-frequency range.

Overall, the absorption performance of CMPPs is not governed by individual structural parameters in isolation. Instead, they collectively determine the absorption peak frequency, peak magnitude, and effective absorption bandwidth. The micro-perforation parameters (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document}, d, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{t}_{0}$$\end{document}) primarily regulate the ability of acoustic energy dissipation by entering the micro-perforations, thereby controlling the absorption peak and its associated resonance frequency. In contrast, the backing cavity depth \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{H}$$\end{document} mainly affects the resonance frequency of the system by regulating the equivalent acoustic properties of the enclosed air. Meanwhile, the corrugation depth h modifies the local cavity-depth distribution and enhances the non-uniformity of the backing cavity, thereby promoting multimodal coupling and influencing the distribution of absorption peaks over a wider frequency range. Owing to the inherent trade-offs among these mechanisms, adjusting a single parameter is insufficient to simultaneously achieve broadband and high-efficiency sound absorption. Therefore, a multi-parameter, multi-objective optimization framework is employed to determine the optimal CMPP design within the target frequency range.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the effects of structural parameters on the sound absorption performance of CMPP absorbers and developed an improved DEU-based adaptive sampling optimization strategy for achieving broadband and high-efficiency sound absorption. The main conclusions are as follows: The five structural parameters exhibit significant coupling effects on acoustic impedance, resonance peak positions, and broadband absorption performance. In particular, the combined effect of h and H, which governs the sinusoidal corrugation–cavity configuration, can induce additional mid–high-frequency absorption peaks and further complicate the absorption mechanism of CMPPs, highlighting the important role of profile-related parameters in the acoustic design of CMPP absorbers.
