Electroconductive Soft Microcarriers for Suspension Culture of Skeletal Muscle Cells
Authors: Aynaz Saket Balgouri, Esfandyar Askari, Mahmood Razzaghi, Hamed Alizadeh Sardroud, Mahdieh Shokrollahi Barough, Sravya Tekumalla, Mohsen Akbari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe advancement of conductive biomaterials is essential for engineering electrically responsive tissues, such as skeletal muscle, cardiac, and neural tissues. Traditional hydrogel‐based microcarriers (MCs) lack inherent conductivity, limiting their potential for cell signaling and differentiation. In this study, we present a soft conductive MC system composed of gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) and poly(3,4‐ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(styrene sulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS), fabricated using microfluidic flow‐focusing technology. Material characterization confirms that increasing PEDOT:PSS content significantly improves electrical conductivity and elasticity while maintaining structural integrity. In vitro biocompatibility studies reveal strong C2C12 myoblast attachment and viability, with no significant cytotoxic effects. Furthermore, immunofluorescence and flow cytometry analyses indicate that PEDOT:PSS‐containing MCs enhance myogenic early differentiation, as evidenced by increased myosin heavy chain (MyHC) expression, nuclear elongation, and MyHC IF staining. These findings establish GelMA‐PEDOT:PSS MCs as a promising bioelectronic platform for tissue engineering applications, with potential implications for skeletal muscle regeneration. Future studies should explore dynamic electrical stimulation strategies to further optimize bio‐functionality and cell‐specific responses.Keywords: bioelectronics, conductive hydrogels, GelMA‐PEDOT:PSS microcarriers, microfluidic fabrication, myogenic differentiation, skeletal muscle regeneration, tissue engineering

Introduction

Producing large quantities of cells is vital for applications in tissue engineering, regenerative medicine, disease modeling, and cell therapy. However, achieving this scale is a significant challenge. Bioreactors address this by providing a controlled environment for large‐scale cell cultivation, ensuring the high cell yields required to meet demand [1, 2]. For adherent cell cultures, microcarriers (MC) serve as a substrate to facilitate cell attachment and growth within a suspension culture system [1, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Commercially available microcarriers are predominantly made out of plastic materials, though recently microcarriers made of soft hydrogels have also been developed for enhanced cell attachment, improved nutrient diffusion, and the ability to harvest cells without using enzymes or with reduced enzyme use [2, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Further, gel‐based MCs can be designed to resemble the physical and chemical features of the extracellular matrix (ECM) more closely, potentially improving cell function and differentiation [2, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Through the rational design of a temperature‐sensitive coating on microcarriers, the use of enzymes can be significantly reduced or entirely eliminated, thereby simplifying downstream processing by facilitating the removal of microcarriers from the final product [1, 3, 7].

Conductive materials have shown great promise in cell manufacturing applications due to their ability to mimic the electrical properties of native tissues, enhance cell signaling and communication, and promote cell proliferation and differentiation [8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. These materials can potentially be used to produce microcarriers for the large‐scale production of therapeutic cells, offering improved scalability, cell viability, and consistency in manufacturing processes. Conductive polymers, as well as metal‐based and carbon‐based nanomaterials, have been widely utilized to impart electroconductivity to hydrogels, enhancing their utility in cell manufacturing [8, 12, 13, 14]. Specifically, conductive polymers offer mechanical flexibility and softness that closely mimic the extracellular matrix (ECM) of soft tissues. This flexibility minimizes mechanical mismatch with host tissues, which is critical for preserving cell viability and function during large‐scale cell production [15, 16, 17]. Moreover, the homogeneous integration of these polymers into hydrogels ensures a smooth surface, avoiding nano topographies that could alter cell‐surface interactions and impact cell behavior in 3D cultures.

PEDOT:PSS is a promising conductive polymer for tissue engineering scaffolds [18, 19, 20, 21]. PEDOT:PSS exhibits several advantageous properties, including adjustable conductivity, good transparency to visible light, excellent thermal stability, and biocompatibility [18, 19, 20, 21]. These characteristics make this material an ideal candidate for creating biomimetic environments that support cell growth and function [20, 21]. PEDOT:PSS is particularly well‐suited for growing cells due to its ability to form stable hydrogels with high water content (80–87 wt.%) and long‐term stability in wet physiological environments [19, 20, 21]. The material has high electrical conductivity (approximately 20 S/cm in phosphate buffer saline (PBS) and 40 S/cm in deionized water), which allows for efficient signal transmission and cell‐cell communication [19, 20]. Additionally, PEDOT:PSS hydrogels exhibit low Young's modulus (∼2 MPa) and high stretchability (>35% ductility), which closely mimic the mechanical properties of native tissues [19, 20, 21].

In this work, we developed conductive MCs made of photocrosslinkable GelMA and PEDOT:PSS for skeletal muscle cell manufacturing. We used a 3D‐printed microfluidic flow focusing system developed previously in our group to produce MCs with high size uniformity and excellent size control [22]. We evaluated the impact of PEDOT:PSS content on cell attachment and earlydifferentiation to ensure its suitability for large‐scale production of skeletal muscle cells.

Experimental Section

Materials

A resin 3D printer (Photon Mono 2K, Anycubic, Shenzhen, China) was fabricating the 3D‐printed microfluidic droplet generator. A clear photopolymer resin (Anycubic 405 nm UV‐curing resin, Shenzhen, China) was used for the print. Key chemicals and reagents were sourced from Sigma–Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), including N,N'‐methylenebis(acrylamide) (CAS# 110‐26‐9), lithium phenyl‐2,4,6‐trimethyl benzoyl phosphinate (LAP) (CAS# 85073‐19‐4), Span 80 (CAS# 1338‐43‐8), porcine‐derived gelatin Type A (CAS# 9000‐70‐8), phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) tablets, and methacrylic anhydride (CAS# 760‐93‐0). PEDOT:PSS (PH 1000) (CAS# 155090‐83‐8) was purchased from Ossila. Additional materials such as fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Cat#16000044), Dulbecco's Phosphate Buffered Saline (DPBS) (Cat#14190144), trypsin/EDTA (Cat#15400054), and Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Cat#11965118) were procured from Gibco (Grand Island, NY, USA). The Live/Dead viability/cytotoxicity kit for mammalian cells (Cat#L3224) was supplied by Invitrogen via ThermoFisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). The PrestoBlue cell viability reagent was supplied by Invitrogen via ThermoFisher Scientific (Cat#A13261). In addition, heavy mineral oil (Cat# 8042‐47‐5) was obtained from Fisher Chemical (Waltham, MA, USA), and dialysis membranes with a molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) of 12–14 000 were sourced from Spectro/Por through Fisher Scientific.

GelMA Synthesis

GelMA was synthesized following a previously established protocol [23]. In brief, 10 g of porcine skin gelatin was dissolved in 200 mL of PBS at 60°C to create a 5% gelatin solution. Eight milliliters of methacrylic anhydride was added drop‐wisely into the solution, which was then stirred at 300 rpm for 3 h to generate GelMA with high degrees of methacrylation (DOM). To halt the reaction, 300 mL of preheated PBS (40°C–50°C) was added, while stirring for 15 min. The resulting GelMA solution was filtered, and then poured into dialysis membranes with a molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) of 12–14 kDa and dialyzed for two weeks at 40°C–50°C. To ensure complete removal of unreacted methacrylic anhydride and other impurities, the membranes were immersed in distilled water, stirred at 50 rpm, and the water was changed twice daily, with the membranes reversed each time. Finally, the GelMA solution was filtered and lyophilized to obtain a solid GelMA product.

Microfluidic Device Fabrication Using 3D Printing

The microfluidic chip, featuring channel dimensions ranging from 500 to 750 µm, was designed using Blender 4.2 and fabricated using a 3D printer (Photon Mono 2K, Anycubic, Shenzhen, China). 3D printing was chosen for its high precision and ability to produce intricate microfluidic features with exceptional accuracy, ensuring reproducibility and fine control over channel dimensions.

Before each printing cycle, the printer and resin vat were carefully inspected to eliminate any potential contaminants that could affect the print quality. Dust particles were removed from the printer using compressed air, while any impurities in the resin vat were extracted using a pipette. Following the printing process, the fabricated components underwent a thorough cleaning procedure with isopropanol to remove any residual uncured resin, ensuring high fidelity of the printed structures. The cleaned components were then dried using compressed air and subjected to post‐curing in a Kudo3D post‐curing chamber for 30 min at room temperature to enhance the mechanical and chemical stability of the microfluidic chip.

Microcarriers Generation

MCs were produced using the hydrogel solution going through a microfluidic flow‐focusing systems as previously described [22, 24]. To generate droplets, the continuous phase consisted of heavy mineral oil mixed with 10% (w/v) Span‐80, a non‐ionic surfactant. The dispersed (hydrogel) and continuous phases were loaded into plastic syringes and connected to the microfluidic device via Tygon tubing. A 50 mL syringe introduced the continuous phase, while a 3 mL syringe, mounted on a syringe pump (Harvard Apparatus PHD 2000, USA), delivered the dispersed phase. The flow rates for the continuous phase and the aqueous phase were 70 and 8 µL/min, respectively. Droplets formed at the chip's flow‐focusing junction and passed through a crosslinking module, where exposure to visible light induced in situ photo‐crosslinking. This module, equipped with 20 standard 405 nm LEDs, crosslinked the MCs as they flowed through (exposure time of each droplet was ∼ 30 s to 25 mW/cm2 light intensity).

The crosslinked MCs were washed via centrifugation to remove residual oil and surfactants. The washing process involved sequential washing: two rinses with PBS containing 1% dish soap, followed by two washes with a 50% ethanol/PBS solution, and three rinses with PBS. Finally, they were soaked in 70% ethanol overnight for sterilization. They were then washed twice using sterile PBS. Purified MCs were either lyophilized and stored at 5°C for future use or soaked in cell culture medium for 72 h before biological studies.

Swelling Capacity Measurement

To investigate the swelling dynamics of the fabricated MCs, they were lyophilized for 24 h. Their average diameter when dried was measured. Then they were soaked in cell culture media, and their diameters were measured during a 70‐h incubation period at 37°C. At specific time intervals, optical microscope images were captured, and the diameters of the MCs were measured using ImageJ (version 1.54d, National Institutes of Health, USA). The swelling capacity was determined by calculating the ratio of the measured diameter to the initial dry diameter, as defined by Equation 2 [25]. In this equation, D0 represents the MC's diameter in its dry state, while Dt corresponds to its diameter after swelling in the medium [25].

The diameter of the MCs increases until it reaches Dfinal . This final diameter was used to calculate the swelling capacity percentage of each MC group after no further change in the diameter was observed.

Resistance Measurement of Hydrogel Solutions

To evaluate the conductivity of the crosslinked hydrogel MCs, electrical resistance measurements were conducted using a four‐point probe setup. A two mL aliquot of the hydrogel precursor solution was poured into a 35 mm petri dish and subsequently crosslinked using Kudo3D post curing chamber for 10 min to form a uniform hydrogel sheet with a height of approximately 2 mm. The four probes were carefully positioned on the surface of the crosslinked hydrogel to ensure consistent contact, and resistance measurements were done using a Keithley 6430 Sub‐Femtoamp Remote Source Meter. In this measurement setup, the probes were positioned 2 mm apart, and the current was applied through the outer probes, and the voltage drop was measured across the inner probes. The four‐point probe method is preferred for soft materials like hydrogels because it eliminates contact resistance errors.

Viscoelastic Measurement of Hydrogels

The influence of PEDOT:PSS incorporation on the viscoelastic behavior of GelMA hydrogels was evaluated using an ElastoSense Bio rheometer (Rheolution Inc.) [27]. For each measurement, 2 mL of the prepolymer solution was dispensed into the device's measurement cups and then photopolymerized using 405 nm light at an intensity of 25 mW cm− 2 for 360 s. After crosslinking, the instrument was used to determine the storage modulus (G′), loss modulus (G″), and damping factor (tan δ) of the hydrogels. All tests were performed at ambient temperature (24°C ± 1°C). Mechanical responses were monitored for 10 min with data acquisition occurring every 15 s. Each experimental condition was evaluated using three independent measurement cups.

SEM Imaging and ATR‐FTIR Measurements

The surface morphology of microgels was examined using SEM imaging to assess changes in morphology and surface properties [28]. The microgels were frozen in liquid nitrogen. And then freeze‐dried. The dried microgels were then coated with a thin layer of gold via sputtering and imaged using a Hitachi S‐4800 FE SEM microscope (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 1 kV.

Lyophilized microgel samples were analyzed to evaluate the chemical interactions within the hydrogel network. Attenuated Total Reflectance–Fourier Transform Infrared (ATR‐FTIR) spectroscopy was performed using a PerkinElmer Spectrum Frontier instrument (Massachusetts, USA). Spectra were recorded over a wavenumber range of 600–4000 cm− 1 with a spectral resolution of 1 cm− 1.

In Vitro Cell Culture

To evaluate the biocompatibility of the conductive MCs, C2C12 murine skeletal muscle cells (American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA, USA, Cat#CRL‐1772) were seeded onto the MCs and cultured under controlled conditions. C2C12 cells were maintained in complete growth media consisting of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 1% GlutaMAX at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Cells from passage three were used for the experiments.

Prior to seeding, sterile 96‐well plates were prepared by coating the bottom of each well with a 2% agarose (Fisher BioReagents) solution dissolved in PBS under sterile conditions. These agarose‐coated plates were then incubated with DMEM at 37°C for 24 h before cell seeding. Meanwhile, the sterile MCs were pre‐incubated in DMEM for 72 h at 37°C to enhance cell compatibility.

On the day of seeding, the DMEM was removed from the agarose‐coated wells, and approximately 100 µL of pre‐incubated MCs were added to each well, ensuring complete surface coverage. C2C12 cells were detached using 0.05% trypsin‐EDTA (Gibco) for 5 min, counted, and seeded at a density of 40 000 cells per well onto the MCs. The plates were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 4 h, with gentle shaking every 15–20 min to facilitate uniform cell attachment. After this initial attachment phase, fresh culture media was added to each well, and the cells were allowed to grow on the MCs for seven days under standard incubation conditions. The cell culture media was changed every other day.

Live/Dead Assay

The viability of C2C12 cells cultured on conductive MCs was assessed using the Viability/Cytotoxicity Kit for Mammalian Cells (L3224, ThermoFisher), following the manufacturer's protocol. This assay utilizes Calcein AM to stain live cells and Ethidium homodimer‐1 (EthD‐1) to stain dead cells.

To run live/dead assay, the culture MCs were removed from the incubator, and the culture media was discarded. Two hundred microliters of the prepared live/dead staining solution was added to the MCs, ensuring complete coverage. The samples were incubated for 2 h at room temperature in the dark to allow the dyes to penetrate the cells while minimizing light‐induced degradation. Following incubation, the live/dead staining solution was removed, and the samples were washed by replacing it with DPBS to remove excess dye. Fluorescence imaging was performed using a Zeiss Axio Observer microscope (Germany). The captured images were then analyzed to determine the viability of C2C12 cells on the conductive MCs.

PrestoBlue Assay

An extraction test was performed on the MCs to evaluate the cytotoxic effects of the released components from MCs with or without conductive material [29]. In this method, the MCs were incubated under standard culture conditions in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic for a total of 14 days. The extracts of the MCs were collected at three different time points: Day 3, Day 5, and Day 14. These extracts were stored under sterile conditions until further use in the cytotoxicity and viability assays.

On Day 13, C2C12 cells were seeded in 2D culture at a density of 12 000 cells per well in a 96‐well plate to allow for attachment and growth overnight. On Day 14, the MC extracts collected from different time points were added to the designated wells containing C2C12 cells. Wells designated as control groups received fresh cell culture media that were not exposed to microcarriers. All wells were then incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2. After 24 h of incubation with MC extracts, the media were removed from all wells. A 1/10 dilution of PrestoBlue reagent was prepared in fresh cell culture media (e.g., 1 mL PrestoBlue + 9 mL media), ensuring sufficient volume to treat all wells, including three additional blank wells without cells. The working solution was warmed to 37°C in an incubator before application. PrestoBlue working solution was then added to each well at the volume of 110 µL per well. Three wells containing only PrestoBlue and media (no cells) were included as blanks. The plate was returned to the incubator for 30–40 min for wells with <50% confluency or 10–20 min for wells with >50% confluency to allow for optimal fluorescence development. After incubation, 100 µL of the supernatant from each well was transferred to a fresh 96‐well plate for measurement. The fluorescence was recorded using a spectrophotometer plate reader (Tecan, Model: Infinite 200 PRO, Switzerland) at an excitation wavelength of 560 nm and an emission wavelength of 590 nm. The scan was performed from the bottom of the plate. The fluorescence readings from the blank wells were subtracted from all experimental wells. Cell Attachment and Proliferation Study on Conductive Microcarriers

Four random wells were selected after four hours of incubation to evaluate the attachment efficiency of C2C12 cells on conductive MCs [30]. The MCs were carefully washed with PBS to remove any unattached cells. Trypsin‐EDTA (5x) was added to the wells to detach adhered cells, followed by incubation at 37°C for 5–10 min. After enzymatic detachment, cell culture medium was added to the suspension to neutralize trypsin. Then, the cell suspension was passed through a cell strainer to separate the MCs from the detached cells. The collected filtrate was centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min, and the cell pellet was resuspended in DMEM for counting. Immunofluorescence Staining Assay

On day 7 of culture, immunofluorescence staining was used to visualize the cytoskeletal structure and nuclear shape of C2C12 cells grown on conductive microcarriers. F‐ Actin filaments were stained using fluorescently labeled phalloidin, while DAPI (4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole) was used to label the cell nuclei. MCs were washed twice with PBS and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (VWR) at 4°C overnight. The following day, the MCs were washed again with PBS and blocked with 5% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA, VWR) in 0.3% Triton X‐100 (BIO BASIC) dissolved in PBS, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C. On the next day, the blocking solution was discarded, and the samples were incubated overnight at 4°C with 4 µg/mL Alexa Fluor 488 Phalloidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) diluted in blocking solution (1:5 dilution) to stain the actin cytoskeleton. After the antibody solution was removed, DAPI (4’,6‐Diamidino‐2‐Phenylindole, Dihydrochloride, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to stain cell nuclei, with samples incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Following two additional washes with PBS, the MCs were transferred onto glass slides and analyzed using fluorescence microscopy to capture cell morphology.

During differentiation, C2C12 cells transition from a round shape to an elongated, tubular morphology, as they adhere, elongate, and fuse into multinucleated myotubes [24, 31]. To quantify these morphological changes, ImageJ software (version 1.54d, National Institutes of Health, USA) was used to analyze cell structure. Nuclei elongation was assessed by measuring the nuclear aspect ratio, defined as the ratio of the longest to the shortest nuclear diameter. Randomly selected nuclei from multiple images per condition were analyzed to quantify morphological changes.

To quantify the population of cells expressing MyHC, indicating progression toward early‐myogenic differentiation, C2C12 cells cultured on electroconductive microgels were first fixed and permeabilized according to the procedure described in the Phalloidin staining section. The samples were then incubated overnight at 4°C with a primary mouse anti‐MyHC antibody (BioLegend) prepared in staining buffer composed of 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.01% Triton X‐100 in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS). Following three rinses with PBS to remove unbound antibody, the samples were treated with an Alexa Fluor 555–labeled secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclear staining was performed by adding DAPI (5 µg/mL) 30 min before imaging. Fluorescence images were collected using a ZEISS LSM 880 confocal microscope equipped with an Airyscan detector.

Flow Cytometry Analysis

Flow cytometry was used to evaluate myosin heavy chain (MyHC) expression, a key late‐stage myogenic marker, to determine the proportion of differentiated cells within the population. This technique enables precise quantification of protein expression at the single‐cell level, allowing for an accurate assessment of differentiation status [32].

For sample preparation, cells were detached from the MCs by washing with PBS, followed by enzymatic dissociation using trypsin‐EDTA (10X). The cell suspension was then filtered through a 0.07 mm cell strainer to remove MCs. A final cell concentration of 5000 cells/µL was prepared for staining. Cells were incubated for 30 min in PBS containing 2% BSA, with PE‐conjugated anti‐mouse MyHC antibody (MF20, BD‐USA). After incubation, cells were diluted in 500 µL PBS and analyzed using a flow cytometer (Attune NXT, Thermo Fisher, USA) to quantify MyHC+ cells and assess the extent of C2C12 differentiation [32]. Flow cytometry data analysis was done using FlowJo software (vX.0‐ BD USA).

Ki‐67 Proliferation Marker and Cell Cycle of C2C12 Cells

C2C12 myoblasts were cultured on microgels containing PEDOT:PSS in complete growth medium for a period of 7 days. On day 7, the cells were harvested by enzymatic dissociation using 0.05% trypsin and subsequently collected by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min. The resulting cell pellets were washed three times with PBS. For fixation, chilled 70% ethanol (−20°C) was slowly added dropwise to the cell suspension while gently vortexing to minimize cellular clumping. The fixed samples were maintained at −20°C for 2 h. Following fixation, cells were pelleted again by centrifugation at 400 × g for 5 min and washed three times using PBS supplemented with 1% fetal bovine serum (FBS). To evaluate proliferative activity, the cells were incubated with Ki‐67–FITC antibody (BioLegend) at a ratio of 0.5 µL antibody per 1 × 106 cells in 100 µL of suspension, followed by incubation at 4°C for 2 h. After washing once with PBS, the cells were resuspended in propidium iodide (PI) staining solution (0.5 µg/mL) containing RNase and incubated for 1 h at room temperature to enable cell‐cycle assessment. The stained samples were then analyzed using an Attune flow cytometer (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were conducted with a minimum of three independent replicates, and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test in GraphPad Prism 7.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) to assess differences between groups. Statistical significance was defined as follows: ns (not significant) for p > 0.05, * for p < 0.05, ** for p < 0.01, *** for p < 0.001, and **** for p < 0.0001.

Results and Discussions

Production of Conductive Microcarriers

We employed a microfluidic emulsion system to fabricate monodisperse conductive microspheres (Figure 1A). This technique enables the continuous production of uniform MCs with precise size control [22, 24]. Additionally, it eliminates the need for downstream sieving typically required in traditional emulsion polymerization methods to achieve consistent MC sizes. By adjusting the flow rate ratios, we could accurately regulate the size of the MCs, and the formulation can be easily modified to obtain the desired properties [24]. The conductive hydrogel precursor solution consisted of GelMA (7% w/v), PEDOT:PSS (1% and 2% vv), and LAP (Lithium phenyl‐2,4,6‐trimethylbenzoylphosphinate) (0.3% w/v) as the dispersed phase (Figure 1A‐i). The concentration of GelMA was based on our previous results [22]. The specific formulations and their corresponding codes are outlined in Table 1.

GelMA provides a biocompatible hydrogel matrix, PEDOT:PSS imparts electrical conductivity, and LAP is a photoinitiator to enable photo‐crosslinking of the hydrogel solution. LAP is a water‐soluble, cytocompatibility Type I photoinitiator widely used to polymerize hydrogels and other polymeric materials for biomedical applications. Its popularity stems from its improved properties compared to alternatives like Irgacure 2959, including increased water solubility, faster polymerization rates with 365 nm light, and absorbance at 400 nm, allowing visible light polymerization. These characteristics enable cell encapsulation at lower initiator concentrations and longer wavelength light, reducing toxicity and increasing cell viability [33].

The hydrogel solution (aqueous phase) was injected into a mineral oil/Span 80 solution (continuous phase) for MC fabrication. Details of the formulations used in this process are provided in the experimental section. Microscopic images in Figure 1B display GelMA (7%) MCs produced with varying concentrations of PEDOT:PSS. The three images correspond to different PEDOT:PSS levels: GP‐7‐0, GP‐7‐1, and GP‐7‐2. As PEDOT:PSS concentration increases, the MCs appear slightly larger with minor variations in uniformity.

The scale bar indicates their size ranges between 200 and 300 µm. MCs were generated at an oil flow rate of 70 µL/min and an aqueous flow rate of 10 µL/min. Figure 1C illustrates the size distribution (n > 100), where the pink curve represents GP‐7‐0, with a mean size of 218.19 ± 12.33 µm. The green curve, corresponding to GP‐7‐1, exhibits a slightly larger mean size of 241.54 ± 17.93 µm, while the blue curve for GP‐7‐2 shows the largest average size of 243.73 ± 13.19 µm. The shift in size is attributed to the increased viscosity and altered surface tension of the hydrogel solution with higher PEDOT:PSS concentrations, which affect droplet formation dynamics during the emulsification process. The polydispersity index (PDI) for the three groups, shown in Figure 1D, is 0.056 for GP‐7‐0, 0.074 for GP‐7‐1, and 0.054 for GP‐7‐2, all below 0.1, indicating size uniformity [36]. These findings suggest that incorporating PEDOT:PSS did not significantly impact the monodispersity of MCs.

Material Characterization

The viscoelastic properties of hydrogels can influence cell–matrix interactions. To investigate this, we used the ElastoSense Bio system to measure the viscoelastic properties of the hydrogels (Figure 2A–C). Photocrosslinked GelMA (GP‐7‐0) exhibited a storage modulus (G′) of 1386 ± 23.12 Pa (n = 500), which falls within the range reported for photocrosslinked GelMA at a concentration of 7 wt.%. Incorporation of PEDOT:PSS into the GelMA network increased the storage modulus to 2666.57 ± 149.54 Pa (n = 500) for GP‐7‐1 and 2978.97 ± 195.33 Pa (n = 500) for GP‐7‐2, indicating enhanced elastic properties of the hydrogels in the presence of PEDOT:PSS, consistent with previous reports in the field (Figure 2A).

The loss modulus (G″) showed a different trend: 190.56 ± 27.07 Pa (n = 500) for GP‐7‐0, 103.25 ± 75.42 Pa (n = 500) for GP‐7‐1, and 412 ± 78.87 Pa (n = 500) for GP‐7‐2 (Figure 2B). These results suggest that PEDOT:PSS interacts with the GelMA network and alters the viscoelastic properties of the hydrogel.

To further examine structural changes within the hydrogel network, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to visualize the surface morphology of freeze‐dried microgels. As shown in Figure S1, GP‐7‐0 exhibited a microporous structure with uniformly distributed pores, which is characteristic of GelMA hydrogels. Incorporation of PEDOT:PSS altered the surface morphology, affecting both pore density and pore size, and this effect became more pronounced with increasing PEDOT:PSS concentration. The observed reduction in pore density and pore size further supports the interaction between PEDOT:PSS and the GelMA network and is consistent with the changes observed in the viscoelastic properties of the hydrogels.

Moreover, as shown in Figure S2, ATR‐FTIR analysis revealed the appearance of characteristic PEDOT:PSS peaks at ∼2920 cm− 1 corresponding to C─H stretching of the PEDOT backbone and ∼1500–1450 cm− 1 associated with C═C stretching of the thiophene ring. In addition, increased absorption in the ∼1180–1030 cm− 1 region corresponding to sulfonate (─SO3 −) stretching of PSS was observed in PEDOT:PSS‐containing hydrogels. These spectral changes suggest potential electrostatic interactions between the negatively charged sulfonate groups of PSS and the primary amine groups of GelMA.

Microcarriers are typically shipped and stored in dry form. Therefore, the extent to which the microcarriers swell during rehydration prior to use and the time needed to complete are important for optimizing pre‐processing stage in cell manufacturing. As shown in Figure 2D, swelling rates for all MC compositions was quantified to investigate the impact of PEDOT:PSS content on this property by measuring the diameter of microcarriers during rehydration. All compositions rapidly swelled to >75% of their complete rehydrated diameter within the first 30 min of the process. However, complete rehydration was achieved within 70 h suggesting a ∼0.4% increase in diameter per hour. Given the slow increase in the diameter, we recommend a half an hour rehydration process for initial seeding, but further studies with more replicates is needed to confirm this recommendation.

Comparing the results of PEDOT:PSS containing groups and pristine GelMA revealed a substantial impact of the conductive polymer on the swelling behavior of microcarriers. While the swelling rate for MCs containing the conductive polymer was independent of the concentration of PEDOT:PSS, these MCs exhibited nearly a twofold increase in their size compared to the pristine GelMA. However, in the study by Wang et al. using 6% GelMA, a composition highly comparable to our own (7%), showed an increase in the swelling ratio when PEDOT:PSS content was increased from 0.15% to 0.2% (v/v) [37].

The swelling results indicate that GP‐7‐1 and GP‐7‐2 exhibit nearly twice the water absorption capacity compared with GP‐7‐0. This observation contrasts with our viscoelasticity and pore size findings, as well as several previous reports showing reduced swelling upon incorporation of PEDOT:PSS [32, 36]. Based on our viscoelastic measurements, the addition of PEDOT:PSS—particularly at higher concentrations (GP‐7‐2)—significantly increased the storage modulus of the hydrogels compared with the GelMA‐only group. For most of the hydrogels. increased elastic modulus is typically associated with reduced swelling capacity due to a denser network structure. However, some studies have also reported enhanced swelling in PEDOT:PSS‐containing GelMA systems despite increased elasticity [38]. Therefore, further investigation is required to elucidate the polyelectrolyte effects of PEDOT:PSS on the swelling behavior of GelMA‐based hydrogels [34, 39].

Moreover, our results are in line with broader trends in GelMA hydrogel behavior. It is well‐documented that lower GelMA concentrations lead to higher swelling ratios due to reduced crosslinking density and increased hydrophilicity. Ghazali et al. confirms that decreasing GelMA concentration enhances swelling, which supports our interpretation that effective crosslinking density in our PEDOT:PSS‐loaded GelMA was lowered, functionally behaving like a lower‐concentration GelMA system [40].

In addition, the concentration of PEDOT:PSS used in our formulation was higher than in many other studies, which typically explore concentrations up to 0.3% (w/v) [34, 37]. In contrast, we used a higher loading (1% (v/v) and 2% (v/v)), which may have amplified the aforementioned effects to an extent where the swelling ratio increased notably instead of decreasing. Figure 2E presents a visual comparison of crosslinked GelMA‐PEDOT:PSS solutions containing varying concentrations of PEDOT:PSS, ranging from 0% to 2% (w/v). The increasing PEDOT:PSS concentration resulted in a more intense blue coloration, indicating successful incorporation of PEDOT:PSS within the hydrogel network. The distinct visual differences between samples reflect the tunability of hydrogel composition, which can influence physical properties.

Figure 2F shows the four‐point probe measurement setup used to determine the electrical resistance of the hydrogel sheet. The image displays four metallic probe tips in contact with the hydrogel surface, positioned under a microscope for precise alignment. In this configuration, the two outer probes are responsible for sourcing current, while the two inner probes measure the resulting voltage drop across the sample. This arrangement minimizes contact resistance and ensures accurate resistance measurements. Refer to the Experimental Section for detailed procedures and measurement parameters

Figure 2G represents electrical resistance measurements of GelMA‐based hydrogels with different concentrations of PEDOT:PSS, evaluated at applied currents of 1 and 10 mA. These two currents were chosen as previous research indicates that the optimal current range for four‐point probe measurements of hydrogels was between 0.1 and 10 mA [41]. This range was determined based on studies showing that hydrogel conductance measurements conducted over a current range from 0.001 to 10 mA exhibited varying degrees of accuracy and structural integrity. At a current level of 0.01 mA, measurement repeatability was compromised due to detection noise, making it unreliable for precise conductance assessments. However, at currents above 0.1 mA, measurement consistency improved, and conductance values remained relatively stable up to 10 mA. Beyond this threshold, the hydrogel's conductivity exhibited gradual degradation, likely due to permanent structural damage caused by excessive current. Thus, the 0.1 to 10 mA range was identified as optimal, as it ensures repeatable conductance measurements while minimizing measurement artifacts and preventing hydrogel degradation [41]. The hydrogels tested include GelMA (7%) without PEDOT:PSS (GP‐7‐0), GelMA (7%) with 1% PEDOT:PSS (GP‐7‐1), and GelMA (7%) with 2% PEDOT:PSS (GP‐7‐2). In Figure 2D, when the applied current was 1 mA, the resistance of GP‐7‐0 was the highest, measuring 13.84 ± 0.84 KΩ, whereas GP‐7‐1 showed a significant decrease in resistance to 5.88 ± 1.17 KΩ. The addition of 2% PEDOT:PSS (GP‐7‐2) further reduces the resistance to 2.22 ± 0.27 KΩ, indicating an increase in conductivity with higher PEDOT:PSS content. When the applied current was 10 mA, a similar trend appears but with overall lower resistance values. GP‐7‐0 exhibits the highest resistance at 1.73 ± 0.13 KΩ, while GP‐7‐1 shows a reduction to 1.18 ± 0.05 KΩ, and GP‐7‐2 demonstrates the lowest resistance, 0.55 ± 0.16 KΩ. The statistical comparisons confirm a significant reduction in resistance with increasing PEDOT:PSS concentration. These results demonstrate that PEDOT:PSS incorporation enhances the electrical conductivity of GelMA hydrogels, as evident from the decrease in resistance with increasing PEDOT:PSS content. The effect is consistent at both low and high currents, though resistance values are naturally lower at higher currents [41].

The electrical conductivity of skeletal muscle tissue typically ranges from 0.04 to 0.5 S/m [12]. However, depending on the type of stimulation applied and the orientation of the measurement relative to muscle fibers (i.e., parallel or perpendicular), reported values can vary from 0.05 up to 2.58 S/m [42, 43]. Evaluating Cytotoxicity, Attachment, Proliferation, and Viability of C2C12 Cells on Conductive Microcarriers

Assessing biocompatibility is crucial to ensure biomaterials support viability, cell adhesion, and proliferation. PrestoBlue viability test was performed to further investigate the cytotoxicity of the PEDOT:PSS (See Experimental Section). To evaluate initial adhesion efficiency, a cell attachment assay was performed on C2C12 cells, a key factor in biocompatibility and tissue engineering applications. Additionally, a proliferation study tracked cell growth over a 7‐day period, examining whether PEDOT:PSS enhances expansion through bioelectrical interactions. A live/dead staining assay was conducted on day 7 of the cell culture process on the MCs to visualize viable (green) and non‐viable (red) cells, providing insight into the cytotoxic effects of PEDOT:PSS. First, we conducted an extraction assay to evaluate the potential cytotoxicity of PEDOT:PSS. As shown in Figure 3A, the PrestoBlue assay assessed the effects of MC extracts on C2C12 cell viability over a 14‐day period. Cell viability remained consistently high across all groups, with no significant differences observed at any time point. Next, we investigated cell attachment efficiency, proliferation, and viability using a live/dead assay over a 7‐day period with MCs seeded in a 96‐well plate. Attachment efficiency was evaluated after four hours of incubation with C2C12 cells on MCs containing varying concentrations of PEDOT:PSS (Figure 3B). The results indicate that attachment percentages were comparable among GP‐7‐0, GP‐7‐1, and GP‐7‐2 groups, with no statistically significant differences. This suggests that PEDOT:PSS, at the tested concentrations, does not significantly affect initial cell adhesion.

Figure 3C presents the proliferation data over the 7‐day period. All groups demonstrated increased proliferation over time; however, GP‐7‐0 exhibited significantly higher proliferation than GP‐7‐1 and GP‐7‐2 on day 7 (***p < 0.001). Proliferation rates between GP‐7‐1 and GP‐7‐2 were similar, indicating that higher concentrations of PEDOT:PSS may modestly reduce cell proliferation. This reduction correlates with an increased percentage of early‐myogenically differentiated (MyHC+) cells, supporting the hypothesis that PEDOT:PSS may promote early‐ myoblast differentiation while limiting proliferation. This trend reflects the natural progression of muscle development, where proliferating myoblasts exit the cell cycle to undergo fusion and maturation upon receiving differentiation media [44, 45, 46, 47].

Figure 3D presents fluorescent live‐dead staining images of C2C12 cells cultured on MCs with different PEDOT:PSS concentrations (GP‐7‐0, GP‐7‐1, and GP‐7‐2) at day 7. The green fluorescence indicates viable cells, while the merged images combine bright‐field, live (green), and dead (red) staining for a comprehensive view of cell distribution and morphology. The images show high viability among all MC groups. By increasing the culture time of C2C12 cells on the surface of microgels, overall cell coverage gradually extends beyond the microgel surface. As a result, cells begin to bridge between adjacent microgels, leading to the formation of microgel aggregates (Figure 3D). We previously demonstrated that such microgel aggregation during static culture, driven by cell‐mediated bridging, can reduce the number of viable cells [28].

These findings confirm that PEDOT:PSS, at the tested concentrations, is non‐cytotoxic to C2C12 cells and supports its biocompatibility. This study confirms that the integration of PEDOT:PSS does not impair C2C12 attachment; however, it negatively affects long‐term cell expansion. One possible explanation is that, during muscle development, myoblasts initially proliferate before undergoing early differentiation. Once committed to differentiation, they exit the cell cycle, fuse, and express myogenic markers [48, 49]. The decrease in proliferation in PEDOT:PSS‐containing conditions may be directly related to the increase in early differentiation, as cells shift from a growth phase to a maturation phase using differentiation media. To test this hypothesis, C2C12 cells cultured on GP‐7‐0, GP‐7‐1, and GP‐7‐2 microgels were stained for Ki‐67 and propidium iodide (PI) to distinguish proliferative and non‐proliferative cells (Ki‐67+ or Ki‐67−) and to determine the distribution of cells across different phases of the cell cycle, including G0, G1, S, and G2/M. Ki‐67 is a proliferation marker that is expressed in actively proliferating cells; therefore, the absence of Ki‐67 indicates quiescent or non‐proliferative (potentially early differentiated) cells. The inclusion of this marker provides complementary information to PI‐based cell‐cycle analysis and allows identification of cells in the G0 phase. As shown in Figure 4A,B, the percentage of Ki‐67+ cells in the GP‐7‐0 group was 94.43% ± 0.30% (n = 3), whereas the values decreased to 85.6% ± 0.78% (n = 3) and 33.2% ± 0.91% (n = 3) for the GP‐7‐1 and GP‐7‐2 groups, respectively. The presence of PEDOT:PSS within the GelMA matrix significantly reduced the proportion of proliferative cells, and this effect became more pronounced with increasing PEDOT:PSS concentration. These findings suggest that electroconductive microgels influence the proliferation of C2C12 cells when cultured at high confluency.

Next, double staining of Ki‐67 and PI was used to determine the distribution of cells across cell‐cycle phases. As shown in Figure 4C,D, proliferating cells progress through three main phases during growth and division: G1, S, and G2/M. The G1 phase represents the initial growth stage, S phase corresponds to DNA synthesis, and G2/M represents the mitotic stages leading to cell division. Cells withdrawn from the proliferative cycle typically reside in the G0 phase, where they remain quiescent or may initiate differentiation. In the GP‐7‐0 group, 74.5% ± 0.31% (n = 3) of cells were in G1, while 8.12% ± 0.21% (n = 3) and 5.74% ± 0.14% (n = 3) were in the S and G2/M phases, respectively, and 7.28% ± 0.31% (n = 3) were in G0. These results indicate that the majority of cells remained actively engaged in the proliferative cycle. In contrast, the proportion of cells in G1 decreased in the GP‐7‐1 and GP‐7‐2 groups to 64.1% ± 0.75% (n = 3) and 10.86% ± 0.70% (n = 3), respectively, while the population of cells in the G0 phase increased with increasing PEDOT:PSS concentration. This trend suggests that a larger fraction of cells cultured on electroconductive microgels exited the proliferative cycle. Overall, the cell‐cycle analysis and Ki‐67 staining results are consistent with the proliferation data, indicating that electroconductive microgels modulate C2C12 cell proliferation.

Functional Assessment: Impact of PEDOT:PSS on C2C12 Cell Morphology and Myogenic Differentiation

Based on the previous section, the use of PEDOT:PSS in MCs leads to a decrease in cell proliferation, which may be attributed to the differentiation of C2C12 cells. To investigate this hypothesis, we examined two key indicators of cell early differentiation: nuclear elongation and MyHC expression. Nuclear elongation was assessed using the nuclear aspect ratio. Previous research by T. M. Watanabe et al. has demonstrated that the differentiation of C2C12 myoblasts into myotubes is associated with significant changes in nuclear morphology, particularly an increase in the nuclear aspect ratio. As C2C12 cells progress from rounded myoblasts to elongated myotubes, their nuclear aspect ratio increases [50]. This change in nuclear shape is attributed to forces acting on the nucleus during the differentiation process, indicating a reorganization of nuclear architecture [50]. The increase in nuclear aspect ratio serves as a useful morphological indicator of myogenic differentiation in C2C12 cells [51]. These alterations in nuclear morphology are part of a broader set of changes that occur during C2C12 differentiation, including chromatin remodeling and changes in histone dynamics, which collectively contribute to the determination of cell fate in muscle development [50].

To assess the impact of conductive MCs on C2C12 behavior, we conducted immunofluorescence staining of cells cultured on GelMA‐PEDOT:PSS MCs with increasing concentrations of PEDOT:PSS (Figure 5A). These images provide insight into cytoskeletal organization and nuclear morphology, which serve as critical markers of cellular alignment and early differentiation. Actin (green) and DAPI (blue) staining revealed clear structural differences across conditions, with elongated nuclear shapes observed at higher PEDOT:PSS concentrations. Figure 5B presents a quantitative analysis of the nuclear aspect ratio, reflecting cellular elongation in response to substrate conductivity. The results demonstrate a significant increase in nuclear elongation between GP‐7‐0 (GelMA only, 1.41 ± 0.20) and GP‐7‐1 (GelMA with 1% PEDOT:PSS, 1.48 ± 0.2; *p = 0.0384). However, no further increase was observed at 2% PEDOT:PSS (GP‐7‐2, 1.42 ± 0.2), suggesting a plateau in the elongation response. Since nuclear elongation is considered a hallmark of early myogenic differentiation, these findings indicate that 1% PEDOT:PSS is sufficient to promote this phenotype, while higher concentrations do not confer additional morphological benefits. In addition to morphological indicators, we assessed a molecular marker of differentiation, MyHC, as previously discussed in Section 3.3 [48, 49]. C2C12 cells were cultured on MCs for seven days at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. On day seven, cells were enzymatically detached and MyHC expression was analyzed via flow cytometry (Figure 5C). The percentage of MyHC+ cells was significantly higher in groups containing PEDOT:PSS: 24.93% ± 2.36% for GP‐7‐0, 52.56% ± 2.06% for GP‐7‐1, and 59.93% ± 6.27% for GP‐7‐2.

These results are summarized in the column graph in Figure 5D. One‐way ANOVA confirmed a statistically significant increase in MyHC+ cells between 0% and 1% PEDOT:PSS (****p < 0.0001). However, no significant difference was observed between 1% and 2%, reinforcing the observation that 1% PEDOT:PSS is sufficient to promote early myogenic differentiation, and that further increases do not yield enhanced differentiation. Together, these results suggest that conductive MCs, particularly those with 1% PEDOT:PSS, provide a favorable microenvironment for myogenic commitment by enhancing both cellular morphology and MyHC expression. Additionally, applying external electrical stimulation as well as differentiation media could further enhance myogenic differentiation and counteract the proliferation decline. Previous studies have shown that electrical stimulation synergizes with conductive biomaterials to promote muscle cell alignment, fusion, and functionality [52, 53]. These findings are consistent with previous proliferation results (Figures 3C and 4), which showed that C2C12 proliferation was significantly lower on PEDOT:PSS‐containing MCs compared to the 0% PEDOT:PSS condition. The relationship between reduced proliferation and increased early differentiation follows a well‐documented trend in myogenic biology [48, 49], and our results confirm this relationship.

Immunofluorescence imaging revealed that C2C12 cells cultured on all microgel formulations expressed MyHC, indicating entry into early myogenic differentiation (Figure 6). Cells were observed to localize and wrap around the spherical microgels, forming a continuous cellular layer along the microgel surfaces while maintaining well‐defined nuclei distribution as confirmed by DAPI staining. Despite the presence of MyHC‐positive cells across all groups, multinucleated myotube formation was not observed across all groups, suggesting that the cells remained at an early differentiation stage under growth medium conditions.

MCs containing PEDOT:PSS promote nuclear elongation and MyHC expression, both key indicators of early myogenic differentiation. The increased nuclear aspect ratio suggests that PEDOT:PSS enhances cytoskeletal remodeling by improving conductivity and mechanical properties, thereby serving as a bioelectronic cue for myoblast differentiation. This finding aligns with previous studies demonstrating that conductive materials mimic the native muscle environment, thereby promoting cell maturation [44, 45, 46, 47]. The findings indicate that 1% PEDOT:PSS optimally promotes early differentiation, whereas higher concentrations provide no additional benefit. Future research should investigate the effects of dynamic electrical stimulation to further optimize its application in muscle regeneration. Furthermore, investigating the signaling pathways associated with myogenic differentiation when cells are cultured on electroconductive substrates would help clarify how biomaterials influence and regulate cellular differentiation.

Conclusions

This study presents a novel electroconductive MC system composed of GelMA and PEDOT:PSS designed to support cell adhesion, differentiation, and electrical functionality in tissue engineering applications. Using a microfluidic flow‐focusing approach, monodisperse MCs with controlled size and tunable physicochemical properties were successfully fabricated, providing a scalable and reproducible platform for regenerative medicine. In terms of scalability, the proposed hydrogel system could be translated to high‐throughput production methods such as STEP emulsification or parallelized microfluidic flow‐focusing devices, which are established approaches for large‐scale generation of electroconductive soft microcarriers [54, 55].

Material characterization confirmed structural stability and enhanced electrical properties, with the 2% PEDOT:PSS formulation (GP‐7‐2) exhibiting the lowest electrical resistance. Electrostatic interactions between PEDOT:PSS and GelMA chains influenced the pore size and network density of the hydrogel, as observed by SEM, and significantly altered its viscoelastic properties. These results demonstrate that PEDOT:PSS can serve as an effective modifier to tune key material parameters, including conductivity, stiffness, and hydrogel network architecture. Future studies should further investigate the effect of PEDOT:PSS on hydrogel swelling behavior and long‐term degradation to better understand the dynamic interactions between the conductive polymer and the GelMA matrix [38].

Biocompatibility assays demonstrated high cell viability (>90%), minimal cytotoxicity, and efficient cell attachment across all MC formulations, indicating that PEDOT:PSS incorporation does not adversely affect cellular compatibility. Additionally, implementing these MCs in dynamic suspension culture systems, such as spinner‐flask bioreactors, could reduce interparticle cell bridging observed in static culture and further enhance cell expansion efficiency [22, 24, 28].

Proliferation analysis revealed that GelMA‐only MCs (GP‐7‐0) supported higher cell proliferation, whereas PEDOT:PSS‐containing formulations (GP‐7‐1 and GP‐7‐2) showed reduced proliferation over time. This reduction coincided with an increased proportion of MyHC+ cells, suggesting that PEDOT:PSS promotes early myogenic differentiation at the expense of continued proliferation, consistent with the natural progression of muscle development where myoblasts exit the cell cycle prior to differentiation [56]. Ki‐67/PI cell cycle analysis further demonstrated that increasing PEDOT:PSS concentration promoted cell cycle withdrawal, resulting in a significant increase in the proportion of cells in the G0 phase. Differentiation assays confirmed enhanced MyHC expression in PEDOT:PSS‐containing MCs, although no significant difference was observed between the 1% and 2% formulations, suggesting that 1% PEDOT:PSS may provide an optimal balance between material conductivity and cellular response. Immunofluorescence imaging also revealed increased nuclear elongation in PEDOT:PSS groups, commonly associated with early myogenic differentiation. MyHC/DAPI staining at day 7 confirmed MyHC‐positive cells in all groups; however, multinucleated myotube formation was not observed under growth medium conditions. Future studies integrating these MCs into established differentiation protocols and appropriate differentiation media could further elucidate their role in supporting mature muscle formation. In addition, applying this platform to induced pluripotent stem cell–derived myogenic models may broaden its applicability for disease modeling and regenerative medicine [57, 58]. Investigating intracellular signaling pathways involved in myogenic differentiation in response to electroconductive MCs would also provide important mechanistic insight into how conductive biomaterials regulate cellular behavior [47].

While PEDOT:PSS incorporation reduced proliferation, it significantly enhanced early myogenic differentiation. External electrical stimulation may further amplify these effects by providing active electrical cues that synergize with the conductive MC environment. Previous studies have shown that electrical stimulation combined with electroconductive scaffolds improves muscle cell alignment, fusion, and functional maturation [52, 53].

This study shows that GelMA–PEDOT:PSS microcarriers represent a promising platform for electroconductive tissue engineering and scalable cell manufacturing, with potential applications in skeletal, cardiac, and neural tissue regeneration. Future work will focus on integrating electrical stimulation strategies with this MC platform, both at the microgel level and in assembled tissue constructs, to further promote functional maturation of engineered tissues. This work contributes to the development of next‐generation bioelectronic biomaterials designed to guide functional tissue repair and regeneration.
