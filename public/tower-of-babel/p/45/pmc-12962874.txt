Poland Syndrome With Dextrocardia: A Rare Association Leading to Complex Cardiopulmonary Challenges
Authors: Sai Kommineni, Anna Morse, Saisreeja Jekkireddy, Shyam Ganti, Maneesh Gaddam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPatients with Poland syndrome, especially those with left‐sided defects accompanied by dextrocardia, may be predisposed to significant respiratory and cardiac complications. This case underscores the importance of early recognition, thorough cardiopulmonary assessment, and proactive planning for airway management and ventilatory support to optimize outcomes during acute illness.Keywords: cardiomyopathy, dextrocardia, Poland syndrome, respiratory failure, rib hypoplasia

Introduction

Poland syndrome (PS) is a congenital condition characterized by a spectrum of deformities affecting the chest wall and the ipsilateral upper extremity. This condition has an incidence of 1 in 36,000–50,000 newborns [1]. Multiple theories have attempted to explain the pathogenesis and suggest a disruption of blood supply from the subclavian arteries and their branches during embryogenesis. Dextrocardia is another rare cardiac anomaly with an incidence of about 1 in 12,000 births that usually occurs in isolation or is associated with situs inversus or structural heart abnormalities [2]. The co‐occurrence of PS and isolated dextrocardia is exceedingly rare, with less than 100 cases described in the literature [3]. However, it raises an intriguing question about a possible association in the pathogenesis during embryogenesis. We present a unique case of PS with isolated dextrocardia.

Case Presentation

Patient was a 52‐year‐old with a medical history significant for hypertension, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and alcohol use disorder who presented to the emergency room after a reportedly accidental ingestion of isopropyl alcohol. He was comfortable upon presentation but reported chronic symptoms of shortness of breath and palpitations that worsened following the ingestion. He denied any cough, fevers, chills, chest pain, hemoptysis, orthopnea, or paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea. He reported chronic bilateral lower extremity swelling. Upon presentation, he was hemodynamically stable, tachycardic, afebrile, with a normal respiratory rate. Physical examination revealed an irregularly irregular pulse, with normal cardiac and breath sounds. Notably, he had a visible chest wall deformity along with a hypoplastic left upper extremity (Figure 1). In the absence of any history of trauma or amputation, and with the patient reporting that the abnormalities had been present since birth, these findings raised early suspicion for an underlying congenital anomaly. The patient also reported that, during childhood, he had been told his congenital abnormalities might have been related to a toxic exposure experienced by his father; however, no documentation supporting this explanation was available.

Investigations

An electrocardiogram showed atrial fibrillation with a rapid ventricular response. Complete blood count was unremarkable. With a sodium of 135 mmol/L, chloride of 99 mmol/L, bicarbonate of 25 mmol/L, blood urea nitrogen of 8 mg/dL, glucose of 104 mg/dL, and a measured serum osmolality of 340 mOsm/kg, the patient was found to have a normal anion gap with an elevated osmolal gap consistent with isopropyl alcohol ingestion. Toxicology screen and ethanol levels were negative. Troponin and B‐natriuretic peptide levels were normal. Chest X‐ray demonstrated dextrocardia and mild scoliosis (Figure 2), findings that, together with the chest wall deformity, suggested PS.

Anteroposterior chest radiograph showing dextrocardia with rightward cardiac apex (blue arrow) and mild thoracic scoliosis (red arrow).

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a dilated left ventricle with a dyskinetic apex, severely hypokinetic septum, anterior wall, and anterolateral wall, severe systolic dysfunction with an ejection fraction of 15%–20%. Cardiac catheterization was performed and revealed no evidence of obstructive coronary artery disease, suggesting a nonischemic cardiomyopathy, likely related to cardiac arrhythmia or alcohol‐induced. His dextrocardia required a modified interpretation of cardiac imaging and contributed to diagnostic complexity during his evaluation for arrhythmia and cardiomyopathy. While not the cause of his cardiac dysfunction, it remained an important anatomic consideration throughout his workup. A computed tomography of the chest revealed absence of the left pectoralis muscle, underlying left chest wall and rib hypoplasia, and distortion of the thoracic cage (Figure 3).

Axial computed tomography (CT) scan of the chest demonstrating absence of the left pectoralis major muscle (red *), hypoplasia of the underlying left chest wall and ribs (red arrow), and distortion of the left hemithorax (red #). Corresponding blue (*), arrow, and (#) markers on the right side indicate normal anatomy for comparison.

Treatment

The patient was started on an amiodarone infusion and therapeutic anticoagulation with low‐molecular‐weight heparin for atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response. Because rate control remained inadequate, he underwent electrical cardioversion. Following cardioversion, he developed worsening mental status and aspirated, resulting in hypoxic respiratory failure that required intubation and mechanical ventilation. An automatic implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator was later placed for secondary prevention. His congenital chest wall abnormalities, with reduced thoracic compliance and asymmetric ribcage mechanics, may have contributed to increased ventilatory requirements and limited pulmonary reserve throughout his hospitalization.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

After being on the ventilator for 10 days, he was able to be extubated upon successful spontaneous awakening and spontaneous breathing trials. However, he required noninvasive ventilatory support due to evidence of poor respiratory effort before needing to be reintubated. His difficulty sustaining adequate ventilation was possibly compounded by the underlying rib hypoplasia and distorted thoracic mechanics associated with PS. Further efforts at extubating were unsuccessful, and a tracheostomy was eventually performed. After a prolonged stay in the long‐term acute care hospital with extensive physical therapy and nutritional support, the patient recovered; the ventilator was weaned off, and eventually, the tracheostomy was successfully decannulated.

Discussion

Poland syndrome is a rare congenital disorder characterized by a unilateral developmental anomaly of the pectoralis major muscle, frequently accompanied by ipsilateral upper limb anomalies. This condition was first observed by Sir Alfred Poland in 1841 on the autopsy findings of the absence of the sternocostal head of the pectoralis major muscle and hypoplasia of the ipsilateral upper limb in a 27‐year‐old male with significant chest wall and upper limb abnormalities [4]. However, it was only in 1962 that the constellation of features was linked to a common pathological origin, labeling it as “Poland Syndrome” [5].

While the exact etiology of PS remains uncertain, several theories have been proposed, with the most widely accepted hypothesis suggesting vascular disruption. A vascular injury during embryogenesis, particularly disrupting the blood flow in the subclavian artery or its branches around the sixth week of gestation, affects the development of pectoral muscles, chest wall structures, and the ipsilateral upper extremity [6]. The vascular disruption can be due to intrinsic factors like thromboembolic events or mechanical external factors associated with a cervical rib or aberrant musculature [7].

Another theory suggests an embryologic mesodermal defect. Since mesoderm is the common source of origin for vascular structures, the pectoralis muscle and limb buds, a primary defect in this layer during the fifth to seventh week of development could cause the various anomalies in PS [8]. Although most cases of PS are sporadic with no consistent chromosomal abnormalities, rare familial cases have been reported, hinting at the possibility of autosomal dominant inheritance with variable penetrance [9].

Of particular interest is whether environmental or teratogenic factors could have contributed to this patient's presentation. Although there is no direct evidence of teratogenic exposure in this case, the patient's father served in Vietnam during a period associated with possible dioxin exposure, making this a theoretical consideration based solely on the patient's reported family history and available epidemiologic literature. Dioxin binds to the Aryl hydrocarbon receptor, and paternal exposure to it can lead to epigenetic alterations in sperm, which could lead to vascular disruptions in the offspring [10]. Dioxin is known to cause changes in DNA methylation in the sperm that can result in mutations affecting the genetic material inherited by the offspring [10]. A systematic review and meta‐analysis have suggested an increased risk of birth defects associated with parental exposure to dioxin [11]. However, the evidence remains inconsistent, and no causal relationship has been established, making this a strictly hypothetical consideration rather than an established contributor.

The hallmark of PS is the unilateral absence of the sternal head of the pectoralis major muscle with resultant absence of the anterior axillary fold [12]. Other notable chest wall deformities include hypoplasia of pectoralis minor, hypoplastic or absent ribs (especially ribs 2–5), hypoplastic or displaced nipple and areola, absence of subcutaneous tissue or mammary gland (in females), and herniation of lung tissue in severe cases due to rib defects [13]. Upper limb defects occur ipsilateral to the chest wall deformities and can include syndactyly (cutaneous or osseous), brachydactyly, hypoplasia or aplasia of the middle phalanges, shortened forearm or hand, and partial absence of the radius or carpal bones [14]. PS predominantly affects the right side, though left‐sided involvement is not rare. However, bilateral cases are extremely uncommon [15].

The combination of developmental deformities involving the chest wall and the upper extremity can result in functional impairment and cosmetic deficits. Functional limitations are primarily related to impaired shoulder mobility and difficulty performing tasks with the affected upper extremity. Rib anomalies, especially absent or hypoplastic ribs in the upper thoracic segments, can affect respiratory mechanics with reduced thoracic compliance and a restrictive physiology [13], clinically presenting as exercise intolerance. In severe cases, lung herniation can occur through the defective anterior chest wall, presenting as a paradoxical inward movement of the herniated tissue during inspiration [16]. Mechanical displacement of bony structures and internal organs can lead to scoliosis and diaphragmatic hernia [17]. The chest wall abnormalities, along with scoliosis affecting the respiratory mechanics and thoracic compliance, could have contributed to the difficulty in liberating our patient from the ventilator and requiring tracheostomy with prolonged weaning. With poor lung mechanics and compliance at baseline, it is reasonable to consider that any further insult places patients with PS at a disadvantage during respiratory recovery. However, this remains a possible association rather than a definitive explanation, given the multifactorial nature of respiratory failure in critical illness.

Rib anomalies can also affect lung development and the displacement of thoracic organs, leading to dextrocardia and situs abnormalities in severe cases [18]. Dextrocardia refers to the positioning of the heart in the right side of the thorax with a rightward apex. This anomaly can occur in isolation or with associated situs abnormalities or other structural heart abnormalities. The combination of dextrocardia with PS can point to a shared pathogenic mechanism. This led to the development of multiple theories to explain the overlap. One study hypothesized extensive vascular compromise affecting the arterial supply of the anterior thoracic wall and pericardial ligaments, which could contribute to this anomaly [19]. The isolated dextrocardia in our patient could be explained by an asymmetric or localized vascular insult rather than a more extensive insult, which could have resulted in a complete situs abnormality. Another theory points to the possibility of mechanical factors influencing the positioning of the cardiac tissue due to ribcage anomalies. Studies have noted that patients with agenesis of two or more ribs presented with dextrocardia, but it was never associated with partial agenesis of a single rib [20]. Despite our patient having a complete set of ribs, their hypoplastic morphology created an analogous mechanical environment to facilitate dextrocardia. Another interesting association is that almost all PS patients with dextrocardia are associated with left‐sided anomalies [20]. Our patient had a lot of cardiac complications, including atrial fibrillation, nonischemic cardiomyopathy. Although they cannot be strongly attributed to the presence of dextrocardia, how much its presence contributed to the morbidity remains a question.

PS is primarily a clinical diagnosis based on the constellation of clinical findings. Further evaluation, including various imaging modalities, is considered to support the diagnosis and help in surgical planning. Chest X‐rays can identify rib and chest wall anomalies. CT and MRI scans provide detailed views of the thoracic musculature and skeletal structure, which are crucial for preoperative assessment [21]. Hand X‐rays can delineate bone abnormalities in the digits, and 3D reconstruction aids in visualizing the extent of musculoskeletal defects. Echocardiography is essential in cases suspected of dextrocardia to assess cardiac anatomy and function.

Treatment is tailored according to the severity of symptoms and the patient's functional and cosmetic concerns. In mild cases, no intervention may be necessary. Management otherwise involves a multidisciplinary approach, with plastic and reconstructive surgery forming the cornerstone of treatment. Life‐threatening anomalies such as lung herniation may require immediate postnatal chest wall correction by a pediatric surgeon. A variety of surgical reconstruction procedures exist currently and include chest wall reconstruction using muscle flaps, prosthetic ribs, or tissue expanders [13, 22], syndactyly release, tendon transfer, and phalangeal reconstruction, and prosthetic devices for patients with severe limb abnormalities [14].

Breast and nipple reconstruction is a key component of cosmetic surgery and is performed in a staged manner in adolescence or early teenage years for female patients. Surgical treatments include the use of breast implants, fat grafts, and muscle flaps for chest wall asymmetry, followed by a nipple‐areola complex reconstruction for cosmesis [23]. Besides surgery, psychological and psychosocial support constitute another essential pillar of management and are crucial due to the cosmetic impact among adolescent and teenage females. Collaboration with clinical psychologists for counseling, peer support, and body image therapy can improve quality of life [24]. In cases with dextrocardia, cardiologic consultation is essential. While isolated dextrocardia often requires no intervention, associated defects may necessitate medical or surgical management. Our patient was not interested in any surgical intervention for his chest wall or limb abnormalities. Beyond an echocardiogram, no further evaluation has been recommended by the cardiologist.

Most patients with PS have a normal life expectancy, although functional limitations can arise depending on the extent of the skeletal defects. Younger patients, particularly females, may experience psychological stress related to body image and breast hypoplasia. Early counseling and surgical referral for reconstruction can improve quality of life. Dextrocardia serves as a clue in the pathogenesis of the development of these anomalies, but presenting as an isolated finding without situs anomalies or structural heart disease has an excellent prognosis.

Conclusion

Poland syndrome is a heterogeneous congenital disorder characterized by variable chest wall and upper‐extremity abnormalities. It's rare coexistence with isolated dextrocardia expands the clinical complexity of the condition and underscores the importance of identifying the associated anatomic variations. Although long‐term outcomes are often favorable, chest wall deformities and related physiologic limitations may contribute to increasing morbidity as patients age. Early recognition of these consequences can guide cardiopulmonary evaluation and help anticipate challenges during critical illness, particularly in patients with compromised respiratory mechanics.
