Emerging insights into PPARα′s role in lean metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease: Implications for targeted therapies
Authors: Yafei Yang, Furong Zhang, Zhuang Peng, Yafei Wu, Ling Li, Zixia Shen, Qifa Ye, Jiang Yue, Shufang Na
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lean metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), defined as hepatic steatosis in individuals with normal BMI (<25 kg/m2 in non-Asians; <23 kg/m2 in Asians), is a clinically paradoxical phenotype in which patients face risks of progressive fibrosis, cardiovascular events, and liver-related mortality comparable to or exceeding obese counterparts. Visceral adiposity, dietary stressors, cholesterol overload, and genetic susceptibility involving PNPLA3 variants and PPARA polymorphisms synergistically disrupt hepatic lipid homeostasis. At the core of this dysregulation lies peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα), a master transcriptional regulator of hepatic fatty acid oxidation, ketogenesis, and anti-inflammatory signaling. PPARα suppression aries through convergent mechanisms, including aging-related RAGE signaling, fructose-mediated epigenetic silencing, gut‒liver axis disruption, and lipotoxicity, operating independently of systemic obesity. This review demonstrates that: (1) impaired PPARα activity underlies lean MASLD pathogenesis through transcriptional, epigenetic, and post-translational suppression; (2) synthetic PPARα agonists show limited applicability in this metabolically fragile population; and (3) natural product-derived modulators engage PPARα alongside FXR and LXR networks to restore metabolic balance. The PPARα–FGF21–ketogenesis axis and nuclear receptor crosstalk provide the mechanistic rationale for network-level therapeutic approaches. We advocate for spatially selective hepatic PPARα reactivation guided by lean-specific biomarker and genotype-stratified trial design.

Introduction

Lean metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (lean MASLD) refers to hepatic steatosis occurring in individuals with normal body mass index (BMI; <25 kg/m2 in non-Asians and <23 kg/m2 in Asians), highlighting a pathogenic paradigm distinct from obesity-driven MASLD. The terminology used to describe this condition has evolved considerably: from non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD, introduced in 1980), to metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD, proposed in 2020), and ultimately to MASLD, formally adopted in 2023 following multi-society Delphi consensus as the current international standard1,2. Notably, MASLD demonstrates superior applicability to lean individuals compared with MAFLD, as it relies on five routine cardiometabolic parameters while eliminating the requirement for homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein; in a well-characterized lean cohort, 83.5% fulfilled MASLD criteria versus only 49.4% under MAFLD3. In accordance with this consensus, MASLD is used throughout this review, and lean MASLD refers specifically to steatotic liver disease in normal-BMI individuals who may nonetheless harbor occult metabolic dysfunction. However, a subset of lean patients remains unclassifiable under either framework, underscoring the inherent limitations of current diagnostic criteria in fully capturing the heterogeneity of this population4.

Despite these diagnostic complexities, the global burden of MASLD is substantial, affecting approximately 32.4% of the population, with rising prevalence and a notable shift toward younger age groups and normal-BMI phenotypes, especially in Asia, where lean MASLD accounts for a significant proportion of cases5, 6, 7. Although traditionally associated with obesity, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, MASLD can develop in individuals who appear metabolically normal on conventional assessment due to visceral adiposity, excessive dietary fructose or saturated fat intake, and genetic susceptibility (e.g., PNPLA3 rs738409), yet may harbor subclinical metabolic disturbances such as insulin resistance and low-grade inflammation8,9. These factors converge to disrupt hepatic lipid homeostasis through mechanisms that are only partially shared with obesity-driven MASLD. Importantly, hepatic steatosis can be detected by sensitive imaging modalities such as controlled attenuation parameter even in some healthy, non-obese individuals without suspected liver disease, underscoring that steatotic liver disease is not confined to overt obesity-associated presentations; moreover, lean steatotic liver disease is now recognized as a distinct clinical entity, although the degree of accompanying metabolic or biochemical abnormality varies across cohorts10,11.

The pathogenesis of MASLD has evolved from the outdated “two-hit” hypothesis to the “multiple parallel hits” model12, reflecting the disease's inherent heterogeneity. This framework integrates gut dysbiosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, endoplasmic reticulum stress, sarcopenia, and genetic variants as co-drivers of steatosis and inflammation, operating collectively against a background of concealed metabolic dysfunction that often escapes conventional biochemical detection13, 14, 15, 16, 17. Notably, despite lower BMI, affected individuals often harbor elevated visceral fat, which promotes portal delivery of free fatty acids (FFAs) and pro-inflammatory cytokines to the liver, thereby fueling steatosis and injury18. Furthermore, genetic variants such as PNPLA3 rs738409 may amplify this hepatic vulnerability by impairing lipid droplet remodeling and augmenting lipotoxic signaling, particularly in the absence of the metabolic buffering capacity conferred by adipose tissue expansion in obese individuals.

Among the multiple pathogenic contributors to lean MASLD, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α (PPARα, NR1C1), a master transcriptional regulator of hepatic fatty acid oxidation (FAO) and very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) secretion19,20, has emerged as a particularly relevant factor. While circulating FFAs contribute significantly to hepatic triglyceride (TG) accumulation, one of the central defects in MASLD stems from impaired lipid catabolism, to which suppressed PPARα activity is a key contributor. It should be noted that PPARα suppression likely represents a downstream convergence point of multiple upstream perturbations, including altered lipid droplet dynamics, mitochondrial dysfunction, gut–liver axis dysregulation, and genetic susceptibility, rather than an isolated primary defect. Studies in hepatocyte-specific PPARα knockout mice demonstrate that loss of hepatic PPARα alone is sufficient to induce steatosis and inflammation during aging, even in the absence of obesity or systemic insulin resistance21,22. Recent evidence further confirms that diminished hepatocyte PPARα expression independently drives lean MASLD, dissociated from adipose tissue dysfunction, suggesting it may represent an important liver-intrinsic contributor to disease pathogenesis23.

This pathophysiological specificity in lean MASLD offers a compelling rationale for PPARα-targeted interventions. Although fibrates have been used for decades to modulate lipid profiles via PPARα activation, their efficacy in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) remains limited partly due to relatively low hepatoselectivity and potential off-target effects, and safety concerns persist. In contrast, natural PPARα modulators, particularly flavonoids, terpenoids, and active constituents from traditional Chinese herbal medicines (e.g., berberine, tanshinone IIA, puerarin), may exhibit multi-target effects that concurrently enhance FAO, suppress inflammation, and restore gut–liver axis integrity. These properties make them promising candidates for phenotype-specific management of lean MASLD, though large-scale clinical validation remains warranted.

In this review, we explore the potential involvement of PPARα dysregulation in lean MASLD, acknowledging that direct evidence specifically linking PPARα to this subtype remains limited and that much of the current understanding is extrapolated from broader MASLD research. We synthesize available evidence across: (1) the unique molecular drivers that impair PPARα signaling in lean individuals; (2) the mechanistic basis of synthetic and natural PPARα agonists, with emphasis on herbal-derived compounds; and (3) emerging strategies for precision targeting of the PPARα axis. By integrating insights from genetics, diet, and traditional medicine, we aim to provide a scientific foundation for developing safer, more effective interventions tailored to this underrecognized yet high-risk MASLD subtype.

Lean MASLD is not merely a mild variant of obesity-driven liver disease. Instead, this review synthesizes current evidence to suggest it is a heterogeneous and pathophysiologically complex subtype, often driven by concealed metabolic dysfunctions that evade routine clinical detection. Within this multifactorial framework, hepatic PPARα dysregulation emerges as a critical contributor. Consequently, we propose that restoring PPARα activity may offer a promising therapeutic pathway, a hypothesis that now requires rigorous validation through clinical trials specifically targeting the lean population.

Lean MASLD: The metabolic paradox and comparable prognostic risk

A 2020 meta-analysis reported 19.2% of MASLD patients were lean, with a global lean MASLD prevalence of 5.1%24. Notably, while this study identified the highest prevalence in Europe and the lowest in East Asia, a subsequent meta-analysis revealed an inverse pattern: Asia showed the highest lean MASLD prevalence (4.8%) and Europe the lowest (2.2%)25. This discrepancy may reflect Asia's larger non-obese population base, genetic predispositions (e.g., PNPLA3 variants), and visceral adiposity despite normal BMI. Compared with individuals with obese MASLD, those with lean MASLD exhibit lower levels of multiple metabolic and biochemical parameters, including metabolic markers (mean systolic and diastolic blood pressure, hemoglobin A1C, and homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance, TG), biochemical markers (mean serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT), albumin, and creatinine levels), as well as mean uric acid levels and liver stiffness10,26,27. Multiple studies have consistently found that lean MASLD is associated with female sex, whereas other characteristics have depended on the sample population and research period28,29. Yet paradoxically, despite these seemingly favorable metabolic and histological profiles, lean MASLD patients face disease trajectories and long-term outcomes comparable to, or in some respects worse than, their non-lean counterparts30,31. This metabolic paradox cannot be explained by conventional anthropometric measures alone, and its underlying mechanisms span four interconnected dimensions: histological severity independent of metabolic burden, visceral adiposity beyond BMI, genetic susceptibility involving both established and candidate loci, and environmental exposures that interact with individual metabolic backgrounds, each contributing to the suppression of hepatic PPARα signaling as a shared pathogenic node.

Collectively, these observations reveal a fundamental paradox: lean MASLD patients present with more favorable metabolic profiles yet suffer comparable, or worse, long-term outcomes, a discordance that implicates mechanisms beyond adiposity alone. Emerging evidence points to the suppression of PPARα signaling as a central pathogenic node, impairing hepatic FAO, promoting lipid accumulation, and attenuating anti-inflammatory responses in the absence of overt obesity. This suppression may arise through distinct but convergent mechanisms, including transcriptional downregulation, post-translational modification, and insufficient endogenous ligand availability, each of which represents a potential point of therapeutic intervention (Fig. 1). This molecular vulnerability, shaped by the interplay of visceral adiposity, environmental stressors, and genetic susceptibility, not only underlies the paradoxical severity of lean MASLD but also defines a potential therapeutic target. Heightened histological severity in lean MASLD patients

MASLD is characterized by distinct pathological changes that range from simple hepatic steatosis to MASH, fibrosis, and cirrhosis. Hepatic steatosis involves fat accumulation in at least 5% of hepatocytes, graded as mild (5%–33%), moderate (33%–66%), or severe (>66%)32, 33, 34. MASH is diagnosed when steatosis, inflammation, and hepatocellular injury are present, with markers including ballooning degeneration and Mallory-Denk bodies. Fibrosis begins as perisinusoidal fibrosis and can progress to bridging fibrosis and ultimately cirrhosis. Additional features include abnormal lipid metabolism and oxidative stress, both of which contribute to liver damage35. Histological evaluation via liver biopsy is essential for distinguishing simple hepatic steatosis from MASH, with the degree of fibrosis being a key prognostic factor, particularly in identifying patients at higher risk for liver-related mortality.

Pathological diagnosis remains the gold standard for MASLD; however, liver biopsy is rarely performed in clinical practice due to its invasive nature and an unfavorable perceived risk–benefit profile. Lean MASLD patients are predominantly identified incidentally during routine physical examinations, with subsequent pathological confirmation often omitted. This diagnostic gap has resulted in limited comparative studies on histopathological distinctions between lean and obese MASLD subtypes. A clinical study involving 466 MASLD patients showed that lean MASLD patients displayed higher rates of lobular inflammation, ballooning degeneration, and cirrhosis compared to overweight (BMI >25.0 and ≤30.0 kg/m2) and obese patients (BMI >30.0 kg/m2), with a significant proportion of lean patients diagnosed with MASH36. A large Asian study involving 1273 patients with MASLD, including 267 lean cases, reported consistent findings: lean individuals accounted for a significant proportion of biopsy-diagnosed MASLD cases, and showed unique metabolic profiles as well as more pronounced histologic severity compared with other body mass index groups. Notably, that study further emphasized that interactions among hepatic progenitor cells, stellate cells, and macrophages are primarily governed by the progression of hepatic fibrosis rather than BMI, suggesting that body weight alone is an inadequate surrogate for histological disease burden37.

These histological observations carry direct prognostic implications that extend well beyond liver biopsy findings. While individuals with lean MASLD demonstrate better metabolic profiles at baseline, their rates of long-term hepatic and extrahepatic events, as well as overall mortality, do not significantly differ from those with non-lean MASLD and may in some respects be worse. A multinational study spanning four countries demonstrated that lean MASLD patients are at risk of progressing to severe liver disease and developing metabolic comorbidities and cardiovascular complications, independent of obesity progression and PNPLA3 genotype, underscoring that disease trajectory in this population is driven by mechanisms beyond adiposity alone38. Critically, after adjusting for age, BMI shows no significant correlation with clinical events in MASLD patients, highlighting its limited utility in risk stratification. This limitation is further illustrated by a 19.9-year longitudinal follow-up study, which found that lean MASLD patients, despite exhibiting lower baseline fibrosis scores, faced a higher risk of developing severe liver disease compared to their non-lean counterparts, a finding that directly challenges the assumption that a favorable histological starting point confers long-term protection39.

The principal etiological determinants of lean MASLD, encompassing visceral adiposity, dietary patterns characterized by excessive fructose or saturated fat consumption, and inherited genetic susceptibility, synergistically induce a pathological metabolic milieu, culminating in hepatic lipid accumulation and subsequent inflammatory cascades, a process exacerbated by impaired PPARα-mediated FAO and anti-inflammatory signaling. Visceral fat drives the development of lean MASLD

The inadequacy of BMI as a risk stratification tool in lean MASLD is most clearly explained by the distinct metabolic roles of different adipose tissue depots. Longitudinal studies demonstrate that visceral adipose tissue (VAT) accumulation increases MASLD risk, while subcutaneous adipose tissue promotes regression by sequestering TGs and reducing hepatic FFAs exposure40,41. Visceral obesity significantly elevates MASLD risk even in non-obese individuals, with Asian populations exhibiting higher ectopic liver fat deposition at comparable BMI levels42, 43, 44. This aligns with the “metabolically obese, normal-weight” (MONW) phenotype, characterized by elevated visceral fat, TGs, and insulin resistance despite normal BMI, underscoring visceral adiposity as a key driver of lean MASLD pathogenesis45, 46, 47.

Clinically, waist circumference (WC) has emerged as a more sensitive proxy for visceral adiposity than BMI in this population. A notable study classifying MASLD patients by both BMI and WC revealed that individuals with a lean BMI but elevated WC carry the highest risk of all-cause mortality and increased cardiovascular mortality compared to those with a lean BMI and normal WC48. This finding reframes WC not merely as an anthropometric measure, but as a clinically actionable indicator of visceral fat burden, one that captures disease risk invisible to BMI-based screening and is frequently overlooked in prognostic studies of lean MASLD. The mechanistic basis of this relationship lies in the unique properties of VAT. Unlike subcutaneous adipocytes, VAT adipocytes exhibit higher insulin resistance and TG synthesis capacity, and release their lipolytic products directly into the portal vein, exerting hepatotoxic effects49. VAT expansion elevates hepatic FFA delivery via lipolysis, driving substrate-dependent TG synthesis and gluconeogenesis, thereby exacerbating hepatic insulin resistance and steatosis independent of overall obesity18,50. VAT-derived FFAs further activate hepatic macrophages, directly linking visceral adiposity to hepatic inflammation in lean MASLD51. Adiponectin, inversely associated with insulin resistance and MASLD severity, shows reduced levels in both lean and obese MASLD despite lower fasting insulin in lean individuals, suggesting that shared early metabolic dysfunction precedes overt weight gain43.

Critically, excess FFA flux from VAT can overwhelm hepatic lipid-handling capacity and is associated with reduced PPARα expression and activity, likely mediated through inflammatory signals, insulin resistance, and lipotoxic intermediates, thereby impairing mitochondrial β-oxidation and accelerating hepatic steatosis. This mechanistic link between visceral adiposity and hepatic PPARα dysregulation characterizes the metabolic landscape of lean MASLD. It explains why individuals with a high WC, despite a normal BMI, face a disproportionately high risk of disease progression. Therefore, visceral fat metrics, particularly WC, are essential for early screening and risk stratification, especially in Asian populations where the MONW phenotype is prevalent.

The genetic inheritance of lean MASLD

Genetic factors are increasingly recognized as determinants of MASLD pathogenesis, particularly with advances in genomic research52. The first genome-wide association study on MASLD identified PNPLA3 variants as contributors to racial differences in disease susceptibility53, thereby establishing PNPLA3 as the strongest genetic risk factor. Subsequent studies have identified additional genetic variants (TM6SF2, MBOAT7, GCKR, HSD17B13) encoding proteins involved in hepatic lipid metabolism54,55. However, most research focuses on obese populations, where genetic effects are amplified by concurrent metabolic dysfunction. While one Chinese study found no significant genetic differences between lean MASLD and non-lean MASLD subjects56, multiple international studies demonstrate that lean MASLD patients exhibit higher frequencies of the PNPLA3 rs738409 GG genotype compared to overweight/obese MASLD counterparts57, 58, 59, suggesting that racial background, lifestyle, and body composition modulate genetic penetrance. Notably, the TM6SF2 rs58542926 TT genotype correlates with reduced serum TGs exclusively in lean individuals58, and cholesteryl ester transfer protein variants also show lean-specific associations60. Whole-exome sequencing of lean MASLD patients has further revealed that PEMT variants confer a threefold increased risk, while the TBC1D1 rs2279028 variant has been implicated in lean MASLD progression via high-density lipoprotein (HDL) modulation61,62. At the epigenetic level, lean MASLD patients exhibit reduced serum macroH2A1.1/1.2 histone variants, implicating chromatin remodeling as an additional layer of disease regulation63. A recent genomic analysis further found that two of six lean MASLD individuals without visceral fat harbored unique mutations potentially associated with fatty liver, reinforcing the view that lean MASLD may harbor a distinct genetic substrate64.

Beyond common variants, several monogenic disorders have been associated with hepatic steatosis in non-obese individuals, including lysosomal acid lipase deficiency, familial hypobetalipoproteinemia, abetalipoproteinemia, and lipodystrophy65, 66, 67. With the transition from NAFLD to the MASLD nomenclature, the previous exclusionary diagnostic criteria have been replaced by the requirement for at least one of five cardiometabolic risk factors. Under this revised framework, steatosis arising from monogenic disorders may no longer fall within the MASLD definition, necessitating a reappraisal of how genetic disorders intersect with current disease classification.

In addition to these established loci, polymorphisms in PPARA, encoding the master transcriptional regulator of hepatic FAO and lipid homeostasis, have emerged as candidate modifier genes in this context. Unlike core MASLD loci, PPARA variants manifest primarily as metabolic effect heterogeneity: different polymorphisms differentially influence lipid profiles, FAO efficiency, insulin sensitivity, and responsiveness to dietary and exercise interventions. The L162V variant (rs1800206) is associated with less favorable lipid metabolic phenotypes, including elevated TGs, apolipoprotein B, and WC-related indices68. In contrast, V227A (rs1800234), which is more prevalent in East Asian populations, is generally associated with lower TGs and a more favorable lipid profile69, 70, 71. A mouse model recapitulating this variant validated these observations: V227A reduced plasma TGs without altering body mass or hepatic lipid accumulation, an effect mechanistically attributable to enhanced hepatic PPARα target gene expression and upregulation of Lpl, the predominant enzyme responsible for circulating TG hydrolysis72. The relatively favorable lipid phenotype conferred by V227A may partly explain the metabolic heterogeneity observed among lean MASLD patients in Asian cohorts, where this variant is enriched, despite their overall higher disease prevalence. Furthermore, loci such as rs4253778 may modulate metabolic responses to aerobic training, suggesting that PPARA variation is particularly relevant in shaping how individuals respond to lifestyle exposures73. TM6SF2 knockdown has been shown to impair FAO and reduce PPARα expression, directly linking impaired VLDL secretion to suppressed hepatic lipid catabolism74. Similarly, patatin like phospholipase domain containing 3 (PNPLA3) preferentially hydrolyzes polyunsaturated TGs to mobilize polyunsaturated fatty acids for phospholipid remodeling and lipoprotein secretion, and liver-specific knockout or knockdown of Pnpla3 under lipogenic conditions results in more severe hepatic steatosis and reduced plasma VLDL-TG levels, consistent with a lipid-trapping environment that may further constrain PPARα-driven oxidative capacity75. Together, these findings suggest that the lipid-handling deficits and altered lipid species imposed by core MASLD loci create a metabolic milieu permissive to reduced PPARα expression and activity, through downstream inflammatory signaling, insulin resistance, and lipotoxic intermediates. This interaction suggests that the effects of core loci on hepatic lipid metabolism may be further amplified or attenuated by concurrent PPARA polymorphisms. Taken together, PPARA polymorphisms are best understood not as direct susceptibility variants for MASLD, but as modifier genes that sculpt the upstream metabolic landscape and thereby indirectly modulate hepatic fat deposition and disease heterogeneity. This indirect, metabolism-mediated mode of influence distinguishes PPARA from core MASLD loci, while underscoring its potential relevance in explaining inter-individual variation, particularly within lean populations.

Overall, the genetic architecture of lean MASLD partially overlaps with that of obese MASLD but is distinguished by lean-specific variant associations, differential genetic penetrance across body composition strata, and a greater relative contribution of modifier genes operating through metabolic background modulation. Research specifically targeting genetic determinants in lean individuals remains limited and represents a critical gap, given the unique phenotypic and prognostic profile of this population.

Environmental factors leading to lean MASLD

In addition to genetic susceptibility, acquired environmental factors, including dietary habits, physical activity, and socioeconomic environment, play a crucial role in MASLD pathogenesis. Critically, many of these environmental exposures converge on a shared molecular mechanism: the suppression of hepatic PPARα signaling, which impairs FAO and promotes lipid accumulation. In lean individuals, where the metabolic buffer provided by subcutaneous adipose tissue is limited, this PPARα-mediated vulnerability may be disproportionately consequential, rendering environmental stressors particularly potent drivers of disease initiation and progression.

Fructose. MASLD, recognized as the hepatic manifestation of metabolic syndrome, has risen in prevalence alongside increased global consumption of added sugars, notably sucrose, fructose, and high-fructose corn syrup, in sugar-sweetened beverages, with global consumption increasing by 16% between 1990 and 201876. Comparative studies reveal that fructose, more than glucose, strongly correlates with obesity, hepatic steatosis, and lipogenesis in mice, as well as ectopic fat accumulation and MASLD in humans77, 78, 79, 80, 81. This dose-dependent association is mediated through multiple pathways: fructose disrupts central appetite regulation by enhancing hunger signals and suppressing satiety hormones, promotes hepatic lipogenesis, and compromises intestinal barrier integrity, triggering endotoxemia82, 83, 84. Mechanistically, fructose suppresses hepatic PPARα expression and activity through multiple converging mechanisms: fructose intake has been shown to induce methylation of the PPARα promoter region, constituting an epigenetic mechanism that directly silences PPARα transcription and contributes to insulin resistance and hyperlipidemia85,86. Additionally, fructose downregulates the expression and activity of sirtuin 1 (SIRT1), a key positive regulator of PPARα that enhances its capacity to drive FAO through direct deacetylation of PPARα87,88. Through these epigenetic and post-translational mechanisms, fructose shifts the hepatic metabolic balance away from lipid catabolism and toward accumulation.

Recent models challenge the traditional view of fructose-derived acetyl-CoA as the primary lipogenic substrate, proposing instead that hepatic fructose metabolism transcriptionally activates de novo lipogenesis (DNL), while gut microbiota-derived acetate fuels DNL89. In high-fat/high-fructose diet models, which best recapitulate human MASLD phenotypes in mice, PPARα downregulation has been consistently observed alongside steatosis, and pharmacological PPARα activation in these models has been shown to ameliorate fructose-induced metabolic disturbances, including hepatic insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and endoplasmic reticulum stress, collectively underscoring PPARα as a central mediator of fructose hepatotoxicity83.

Emerging evidence more directly links fructose-mediated PPARα suppression to lean MASLD. Lean MASLD patients exhibit higher soft drink consumption than healthy controls, independent of metabolic syndrome90, 91, 92. Animal studies demonstrate that isocaloric fructose-rich diets induce MASLD without obesity, confirming that fructose's hepatotoxic effects operate independently of adiposity93,94. More specifically, adolescent mice fed low-fat/high-fructose diets developed MASLD despite maintaining normal weight, with consistent downregulation of hepatic PPARα and its target genes, including Cpt1a and Acox195. These findings collectively position PPARα suppression, operating through epigenetic silencing, SIRT1-mediated post-translational regulation, and transcriptional downregulation, as the central mechanistic node linking dietary fructose exposure to lean MASLD independent of obesity, and highlight PPARα restoration as a rational and specific therapeutic target in this population.

Dietary fats. The Western diet, characterized by high consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages, processed/red meats, refined grains, animal fats, and food additives, consistently correlates with MASLD risk96, 97, 98, 99. In contrast, the Mediterranean diet demonstrates protective effects and is recommended for MASLD management100,101. Beyond sugar-driven DNL, saturated fats exacerbate MASLD by enhancing adipose tissue lipolysis and intrahepatic TG accumulation, with comparative studies showing that high-fat diets induce greater intrahepatic TG deposition than isoenergetic high-carbohydrate regimens102,103. The impact of dietary fat on hepatic lipid metabolism is directly mediated, in part, through PPARα, with effects that vary substantially by fatty acid chain length, saturation, and dose. In vitro evidence demonstrates that saturated fatty acids such as palmitic and stearic acids suppress PPARα transactivation, whereas α-linolenic acid significantly enhances it, and long-chain omega-3 fatty acids activate PPARα at physiological doses but may inhibit it at supraphysiological concentrations, indicating that both the type and quantity of dietary fat determine the direction of PPARα modulation104. Saturated fats disproportionately promote hepatic lipid storage, metabolic dysregulation, and insulin resistance compared to unsaturated fats105. These detrimental effects are further compounded at the level of PPARα regulation: saturated fats may in part suppress PPARα transcriptional activity through activation of the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) inflammatory signaling, thereby impairing FAO and amplifying lipid accumulation. The hepatoprotective role of PPARα against adverse dietary fat exposure is further underscored by evidence that Pparα-null mice fed a trans-fatty acid-enriched diet develop more severe hepatic steatosis, impaired fatty acid catabolism, and heightened NF-κB-driven inflammation compared to wild-type counterparts, directly demonstrating that PPARα restrains both lipid accumulation and inflammatory cascades triggered by dietary fat106.

Clinical evidence supports the replacement of saturated fatty acids (SFAs) with monounsaturated fatty acids to reduce hepatic fibroinflammation, while omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (n-3 PUFAs), which serve as natural PPARα ligands that enhance its transcriptional activity and downstream oxidative capacity, ameliorate obesity-related hepatic steatosis in preclinical models, in contrast to omega-6 PUFAs which lack this effect. Notably, declining plasma PUFA levels correlate with accelerated hepatocellular carcinoma progression in lean MASH mice, suggesting potential prognostic utility107,108.

Lean MASLD patients exhibit dietary patterns comparable to obese MASLD counterparts in total caloric intake, macronutrient distribution, and lifestyle factors109. Combined dietary modification (reduced carbohydrates/fats) and exercise effectively achieve MASLD remission in non-obese populations110. In lean individuals, who lack the metabolic buffering capacity of subcutaneous adipose tissue, the quality of dietary fat intake may exert a disproportionate influence on hepatic PPARα activity and disease trajectory, an effect further modulated by PPARA genetic variants such as V227A, as discussed in Section 2.371. Taken together, the differential effects of dietary fat subtypes on lean MASLD risk converge on a shared mechanistic axis: SFAs and trans fatty acids suppress PPARα through inflammatory signaling and impaired fatty acid catabolism, while specific PUFAs restore its activity through direct ligand binding. The magnitude of this response is further shaped by individual PPARA genotype. Dietary fat quality, particularly the reduction of SFAs and trans fatty acids alongside enrichment of n-3 PUFAs, therefore represents a modifiable and genotype-sensitive determinant of hepatic PPARα activity, and a rational dietary target for lean MASLD management.

Cholesterol and choline. Unlike dietary fatty acids, which modulate PPARα activity primarily through direct ligand binding and inflammatory signaling as discussed above, cholesterol and choline influence lean MASLD through distinct metabolic axes, namely cholesterol absorption and phosphatidylcholine-dependent VLDL secretion, yet ultimately converge on shared downstream consequences for hepatic lipid homeostasis and PPARα function. Dietary cholesterol plays a particularly prominent role in lean MASLD pathogenesis.

Paigen diet, which contains standard components supplemented with 1.25% cholesterol and 0.5% sodium cholate, induces features of lean MASLD with severe inflammation in mice via gut microbiota dysregulation and immune activation111. Sterol-regulatory element binding protein (SREBP) cleavage-activating protein (SCAP), a cholesterol sensor, mediates aggravated inflammatory responses in paigen-diet-fed mice, promoting adipose lipolysis and hepatic lipid deposition to drive lean MASLD112. Clinically, non-obese MASLD patients exhibit higher dietary cholesterol intake than obese MASLD counterparts, with excessive cholesterol driving MASLD through liver X receptor (LXR)-SREBP-1c-mediated DNL113,114. Serum cholesterol emerges as a key biomarker for advanced liver disease in non-obese patients, underscoring its pathogenic relevance115. The intersection of cholesterol metabolism and PPARα signaling is supported by multiple lines of evidence: single nucleotide polymorphisms in PPARA have been associated with cholesterol and LDL levels, and PPARA polymorphisms have been shown to influence cholesterol responses to statin therapy, modulating the efficacy of simvastatin across individuals116,117. Furthermore, high-cholesterol diet feeding in Pparα-null mice exacerbates TG accumulation and hepatic inflammation compared to wild-type counterparts, directly demonstrating that PPARα restrains the hepatotoxic consequences of cholesterol overload118. Prenatal alcohol exposure has further been shown to suppress hepatic expression of both Pparα and the low-density lipoprotein receptor Ldlr in male offspring, implicating coordinated downregulation of fatty acid catabolism and cholesterol re-uptake as a mechanism linking early environmental insults to lasting metabolic vulnerability119.

Methionine-choline-deficient (MCD) diets induce steatosis and necroinflammation with weight loss in rodents, mirroring human choline deficiency-related hepatic pathology reversible by choline supplementation120,121. A Chinese cohort study links higher dietary choline intake to reduced MASLD risk in normal-weight women122. Choline restriction promotes SREBP-1-dependent lipogenesis, while phosphatidylethanolamine N-methyltransferase (PEMT) deficiency, which impairs phosphatidylcholine synthesis required for VLDL secretion, paradoxically protects against systemic metabolic syndrome yet exacerbates hepatic steatohepatitis and promotes liver tumor development in knockout models123, 124, 125. This apparent contradiction reflects the divergence between whole-body metabolic phenotype and liver-specific pathology: impaired VLDL export traps lipids within hepatocytes, creating a lipotoxic environment independent of adiposity. Reduced PEMT expression correlates with lean MASH progression in patients, with estrogen-regulated PEMT activity explaining sex-specific choline sensitivity in premenopausal women126. The relevance of PPARα to choline-deficient liver disease is supported by evidence that both selective PPARα-modulator (SPPARMα) and fenofibrate ameliorate MCD diet-induced non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, with increased hepatic FAO gene expression, suggesting that pharmacological restoration of PPARα activity can compensate for the lipid export failure imposed by choline deficiency127.

Collectively, cholesterol overload and choline deficiency suppress PPARα activity through distinct but complementary mechanisms: cholesterol excess drives hepatic inflammation and lipotoxicity that impairs PPARα transcriptional activity, while choline deficiency impairs VLDL secretion and creates a lipid-trapping environment that further constrains PPARα-driven oxidative capacity. Together with the fructose and dietary fat quality mechanisms discussed above, these findings position PPARα as a shared molecular nexus through which multiple nutritional stressors drive lean MASLD progression, and highlight dietary cholesterol restriction and choline adequacy as additional modifiable targets for PPARα-centered management strategies in this population.

Lifestyle. Beyond dietary composition, behavioral and psychosocial environmental factors represent an additional dimension through which lean MASLD risk is shaped, similarly converging on visceral adiposity and hepatic metabolic dysregulation as common downstream pathways.

Sedentary behavior (SB) independently correlates with cardiometabolic risk, WC, and MASLD progression, irrespective of physical activity levels128, 129, 130. While meta-analyses show no direct association between SB and body weight, SB-linked MASLD likely stems from metabolic dysregulation involving insulinemic/lipidemic pathways131,132. Both SB and psychosocial stress exert their influence on lean MASLD in part through promotion of visceral adiposity independent of total body weight. Elevated perceived stress promotes visceral adiposity and WC expansion without BMI changes, mediated by hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis activation133, 134, 135. Stress-induced glucocorticoid surges drive hyperphagia, particularly for calorie-dense foods, via reward circuitry modulation, while visceral fat accumulation is exacerbated by 11β-HSD1-mediated glucocorticoid redistribution in adipose tissue136, 137, 138, 139, 140. These mechanisms collectively explain stress-associated visceral obesity and its potential role in lean MASLD, though causal evidence remains limited.

Lifestyle interventions, including diet, exercise, and stress management, remain cornerstone approaches for MASLD management across BMI categories141. Resistance training directly improves hepatic steatosis independent of weight loss, while stress reduction mitigates HPA axis overactivation142,143. Notably, exercise has been shown to upregulate hepatic PPARα expression and stimulate mitochondrial biogenesis, representing a non-pharmacological counterpart to the PPARα suppression driven by fructose, adverse dietary fat composition, cholesterol overload, and choline deficiency discussed above. In lean individuals, where pharmacological intervention thresholds may be higher and metabolic vulnerability is disproportionately driven by lifestyle exposures, exercise-mediated PPARα restoration may be of particular therapeutic relevance144,145. Further molecular evidence for lean MASLD-specific PPARα dysregulation comes from Gm4951 knockout mice, which develop MASLD without alterations in body weight or glucose metabolism, driven by downregulation of lipid oxidation genes including Pparα, Acox1, Acot1, and Acadl, directly implicating PPARα suppression as a sufficient driver of lean MASLD independent of obesity146.

Though lean and obese MASLD share overlapping risk factors (gut–liver axis dysfunction, inflammation, nuclear receptor dysregulation), lean-specific therapeutic targets require deeper exploration. Current strategies focus on modulating gut microbiota, intestinal barrier integrity, inflammatory pathways, and nuclear receptors (PPARs, farnesoid X receptor (FXR), LXR), with preclinical studies having identified lean MASLD-specific molecular mechanisms warranting clinical validation147, 148, 149, 150. Among these, adipose Snail1 deletion exacerbates hepatic steatosis in non-obese mice by suppressing adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL)-mediated fatty acid mobilization, and lysyl oxidase like 1 (LOXL1) mediates fibrosis progression in non-obese MASLD, together identifying adipose-hepatic crosstalk and extracellular matrix remodeling as lean-specific pathogenic axes distinct from PPARα-centered lipid oxidation deficits. Herbal medicine-derived compounds that target PPARα have emerged as a promising avenue in this context, given the convergence of multiple environmental stressors on PPARα suppression and the need for interventions that can restore oxidative capacity without relying solely on weight reduction.

Collectively, these findings underscore that lean MASLD is not a benign condition. Its pathogenesis reflects the convergence of multiple interconnected stressors, including visceral adiposity, genetic susceptibility, and a broad range of environmental exposures such as excess fructose, adverse dietary fat composition, cholesterol overload, choline deficiency, SB, and psychosocial stress, each contributing, through distinct but complementary mechanisms, to the suppression of hepatic PPARα signaling. This shared molecular outcome impairs FAO, promotes lipid accumulation, and diminishes anti-inflammatory responses, collectively explaining the paradoxical severity and adverse long-term outcomes observed in lean individuals despite their favorable anthropometric profiles. The limitations of BMI-based risk stratification are therefore not merely practical but mechanistically grounded: body weight fails to capture the visceral, genetic, and nutritional determinants that converge on PPARα dysregulation in this population. Consequently, therapeutic strategies that restore PPARα activity, particularly those derived from herbal medicines, warrant focused investigation in this high-risk subgroup as a precision approach for this phenotypically distinct yet clinically underrecognized population.

PPARα in hepatic lipid homeostasis: Physiological roles and pathological dysregulation in lean MASLD

PPARα is a ligand-activated transcription factor belonging to the nuclear hormone receptor superfamily151. It is predominantly expressed in tissues with high fatty acid oxidative capacity, most notably the liver, but also skeletal muscle, heart, kidney, and brown adipose tissue152. Among the three PPAR isotypes (α, β/δ, and γ), PPARα serves as the master regulator of hepatic FAO153. Its transcriptional program coordinates fatty acid uptake, mitochondrial and peroxisomal oxidation, ketogenesis, lipoprotein metabolism, and anti-inflammatory signaling, collectively maintaining hepatic lipid homeostasis across a range of nutritional states. Understanding how PPARα normally governs hepatic lipid homeostasis and energy metabolism is prerequisite to characterizing its dysregulation in lean MASLD and identifying rational strategies for its restoration, which the following sections address in turn.

PPARα as a systemic metabolic regulator: Lipid catabolism, glucose homeostasis, and amino acid metabolism

PPARα adopts a canonical modular architecture comprising five functional domains: an N-terminal activation function-1 (AF-1) domain, a highly conserved DNA-binding domain (DBD) with two zinc fingers that recognize peroxisome proliferator response elements (PPREs), a flexible hinge region, a C-terminal ligand-binding domain (LBD), and an activation function-2 (AF-2) motif that mediates coactivator recruitment upon ligand engagement154,155. The extreme N-terminal A/B domain contains AF-1, mediating constitutive ligand-independent transcriptional activity. The DBD incorporates two conserved zinc finger motifs that recognize six-nucleotide PPREs in target gene promoters. The LBD governs ligand specificity, facilitates PPARα-retinoid X receptor (RXR) heterodimerization, and coordinates coactivator and corepressor binding, with its activation enabling PPRE engagement to upregulate target genes156, 157, 158, 159, 160, 161. The AF-2 interface stabilizes ligand entry into the LBD pocket, enabling coactivator recruitment156,158. Endogenous PPARα ligands include long-chain fatty acids, acyl-CoA thioesters, eicosanoids, endocannabinoids, oxidized phospholipids, and structurally analogous compounds such as phytanic acid, which bind the LBD with varying affinities and activate the transcriptional program in a ligand- and context-dependent manner. Clinically approved PPARα agonists such as fenofibrate, clofibrate, and gemfibrozil primarily interact with the LBD's central cavity and four arm regions, where coordination and carboxylic acid binding critically determine activity and selectivity159,160. Lipid metabolism. In the liver, PPARα functions as the principal transcriptional orchestrator of fatty acid catabolism. It directly regulates genes involved in fatty acid uptake (CD36, fatty acid transport protein [FATP]), intracellular activation via acyl-CoA synthetases, mitochondrial and peroxisomal β-oxidation, ketogenesis, and lipoprotein metabolism, many of which harbor PPREs in their promoters164, 165, 166. Fatty acids activate PPARα in part by inducing glycogen synthase kinase-3α, which phosphorylates PPARα at Ser280 in the LBD to selectively enhance transcription of fatty acid uptake and storage genes167. Upon activation, PPARα induces the expression of carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A) and CPT2, which mediate the rate-limiting translocation of long-chain acyl-CoA into mitochondria for β-oxidation. PPARα primarily orchestrates β-oxidation through mitochondrial and peroxisomal pathways differentiated by substrate specificity: mitochondria process short-to-long-chain fatty acids, while peroxisomes degrade long- and very-long-chain fatty acids via peroxisomal acyl-coenzyme A oxidase 1 (ACOX1) regulation155,164,168. β-Oxidation involves four enzymatic steps, with acyl-CoA dehydrogenase initiating the process and mitochondrial trifunctional protein completing subsequent reactions. Acetyl-CoA generated from FAO enters the Krebs cycle or is channeled toward ketone body synthesis for systemic energy supply. PPARα regulates medium-, long-, and very-long-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenases in hepatic β-oxidation, and orchestrates peroxisomal β-oxidation via transcriptional control of ACOX1, L-bifunctional protein, and thiolase.

During fasting, adipose tissue releases FFAs that are shuttled to the liver, where PPARα activation channels them toward β-oxidation and ketone body synthesis. PPARα interacts with energy sensors including adenosine 5′-monophosphate (AMP)-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α) to coordinate the metabolic shift toward ketosis, inducing fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) as a key hormonal mediator of this adaptive response. This adaptive response is critical for maintaining energy homeostasis; indeed, Pparα-null mice subjected to fasting develop profound hepatic steatosis, hypoketonemia, and ultimately fatal hypoglycemia due to failure to induce mitochondrial 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA synthase 2 (HMGCS2), the rate-limiting enzyme in ketogenesis169, 170, 171, 172.

Beyond their role as energy substrates, ketone bodies, particularly β-hydroxybutyrate (BHB), function as signaling molecules with broader metabolic relevance169,171. Early work established BHB as an endogenous inhibitor of class I and IIa histone deacetylases, inducing epigenetic changes that upregulate oxidative stress resistance genes and modulate inflammatory pathways independently of its energetic role173,174. More recent evidence has substantially expanded this framework: BHB has been shown to drive histone β-hydroxybutyrylation (Kbhb), a post-translational modification distinct from deacetylation175. Specifically, BHB elevates total protein Kbhb and histone H3K9bhb modifications in hepatocytes, and blockade of Kbhb, through inhibition of the acetyltransferase P300 or acyl-CoA synthetase 2, leads to significant downregulation of PPARα and its target gene expression, establishing a BHB-Kbhb-PPARα regulatory axis in which BHB not only responds to PPARα activation but reciprocally sustains it. Furthermore, parabacteroides distasonis has been shown to increase BHB production and drive BHB-mediated signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling, significantly upregulating PPARα expression and promoting hepatic regeneration, suggesting that gut microbiota-derived modulation of BHB may represent an additional layer of PPARα regulation with relevance to MASLD pathogenesis176.

A key downstream mediator linking hepatic PPARα activity to systemic energy regulation is FGF21, a hepatokine directly transcribed from a PPARα-responsive promoter. FGF21 is robustly induced during fasting and by fatty acid loading in a PPARα-dependent manner, and coordinates adaptive responses including enhanced FAO, suppression of growth hormone signaling, and promotion of ketogenesis177, 178, 179. The liver integrates lipid and glucose metabolism through PPARα-regulated FGF21, which acts as a key peptide hormone to coordinate inter-tissue fatty acid and glucose homeostasis and promote thermogenesis. PPARα in hepatocytes additionally supports carbohydrate-responsive element-binding protein (ChREBP)-mediated glucose-responsive induction of FGF21, improving glucose tolerance and expanding PPARα′s role beyond fasting regulation to physiological sugar adaptation180. However, the relationship between FGF21 and ketone bodies as metabolic biomarkers in MASLD requires nuanced interpretation. The PREVEND cohort study demonstrated that both FGF21 and ketone bodies independently associate with MASLD and mortality; however, after adjustment for potential confounders, only ketone bodies retained independent association, while the association of FGF21 became dependent on ketone body levels181. These findings position ketone bodies, rather than FGF21 alone, as the more robust independent marker of metabolic dysregulation in MASLD, and suggest that PPARα-driven ketogenesis, rather than FGF21 induction per se, may be the functionally dominant axis in disease pathogenesis. Beyond the liver, FGF21 acts on skeletal muscle and adipose tissue to enhance peripheral fatty acid utilization, establishing a liver-muscle-adipose axis through which hepatic PPARα activity influences whole-body energy partitioning182, 183, 184. In lean individuals, where skeletal muscle constitutes a proportionally greater metabolic mass than in obese counterparts, impairment of this hepatokine-mediated crosstalk may exert a disproportionate influence on systemic lipid homeostasis, rendering both FGF21 signal impairment and ketogenesis deficiency particularly complicates in lean MASLD185,186.

The clinical relevance of PPARα-driven ketogenesis in MASLD is further supported by preclinical and clinical intervention data. Adeno-associated-virus-mediated gene therapy targeting key ketogenic enzymes HMGCS2 and 3-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase 1 (BDH1) has successfully reduced steatosis, fibrosis, and inflammation in preclinical MASLD models, directly demonstrating that restoration of the ketogenic axis downstream of PPARα can modify disease course. Ketogenic dietary interventions, by inducing a fasting-like metabolic state, achieve greater reductions in intrahepatic TG content than conventional dietary approaches; however, this benefit is accompanied by elevation of hepatic mitochondrial redox state and suppression of Krebs cycle oxidation, highlighting the need for careful metabolic monitoring in clinical application187. These findings collectively underscore that PPARα-driven ketogenesis is not merely an energy-supply mechanism but a multifaceted regulatory axis governing epigenetic reprogramming, inter-organ signaling, and disease modification in lean MASLD.

The extrahepatic lipid-regulatory functions of PPARα further underscore its role as a systemic coordinator. In brown adipose tissue, PPARα induces thermogenic genes including Pgc-1α and Prdm16, while its activation in white adipose tissue promotes a brown adipocyte-like gene expression profile, suggesting a role in adipose browning188. Intestinal PPARα activation reduces cholesterol esterification, suppresses chylomicron secretion, and enhances HDL production189. In skeletal muscle, PPARα activation increases FAO and decreases TG accumulation190. Cardiac PPARα governs myocardial fatty acid utilization while reciprocally inhibiting glucose uptake pathways. Clinically, fibrate-activated PPARα enhances VLDL-TG lipolysis, reducing plasma TGs, while boosting apoA-I and apoA-II production to elevate HDL cholesterol191. This interconnected regulation across tissues underscores PPARα′s central role in maintaining systemic lipid homeostasis.

Glucose homeostasis. Beyond lipid metabolism, PPARα plays an integral role in hepatic glucose production and systemic glucose homeostasis. During fasting, PPARα governs glucose production through transcriptional regulation of gluconeogenic genes including Pck1, Pcx, Gpd1, and Ldha, as well as through fuel supply via FAO-derived energy and substrates, hepatic glycerol conversion to plasma glucose, and glycogen dynamics192. Impaired lactate-to-glucose recycling and reduced glycogen deposition are observed in PPARα deficiency, collectively impairing fasting glucose maintenance193. PPARα deficiency also modulates glucose partitioning by suppressing glucose-6-phosphatase hydrolase expression, diverting glucose-6-phosphate toward glycogen storage and exacerbating short-term fasting hypoglycemia194,195. Reduced hepatic lipid oxidation additionally lowers gluconeogenesis efficiency, exacerbating hypoglycemia through depleted ketogenic cofactors and hepatocyte dysfunction. Notably, systemic PPARα knockout mice exhibit more severe fasting hypoglycemia than hepatocyte-specific knockouts despite comparable FGF21 suppression, revealing that extrahepatic PPARα makes a critical contribution to glucose homeostasis independent of FGF2121. These observations are particularly relevant in lean MASLD, where fasting hypoglycemia and impaired gluconeogenic adaptation may contribute to the adverse disease trajectory observed independently of obesity.

Amino acid metabolism. PPARα additionally regulates key enzymes in amino acid metabolism, including those governing transamination, deamination, urea cycle function, α-keto acid oxidation, amino acid interconversion, and derivative synthesis 196,197. It broadly suppresses amino acid catabolism, evidenced by reduced plasma urea levels, while preserving nitrogen stores through hepatocyte nuclear factor 4 alpha (HNF4α)-mediated downregulation of catabolic genes198. This metabolic shift prioritizes fatty acid utilization over protein breakdown during periods of energetic stress. PPARα deficiency disrupts systemic nitric oxide (NO) synthesis via l-arginine pathway impairment, linking PPARα activity to vascular-metabolic homeostasis199, 200, 201. Carnitine depletion observed in PPARα-deficient models reduces mitochondrial acyl-CoA transport, a deficit partially reversible through carnitine supplementation202. Amino acid dysregulation in MASLD is further compounded by disease progression: in early MASLD, branched-chain amino acid concentrations are relatively elevated, but decline rapidly with the onset of cirrhosis as hepatocyte mass diminishes, reflecting a dynamic shift in amino acid homeostasis that tracks disease severity203, 204, 205. This trajectory suggests that PPARα-regulated amino acid metabolism is not static but evolves across the disease continuum, with early-stage dysregulation potentially serving as both a contributor to and a marker of progressive hepatic dysfunction. While the direct contribution of PPARα-regulated amino acid metabolism to lean MASLD pathogenesis remains to be fully characterized, the suppression of nitrogen homeostasis and NO synthesis under PPARα deficiency, together with the dynamic changes in branched-chain amino acid metabolism across disease stages, may collectively contribute to the vascular, inflammatory, and fibrotic phenotype observed in this population, and warrant targeted investigation in the lean MASLD context where metabolic reserve is inherently limited206.

Beyond its metabolic role, PPARα exerts potent anti-inflammatory effects in the liver. It suppresses NF-κB signaling by upregulating NF-κB inhibitor alpha (IκBα), thereby inhibiting transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and acute-phase proteins like C-reactive protein207, 208, 209. PPARα also downregulates IL-15 and IL-18 in kupffer cells (KCs) and hepatocytes and further modulates monocyte mobilization via the AMPK–PPARα axis during fasting210,211. Notably, liver-specific PPARα activation can attenuate MASH-related fibrosis through transrepression mechanisms independent of lipid metabolism212, underscoring its dual protective role in both steatosis and inflammation.

PPARα in hepatic inflammation, fibrosis, and cell-type-specific regulation

While Section 3.1 established PPARα′s canonical metabolic and anti-inflammatory functions primarily from a hepatocyte-centric perspective, the liver is a highly heterogeneous organ comprising multiple functionally distinct cell types. Advances in single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics have fundamentally reshaped our understanding of PPARα expression across the hepatic cellular landscape, revealing that its regulatory influence extends well beyond parenchymal cells, albeit with markedly different expression levels and functional implications across cell types.

Historically, research on PPARα as a transcription factor focused almost exclusively on hepatocytes. This emphasis was justified: PPARA RNA expression is significantly higher in hepatocytes than in non-parenchymal cells, and the canonical functions of lipid catabolism and gluconeogenesis described in Section 3.1 are performed predominantly by hepatocytes. Current protein-level data from The Human Protein Atlas confirm that detectable PPARα protein expression in the liver is concentrated in hepatocytes and bile duct epithelial cells. This hepatocyte-centric perspective shaped the early development of PPARα agonists, with research almost entirely focused on parenchymal cells. Nevertheless, PPARα is also expressed in bile duct cells and endothelial cells, with weak expression in KCs, hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), and some natural killer (NK) cells, and extremely low expression in T cells, B cells, and monocytes.

The emergence of single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics has allowed deeper characterization of the hepatic cell atlas and has strengthened our understanding of PPARα expression heterogeneity213. PPARα expression and function exhibit spatial heterogeneity across hepatocyte zones within the liver lobule: while fatty acid synthesis is primarily concentrated in the periportal zone, fatty acid catabolism, especially peroxisome-associated β-oxidation, predominantly occurs in the pericentral zone, where PPARα function and activity are correspondingly enriched214 (Fig. 4). Whether this zonated activity reflects metabolic substrate gradients, ligand concentration gradients, or zone-specific post-transcriptional modifications remains to be fully characterized. In the context of MASLD, the hepatic macrophage landscape is highly heterogeneous, composed of distinct resident and recruited macrophage subsets that collectively shape disease progression. PPARα is expressed in resident hepatic macrophages, where it acts as a metabolic-immune switch inhibiting pro-inflammatory signals including NF-κB. Upregulation of PPARα in angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE)-highly expressing macrophages has been shown to be essential for ACE-dependent macrophage functional enhancement, including antitumor and bactericidal activity, underscoring that PPARα′s immunomodulatory role in macrophages extends beyond liver disease216. Hepatic macrophage activation is considered a key driving factor in the progression of MASLD to MASH, promoting HSCs activation and accelerating fibrosis. Among macrophage subsets, lipid-associated macrophages (LAMs) represent a particularly relevant population: in steatohepatitis, LAMs accumulate around lipid-laden hepatocytes, forming hepatic crown-like structures that initially contain locally generated lipotoxic signals and limit inflammation217,218. However, following sustained injury, LAMs acquire a markedly pro-inflammatory phenotype through exposure to toxic lipid signals mediated in part by macrophage scavenger receptor 1 (MSR1), lose their containment function, and ultimately contribute to the fibrotic process219. The relationship between hepatocellular steatosis and macrophage activation is bidirectional: inflammatory macrophages can perpetuate steatotoxic liver injury by suppressing β-oxidation in fatty hepatocytes through secretion of TNF-α and IL-1β, creating a feedforward cycle of lipid accumulation and inflammation220. In KCs and infiltrating macrophages, PPARα activation inhibits M1 polarization and reduces secretion of TNF-α and IL-1β, promoting a shift toward an anti-inflammatory M2-like phenotype. Specifically, PPARα agonists increase the proportion of anti-inflammatory F4/80hi CD44+ MHCII− M2-like macrophages while decreasing the proportion of pro-inflammatory CD64+ CX3CR1+ CCR2hi VISTAhi CD172a+ CD44hi M1-like monocyte-derived macrophages, regulating the developmental trajectory and homeostasis of hepatic macrophages221. This macrophage-targeting PPARα function is particularly relevant in lean MASLD, where, in the absence of systemic obesity, VAT-derived FFAs activate hepatic macrophages via TLR4 signaling, as discussed in Section 2.2. Beyond the liver, ligand-activated hepatocyte-specific transrepressive PPARα activity reduces the pro-inflammatory phenotype of hepatocytes, decreases leukocyte recruitment, lowers plasma IL-1β concentrations, and reduces macrophage content in atherosclerotic plaques, highlighting that PPARα′s anti-inflammatory influence extends to cardiovascular disease, a comorbidity of particular relevance in lean MASLD given its disproportionate cardiovascular mortality risk222.

PPARα is expressed at low levels in HSCs. In the MASH mouse model, intracellular PPARα expression in HSCs is significantly reduced, and this reduction is associated with HSC activation, suggesting that PPARα loss in HSCs may contribute to the fibrogenic process independently of its hepatocyte-mediated effects on lipid metabolism223. PPARα regulation has also been implicated in autophagy and lipid droplet loss during HSC activation224. It is worth noting that the majority of HSC-related nuclear receptor research has focused on PPARγ, a marker of the quiescent HSC phenotype whose expression is attenuated upon transdifferentiation into myofibroblasts; PPARγ activation reduces HSC activation and fibrosis. The relative paucity of PPARα-specific data in HSCs, compared to PPARγ, represents a significant gap in the field, particularly given that PPARα and PPARγ may exert complementary antifibrotic functions through distinct mechanisms.

Beyond macrophages and stellate cells, PPARα expression has been detected in liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) and bile duct epithelial cells, though functional characterization in these populations remains limited. In LSECs, PPARα has been implicated in the regulation of lipid uptake and suppression of inflammatory adhesion molecule expression, potentially contributing to the maintenance of hepatic vascular homeostasis and limiting leukocyte recruitment225. In cholangiocytes, PPARα expression may intersect with bile acid (BA) metabolism through its interaction with FXR signaling, though direct evidence for a functional PPARα role in cholangiocyte biology in the context of MASLD remains to be established226.

Collectively, the cell-type-specific distribution and function of PPARα in the liver reveal a regulatory landscape considerably more complex than the hepatocyte-centric model that dominated early research. Single-cell and spatial transcriptomic data confirm that PPARα activity is zonated within the hepatic lobule, differentially expressed across parenchymal and non-parenchymal cells, and dynamically altered during MASLD progression. In lean MASLD specifically, where disease severity is disproportionate to metabolic burden, the non-hepatocyte contributions of PPARα, particularly its macrophage-mediated anti-inflammatory functions and its nascent antifibrotic role in HSCs, may be especially consequential. However, several critical gaps remain: the functional significance of PPARα in LSECs and cholangiocytes is largely uncharacterized; cell-type-specific PPARα knockout models for HSCs and LSECs in the context of MASLD are lacking; and the dynamic interplay between PPARα activity across cell types during disease progression has not been systematically mapped. Addressing these gaps will be essential for developing cell-type-selective PPARα-targeting strategies that can safely and effectively modify disease course in this high-risk population.

Mechanisms of PPARα suppression in lean MASLD: From molecular triggers to phenotypic consequences

The causal link between PPARα deficiency and MASLD was first established in 1999, when PPARα-null mice developed severe hepatic lipid accumulation after 24 h fasting, accompanied by hypoketonemia, elevated plasma FFAs, and hypothermia172. Subsequent studies confirmed that hepatic PPARα dysfunction is sufficient to drive steatosis even in the absence of obesity or insulin resistance, a defining feature of lean MASLD21, 22, 23. Notably, systemic PPARα knockout results in more severe steatosis and higher plasma FFAs than hepatocyte-specific deletion, likely because extrahepatic tissues such as skeletal muscle and heart can partially compensate via FAO in the latter model227. However, lean individuals often lack this metabolic flexibility, rendering them particularly vulnerable to even modest reductions in hepatic PPARα activity.

In human MASLD, hepatic PPARα mRNA levels remain relatively stable during simple steatosis but decline with progression to MASH and fibrosis. Critically, PPARα protein expression is already reduced at the steatosis stage, suggesting post-transcriptional or post-translational suppression mechanisms228. This dissociation between mRNA and protein provides an important mechanistic clue: the initial suppression of PPARα in lean MASLD is unlikely to reflect simple transcriptional downregulation alone, but rather a convergence of multiple molecular triggers acting at distinct regulatory levels.

Lean-specific molecular triggers of PPARα suppression. Several lean-specific drivers contribute to this early impairment. Aging represents a main factor, as lean MASLD patients are often older than their non-lean counterparts229. Age-related upregulation of the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) in hepatocytes suppresses PPARα expression and β-oxidation, leading to TG accumulation even in individuals with a BMI of 19–23 kg/m2 230. In parallel, reduced glucocorticoid receptor activity and heightened NF-κB signaling in aging further diminish PPARα transcription231. These age-related mechanisms are of particular relevance in lean MASLD, where the absence of obesity-related metabolic amplifiers means that aging-driven PPARα suppression may be the primary initiating event.

Epigenetic silencing serves as a critical and increasingly recognized mechanism. As described in Section 2.4, dietary fructose induces hypermethylation of the PPARα promoter region, constituting a direct epigenetic mechanism for transcriptional silencing. Fructose also downregulates the expression of SIRT1, which is a positive regulator that enhances PPARα transcriptional activity through direct deacetylation232,233. Although livers from MASLD patients exhibit lower global methylation that further declines during fibrosis progression234, the PPARA gene specifically displays heavy methylation at multiple CpG sites in fatty liver models, thereby hindering mRNA and protein expression. This effect can be reversed by the DNA methylation inhibitor 5-Aza-CdR or curcumin235. Furthermore, ten-eleven translocation 1 (TET1) binds directly to the PPARα promoter to increase hydroxymethylation levels and inhibit MASLD progression236. Regarding histone modifications, the demethylase Jhdm2a binds to the PPRE of Ppara, and its deficiency leads to elevated H3K9me2 levels and reduced PPARα expression237. In terms of acetylation, sodium butyrate acts as a histone deacetylase inhibitor to upregulate PPARα by increasing H3K9Ac modification at its promoter238. Additionally, the BHB-Kbhb-PPARα axis established in Section 3.1 creates a reciprocal vulnerability. Because PPARα inhibition reduces ketogenesis and BHB production, the resulting decline in histone β-hydroxybutyrylation further impairs target gene expression to form a self-reinforcing epigenetic loop. Finally, specific miRNAs including miR-200, miR-20b, miR-181a, miR-30a-3p, miR-519d, miR-21, and miR-22 directly target PPARα mRNA and are elevated in MASLD239, 240, 241.

Circadian rhythm disruption is a frequently overlooked trigger. PPARα is a circadian-sensitive transcription factor that interacts with core clock components including brain and muscle ARNT-Like 1 (BMAL1) and REV-ERBα242,243. The expression of Ppara is directly activated when the BMAL1-circadian locomotor output cycles kaput (CLOCK) heterodimer binds to E-box elements in its promoter, facilitating the upregulation of target genes that regulate lipid metabolism. Circadian disturbances, such as shift work, irregular sleep-wake patterns, or social jetlag, impair the diurnal activation of PPARα-driven FAO. This creates a state of functional insufficiency even when absolute PPARα expression levels are not reduced. Evidence suggests that nocturnal metabolic dysfunction is a hallmark of MASLD and a primary driver of hepatic steatosis244. This mechanism may be especially significant in lean MASLD populations, where circadian misalignment correlates with visceral fat accumulation and metabolic dysfunction. Numerous studies have established associations between shift-work-induced circadian disruption, the development of MASLD or MASH, and the risk of liver cancer245.

Disruption of the gut–liver axis also plays a role. While intestinal PPARα deletion can reduce enterocyte lipid absorption by downregulating fatty acid transporters and perilipin 2 (PLIN2), thereby alleviating steatosis246,247. Yet intestinal PPARα deficiency under high-fructose or high-fat conditions compromises epithelial barrier integrity. This promotes lipopolysaccharides (LPS) translocation to the liver and triggers TLR4-mediated inflammatory signaling, further suppresses hepatic PPARα248. Recent evidence reveals that hepatic LPS accumulation inhibits circulating pigment epithelium-derived factor (PEDF) via PPARα pathway, impairing intestinal repair249, which highlights bidirectional crosstalk that may be especially relevant in lean individuals with subtle metabolic reserve. Gut microbiota dysregulation additionally alters secondary BA composition, which modulates FXR signaling in the liver; given that FXR and PPARα interact reciprocally to regulate hepatic lipid metabolism, microbiota-driven BA dysregulation may represent an additional upstream suppressor of PPARα activity in lean MASLD, particularly relevant given the gut–liver axis abnormalities documented in this population250.

Finally, the genetic background modulates the severity of PPARα suppression across all the above triggers. As discussed in Section 2.3, core MASLD loci including PNPLA3 I148M and TM6SF2 E167K create a hepatic lipid-trapping environment that may reduce the availability of endogenous PPARα ligands and amplify lipotoxic suppression. Concurrently, PPARA polymorphisms such as L162V modulate baseline PPARα transcriptional capacity, determining the threshold at which environmental triggers produce clinically significant PPARα insufficiency. This gene-environment interaction implies that the same dietary or lifestyle exposure may produce markedly different degrees of PPARα suppression depending on individual genetic background, contributing to the phenotypic heterogeneity of lean MASLD across populations.

Phenotypic consequences of PPARα suppression in lean MASLD. The convergence of these molecular triggers produces a characteristic pattern of phenotypic consequences that collectively define the lean MASLD disease state. At the hepatic level, impaired PPARα-driven FAO shifts the metabolic balance toward lipid accumulation, driving steatosis in the absence of obesity. As PPARα suppression deepens with disease progression, the loss of its anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic functions, across hepatocytes, KCs, and HSCs as detailed in Section 3.2, promotes the transition from simple steatosis to MASH and fibrosis, explaining the histological severity disproportionate to metabolic burden documented in Section 2.1.

At the systemic level, PPARα suppression impairs the fasting adaptive response by reducing ketogenesis and FGF21 induction251. As established in Section 3.1, the PREVEND cohort data position ketone bodies as a more robust independent marker of MASLD severity than FGF21, suggesting that the ketogenic axis is a functionally dominant mediator of PPARα deficiency consequences. In lean individuals, impaired fasting adaptation manifests as reduced ketone body availability during caloric restriction, blunted FGF21-mediated peripheral fatty acid utilization, and diminished BHB-driven epigenetic maintenance of PPARα target gene expression, collectively creating a state of metabolic inflexibility that is disproportionate to the degree of hepatic fat accumulation and that may explain the adverse disease trajectory observed in lean MASLD independently of obesity progression.

The loss of metabolic flexibility in lean MASLD is further compounded by the absence of the compensatory mechanisms available to obese individuals. In obesity, subcutaneous adipose tissue serves as a metabolic buffer that can sequester excess lipids and modulate FFA delivery to the liver; in lean individuals, this buffer is absent, meaning that even modest PPARα suppression produces a disproportionate hepatic lipotoxic burden. Furthermore, while extrahepatic PPARα in skeletal muscle and heart can partially compensate for hepatocyte-specific PPARα deficiency in experimental models21,227, this compensation is contingent on intact extrahepatic PPARα function; aging, SB, and circadian misalignment, all disproportionately relevant to lean MASLD, may impair extrahepatic compensation, further concentrating the metabolic consequence of PPARα insufficiency within the liver.

Collectively, these findings establish that PPARα suppression in lean MASLD is not a single-mechanism event but the product of multiple converging molecular triggers, including aging, epigenetic silencing, gut–liver axis disruption, circadian misalignment, and genetic predisposition, acting across transcriptional, post-transcriptional, and post-translational regulatory levels. The phenotypic consequences extend from hepatic steatosis and MASH progression to systemic metabolic inflexibility and impaired inter-organ energy coordination, collectively explaining the paradoxical severity of lean MASLD (Fig. 5). Therapeutic targeting of PPARα in lean MASLD: Toward precision and metabolic compatibility

Evidence suggests that the suppression of PPARα activity serves as a convergent pathogenic node in lean MASLD, driven by a complex interplay of visceral adiposity, dietary stress, epigenetic silencing, gut‒liver axis dysregulation, and genetic predisposition. This convergence not only defines PPARα restoration as a primary target for precision therapy but also dictates specific requirements for the design of PPARα-targeted strategies in this population. Unlike obese MASLD, the lean phenotype is characterized by limited subcutaneous adipose buffering capacity and a disproportionately high burden on hepatic FAO. When combined with increased mitochondrial vulnerability associated with aging and genetic factors, therapeutic PPARα activation in lean individuals requires meticulous calibration to restore oxidative capacity without overwhelming a metabolically constrained system.

Classical non-selective PPARα agonists, such as fibrates, tend to enhance fatty acid uptake alongside oxidation, which can perturb cardiac and renal PPARα signaling and lead to poorly tolerated systemic side effects. In this chapter, we evaluate current PPARα-targeted therapeutic modalities, spanning synthetic agonists, dietary and natural product-derived compounds, traditional herbal formulations, and emerging precision delivery systems. As our understanding of chemical structures and mechanistic complexity deepens, the therapeutic paradigm is evolving. The field is currently transitioning from single-target receptor activation to multi-target network regulation, from systemic exposure to tissue-specific action, and from small-molecule compounds to targeted biologics. Within this framework, we discuss the recent progress and inherent limitations of each therapeutic category.

Synthetic PPARα agonists: Application prospects and limitations in lean MASLD

A diverse array of synthetic PPARα agonists has been developed and categorized into three classes: conventional fibrates, novel SPPARMs, and dual/pan PPARs agonists (Table 1). While these compounds demonstrate lipid-modifying effects, their efficacy in MASLD, particularly in lean individuals, remains limited by off-target actions, safety concerns, and insufficient tissue selectivity. Current first-line pharmacotherapies for MASLD, such as pioglitazone (a PPARγ agonist), glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs), or high-dose statins, are primarily validated in obese or diabetic cohorts. In lean individuals, pioglitazone may induce undesirable weight gain and fluid retention252; GLP-1 RAs can exacerbate pre-existing low adiposity and muscle loss253; and statins, while generally safe, carry heightened risk of myopathy in those with limited metabolic reserve254. Synthetic PPARα agonists were primarily developed to treat dyslipidemia in the context of obesity and insulin resistance. However, their mechanisms, centered on systemic lipid lowering and insulin sensitization, are poorly matched to the pathophysiology of lean MASLD, which features low metabolic reserve, normal or mild hypertriglyceridemia, and heightened susceptibility to drug-induced toxicity. Consequently, these agents often lack efficacy or pose unacceptable safety risks in lean individuals. The following subsections critically evaluate three classes of synthetic agonists, conventional fibrates, SPPARMs, and dual/pan PPAR agonists, in the context of lean-specific therapeutic needs.

Conventional PPARα agonists

Fibrates, the first-generation PPARα agonists, have been used clinically since the 1960s to treat hypertriglyceridemia255. Gemfibrozil and fenofibrate are currently in use, whereas clofibrate was discontinued due to safety issues256, 257, 258, 259. Fibrates lower plasma TGs and increase HDL-cholesterol (HDL-C) primarily by upregulating lipoprotein lipase (LPL) and suppressing apolipoprotein C-III (ApoC-III)260, 261, 262, 263, 264, thereby enhancing the clearance of TG-rich lipoproteins.

In MASLD, fibrates are recommended only for severe hypertriglyceridemia254. Fenofibrate shows modest benefits in reducing hepatic steatosis and inflammation through antioxidant and lipophagy-inducing effects265, 266, 267, but clinical evidence remains inconsistent. Gemfibrozil and clofibrate lack robust efficacy in MASH268,269. Despite early small-scale trials suggesting moderate reductions in imaging-based steatosis, subsequent large-scale randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses confirm that fenofibrate is largely neutral regarding hepatic fat content and fails to improve histological endpoints such as inflammation, ballooning, or fibrosis270. Furthermore, its clinical utility in long-term management is constrained by potential hepatotoxicity, myopathy, gallstones, and complex drug interactions, particularly with statins254,271. For lean MASLD individuals, who often present with normal or only mildly elevated TGs, this lipid-centric mechanism provides limited therapeutic rationale. The lack of significant impact on WC or underlying metabolic fragility further suggests that fenofibrate is not the preferred option for this specific phenotype.

Novel selective PPARα agonists

To overcome the weak potency and poor selectivity of fibrates, next-generation SPPARMs were developed. Preclinical tool compounds like GW7647 have demonstrated proof-of-concept for potent PPARα-mediated lipid clearance, but lack drug-like properties for clinical translation272, 273, 274.

LY518674 is a highly selective PPARα agonist developed earlier that belongs to the phenoxyisobutyrate class, similar to pemafibrate. However, its clinical development was terminated because, despite lowering TGs and increasing HDL-C, it failed to demonstrate incremental LDL-cholesterol (LDL-C) reduction when used in combination with atorvastatin and raised significant safety concerns275. Similar to fenofibrate, LY518674 significantly elevated plasma homocysteine levels. Furthermore, patients receiving LY518674 exhibited dose-dependent increases in serum creatinine. Subsequent efforts led to the development of novel agonists represented by DY121, which were designed through scaffold modifications of LY518674, though these candidates currently remain in the prodrug development stage276. The failure of LY518674 provides a profound lesson for the pharmaceutical industry: in the context of PPARα activation, greater potency does not necessarily translate into superior therapeutic outcomes.

Pemafibrate, approved in Japan for hyperlipidemia, represents a paradigm shift in PPARα targeting. Its Y-shaped structure induces a unique conformational change in the PPARα ligand-binding domain, promoting selective recruitment of coactivators like PGC-1α while avoiding cofactors linked to adverse effects277,278. This “gene-selective” modulation enhances beneficial metabolic pathways (e.g., FAO, ketogenesis) without activating pro-fibrotic or inflammatory genes279. In MASLD patients, pemafibrate significantly reduced liver fat content and stiffness280, 281, 282, with an improved safety profile, no hepatotoxicity, renal injury, or myopathy283. In a six-month retrospective study, pemafibrate demonstrated superior therapeutic efficacy in the lean MASLD phenotype, yielding significantly more pronounced reductions in hepatic inflammation and fibrosis compared to the obese group284.

Dual and pan PPARs agonists

PPARβ/δ and PPARγ are isoforms within the same family as PPARα. PPARβ/δ is widely expressed across various tissues and plays a pivotal role in regulating hepatic carbohydrate and lipid metabolism, which contributes to improved glucose homeostasis and insulin sensitivity. However, the development of selective agonists targeting PPARβ/δ has faced significant setbacks. Early candidate GW501516 was terminated during clinical development due to its potential carcinogenicity285. Similarly, clinical trials for Seladelpar (MBX-8025) were suspended in November 2019 following reports of abnormal histological findings, specifically interface hepatitis286. PPARγ is highly expressed in white adipose tissue and serves as a master regulator of adipocyte differentiation and lipid storage, influencing hepatic ectopic lipid deposition, insulin sensitivity, and anti-inflammatory pathways. Thiazolidinediones represent the prototypical class of PPARγ agonists and are recognized as potent insulin sensitizers, yet their use is constrained by side effects such as weight gain and cardiovascular risks. While pioglitazone has demonstrated superior therapeutic outcomes in MASH patients without diabetes, concerns persist regarding its association with weight gain and peripheral edema287,288. Consequently, research has shifted toward the development of dual and pan-agonists in an effort to enhance clinical efficacy while mitigating the limitations associated with single-subtype activation.

Dual PPARα/γ agonists (glitazars) were initially developed for diabetic dyslipidemia but largely abandoned due to urothelial and cardiovascular toxicity289, 290, 291. Saroglitazar, a newer agent, appears safer and has shown promise in reducing liver fat, ALT, and insulin resistance in MASLD/MASH patients292,293. In a randomized controlled trial, Saroglitazar demonstrated the capacity to reduce hepatic lipid content across various doses, with the high-dose group (4 mg per day) also showing histological improvements in fibrosis294. Despite its approval in India for the management of MASH, clinical research specifically targeting the lean MASLD phenotype remains absent.

Elafibranor, a PPARα/δ dual agonist, demonstrated anti-steatotic and anti-inflammatory effects in early-phase MASH trials295, 296, 297. However, a Phase 3 trial (NCT02704403) failed to meet its primary endpoint of MASH resolution without fibrosis worsening, halting its development for this indication.

Lanifibranor, a pan-PPAR agonist, activates all three isoforms to exert synergistic metabolic, anti-inflammatory, and antifibrotic effects. It significantly improved histological features of MASH in Phase 2 trials298,299, and is currently undergoing Phase 3 evaluation. However, the occurrence of weight gain as an adverse event warrants careful consideration.

Bezafibrate, another pan-agonist, also reduced steatosis and improved insulin sensitivity in preclinical models300,301, though human data in lean MASLD are lacking. In patients with coronary artery disease (CAD) and MASLD, pemafibrate demonstrated superior ALT reduction compared to bezafibrate despite both agents providing comparable cardiovascular and insulin-sensitizing benefits. These results suggest that the enhanced selectivity of pemafibrate offers a distinct therapeutic advantage over non-selective pan-PPAR activation in specifically attenuating hepatic inflammation and injury302.

In summary, the clinical utility of classical fibrates in lean MASLD is constrained by established safety concerns, including elevations in serum creatinine and homocysteine, as well as an increased risk of myopathy when co-administered with statins. These limitations render traditional fibrates suboptimal for long-term MASLD management. In contrast, the selective modulator pemafibrate minimizes off-target effects and has demonstrated potential superiority in the lean phenotype, though further clinical validation is required. Broadly, while PPARα-targeted therapies offer a promising mechanistic rationale, current trials have yielded inconsistent histological outcomes, shifting the research focus toward combination therapies and selective modulation strategies. Crucially, despite evidence suggesting that lean individuals may exhibit diminished responses to insulin-sensitizing therapies due to lower adipose mass and altered adipokine profiles, these agents have not yet been rigorously evaluated in dedicated lean MASLD cohorts. This significant research gap underscores the need for therapeutic strategies tailored to the unique pathophysiological constraints of the lean population.

Natural product-derived PPARα modulators: From dietary compounds to herbal monomers and formulations

The limitations of synthetic PPARα agonists have prompted investigation of alternative approaches capable of achieving metabolically compatible PPARα restoration without their associated adverse effects. Natural product-derived compounds represent a logical first candidate class, encompassing dietary lipid components and plant-derived phytochemicals including flavonoids, polyphenols, and terpenoids. These compounds engage PPARα through partial or selective activation rather than maximal agonism, offering structural diversity, generally favorable safety profiles, and frequent multi-target activity that more closely approximates physiological receptor activation. Beyond direct ligand binding, many natural compounds modulate PPARα indirectly through upstream nodes including AMPK and SIRT1, mechanisms of particular relevance in lean MASLD, where PPARα suppression operates across transcriptional, post-transcriptional, and post-translational levels simultaneously. While endogenous fatty acids also bind PPARα, their therapeutic utility is constrained by weak activation potency and concentration-dependent lipotoxicity104,334. The following section reviews natural compounds with known capacity to activate PPARα expression or activity. It must be noted that the majority of evidence derives from general NAFLD or obesity models rather than lean MASLD-specific investigations, and these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating with respect to this distinct population, pending lean-specific validation.

Omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids: Direct PPARα ligands with clinical evidence

n-3 PUFAs, notably eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), are among the most extensively characterized endogenous-class PPARα ligands, potently enhance mitochondrial and peroxisomal β-oxidation, suppress hepatic VLDL secretion, and inhibit NF-κB-driven inflammation335,336. Clinical trials consistently demonstrate reductions in liver fat, ALT, and systemic inflammatory markers in MASLD patients337, 338, 339, 340. Crucially, n-3 PUFAs exert these benefits without promoting weight loss or impairing glucose homeostasis, making them especially suitable for lean MASLD patients with isolated hypertriglyceridemia or early steatosis with otherwise preserved metabolic function. As established in Section 2.3, the V227A polymorphism at the PPARA locus modulates the lipid response to dietary PUFA intake, suggesting that the magnitude of n-3 PUFA-mediated PPARα activation may be genotype-dependent and that lean MASLD patients carrying specific PPARA variants may derive differential therapeutic benefit from omega-3 supplementation, a hypothesis that warrants prospective evaluation in genotype-stratified clinical studies.

Several compounds have been confirmed to bind PPARα via luciferase reporter assays, including linalool, β-caryophyllene, phytol, geranylgeraniol, oleanolic acid, bixin, panduratin A, resveratrol, hispidulin, genistein, biochanin A, mangiferin, isohumulone, rosmarinic acid, osthole, gallotannins, picrasidine C, berberine, etc. In contrast, a subset of phytochemicals listed in Table 2—including methyl dehydroabietate (mDA), stevioside, fucoxanthin, sargaquinoic acid, sargahydroquinoic acid, icariin, epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), nobiletin, tectoridin, curcumin-3,4-dichloro phenyl pyrazole (CDPP), verbascoside, umbelliferone, sesamin, sesamol, and oxymatrine—have no validated evidence from multiple direct binding assays to support direct PPARα interaction, suggesting their effects may be indirect or mediated through upstream regulators. Likewise, bilobetin does not directly bind PPARα but modulates its activity through alternative pathways. This mechanistic distinction does not diminish their therapeutic relevance but does require that claims of PPARα engagement be appropriately qualified.

From a structural and pharmacological perspective, natural PPARα modulators can be broadly categorized into several representative classes with distinct functional characteristics. Alkaloids, exemplified by berberine, enhance insulin sensitivity and lipid oxidation without promoting catabolism, a critical feature in lean individuals with limited metabolic reserve. Berberine is widely used in Asia for the management of obesity, diabetes, and inflammation, with extensive clinical evidence supporting its safety and multi-target mechanisms, including synergistic activation of the AMPK–PPARα axis341,342. Diterpenes, such as dehydroabietic acid and pseudolaric acid B, activate PPARα, often in conjunction with partial PPARγ modulation, thereby enhancing lipid clearance while avoiding strong adipogenic effects. Flavonoids including hispidulin directly activate PPARα, contributing to improvements in dyslipidemia in preclinical models. Carotenoids such as fucoxanthin enhance lipid metabolism, likely through indirect upregulation of PPARα signaling. Coumarins, represented by auraptene, function as dual PPARα/γ agonists that fine-tune the expression of lipid-handling genes. Finally, triterpenoids such as oleanolic acid stimulate PPARα activity across multiple cell types, including keratinocytes and hepatocytes, which may underlie their broad hepatoprotective profile. Oleanolic acid has been marketed in China for decades as an over-the-counter hepatoprotective agent and has demonstrated efficacy in metabolic and immune-related disorders343. Resveratrol supplementation has been shown to reduce body weight, BMI, and WC in obese individuals after 3 months of intervention and has shown promise in cardiovascular disease344,345. In the context of lean MASLD, these effects may translate into improvements in mitochondrial function and anti-inflammatory capacity that are independent of adiposity loss.

Importantly, many of these natural products concurrently modulate the gut microbiota, strengthen intestinal barrier integrity, and attenuate endotoxemia, key pathophysiological features frequently observed in lean MASLD248,249. Their low cytotoxicity, oral bioavailability, and compatibility with long-term use position them as foundational components of a precision nutrition strategy tailored to metabolically unhealthy lean individuals. While a comprehensive catalog of PPARα-targeting natural products was provided in a 2017 review346, recent discoveries continue to expand this pharmacopeia, emphasizing the need for lean-specific clinical validation.

Herbal monomers with mechanistic validation in MASLD models

While phytochemical compounds reviewed in Section 4.2.2 encompass broadly characterized natural product classes, a growing number of structurally defined herbal monomers have been evaluated for PPARα engagement in MASLD-specific experimental models, providing a more disease-contextualized evidence base.

mDA, a derivative of dehydroabietic acid (DAA), functions as a PPARα/γ agonist and effectively mitigates insulin resistance, hepatic steatosis, and inflammation in preclinical models. Stevioside, acting as a pan-PPAR agonist, confers antidiabetic, cardiovascular, and hepatoprotective benefits, properties that may be particularly valuable in lean individuals with early metabolic dysregulation despite preserved BMI. Celastrol has been shown to attenuate carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)-induced acute liver injury, characterized predominantly by periportal parenchymal necrosis, via hepatic PPARα activation, highlighting its cytoprotective potential in oxidative stress-driven liver damage, a feature often amplified in lean MASLD. Kaempferol exerts anti-hyperlipidemic effects by reducing TG levels in both HepG2 cells and the livers of high-fat diet (HFD)-fed ApoE−/− mice, likely through PPARα-mediated upregulation of FAO-related genes. CDPP protects Syrian golden hamsters from HFD-induced dyslipidemia via activation of the PPARα–LXRα–ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1) pathway, thereby promoting reverse cholesterol transport, a mechanism that may be beneficial for lean MASLD patients with atherogenic dyslipidemia despite normal body weight.

Beyond these well-characterized compounds, additional monomers demonstrate robust PPARα-dependent efficacy in MASLD models. Dendrobine (DNE), isolated from Dendrobium officinale, alleviates MASLD by improving hepatic lipid metabolism and reducing lipoatrophy through PPARα-mediated gene regulation, while also exerting lipid-lowering, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and glucose-regulating effects408. The triterpenoid-rich Alisma orientale extract (AE) ameliorates MASH by reducing lipid accumulation and oxidative stress, as evidenced by upregulation of the PGC-1α/PPARα axis in murine models409. Extracts of Poria cocos modulate hepatic cholesterol homeostasis through activation of PPARα signaling, leading to marked improvements in hyperlipidemia and hepatic steatosis410. Total flavonoid C-glycosides derived from trichosanthes vulgaris mitigate MASLD both in vitro and in vivo by modulating PPARα and its downstream targets while concurrently suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines411. Gentian picroside (GPS), the principal secoiridoid glycoside in gentian, counteracts MASH progression through dual mechanisms: inhibition of hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α) via restoration of the l-serine/glycine metabolic axis, and activation of PPARα mediated by elevated endogenous EPA/DHA levels, collectively exerting anti-steatotic and anti-inflammatory effects412. Salidroside exhibits both preventive and therapeutic efficacy against MCD diet-induced MASH by modulating hepatic autophagy/apoptosis balance, restoring immune homeostasis, and suppressing inflammation through PPAR pathway activation413. Finally, scoparone (Scop), derived from Artemisia capillaris thunb., has been confirmed to directly bind and activate human PPARα through molecular docking, luciferase reporter assays, and in vivo functional studies, resulting in improved lipid metabolism and reduced inflammation414. In addition, clinopodium gracile extract (CGE) has emerged as a promising agent for MASLD treatment by upregulating PPARα to mitigate lipid accumulation and mitochondrial oxidative stress, with in silico analyses confirming strong binding affinity between its bioactive constituents and the PPARα ligand-binding domain415.

Across these compounds, a consistent mechanistic pattern emerges: PPARα activation is frequently accompanied by concurrent modulation of oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, and gut–liver axis integrity, precisely the multi-dimensional pathogenic landscape that characterizes lean MASLD. However, it must be emphasized that the evidence for all compounds reviewed in this section derives from general NAFLD or obese MASLD experimental models, and direct lean MASLD-specific validation is lacking for each. The mechanistic alignment between these compounds’ pharmacological profiles and the pathogenic drivers of lean MASLD provides a strong rationale for lean-specific investigation, but does not substitute for it.

Traditional Chinese medicine formulations: Systems-level PPARα modulation

While individual herbal monomers engage PPARα through defined molecular interactions, traditional Chinese medicine formulations operate through a fundamentally different pharmacological paradigm: multi-component mixtures whose constituent bioactive compounds act synergistically across complementary pathogenic pathways, achieving network-level metabolic recalibration that single compounds cannot replicate. Emerging evidence underscores the therapeutic potential of multi-component traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) formulations that converge on PPARα as a central regulatory node. Gegen Qilian Decoction has been shown to alleviate MASH by upregulating PPARα, thereby enhancing FAO, reducing oxidative stress, and inhibiting TLR4/NF-κB-mediated inflammation416. Qige Decoction activates the SIRT6–PPARα axis to promote FAO, illustrating a synergistic mode of metabolic regulation that integrates epigenetic control with transcriptional activation417. Da-Chai-Hu-Tang (DCHT) contains multiple bioactive constituents that bind PPARα with high-affinity, as validated by dual-luciferase assays, and suppresses intrahepatic cholestasis and inflammation via PPARα-dependent inhibition of the c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK)/IL-6/NF-κB/STAT3 signaling cascade418. In addition, Lanzhang Granules coordinately upregulate PPARα target genes, including ACOX1 and enoyl-CoA hydratase and 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (EHHADH), while concomitantly downregulating TNFα, thereby restoring lipid homeostasis and attenuating inflammation in MASH models419.

These formulations exemplify a “network pharmacology” approach, simultaneously targeting lipid metabolism, redox balance, immune tone, and BA signaling through PPARα-centric coordination. This multi-system modulation profile confers a specific advantage in lean MASLD that extends beyond the metabolic fragility considerations applicable to all natural products: the multi-component nature of TCM formulations allows simultaneous engagement of the multiple converging PPARα suppressors, aging-related epigenetic silencing, gut–liver axis disruption, oxidative stress, and lipotoxicity, that single monomers can only partially address. The gentle, titratable nature of this pharmacological activity aligns with the therapeutic requirements of lean individuals, where the absence of metabolic buffering capacity demands interventions that restore PPARα function incrementally rather than through supraphysiological receptor activation.

It must be explicitly acknowledged that the evidence base for TCM formulations in PPARα-targeted MASLD therapy shares the limitations outlined for natural product monomers, and is further complicated by the multi-component nature of these preparations: the attribution of efficacy specifically to PPARα engagement, rather than to the numerous other pharmacological activities of constituent compounds, is methodologically challenging and has rarely been rigorously established. Lean MASLD-specific evidence for TCM formulations is virtually absent, and the translation of findings from general NAFLD or obese MASLD models to the lean phenotype requires prospective validation in appropriately designed studies.

Collectively, the natural product-derived and TCM-based PPARα modulators reviewed in Sections 4.2.1 through 4.2.4 demonstrate that PPARα restoration can be approached through a spectrum of pharmacological strategies, from direct dietary ligand supplementation to multi-component network modulation, each with distinct mechanistic profiles, evidence bases, and translational challenges (Fig. 6). Limitations and translational challenges

Despite their mechanistic promise, natural product-derived and TCM-based PPARα agonists face several limitations that constrain clinical translation. Poor bioavailability is the primary challenge: most compounds, including berberine and curcumin, are lipophilic with low intestinal solubility and undergo rapid hepatic first-pass metabolism, making therapeutically relevant concentrations difficult to achieve. This is compounded by the persistent challenge of standardization, as active constituent concentrations in herbal preparations vary substantially across geographic origins, harvest seasons, batches, and extraction methods, compromising reproducibility in both mechanistic research and clinical application. Clinical evidence is currently limited to small, short-term studies predominantly derived from in vitro or rodent models, with a lack of rigorous human data specifically linking PPARα activation to clinical endpoints. While most studies focus on general or obese MASLD populations, lean MASLD-specific trial data is virtually absent; the distinct pathophysiological context of this subpopulation has rarely been explicitly addressed, representing a significant research gap. Safety concerns include hepatotoxicity at high doses, risks of heavy metal and pesticide contamination, and cytochrome P450 inhibition by compounds such as berberine, raising significant herb–drug interaction concerns in MASLD polypharmacy patients. The multi-target and multi-component nature of these TCM formulations, while mechanistically advantageous, impedes the precise target attribution required to isolate the contribution of PPARα engagement from other pharmacological activities for regulatory approval, while limited patentability reduces commercial incentive.

These limitations do not, however, represent insurmountable barriers but rather define the research agenda for the next stage of development. Natural product-derived agonists are most productively understood as lead compounds for rational drug discovery: structural modification of their core scaffolds can address pharmacokinetic deficiencies, enhancing potency and reducing toxicity. Integrated multi-omics analysis combined with artificial intelligence-driven target deconvolution can provide the mechanistic resolution required to satisfy regulatory standards by mapping drug-target networks and identifying core molecular drivers of efficacy. Furthermore, the development of targeted hepatic delivery systems, including lipid nanoparticles and GalNAc-conjugated platforms, can overcome absorption barriers and enable liver-enriched drug accumulation. Looking further ahead, wearable biosensor technologies will provide continuous pharmacokinetic monitoring, accelerating the translation of these therapeutics from preclinical promise to clinical validation.

Integrated therapeutic framework: From multi-target crosstalk to precision PPARα activation in lean MASLD

The multi-target pharmacological activity of natural product-derived PPARα modulators reviewed in Section 4.2 acquires its fullest mechanistic justification when viewed within the broader nuclear receptor network that governs hepatic lipid homeostasis in lean MASLD. Single-target PPARα agonism, while mechanistically rational, addresses only one node of a dysregulated regulatory landscape in which PPARα insufficiency is embedded within, and perpetuated by, concurrent dysfunction of interacting nuclear receptors and metabolic sensors. Understanding this crosstalk is therefore prerequisite to designing precision strategies that achieve spatially and contextually appropriate PPARα restoration without compromising the limited energy and redox reserves that characterize the lean MASLD phenotype.

Nuclear receptor crosstalk: Mechanistic rationale for network-level PPARα restoration

The therapeutic targeting of PPARα in lean MASLD does not occur in isolation but within a complex network of interacting nuclear receptors whose crosstalk shapes both the efficacy and the safety profile of PPARα-directed interventions. In MASLD, pathological LXR activation drives de novo lipogenesis (DNL) and sequesters RXR into LXR/RXR complexes, reducing RXR availability for PPARα and inducing functional PPARα insufficiency, independent of PPARα expression or ligand status. Reciprocal antagonism amplifies this imbalance: LXR promotes lipogenesis via sterol-regulatory element binding protein (SREBP)-1c upregulation while repressing PPARα-driven fatty acid oxidation (FAO) genes, creating a pro-steatotic, pro-inflammatory pathological state. PPAR was the first orphan nuclear receptor demonstrated to heterodimerize with RXR: upon ligand binding, the PPAR‒ligand complex translocates into the nucleus, forms a heterodimer with RXR, and binds to PPREs in target gene promoters to regulate transcriptional activity421. RXR serves as an obligate prerequisite for the function of all core nuclear receptors including PPARα, LXR, and FXR, and is subject to competitive binding among these partners422. This competitive dynamic has direct pathophysiological consequences in lean MASLD. In MASLD, LXR is upregulated, driving DNL and promoting hepatic steatosis423,424. Under conditions of limited intracellular RXR availability, LXR activation sequesters RXR within LXR/RXR complexes, substantially reducing the pool of RXR available for PPARα heterodimerization and thereby generating a state of functional PPARα insufficiency that is independent of PPARα expression or ligand availability425. LXR has been associated with MASH progression and plays a critical role in regulating SREBPs426,427. The reciprocal antagonism between PPARα and LXR is well established: PPARα and LXR recognize similar response elements and share overlapping target genes428,429. PPARα suppresses LXR signaling by inhibiting the SREBP-1c promoter, while conversely, LXR suppresses PPARα signaling to inhibit gene promoters supporting lipid catabolism430. The competitive formation of PPARα/RXR versus LXRα/RXR heterodimers represents a central mechanism through which this reciprocal antagonism is enacted at the molecular level. Inverse LXR agonism has emerged as one of the therapeutic research directions for MASLD treatment423. The development of agents that simultaneously agonize PPARα and inverse-agonize LXR therefore represents a mechanistically rational and therapeutically promising direction for lean MASLD, addressing the dual pathological imbalance of suppressed FAO and enhanced DNL within a unified pharmacological strategy.

PPARα and FXR interact in the regulation of lipid metabolism genes, control of hepatocellular lipid utilization, and modulation of BA synthesis and transport431,432. The crosstalk between PPARα and FXR has been summarized in several reviews250,431; here we highlight selected findings published relevance to lean MASLD. Elevated serum secondary BAs and increased FGF19, a surrogate marker of FXR activity, have been confirmed in lean MASLD patients433, suggesting that FXR signaling is not simply deficient in this population but may be differentially activated in a BA species-dependent manner, with implications for its interaction with PPARα. FXR is the primary receptor for BAs and participates in their synthesis and transport. PPARα also exerts regulatory influence over BA metabolism: the PPARα agonist gemfibrozil upregulates the key BA synthesis enzymes cytochrome P450 (CYP) 7A1 and CYP8B1, leading to increased BA production, an effect that is abolished in PPARα-knockout mice434, directly demonstrating that PPARα contributes to BA homeostasis through a mechanism dependent on intact receptor signaling. The therapeutic potential of simultaneously targeting both receptors is supported by several lines of preclinical evidence. The Poria cocos extract referenced in Section 4.2.3 simultaneously activates FXR and PPARα expression to ameliorate MASLD435. Furthermore, benzoxazole derivatives effectively activate both FXR and PPARα in hepatocytes and demonstrate significant antifibrotic and metabolic regulatory properties in liver and kidney fibrosis models, offering a dual-receptor pharmacological strategy with therapeutic potential for fibrosing MASLD436. Mechanistically, PPARα activation enhances β-oxidation to promote hepatic lipid disposal, while FXR agonism suppresses DNL by inhibiting SREBP-1c. Although potent FXR agonists have been shown in clinical trials to elevate LDL-C and reduce HDL-C, thereby increasing cardiovascular risk, PPARα, a classical target for lipid regulation—can counteract this potential risk. Furthermore, the two receptors work synergistically to attenuate inflammation and fibrosis. While this dual-target strategy represents a promising therapeutic candidate for MASLD, the competition for RXR remains unavoidable, and optimizing the ratio of activation intensity between the two targets persists as a formidable challenge.

The nuclear receptor crosstalk landscape described above establishes that functional PPARα insufficiency in lean MASLD is perpetuated not only by direct suppression of the receptor itself but by the competitive sequestration of its obligate heterodimeric partner RXR and by the opposing transcriptional activity of pathologically activated LXR. Restoring PPARα function in this context therefore requires strategies that achieve cell-type-selective receptor reactivation within a dysregulated nuclear receptor environment, a requirement that motivates the hepatocyte-specific delivery approaches detailed in the following section.

Hepatocyte-specific PPARα reactivation: Restoring intrinsic metabolic resilience

The therapeutic imperative in lean MASLD lies not in maximal systemic PPARα activation, but in spatially and contextually precise modulation that restores hepatic metabolic competence without compromising the limited energy and redox reserves characteristic of this phenotype.

While clinically approved hepatocyte-targeted PPARα agonists remain under development, preclinical studies using adenovirus-mediated PPARα overexpression (Ad-PPARα) provide compelling proof-of-concept for cell-autonomous rescue of lipid and redox homeostasis. In murine models, Ad-PPARα administration fully reversed hepatic TG accumulation induced by hepatocyte-specific deletion of friend of GATA 2 (FOG2)437, underscoring PPARα′s sufficiency to override upstream transcriptional deficits.

Even more relevant to lean MASLD is the role of Krüppel-like factor 16 (KLF16), a transcriptional regulator whose expression is consistently downregulated in both human MASLD livers and diet-induced mouse models. KLF16 directly binds the PPARα promoter to sustain its basal expression, thereby coordinating FAO and antioxidant defense438. In KLF16 liver-specific knockout mice, a model mimicking the subtle transcriptional insufficiency characteristic of metabolically unhealthy lean individuals, Ad-PPARα infection not only reversed lipid accumulation in primary hepatocytes but also conferred multisystemic benefits: significant reduction in body and liver fat mass, marked decrease in plasma TG levels, increased mitochondrial number and improved ultrastructural integrity, suppression of hepatic reactive oxygen species (ROS) overproduction and pro-inflammatory gene expression (e.g., TNFα, IL-6), restoration of insulin signaling, and overall improvement in liver function markers. These findings highlight that even modest deficits in PPARα transcriptional tone, insufficient to cause overt obesity, can drive steatosis and inflammation in lean hosts, and that targeted reactivation of hepatocytic PPARα alone is sufficient to reverse this cascade.

Future translation will depend on the development of non-viral, liver-directed delivery platforms (e.g., GalNAc-conjugated small molecules or lipid nanoparticles) capable of achieving selective hepatocyte engagement without systemic exposure, a critical safety requirement for lean patients prone to drug-induced myopathy or energy depletion. This requirement is particularly stringent in lean MASLD patients, where the absence of metabolic buffering capacity renders systemic drug exposure more likely to produce off-target consequences including myopathy and energy depletion, and where the hepatocyte-specific precision of delivery is therefore both a pharmacological and a patient safety imperative.

While hepatocyte-targeted PPARα reactivation addresses the cell-autonomous deficit in FAO capacity, it does not directly resolve the gut‒liver axis disruption that constitutes an independent and self-perpetuating driver of PPARα suppression in lean MASLD. Intestinal PPARα activation represents a complementary and spatially distinct therapeutic axis that targets this upstream pathogenic input, and is reviewed in the following section.

Intestinal PPARα activation: Modulating the gut–liver axis to attenuate systemic drivers

Parallel to hepatocyte-centric strategies, emerging evidence positions intestinal PPARα as a critical gatekeeper of gut barrier integrity and microbial homeostasis, two processes that are frequently disrupted in lean MASLD despite normal BMI439,440. Activation of intestinal epithelial PPARα exerts multimodal protective effects on gut–liver axis function. Both synthetic PPARα agonists, such as fenofibrate, and endogenous ligands upregulate tight junction proteins including occludin, zonula occludens (ZO)-1, and claudins, thereby restoring paracellular sealing and reducing bacterial translocation441, 442, 443. In addition, specific commensal strains, exemplified by lactobacillus plantarum, promote epithelial repair and enhance mitochondrial β-oxidation in enterocytes through PPARα-dependent mechanisms444, highlighting a role for PPARα in maintaining host–microbe symbiosis. Consistently, fibrate treatment ameliorates diet-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction by correcting aberrant protein kinase C alpha (PKCα) signaling and preserving junctional architecture445.

A key mechanistic link involves CYP2E1-driven nitroxidative stress, which promotes tyrosine nitration of tight junction proteins, particularly under high-fructose or alcohol exposure, leading to endotoxemia446. PPARα activation counteracts this pathway: the agonist WY14643 reverses alcohol-induced PPARα suppression mediated by CYP2E1 upregulation, while Cyp2e1−/− mice exhibit amplified PPARα signaling due to the accumulation of lysophosphatidylcholine and PUFAs447, further validating the reciprocal regulation between oxidative stress and PPARα activity.

Clinically, lean MASLD patients frequently exhibit features of subclinical gut dysbiosis, including reduced Bacteroidetes abundance that correlates with adiposity-independent metabolic dysfunction448, elevated circulating levels of TNFα, and an increased prevalence of small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO)449. These alterations highlight a gut–liver axis disturbance that occurs independently of overt obesity and may contribute to disease progression in lean individuals. Accordingly, therapeutic modulation of the intestinal microbiota has emerged as a promising intervention strategy in this population. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from lean donors has been shown to enrich butyrate-producing taxa and improve insulin sensitivity450. Probiotic supplementation, exemplified by Lactobacillus acidophilus, enhances intestinal barrier function through valerate secretion and attenuates hepatic steatosis451,452. In addition, administration of bacteroides thetaiotaomicron normalizes lipid metabolism and body weight in HFD-fed mice453.

Central to this crosstalk are short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), acetic, propionic, and butyric acids, produced by microbial fermentation of dietary fiber454,455. Though historically viewed as energy substrates, SCFAs predominantly exert protective, PPARα-dependent effects: they strengthen the gut barrier, modulate immune cell function, and serve as endogenous PPARα ligands in both intestinal and hepatic tissues248,456,457. Most notably, colon-targeted SCFA prodrugs (e.g., polymer-conjugated butyrate) selectively restore PPARα expression in periportal hepatocytes, downregulate CPT1A, thereby fine-tuning FAO flux, and significantly ameliorating fibrosis in MASH models458, demonstrating that non-systemic, gut-localized interventions can exert profound hepatic benefits via PPARα-centric signaling.

The natural PPARα agonists described in Section 4.2, many of which exhibit inherent tissue selectivity, low cytotoxicity, and gut-modulatory effects, represent ideal candidates for integration into next-generation precision delivery platforms (e.g., hepatocyte-targeted nanoparticles or colon-restricted prodrugs), thereby integrating evolutionary wisdom with modern pharmacokinetic control. The convergence of hepatocyte-targeted and intestinal PPARα activation strategies with the multi-target nuclear receptor network modulation described in Section 4.3.1 defines the most mechanistically complete approach to PPARα-centered therapy in lean MASLD currently supported by preclinical evidence: one that simultaneously addresses the hepatic lipid oxidation deficit, the gut–liver axis disruption, and the upstream nuclear receptor dysregulation that collectively sustain PPARα insufficiency in this population.

The hepatocyte-specific and intestinal PPARα reactivation strategies reviewed above address PPARα insufficiency at the level of receptor activation itself. However, in lean MASLD patients where PPARα suppression is deeply entrenched through epigenetic silencing, aging-related transcriptional decline, or genetic predisposition upstream receptor reactivation alone may be insufficient to fully restore the downstream metabolic effector functions that PPARα normally coordinates. In such cases, therapeutic strategies that directly target PPARα′s downstream mediators, particularly the FGF21-ketogenesis axis, offer a complementary approach that bypasses upstream suppression to restore functional metabolic output, and represent the most clinically advanced PPARα pathway-targeting strategies currently under investigation.

PPARα‒FGF21 axis and emerging downstream mediator-based strategies in lean MASLD

As established before, FGF21 is a hepatokine directly transcribed from a PPARα-responsive promoter, representing one of the most functionally significant downstream effectors of hepatic PPARα activity. The PPARα–FGF21 axis therefore occupies a unique position in the therapeutic landscape of lean MASLD: impaired PPARα activity reduces FGF21 induction, while FGF21 deficiency in turn compromises the peripheral fatty acid utilization and inter-organ metabolic coordination that PPARα-driven ketogenesis normally sustains459,460. This bidirectional dependency suggests that therapeutic strategies targeting FGF21 directly may partially compensate for upstream PPARα insufficiency, bypassing transcriptional suppression mechanisms to restore downstream metabolic effector function. Given that ketone bodies retain an independent prognostic association with MASLD severity alongside FGF21, the PPARα–ketogenesis–FGF21 axis is positioned as a functionally integrated therapeutic target rather than a collection of independent nodes.

FGF21-based therapeutics have emerged as one of the most actively investigated treatment strategies in MASLD over the past several years, with multiple clinical programs now reporting Phase 2 and Phase 3 data461, 462, 463.

Pegbelfermin, a PEGylated FGF21 analogue, reduced hepatic fat fraction and improved multiple fibrosis-related biomarkers in the Phase 2 FALCON program, thereby providing clinical proof-of-concept that pharmacologic activation of the FGF21 pathway may represent a therapeutically relevant strategy for steatohepatitis and fibrosis464, 465, 466. Efruxifermin, an Fc-fused long-acting FGF21 analogue, has likewise demonstrated significant histologic benefit in patients with biopsy-confirmed MASH and advanced fibrosis, including improvement in fibrosis stage without worsening of steatohepatitis in Phase 2 studies, and has subsequently advanced to Phase 3 evaluation467, 468, 469. Its combined effects on hepatic steatosis, inflammatory activity, and fibrosis are broadly consistent with restoration of the downstream metabolic program expected from reactivation of the PPARα–FGF21 axis. Additional endocrine fibroblast growth factor-based or metabolically related agents also remain under active investigation. Notably, aldafermin is an FGF19 analogue rather than an FGF21 analogue, whereas cotadutide is a dual GLP-1/glucagon receptor agonist; nevertheless, both have shown improvements in liver fat content as well as in metabolic and fibrosis-related parameters in MASH/MASLD populations470, 471, 472. Critically, however, these interventional studies have not prospectively focused on lean MASLD as a distinct therapeutic subgroup, and whether the magnitude or pattern of benefit differs between lean and obese phenotypes therefore remains unclear. Given the distinctive pathogenic drivers of lean MASLD, particularly its greater dependence on hepatic oxidative and endocrine buffering pathways when adipose metabolic reserve is limited, lean-specific evaluation of FGF21-directed therapies remains a high-priority unmet need.

Beyond recombinant FGF21 analogues, strategies that enhance endogenous FGF21 production through PPARα reactivation represent a mechanistically upstream and potentially more physiological therapeutic approach. The ketogenic axis downstream of PPARα, including HMGCS2-driven ketone body synthesis and the BHB-centered regulatory circuitry described in Section 3.1, provides additional entry points for intervention. Preclinical studies already support this concept: hepatic Bdh1 overexpression delivered by adeno-associated virus attenuated liver injury, fibrosis, inflammation, and apoptosis in a MASLD mouse model, whereas Hmgcs2 overexpression improved hepatosteatosis and glucose homeostasis in diet-induced MASLD mice473,474. Taken together, these findings support the therapeutic relevance of restoring PPARα-driven ketogenesis as a disease modifying strategy, although direct evidence for the pharmacological activation of HMGCS2 or BDH1 remains limited. Whether small-molecule activators of these enzymes can recapitulate the benefits observed with genetic restoration remains an important open question, particularly in lean MASLD, in which ketone-body insufficiency may amplify the consequences of impaired PPARα signaling.

Several upstream coregulatory nodes, including AMPK and SIRT1, have been implicated in modulating PPARα transcriptional capacity, and their engagement by the natural compounds reviewed in Section 4.2 provides an additional mechanistic rationale for multitarget natural-product strategies. However, their specific contributions as independent therapeutic targets in lean MASLD remain to be established, and they are therefore not discussed further here. Collectively, the PPARα–FGF21–ketogenesis axis defines a functionally integrated downstream effector network whose therapeutic restoration may be pursued from multiple directions: direct administration of FGF21 analogues to bypass upstream PPARα suppression, induction of endogenous FGF21 through the PPARα reactivation strategies detailed in Sections 4.3.1 and 4.3.3, or restoration of ketogenic capacity through targeted enzymatic or gene-based interventions. The convergence of these approaches on a shared downstream metabolic program, enhanced FAO, improved inter-organ energy coordination, and maintenance of PPARα target-gene activity, positions this axis as a particularly compelling therapeutic focus in lean MASLD, where the functional consequences of its impairment may be disproportionately severe relative to the absolute degree of hepatic fat accumulation.

The therapeutic strategies reviewed in this section reflect a progressive shift from maximal systemic PPARα activation toward spatially precise, metabolically compatible, and network-level receptor restoration. Synthetic agonists provide the strongest pharmacological basis but lack lean MASLD-specific trial data; natural product-derived compounds and TCM formulations offer multi-target activity aligned with lean MASLD pathophysiology but remain predominantly preclinical; FGF21 analogues represent the most clinically advanced PPARα pathway strategy yet have systematically excluded lean patients; and precision delivery platforms remain at early translational stages. Across all modalities, the absence of lean MASLD-specific randomized controlled trial data represents the most critical unmet need, one that reflects a broader failure to recognize lean MASLD as a pathophysiologically distinct entity requiring its own evidence base, and that the mechanistic framework established in this review seeks to address.

Conclusion and discussion

The study of a single transcription factor invariably reveals itself as a window into a vast and interconnected biological network. This review systematically summarizes recent advances in research on the role of PPARα in lean MASLD, building upon established knowledge of hepatic PPARα functions to examine its broader regulatory landscape, encompassing extrahepatic signaling, epigenetic programming, inter-organ energy coordination and gut–liver axis integrity. PPARα is a well-established therapeutic target for MASLD, and its significance has been thoroughly validated in the scientific literature. Our focus is on emerging discoveries in recent years, including novel agonists, natural compounds, and traditional herbal medicines. We build upon the established understanding of hepatic PPARα functions and further examine the influence of extrahepatic PPARα signaling and lipid metabolism on MASLD.

The findings demonstrate that PPARα plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of lean MASLD by regulating key pathways such as FAO, inflammatory responses, ketogenesis, and inter-organ metabolic coordination. This research provides a theoretical foundation for targeted therapies. For lean MASLD specifically, this mechanistic depth is clinically necessary: the paradox of severe liver disease in the absence of obesity cannot be resolved by frameworks built around adiposity, and PPARα, as the convergent node through which aging-related epigenetic silencing, visceral adiposity-independent lipotoxicity, gut–liver axis disruption, and genetic susceptibility collectively impair hepatic lipid homeostasis, occupies a uniquely privileged position as both a pathophysiological hub and a therapeutic target.

Several challenges persist in current research on lean MASLD. The direct evidence chain linking PPARα suppression specifically to lean MASLD initiation and progression remains incomplete: foundational mechanistic studies were conducted predominantly in obese or diet-induced obesity models, and the mechanistic framework assembled here rests on convergent indirect evidence rather than integrated lean MASLD-specific investigation. First, there is a lack of animal models that accurately replicate human lean MASLD, as its pathogenesis involves complex interactions among genetic, environmental, and behavioral factors. Existing studies, which primarily rely on mouse models, show significant discrepancies from human disease manifestations. Lean-specific conditional knockout models, patient-derived hepatocyte organoids, and prospective biopsy cohorts stratified by BMI represent critical priorities for strengthening this evidence base. Second, while visceral and abdominal fat accumulation is a hallmark of lean MASLD, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, particularly concerning how hepatic TGs are preferentially directed to visceral adipocytes rather than to other fat depots. Furthermore, the heightened lipolytic activity of visceral fat and its direct effects on the liver warrant additional investigation475. Modern lifestyle changes, including SB, fast-food consumption, and chronic stress due to social competition, may promote visceral fat accumulation through increased catecholamine sensitivity, exacerbating hepatic metabolic dysfunction. Clinically, lean MASLD patients remain systematically excluded from major MASH trials, and no randomized controlled trial has evaluated any PPARα-targeting therapy specifically in this population. BMI-stratified post-hoc analyses of completed trials, biobank-based pharmacoepidemiological studies, and adaptive trial designs incorporating PPARα activity surrogates such as ketone bodies and FGF21 represent immediately actionable priorities. Gender differences are also significant. Epidemiological data indicate a higher prevalence of lean MASLD among women, contrasting with overall MASLD epidemiology patterns, which show male predominance, and recent studies highlight sexual dimorphism in PPARα pathways, with pemafibrate demonstrating stronger PPARα induction in females while male livers exhibit greater enrichment of PPARα-related genes associated with inflammatory and lipid metabolic pathways476. The development of tissue-selective PPARα agonists remains technically unresolved: current agents distribute systemically, cannot discriminate between hepatic cell populations, and do not recapitulate the physiological pericentral zonation of PPARα activity. The multi-target complexity of nuclear receptor crosstalk, in which PPARα transcriptional output depends on RXR availability, LXR competitive dynamics, and FXR bile acid signaling across varying cellular and disease contexts, further complicates rational therapeutic design and represents a mechanistic priority that current experimental tools cannot fully address.

Looking ahead, breakthroughs in lean MASLD research will require multidimensional approaches. To begin with, investigations should enhance our understanding of the crosstalk between extrahepatic tissues (e.g., the central nervous system, gut, and adipose tissues) and the liver, and promote the development of more physiologically relevant multifactorial animal models. A deeper exploration of PPARα′s sexual dimorphism and interactions with sex hormones will facilitate the development of gender-specific therapies. The role of other nuclear receptors including FXR and LXR, in lean MASLD warrants attention, particularly in the context of RXR competitive dynamics and the therapeutic potential of agents that simultaneously agonize PPARα and inverse-agonize LXR. Advancements in omics technologies, particularly integrated single-cell sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and metabolomics, will offer unprecedented opportunities to elucidate molecular mechanisms and identify novel therapeutic targets at the cellular resolution required to characterize PPARα′s zonated hepatic activity and cell-type-specific regulatory functions. Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning are poised to revolutionize drug discovery for lean MASLD by enabling rapid screening of PPARα modulators, predicting compound–target interactions, optimizing pharmacokinetic profiles and deconvoluting the multi-target activity of natural product and herbal formulations at the level of individual nuclear receptor engagement. AI-driven integration of multi-omics data with clinical phenotypes could further facilitate the identification of lean-specific endotypes and accelerate the design of precision therapeutics tailored to this metabolically fragile population. Research examining the associations between sociobehavioral factors (including stress, sedentary lifestyle, and circadian disruption) and metabolic dysregulation will inform comprehensive intervention strategies. Ultimately, the work of understanding PPARα fully, across all its cellular contexts, regulatory interactions, and therapeutic entry points, is neither complete nor simple. It is, however, necessary and the lean MASLD population, long neglected by both clinical trial design and mechanistic investigation, stands to benefit most directly from its completion.

In conclusion, while significant progress has been made in understanding the pivotal role of PPARα in lean MASLD, much remains to be explored. Future research must address the limitations of current models and deepen our understanding of the intricate interplay between PPARα and other physiological systems. By integrating cutting-edge technologies and considering sociobehavioral influences, this will advance our comprehension of lean MASLD as an increasingly prevalent metabolic disorder, especially among younger populations. Ultimately, these efforts will lay the groundwork for developing precision medicine approaches tailored to the unique needs of lean MASLD patients, thereby enhancing treatment efficacy and patient outcomes.
