Exploring Taphrina deformans, the Springtime Scourge of Peach

Introduction

Peach leaf curl is a fungal disease found in stone fruit mainly affecting the peach crop ( Prunus persica ). Characterized by spectacular reddish leaves deformations, it can damage the entire tree canopy (Rossi et al. 2006). The disease reduces the photosynthesis efficiency in infected leaves that induces extensive defoliation, which can lead to tree weakening and death (Pierce 1900). Owing to the disease's timing in early spring, a critical period for fruit set and development, peach production potential in particular is severely reduced. Moreover, some years, fruits can be directly infected and fall off the trees (Klee 1940; Rossi et al. 2007a). Consequently, orchards are routinely treated to prevent this devastating disease. In 2018, fungicides accounted for one in two of the 21.2 treatments applied in French peach orchards, with 4–5 of these specifically targeting peach leaf curl (Agreste 2021, 2023). However, farmers are compelled to limit the use of chemical products in order to preserve human health and the environment, as promoted by the European Parliament through the Directive 2009/128/EC on the sustainable use of pesticides (European Union 2009). Previously regarded as a minor disease due to effective chemical control in orchards, peach leaf curl is now emerging as a significant concern for peach production in the context of reduced fungicide use and the ban of some molecules. Consequently, research on peach leaf curl has become essential to secure future peach production in the transition towards more sustainable management practices. While scientific interest triggered by peach leaf curl can be traced back to the 19th century, basic knowledge was acquired in the following century. Although peach leaf curl has received renewed attention in recent years, studies employing modern methodologies remain limited, and a holistic understanding of the disease is still lacking. Two recent short reviews are available and offer valuable concise overviews of the topic; however, their scope limits the depth of coverage of several key aspects of the fungal pathogen (Binil et al. 2025; Sodikov et al. 2025). This highlights the need for a more integrated and detailed synthesis that brings together historical and contemporary findings to advance our understanding of T. deformans and its interaction with the peach host.

In the present review, we compile a very large number of available studies on peach leaf curl to report on current knowledge and highlight the main gaps that warrant further investigation or updating. Several fundamental pieces of knowledge and hypotheses were published in the 20th century, and this review brings them together before they fall into obscurity. Whenever possible, we have examined all relevant resources concerning pathogen description and life cycle, its interactions with the host plant, and current orchard management strategies. Because resistant peach varieties are widely regarded as a key solution for managing peach leaf curl in orchards without the use of chemical treatments (Plénet et al. 2023), we conclude this review with a plant breeding perspective.

Taphrina deformans, the Fungus Responsible of Peach Leaf Curl Disease

A Long Way From Taxonomy to Naming Taphrina deformans

The symptoms of peach leaf curl disease have been described for a long time, starting with gardeners. The earliest accessible written description appears in a French book from 1811, where the disease was referred to as ‘maladie du rouge’ (de Ville‐Sur‐Arce and Louis 1811). At first, these symptoms were considered a simple consequence of cold rain (de Saint‐Martin 1837). Only later, the mycologist M.J Berkeley attributed the disease to a fungus, named Ascomyces bullatus (Berk.) (Berkeley 1857) after observing similar curl symptoms on pear trees (Berkeley and Horticultural Society of London 1855). However, in 1860, he revised this assignment and proposed a specific species for leaf curl affecting peach trees: Ascomyces deformans (Berk.) (Berkeley 1860). In the following years, the fungus appeared with different names, including Exoascus deformans (Berk.) Fuckel (1869), Taphrina deformans (Berk.) Tulasne (1866), Taphrina amygdali (Jacz.) Mix or Exoascus amygdali (Jacz.) (Mix 1949). Finally, the currently accepted name for the causal agent of peach leaf curl disease was established as Taphrina deformans (Berk.) Tulasne (Tulasne 1866). This ascomycete belongs to the genus Taphrina, within the family Taphrinaceae, order Taphrinales (Mix 1949). In 1990, mycologists named differently the anamorph and telomorph form of the fungus, so T. deformans was the telomorph name for the sexual form and Lalaria deformans the anamorph name for the asexual form, but this distinction is rarely used (Moore 1990). Today the taxonomic assignment of T. deformans has been confirmed by molecular studies and genome analyses (Bacigálová et al. 2003; Cissé et al. 2013; Rodrigues and Fonseca 2003; Sjamsuridzal et al. 1997; Sugiyama et al. 2006).

Is T. deformans Diversity Structured in Relation to the Prunus Host?

For a long time Taphrina species were classified considering a combination of the host plant and of the type of symptoms (Mix 1949). This classification was supported by one of the rare molecular studies available (Rodrigues and Fonseca 2003) grouping together samples from curled leaves of P. persica and Prunus dulcis trees. In the case of T. deformans, this Taphrina species is so far considered as specifically responsible for leaf curl symptoms. While this is mostly known for recurrent spectacular damage in peach, leaf curl symptoms can also be observed on other Prunus species: P. dulcis , P. armeniaca (apricot) and P. domestica trees (Cunningham 1923; Fuller 1910; Frisullo et al. 2000; Gupta et al. 1973; Khalil et al. 2020; Kövics and Tarcali 2011; Macek 1970; Smith and Cass 1959; Syrop 1975; Tanova et al. 2019; Yugakova 1970). A single study suggested T. deformans to be the causal agent of plum pocket on P. salicina (Japanese plum, Oh et al. 2020). Some authors wondered whether T. deformans was responsible for every leaf curl symptom on Prunus and if Prunus hosts allowed different Taphrina species or T. deformans subspecies to be distinguished. This idea led Mix (1949) to distinguish and name specifically the fungus responsible for leaf curl according to its Prunus host (T. deformans for Prunus persica and P. dulcis, T. mume for P. armeniaca and P. mume , and T. cerasi for P. avium ). Schneider reached similar conclusions in 1971 as he tried, with no success, to trigger leaf curl by inoculating a strain from almond tree on peach trees. Not questioning the efficacy of his inoculation method nor the role of host/pathogen genetic diversity, he distinguished two T. deformans forms, ‘persicae’ and ‘amygdali’, depending on the host (Schneider 1971). A more recent investigation at the molecular level evidenced two different subspecies of T. deformans (Fonseca and Rodrigues 2011). T. deformans intraspecific diversity may be also expected because T. betulina, a related species attacking birch, showed an intraspecific diversity (Christita et al. 2022). Despite such evidence, and the prominent role of pathogen diversity in plant–pathogen interactions, the vast majority of leaf curl symptoms on Prunus trees are still attributed to T. deformans and genomics have not been mobilized to explore Taphrina inter‐ and intraspecific diversity. This is a strikingly significant gap in the leaf curl literature so far.

Current research efforts should thus aim at addressing these remaining uncertainties by undertaking a comprehensive study, aimed at classifying Taphrina species according to host–symptom associations and elucidating the population structure of T. deformans strains. A large phylogenetic analysis could shed light on all these questions. The genome sequence of T. deformans, available since 2013 (Cissé et al. 2013) will facilitate the design of primers for molecular diversity analyses to this aim. Collecting symptomatic Prunus leaves across the world with special attention to the diversity of symptoms and host species would allow (1) the construction of a solid phylogenetic tree to classify Taphrina species in a robust way to identify, or refute, subspecies, and (2) further exploration of the structure of T. deformans species causing leaf curl according to the hosts. In addition, no study to date has investigated other factors impacting pathogen genetic diversity such as the geographical location, agricultural practices—including plant protection methods, or peach cultivars.

For many years, the combination of host plant and symptoms was considered as sufficient to identify Taphrina species (Rodrigues and Fonseca 2003) but nowadays a reliable and more precise method should be used to routinely recognize T. deformans, thereby preventing any misidentification. Numerous studies have characterized the morphology of spores and mycelium of T. deformans (Knowles 1887; Martin 1940; Mix 1949; Pierstorff 1935). However, based on the available studies, it is not obvious for a non‐specialist to distinguish T. deformans from other Taphrina species with very similar characteristics by the means of a microscope. Often, a more detailed investigation is necessary to discriminate two Taphrina species based on morphology, as is the case for a geomorphometry study of asci between T. deformans and T. caerulescens (Evans 2016). The most common and reliable way to identify T. deformans remains the use of molecular tools to highlight distinctive genetic polymorphisms (Rodrigues and Fonseca 2003). Several authors have already described robust PCR‐based diagnostics and specific primers have been validated for T. deformans detection (Maniatis et al. 2024; Svetaz et al. 2017; Tavares et al. 2004).

In the remaining body of this review and unless otherwise mentioned, we will focus on the specific interactions between the host P. persica and the pathogen T. deformans.

A Likely Worldwide Distribution Although Data Is Missing

Based on their correspondence with scientists around the world, several authors claimed that peach leaf curl disease was linked to the distribution of its hosts (Mix 1949; Pierce 1900). There is currently no study listing damage caused by the disease, which would make it possible to analyse the distribution of T. deformans worldwide. Databases like the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF, https://www.gbif.org/fr/) do contain data on T. deformans occurrences but historical samplings are unlikely to reflect the real distribution of the disease. This database compiles 19th and 20th century sample collections from national museums, along with observations made by mycologists fascinated by this spectacular disease, even though it was barely present in their countries. This has resulted in a large number of T. deformans occurrences in the GBIF database in countries where mycologist communities were active, but where peach production was minor or negligable. On the other hand, major peach‐producing countries are not referenced, even though scientific publications confirmed the presence of the disease in these areas (i.e., Argentina [Goldy et al. 2017], Iran [Hossein Abadi et al. 2024; Esfandiari 1947], Morocco [Boulif 2016]). Information is hence heterogeneous and patchy, making it difficult to draw up a reliable distribution map of T. deformans. We currently lack a comprehensive international survey of peach leaf curl disease to validate the ubiquity of T. deformans in the adaptative zones of this crop.

Taphrina deformans, a Life Cycle Divided Between Leaf Mycelium in Spring and Spores on Tree Surface Year‐Round

Taphrina deformans is a dimorphic fungus with mycelium invading leaves during the obligate biotrophic phase and spores occurring during the saprophytic phase (Fitzpatrick 1934; Mix 1935; Pierce 1900). Known since the 19th century by microscopic observation (Berkeley 1860; Tulasne 1866), these two phases were recently confirmed by genome sequencing analysis (Cissé et al. 2013). Synthetized in Figure 1, each step of the T. deformans life cycle will be described in detail in the following parts.

Taphrina deformans life cycle.

Late Winter to Early Spring: Infection by Spores Initiates the Mycelium Phase

Marked by impressive symptoms on leaves, the mycelium phase is the most characteristic moment of the T. deformans biological cycle (see Figure 1, ‘filamentous stage’). This phase begins with leaf infection at the end of winter and early spring, transitioning from the spore form to the mycelium form. T. deformans spores form a germ tube able to penetrate the cuticle and epidermis (Knowles 1887; Martin 1940; Matuyama and Misawa 1962; Mix 1935; Pierce 1900). Genes coding for cutinases and lipases may be involved in host cell penetration during this phase (Cissé et al. 2013). Occasionally, T. deformans enters the leaf through the stomata, as shown by microscopic observations (Giordani et al. 2013), although this seems not to be the prevalent route of infection (Mix 1935). Infection usually begins on the abaxial surface (lower side) of the leaf, and more rarely on the adaxial surface (upper side) (Giordani et al. 2013). At the beginning of the 20th century, extensive discussions led to the conclusion that germination begins with the division of a single uninucleate spore. This process initiates the formation of a germ tube, which in turn opens the dikaryotic mycelium phase (see Figure 1 ‘Dicaryophase N + N’), characteristic of homothallism (i.e., single self‐compatible mating type) (Fitzpatrick 1934; Gavériaux 2010; Martin 1940; Mix 1935). However, rare observations of heterothallism have also been reported, where the conjugation of two spores is required to trigger this transition (Mix 1935; Pierce 1900). The recent genome analysis confirms the primary homothallic status (Almeida et al. 2015; Cissé et al. 2013).

During Spring: Mycelium Growth Causes Characteristic Symptoms of Peach Leaf Curl

Once the infection has started, mycelium grows inside the leaves (see Figure 1 ‘symptomatic phase’). The vegetative mycelium of T. deformans (Knowles 1887; Martin 1940; Mix 1949) is composed of binucleate, branched hyphae with unperforated septa (Caporali 1965; Syrop 1975). These hyphae have irregular forms, thickness and branching patterns to fill the available space between the plant cells (Caporali 1965; Knowles 1887; Pierce 1900; Martin 1940; Mix 1949). Except for the germ tube, which penetrates the cuticle, hyphae spread only in the intercellular spaces, as observed in all Taphrina species (Mix 1949; Svetaz et al. 2017). Several typical hyphal forms have been characterized: short or long (ranging from 10 to 40 μm), cylindrical and ovoid (Caporali 1965; Martin 1940).

End of Spring to Early Summer: Towards the Yeast Phase With Production of Spores

At the end of spring and early summer, leaf infection ends with mycelium entering a sporulation stage (see Figure 1 transition from ‘filamentous’ to ‘yeast’ stage). Hyphae grow in the sub‐epidermal area and branch out, spreading perpendicularly to penetrate deeper into the leaf tissues (Caporali 1965). When the mycelium reaches the adaxial leaf side, it spreads by branching under the cuticle (Knowles 1887; Martin 1940) to develop ascogenous cells (see Figure 1 ‘Diplophase 2N’) (Mix 1949; Syrop and Beckett 1976). These specialized cells, measuring 5–20 μm in length and 5–15 μm in width (Mix 1949), are cylindrical with walls of various thickness (Martin 1940; Mix 1949). The ascogenesis of T. deformans is well described in detail in literature (Mix 1949; Syrop and Beckett 1976). Differentiation of these ascogenous cells produces cylindrical and rounded naked asci with a dome‐shaped structure and thick walls (Martin 1940; Mix 1949; Syrop and Beckett 1976). The asci measure 17–56 μm in length and 7–15 μm in width (Mix 1949). Their growth pushes against and ruptures the cuticle, allowing the asci to emerge on the leaf surfaces (Knowles 1887; Martin 1940). Asci appear generally on the adaxial leaf side (Giordani et al. 2013; Knowles 1887; Martin 1940; Mix 1949), and more rarely on the abaxial side (Mix 1949).

Easily observable under a light microscope (Giordani et al. 2013), each ascus contains eight primary spores known as the ascospores. These ascospores are round or oval, uninucleate (Giordani et al. 2013; Mix 1949; Pierstorff 1935), and measure 3–7 μm in both length and width (Mix 1949). There are two ways for the ascospores discharge from asci. Either the basal vacuole containing ascospores enlarges until the ascus breaks when the ascospores are mature and environmental conditions allow their survival. Alternatively, ascospores can start budding in the vacuole, creating a pressure triggering the breakage of the ascus apex (Syrop and Beckett 1976).

At the end of the mycelium phase, once sporulation is complete (see Figure 1 start of ‘Haplophase N’), symptomatic leaves become necrotic and fall from the tree (Pierstorff 1935). New leaves show progressively fewer symptoms and eventually remain symptom‐free as summer approaches.

Summer to Winter: Spores as Overwintering Structures

Highly tolerant to extreme conditions, spores are the overwintering structure of T. deformans (Koch 1934; Lorenz 1976) and are found mostly within the bud scales and on the bark of the host tree (Mix 1949).

Two types of spores, namely ascospores and blastospores, are distinguished. The former are spores released from the asci, representing the sexual reproductive stage of T. deformans. The latter are secondary spores produced by the budding of ascospores or other blastospores, representing the asexual reproductive stage of T. deformans (Martin 1940; Mix 1949). Budding begins quickly after ascospore release and can even start prematurely within the asci (Martin 1940; Valleau 1940).

Some late spring infections are observed, but it remains unclear whether they are caused by late weather events or by secondary contamination, meaning that freshly produced spores infect healthy new leaves during the same spring.

While not calling into question these historical observations, made during an era of meticulous microscopic study, this literature is largely descriptive. Reassessing the life cycle using modern approaches is essential to validate, consolidate and potentially reveal new steps in the life cycle of T. deformans.

Multiscale Characteristics of Plant–Host Interaction Over the T. deformans Cycle on Peach

Peach Leaf Curl Symptoms in the Orchard

In orchards, peach leaf curl disease triggers characteristic symptoms that appear mainly on leaves (Figure 2A–D), although this disease can also affect fruits (Figure 2E,F), flowers (Figure 2G) or twigs (Pierce 1900). Leaves swell, thicken and deform to the point of presenting a hypertrophic aspect (Figure 2A,B, Knowles 1887). Initially red, purple or light green in colour (Pierstorff 1935), they turn brown and dry out to end up falling (Figure 2C). In years of severe attacks, massive defoliations can in turn lead to tree death (Pierce 1900). It should be noted that the intensity of the symptoms does not appear to be correlated with the fungal load (Novello et al. 2024). In the same way, infected fruits exhibit rough, irregular surface deformities accompanied by abnormal colouration, and their firmness is altered (Figure 2E,F; Klee 1940; Racskó et al. 2004). Infected fruits stop growing and quickly fall off in spring (Klee 1940). Spore release typically appears in orchards as a white to grey powdery coating on infected leaves showing symptoms (Figure 2D, Knowles 1887).

Histological Modifications Induced by T. deformans Infection

Numerous studies have documented the pronounced histological alterations induced by T. deformans infection in leaves. Infected tissues are often completely disorganized, with leaf structure being indistinguishable. This disruption is accompanied by abnormal and uncontrolled parenchymal cell division, resulting in increased mesophyll thickness (Knowles 1887; Matsuyama and Misawa 1966a; Piperkova and Vassilev 2023). Disruption of cell polarity during division leads to this anarchic leaf structure (Matuyama and Misawa 1962). Hyperplasia and hypertrophy are the two major cellular phenomena characterizing T. deformans‐infected leaves. Hyperplastic cells exhibit abnormal mitotic and amitotic activity (Giordani et al. 2013; Marte and Gargiulo 1972; Matuyama and Misawa 1962; Piperkova and Vassilev 2023; Syrop 1975), resulting in errors during cell division. This frequently leads to cells with an abnormal number of nucleoli and nuclei or even genome polyploidization (Matuyama and Misawa 1962; Matsuyama and Misawa 1966b). Hypertrophied cells display enlarged vacuoles that occupy most of the cell volume, pushing organelles to the periphery—in such a way that the cell might appear empty (Giordani et al. 2013; Knowles 1887; Lili et al. 1993; Piperkova and Vassilev 2023; Semerdjieva et al. 2014). These cells also vary in size and shape, and thickening of cell walls is commonly observed (Giordani et al. 2013; Lili et al. 1993; Matuyama and Misawa 1962; Piperkova and Vassilev 2023; Syrop 1975). Thickened cell walls, combined with the invasion of mycelium into the middle lamella, lead to a marked reduction in intercellular spaces (Knowles 1887; Giordani et al. 2013; Piperkova and Vassilev 2023; Semerdjieva et al. 2014; Schneider and Dargent 1977). The characteristic leaf symptoms are in turn caused by the mechanical tension exerted by the swollen mesophyll on unaltered turgescent vascular structures (Knowles 1887). At later stages of infection, particularly during spore release by asci, impacted tissues become necrotic (Marte and Gargiulo 1972). Of important note is the limited deformation of leaves when infected at the mature stage, which seems to be related to their ability to induce rapid cell suberization fortifying the leaf and apoptosis causing necrotic spots, which contributes to the containment of symptoms (Matuyama and Misawa 1962).

A Cascade of Metabolic Dysfunctions Involving Host Manipulation by T. deformans

First, T. deformans infection appears to induce profound metabolic changes related to organelle dysfunction in peach leaves. Anthocyanin accumulation, driven by T. deformans‐released regulatory molecules, causes the reddish colouration in infected leaves (Matuyama and Misawa 1962; Moscatello et al. 2017; Syrop 1975). Despite the absence of chlorophyll‐degrading enzymes in T. deformans—such as chlorophyllase—(Montalbini and Buonaurio 1986) infected leaves exhibit a marked degradation of chloroplasts, particularly due to the inhibition of chlorophyll pigment biosynthesis (Marte and Gargiulo 1972; Nicolae and Mitrea 2009; Raggi 1994). Furthermore, rapid and extensive cell division leads to organelle depletion like chloroplasts (Koleva‐Valkova et al. 2017; Montalbini and Buonaurio 1986; Nicolae and Buse‐Dragomir 2012; Semerdjieva et al. 2014; Syrop 1975). Thus, photosynthetic capacity is significantly reduced (Goldy et al. 2017; Moscatello et al. 2017; Nicolae and Mitrea 2009; Nicolae and Buse‐Dragomir 2012; Novello et al. 2024; Riczu et al. 2012; Semerdjieva et al. 2014). The degradation of mitochondria, coupled with alterations in molecular compounds, results in the complete deregulation of cellular respiration (Marte and Gargiulo 1972; Nicolae and Buse‐Dragomir 2012; Raggi 1994; Semerdjieva et al. 2014). The combination of impaired chlorophyll biosynthesis and accelerated mitochondrial respiration results in elevated CO2 levels in the leaves (Raggi 1994, 1995). Finally, evapotranspiration increases due to abnormal stomatal opening, thereby reducing leaf water content (Ciobanu 2013; Moscatello et al. 2017; Nicolae and Mitrea 2009; Nicolae and Buse‐Dragomir 2012; Raggi 1987).

Second, many studies reported on hormonal imbalances in infected leaves linked to the formation of hyperplastic and hypertrophied cells. Compounds such as indole‐3‐acetic acid (IAA) and other auxins, cytokinins, phytokinin, along with their precursors like tryptophan, are known to regulate normal cell growth and division (Crady and Wolf 1959; Maniatis et al. 2024; Matsuyama and Misawa 1966a; Perley and Stowe 1966; Svetaz et al. 2017; Sziráki et al. 1975; Yamada et al. 1990). However, in infected leaves, an imbalance of these hormones is suspected of stimulating abnormal cell division and growth. The T. deformans genome contains genes for IAA biosynthesis, suggesting a direct role of the fungus in manipulating host metabolism, but so far the very origin of these hormones—whether fungal‐ or plant‐derived or both, remains unclear (Svetaz et al. 2017; Novello et al. 2024).

Last, infected leaves appear to divert a significant proportion of tree nutrients to sustain fungal growth. Indeed, studies have highlighted marked increases in sugar composition, including simple sugars such as glucose, fructose, galactose and rhamnose, as well as complex sugars like sucrose, raffinose, and its precursor, galactinol. These metabolites are essential for fungal cell wall formation and T. deformans can use them directly as a source of carbon or energy. Additionally, other carbon sources are suspected to be altered, such as glycerol, anthocyanin or malate. Amino acids, peptides and urea have been identified as potential nitrogen sources for the fungus (Goldy et al. 2017; Koleva‐Valkova et al. 2017; Maniatis et al. 2024; Svetaz et al. 2017).

Plant Defence Responses Against T. deformans Infection

At every stage of infection, basal host response mechanisms allow the tree to deploy a broad‐spectrum defence arsenal that can limit fungal infection to a certain extent (Butassi et al. 2022). Both yeast and mycelium forms of T. deformans are recognized via pattern recognition receptors, triggering responses adapted to each fungal form (Maniatis et al. 2024; Svetaz et al. 2017). Upon fungal contact, host cells rapidly modify their walls through the biosynthesis of cutin, suberin and waxes, creating a physical barrier that may limit fungal penetration (Maniatis et al. 2024). However, in susceptible plants, these structural defences do not prevent T. deformans infection of leaf tissues, allowing hyphae to penetrate the intercellular spaces (Maniatis et al. 2024). During this process, the host continuously activates multiple defence pathways, mostly related to stress response and fungal infection. These include the shikimic acid, phenylpropanoid, mevalonate, jasmonic acid and ethylene pathways (Butassi et al. 2022; Goldy et al. 2017; Maniatis et al. 2024; Novello et al. 2024; Svetaz et al. 2017). Additionally, both primary and secondary metabolites (proline, chlorogenic acid, linolenic acid, spermidine, carotenoid, anthocyanin, benzoic acid) or enzymes (guaiacol peroxidase, syringaldazine peroxidase) are also mobilized (Ciobanu 2008; Goldy et al. 2017; Koleva‐Valkova et al. 2017; Maniatis et al. 2024; Svetaz et al. 2017). Pathogenesis‐related (PR) genes are prominently expressed during infection, including PR2 (β‐1,3‐glucanases), PR3 (chitinases‐like proteins), PR5 (thaumatin‐like proteins) and PR10 (ribonuclease‐like proteins), which participate in antifungal defence, RNA degradation and signalling (Butassi et al. 2022; Goldy et al. 2017; Maniatis et al. 2024; Novello et al. 2024; Svetaz et al. 2017). PR10 contributes directly to defence pathways by regulating jasmonic acid (JA) biosynthesis (Maniatis et al. 2024).

Among this body of studies, some were conducted in parallel on a resistant variety and highlighted specific defences mechanisms allowing for efficient plant protection. To our knowledge, only three studies on peach leaf curl genetic resistance have been carried out so far (Butassi et al. 2022; Goldy et al. 2017; Svetaz et al. 2017). They all rely on the progeny DOFI‐84.364.060 originating from an Italian breeding programme (Bellini et al. 2002). First, resistant genotypes appear to inhibit spore germination via the expression of PR12 gene. Fungal hyphae spread is further restricted by the activation of specific genes including defensin, PR4, PR6, PR17 and snakin‐1/‐2 genes (Butassi et al. 2022). Because fungal hyphae are still detectable in leaves, this defence mechanism seems to be only partial (Svetaz et al. 2017). Additionally, several molecules are suspected to contribute to resistance in this genetic background, such as β‐alanine, putrescine and trehalose (Goldy et al. 2017). Although certain molecules, such as thaumatin (PR5 genes) and JA, are found in both resistant and susceptible genotypes, their role appears particularly relevant in studies focusing on resistant genotypes (Butassi et al. 2022; Maniatis et al. 2024). Last, the activation of the salicylic acid (SA) pathway in resistant trees may reduce susceptibility to peach leaf curl by triggering defence responses and repressing auxin signalling, thus reducing erratic cell divisions (Svetaz et al. 2017). Conducting similar studies on other resistant varieties could further validate the role of these defence factors, and specify whether they are generic or variety‐specific.

Fungal Mechanisms of Infection

Taphrina deformans leaf infection begins with the switch from yeast form to filamentous, which seems to be triggered by complex molecular pathways. Several amino acids known for their key roles in fungal dimorphism are detected during early infection. For example, asparagine levels increase in the presence of T. deformans spores and the concentration of ornithine decreases during early infection (Maniatis et al. 2024; Novello et al. 2024; Svetaz et al. 2017). Moreover, T. deformans might induce the upregulation of plant susceptibility genes, facilitating leaf infection (Maniatis et al. 2024).

Once T. deformans has penetrated inside the leaves, hyphae spread in the intercellular space through a combination of mechanical pressure and the secretion of plant cell wall‐degrading enzymes, such as cellulases (Maniatis et al. 2024; Piperkova and Vassilev 2023; Bassi et al. 1984). The genome of T. deformans encodes numerous pathogenesis‐related genes, such as proteases that degrade plant tissues (Koleva‐Valkova et al. 2017; Schneider and Dargent 1977), but no lignin‐degrading enzymes so that T. deformans cannot penetrate inside plant cells (Cissé et al. 2013; Piperkova and Vassilev 2023). In fact, mycelium manipulates host metabolism cells externally, redirecting essential resources for its own growth towards the intercellular space. In turn, the strategy of T. deformans has been compared to that of haustoria (Maniatis et al. 2024; Svetaz et al. 2017; Syrop 1975) although no such specialized fungal structure has been reported (Knowles 1887; Mix 1949). The T. deformans genome contains genes encoding enzymes for the degradation of primary compounds, including urease, asparaginase and raffinose catabolism, along with transporters to redirect resources like sugars, amino acids and ions like calcium (Goldy et al. 2017; Maniatis et al. 2024; Svetaz et al. 2017).

Genomic Advances Promoting Knowledge on Interactions Between T. deformans and P. persica

A major advance in the study of peach leaf curl disease came with the sequencing of the T. deformans genome in 2013 (Cissé et al. 2013). The genome, consisting of four chromosome pairs with a total length of 13.3 Mb, provided unprecedented insights into the genetic basis of plant–host interactions between P. persica and T. deformans (Cissé et al. 2013; Martin 1940; Mix 1949). This landmark enabled the identification of thousands of genes involved in nutrient acquisition, hormone modulation, cell wall degradation and pathogenicity (Cissé et al. 2013). Importantly, it also represents the first genome sequenced within the Taphrina genus, which facilitated the development of DNA‐based molecular tools for the whole Taphrina genus (Christita et al. 2023). Following the initial sequencing, three additional T. deformans isolates have been sequenced and are available in NCBI database (Tsai et al. 2014). Functional genomics approaches, including transcriptomics, proteomics and metabolomics, can now be integrated with genome data to elucidate the specific pathways involved in T. deformans infection and host response.

To conclude this section, it appears that molecular compounds and pathways potentially involved in the complex interaction between T. deformans and P. persica have been suggested by several recent proteomic, metabolomic or transcriptomic studies. However, convergence of clues is missing among these relatively few studies, and the lack of an efficient inoculation protocol still impedes the molecular validation of candidate molecules in host–pathogen interactions. To date, only one study has attempted to validate the antifungal properties of chlorogenic acid on peach leaf curl infection during spore budding and did not address infection or mycelium growth stages (Goldy et al. 2017). Altogether, the robustness of hypotheses on molecular mechanisms is thus limited and deserves further experimental evidence.

In general, Taphrina and plant host interactions remain poorly characterized, with most studies being centred on the T. deformans– P. persica pathosystem. However, the discovery of a Taphrina strain infecting Arabidopsis thaliana (Auzane et al. 2025) may be an opportunity to accelerate the elucidation of infection mechanisms of Taphrina species.

Managing Peach Leaf Curl in Orchard: Epidemiology, Risk Prediction and Control

Fully understanding the factors influencing the infection cycle of T. deformans on peach (Figure 1) is crucial for improving disease management strategies. Despite the fact that current knowledge remains limited, a first generation of epidemiological models for risk prediction has been produced by a few research teams, providing a foundation for developing more accurate models.

Understanding the Epidemiology of T. deformans: From Infection to Dispersal

Infection: Triggered During a Specific Vulnerability Window

Infection of peach by T. deformans occurs during a specific time window when host susceptibility coincides with favourable environmental conditions. Susceptible stages start at bud break (Giosuè et al. 2000; Mix 1935; Pierstorff 1935), and at petal fall (Rossi et al. 2007a), respectively, for leaf and fruit infection. The susceptibility period lasts about 6 weeks (Giosuè et al. 2000; Safran and Levy 1995) or until the temperature exceeds 16°C (Rossi et al. 2007a). One study suggested that infection occurs when spores come into contact with undifferentiated leaf tissues, requiring further confirmation (Lorenz 1976).

Globally, environmental factors triggering T. deformans infection remain poorly understood, mostly because the few available results are produced in a unique project and remain to be fully validated across studies. It has been proposed that T. deformans infection is favoured by temperatures below 16°C, rainfall exceeding 3 mm and a wetness period of at least 12.5 h (Martin 1940; Rossi et al. 2006). Lower temperatures during wetness periods also tend to increase symptom severity (Rossi et al. 2006). Conditions for fruit infection are more restrictive and less well documented, requiring temperatures below 17°C, rainfall greater than 12 mm, and a wetness period of at least 24 h (Rossi et al. 2007a). While rain was long considered one of the primary triggers for the onset of peach leaf curl (Pierstorff 1935), recent findings suggest that prolonged leaf wetness plays a more critical role than rainfall itself. Indeed, high humidity alone can be sufficient to initiate infection in the absence of rain (Rossi et al. 2006).

Asymptomatic Incubation: Waiting for the First Signs of Disease

The interval between the initial infection and the appearance of the first symptoms in the orchard is known as the incubation period, during which the mycelium proliferates within the intercellular spaces of the leaves. The meteorological factors governing hyphal growth in leaf tissues remain poorly characterized. During this phase, the reported incubation temperatures leading to the development of peach leaf curl symptoms usually range from 3°C to 19°C, but it seems that incubation temperature per se has no impact on symptom severity (Rossi et al. 2006, 2007a). B temperature affects the length of the incubation period, a degree‐day accumulation threshold was suggested as determining the precise timing of symptom apparition (Rossi et al. 2006). For fruit infections, this thermal requirement is estimated between 240 and 290 degree‐days, corresponding to approximately 3 weeks under spring conditions (Rossi et al. 2007a). Comparable values have also been reported for leaf infections (Rossi et al. 2006).

Sporulation: The Role of Climate Still Unclear

In orchards, asci form about 5–10 weeks after leaf infection (Rossi et al. 2007b), which corresponds roughly to 715 degree days. From mid‐spring onwards, they begin releasing ascospores for a brief window of 9–15 days (Rossi et al. 2007b), as the ascus apex ruptures (Martin 1940; Pierce 1900). This release is far from random because it depends on a precise set of environmental cues (Syrop and Beckett 1976). Laboratory studies showed that sporulation is most efficient at 10°C, while in orchard conditions, temperatures between 10°C and 20°C prevail (Rossi et al. 2007b; Rossi and Languasco 2007). High humidity is also essential: ascospore production requires relative humidity above 80% or leaf wetness lasting longer than 8 h, though rainfall itself does not seem to be necessary (Rossi et al. 2007b). Some observations even suggest that ascospore production may follow a diurnal cycle (Yarwood 1941), but this intriguing possibility remains controversial (Syrop and Beckett 1976; Martin 1940) and still awaits confirmation.

The Asexual Multiplication: Spore Budding for Dispersal

Several studies have explored the environmental conditions favouring ascospore and blastospore budding, the asexual multiplication process (see Figure 1, ‘asexual reproduction’). For ascospores, the optimal temperature for budding is around 14°C (Rossi and Languasco 2007), while blastospores prefer slightly warmer conditions near 20°C (Kardam, Gupta, Shukla, and Sharma 2022; Lorenz 1976; Rossi and Languasco 2007; Thomidis and Paresidou 2023). In general, spores are robust and can initiate budding under both dry and wet conditions (Rossi and Languasco 2007), although a relative humidity above 95% seems to ensure optimal growth (Lorenz 1976). Furthermore, spores may be capable of initiating budding without external nutrient sources for a period of up to 12 h (Rossi and Languasco 2007). In early spring, ascospores dominate due to their recent release from asci. As the season progresses into summer, blastospores become increasingly prevalent through repeated budding cycles (Rossi et al. 2007b). By the following year, blastopores are considered the primary agents responsible for leaf infection, outnumbering ascospores (Mix 1949; Valleau 1940).

The Dispersal Phase: A Secret Phase Still Uncovered

Buds and bark are thought to serve as the primary sites for T. deformans spores dormancy (Buck et al. 1998; Mix 1949; Tavares et al. 2004). However, given the remarkable resistance of spores to extreme temperatures and desiccation (Fitzpatrick 1934), some authors have hypothesized the existence of additional long‐term reservoirs (Mix 1949). Yet, no recent work has investigated other potential sources of inoculum in orchards, such as soil, irrigation systems, alternative host plants, or orchard infrastructure.

To date, the mechanisms by which T. deformans spreads between trees or orchards remain unknown. Although T. deformans is considered part of the natural mycoflora of peach bark (Buck et al. 1998), it is still unclear how its spores reach young peach trees or what vectors might be involved in their dissemination. Could wind, rain, grafting or other means play a role? Interestingly, T. deformans spores have been detected on PVC structures outside orchard areas (Webb et al. 2000) and overall, no stronger concentration effect is observed in the orchards, suggesting that dispersal can occur independently of direct tree‐to‐tree contact. Rainfall events play a crucial role in dispersing spores throughout the orchard, helping maintain this cycle year after year. Only one study has addressed the annual quantity and seasonal dynamics of T. deformans spores, but it relied on visual identification, which is prone to error due to the difficulty of distinguishing T. deformans spores (Rossi et al. 2007b). Modern molecular tools now enable precise monitoring of spore abundance and distribution throughout the year, providing a critical view to elusive conclusions and clearer insights into the dispersal pathways of T. deformans.

Overall, a better understanding of dispersal pathways is needed to improve disease management, allow for better timing of treatments to reduce spore loads, prevent initial contamination, and limit further spread.

Infection Risk Models: Still a Long Way to Build a Reliable Decision Support Tool for Peach Leaf Curl Disease

The link between specific environmental conditions and the onset of peach leaf curl infection has been widely accepted (Mix 1935; Pierstorff 1935) and is now well established, particularly regarding temperature (Rossi et al. 2006) and wetness duration (Rossi et al. 2006, 2007a). Building on this knowledge, models to predict a risk index for peach leaf curl occurrence in orchards have been designed under the initiative of several teams in the Mediterranean basin. These models hold significant promise for optimizing treatment strategies and their improvement will allow for their practical application in peach orchards.

An initial, simple model was developed about 30 years ago in Israel, incorporating tree phenology, rainfall and temperature (Safran and Levy 1995). This model was refined after 5 years by adding environmental thresholds and validated using data from Italian orchards (Giosuè et al. 2000). The most recent version from 2010 integrates historical weather data, replaces rainfall with leaf wetness as a more accurate predictor, and calculates infection risk on an hourly basis. This model has been validated using observations from Greek orchards but needs further adjustments to be applied in the peach production context (Thomidis et al. 2010).

A second model was developed using a different approach, focusing on the conditions required for spore budding, namely temperature and wetness (Thomidis and Paresidou 2023). However, because the biological link between spore budding and the onset of infection remains unclear, this model seems less adapted for predicting disease risk in field conditions.

Model improvement is currently impaired by a lack of clear understanding of plant–host interactions, especially on within‐host infection processes. Additionally, current models overlook the varietal diversity in terms of disease susceptibility levels and mechanisms, despite its critical role in shaping epidemic outcomes. Predictive power remains limited because host genetics directly influence infection success and disease progression. As originally considered in the first version of the Safran and Levy model (Safran and Levy 1995), it would be useful to reintroduce parameters related to varietal susceptibility to leaf curl for risk prediction. Environmental data used in these models also remain a challenge: while temperature and rainfall are commonly included, finer‐scale parameters like leaf wetness duration and microclimates—both crucial for spore germination and infection—are rarely available at a fine scale. Without high‐resolution, spatially explicit data, models struggle to capture the very complexity of pathogen–host–environment interactions.

In summary, the path towards a robust risk model requires addressing these gaps by integrating mechanistic within‐host processes, accounting for cultivar‐specific susceptibility and refining environmental data resolution. Mechanistic models for predicting plant disease risk are often limited by incomplete knowledge of infection processes. Deep learning offers a promising alternative, using complex data patterns to forecast risks even when biological mechanisms are unclear (González‐Domínguez et al. 2023). While deep learning complements mechanistic approaches, challenges like data availability and model interpretability might remain. Combining both methods could lead to more reliable and flexible risk prediction tools.

Optimizing Treatment Strategies in Peach Orchards: Challenges and Alternatives

The most widely used measures against peach leaf curl have remained largely unchanged since the last century, which is why efficacy experiments are often outdated. However, numerous methods and products continue to be tested in the pursuit of more effective, cost‐efficient and environmentally sustainable solutions.

Once the mycelium of T. deformans has penetrated leaf tissue, no curative treatment can halt fungal spread (Fuller 1910). Preventive measures, targeting spores before infection, remain the only effective option (Fuller 1910) although calcium polysulphide could be used as a last resort to stop the ongoing infection just after a rain (Guiraud 2025). Without reliable epidemiological models or curative solutions, growers depend on systematic preventive applications (Pierstorff 1935), typically applied during the dormant period before bud break (Fuller 1910; Pierce 1900) and this until leaf maturity or until the end of favourable weather to infection in case of the worst years (V. Gallia, SudExpé France, personal communication). While one or two well‐timed treatments are theoretically sufficient to prevent infection by T. deformans (Kardam, Gupta, Shukla, Sharma, and Kumar 2022), they are insufficient to ensure orchard‐level protection, and field practices typically involve additional applications. When properly managed, chemical treatments can reduce peach leaf curl incidence by 95%–98% (Fuller 1910), but the core challenge lies in their accurate timing.

Copper‐based products, including Bordeaux mixture and other formulations, have long been the backbone of preventive control in both conventional and organic orchards (Burgaud and Charmet 1960; Fuller 1910; Hofmann 1970; Hopperstead and Kadow 1940; Kapur 1946; Long 1930; Maloy and Byther 1979; Turri 1960). While some strains of T. deformans may exhibit reduced copper susceptibility (Cheah et al. 1993), further studies are needed to confirm resistance patterns. To face the main issue of copper accumulation in soil, the European Union has reduced the annual input limitation from 6 kg/ha to 4 kg/ha in 2019 (Katsoulas et al. 2020) and is planning for a complete ban of copper‐based products in the coming years (European Commission 2025). Moreover, in France in 2025 several copper‐based products have faced restrictions on their terms of use, or have even been prohibited (ANSES 2025). These restrictions impose a sword of Damocles over European peach production as long as copper remains a fundamental treatment against peach leaf curl. To mitigate a potential resistance bypass, the risk of phytotoxicity on plant and avoid soil copper accumulation, diversifying chemical modes of action is strongly recommended. Other synthetic fungicides—such as ziram, dodine, captan, thiram, pyraclostrobin, boscalid, carbendazim and mancozeb—are more effective than copper to avoid infections but their deployment is also subject to regulatory authorizations (ephy.anses.fr, Karkach et al. 2025; Brunelli et al. 2010; Burchill et al. 1976; Burgaud and Charmet 1960). Mineral‐based coatings are used as effective preventive treatments, acting as a physical barrier against peach leaf curl, but their use in orchards entails significant practical constraints (Guiraud 2025).

The dynamics of inoculum accumulation over successive years also warrant greater attention to improve management practices against leaf curl. While some authors recommend removing and destroying symptomatic leaves (rather than composting) to reduce the inoculum (Maloy and Byther 1979; Pierce 1900), the efficacy of this practice lacks robust documentation. Although it seems efficient, this prophylactic approach is not applicable to mature orchards and is economically viable only in young orchards with localized peach leaf curl outbreaks (V. Gallia, SudExpé France, personal communication).

A deeper understanding of T. deformans and its interactions with the host would facilitate the development of highly specific treatments for peach leaf curl, offering efficient alternatives to current broad‐spectrum fungicides with significant environmental impact. In this way, a recent proteomic study has identified glutamate–cysteine ligase as a promising target for such development, providing a potential avenue to limit T. deformans infection (Ahmad et al. 2024).

Regarding alternative methods of control, a recent review highlights promising non‐chemical approaches to prevent peach leaf curl infections (Akbar 2023). While the potential of plant extracts and nanoparticles against T. deformans remains poorly documented (Akbar 2023), the effect of algae and seaweed extracts to protect orchards against peach leaf curl disease seems to be promising (Cmikova et al. 2024). Biological control agents, such as Bacillus subtilis (Inam Ul Haq et al. 2020), Trichoderma viride, Trichoderma harzianum, Pseudomonas fluorescens , and combinations thereof (Kardam, Gupta, Shukla, Sagar, and Kashyap 2022; Leonov 2010), have also shown encouraging results. Additionally, plant immunity inducers, used alone or combined with copper (Kavroumatzi et al. 2024) and plant growth regulators (Mikhailova et al. 2020), offer a sustainable path by potentially halving chemical inputs (Karpun et al. 2016). Overall, a diverse panel of alternatives to copper has been identified and is currently subject to further investigations to fully validate their practical use in production orchards.

Breeding for Peach Leaf Curl Resistance: Progress, Gaps and Promising Avenues

Unravelling the Genetic Basis of Peach Leaf Curl Resistance: Current Knowledge and Future Directions

So far, low‐input systems have shown limited effectiveness in reducing the impact of T. deformans in peach orchards. For this reason, peach tree breeding remains a fundamental pilar for managing this disease, through the selection of varieties resistant to T. deformans (Plénet et al. 2023). Over the years, numerous research institutes have studied the susceptibility and resistance of peach varieties to T. deformans infections, providing an overview of the most promising candidates (Table S1). However, it is important to note that no peach variety is fully resistant to peach leaf curl (Roselli et al. 1997). While some varieties exhibit lower susceptibility, it is now long known that resistance to this pathogen is polygenic, meaning it is controlled by multiple genes with quantitative effects rather than by a single major resistance gene (Giordani et al. 2022; Monet 1985; Roselli et al. 1997; Viruel et al. 1998). To date, only one locus of interest has been identified in peach on chromosome 4 through a genome‐wide association study (Marie Serrie et al. 2025). To further elucidate the genetic basis of peach leaf curl resistance, two key research directions could be pursued. First, analysing the pedigree of resistant peach varieties to determine whether they share common resistance factors. Second, expanding linkage and association analyses to identify additional loci involved in partial resistance. Understanding the diversity of resistance factors would not only improve our knowledge of genetic resistance mechanisms but also enable gene pyramiding, that is, the stacking of multiple partial resistance, to enhance both the level and durability of leaf curl resistance in commercial varieties.

Peach leaf curl resistance seems to be more commonly found outside the elite gene pool of peach. Indeed, wild relatives, such as P. mira, P. davidiana and P. kansuensis , have shown higher resistance than peach trees (Roselli et al. 1997; Komar‐Tyomnaya 2015). In a biparental study, two resistance QTLs were detected on chromosomes 3 and 6 from P. davidiana (Viruel et al. 1998). Additionally, almond varieties ( P. dulcis ) are known to be more resistant than peach trees (Gradziel 2002; Kavak 2005), with some partially resistant varieties already identified (Kavak 2005). The high sensitivity of modern cultivars to the disease might stem from decades of peach breeding focused primarily on fruit quality, yield and agronomic traits under complete phytosanitary protection, overlooking the inheritance of potential disease resistances (Roselli et al. 1997).

Despite the potential of genetic resistance, few breeding programmes have specifically targeted peach leaf curl. Notable exceptions include a programme in Italy and the creation of the Rebolla 1 variety, which limits symptom spread and promotes new shoot growth. This variety resulted from a long‐term breeding programme using the Cesarini cultivar as the initial parent (Bellini et al. 1996, 2002; Nencetti et al. 2015). In Bulgaria, the Evmolpiya variety was developed by incorporating resistance from P. davidiana . In Russia, several varieties have been developed through crossbreeding with related wild species in order to select for resistance to peach leaf curl (Komar‐Tyomnaya 2015). To further enhance the development of resistant varieties, research should contribute to (1) identifying and conserving resistant parents, (2) identifying genes underlying resistance, and (3) developing protocols for the early selection of resistance in breeding programmes (marker‐assisted selection or controlled infection).

Phenotyping

Phenotyping disease susceptibility in plants relies either on natural infections or on inoculations. While combining plant inoculation with controlled environments is theoretically ideal for overcoming the uncertainties of field weather, inoculation of peach with T. deformans in climatic chambers remains poorly mastered. Although spore cultivation and reproduction on artificial media are well documented (Martin 1940; Mix 1949), the critical challenge lies in controlling the environmental parameters required to trigger the dimorphic switch between spore and mycelium phases that is essential for infection. While a few studies have explored environmental conditions for peach leaf curl, none have managed to put these findings into practice with successful controlled inoculations. Attempts using detached inoculated leaves (Svetaz et al. 2017) have proven reproducible for large‐scale breeding screening. In the absence of a robust controlled inoculation protocol, some researchers have turned to inoculation in the field to ensure the presence of the pathogen, yet with strong reliance on weather conditions to trigger leaf infection (Mix 1935; Tate et al. 1991).

Scoring disease symptoms under natural infection in the field remains the most common phenotyping method. However, this approach introduces significant variability because climatic conditions are not always conducive to infection during the susceptibility stage. In particular, some susceptible trees might appear as asymptomatic as a result of phenoclimatic escape. This can lead to incorrect interpretations regarding the occurrence and severity of symptoms, and to a wrong assignment of varieties in final susceptibility rankings. To mitigate these issues, multi‐site and multi‐year observations have been explored to quantify variety susceptibility (Hilaire et al. 2021) or identify resistance sources (Serrie et al. 2023). Collaborative efforts, such as the PeachRefPop project (Cirilli et al. 2020), are essential to phenotype across diverse production areas affected by peach leaf curl.

A key challenge with natural infection by T. deformans is orchard decay over time, impairing the experimental design and statistical analyses. Indeed, repeated infections weaken and eventually kill trees. In the absence of fungicides, prophylaxis fails to control peach leaf curl disease, allowing inoculum build‐up, while other fungal diseases further stress the trees and can amplify the problem. This extreme experimental setup can be difficult to justify given the cost and efforts needed for orchard establishment and maintenance.

Finally, we provide a critical view on visual symptom assessment. To capture the full dynamics of disease expression, kinetic observations tracking symptom evolution over time (Marie Serrie et al. 2025) should be preferred over the classical scoring at peak symptom severity. To save time, gland traits (e.g., glandless or reniform glands) have been proposed as resistance markers (Bellini et al. 1996; Fideghelli et al. 1983), though their correlation with resistance remains debated (Roselli et al. 1997; Scorza 1992). To improve accuracy and resolution in time, high‐throughput phenotyping tools are gaining interest. Because peach leaf curl symptoms have a very specific appearance characterized by distorted and coloured leaves, digital phenotyping might be developed without major difficulty. For example, image analysis with machine learning approaches is promising to detect peach leaf curl symptoms (Zúñiga Castro et al. 2025). Similarly, near‐infrared spectroscopy has been used to quantify chlorophyll in infected leaves (Riczu et al. 2012), demonstrating its potential for rapid, non‐destructive phenotyping. Other digital phenotyping tools that have proven effective for plant disease phenotyping could be tested on T. deformans infected orchards, such as hyperspectral sensors (Mahlein et al. 2019) or thermal imagery (Messina and Modica 2020).

Addressing these phenotyping challenges—through controlled inoculation refinement, collaborative multi‐site trials, and high‐throughput technologies—will be key to unlocking the genetic potential of peach varieties and developing durable resistance against T. deformans.

Conclusion

This review synthesizes insights from over 169 scientific publications on peach leaf curl ranging for year 1811–2025, revealing an extensive but fragmented research landscape. The main findings of this review are summarized in Table 1. Early studies on peach leaf curl focused on identifying the taxonomy of T. deformans and for many years, the disease was primarily studied through a mycological lens to describe fungal features (Figure 3). Numerous papers have also been published on orchard management strategies to combat peach leaf curl, reflecting a sustained effort to innovate in controlling this fungal disease. Only more recently has research addressed host–fungus interactions, variety screening and breeding programmes for resistant peaches, as well as phylogeny and epidemiology. However, most advances remain confined to specific, often isolated approaches, without cross‐study comparisons to validate findings. Much of our current knowledge—particularly regarding pathogen life cycle—still dates back to the early 20th century (Mix 1935; Pierce 1900) and warrants reassessment using modern methods. Research also lacks continuity, as many teams have contributed only one or two papers before shifting focus. Notable exceptions include work by Mix on the Taphrina genus, research by Syrop on almond leaf curl, the Italian team led by Rossi on climatic triggers of infection, as well landmark contributions such as the complete genome sequencing of T. deformans by Cissé et al. (2013) and the Argentinian team on T. deformans and P. persica interactions (Butassi et al. 2022; Goldy et al. 2017; Novello et al. 2024; Svetaz et al. 2017). Overview of current scientific knowledge on peach leaf curl disease.

This corpus of scientific papers reveals significant gaps in peach leaf curl knowledge that are critical to meeting the needs of the peach sector (Figure 4). Firstly, aside from microscopic observations, the lack of in‐depth T. deformans characterization is striking, which hampers our ability to tailor control strategies to specific contexts. Despite their importance for understanding the variability of infections across regions, climate and peach varieties, several research areas on T. deformans remain entirely unexplored. We lack in particular robust results on pathogen worldwide distribution, spore dispersal, strain diversity and virulence, as well as on the diversity of peach leaf curl symptoms. Secondly, while numerous studies have evaluated peach varieties and identified resistant ones, few have successfully identified resistant genes/QTLs and to our knowledge no breeding programme has allowed for vast plantations resistant varieties. Last, but certainly not least, the most critical knowledge gap concerns the weather conditions triggering infections. Leveraging this knowledge could help farmers to optimise their treatments, as illustrated in the well‐characterised pathosystem of apple scab, for which decision‐making tools are already in use (e.g., RIMPRO, https://rimpro.cloud/fr/platform/apple‐scab‐venturia‐inaequalis/).

To address these gaps, we identified several key tools and methods requiring research and development reinforcement: (i) a spore trap to allow epidemiological studies; (ii) high‐throughput phenotyping pipelines; and (iii) robust controlled inoculation protocols. Such protocols would not only allow to screen peach varieties independently from orchard constraints but also to characterize fungal strains diversity and host response thereof and to fully elucidate the triggers of plant infection. Ultimately, while existing knowledge provides a solid foundation, updating and deepening our understanding is crucial. Integrating traditional knowledge with modern approaches will enable the transformation of peach leaf curl management for an effective reduction of fungicide applications, ultimately paving the way towards more sustainable peach production.
