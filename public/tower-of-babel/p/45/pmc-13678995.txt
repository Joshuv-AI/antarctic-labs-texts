Development and validation of a risk prediction model for preterm birth in women concurrent with Hepatitis B virus carrier status and gestational diabetes mellitus
Authors: Zaidi Gui, Xiaoxiao Fan, Tianzi Zang, Hongyan Xu, Qi Wu, Lijun Chen, Jinbing Bai, Yanqun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreterm birth is a major adverse neonatal outcome that has been associated with maternal hepatitis B virus carrier status. Co-occurrence of hepatitis B virus carrier status carrier status and gestational diabetes mellitus is associated with an increased risk of preterm birth compared with hepatitis B virus carrier status alone. This study aimed to develop and externally validate a clinically applicable multivariable prediction model incorporating both clinical risk factors and maternal blood biomarkers to estimate preterm birth risk among women with concurrent hepatitis B virus carrier status and gestational diabetes mellitus.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed using clinical data and maternal blood biomarkers from 882 pregnant women with hepatitis B virus carrier status and gestational diabetes mellitus who delivered at Women’s Hospital, in Zhejiang Province, China, from January 2015 to December 2024. Following data preprocessing, participants were randomly allocated to training and validation sets at a 7:3 ratio. Variable selection was performed using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), and univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were subsequently conducted to assess the associations of clinical variables and maternal blood biomarkers with preterm birth in women with concurrent hepatitis B virus carrier status and gestational diabetes mellitus. A nomogram was developed to facilitate clinical application. Model discrimination and calibration were evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis and calibration plots, respectively, while clinical utility was assessed through decision curve analysis.ResultsA total of 847 women with hepatitis B virus carrier status and gestational diabetes mellitus were included in this study, of whom 187 (22%) experienced preterm birth. Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified pre-pregnancy body mass index, diastolic blood pressure, systolic blood pressure, alanine aminotransferase, placenta previa, white blood cell count, and maternal blood biomarkers—including total protein, total bile acid, total thyroxine, mode of membrane rupture, and prothrombin time—as independent predictors of preterm birth. These variables were incorporated into a nomogram, which demonstrated good calibration and discrimination. The area under the curve was 0.854 (95% CI: 0.823–0.881) in the training set and 0.754 (95% CI: 0.697–0.806) in the validation set. Decision curve analysis indicated that the nomogram provided favorable clinical utility for predicting preterm birth.ConclusionThe model demonstrated good discriminative ability and calibration, enabling accurate prediction of preterm birth risk in women with concurrent hepatitis B virus carrier status and gestational diabetes mellitus.Keywords: Hepatitis B Virus, Nomogram, Gestational diabetes mellitus, Prediction model, Preterm birth, Machine learning, Risk factors

Introduction

Preterm birth (PB) is defined as delivery occurring before 37 weeks of gestation, with a global prevalence of 9.9% [1]. Complications related to PB are a leading cause of death among children under five years old [2]. In addition, PB is associated with a higher risk of developing various complications between the ages of 10 and 18 years [3]. Machine learning models used to predict preterm birth have the potential to improve prenatal care and support clinical decision-making [4]. Therefore, identifying risk factors for PB is crucial for effectively preventing and reducing PB rates.

Predictive models developed and validated for healthy pregnant women or those with PB complicated by a single condition have been extensively studied based on clinical data and biomarkers; However, there are very limited studies focusing on women with concurrent hepatitis B virus (HBV) carrier status and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) [5–7]. HBV carrier status represents a significant public health issue today. In China, the prevalence of HBV carrier status among pregnant women is 6.27% [8]. Compared with other complications or normal pregnancies, the incidence of PB is significantly higher among women who are carriers of HBV [5, 9]. Previous studies have shown that HBV carriers can induce placental inflammation through maternal blood [10], and, inflammatory factors stimulating uterine smooth muscle contractions and upregulating the expression of the prostaglandin G/H synthase-2 (PGHS-2) gene, thereby increasing prostaglandin synthesis and inducing uterine contractions, which in turn increases the risk of PB [11]. In addition, research has shown that HBV carriers may trigger systemic inflammation, leading to elevated levels of tumor necrosis factor-α, which in turn impairs pancreatic β-cell function and insulin signaling, resulting in insulin resistance, elevated blood glucose levels, and an increased risk of GDM [12].

Concurrently, GDM has been identified as a key factor associated with an increased risk of PB [13]. A prospective cohort study of 1,049 pregnant women found that women with GDM had higher levels of inflammatory factors (e.g., IL-1RA, IL-17D, and eotaxin-3) [14], and, abnormalities in lipid metabolism [15]. Lipid metabolites and circulating inflammatory factors further contribute to PB [16]. A seven-year retrospective study conducted in China indicates that pregnancies complicated by both HBV carriers and GDM are associated with PB, with an 3.91% of PB incidence [17]. However, there is currently a lack of studies based on clinical data and maternal blood biomarkers that examine the effects of women with concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM on PB. For women who have concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM, the risk factors for PB remain unclear. Further research on this predictive model is warranted, and efforts should be made to develop and validate a predictive model for PB in women with concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM.

The purposes of this study were to: (1) develop a prediction model based on the clinical data and maternal blood biomarkers for PB in women with concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM; (2) validate the risk prediction model for PB in women with concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM; and (3) assess the predictive performance of the nomogram model in clinical practice. The findings of this study could help clinicians to classify PB in women with concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM into low and high-risk groups, ultimately improving management efficiency and, reducing intervention intensity for the low risk PB group.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective observational study included 882 women with concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM who delivered at the Women’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, in Hangzhou between January 2015 and December 2024. Inclusion criteria were pregnant women diagnosed with concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM at the time of admission. Exclusion criteria were pregnant women with: (1) the incomplete electronic medical records; (2) with human immunodeficiency virus, syphilis, hepatitis A, hepatitis C, and hepatitis D; (3) multiple pregnancy; and4) miscarriage (i.e., pregnancy loss occurring before 20 weeks of gestation) and stillbirth (defined as fetus birth was no signs of life and Apgar scores 1 min and 5 min at 0,). In this study, women with multiple pregnancies (n = 14) or incomplete medical records (n = 21) were excluded, leading to 847 women our analysis (Fig. 1). In machine learning, a common practice is to allocate 70% of the data for model training and 30% for validation, allowing effective model development while maintaining unbiased evaluation of model performance [18]. Accordingly, the study cohort was randomly divided to training (n = 592) and validation sets (n = 255) in a 7:3 ratio. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Women’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (IRB-20230269-R). The study was conducted in compliance with applicable guidelines and regulations.

Data collection

PB was defined as delivery before 37 weeks of gestation. Clinical data and maternal blood biomarkers were collected from electronic medical records (EMR), with PB (gestational age at birth 24–36 weeks) as the primary outcome [22].

In this study, the diagnostic criteria for GDM were based on those established by the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Group (IADPSG) standards. GDM was diagnosed with a 75–g oral glucose tolerance test during 24–28 gestational weeks [23]. GDM is diagnosed if a pregnant woman’s blood glucose reaches any of the following levels: fasting ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, 1–hour ≥ 10.0 mmol/L, or 2–hour ≥ 8.5 mmol/L [24]. In our study, HBV infection was defined as a positive serological test for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg). HBsAg-positive women with a history of HBV infection exceeding 6 months and normal alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels were included.

The following variables were collected as candidate predictors of PB, including maternal demographic characteristics (i.e., age, gravidity, parity, education level, history of adverse pregnancy outcomes, including use of anti–HBV medications during pregnancy (yes/no), and history of hepatitis B infection, systolic (SBP) and diastolic (DBP) blood pressure measured at admission for delivery, Body mass index (BMI, kg/m2)); Maternal pregnancy complications: pregnancy-induced hypertension, anemia in pregnancy, the hypertensive disorders of pregnancy; preeclampsia, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP), and hypothyroidism.

Laboratory parameters of maternal blood included White blood cell (WBC) count (× 109 /L), platelet counts (PLTs), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), hemoglobin, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), Total bile acid (TBA), Total protein, fibrinogen (FIB), prothrombin activity (PA), prothrombin time (PT), thyroid function parameters.

Fetal variables: assisted reproductive reproduction, fetal growth restriction (FGR), mode of premature rupture of membranes (premature rupture of membranes (PROM), spontaneous rupture of membranes (SROM), artificial rupture of membranes (AROM)); placenta previa, placental abruption, intrauterine distress (IUD).

Laboratory data, including OGTT–FBG, OGTT–1 h, OGTT–2 h, serum ferritin, Thyroid function, were collected during prenatal visits at 24–28 weeks of gestation; Complete blood count, liver function, and coagulation parameters obtained closest to the time of delivery were included in the analysis. Laboratory results, prescribed medications, and obstetric records were also extracted from the electronic medical records (EMR). All tests were conducted following standardized protocols and manufacturer guidelines to maintain accuracy and reliability.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 27.0 and R 4.4.3. Descriptive statistics, comparisons between groups, and univariate and multivariate logistic analyses were conducted in SPSS 27, while LASSO logistic regression, nomogram construction, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, the area under the curve (AUC), calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA) were performed in R 4.4.3. The proportion of missing data for the variables included were less than 5%. Missing data were imputed using the mean or median of the observed values, as appropriate based on the variable distribution. The key predictor total thyroxine (TT4) had less than 5% missing data and was imputed using the mean value, while the other risk predictors were routine antenatal examination items without missing values. The complete dataset was randomly divided into training and validation sets in a 7:3 ratio, and baseline characteristics were compared between the two groups. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using the independent-samples t-test. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables are presented as the median and interquartile range (IQR), and were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test.

Univariate analysis was used for screening variables that were notably correlated with dependent variables. LASSO logistic regression was performed on the training set to identify independent predictors and construct a predictive model for PB. The optimal λ was identified via 10-fold cross-validation within the LASSO regression framework, and the variables were identified to contribute the most to the model. Selected variables were included in the multivariable logistic regression and nomogram models.

In this study, the discriminative performance of the model was assessed using the ROC curve and AUC. A random seed (111111) was specified for this study. Calibration curve using a 1,000-fold bootstrap resampling method. Model calibration was evaluated using calibration curves and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test for goodness of fit test. The agreement between the predicted probabilities and observed outcomes was further assessed through calibration curves, while the clinical utility and net benefit of the model were evaluated using DCA. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 847 women were included concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM in this study. Of those, 187 (22%) women with concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM experienced PB, whereas 660 (78%) did not. A total of 847 women with concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM were randomly divided into a train set (n = 592), of whom 125 (21%) experienced PB, and a validation set (n = 255), of whom 62 (24%) experienced PB at a 7:3 ratio. The baseline of the train set and validation set in women with concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM is shown in Table 2. No significant differences were observed in the distributions of most continuous and categorical variables between the groups (all P > 0.05). Therefore, the overall data characteristics between the training and the validation sets are quite similar, thereby providing a stable and reliable foundation for subsequent model development (Table 3).

Risk factors for PB among women with concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM

LASSO regression identified 15 key predictors from a pool of 80 candidate variables, which were then used to build a model using logistic regression. LASSO regression analysis was also used to select predictors of PB (Fig. 2) in the training set. The optimal λ value identified through cross-validation was 0.033, at which the LASSO model selected the following variables with non-zero regression coefficients: variables such as GWG, pre-BMI, SBP, DBP, amniotic membrane rupture method, pre-eclampsia, placenta previa, WBC, total protein, ALT, total bile acid (TBA), OGTT–FBG, TT4, PT, and TT. Univariable analysis was conducted to screen for candidate predictors of preterm delivery among women with concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM between PB and non- PB in the training set, such as GWG, pre-BMI, SBP, DBP, ALT, placenta previa, WBC, TP, TBA, TT4, mode of membrane rupture and PT (Table 4).

Multivariate logistic regression was conducted to identify potential predictors of preterm delivery among women with concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM, comparing PB and non-PB groups in the training set (Table 4). The results indicated that pre-pregnancy BMI (OR = 1.106, 95%CI: 1.025–1.192), diastolic blood pressure (OR = 1.028, 95%CI: 1.012–1.045), SROM (overall P = 0.023) including PROM (OR = 2.977, 95%CI: 1.205–7.352) and AROM (OR = 3.067, 95%CI: 1.357–6.934), placenta previa (OR = 25.838, 95%CI: 6.339-105.316), WBC (OR = 1.195, 95%CI: 1.082–1.321), ALT (OR = 1.009, 95%CI: 1.002–1.016), TBA (OR = 1.047, 95%CI: 1.018–1.077), TT4 (OR = 1.012, 95%CI: 1.000-1.023) and PT (OR = 1.840, 95%CI: 1.172–2.888) were independent risk factors for PB. SBP (OR = 0.982, 95%CI: 0.967–0.996) and Total protein (OR = 0.949, 95%CI: 0.908–0.992) acted as independent protective factors against PB. Although GWG, pre-eclampsia, FBG and TT were correlated with PB in univariate analysis, they showed no statistically significant independent association after multivariate adjustment (P > 0.05). Ultimately, a total of 11 predictors were included in the PB risk prediction model.

Development of the predictive model in the training set using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)

Eighty variables were retrieved from electronic medical records, encompassing demographic characteristics, laboratory parameters at 24–26 weeks of gestation and at admission before delivery, comorbidities, HBV infection history, and anti-HBV therapy. We used LASSO regression analysis to screen variables, effectively avoiding multicollinearity between variables. Based on the findings from LASSO regression and subsequent univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses, A better model fit resulted in a predictive model comprising the following variables: pre-BMI (OR = 1.134, 95% CI: 1.056 ~ 1.217), SBP (OR = 0.986, 95% CI: 0.974 ~ 0.999), DBP (OR = 1.037, 95% CI:1.023 ~ 1.051), PROM (OR = 3.194, 95% CI: 1.325 ~ 7.701), AROM (OR = 3.034, 95% CI:1.378 ~ 6.681), placenta previa (OR = 22.567, 95% CI: 5.682 ~ 89.63), WBC (OR = 1.189, 95% CI: 1.079 ~ 1.31), total protein (OR = 0.940, 95% CI: 0.901 ~ 0.981), ALT (OR = 1.011, 95% CI: 1.004 ~ 1.018), TBA (OR = 1.044, 95% CI: 1.015 ~ 1.073), TT4 (OR = 1.014, 95% CI: 1.003 ~ 1.025), and PT (OR = 1.799, 95% CI: 1.157 ~ 2.798) (Table 5).

Validation of the predictive model

Discrimination

The AUC values were calculated to evaluate the model’s ability to distinguish between women with concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM in terms of predicting spontaneous abortion in both the training and validation sets. As illustrated in Fig. 4A and B, the model achieved AUC of 0.854 (95% CI: 0.823 ~ 0.881, P < 0.001) in the training set and 0.754 (95% CI: 0.697 ~ 0.806, P < 0.001) in the validation set. These findings indicate that the nomogram demonstrates good discriminatory power and predictive accuracy, effectively distinguishing between preterm and full-term deliveries among women with concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM.

Calibration of the prediction model

Model calibration was evaluated using a calibration plot and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, with P > 0.05 indicating an adequate model fit. The results of the test indicated that the model demonstrated good fit in both the training set (χ² = 3.725, P = 0.881) and the validation set (χ² = 14.28, df = 8, P = 0.071). Calibration plots for the training cohort, derived from LASSO regression and subsequent univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses, are presented in Figure. 5 A and 5B. We performed internal validation using a 1,000-fold bootstrap resampling method. The results showed that the calibration curve of the nomogram model closely matched the ideal curve, with Mean Absolute Errors (MAE) of 0.032 and 0.027 for the two datasets, respectively. The calibration curves showed good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities of PB in training and validation sets.

Discussion

Principal findings of this study

In this study, we developed and validated a clinically applicable multivariate predictive model for pregnant women admitted for PB between 24 and 37 weeks of gestation who were co-infected with HBV carrier status and GDM. This model can assist clinicians in effectively managing patient groups with varying risks of cesarean section and vaginal delivery. The strong diagnostic performance of the models suggests that clinicians should prioritize blood pressure and liver function indices at admission over hepatitis B virus–related markers, including HBV DNA titers, Hepatitis B Surface Antigen Load (HBSA) and Hepatitis B core antigen viral load, as a part of the management of PB, particularly in pregnancy of HBV carrier status combine with GDM women with early PB.

Model variables and their implications for the pathophysiology of PB in women with HBV carriers and GDM

PB is a multifactorial condition that contributes to significant maternal and neonatal health complications worldwide [25–28]. Multiple factors may contribute to the onset and progression of PB; the association between maternal HBV carriage or GDM and PB may be mediated by inflammatory processes that affect placental function and angiogenesis [9, 29, 30]. A retrospective cohort study based on 2,567,040 pregnant women found that pre-BMI was a predictor for PB [31], which is consistent with this study. An observational retrospective cohort study of 1263 preterm newborns have found that the chronic hypertension of maternal associated with PB [28]. In our study, we found that SBP, DBP, and ALT at admission related to the PB were similar to previous findings. Analysis of clinical data from a retrospective cohort of 3,082 pregnant women revealed that DBP, ALT, and SBP were critical indicators for spontaneous PB in pregnant women [18]. Meanwhile, a population-based prospective pregnancy cohort study revealed that high levels of DBP and SBP during late pregnancy are associated with an elevated risk of PB in rural Nepal [32]. The potential mechanisms were that fetal trophoblast cells, in terms of angiogenesis, hypoxia, and placental development, influence the placental function and lead to intrauterine growth restriction, ultimately leading to medically induced preterm delivery [33]. Our findings suggest that women with concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM should undergo continuous monitoring of blood pressure throughout pregnancy.

Intrauterine infection has been identified as one of the primary causes of PB and is closely associated with its occurrence [34, 35]. Intrauterine infections have a significant association with higher levels of maternal WBC in pregnant women [36]. We found that in pregnancies complicated by both HBV carrier status and GDM, the preterm group had higher white blood cell counts compared to the non-preterm group, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies. Intrauterine infection and PB are the primary complications of preterm PROM [37]. In our study have also found that PROM is a predictive risk for PB. Therefore, intervention strategies should focus on preventing respiratory infections during pregnancy and monitoring blood biomarkers during routine prenatal checkups to reduce the risk of PB. In accordance with common clinical practice in China, tocolytic therapy is recommended for women with threatened preterm labor or preterm labor when time is needed to administer antenatal corticosteroids for fetal lung maturation, facilitate in utero transfer to a facility capable of caring for preterm infants, or provide fetal neuroprotection with magnesium sulfate. However, the duration of tocolytic treatment should not exceed 48 h [38].

An analysis of the causes of preterm birth over the past 25 years has shown that preterm rupture of membranes is not the primary cause of preterm birth; rather, iatrogenic preterm birth is the main cause [39]. In this study, a higher proportion of preterm births among women concurrent with HBV carrier status and GDM is associated with liver-related complications, including abnormal liver function, elevated total bile acids, and coagulation disorders. This phenomenon may be caused by increased strain on the liver during pregnancy concurrent with HBV carrier status and GDM, leading to abnormal liver function, which may in turn result in iatrogenic preterm. This may explain why preterm rupture of membranes and blood pressure scored lower in the model than other indicators related to liver function.

A multicenter retrospective study in China found that pregnancy with placenta previa was more likely to result in PB compared to a normal placenta [40]. This study also found that placenta previa is a risk factor for PB. Placenta previa in late pregnancy will frequently appear unexplained, which increases the risk of uterine infection and PB [41]. Management strategy is screening for the health status of women with HBV carrier status in pre-pregnancy to decrease the risk of placental abnormalities. Several studies have identified that TBA levels are a major risk factor among patients diagnosed with ICP who are at risk of PB [5, 42], which is consistent with our findings. The potential mechanism by which bile acids cause PB may be that bile acids activate oxytocin receptors, thereby promoting uterine contractions [43]. Therefore, it is hypothesized that the combination of HBV carrier status and GDM in pregnancy will increase the level of TBA to some extent, which will lead to the development of PB. This study also found that TT4 was a predictor of PB. The levels of total thyroxine are just as effective in identifying women with subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH) who are at increased risk of PB compared to women with normal thyroid function [44]. A prospective cohort study involving 8,012 pregnant women demonstrated that participants with SCH exhibited an increased risk of PROM [45]. TT4 is an important indicator for evaluating hypothyroidism. Our findings also indicate that PT is associated with an elevated risk of PB. Previous research has shown that a PT below 12.5s is linked to a 2.11–fold higher risk of PB [46]. Our findings diverge from the results reported in prior investigations. In our study, the PT in the PB group was longer than that in the full-term group, which may be explained by variations in the characteristics of the study populations. GDM in pregnant women complicated by HBV carrier status is associated with abnormal coagulation parameters.

Clinical and research implications

Although the prevalence of HBV carrier status among women of reproductive age has declined in China, it remains high among this population in other countries. In total, 5.5% of the women tested positive for HBsAg. The prevalence was higher among women from Africa (7.3%) than among those from South Asian countries (2.2%). However, in Mozambique, the prevalence reached as high as 12.9% [47]. HBV carrier status in women had been associated with an elevated risk of GDM during pregnancy. Moreover, the variables included in this study are routinely assessed during antenatal care and do not incur additional costs, therefore, the model developed in this study is readily generalizable for widespread clinical use. From a clinical perspective, the classification of the risk of PB into low and high-risk group might assist clinicians to take effective action to manage women with a diagnosis of combined HBV carrier status and GDM during regular maternity visits, which could decrease the risk of neonatal complications, reduce postpartum hemorrhage, and ameliorate adverse maternal and infant outcomes. For the higher risk, prenatal strategies should be conducted in conditions early, intervention included monitoring contractions using ultrasound and fetal, using antenatal steroids, preparation of equipment for resuscitation in Neonatal Intensive Care Units (NICU). Further studies should be planned to assess the impact of the model on ameliorating clinical management, the potential benefit of early use of hepatoprotective medications in women with a higher risk of PB in concurrent with HBV carrier status and GDM pregnant women in the future. The model of cost-effectiveness should also be further studied, such as the day of charge in the hospital, costs of hospitalization, and therapy. Finally, developing a model to predict PB among women who are concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM is particularly important in remote and resource-limited settings, where the burden of PB–related morbidity and mortality remain substantial.

Comparisons with other studies

To our knowledge, there were the first prediction models for PB in women with concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM. For example, a PB prediction model for GDM was developed using clinical data from 1,879 women with singleton pregnancies and the AUC was 0.722 [6]. The AUC for our prediction model for PB using maternal biomarkers and clinical information was 0.845. This result is higher than the area predicted by the model based on clinical and biomarker risk factors for other pregnancy complications in women of PB [31, 48]. In previous studies, the training models of predicting risk factors that need laboratory testing that was not incorporated into routine clinical practice and are too costly to use in remote areas with limited medical resources [35, 49, 50]. Our predictive model utilizes readily available biomarkers and supports sequential screening at each prenatal visit. This approach can be integrated with first-trimester screening to provide real-time updates to each pregnant woman’s risk assessment as the pregnancy progresses. The study found that this indicator in the model increases with gestational age, and that biomarkers from the third trimester provide greater predictive power than clinical features from the first trimester alone.

Strengths and limitations of this study

A major strength of this study is that it represents the first attempt to investigate risk factors for PB in women with concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM. Combining Univariate and LASSO regression analyses to screen variables. The model was subsequently validated using a separate cohort. We choose clinical information and biomarkers to construct predictive models. The predictors in this model are objective, reproducible, and easily quantifiable, while also being practical and cost-effective. The model significantly improves usability and accessibility in remote, resource-limited settings. Finally, the prediction model constructed using pre-BMI, SBP, DBP, Amniotic membrane rupture, placenta previa, WBC, Total protein (TP), ALT, TBA, TT4, and PT demonstrated good discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility, indicating its value in effectively identifying individuals concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM at high risk of PB. The model shows good clinical applicability.

This study also has some shortcomings. First, this model was developed using data from a tertiary hospital in southeastern China, and the sample size is relatively limited; therefore, larger-scale, multicenter studies are needed to determine whether these findings are applicable to other regions of China. Second, this study is not intended to assess whether these predictive models can improve maternal and neonatal outcomes. Third, baseline cervical parameters were not assessed in this study. As a result, the model may have omitted an important predictive factor and overestimated the contribution of certain laboratory biomarkers. In the future, we will conduct a large-scale, prospective, multicenter cohort study to validate the stability and accuracy of this model.

Conclusion

This study established and validated a risk prediction model incorporating eleven pre-labor clinical indicators to predict PB in women with concurrent HBV carrier status and GDM. This nomogram model shows promise for the early identification of high–risk patients and may facilitate timely clinical management and intervention, thereby improving maternal and neonatal outcomes.
