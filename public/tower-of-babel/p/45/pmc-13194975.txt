Fusing transportation rules and diverse motion behaviors for trajectory prediction in traffic intersections
Authors: Yuchao Su, Can Pei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Predicting future trajectories of traffic participants (also called agents) plays an important role in realizing autonomous driving. However, accurately forecasting future trajectories at traffic intersections poses significant challenges. Drivers pay varying attention levels to vehicles in different lanes, and their driving behaviors are influenced by traffic signals. Existing models struggle to effectively establish interaction relationships between vehicles in different lanes to reflect drivers’ attention, and often overlook the impacts of traffic signals. Moreover, different types of agents display diverse motion patterns at traffic intersections, rendering a single trajectory prediction method difficult to model such diversity. This paper proposes a traffic intersection trajectory prediction model to address these challenges. Firstly, a directed graph is designed to characterize the high attention levels to vehicles in relevant lanes, while minimizing the impacts of other vehicles. Secondly, an action prediction method is proposed to estimate the future motion actions of vehicles based on traffic signal information. Finally, multiple t-distributions are used to separately forecast the future paths of vehicles with different motion actions and pedestrians. The experiments demonstrate the superiority of our algorithm over twenty-four trajectory prediction models on two datasets with traffic intersection scenes.

Introduction

Autonomous driving is a pivotal vehicular technology for enhancing transportation safety and efficiency, and it generally requires the integration of three core functions1: environmental perception2, trajectory prediction3, and motion planning4. The trajectory prediction module utilizes features obtained from the environmental perception module to predict the future motions of surrounding agents. Subsequently, the trajectory prediction results are used to guide the control module to execute a safe and efficient driving strategy. Thus, accurate trajectory prediction is crucial for achieving high-level autonomous driving.

For autonomous driving, different traffic scenarios pose dissimilar challenge levels. Vehicle road scenes often encompass straight roads, curved roads, roundabouts, and intersections. Intersection scenes are generally highly complex due to the special interactions based on lane rules, the impacts of traffic signals, and the diverse motion modes of agents. The following three key challenges are particularly pertinent to trajectory prediction in traffic intersection scenarios.Lane influences on driving attention: Drivers must pay close attention to vehicles in non-adjacent or distant lanes, but existing methods struggle to accurately describe the effects of lane rules at traffic intersections.Traffic signal impacts on motion actions: Traffic signals can significantly constrain the movement actions of cars. However, cyclists and motorcyclists do not always comply with the rules based on traffic signals5, complicating the accurate prediction of vehicle trajectories in traffic intersection scenarios. Existing methods often overlook the impacts of traffic signals for trajectory prediction.Diverse motion modes of vehicles and pedestrians: The turning, stopping, and going straight behaviors of vehicles are obviously different motion modes, whereas pedestrians generally move at slow speeds and have diverse movement directions. A single prediction model often fails to accurately forecast trajectories based on different motion modes at traffic intersections.To address these challenges, this paper proposes the Traffic Intersection Trajectory Prediction model (TITP), which includes a directed graph for describing the interactions based on lane rules, an action prediction method with traffic signal information, and a multi-distribution trajectory estimation method for modeling different motion behaviors. The main contributions are summarized as follows.Lane rule graph: We design a directed graph to characterize the strong interactions between vehicles in the relevant lanes while minimizing the impacts of vehicles in other lanes, at traffic intersections. This method can model the attention of drivers to vehicles in different lanes.Action prediction: We convert the states of traffic signals into sequential features to represent potential motion modes, and then forecast future motion actions of vehicles, thereby capturing the influences of traffic signals on future trajectories.Multi-distribution trajectory estimation: We employ multiple t-distributions to characterize the turning, stopping, and going straight motions of vehicles. Additionally, we incorporate an extra t-distribution to model the movement patterns of pedestrians, which typically involve multi-directional and slow motions.The subsequent sections of the paper are organized as follows. Finally, we conclude this paper in Section “Conclusions”.

Background

Historical behavior learning

Sequence-based deep learning models, such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Gated Recurrent Units (GRU), have been widely adopted for modeling agents’ historical motions6. In7, a hierarchical LSTM simultaneously captures the dual influences of surrounding agents and the scene. This model leverages three specialized LSTM networks to extract distinct levels of information, including the historical trajectory, social interactions, and scene contexts. A bidirectional LSTM classification network8 utilizes different sub-LSTM structures to generate trajectories corresponding to the predicted target regions, thereby enhancing prediction accuracy and contextual understanding. Similarly, a bidirectional GRU-based network9 is applied to finely extract the sequential features of trajectories from different aspects, improving the prediction performance for pedestrian trajectory prediction. Accurate trajectory prediction for pedestrians at intersections remains a challenge, mainly caused by the crossing behaviors of pedestrians. To tackle this issue, a set of risk factors is established to quantitatively evaluate the risk associated with crossing behaviors. Subsequently, an LSTM network is used to integrate trajectories, traffic signal lights, and risk factor features to improve the prediction accuracy10.

The above methods struggle to directly describe the impacts of other agents on a target agent. To effectively characterize the impacts between agents, researchers have used Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)11 in trajectory prediction tasks, where Graph Attention Networks (GATs)12 and Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs)13 are two widely used types of GNNs. GATs construct the connections between the nodes by the edges and then update the features of the nodes and edges, capturing the mutual influence between agents. In14, a heterogeneous edge-enhanced GAT is introduced to extract the inter-agent interactions using a directed heterogeneous graph. A group-constrained hierarchical GAT15 is designed to meticulously capture intra-group, out-group, and inter-group interactions, thereby integrating complicated interaction information to enhance prediction accuracy. Unlike GATs, GCNs determine their edges at the initial stage and don’t update the edge states. They focus on learning node features and preserving deterministic spatial relationships via the edges. Although GCNs can establish stable spatial relationships between agents, these spatial relationships are often symmetric and fail to capture the common asymmetric influences. To characterize the asymmetric impacts, a GCN with three directed graphs16 is proposed to model diverse interaction behaviors, where the three graphs respectively model the asymmetric interaction relationships based on view fields, motion directions, and motion rates of agents, effectively describing complex interactions between agents. A heterogeneous context-aware GCN17 is designed to simultaneously extract latent contextual information from historical trajectories, scene elements, and interaction behaviors. In this model, trajectory features and scene information are extracted by two convolutional neural networks, and a spatio-temporal GCN is then used to integrate these features for presenting scene and motion influences. In18, a graph-based model treats vehicle movements as nodes within a temporal graph, and the interactions between agents are represented by a dynamic adjacency matrix to comprehensively capture the intricate spatial and temporal dependencies. Agent motion behaviors are often simultaneously governed by interactions and traffic elements. A hybrid graph19 is designed to represent both agents and traffic elements as the graph nodes and to construct interactions between agents and traffic elements through the graph edges. Understanding interactions and motion tendencies of agents is important for trajectory prediction in intersection scenes. A trajectory prediction approach using traffic light information is introduced in20. This method utilizes a dynamic graph to capture global spatial interactions and reconstruct subgraphs for modeling local interaction behaviors. Additionally, acceleration, deceleration, and turning direction are used to infer motion tendencies and reduce prediction errors.

Sequence deep learning models excel at capturing temporal relationships, while GNNs are effective at modeling spatial dependencies. However, effectively integrating spatio-temporal features to enhance trajectory prediction performance remains a challenge. Transformer21 has demonstrated its high performance in many tasks. It provides an effective framework for integrating spatial features into sequential modeling. The early work22 adopts Transformer to incorporate both the sequence features of trajectories and the interaction relationships between agents, thereby enhancing the accuracy of trajectory prediction. Subsequently, a stacked Transformer23 is proposed to extract scene features and the trajectories of the target agent and the surrounding agents. In24, a novel Transformer architecture jointly models temporal and social features of agents. This model captures sequence representations of multi-agent trajectories by flattening trajectory features across time and agents, and employs an agent-aware attention mechanism to model inter-agent interactions. Previous methods often overlook group-level motion characteristics. To address this issue, a group-based annotation method25 is introduced to quantify group interactions, and the complex spatial-temporal interactions are modeled by a spatio-temporal Transformer module with a cross-attention mechanism. In26, a spatio-temporal Transformer network is adopted to learn driving maneuvers, thereby enhancing trajectory prediction accuracy in intersection scenarios.

Future motion forecasting

For an agent, its motion destination is generally fixed, but there are often multiple feasible paths to reach the destination. Such diversity in motions is often termed ‘multi-modal’ in trajectory prediction27,28. Although the aforementioned models focus on modeling the spatio-temporal features of agents, they have difficulty generating feasible and diverse motion predictions. Generative models, including Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)29, Conditional Variational AutoEncoders (CVAEs)30, flow-based models31, and diffusion-based models32, have employed to characterize the diverse motions.

The early work33 adopts a GAN model to learn the motion behaviors of pedestrians and generate multiple predicted trajectories to approximate the true future trajectory. In34, a GAN-based model is designed to learn stochastic predictions from past movements, social interactions, and scene constraints. A goal-based GAN model35 is introduced to generate diverse predicted destinations, and then these destinations are used to predict trajectories between the historical trajectory and the destinations, thereby depicting different motion behaviors. In36, a GAN-based model is integrated with a social self-attention mechanism, which selectively extracts interaction information with strong impacts to enhance the rationality of the predicted trajectories. Agent interactions significantly influence their motions at traffic intersections. In37, the interaction behaviors are encapsulated as a social context in a GAN model, thereby obtaining the most plausible future trajectory from many predictions.

Although GANs have been widely applied to trajectory prediction tasks, they still suffer from challenges, such as mode collapse and training instability38. Compared with GANs, CVAEs are generally more stable to train and achieve higher prediction accuracy. In39, a CVAE is combined with a GAN discriminator for trajectory prediction. The model generates trajectory samples via the CVAE and evaluates them using the GAN discriminator, ensuring that the outputs follow realistic motion statistics and closely resemble actual vehicle trajectories. This combination enriches the diversity of trajectory generation and enhances the robustness and realism of predictions. In40, a goal-based CVAE is introduced to generate different predicted destinations. Then, these destinations and past motions are fed into a multi-layer perceptron to model diverse motion behaviors. The prediction error of a future trajectory often grows with the increase in the number of future time steps, and generated trajectories may deviate from historical motion patterns. To address these challenges, a cascaded sliding CVAE with a social interaction module41 is devised to reduce the accumulated prediction error. Moreover, the social interaction module evaluates the compatibility between historical and predicted trajectories to enhance prediction plausibility. For intersection scenes, a variational neural network42 is proposed to learn conditional variational distributions, which provide the confidence levels of predicted trajectories to estimate the rationality of the trajectories.

CVAE-based models optimize the variational lower bound of the log-likelihood, which may lead to a mismatch between the learned distribution and the true data distribution, resulting in inaccurate trajectory predictions. Therefore, a generative flow model31 is adopted in43 to address the limitation of CVAEs. Compared with previous GAN-based and CVAE-based models, the generative flow model43 uses a sequence of invertible transformations to map complex trajectory distributions into simple, tractable ones, enabling accurate modeling of diverse motion patterns. Recently, diffusion models have been widely applied to many data generation tasks44,45. A diffusion-based trajectory prediction model32 is designed with a trainable initializer to estimate the denoised distribution, reducing the number of required denoising steps and accelerating inference while preserving the representation capacity for motion modes.

Accurately forecasting the driving intentions can effectively enhance the prediction accuracy of the corresponding vehicle motions at traffic intersections. In46, an intention prediction method is designed to cluster a large number of trajectories and assist in the estimation of trajectory distributions. Unlike many approaches, which significantly rely on historical trajectories, the current states and motion intentions of vehicles at traffic intersections are used to produce future trajectories47. This is more flexible when historical trajectories are incomplete or scarce. A behavior prediction framework48 is proposed to capture different driving intentions. This framework uses a Bayesian network to forecast a discrete intention, and then the prediction result is refined by an inverse reinforcement learning to generate continuous trajectories. In49, an inverse reinforcement learning method is adopted to rank and select the optimal trajectory among multiple predictions at traffic intersections. Vehicles in intersection scenes often execute a sudden deceleration action when facing a red light, which is one of the main reasons for traffic oscillation. To mitigate this issue, a deep reinforcement learning method50 is proposed to predict the trajectory of the following vehicle and adjust the speed of the leading vehicle, thereby suppressing traffic oscillations during the red light phase.

Modeling spatio-temporal feature

Except for using the lane rule graph, three directed graphs proposed in the prior work16 are employed to model common spatial interactions between agents. We briefly introduce the designs of the three directed graphs in Fig. 316. The agents’ positions, motion directions, and movement rates are provided in Fig. 3(a). In Fig. 3(b), the view impacts are modeled based on the view fields of agents, where a target agent is only influenced by agents within the view field. In Fig. 3(c), the influences based on movement directions are defined, and only agents with conflicting motion directions present interaction behaviors. Modeling diverse motion modes via multi-distribution trajectory estimation

In road scenarios outside traffic intersections, the motions of vehicles usually follow the directions prescribed by lanes. However, at traffic intersections, the motion behaviors are more diverse. We use the t-distribution to model these diverse motion modes. The probability density function of the t-distribution is defined as follows:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} f(z;m,v) = \frac{{\Gamma \left( {\frac{{v + 1}}{2}} \right) }}{{\sqrt{v\pi } \Gamma \left( {\frac{v}{2}} \right) }}\left( {1 + \frac{{(z - m)^2 }}{v}} \right) ^{ - (v + 1)/2}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Gamma$$\end{document} is the Gamma function, and z, v, and m indicate a random variable, a degree of freedom, and the position of the target agent, respectively. The t-distribution can exhibit the characteristics of the Gaussian distribution when the degree of freedom v is high, and it resembles the Cauchy distribution when v is low. Typically, the t-distribution converges to the standard Gaussian distribution when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v\rightarrow \infty$$\end{document}, whereas it coincides with the standard Cauchy distribution when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v = 1$$\end{document}.

By learning v from trajectory samples, we can characterize the different motion behaviors of vehicles and pedestrians. For instance, the motion directions of pedestrians are very diverse. These motion behaviors exhibit long-tailed data characteristics, which are easily modeled by the Cauchy distribution16. For cars, the steering is constrained by the mechanical structure, and the Gaussian distribution is suitable to yield samples near the mean to describe constrained motion behaviors52. Therefore, the t-distribution is used in our algorithm to model the motion behaviors of vehicles and pedestrians.

Although the t-distribution can describe diverse motions, different motion behaviors often follow disparate probability distributions. Therefore, we propose a multi-distribution trajectory estimation to precisely model the different motion behaviors. Experiment

This section validates the prediction performance of TITP using two trajectory prediction datasets with traffic intersection scenarios5,53, by comparing TITP against 24 recently proposed models. Additionally, the ablation experiments analyze the performance contributions of the lane rule graph, action prediction, and multi-distribution trajectory estimation to the overall performance.

Experimental setup

The experiments in this study are conducted using PyTorch on the following hardware environment: AMD Ryzen 9 5900X (CPU) and NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4080 (GPU). The Adam optimizer54 is used for the model parameter optimization, with a learning rate of 1e−4. The learning rate is reset to 0.9 times its current value every 10 epochs. The batch size and the training epoch are set to 64 and 100, respectively.

Two performance evaluation metrics widely used in trajectory prediction tasks, i.e., minimal Average Displacement Error (ADE) and minimal Final Displacement Error (FDE), are employed to verify the prediction errors of the compared algorithms. Short-term prediction

Table 1 displays the short-term prediction results for different types of agents in terms of ADE and FDE. From Table 1, we can observe that TITP exhibits the highest prediction accuracy across all types of agents, except for bicycles. It is worth noting that bicycles have more flexible movement directions and rates, which makes it challenging for the action prediction method to accurately forecast their motion actions. In terms of average ADE and FDE results, TITP achieves the best prediction performance. These experimental results validate the effectiveness of TITP in predicting the short-term future trajectories of various types of agents in traffic intersection scenarios.

In traffic intersection scenarios, changes in traffic light states significantly affect vehicle motion behaviors. For example, when a red traffic light turns green, vehicles originally waiting in the entry areas (as shown in one of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_1$$\end{document}-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_4$$\end{document} in Fig. 2) begin to move. Ideally, these vehicles should proceed in an orderly sequence, with the vehicle behind closely following the preceding one at a safe distance to avoid collisions or traffic congestion. Therefore, it is crucial to accurately predict the motion rates of vehicles. Fig. 4 illustrates a traffic intersection scene with red traffic lights turning green. In this scenario, cars A, B, and C start to move from right to left as the traffic light changes from red to green. Car A prioritizes acceleration, closely followed by cars B and C. Thus, accurately predicting their motion rates is essential for effectively describing the collaborative motion behaviors of these cars. For car A, the predicted trajectories obtained by the three algorithms closely align with the actual trajectory because the motion of car A is less affected by cars B and C. However, VDRGCN and Social-STGCNN cannot effectively predict the future trajectory of cars B and C, potentially leading to traffic congestion in the short term. This limitation can be attributed to two primary factors. First, the slow historical motion rates of both cars result in insignificant changes in the future positions predicted by VDRGCN and Social-STGCNN. Second, both algorithms are unable to accurately perceive the impact of car A in the related lane on cars B and C. Unlike VDRGCN and Social-STGCNN, TITP utilizes the proposed lane rule graph to effectively transmit vehicle rate information across related lanes, enabling accurate predictions for the future motion rates of cars B and C. Ablation experiment

To further validate the contributions of the three proposed methods, we compare TITP with the prediction models based on a single Gaussian distribution, a single Cauchy distribution, and a single t-distribution, as well as those without the lane rule graph or action prediction. To analyze the differences between existing methods and our methods, we replace the multi-distribution prediction method in Section "Modeling diverse motion modes via multi-distribution trajectory estimation" with a GAN-based method35 or a CVAE module40. Additionally, we use a distance-based graph61 to displace the lane rule graph in Section "Modeling lane influences via lane rule graph". All experimental results are reported in Table 3 and Table 4.

From Table 3 and Table 4, we can observe the superior performance of TITP in both short-term and long-term predictions. In the short-term prediction, the average prediction performance of TITP outperforms other models by at least \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$13\%$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$11\%$$\end{document} in terms of ADE and FDE, respectively. Compared with the models based on the Gaussian and Cauchy distributions, the model based on the t-distribution demonstrates better prediction results because the t-distribution can model the characteristics of the Gaussian or Cauchy distribution to generate similar or diverse trajectories. On the one hand, the t-distribution can fit the Gaussian distribution to sample similar motion behaviors, such as those of vehicles in the same lanes. On the other hand, the t-distribution can also exhibit the long-tail characteristics of the Cauchy distribution, thereby capturing the diverse movement directions of pedestrians. TITP consistently outperforms the model based on a single t-distribution across all the types of agents. This demonstrates that using multiple t-distributions can more precisely characterize the motion behaviors of different types of agents and vehicles performing various actions. Compared with the model without the lane rule graph (denoted as w/o lane rule graph in Tables 3-4 and Fig. 8), TITP achieves relative performance improvements of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$19\%$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$17\%$$\end{document} in terms of average ADE and FDE results, respectively. When compared with the model without the action prediction (denoted as w/o action prediction in Tables 3-4 and Fig. 9), TITP achieves average improvements of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$24\%$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$23\%$$\end{document} based on the ADE and FDE metrics. These experimental results further substantiate the contributions of the lane rule graph and action prediction methods. Similarly, the experimental results for long-term predictions in Table 4 also support the above conclusions.

Comparing the GAN-based and CVAE-based models, TITP achieves average improvements of 40%/30 % and 25%/11% in terms of ADE/FDE, respectively. We analyze the limitations of the two methods as follows. GAN implicitly models the data distribution through a generator and a discriminator and suffers from training instability and mode collapse issues38. As a result, GAN-based models often underperform models using the Gaussian distribution and CVAE40,61. In Tables 3 and 4, the GAN-based model exhibits the worst overall performance among the five trajectory generation methods. The CVAE-based model outperforms the models based on the Gaussian distribution and GAN, achieves comparable performance to the Cauchy distribution-based model, but falls short of the t-distribution-based model, as shown in Tables 3 and 4. CVAE uses historical trajectories as conditions and employs a Gaussian distribution to approximate the future trajectory distribution. Thus, the generated trajectories easily cluster in a region, which is described by the mean of the Gaussian distribution, struggling to model diverse motion behaviors at traffic intersections. Compared with CVAE, the t-distribution can produce trajectories with moderate diversity because it can approximate both the Gaussian distribution or the Cauchy distribution, as discussed in Section "Modeling diverse motion modes via multi-distribution trajectory estimation". Consequently, it neither generates overly diverse trajectories like the Cauchy distribution, which exhibits a difficulty in stably representing vehicle stationary states, such as the stopping, nor produces trajectories that are too concentrated and lack diversity like the Gaussian distribution.

Compared with the model using the distance-based graph, TITP achieves average improvements of 31%/20% in terms of ADE/FDE. Additionally, the performance of the model using the distance-based graph is inferior to that of TITP w/o lane rule graph. The literature16 has pointed out that the interaction relationships constructed by distance-based graphs are symmetric for two agents, which often fails to reflect real-world interaction states. For example, when two pedestrians are moving in the same direction and one is behind the other, the front pedestrian’s motion is less likely to be influenced by the rear pedestrian, while the reverse is not necessarily true. Moreover, TITP w/o lane rule graph still utilizes three directed graphs16 to model interactions between agents, as shown in Fig. 3. The asymmetric interactions established by the three directed graphs are disrupted by the distance-based graph, thereby degrading the prediction performance. Conversely, our lane rule graph enhances the asymmetric interactions through the asymmetric driving attention. This complementary design is crucial for accurate trajectory prediction at traffic intersections.

Based on the experimental results and the above analysis, we can demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed lane rule graph and multi-distribution prediction method at traffic intersections. Next, we provide visual cases to further verify the superiority of our methods.

Figure 8 demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed lane rule graph. The areas marked by dotted boxes in Fig. 8 are enlarged to provide a clear presentation of prediction results. In this case, the traffic lights have just turned from red to green, prompting cars A, B, and C to begin moving forward. It is worth noting that the motorcycle near car A is violating traffic rules. In Fig. 8(a), TITP without the lane rule graph obtains an acceptable path for car B that is close to the actual trajectory. However, for cars A and C with slow motion rates, TITP without the lane rule graph fails to accurately forecast the acceleration behaviors because it only considers the historical motions of both cars. The inaccurate predictions of cars A and C in Fig. 8(a) may cause traffic congestion. In contrast, Fig. 8(b) shows that the prediction results of TITP with the lane rule graph are significantly improved. The lane rule graph provides effective interactions to harmonize the motion rates of cars in related lanes. This case verifies that the proposed lane rule graph can effectively enhance the prediction accuracy for vehicles in related lanes, which is crucial to avoid traffic congestion and collisions in traffic intersection scenes.

Figure 9 emphasizes the significance of the action prediction method in traffic intersection scenarios. It is evident that the prediction results of TITP outperform those of the model without the action prediction. In this case, motorcycle A is clearly making a right turn. The prediction for motorcycle A in Fig. 9(a) presents a forward motion. While in Fig. 9(b), the prediction closely aligns with the actual trajectory. For motorcycle B and car C, whose turning actions are less pronounced than that of motorcycle A, TITP still accurately estimates their action tendencies. In this case, the motorcycle moves in a straight line while the car performs a straight action with a slight turn. Prediction on Lyft dataset

We adopt the Lyft dataset53 to further validate the effectiveness of our algorithm. The dataset was collected by a fleet of unmanned aerial vehicles in Palo Alto, USA, and included 1118 hours of logs between October 2019 and March 2020 (approximately 162,000 scenes, each 25 seconds in length), with the movement of traffic participants, lane geometry, and traffic light information. Trajectories in the Lyft dataset were sampled from urban road scenarios including both intersection and non-intersection scenes, with a sampling rate of 10 Hz.

We evaluate the prediction performance of TITP by comparing it with 12 trajectory prediction models. These compared models are briefly introduced as follows: 1) MATS65 employs a linear-affine dynamical structure by integrating a GRU, a CVAE, and a spatiotemporal graph for highly non-linear trajectory prediction tasks. 2) Trajectron++66 is a graph-based recurrent model that considers agent motions with heterogeneous data and it incorporates kinematic constraints into learning-based methods for multi-agent trajectory prediction. 3) MultiXNet67 encodes the lidar data in a scene image and then employs a CNN to perform detection and motion prediction tasks. 4) HAICU68 employs an inference-time probabilistic graphical model for agent interactions and incorporates agent class uncertainty by encoding the states of agents. 5) Real-Time69 uses hierarchical convolutional networks to extract global interaction and local features of a scene and employs a Transformer structure to model interaction features. 6) MTP70 utilizes a rasterized representation as input and then utilizes a CNN to generate multiple trajectories. 7) SGNet71 is a goal-based prediction model that predicts goals at multiple time scales and subsequently predicts future trajectories based on the goals. 8) STGM72 employs CVAEs to construct the framework, including a CVAE-based spatial encoder and a CVAE-based temporal encoder to model spatial-temporal features. 9) CVM73 observes the historical positions of a target agent and assumes the agent continues to move with the same velocity. 10) Trajectron74 combines variational deep generative models, LSTMs, and dynamic spatio-temporal graphical structures to produce multiple future trajectories. 11) ResNet-5053 is an official baseline for the Lyft dataset and it only uses semantic maps to predict future trajectories. 12) CATF55,75 is a trajectory prediction framework based on a Transformer with a hierarchical-structured context-aware module, which captures the motion information of surrounding agents and scene features.

Table 5 summarizes the prediction results for vehicles, bicycles, and pedestrians. Please note that the settings of h, p, and K follow those in69 and the results of the compared algorithms are directly cited from69. When predicting the trajectories of different types of agents, our model is superior to other algorithms across almost all metrics, except for the ADE results for vehicles. From Table 5, it is evident that MATS has the worst performance among all models. MATS employs two LSTMs to model the historical motions of two agents separately, and then applies the differential vector based on the two historical motions for interactions. However, this approach may be insufficient to accurately describe complex interaction behaviors. Trajectron++ and HAICU adopt a distance-based graph structure to model the interactions between agents, but the distance-based graph cannot effectively describe the impacts of traffic rules on future motions in traffic intersection scenes. MultiXNet crops local regions of interest from a raw scene image. However, this strategy may overlook some scene elements that significantly affect the future motions of agents. To overcome the drawback of MultiXNet, Real-Time employs a global CNN and a local CNN to extract scene features and local features of interest, and then uses the attention of a Transformer to fuse the spatial and temporal features. However, the above algorithms often ignore constraints imposed by traffic lights and rules. By utilizing the proposed action prediction and lane rule graph, TITP can consider the impacts of traffic lights and rules on agent motions, thereby improving the prediction performance. Although TITP does not show a significant superiority over Real-Time for vehicle trajectory predictions, our algorithm outperforms Real-Time by 10%/15% and 7%/9% for bicycles and pedestrians, in terms of ADE/FDE. Thus, the prediction results in Table 5 confirm the superiority of our model in both intersection and non-intersection scenarios.

Conclusions

Accurately predicting future trajectories at traffic intersections is critical for achieving high-level autonomous driving and presents three key challenges. Firstly, drivers pay significantly different attention levels to vehicles in distinct lanes, but existing methods have difficulty capturing these attention levels. Secondly, existing algorithms struggle to effectively model the influences of traffic signals on vehicle motions. Thirdly, the diverse motion patterns of vehicles and pedestrians complicate trajectory prediction tasks at traffic intersections. To solve the first problem, we propose a directed graph based on lane rules to describe the interaction relationships between vehicles in relevant lanes, thereby effectively presenting the different attention levels of drivers. For the second challenge, an action prediction method is devised to transform traffic signal states into sequential features for assessing future motion actions of vehicles. Based on different motion actions, we employ multiple t-distributions to address the last challenge. These t-distributions separately characterize the turning left, turning right, going straight, and stopping behaviors of vehicles, as well as the movement modes of pedestrians, thereby accurately predicting diverse trajectories in complex traffic intersection scenarios. Compared with twenty-four algorithms on two datasets with traffic intersection scenes, our model shows notable advantages across various prediction requirements for different agent types.
