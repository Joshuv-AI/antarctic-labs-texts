Machine learning predictive system to predict the risk of developing pre-eclampsia
Authors: Ing-luen Shyu, Chung-Feng Liu, Yung-Chieh Tsai, Yu-Shan Ma, Tian-Ni Kuo, Shiue Yow-Ling
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo develop a machine learning (ML)-based predictive model for assessing the risk of pre-eclampsia using routinely collected clinical data.MethodsWe retrospectively analysed data from 2444 pregnant women who delivered at Chi Mei Medical Center between 2015 and 2019, excluding patients under 20 years old. Pre-eclampsia was defined as blood pressure >140/90 mm Hg with proteinuria. Key features included gestational age, body weight, blood pressure and medical history. Five ML models were trained—logistic regression, random forest, light gradient boosting machine, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) and multilayer perceptron—using a 70/30 train-validation split. Synthetic minority oversampling technique was applied to address class imbalance. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was used for feature importance.ResultsAmong the five models, XGBoost showed the best performance with the highest accuracy, sensitivity, specificity and an area under the receiver operating characteristics curve of 0.921. SHAP analysis identified diastolic blood pressure, systolic blood pressure and urine glucose as top predictors.DiscussionOur findings demonstrate that ML, particularly XGBoost, can effectively predict the risk of pre-eclampsia using standard clinical data. This approach avoids the need for expensive tests while maintaining high accuracy.ConclusionThe XGBoost model offers a cost-effective and accurate method for pre-eclampsia risk prediction, enabling real-time assessment and supporting early intervention. Future studies will focus on larger data sets and clinical integration.Keywords: Machine Learning; Decision Making, Computer-Assisted; Artificial intelligence; Medical Informatics Applications; Safety Management

Introduction

Pre-eclampsia is a hypertensive disorder of pregnancy that can vary in severity and occurs in approximately 2%–8% of pregnant women.1 It can lead to severe maternal or fetal complications and even death. Chronic hypertension refers to pre-existing high blood pressure (≥140/90 mm Hg) before pregnancy. Gestational hypertension is diagnosed after 20 weeks of gestation and is characterised by high blood pressure without concurrent proteinuria. Pre-eclampsia involves new-onset hypertension and proteinuria after 20 weeks of gestation. Chronic hypertension with superimposed pre-eclampsia refers to chronic hypertension with new-onset proteinuria or other signs of pre-eclampsia after 20 weeks, or chronic proteinuria with new-onset hypertension.2 Delivery is the only cure for pre-eclampsia, often resulting in premature birth and its associated complications. Severe maternal complications include stroke, renal failure and liver failure, impacting long-term maternal health.3 Early stage pre-eclampsia is difficult to predict, leading to preterm delivery and unnecessary neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) use, wasting healthcare resources. Daily low-dose aspirin (100 mg/day) before 16 weeks can reduce early onset pre-eclampsia risk by 60%.4 Early onset pre-eclampsia, accounting for 20% of cases, is linked to inadequate trophoblast invasion and placental ischaemia,5 while late-onset pre-eclampsia is related to maternal vulnerability to vascular and metabolic stress.6 Current prediction methods integrate maternal history, biomarkers and uterine artery pulsatility index to estimate pre-eclampsia risk.7 8 However, these models have low sensitivity for late-onset pre-eclampsia, are costly and maintain a 10% false positive rate.9 Late-onset pre-eclampsia accounts for over 50% of related maternal deaths10 and is associated with more severe complications than early onset cases.11 Predicting pre-eclampsia is crucial. Machine learning (ML) models offer a promising approach by integrating diverse data and providing low-cost, efficient predictions.12 Many studies have applied artificial intelligence (AI)/ML methods to predict the risk for pre-eclampsia,13,18 but often use data from national health databases with varying diagnostic criteria. For example, Sufriyana18 used Indonesia’s national health insurance data set and applied ML algorithms such as logistic regression (LR), decision trees and random forests (RFs) to develop prediction models for pre-eclampsia. However, due to the variability in diagnostic criteria across multiple centres, such models may lack the consistency required for precise prediction in specific clinical settings.18 Similarly, Maric et al used elastic net and gradient-boosting techniques, incorporating biomarkers like pregnancy-associated plasma protein-A and placental growth factor; but their reliance on large, diverse data sets from multiple sources presented challenges in standardising results for direct clinical application.14 Jhee et al developed a model using maternal age, blood pressure and medical history as key variables,13 but they focused on the prediction of late-onset pre-eclampsia. According to previous literature, history of pre-eclampsia, multiple gestations, high body mass index (BMI), advanced maternal age, nulliparity, use of assisted reproductive techniques, as well as comorbid conditions such as chronic hypertension, kidney disease, type II diabetes mellitus and antiphospholipid syndrome, are all risk factors for pre-eclampsia.19,22 The present study aims to use data from a single medical centre, allowing for uniform diagnostic standards, which may enhance model performance and clinical relevance. The goal is to develop a clinically applicable predictive model for assessing pre-eclampsia risk at different gestational ages. It also aims to create a prediction tool based on AI/ML models, gather feedback from physicians during pilot trials and confirm the models’ clinical usability. This tool can help physicians develop personalised treatment plans and provide patients with quantified risk assessments, offering clearer insights into their risk levels.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study used labelled medical records of pregnant women from Chi Mei Medical Centre in Tainan, Taiwan, collected between 1 January 2015 and 31 December 2019. All pregnant women who received prenatal care were included, except those under 20 years old. Five ML methods—LR, RF, light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) and multilayer perceptron (MLP)—were employed to develop and assess the prediction models. All prenatal records from 10 weeks of gestation up to but not including the visit at which pre-eclampsia diagnostic criteria were first met were labelled as positive (outcome = 1), while all records from that diagnostic visit onwards were excluded. For those who were never diagnosed with pre-eclampsia, all records were labelled as negative (outcome = 0). This outcome labelling rule ensured that the model predicts risk at each antenatal visit prior to clinical diagnosis, thereby establishing a per-visit prediction framework in which every visit record generates an independent risk score.

The data set was randomly divided into a training set (70%) and a hold-out testing set (30%) at the individual level, ensuring that all records from the same woman were allocated exclusively to one set to avoid data leakage. The overall workflow for integrating the AI prediction model is illustrated in figure 1. In this study, pre-eclampsia is defined as new-onset blood pressure of >140/90 mm Hg on two occasions at least 4 hours apart and proteinuria (random urine dipstick ≥1+ on two occasions). To predict these outcomes, influential factors (feature variables) were identified through a literature review and clinical expertise. To ensure maximum predictive accuracy while preserving the clinical relevance of all variables, all available features for model development were used without conducting feature selection beforehand. Univariate filter methods (p≤0.05), Spearman’s correlation coefficient and expert opinions for feature selection were used. The 17 selected variables were gestational age (days), maternal age, maternal body weight, diastolic blood pressure (DBP) and systolic blood pressure (SBP), gravida, number of fetuses, natural pregnancy, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), urine glucose, urine protein, and histories of diabetes, hypertension, systemic lupus erythematosus, chronic kidney disease, pre-eclampsia and preterm birth. HbA1c was categorised into ‘Not tested/less than 6.5’ and ‘Equal or greater than 6.5’. Urine protein and glucose were categorised into ‘Negative/Trace’, ‘1+’ and ‘2+, 3+, 4+’ groups. Following the ‘Cross-validation: evaluating estimator performance procedure’ guidelines from Scikit-learn, the data set was split into 70% for model training and 30% for hold-out validation. To address class imbalance, the synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) was employed on the training data set.23,25 SMOTE generates synthetic minority samples along line segments between nearest neighbours and is widely used in imbalanced data sets. Five supervised algorithms— LR, RF, LightGBM, XGBoost and MLP—were implemented using Scikit-learn library.26 Hyperparameters were tuned by grid search with fivefold cross-validation, with ranges listed in online supplemental table 1. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity and area under the receiver operating characteristics curve (AUC), which are standard metrics in both clinical research and ML. The model with the highest AUC was deemed the best and was used to develop an interactive web-based prediction tool. This was then presented to several physicians to collect feedback on its usage. The web-based prediction tool was developed using Microsoft Visual Studio (V.17.7). When the optimal model is established, it can be integrated into the hospital information system (HIS) using shortcut keys. This integration allows for the automatic extraction of features from the HIS, enabling rapid calculation of risk values within the model.

We used SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values to interpret feature contributions in the best-performing XGBoost model. As shown in onlinesupplemental figures 2 3, DBP and SBP were the strongest predictors of pre-eclampsia, followed by gestational age, urine protein, maternal body weight and conception method. SHAP plots indicated that higher DBP and SBP values consistently increased risk, whereas natural conception was associated with lower risk. These findings highlight the dominant role of haemodynamic and maternal clinical factors in model predictions, supporting their clinical relevance and interpretability.

To evaluate feasibility and clinical acceptance, we developed a web-based prediction tool based on the best-performing model and piloted it with obstetrics, who provided positive feedback. The system allows real-time risk estimation during prenatal visits and can be integrated into the HIS for automated data extraction (see online supplemental figure 4). By dynamically updating risk according to changes in clinical parameters, the tool enables clinicians to identify high-risk patients early, consider preventive measures such as aspirin, and tailor follow-up or delivery planning accordingly. Discussion

This study developed an ML model for predicting pre-eclampsia using routinely collected clinical data, achieving excellent discrimination (AUC 0.942). The model enables clinicians to provide patients with quantitative risk estimates, supporting earlier preventive interventions and individualised monitoring throughout pregnancy.

A major advantage of this approach is its reliance on variables readily available from electronic medical records, without the need for costly biomarkers or specialised ultrasound. The model can generate predictions from the tenth week of gestation and update risk dynamically as clinical parameters change, facilitating timely measures such as low-dose aspirin or intensified surveillance. Moreover, integration into the HIS allows seamless use during routine consultations, with outputs that are easy to interpret and communicate to patients. This feature enhances shared decision-making and empowers women to participate actively in their care. By accurately identifying both high-risk and low-risk patients, the tool has the potential to optimise resource utilisation, reduce unnecessary hospitalisations and improve overall pregnancy outcomes. As summarised in table 3, our study differs from previous work by covering all occurrences of pre-eclampsia rather than restricting to early onset or late onset.13 14 Unlike Li et al27 who examined intrapartum and postpartum cases, our model predicts risk as early as the tenth week and continues to update throughout pregnancy. Unlike prior studies using multi-institutional databases with variable diagnostic criteria,18 27 28 our single-centre design ensured consistent standards, which likely contributed to the higher AUC. This uniformity in data collection is a key strength, yielding a reliable data set and supporting the superior performance of our model based on readily available clinical variables. Consistent with prior literature, our study confirmed significant associations between pre-eclampsia and patients’ comorbidities, as well as routine measures such as HbA1c. SHAP analysis identified DBP as the strongest predictor, followed by SBP and urine glucose. Clinically, this highlights the need to monitor DBP with equal attention as SBP and to consider urine glucose in routine assessments, which may further improve early identification and management of high-risk patients. Consequently, following the completion of the medical consultation, this AI prediction model can offer personalised outcome predictions. These predictions are provided in real time and are easily comprehensible for both patients and clinicians, with quantifiable data. By using this prediction model, clinicians and patients can make more confident decisions regarding subsequent treatment strategies. Importantly, the clinical interpretation of a high predicted risk at a given prenatal visit warrants clarification. Because our model was designed to generate a risk probability at each antenatal encounter prior to the actual diagnosis of pre-eclampsia, a high score should be viewed not as a marker of imminent diagnosis at the next visit, but rather as an indication that the woman is at a substantially elevated likelihood of developing pre-eclampsia later in pregnancy. In practice, this per-visit risk stratification can serve two complementary purposes: first, to flag women who may benefit from intensified monitoring in the short term (eg, closer follow-up, repeat laboratory testing); and second, to inform longer-term management plans such as prophylactic aspirin, increased surveillance in the third trimester or early delivery planning if clinically indicated. This dual interpretation underscores that the model is not intended to replace clinical judgement, but to provide dynamic, evidence-based risk estimates that support timely intervention across different stages of pregnancy. Despite its strengths, there were several limitations in this study. First, the limited sample size and shortage of input data may have adversely impacted the model’s performance. Additionally, the study was conducted using data from a single hospital, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to broader populations. However, we believe that the consistent diagnostic criteria used within the hospital helped strengthen the reliability of the data. Moreover, Taiwan, being a relatively small region with an integrated healthcare system, has generally comparable patient populations across different hospitals. Nonetheless, future studies should aim to include multicentre data to enhance the external validity of the findings. Furthermore, because diagnostic criteria and antenatal care schedules may vary across institutions, the outcome labelling rule and per-visit prediction framework established in this study may require adaptation before being applied in other clinical settings. This underscores the importance of external validation to confirm the broader applicability of our approach. Second, we did not assess other laboratory feature variables such as pregnancy-associated plasma protein-A, soluble Fms-like tyrosine kinase-1 and placenta growth factor. Including these variables could potentially improve the accuracy of the results. However, since the aim of this study was to develop a prediction model that could be implemented solely using electronic medical record data, we opted not to include these expensive and less accessible laboratory tests. Third, the patient information we collected did not differentiate whether aspirin was used, which could potentially affect the predictive outcomes and subsequent effectiveness of using medication for prevention. Despite these limitations, we believe that establishing an ML model and integrating it with the HIS can assist clinicians in predicting patient risks and developing personalised treatment plans. To enhance the robustness and predictive accuracy of the model, we recommend applying various balancing techniques, including SMOTE, to mitigate the risks of model bias and overfitting. In addition, although all available variables were included to maximise predictive accuracy, future studies may benefit from applying systematic feature selection techniques. Such approaches could reduce model complexity, enhance interpretability and further improve predictive performance. While this study focused on clinical and personal history data, future studies could benefit from incorporating molecular-level data, such as transcriptomics or proteomics, to capture the heterogeneity and complexity of pre-eclampsia.29 30 Integrating such data with ML models may improve the accuracy and early prediction of pre-eclampsia, especially in more complex cases.

Conclusion

We developed ML prediction models to forecast the likelihood of developing pre-eclampsia in pregnant women. The model constructed using XGBoost demonstrated superior performance compared with other ML algorithms in this study. These AI models offer dependable outcome predictions and represent a promising approach to personalised hospital care planning. For future endeavours, we intend to augment the data set size, by including a longer study period and multicentre data, and broaden the scope to include the prediction of adverse outcomes. Currently, we are in the process of developing a mobile application and a web-based service program for the clinical utilisation of AI prediction models. Additionally, we plan to integrate these predictive models into our HIS to aid in shared decision-making, thereby enhancing patient outcomes.
