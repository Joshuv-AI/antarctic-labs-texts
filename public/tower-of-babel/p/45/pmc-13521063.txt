Lessons learned from the validation of a machine learning-based colorectal carcinoma screening pipeline in sub-Saharan Africa
Authors: Alfred Githuka, Ulysses G.J. Balis, Ye Chan Kim, Eileen M. Weinheimer-Haus, Christopher L. Williams, Jerome Y. Cheng, Kelou Yao, John Blau, Jessica A. Baker, Priscilla Njenga, Winny Chepkemoi, Robert K. Parker, Akbar K. Waljee, Shahin Sayed, Mansoor N. Saleh

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArtificial intelligence for digital pathology may improve cancer detection and workflow efficiency in low- and middle-income countries, but most models are trained and validated in high-income settings, creating uncertainty about generalizability and real-world deployability under differing pre-analytic and infrastructure conditions.ObjectiveTo validate a locally optimized colorectal carcinoma screening pipeline, retrained on data from a local Kenyan cohort, and to assess its feasibility as a sensitivity-forward assistive pre-screening workflow.MethodsFormalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded hematoxylin and eosin-stained slides from 136 biopsy-proven colonic adenocarcinoma cases and 20 normal controls from 2 Kenyan institutions were digitized at 40× using a Grundium Ocus scanner. Whole-slide images were partitioned at the case level. A feature-enrichment and candidate tile-selection step identified adenocarcinoma-rich regions. Candidate 512 × 512 RGB tiles were then adjudicated by a gastrointestinal pathologist into adenocarcinoma-containing and benign/non-neoplastic tile libraries. These expert-curated tiles were used to train and validate a supervised convolutional neural network classifier, which generated tile-level malignancy probabilities. Tile scores were aggregated into case-level predictions using Top-K pooling and positive-tile burden rules to support sensitivity-forward screening.ResultsAmong 14,452 tiles from 70 quality-controlled cases and 19 controls, the model achieved strong tile-level discrimination (sensitivity 0.9548, specificity 0.9926, F1 score 0.9686, and AUROC curve 0.966); case-level Top-K aggregation achieved an AUROC of 1.0.ConclusionA locally optimized computational pathology screening pipeline, based on data from a local population, demonstrated robust colorectal adenocarcinoma detection in a Kenyan cohort. These findings suggest that existing machine-learning models can be adapted to local populations through retraining with locally derived data.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) and digital pathology have the potential to transform access to diagnostic services for cancer worldwide. This opportunity is particularly significant in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC), where severe shortages of trained pathologists limit the availability and timeliness of histopathological diagnosis, contributing to delays in diagnosis and treatment for cancers. In sub-Saharan Africa, many countries have fewer than one pathologist for every 1 million people, values far below those in high-income countries (HIC) such as the United States, where there is approximately 1 pathologist for every 20,600 people.1, 2, 3 Although expanding the pathology workforce remains essential, it would take around 400 years for Africa to reach the ratio of pathologist-to-population seen in the HICs at the current rate of workforce growth.4

Computational tools capable of augmenting pathology workflows, therefore, represent a promising strategy to expand diagnostic capacity and improve cancer care in resource-constrained settings.5, 6, 7, 8, 9 Ethically deployed computational pathology tools may provide beneficence by augmenting, rather than replacing, pathologists: triaging likely malignant cases, focusing review on high-yield regions, reducing avoidable screening workload, and expanding access to timely diagnostic interpretation while preserving human oversight and accountability. However, most digital pathology AI models and tools are developed and validated in HIC settings, raising concerns about their generalizability to LMIC contexts. Differences in patient demographics, disease presentation, tissue processing protocols, staining quality, slide digitization platforms, and imaging conditions can significantly alter histopathological appearance and impact algorithm performance.10, 11 These sources of variation raise important concerns regarding algorithmic robustness, fairness, and real-world clinical impact when AI tools are deployed outside the environments in which they were originally developed. Accordingly, there is a need for locally adaptable AI workflows that can be trained, tuned, and validated using locally derived histology images and expert local ground truth. Such approaches can reduce the risk of deploying “one-size-fits-all” machine-learning systems that fail silently under distribution shift.

To address this, we set out to evaluate whether an existing AI-based cancer screening pipeline originally developed in a North American setting could be locally optimized and validated in a Kenyan cohort, using colorectal adenocarcinoma as a use case. As the fifth most common malignancy in sub-Saharan Africa, colorectal cancer is associated with poor clinical outcomes, largely driven by advanced-stage presentation, underscoring the need for scalable early-detection strategies that can be implemented in settings with limited screening, pathology, and endoscopy infrastructure.12 Prior studies have demonstrated that this image-based computational pathology pipeline achieves strong performance for histopathological diagnostic entities, including malignancy detection across cancer types.13, 14, 15, 16 These studies establish the feasibility of using computer vision and machine-learning approaches to identify diagnostic image patterns in whole-slide images. Rigorous validation in the target setting is, however, necessary before such tools can be responsibly considered for assistive screening use in the LMIC setting.

The goal of this study was to assess whether local adaptation preserved colorectal adenocarcinoma screening performance relative to prior North American results and to evaluate the feasibility of locally adapting an image-based computational pathology workflow for use in a distinct global health population. We assessed whether local retraining, expert-adjudicated tile libraries, supervised malignancy scoring, and case-level aggregation could preserve sensitivity-forward screening performance despite differences in population, pre-analytic conditions, staining, scanning, and computational environment. In addition to evaluating diagnostic performance, this study describes the methodological and infrastructure lessons learned while adapting a whole-slide image analysis workflow for use in a resource-constrained global health setting.

Materials and methods

Study design and cohort

This study was a retrospective, multi-institutional external validation of a digital pathology-based machine-learning pipeline for colorectal adenocarcinoma detection. An assistive, case-level screening workflow was evaluated against expert pathologist interpretation. Cases were drawn from two Kenyan institutions selected to span contrasting practice settings: Aga Khan University Hospital Nairobi, a tertiary teaching and referral center in Nairobi, and Tenwek Hospital, a non-academic community hospital in rural Bomet. This pairing was intended to capture real-world variation in tissue handling, staining, and case mix across academic and community pathology services in sub-Saharan Africa.

The source cohort comprised 136 cases of histologically confirmed colonic adenocarcinoma diagnosed between 2016 and 2019, together with 20 normal colonic control cases used to supply benign reference tiles for model training. Each case underwent slide-level quality review by a gastrointestinal (GI) trained pathologist before inclusion. Of the 136 adenocarcinoma cases, 95 met quality-control criteria and were retained for analysis, as were 19 of the 20 control cases. Ground-truth case labels were taken from the original clinical pathology diagnoses rendered by board-certified pathologists before digitization and model development, ensuring that reference labels were independent of any algorithmic output.

Whole-slide imaging

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue blocks were sectioned and stained with hematoxylin and eosin according to institutional protocols. Slides were digitized at 40× magnification using the Grundium Ocus® 40 scanner (Grundium, Tampere, Finland), and whole-slide images were stored in SVS format.

Analytic overview: Two-stage workflow for training set enrichment and CNN classifier development

The workflow consisted of two sequential stages with distinct purposes. The first stage used feature enrichment to identify candidate regions for expert review and construction of an adjudicated training dataset. The second stage used this curated dataset to train a compact convolutional neural network (CNN), which was then evaluated as the deployable classifier in the validation experiments.

Stage 1: Tile generation and expert adjudication

Feature enrichment was performed using VIPR/SIVQ—a computational pipeline that resides in a fully operational application suite (VIPR Studio) and leverages a computer vision-based algorithm, Spatially Invariant Vector Quantization (SIVQ), to perform pixel-level classification of histology images. This pathologist-guided feature-matching system has been previously developed and validated in a North American cohort.13, 16 Detailed methods are described in Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Methods S1).

Briefly, each whole-slide image was partitioned into 512 × 512 pixel RGB tiles at approximately 10× effective magnification. Blank and low-information tiles, including background glass and fields without meaningful histological content, were discarded, and only tissue-containing (informative) tiles were retained for scoring. For each tile, a set of pathologist-selected archetype slits, short one-dimensional pixel sequences centered on diagnostic reference points, was matched against the tile to produce per-archetype similarity heatmaps, in which brighter signal indicates closer correspondence to the selected feature (Fig. 1). A GI-trained pathologist adjudicated these candidate tiles into two libraries: a tile was assigned to the adenocarcinoma-containing (positive) library when malignant glands or unequivocal invasive carcinoma were present in the tile field, and to the benign/non-neoplastic (negative) library otherwise. Negative tiles were drawn both from non-neoplastic fields within tumor cases and from the normal control cases. Adjudication was performed on a representative subset of 70 of the 95 quality-controlled adenocarcinoma cases, with approximately 200–500 tiles labeled per case, yielding an expert-curated tile dataset of high-confidence labels. Adjudication was limited to these 70 cases because the resulting tiles were already sufficient to train the classifier to strong performance on the validation cases, making further adjudication unnecessary. The remaining 25 quality-controlled cases were therefore not tiled; their whole-slide images were retained and could be incorporated to expand the dataset in future work. This expert-curated tile dataset served as the supervised training input for the classifier described below. Throughout, VIPR/SIVQ functioned only to prioritize tiles for review; final tile labels, and therefore the diagnostic ground truth, were determined entirely by the pathologist. Stage 2: Tile-level classifier

The tile-level classifier was a supervised CNN based on the MobileNet architecture, selected for its compact size and suitability for inference on commodity hardware. The model was initialized from a MobileNet image classifier exported from the Google Teachable Machine platform and retrained on the expert-curated tile libraries within a TensorFlow/Keras environment. During this initial training, the entire network was updated, including the convolutional backbone. Optimization used the AdamW optimizer with a cosine learning-rate decay schedule, and class weighting was applied to compensate for the imbalance between positive and negative tiles. The trained model was saved in the portable Keras .h5 format for downstream inference and deployment.

Because the MobileNet backbone operates on a fixed 224 × 224 pixel input, the 512 × 512 pixel tiles were resized to 224 × 224 and their pixel intensities normalized to the range [−1,1] before being passed to the model. For each tile, the classifier returns a continuous malignancy probability between 0 and 1, where values near 1 indicate adenocarcinoma-containing morphology and values near 0 indicate benign tissue. These tile-level probabilities were the input to all subsequent tile-level performance analyses and case-level aggregation.

The trained model is a single portable file and can be adapted to a new site by fine-tuning on locally labeled tiles, holding the convolutional backbone frozen and retraining only the classification head, so that previously learned image features are preserved. This local fine-tuning capability is provided as an interactive component of the Jupyter notebook workflow and is described in Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Methods S2).

Case-level aggregation and screening rule

Diagnostic decisions are rendered at the case level rather than the tile level, so tile-level malignancy probabilities were aggregated into a single case-level score using a Top-K mean pooling rule. For each case, all tiles were ranked by their CNN malignancy probability, the top 5% of tiles were retained, and the case-level score was computed as the mean probability of those top-ranked tiles. A case was classified as malignant when this score met or exceeded a decision threshold of 0.5 and benign otherwise. Using a fraction of the highest-scoring tiles, rather than a fixed count, accommodates the wide variation in tile yield across slides of differing tissue area.

This pooling strategy reflects the intended use of the pipeline as a sensitivity-forward screening tool. A truly malignant biopsy typically contains many tiles with high malignancy probability, whereas benign cases rarely produce more than isolated high-scoring tiles. Averaging only the top-ranked tiles, therefore, emphasizes the most diagnostically suspicious regions while remaining robust to extensive benign background, so that case-level sensitivity is preserved even when individual tiles are ambiguous or occasionally misclassified. A formal statistical justification, showing that this rule preserves case-level sensitivity while suppressing benign-case false positives, is provided in Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Methods S3).

To further reduce isolated false-positive tiles before aggregation, tile-level predictions could optionally be refined by binary morphological opening applied to the spatial grid of tile labels, which removes lone positive tiles unsupported by neighboring positive tiles while preserving spatially coherent malignant regions. The full morphological procedure is described in Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Methods S4).

Operating-point selection under a sensitivity constraint

The operating point was selected to maximize case-level sensitivity to satisfy the primary requirement of a screening tool before considering efficiency. A tile was treated as positive, when its CNN malignancy probability exceeded a tile-level threshold, and each case was summarized by its positive-tile evidence. In the fraction-based rule used in this study, a case was classified as malignant when the mean probability of its top-scoring 5% of tiles met or exceeded the case threshold:y^i=1ifSi≥τSi=1∣Top5%Ti∣∑t∈Top5%Tipitwhere Ti is the set of informative tiles in case i, pit is the tile-level malignancy probability, Si is the case-level score, and τ is the case decision threshold.

The decision parameters were selected by a grid search over the tile fraction and case threshold, evaluating case-level sensitivity and specificity at each combination. Among all parameterizations achieving 100% case-level sensitivity, the one minimizing false-positive cases was selected, yielding the operating point used throughout this work: mean pooling over the top 5% of tiles with a case threshold of τ = 0.5. This choice reflects the screening principle that a truly malignant case should yield a cluster of high-probability tiles, whereas benign cases should not. Because the adenocarcinoma cohort contained no benign cases, the 19 normal control cases served as the benign cases for this false-positive minimization; the selected operating point was therefore the one that flagged none of the controls while retaining 100% case-level sensitivity. Because the control cases informed operating-point selection in this way, the absence of flagged controls reflects the chosen operating point rather than an independent test of specificity. An equivalent count-based formulation, in which a case is called positive when the number of tiles exceeding the tile threshold meets a fixed count, was also evaluated; its definition and a comparison with the fraction-based rule are provided in Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Methods S3).

Performance metrics and validation

Model performance was assessed at both the tile and case levels. At the tile level, the CNN was evaluated against the expert-adjudicated labels using sensitivity, specificity, precision, accuracy, negative-predictive value, F1 score, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), computed by sweeping the tile-level decision threshold across its range. At the case level, the same metrics were applied to the pooled case scores described above, with case-level ground truth taken from the original pathology diagnoses described in the Study design and cohort section. The F1 score was emphasized as a compact summary of the balance between sensitivity and precision at the chosen operating point, consistent with the screening aim of preserving sensitivity while limiting false positives.

To guard against bias from any single data partition and to quantify stability, cases were partitioned at the case level into a 25% holdout set, reserved for final performance reporting, and a 75% development set. The development set was used for model selection and stability assessment through 5-fold cross-validation, in which the model was trained on four folds and evaluated on the held-out fold in turn; cross-validation results are reported as the mean and standard deviation across folds. The final model was then evaluated once on the untouched 25% holdout set. The full sequence of validation computations is given in Supplementary Methods S5.

Evaluation against expert interpretation

This evaluation compared an assistive machine-learning screening workflow against expert pathologist interpretation. The reference standard was diagnosis of colorectal adenocarcinoma by a GI-trained pathologist using whole-slide review, the established basis for clinical sign-out. Expert judgment served two complementary roles in this study: as the tile-level ground truth during training-set construction, and as the case-level benchmark comparator during evaluation. The test method was defined not as the tile classifier alone but as the complete composite pipeline, tile-level CNN scoring followed by the case-level Top-K pooling rule, because clinical decisions are rendered at the case level. Accordingly, the objective was not to match expert agreement on every intermediate tile, but to evaluate whether the pipeline achieved case-level performance comparable to expert interpretation for adenocarcinoma presence versus absence while providing operational advantages in throughput, triage, and scalable deployment. Consistent with this aim, the operating point was selected under a sensitivity-first operating-point selection strategy described above, reflecting the clinical principle that false negatives are unacceptable for a screening tool, whereas false positives primarily increase pathologist review burden.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to determine whether a colorectal adenocarcinoma screening pipeline originally developed in a North American setting could be locally adapted and validated in a Kenyan cohort as a sensitivity-forward assistive prescreening tool. The locally retrained VIPR/SIVQ-assisted CNN–Top-K pipeline preserved strong tile-level discrimination and case-level screening performance in this setting, supporting the feasibility of adapting a HIC-developed computational pathology workflow for use in a LMIC context. These results suggest that local retraining and validation can preserve clinically meaningful screening performance despite differences in tissue handling, staining, scanner platform, patient population, and computational infrastructure. Nevertheless, validation in the target setting remains necessary before future deployment, as variation in tissue processing, staining, scanner characteristics, and case mix can introduce clinically meaningful distribution shifts not fully captured by aggregate metrics.

This work presents a pragmatic approach to adapting image-based ML pipelines for LMIC and resource-constrained settings. Whereas previous studies have applied frozen HIC-trained models to LMIC data,17, 18, 19 the pipeline was adapted to the target Kenyan setting through local retraining and operating-point selection using Kenyan histology images and expert local ground truth. Local retraining is important because domain shift can arise from pre-analytic variables such as fixation, tissue processing, section quality, staining intensity, scanner optics, and image compression, as well as from population-level morphological variation and case-mix differences.11, 20, 21 Locally retraining the CNN on Kenyan tissue directly addresses this issue. In resource-constrained settings, adapting existing models to local data may be more feasible than developing a new model de novo, while still addressing domain shifts related to sample processing, scanner optics, infrastructure, and case mix.

The pipeline architecture operationalized a sensitivity-forward screening design. In resource-constrained screening contexts, false negatives carry far greater consequences than false positives: a missed cancer may delay staging, referral, treatment selection, and initiation of therapy, whereas a false-positive screen primarily increases pathologist review time, provided final diagnostic authority remains with a human expert. Consistent with this clinical principle, the operating point was selected to preserve case-level sensitivity before optimizing specificity or workload efficiency. VIPR/SIVQ first enriches for adenocarcinoma-rich regions to reduce annotation burden. Expert-adjudicated tiles are then used to train a supervised CNN to generate tile-level malignancy probabilities that are aggregated into case-level predictions using Top-K pooling. This design separates tile-level detection from case-level decision-making. Rather than requiring perfect classification of every field, the goal is to ensure that malignant cases generate multiple high-confidence positive tiles. This provides resilience to ambiguous regions, including tumor margins, diathesis, and desmoplastic response. Top-K pooling then leverages the observed separation between malignant and benign cases: negative controls produced far fewer high-scoring tiles, whereas true-positive cases remained well above the case-level threshold. In practice, this approach prioritizes cases and regions most likely to contain carcinoma while reducing low-yield review burden in LMIC pathology settings. The case-level AUROC of 1.0 reflects the aggregation design rather than overfitting. Tile-level discrimination was strong but imperfect (AUROC 0.966), and Top-K pooling converts this tile-level signal into a clean case-level separation by averaging only the most confident tiles, where malignant and benign cases differ by more than an order of magnitude in high-scoring tile burden. The perfect case-level separation is thus an expected consequence of aggregating a strongly discriminative tile-level classifier across many tiles per case.

A central motivation for this work is the persistent and severe shortage of practicing pathologists across sub-Saharan Africa, where many countries have only a handful of practicing specialists or no verified country-level total, and only a small number report more than 100 pathologists nationally (Fig. 6). This imbalance is especially consequential for cancer care, where timely tissue diagnosis is a prerequisite for staging, referral, treatment selection, and initiation of therapy. Longitudinal workforce modeling underscores that conventional training expansion alone is unlikely to close this gap in the foreseeable future, even over a 100-year horizon (Fig. 7). This reality creates a need for complementary capacity-building strategies that extend the functional reach of existing pathologists. The goal is not to remove the pathologist from the diagnostic process, but rather to reduce avoidable workload, prioritize the most diagnostically relevant fields, support triage, and allow scarce expert time to be focused where it adds the greatest value. A high-sensitivity screening pipeline can help identify cases or regions most likely to contain malignancy, allowing pathologists to review enriched regions more efficiently while retaining final diagnostic authority. By shifting the pathologist's task from unaided whole-slide review to directed review of pre-flagged regions, such a workflow could substantially reduce per-case reading time. As a rough illustration, a case that might require 10–15 min of conventional review could be reduced to roughly 30 s (less than a minute) of targeted confirmation, an efficiency gain of approximately 20- to 30-fold. In line with this motivation, a key design feature of this work is the staged architecture that concentrates the resource-intensive, expert-intensive effort into a single upstream investment. In the first stage, VIPR/SIVQ-based feature enrichment is used to prioritize candidate tiles with adenocarcinoma-associated morphology, after which a GI-trained pathologist adjudicates these candidate fields to generate a high-confidence, locally curated training corpus. The curated dataset is then used to train a compact MobileNet-based CNN, which is exported as a single portable Keras (.h5) model artifact for downstream inference. Once this model artifact has been generated, deployment no longer requires the VIPR/SIVQ enrichment environment or repetition of the full expert-curation workflow; instead, downstream sites can apply the trained classifier directly to extracted whole-slide image tiles. Because the deployable model uses a lightweight MobileNet backbone, inference can be performed on commodity hardware without a dedicated GPU, reducing computational and operational barriers to implementation. The workflow also supports transfer-learning-based local adaptation, in which the convolutional feature extractor is frozen, and only the terminal classification layers are retrained using a modest site-specific tile set. This allows local calibration to staining characteristics, scanner optics, tissue-processing variation, and population-specific morphology without full-network retraining or access to the original VIPR/SIVQ pipeline. Combined with local digitization on a portable whole-slide scanner and regionally located cloud storage that keeps imaging data within its jurisdiction of origin, these choices are intended to make the pipeline practical to adopt and scale in resource-constrained pathology settings.

This deployment model is reinforced by a democratizing workflow design that uses accessible components to address common barriers to computational pathology adoption in resource-constrained settings, including cost, technical expertise, infrastructure requirements, and dependence on proprietary or institutionally exclusive systems. The pipeline is delivered as a modular Jupyter notebook workflow built on broadly available tools such as Teachable Machine and Keras/TensorFlow, lowering the threshold for sites to generate local histology data, train or fine-tune models, and validate performance within their own lab context. The workflow also describes the full deployment stack, including local digitization using a portable Grundium Ocus scanner, regionally appropriate image storage, data-governance compliance, and reproducible Jupyter notebook-based execution. In this study, images were hosted on a cloud-based Microsoft Azure server with redundant storage located in Microsoft's South Africa data center, consistent with Kenya Data Protection Act requirements. More broadly, however, the same deployment model can be adapted to future use cases by using locally or regionally appropriate storage infrastructure with appropriate technical, organizational, and regulatory safeguards. Infrastructure and deployment details are almost universally absent from LMIC AI studies, making this a significant contribution.22, 23 Together, these choices are intended to make the pipeline practical to adopt, inspect, validate, and scale in resource-constrained pathology settings.

The intended use of the VIPR/SIVQ-assisted CNN–Top-K screening pipeline is an assistive system embedded within governance structures that preserve human oversight and accountability. Beneficence requires that implementation avoids a double standard in which LMIC deployment accepts weaker safeguards or lower performance thresholds than would be tolerated in HIC contexts. Local regulatory context, including the evolving requirements for digital health governance and data stewardship, should be treated as a design input rather than an afterthought, informing policies for data handling, hosting locality, access controls, and documentation of clinical use. Four governance pillars should guide future deployment: (1) pathologist final sign-out; (2) transparency about intended use and limitations; (3) traceability and auditability of algorithm outputs; and (4) continuous performance monitoring for drift. This orientation is consistent with WHO-aligned ethical principles for AI in health, emphasizing human oversight, safety, equity, and accountability.9

Several limitations should be considered. The evaluated cohort was modest, including 95 quality-controlled cases and a small holdout set, with 19 negative controls available for false-positive optimization; therefore, the perfect case-level performance should be interpreted cautiously, particularly because no formal power calculation or pre-specified non-inferiority margin was included. The task was also limited to binary adenocarcinoma-versus-benign classification and did not systematically test difficult diagnostic mimics, grading, subtyping, mucinous variants, dysplasia, or inflammatory backgrounds. This was beyond the scope of the study. Because the pipeline was locally retrained on Kenyan data and evaluated on an internal holdout set, the study represents local adaptation with internal validation rather than external validation of a frozen model on fully independent data. Generalizability beyond two Kenyan institutions, one scanner platform, and retrospective evaluation remains unknown.

Future directions should include multi-institutional, multi-country validation efforts, representing a broader sub-Saharan cohort, while also targeting multiple organ systems and diagnostic entities. This expansion would directly test the central hypothesis that image-based ML algorithms can be made truly generalizable when trained and validated across sufficiently broad cohorts that span the real-world diversity of biology and pre-analytics. Future evaluations should also use pre-defined performance targets aligned with intended use. For pre-screening or triage, sensitivity-forward criteria remain appropriate, but specificity, false-discovery rate, and workload impact should be explicitly reported. Larger prospective studies would also allow formal non-inferiority testing against expert interpretation with adequate power calculations and pre-specified margins, as well as benchmarking against contemporary pathology foundation models and attention-based multiple-instance learning architectures.

Prospective deployment studies should also move beyond algorithmic performance metrics to evaluate operational and clinical impact, such as time-to-sign-out, backlog reduction, pathologist workload/cognitive fatigue proxies, and downstream patient impact (time-to-treatment, referral patterns), while preserving quality and safety. Implementation studies should also include lifecycle governance from the outset, monitoring plans to detect changes due to stain lot shifts, scanner recalibration, workflow changes, or epidemiological shifts. Periodic revalidation, controlled model update procedures, version documentation, and site-specific performance dashboards will be necessary to ensure that models remain calibrated over time. Equity and safety assessments should evaluate performance stability across clinically important variants and under variable slide quality. Use error analysis to characterize failure modes and to guide targeted data augmentation and retraining.

Conclusion

In summary, this study supports the feasibility of locally adapting a previously developed computational pathology pipeline for colorectal adenocarcinoma screening in a Kenyan cohort. The findings suggest that model localization, supported by local histology, expert adjudication, regional infrastructure, and sensitivity-forward case-level aggregation, can help preserve screening performance while addressing some sources of domain shift. At the same time, the results reinforce the broader principle that computational pathology tools should earn generalizability through rigorous, context-specific validation rather than assume it from performance in high-income–country cohorts. With larger prospective and multi-site validation, locally adapted digital pathology workflows may become an important component of pathology capacity-building in resource-constrained global health settings.
