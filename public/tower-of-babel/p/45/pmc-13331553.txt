Dose‐dependent pathogenicity and inflammatory imbalance in severely immunodeficient mice infected with mpox virus
Authors: Na Li, Ziqing Jia, Zhe Cong, Jianrong Ma, Jiahan Lu, Yongzhi Hou, Fei Wang, Jiasen Yang, Ting Chen, Jingjing Zhang, Dong Zhang, Jing Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe global spread of mpox continues to pose a threat to public health, with immunocompromised patients being at greater risk for severe disease. This study aimed to establish a lethal severe infection model using NPG (NOD.Cg‐Prkdc scid Il2rg tm1 /Vst) mice, a strain with profound immunodeficiency, to simulate severe mpox in immunocompromised patients and investigate disease progression, viral replication kinetics, tissue tropism, and immune responses. In our study, mice were inoculated with three doses of mpox virus (high: 2 × 107, medium: 2 × 106, low: 2 × 105 TCID50 [50% tissue culture infective dose]) or phosphate‐buffered saline as a negative control. Body weight, clinical signs, and survival were monitored. Viral loads in plasma and tissues, histopathological changes, and inflammatory responses were evaluated to systematically characterize the pathogenicity of mpox virus in severely immunodeficient mice. The results showed that NPG mice were highly susceptible to mpox virus, showing a clear dose‐dependent response. The high‐dose group exhibited 100% lethality, and the medium‐dose group had a 50% survival rate, whereas the low‐dose and control groups survived throughout the study. Plasma and tissue viral loads, as well as histopathological severity, followed a dose‐dependent pattern. Viremia remained persistently high, and animals that died at different time points exhibited uniformly high viral loads across multiple tissues. By day 10 postinfection, markedly elevated levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines were detected in plasma, whereas anti‐inflammatory cytokines remained largely uninduced. We found that the infection of severely immunodeficient mice with mpox virus results in dose‐dependent lethality, persistent high viral loads, and a pronounced pro−/anti‐inflammatory imbalance. These findings provide important insights into the pathogenesis of mpox in immunocompromised populations and develop a practical animal model for evaluating therapeutic interventions.Keywords: inflammatory response, lethal model, mpox virus, NPG (NOD.Cg‐Prkdc scid Il2rg tm1 /Vst) mice, viremia

INTRODUCTION

Mpox, caused by monkeypox virus (MPXV), has emerged as a global public health threat since the 2022 outbreak. 1 , 2 Whereas immunocompetent individuals typically experience self‐limited disease, immunocompromised populations, particularly those with untreated or advanced human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), are at substantially increased risk for severe outcomes. 1 , 3 , 4 , 5 Epidemiological data highlight this vulnerability. During a 2025 outbreak in Sierra Leone, up to 83% of hospitalized mpox patients were living with HIV, most with CD4+ T‐cell counts <200 cells/μL. In Africa, between January 2024 and June 2025, 148 308 suspected and 40 674 confirmed mpox cases were reported, with 1816 deaths (fatality rate: 1.2%). 6 More recently, six fatal cases of mpox coinfected with advanced HIV were reported in Beijing, China, with a median CD4+ T‐cell count of 51 cells/μL. 3

Clinically, immunocompromised patients experience more severe manifestations than immunocompetent individuals, including extensive mucocutaneous lesions, necrotic skin involvement, secondary bacterial infections, and multiorgan dissemination. 3 , 7 , 8 Fatal cases often present with complications such as pneumonia, intestinal obstruction, and sepsis, ultimately leading to multiorgan failure. 9 , 10 Therefore, understanding the pathogenesis of mpox virus in immunocompromised hosts and establishing an animal model that mimics severe clinical disease are essential for exploring effective intervention strategies.

An ideal animal model should combine high reproducibility, human‐relevant disease phenotypes, cost‐effectiveness, and suitability for high‐throughput screening. Nonhuman primate models are the gold standard for simulating human mpox infection, 11 but their high cost, complex handling, ethical constraints, and low throughput limit large‐scale use. 12 , 13 Mouse models offer a practical alternative due to affordability and well‐defined genetic backgrounds. However, most standard immunocompetent strains, such as C57BL/6, are largely resistant to MPXV and develop only mild or no clinical signs, 14 making it difficult to replicate severe human pathology. 15 To overcome this, researchers have turned to mice with specific genetic backgrounds or immune deficiencies, including CAST/EiJ mice as a viable immunocompetent lethal model. 14 The use of severely immunodeficient mouse models has become an important approach to study viral replication and pathogenicity in the absence of effective adaptive immunity.

In this study, we utilized severely immunodeficient NPG (NOD.Cg‐Prkdc scid Il2rg tm1 /Vst) mice, characterized by triple defects in T cells, B cells, and NK cells that prevent adaptive immune responses, to investigate the pathogenicity, viral dynamics, and inflammatory responses after MPXV infection. We provide a stable, efficient, and clinically applicable preclinical research tool for understanding the pathogenic mechanisms of MPXV and developing novel prevention and treatment strategies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Virus and animals

The MPXV clade IIb (MPXV‐B.1‐China‐C‐Tan‐CQ01) was obtained from Prof. Wenjie Tan. 16 The virus was cultured in Vero‐E6 cells, and titers were determined using the plaque assay. All female NPG (NOD.Cg‐Prkdc scid Il2rg tm1 /Vst) mice used in the experiments were obtained from Beijing Vitalstar Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All animals were housed in specific‐pathogen‐free‐grade independent ventilation cages, with all procedures conducted in an Animal Biosafety Level 3 laboratory in strict compliance with animal ethics and welfare guidelines. All the animals were provided with sufficient food and water. A humane endpoint was determined as greater than 25% weight loss or the presence of any signs of acute distress.

50% tissue culture infective dose assays

Viral titers were determined using the 50% tissue culture infective dose (TCID50) method. For tissue samples, sample preprocessing is required. Tissue samples were weighed, placed in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), and homogenized using a tissue homogenizer. The homogenates were subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles and then centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected for titration. The supernatant, plasma, and tissue homogenates were serially diluted two‐ or four‐fold in Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium and added to Vero‐E6 cells in 96‐well plates. After incubation at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 2 days, cytopathic effects were examined microscopically. TCID50 values per gram of spleen or per milliliter of plasma were calculated using the Reed–Muench method.

Pathogenicity and survival rate of MPXV infection

Twenty‐four 4‐ to 6‐week‐old NPG mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6 per group). Groups A, B, and C were intraperitoneally administered 200 μL of MPXV containing 2 × 107 (high dose), 2 × 106 (medium dose), and 2 × 105 (low dose) TCID50, respectively. Group D (control group) received 200 μL of sterile PBS. Clinical symptoms, body weight changes (expressed as percentage of initial weight), and survival status were observed and recorded daily for 20 days. Mice that were nearing death or had reached predetermined humane endpoints were sacrificed (e.g., weight loss exceeding 25%), and surviving mice were euthanized at the end of the experiment. Blood samples were collected aseptically for plasma preparation, and tissues, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, brain, stomach, and intestine, were harvested. Some tissues were used for viral load detection, whereas others were used for pathological analysis.

Virus replication dynamics of MPXV

A hundred 10‐week‐old NPG mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 25 per group) and intraperitoneally inoculated with high, medium, and low doses of MPXV. At predetermined time points (0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 days postinfection [dpi]), five mice were randomly selected from each group and euthanized. The plasma and tissue samples were collected at different time points to dynamically monitor viral load changes.

Quantitative viral load assay

The MPXV DNA in plasma and tissues was quantified using real‐time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). Viral DNA was extracted and purified using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, 51106). qPCR was performed using a TaqMan gene expression principal component mixture (Applied Biosystems, 4369016) on the ABI 7500 real‐time PCR system. The following are the cycling schemes and primers for MPXV. The qPCR cycling conditions were as follows: 95°C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min. The specific primers and probes that target the conserved region F3L of MPXV were as follows: F3L forward 5′‐CTCATTGATTTTTCGCGGGATA‐3′, F3L reverse 5′‐GACGATACTCCTCCTCGTTGGT‐3′, and F3L probe 5′‐FAM‐CATCAGAATCTGTAGGCCGT‐MGB‐3′. A standard curve was established to convert Ct values into viral DNA copies, with results expressed as log (copies/mL).

Histopathological examination

To characterize organ‐specific pathogenesis, lung and spleen tissues were harvested from representative animals in each cohort at the time of death or when euthanized. Specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned (4 μm) for hematoxylin and eosin staining. Whole‐slide digitization was performed using an LG‐S80 scanner (Servicebio), and morphological analysis was conducted using SAI Viewer software (version 1.0.9).

Histopathology was evaluated in a blinded manner to minimize bias. A semiquantitative scoring system based oninternational harmonization of nomenclature and diagnostic criteria for lesions (INHAND) guidelines was employed to assess tissue injury severity (scale 0–4). 17 Pulmonary pathology was graded on alveolar septal thickening, inflammatory infiltration, hemorrhage, and vascular congestion. Splenic pathology was assessed based on white pulp atrophy (lymphoid depletion), red pulp expansion, and granulocytic infiltration.

Chemokine analysis

The plasma of NPG mice at 10 dpi with MPXV was incubated in a water bath at 56°C for 30 min for inactivation, and the ProcartaPlex Mouse Custom 12‐Plex Panel Kit (ThermoFisher, PPX‐12) was used for measuring cytokines and chemokines according to the manufacturer's instructions. The data were collected on a Luminex 200 Instrument System (Luminex Corporation) and quantified by comparing to a standard curve.

Statistical analysis

Graphical presentation and statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software). Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Differences among samples were analyzed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Significance levels were defined as follows: ns, not significant; *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

RESULTS

MPXV infection induces dose‐dependent death and viremia in NPG mice

To evaluate the pathogenicity of MPXV in NPG mice, we administered three different doses (2 × 107 TCID50, 2 × 106 TCID50, and 2 × 105 TCID50) of the virus intraperitoneally and monitored body weight and survival for 20 consecutive days (Figure 1A). A control group was also injected with the same volume of PBS in parallel. On day 4 postinfection, mice exposed to the high‐dose MPXV (group A, 2 × 107 TCID50) began to experience rapid and sustained weight loss. Mice in group B (2 × 106 TCID50) also developed a measurable degree of weight reduction beginning on 10 dpi, whereas those in group C (2 × 105 TCID50) exhibited no significant change in body weight (Figure 1B). Mortality in group A was observed on 11, 12, and 17 dpi (Figure 1C,D). In group B, three mice died on day 19. By the end of the experiment, all mice in group A had died, whereas three mice survived in group B. All mice in groups C and D survived until the end of the experiment.

(B) Percentage change in body weight relative to baseline (day 0) over a 20‐day observation period. Data are presented as mean ± SD (standard deviation). To determine the MPXV infection status in all mice, plasma was collected from each animal at death or at the experimental endpoint for viral load quantification. Despite variations in the timing of blood collection, mice in each group exhibited similar plasma viral loads (Figure 1E). Interestingly, there was no significant difference in viral load between groups A and B (both around 107 copies/mL, p = 0.709), suggesting that the two distinct viral doses resulted in comparable levels of viral replication. In contrast, the six mice infected with the low‐dose MPXV exhibited a significantly reduced plasma viral load. The results showed that NPG mice were highly susceptible to MPXV, with clinical characteristics and survival rates showing significant dose‐dependent relationships.

MPXV induces systemic infection and significant pathological organ damage in NPG mice

To investigate the viremia and distribution of MPXV in NPG mice, we detected the viral load in multiple vital organs at the end of the experiment (humanitarian endpoint or day 20). MPXV‐DNA was detectable in all eight tissues (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, brain, stomach, and intestines) tested in all three infection groups (Figure 2A), demonstrating that MPXV effectively overcomes local tissue barriers after intraperitoneal injection and establishes a comprehensive systemic infection. A comparative analysis of viral loads in tissues revealed particularly high concentrations in the spleen and lungs, averaging 105–106 copies/mL in high‐dose groups. Replication‐competent virus was detected in mice from the virus‐infected groups, with high viral titers observed in the lungs and spleens of the high‐dose group (Figure 2B). Despite variations in time to death, the viral loads in the plasma and tissues of these mice were highly consistent within each group. This uniformity suggested that stable viral replication was maintained in mice between 10 and 20 days after MPXV infection. Pathological examination further revealed significant tissue damage in both the lungs and spleens of MPXV‐infected mice (Figure 2C). In the high‐dose group, the lungs exhibited considerable granulocyte infiltration, widespread moderate thickening of alveolar walls, and widening of alveolar septa. And the spleens exhibited a significant infiltration of megakaryocytes and granulocytes. To sum up, these findings demonstrate that MPXV replicates and spreads effectively in NPG mice, affecting nearly all major organs and causing severe systemic infection.

Viral replication dynamics in MPXV‐infected NPG mice

To further determine the replication pattern of the virus in vivo, we conducted multi–time point continuous sampling. Mice were euthanized at 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 days post‐MPXV infection (dpi), and the viral load in plasma and multiple tissues and organs was analyzed to study the dynamic changes in the virus in NPG mice (Figure 3A). The trend of body weight changes was consistent with the results described earlier, with the high‐dose group (group A) exhibiting a sustained decrease in body weight (Figure 1B). The dynamic monitoring of plasma viral load (Figure 3B) revealed that the virus rapidly entered the bloodstream and began replication after infection. Both the high‐dose group (group A) and the medium‐dose group (group B) developed viremia on 5 dpi, with plasma viral load surging sharply to peak levels of ~107.5 and 106.5 copies/mL, respectively, about 15 dpi and remaining elevated thereafter. The low‐dose group (group C) exhibited lower viremia levels, fluctuating ~104 copies/mL.

Five mice per group were sequentially euthanized at 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 days postinfection (dpi). (A) Temporal changes in body weight throughout the course of infection. (B) Kinetics of viral load in plasma over time and time‐course analysis of viral loads in tissues: heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, brain, stomach, and intestines. Data represent mean ± SD (standard deviation).*p < 0.05.

The viral load dynamics in tissues followed a similar pattern. In all tested tissues, viral loads increased significantly over time, indicating sustained viral replication. The high‐ and medium‐dose groups exhibited faster replication rates and higher viral loads, typically reaching a plateau by day 10 or 15. The spleen and lungs were the primary sites of viral proliferation, with consistently high viral loads at all time points. Particularly, although the brain tissue viral load also increased, its growth rate and final level were relatively lower than those in peripheral organs, suggesting the blood–brain barrier partially delayed viral invasion. In summary, kinetic data revealed the rapid, efficient replication and establishment of persistent infection by MPXV in immunodeficient NPG mice.

Pro−/anti‐inflammatory imbalance in MPXV‐infected NPG mice

To evaluate the level of inflammatory responses in NPG mice, the plasma of NPG mice infected with MPXV at 10 dpi was collected to test the level of pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory cytokines. The plasma levels of inflammatory cytokines in NPG mice exhibited a dose‐dependent pattern across the four groups (groups A–D). Group A, which received the highest viral dose, exhibited the highest levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (Figure 4A), including interferon‐gamma (IFN‐γ), granulocyte‐macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (GM‐CSF), interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β), IL‐6, tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), and IL‐12p70. These values progressively decreased in groups B, C, and D, corresponding to lower inoculation doses. In contrast, the anti‐inflammatory cytokines IL‐10 and IL‐13 remained at relatively low levels across all groups (Figure 4B), with only marginal differences between groups. Particularly, even in the high‐dose group (group A), anti‐inflammatory cytokine levels did not increase proportionally to the robust pro‐inflammatory response, indicating a pronounced imbalance between pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory responses after MPXV infection in severely immunodeficient NPG mice.

DISCUSSION

The ongoing global circulation of MPXV and the emergence of more virulent strains (e.g., clade Ia, Ib) impose a significant and growing health burden worldwide, particularly in African populations with limited vaccine access. 8 , 18 , 19 , 20 Clinical evidence from HIV‐positive individuals 21 and our previous studies of simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV)‐infected nonhuman primates 22 indicates that immunodeficiency compromises both humoral and cellular immune responses against mpox virus, underscoring the immediate need to study the pathogenic mechanisms of MPXV in immunocompromised populations and develop intervention measures. This study established a severe immunodeficient mouse model of MPXV infection. The model serves as an effective tool for investigating severe monkeypox in immunocompromised individuals and provides a valuable platform for systematically observing viral replication alongside host immune and inflammatory responses.

The unique viral kinetics and inflammatory response profile observed in NPG mice after MPXV infection provided crucial insights into the pathological progression of viral infection under the extreme condition of near‐complete absence of adaptive immunity and innate lymphoid cells. Unlike the typical curve of viral load (rise‐peak‐clearance) observed in wild‐type or partially immunodeficient mouse models, 23 the plasma viral load in NPG mice reached a high level and then entered a stable plateau phase, which persisted until 20 dpi. This phenomenon strongly suggests that in the absence of T‐, B‐, and NK cells, the host completely loses the ability to effectively clear virus‐infected cells and neutralize free virions. 24 This persistently high level of viral antigen constitutes a continuous and intense stimulus to the residual innate immune system (e.g., macrophages, dendritic cells). It is this unremitting stimulation that directly drives the significantly elevated and sustained high viremia we observed. 25

MPXV infection in NPG mice induced a pronounced imbalance between pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory responses, characterized by significantly elevated levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (IFN‐γ, GM‐CSF, TNF‐α, IL‐6, IL‐1β, IL‐12p70) in plasma and largely undetectable levels of anti‐inflammatory cytokines (IL‐10, IL‐13). The absence of adaptive immune cells in NPG mice, particularly regulatory T cells, eliminates the major source of IL‐10 and disrupts the negative feedback loop that normally restrains inflammation. 26 Such an inflammatory imbalance is consistent with clinical observations in immunocompromised patients with severe mpox, who often exhibit excessive inflammation and poor outcomes. 4 , 27 These findings highlight that in the absence of adaptive immunity, uncontrolled innate immune activation drives lethal pathology, underscoring the importance of immune regulation in mpox pathogenesis.

The sustained high viral load in NPG mice infected with mpox virus, coupled with an extremely robust and persistent inflammatory response, renders this model a valuable experimental system for mimicking the most severe clinical manifestations of human mpox. Although NPG mice are unable to recapitulate the prominent cutaneous lesions observed in human mpox infections, this model partially reproduces the key features of severe mpox as documented in patients with advanced AIDS (characterized by profound CD4+ T‐cell depletion) or those undergoing intense iatrogenic immunosuppression, including systemic inflammatory response syndrome and high mortality rates. Therefore, this model serves as a valuable platform for dissecting the mechanisms underlying severe mpox‐associated immunopathology and for evaluating the combined efficacy of novel antivirals and immunomodulatory strategies in immunocompromised hosts.

CONCLUSION

In summary, the lethal severely immunocompromised mouse model of MPXV infection established in this study provides important supporting data for understanding viral replication and inflammatory responses under immunocompromised conditions, and offers a potential research platform for developing therapeutic strategies against mpox in severely immunosuppressed populations.
