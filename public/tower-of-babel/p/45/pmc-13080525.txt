Coronary artery calcification continues to be a major challenge in percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), often resulting in suboptimal stent expansion, procedural complications, and poor clinical outcomes.1 Intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) has emerged as a new and effective method for modifying calcium in heavily calcified coronary lesions. This technology emits pulsatile mechanical energy as sonic pressure waves that selectively break both superficial and deep calcium deposits within the vessel wall, while maintaining soft tissue integrity.2 A well-known phenomenon related to IVL is the occurrence of ventricular ectopic beats, called “shocktopics,” which result from mechano-electric coupling, where acoustic pressure waves activate stretch-sensitive ion channels in cardiomyocyte membranes.3

While these interactions are well described with established systems, it is essential to document the clinical performance of emerging IVL platforms. This case illustrates frequent ventricular myocardial capture with near R-on-T phenomena using the Shunmei IVL system (Shunmei Medical Co Ltd), demonstrating that mechanical-to-electrical coupling is a consistent feature across different lithotripsy technologies.

Case Presentation

A 74-year-old woman with a history of coronary artery disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and hypertension presented with exertional angina. Her prior history included PCI to the right coronary artery. Physical examination revealed a blood pressure of 145/95 mm Hg and a grade 2/6 diastolic murmur at the mitral focus. A 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) revealed pathological Q waves in the inferior leads, with a normal sinus rhythm. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction of 56% with mild basal inferior hypokinesia and mild to moderate mitral and tricuspid regurgitation. The mitral valve area was >2 cm2 (using the pressure half-time method). Coronary angiography showed a patent right coronary artery stent and a severely calcified lesion in the mid left anterior descending artery extending to the distal left main coronary artery (Video 1). The heart team planned PCI. The patient was taken to the procedure table for PCI, with an initial heart rate of 56 beats/min. Throughout the procedure, the patient remained in sinus rhythm. A 7-F sheath and guiding catheter were used via the right femoral artery. Intravascular ultrasound revealed 360° circumferential calcification in the mid left anterior descending artery and 60% stenosis at the distal left main coronary artery (Video 2). Noncompliant balloons failed to fracture the calcification, prompting IVL with a 3.5 × 15 mm Shunmei IVL catheter.

During IVL pulses, intermittent ventricular myocardial capture approaching an R-on-T phenomenon was observed (Video 3, Figure 1). A total of 70 pulses were delivered during the IVL therapy. Hemodynamically, a modest reduction in systolic blood pressure (∼10 mm Hg) occurred during these cycles, without clinical compromise or symptoms. After IVL, a calcium fracture was achieved, but a distal dissection caused reduced flow (Video 4). Two overlapping sirolimus-eluting stents (3.0 × 22 mm and 3.5 × 22 mm) were deployed, followed by final proximal optimization with a 4.5 × 12 mm noncompliant balloon. Final angiography showed optimal results, with TIMI flow grade 3 (Video 5) confirmed by intravascular ultrasound (Figure 2).

The patient was discharged on aspirin (100 mg once daily) and clopidogrel (75 mg once daily) and remained asymptomatic at the 1-month follow-up.

Discussion

Mechanism of intravascular lithotripsy and myocardial capture

IVL fractures vascular calcium by converting electrical energy into sonic pressure waves via spark-gap discharge.4 A well-recognized consequence of this mechanical energy is “shocktopics,” or ventricular myocardial capture, which arises from the activation of stretch-sensitive ion channels in the cardiomyocyte membranes.5,6 Our observation of this phenomenon with the Shunmei IVL system confirms that such electrophysiological interactions represent a technology-wide class effect rather than a device-specific characteristic.

Predictors and risk factors

IVL-induced capture has been reported in up to 77.8% of cases.3 Predictors include a heart rate of ≤60 beats/min (OR: 16.3; P = 0.004), male sex, and total number of IVL pulses.3,5,7 The mechanism for increased susceptibility in bradycardia may relate to longer diastolic intervals, providing more opportunity for mechano-electric coupling.

In our case, a distal dissection occurred after IVL application, leading to transient flow limitation. While frequent capture episodes were observed during this period, we emphasize that the primary mechanism for these ventricular beats remains the mechanical-to-electrical coupling inherent to IVL technology. Although the patient experienced chest pain suggesting potential myocardial stress, the absence of definitive ST-segment shifts precludes a direct causal link between ischemia and an altered threshold for myocardial capture. Ultimately, whether ischemic conditions synergistically lower the threshold for IVL-induced arrhythmias remains an observation that requires further validation through objective data and larger clinical series.

Hemodynamic effects and safety

The hemodynamic consequences of capture are generally mild and transient, with modest blood pressure reductions (10-35 mm Hg) that resolve upon restoration of the intrinsic rhythm.3 No sustained arrhythmias have been reported in major trials such as DISRUPT CAD III.8 Our case similarly showed frequent capture without sustained arrhythmia or hemodynamic compromise. However, isolated case reports have documented rare but serious arrhythmic complications. Two cases of ventricular fibrillation have been reported when IVL-induced capture beats coincided with the T-wave of the preceding cardiac cycle, highlighting the theoretical risk of R-on-T phenomenon.9,10 Risk factors for these rare events include bradycardia, prolonged QT interval, pre-existing ventricular ectopy, and treatment of dominant coronary vessels during acute ischemia.9 While ventricular capture is the most common manifestation, rare instances of supraventricular disturbances, such as de novo atrial fibrillation or flutter, have also been reported during IVL procedures.11,12

In our case, the patient's baseline heart rate was 56 beats/min. A total of 70 pulses were delivered during the IVL therapy; hemodynamically, a modest reduction in systolic blood pressure (∼10 mm Hg) occurred during these cycles, without clinical compromise or symptoms. This remains consistent with the reported literature, where hemodynamic consequences of capture are generally mild and transient.

Device interactions and clinical implications

Recent data show that IVL is safe in patients with cardiac implantable electronic devices, with no functional interference apart from transient pacing threshold changes.13 Nonetheless, pre- and postprocedure interrogation is recommended.

Clinical management and best practices

Continuous ECG and arterial pressure monitoring are mandatory during IVL. Caution is advised in bradycardic patients, in whom capture risk is highest. Awareness of potential R-on-T phenomena should prompt readiness for defibrillation if sustained arrhythmias occur.

Future directions

Further comparative studies between IVL systems could clarify whether capture characteristics differ by manufacturer. Future development of ECG-synchronized IVL systems could reduce R-on-T risk. Additionally, standardized safety protocols for patients with cardiac implantable electronic devices are warranted.

Conclusions

This case documents to our knowledge the first reported instance of frequent ventricular capture with near R-on-T phenomena using the Shunmei IVL system. Despite frequent capture, no adverse arrhythmias occurred, supporting the benign nature of this class effect. Operators should maintain vigilant ECG and hemodynamic monitoring, particularly in high-risk patients. Ongoing evaluation of novel IVL platforms will refine procedural safety and expand the understanding of mechano-electric interactions during coronary lithotripsy.
