Adapting a commercial sample extraction protocol for biosafety level 3/4 compatible plasma metabolomics analysis
Authors: J Brandsma, JW Thompson, KL Schully, JG Chenoweth, P Genzor, S Krishnan, DA Striegel, LSt John-Williams, A Moseley, G Oduro, N Adams, T Vantha, EL Tsalik, CW Woods, DV Clark
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Comparison between extraction methods for metabolomics analysis of BSL-3/4 samples.•Combining MPLEx and AbsoluteIDQ p180 protocols is a viable option for BSL-3/4 samples.•Sample dilution and extraction efficiency differ from the unmodified p180 protocol.Keywords: Metabolomics, Sample extraction, Infectious diseases, Biosafety, High-risk pathogens

Introduction

Sepsis is a complex, multifaceted organ dysfunction syndrome caused by a dysregulated host response to infection [1]. It affects patients of all ages across multiple comorbidities and underlying diagnoses and often has long-lasting health impacts. Despite treatment, around 10–20 % of patients progress to severe sepsis and septic shock, in which the underlying circulatory, cellular, and metabolic damage becomes profound enough to significantly increase mortality (up to 50 % in some settings) [2]. Consequently, the global health burden of sepsis remains high, with an estimated 48.9 million cases and 11.0 million deaths each year [3], and significant disparities in incidence and outcomes between high- and low-to-middle-income countries (LMICs) [4]. From a military health perspective, severe infections and their complications continue to disproportionately impact operational readiness and costs through the loss of duty days and high treatment rates and medical evacuations [[5], [6], [7]]. In particular, deploying immunologically naïve hosts to austere environments with endemic and/or high-risk pathogens presents a significant challenge to warfighter fitness and readiness [8,9]. Early diagnosis and appropriate clinical management are critical for reducing the risks of sepsis progression and mortality [2], but are hampered by a lack of validated tools and biomarkers to guide clinical decision-making. This unmet need is most acute in poorly resourced environments, including healthcare settings throughout LMICs, pandemic and mass-casualty scenarios, and prolonged field care during military operations [10].

The now widely adopted “Sepsis-3” definition shifts emphasis away from host inflammation, defining sepsis as a “life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection” [11]. This focus on homeostasis implies a major role for metabolism in both the development and resolution of sepsis [12,13]. However, few metabolic host biomarkers have been translated into routine clinical practice. The Biocrates AbsoluteIDQ p180 kit is part of a series of widely used metabolomics assays that enable targeted, mass spectrometry-based quantification of metabolites from different classes in biological samples. The kit covers 188 metabolites and includes its own calibration standards, isotope-labelled internal standards, and QC samples. Use of these standards, according to the detailed analysis protocol validated by Biocrates, assures assay harmonization and standardization within a project, across projects, and across laboratories [14]. For metabolite analysis in human blood plasma, the AbsoluteIDQ p180 workflow prescribes the use of untreated samples without organic solvent extraction. While this is a strength of the assay for most applications, it limits its utility for infectious disease research protocols, which often require inactivation of high-risk pathogens present in samples prior to further handling or analysis.

To address this methodological gap, we assessed the feasibility of adding an organic solvent pre-extraction step to the Biocrates AbsoluteIDQ p180 workflow, with the dual purpose of inactivating pathogens and reliably recovering all metabolites targeted by the assay. We selected the previously published “MPLEx” (Metabolites, Proteins, and Lipids Extraction) protocol [15], as this is one of the few organic solvent extraction methods validated for the inactivation of various high-risk, biosafety level 3/4 (BSL-3/4) pathogens [16,17]. In addition, the MPLEx method has been shown to be robust and reproducible for extracting a range of metabolites [18]. Here, we report the quantification of plasma metabolites with the AbsoluteIDQ p180 assay using matched protocol-conformant and MPLEx pre-extracted samples drawn from an international, observational sepsis cohort [19,20]. The findings can be used to extend the utility of the AbsoluteIDQ workflow, and comparable metabolomics assays, to infectious disease studies that potentially include high-risk samples.

Methods

Peripheral blood plasma samples were obtained from 40 adult patients recruited as part of an international, observational sepsis study at clinical sites in Takéo Province, Cambodia (n = 13), Kumasi, Ghana (n = 22), and Durham, United States (n = 5) [19,20]. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained from the Naval Medical Research Command (NMRC.2013.0019; NMRC.2016.0004-GHA), and the study protocols complied with all applicable U.S. federal regulations governing the protection of human subjects. Patients presented to the hospitals with a suspected infection and met two or more of the Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) criteria [21]. For each patient, two matching plasma aliquots collected at the time of enrollment were selected for analysis.

The first aliquot was prepared in accordance with the standard Biocrates AbsoluteIDQ p180 protocol [14] (Biocrates, Innsbruck, Austria). Briefly, 10 µl of plasma were added to a 96-well plate containing the supplied isotope-labelled internal standard solution. The plate was dried under a gentle stream of N2 gas for 30 min. Samples were derivatized with phenyl isothiocyanate, then eluted with 5 mM ammonium acetate in methanol. Samples were subsequently diluted with either 1:1 (v/v) methanol:water for ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) (final dilution 4:1), or the proprietary AbsoluteIDQ p180 running solvent for flow injection analysis (FIA) (final dilution 20:1).

The second set of aliquots was first inactivated and extracted at a Regional Biocontainment Laboratory (RBL) in Durham, United States, using the MPLEx method [15]. Briefly, 40 µL of plasma were diluted with 20 µL ammonium bicarbonate and 240 µL cold 2:1 (v/v) chloroform:methanol. After incubation on ice for 1 h with intermittent vortexing, samples were centrifuged at 15,000 x g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting upper aqueous and lower organic layers were separated, and 15 µL of each were combined for analysis, instead of the 10 µl of plasma prescribed by the AbsoluteIDQ p180 protocol. This higher volume was chosen to partially offset metabolite concentrations, which were expected to be threefold lower because of the addition of the MPLEx extraction. The remaining sample preparation steps on the 96-well plates were the same as for the unmodified p180 plasma aliquots.

Target metabolites were measured using two pre-specified mass spectrometry methods. Acylcarnitines (n = 40), glycerophospholipids (n = 87), sphingolipids (n = 14), and monosaccharides (n = 1) were measured by flow injection analysis tandem mass spectrometry (FIA-MS/MS), whereas amino acids (n = 20) and biogenic amines (n = 21) were measured by ultra-performance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS). Chromatographic separation for the latter was performed on an Acquity UPLC system with an Acquity 2.1 mm x 50 mm 1.7 μm BEH C18 column, fitted with an Acquity BEH C18 1.7 μm Vanguard guard column (all Waters, Milford, MS, US). A linear 6 min solvent gradient from 0.2 % formic acid in water to 0.2 % formic acid in acetonitrile was used. Analytes were detected on a Xevo TQ-S triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Waters, Milford, MS, US) using positive electrospray ionization (ESI + ) and operating in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode for compound-specific precursor-to-product ion transitions. MRM transitions for each analyte and stable isotope-labelled internal standard were collected over the specific retention times prescribed by the assay workflow. UPLC-MS/MS data were imported into Waters’ TargetLynx™ software for peak integration, calibration, and concentration calculations. Both the TargetLynx™ outputs and FIA-MS/MS data were analyzed using Biocrates’ MetIDQ™ software. Metabolite quantitation was based on the calibration standards supplied in the AbsoluteIDQ p180 kit. Because a single-point calibration was used for FIA-MS/MS quantitation [22], results for acylcarnitines, glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, and monosaccharides should be considered semi-quantitative. In contrast, the UPLC-MS/MS protocol uses a seven-point calibration curve, yielding accurate and reproducible quantitation of amino acids and biogenic amines.

All figures and statistics were generated in R (v4.4.2) using log2-transformed data. Metabolite concentration measurements below the lower limit of detection (LLoD) for each assay were excluded from statistical analyses, and no missing value imputations or data normalizations were applied. Metabolite concentrations were compared between methods using paired t-tests with a significance cutoff of p < 0.05. No technical replicates were performed due to limited sample availability. However, a study pool quality control (SPQC) of the MPLEx samples was prepared in technical triplicate to specifically evaluate post-extraction reproducibility.

Results

Metabolite concentrations and detection rates from matched plasma samples analyzed using the unmodified AbsoluteIDQ p180 method and a protocol with pre-extraction via the MPLEx method for metabolites, proteins, and lipids are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. We defined the assay detection rate as the number of samples in either workup set that yielded a measurement above the LLoD for each analyte. Total concentrations for metabolite classes and subclasses were determined by summing the concentrations of their constituent analytes (e.g., total sphingomyelins) and are shown in Fig. 1. Because of differences in sample workup, we expected metabolite concentrations in the MPLEx-p180 set to be threefold lower than in the unmodified p180 set. In practice, this theoretical dilution factor could be less than threefold if adding the MPLEx-p180 method increased the extraction efficiency of a given metabolite relative to the unmodified p180 protocol, and vice versa. To assess relative extraction efficiencies of the two methods, we calculated a relative dilution factor for each metabolite (Supplementary Table 1). Finally, linear regression was used to examine correlations between the results obtained with both methods.

Total analyte concentrations per metabolite class or subclass differed significantly between the unmodified AbsoluteIDQ p180 protocol and the MPLEx-p180 extraction method (paired t-tests, p < 0.001). However, methodological differences must be considered, and extraction efficiencies varied by metabolite (see main text).

Amino acids

All 20 proteinogenic amino acids in the AbsoluteIDQ p180 assay were reliably measured by both sample processing methods. Most amino acids showed a 2- to 2.7-fold dilution in MPLEx-p180 extracts relative to unmodified p180 samples, while retaining good correlations between methods (R2 = 0.598 to 0.971). Poorer correlations for some analytes resulted from concentrations approaching the LLoD in the more dilute MPLEx-p180 samples (e.g., histidine, tyrosine). The main exception was aspartate, which showed a reduced detection rate in MPLEx-p180 samples (75 %), a different dilution factor (1.1), and a weaker correlation between methods (R2 = 0.538), indicating markedly different extraction behavior when adding the MPLEx protocol.

Biogenic amines

Biogenic amines are a chemically diverse set of metabolites characterised by the presence of one or more amine groups. The AbsoluteIDQ p180 assay includes 21 biogenic amines, which yielded mixed results in this comparison. Eight metabolites were either not detected at all or had very poor detection rates by either sample processing method (0–10 %): acetylornithine, carnosine, dopamine, histamine, cis-4-hydroxyproline, nitrotyrosine, phenylethylamine, and serotonin. Dihydroxyphenylalanine and spermine were reliably detected only in the unmodified p180 samples, suggesting very poor recovery with the MPLEx-p180 method. In contrast, methionine sulfoxide had reduced detection rates in both the p180 and MPLEx-p180 sets (73 % and 50 %, respectively), but those measurements were highly correlated (R2 = 0.915) with comparable concentration ranges (dilution factor 0.9), suggesting higher extraction efficiency with MPLEx-p180. Putrescine and α-aminoadipic acid showed reduced detection rates only in MPLEx-treated samples (68 % and 58 %, respectively), but correlations with the p180 samples were still fairly good. Most measurements for these metabolites were close to their LLoD, resulting in some loss in the more dilute MPLEx-p180 samples. The remaining eight biogenic amines were reliably detected in almost all samples, irrespective of processing method. Spermidine and asymmetric dimethylarginine showed poor correlations (R2 = 0.257 and 0.254, respectively), with spermidine also showing comparable concentration ranges between p180 and MPLEx-p180 samples (dilution factor 1.1). In contrast, creatinine, trans-4-hydroxyproline, kynurenine, sarcosine, symmetric dimethylarginine, and taurine were all highly correlated (R2 = 0.846 to 0.917) with ∼ 2.5-fold dilution factors, indicating high agreement between methods for these metabolites, albeit with slightly higher extraction efficiency in the MPLEx-p180 protocol.

Acylcarnitines

A comprehensive suite of 40 acylcarnitines is included in the AbsoluteIDQ p180 assay, ranging from carnitine to the long-chain C18:2 acylcarnitine. Results for this class were mixed, primarily due to the low abundance of many of these metabolites in human plasma. While the main saturated acylcarnitine species were reliably detected in almost all samples (e.g., acetylcarnitine, butyrylcarnitine, hexadecanoylcarnitine), detection rates for the unsaturated, hydroxylated, and dicarboxylated species were lower and more variable. This suggests that these more complex metabolites have lower extraction efficiencies than the saturated acylcarnitines, particularly with the MPLEx method. The small number of acylcarnitines with high detection rates showed good correlations between the two processing methods (R2 = 0.811 to 0.951). These metabolites also had dilution factors close to the theoretical threefold value (2.0 to 3.9), although extraction efficiencies with the MPLEx-p180 method were somewhat higher for the shorter-chain species (carnitine through butyrylcarnitine).

Glycerophospholipids

Although the AbsoluteIDQ p180 assay includes many glycerophospholipids, they all belong to the same lipid subclass: glycerophosphatidylcholine (PC). The main variation lies in the number and composition of the fatty radyl moieties, with either one fatty acyl at the sn-1 position of the glycerol backbone (lysophosphocholines; lysoPC), two fatty acyls at the sn-1 and sn-2 positions (diacyl-phosphocholines; PC aa), or a combination of one fatty alkyl and one fatty acyl, typically at the sn-1 and sn-2 positions, respectively (alkylacyl-phosphocholines; PC ae). Further speciation occurs at the level of the fatty radyls, for example based on the number of carbon atoms, double bonds, or additional moieties in each carbon chain.

Almost all of the 14 targeted lysophosphocholine species were reliably detected in all samples, except lysoPC a C14:0 (not detected in either set), lysoPC a C20:3, and lysoPC a C28:0 (detection rates of 88 % and 38 %, respectively, in MPLEx-treated samples). Dilution factors for the more abundant lysoPC species were relatively high (3.9 to 4.3), while those for the less abundant longer-chain species were close to the expected threefold value (2.3 to 2.9). This suggests subtle differences in extraction efficiencies between the unmodified p180 and MPLEx-p180 protocols related to the chemical structure and/or abundance of specific lysoPC species.

Of the 35 targeted diacyl-phosphocholine species, only the low-abundance PC aa C26:0 and PC aa C40:1 were not reliably detected by either method. Four species had reduced detection rates in MPLEx-treated samples, including PC aa C24:0 (58 %), PC aa C36:0 (88 %), PC aa C42:2 (60 %), and PC aa C42:6 (88 %), possibly also due to their low abundance combined with the higher dilution used in the MPLEx-p180 method. The remaining diacyl-phosphocholine species were detected in all samples, and most showed good or fairly good correlations between the two methods (R2 ranging from 0.629 to 0.966). These lipids had an ∼ 4-fold dilution factor (3.7 to 5.1), indicating lower extraction efficiency with MPLEx-p180. A notable exception were the species PC aa C34:2, PC aa C34:1, PC aa C36:4, and PC aa C36:2, some of the most abundant diacyl-phosphocholines in human plasma. Their levels in unmodified p180 samples were comparatively high, and their distributions indicate measurements approached the assay’s upper limit of detection (ULoD), making quantitation less reliable for undiluted p180 measurements and likely explaining the lack of correlation with the MPLEx results.

A total of 38 alkylacyl-phosphocholine species are included in the AbsoluteIDQ p180 assay, most of which are present at lower concentrations than diacyl-phosphocholines in human plasma. Only PC ae C42:0 was not reliably detected in either set, while several low-abundance species had reduced detection rates with both methods or only in MPLEx-treated samples. The remaining 29 alkylacyl-phosphocholine species were reliably detected in all samples and showed good or fairly good correlations between methods (R2 ranging from 0.686 to 0.977). As with the diacyl-phosphocholines, the alkylacyl-phosphocholines showed an ∼ 4-fold dilution factor (3.3 to 4.6), indicating somewhat lower extraction efficiency with the MPLEx-p180 method.

Sphingolipids

The diverse sphingolipid class is represented in the p180 assay by 14 sphingomyelins (SM), a subclass of phosphosphingolipids, including five hydroxy-sphingomyelins. All sphingomyelins were reliably detected in all samples, and most showed good correlations between the two processing methods (R2 = 0.736 to 0.844). Correlations were lower for the low-abundance C20:2 and C26:0 sphingomyelins, likely because measurements were near the analytes’ LLoDs, and also for the more abundant C16:0 sphingomyelin (R2 = 0.384) for unclear reasons. Dilution factors for the sphingomyelins were comparatively high (3.9 to 5.4), indicating lower extraction efficiency for this lipid subclass with the MPLEx-p180 method.

Monosaccharides

Monosaccharides are represented in the AbsoluteIDQ p180 assay by a single combined measurement of hexoses (glucose, galactose, fructose). These were reliably detected in all samples, with fairly high correlation between the two processing methods (R2 = 0.744). Monosaccharide levels in the MPLEx-p180 extracts showed a twofold dilution relative to the p180 samples, suggesting higher extraction efficiency with the MPLEx-p180 method.

Discussion and outlook

Recent years have seen an increase in commercially available assays for quantitative metabolomics. Combined with the development of appropriate metabolite standards and reference materials, these assays have greatly improved metabolomics reproducibility both within studies and between different studies and laboratories [14]. The chemical diversity of the metabolome makes assay development and standardisation challenging, and there are no “one-size-fits-all” solutions. Choices of extraction solvents, sample types and volumes, and the application of separation techniques and analytical platforms all influence assay applicability and performance [23].

In this study, we amended a commercially available metabolomics assay, Biocrates’ AbsoluteIDQ p180, by combining it with an established extraction protocol for metabolites, proteins, and lipids (MPLEx). The rationale was the need to safely inactivate high-risk pathogens prior to sample analysis, which often arises in infectious disease studies. The original p180 protocol is unsuitable in this regard, as it uses untreated, and therefore potentially hazardous, biospecimens. In contrast, MPLEx is a robust extraction protocol validated for the inactivation of a wide variety of BSL-3/4 bacterial and viral pathogens [14,15,18]. We note that alternative sample pre-extraction methods may be equally suitable for pathogen inactivation, but to our knowledge MPLEx is the only one fully validated to date. We performed a side-by-side comparison of the combined MPLEx-p180 method and the unmodified p180 protocol using matched blood plasma aliquots from an international, observational sepsis cohort [19,20].

Perhaps unsurprisingly for such a chemically diverse set of analytes, agreement between the methods varied by metabolite class, subclass, and sometimes specific molecule. Most targeted amino acids, glycerophospholipids (phosphocholines), sphingolipids (sphingomyelins), and monosaccharides (hexose sugars) were reliably measured and well correlated between the p180 and MPLEx-p180 methods, with notable exceptions (e.g., aspartate, SM C16:0). The same held for a small number of biogenic amines and unsaturated acylcarnitines, but for most metabolites in these classes the higher sample dilution in MPLEx-p180 reduced detection rates and shifted more analytes closer to their LLoD. This effect persisted despite efforts to offset the higher dilution by using a larger sample volume. An interesting exception was the high-abundance plasma phosphocholines, such as PC aa C34:1, which likely approached the ULoD in the unmodified p180 measurements. An in-depth appraisal of the physicochemical factors affecting extraction efficiencies for specific metabolites or classes was beyond the scope of this study. The metabolite concentration ranges in this limited sample set were representative of those derived from a larger pool of samples from the same sepsis cohort (n = 552, data not shown). Nonetheless, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings, and analyte-specific validation is recommended, particularly for targeted metabolomics studies.

These observed differences, as well as the generally lower metabolite concentrations in the MPLEx-p180 extracts (Fig. 1), necessitate some form of data harmonisation when both methods are applied within the same study. The simplest realignment would be a linear correction based on the relationship between the two sets of measurements (correlation and offset). However, this approach is sensitive to methodological variations and can only be reliably applied if analyte concentrations fall within the linear ranges of both assays. The results shown here indicate that a linear correction may be feasible for some metabolites targeted in the AbsoluteIDQ p180 assay.

An alternative, which does not assume linear relationships between assays, is to treat this as a “batch” problem analogous to those observed for analysis batches or multi-centre studies. Many options exist for post hoc batch correction, but a widely used method is an empirical Bayesian adjustment that aligns pre-specified data sets (i.e., “p180 set” and “MPLEx-p180 set”). An example is shown in Fig. 2, where data from the current study were aligned using the ComBat function of the R package sva [24,25]. This approach should be used only for relatively complete datasets with low levels of missing values, but it has the advantage of being applicable independently of the original analytical assays and their inherent variability or relationships. Although harmonization between the p180 and MPLEx-p180 measurements in this study using sva ComBat was generally successful, including for poorly correlated analytes, a number of metabolites with low detection rates in the MPLEx-p180 extracts still had to be excluded. For these, alternative extraction methods that are compatible with BSL-3/4 study protocols will be required.

Only metabolites with R2 < 0.8 are labeled, and a small number of analytes with low detection rates were excluded from the realignment. Abbreviations: M (Monosaccharides); AC (Acylcarnitines); LPC (Lysophosphocholines); PC aa (Diacyl-phosphocholines); PC ae (Alkylacyl-phosphocholines); SM (Sphingomyelins); AA (Amino acids); BA (Biogenic amines).

In summary, we show that pre-extraction of metabolites using the MPLEx method is a viable option for extending the use of commercial metabolomics assays, such as AbsoluteIDQ p180, to samples that require inactivation of BSL-3/4 pathogens. This significantly improves their utility for high-risk infectious disease studies. However, the higher sample dilution introduced by adding the MPLEx method could only be partially offset by using a larger volume of the original sample. Careful consideration should therefore be given to metabolite-specific extraction efficiencies and detection rates, as well as to data harmonization strategies for aligning results obtained with the original and amended analytical methods.
