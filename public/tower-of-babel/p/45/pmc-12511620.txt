EGFR-STAT1 pathway drives fibrosis initiation in fibroinflammatory skin diseases
Authors: Anahi V. Odell, Nathan M. Newton, Anna Eisenstein, Goran Micevic, Richard A. Flavell, Ian D. Odell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chronic inflammatory skin diseases affect the health of millions of people worldwide, and those that feature fibrosis are refractory to treatments. The signals that determine whether fibrosis occurs during chronic skin inflammation are poorly understood. We generated a scRNA-seq atlas of seven inflammatory skin diseases and their healthy controls. Diseases with fibrosis demonstrate higher expression and activity of STAT1 in fibroblasts. Fibroblast STAT1 is required for skin fibrosis development in mice. STAT1 activation promotes a fibrotic gene expression profile which can be activated directly by EGFR in a JAK-independent manner and abrogated by genetic and pharmacologic STAT1 inhibition. The EGFR-STAT1 pathway is stimulated by high affinity EGFR ligands expressed by activated keratinocytes, suggesting keratinocyte-derived signals as triggers of skin fibrosis. In sum, fibroinflammatory skin diseases are characterized by fibroblast EGFR-STAT1 signaling that controls expression of fibrotic genes, elucidating an interferon independent function of STAT1 to mediate fibrotic skin diseases.

Introduction

The immune system drives an array of chronic inflammatory skin diseases that lead to significant impairment in quality of life for millions of people along with billions of dollars per year of economic burden in the United States1–3. Over the last two decades, treatments for some inflammatory skin diseases like atopic dermatitis (eczema) and psoriasis have been approved and result in dramatic improvement in cutaneous disease through targeting disease-specific cytokines4–7. Inflammatory skin diseases that feature skin fibrosis, however, are difficult to treat, and treatments that are available are unable to reverse fibrosis-associated skin damage. Therefore, understanding signaling mechanisms inherent to fibroinflammatory skin diseases is critical for advancing patient care and reducing the associated societal burden.

Fibroblasts are the principal cell type that overproduces collagen and other extracellular matrix (ECM) components in fibrotic skin. Different subsets of fibroblasts are spatially and functionally organized in the skin8. Under homeostasis, three main groups of skin fibroblasts are distinguished by expression of SFRP1, SFRP2, and APOE9, which have different representation in the upper (papillary) and lower (reticular) dermal compartments10. During skin inflammation, there is expansion of fibroblast subsets, including “inflammatory” fibroblasts, which express CCL19 and other immune-activating cytokines, and profibrotic fibroblasts derived from SFRP2+ precursors that express PRSS23 and THBS111. Both inflammatory and profibrotic fibroblasts are expanded in scleroderma12. Therefore, understanding the signaling pathways that regulate the functions of inflammatory and profibrotic fibroblast subsets during skin inflammation will yield insights into their biologic functions and may elucidate therapeutic targets.

Fibrosis in the skin is characterized by elevated type I interferons, which activate STAT proteins through Janus Kinases (JAKs) to drive interferon signature gene (ISG) expression13–15. Because ISG expression is a fundamental characteristic of SSc and lupus, we set out to examine upstream signaling pathways that may regulate STAT1 signaling in the skin. A lead candidate was EGFR, a receptor tyrosine kinase, which is essential for fibrosis development in multiple organs16–18 and in viral studies has been shown to modulate interferon signaling19,20. Furthermore, in some cancers, STAT proteins are activated by the EGF receptor tyrosine kinase21,22. We therefore hypothesized that EGFR may regulate STAT1 signaling in skin fibrosis. However, how EGFR interacts with interferon signaling during fibrosis remained unclear. We therefore investigated the in vivo consequences of EGFR activation on interferon signaling through studies of its interaction with STAT1 in fibroinflammatory skin diseases.

Here, we created a scRNA-seq inflammatory skin disease atlas from which we compared gene expression and regulatory modules in fibrotic and non-fibrotic inflammatory skin diseases as well as healthy controls. Analysis of differentially expressed genes and their transcription factors highlighted STAT1 as the most significantly upregulated marker of fibrotic fibroblasts across fibroinflammatory skin diseases. STAT1-expressing fibroblasts also showed enrichment in the EGR1 and FOS regulons, known EGFR downstream transcription factors22,23. We performed in vivo validation studies that identified fibroblast STAT1 to be required for skin fibrosis development. Mechanistically, EGFR could directly activate STAT1 and stimulate a profibrotic transcription profile independently and distinct from type I interferon signaling.

Results

Integrated inflammatory skin disease atlas shows upregulated STAT1 expression in SFRP2+ fibroblasts

Chronic inflammatory skin conditions have a wide array of phenotypes (Fig. 1A). Diseases such as atopic dermatitis (AD, colloquially called eczema) and psoriasis feature skin redness and scaling. Systemic sclerosis (SSc) is an autoimmune disease that involves hardening of the skin of the hands and face, which can progress to involve the entire body. Chronic graft-vs-host disease (GvHD), which occurs after hematopoietic stem cell transplant, can mimic the skin fibrosis seen in SSc but tends to occur in different areas of the body. Lupus erythematosus is another autoimmune disease that frequently affects the skin, and its chronic form, called discoid lupus erythematosus, also causes scarring, mostly in sun-exposed skin. Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) occurs typically in the axillae and genital region with acne-like lesions that can progress to pus-filled cysts and result in scarring. scRNA-Seq datasets of human inflammatory skin diseases have been published independently but not compared to one another in an integrated fashion. We downloaded and integrated publicly available scRNA-Seq datasets from NCBI gene expression omnibus from patients with AD (n = 8)24, psoriasis (n = 5)25, SSc (n = 17)11,26, cutaneous lupus erythematosus (n = 7)27, sclerotic GvHD (n = 3)28, HS (n = 9)29, wound repair (n = 1)30, and their corresponding healthy controls (n = 38) (Fig. 1B). After filtering dead cells with high mitochondrial DNA content ( > 7.5%), the combined dataset yielded 206,281 cells. Unsupervised clustering followed by UMAP embedding demonstrated 12 primary clusters with representative cells from each disease, including fibroblasts, pericytes, endothelial cells, T and B cells, myeloid antigen presenting cells, keratinocytes and eccrine cells, lymphatic cells, smooth muscle cells, mast cells, melanocytes, and neurons (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. 1A). Most datasets showed similar proportions of each cell type, except HS which had a higher proportion of keratinocytes (Supplementary Fig. 1B). As fibroblasts are the main producer of collagen, we initially compared gene expression among all fibroblasts from fibroinflammatory skin diseases (SSc, GvHD, HS, lupus, and wound repair) to non-fibrotic diseases (AD and psoriasis) and healthy controls (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Fibroblast clustering was able to identify myofibroblasts, which are a subset of SFRP2+ fibroblast precursors11, as well as inflammatory, TNN+/COCH+, and CLDN1+/ANGPTL7+ clusters (Fig. 1D, E). Among myofibroblasts marked by PRSS23 and POSTN31, they also showed elevated STAT1, CTGF and TNC expression. To compare healthy skin, which have few myofibroblasts, to fibrotic skin, we compared gene expression among all SFRP2+ fibroblasts which includes myofibroblasts and their precursors. Analysis of STAT1 expression by SFRP2+ profibrotic fibroblasts in each disease showed significantly elevated expression in SSc, wound repair, lupus, and HS compared to healthy controls with p values between 10−4 and 10−11 and expression fold changes from 2.4 to 4.4 (Fig. 1F). GvHD trended toward higher STAT1 expression but was not significant, likely due to few SFRP2+ fibroblasts represented (n = 234). Interestingly, psoriasis showed higher SFRP2+ fibroblast STAT1 expression compared to healthy controls, which aligns with its known elevated interferon gamma levels, despite interferon gamma not being a psoriasis pathogenic cytokine32. AD, by contrast, showed no differential expression of STAT1 by SFRP2+ fibroblasts. Therefore, STAT1 expression appears to be a commonly elevated transcription factor among profibrotic fibroblasts in fibroinflammatory diseases and psoriasis.

We next focused on comparison of differential gene expression by SSc, lupus, and HS SFRP2+ fibroblasts because these had the greatest number of SFRP2+ fibroblasts, showed significant differential STAT1 expression, and feature skin fibrosis. We found 39 commonly dysregulated genes, including STAT1, HIF1A, TNC, BST2, IFI27, and S100A8, which highlights interferon signaling33, RTK signaling26, stress response34, and extracellular matrix organization35 (Fig. 1G). Genes uniquely elevated in each disease featured neutrophilic cytokines in HS, additional ISGs in lupus and growth factors, oxidative stress response, and mechanotransduction in SSc. Notably, the recently identified profibrotic fibroblast marker LGR536 was a unique feature of SSc, but not lupus or HS fibroblasts. Given the strong STAT1 signal, we analyzed STAT1 expression by immunohistochemistry among inflammatory skin diseases. Keratinocytes and immune cells showed STAT1 staining across disease types to different extents (Fig. 1H). Examination of fibroblasts in the mid dermis, however, showed strong STAT1 staining in fibroinflammatory skin diseases but not healthy or non-fibrotic conditions (Fig. 1I), corroborating the observed differential STAT1 RNA expression seen in Fig. 1F. Thus, STAT1 is highly expressed on the RNA and protein level in fibroblasts in fibroinflammatory skin diseases.

SCENIC analysis of fibroblasts identifies STAT1 as a central regulon in fibrotic skin

We sought to gain better insight into the transcriptional network and regulators of fibrotic fibroblasts. We analyzed our previously published scRNA-Seq data26 from 5 patients with SSc compared to 6 healthy controls using SCENIC37, a computational method to predict active transcription factors based on coregulated gene expression profiles (Fig. 2A). We calculated differential regulon enrichment among SFRP2+ fibroblasts and found STAT1 as the most enriched transcription factor (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Data 1). The other fibroblast subsets also showed STAT1 enrichment comparing SSc to healthy (Fig. 2C). We focused on the SFRP2+ fibroblast cluster and found enrichment of STAT2, consistent with a type I interferon process (Fig. 2D). We also identified EGFR activated transcription factors, including EGR1 and FOS (Fig. 2E, F), suggesting co-activation of STAT1 and EGFR targets in SFRP2+ fibroblasts. Transcription factors previously associated with SSc were also enriched, including TWIST1, RUNX1, HIF1A, and IRF711,38,39 (Fig. 2G–J). Ingenuity pathway analysis of SSc SFRP2+ fibroblast enriched transcription factors demonstrated co-regulation among them, underscoring a fibrotic fibroblast regulatory network centered around STAT1 (Supplementary Fig. 2). To contextualize the STAT1 regulon among all the skin diseases, we repeated SCENIC analysis on fibroblasts from each disease and found STAT1 as the most consistently upregulated transcription factor among those that feature fibrosis (Fig. 2K). Fibroblast STAT1 and EGFR activity are required for fibrosis development

Our analysis of human scRNA-Seq data suggests STAT1 as a central player in fibroblasts, so we sought to validate these findings in vivo using the bleomycin model of skin fibrosis as previously described26. We initially examined skin fibrosis in mice with whole body knockout of Stat1 and found they were protected from developing skin thickening and increased collagen content compared to wild type controls (Fig. 3A–C). To understand if the immune cell compartment was responsible for this phenotype, we generated bone marrow chimeras by transfer of Stat1-/- or wild type bone marrow to wild type recipients. Stat1-/- neutrophil compared to B and NK cell proportions were inversely correlated in the recipients consistent with type I interferon and STAT1 function in neutrophil and NK cell development40,41 (Fig. 3D). Immune cell proportions were otherwise similar between genotypes. Skin fibrosis induction with bleomycin in the bone marrow chimeras showed similar increases in skin thickness and collagen content, indicating that STAT1 in radiosensitive immune cells is not required for skin fibrosis (Fig. 3E, F). To test fibroblast STAT1 requirement, we generated STAT1 fibroblast conditional knockouts by crossing Stat1 floxed mice with Pdgfra-Cre and tested them with the bleomycin model (Stat1ΔFib). We found Stat1ΔFib mice showed diminished skin thickness compared to Stat1fl/fl control mice (Fig. 3G, H), indicating that the profibrotic functions of STAT1 occur in fibroblasts. Because our human data suggested coregulation of EGFR activated transcription factors with STAT1, we examined whether EGFR inhibition could also prevent fibrosis. Oral administration of the EGFR small molecule inhibitor gefitinib prevented skin thickening by bleomycin similar to STAT1 fibroblast conditional knockouts (Fig. 3I, J). Because STAT1 is also activated by JAK1/2, and type I interferon is a well-established driver of fibrosis in SSc and lupus42–44, we tested the JAK1/2 inhibitor ruxolitinib in the bleomycin model. We found ruxolitinib treatment resulted in reduced skin thickness to a similar extent as gefitinib, suggesting multiple pathways can converge on STAT1 in skin fibrosis, although the antifibrotic effects of JAK inhibition include cell types beyond fibroblasts45. EGFR can directly phosphorylate STAT1 in human dermal fibroblasts in response to TGFA and other high affinity ligands

Given the relationship between STAT1 and EGFR activity in human and mouse data, we investigated the mechanism of how these pathways interact in vitro using primary human foreskin fibroblasts. Because EGFR has been reported to activate STAT121,22, we compared STAT1 phosphorylation in response to each of the seven EGFR ligands (Fig. 4A). We found that high affinity EGFR ligands were capable of inducing STAT1 Tyr 701 phosphorylation. We compared the kinetics of STAT1 phosphorylation at Tyr 701 and Ser 727 in response to TGFA and type I interferon IFNA2 (Fig. 4B). The peak of TGFA induced phosphorylation on STAT1 Tyr 701 occurred at 5 min, whereas IFNA2 induced phosphorylation remained elevated from 20–60 min. Phosphorylation of STAT1 Ser 727, which occurs after phosphorylation of Tyr 701, remained elevated for 60 mins in response to TGFA and 2–4 h in response to IFNA2. EGFR co-immunoprecipitates with Janus Kinases in cancer cell lines22, so we tested whether Janus or EGFR kinases are required for TGFA-induced STAT1 phosphorylation. STAT1 phosphorylation induced by the high affinity ligand TGFA was EGFR kinase dependent as it could be blocked by treatment with the EGFR inhibitor gefitinib (Fig. 4C). In response to interferons, STAT1 phosphorylation is mediated by JAKs. However, inhibition of JAK1/2 with ruxolitinib, TYK2 with deucravacitinib, or combined treatment did not block TGFA-induced STAT1 phosphorylation, indicating EGFR mediated STAT1 phosphorylation is JAK independent (Fig. 4D–F). We therefore considered whether EGFR could directly phosphorylate STAT1. We incubated recombinant EGFR kinase with recombinant STAT1 and found that EGFR kinase could directly phosphorylate STAT1 Tyr 701, which could be blocked by EGFR inhibitor gefitinib in a dose dependent fashion (Fig. 4G). To validate that EGFR and STAT1 directly interact in fibroblasts, we performed the proximity ligation assay and found their specific interaction mainly in the cytoplasm, which was increased upon TGFA treatment (Fig. 4H, I). In summary, our findings support direct phosphorylation of STAT1 by EGFR kinase in a JAK-independent manner in response to high affinity EGFR ligands.

EGFR activation by TGFA leads to a fibrotic STAT1-dependent transcriptional profile distinct from interferon stimulated genes

STAT1 is well appreciated to turn on an ISG transcriptional profile in response to type I interferon but it remained unclear if EGFR mediated STAT1 activation would have similar consequences. Using mouse neonatal dermal fibroblasts (mNDF) from wild type and Stat1-/- mice, we assessed the effects of TGFA on expression of fibrosis associated ISGs. Transcription of canonical ISGs Bst2 and Ifih1 were STAT1-dependent, but not responsive to TGFA, suggesting TGFA may induce a distinct transcriptional profile from type I interferon (Fig. 5A). Since STAT1, EGR1 and FOS regulons are enriched in human fibrotic fibroblasts (Fig. 2C–F) and EGR1 and FOS are downstream of EGFR, we investigated transcription of genes associated with EGFR signaling and fibrosis in response to TGFA including Ccl226, Ctgf 46 and Wisp226. We found that TGFA induced Ccl2, Ctgf and Wisp2 expression in a STAT1-dependent manner (Fig. 5B). Fibrosis associated genes including Egr138, Il647 and Cyr6148 were responsive to TGFA treatment but not in a STAT1-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. 3A). A subset of CCN family genes including Ccn1 (Nov)26, Ccn4 (Wisp1)49 and Ccn6 (Wisp3) as well as Fos were unresponsive to TGFA and STAT1-independent (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Therefore, EGFR activation induces a unique transcriptional profile via STAT1 of fibrotic genes. To confirm the specificity of the fibrotic and ISG transcriptional profiles in fibroblasts, we treated primary human fibroblasts with TGFA, EREG or IFNA2 and assessed RNA expression of canonical ISGs IFI27, BST2, CXCL10, IRF7 and STAT1 (Fig. 5C) or fibrotic genes (Fig. 5D). IFNA2, but not the EGFR ligands, turned on expression of the canonical ISG profile. By contrast, EGR1, CTGF and TNC expression were increased in response to TGFA but little (CTGF) or not at all (EGR1 and TNC) in response to IFNA2. Thus, IFNA2 both activates STAT1 and induces its transcription, whereas TGFA only activates it which further suggests a different signaling pathway. We observed a moderate increase in EGR1 expression by EREG treatment that likely reflects activation of ERK signaling without activation of STAT1. To confirm this, we performed pERK ELISA with TGFA and EREG and found saturating ERK phosphorylation in response to 100 ng/ml of each ligand (Fig. 5E), indicating the low affinity ligand EREG can activate ERK signaling. To directly assess whether TGFA induces STAT1 binding to EGR1 and CTGF promoters, we performed CUT&RUN using the targeted qPCR approach and found TGFA induced STAT1 binding to promoters of both EGR1 and CTGF (Fig. 5F). EGFR initiates fibrotic gene expression through ERK activation

We sought to understand the sequence of regulatory steps triggered by EGFR activation that lead to the fibrotic transcriptional profile. We performed a time course of gene expression in response to TGFA on wild type and Stat1-/- mNDF. We found Egr1 and Fos were transcribed early in a STAT1-independent manner, but persistent expression required the presence of STAT1 (Fig. 6A). STAT1 target genes including Il6, Ccl2, Cyr61, Ctgf, Wisp1 and Wisp2 expression peaked at 4 h and were STAT1-dependent, whereas expression of ISGs Bst2 and Ifih1 remained unchanged over 24 h. Other CCN family members Nov and Wisp3, which do not have STAT1 promoter binding sites, remained unchanged. To appreciate the transcription factors that might regulate each of these genes, we mapped the promoters of the EGFR-STAT1 fibrotic genes using the ORegAnno database50 (Fig. 6B). Because initial Egr1 and Fos expression were STAT1-independent, we considered alternative EGFR signals driving initiation of the pathway. Since EGFR signals through ERK1/2 pathway and Egr1, Fos, and Stat1 each have an Elk4 (a transcription factor downstream of ERK1/251) binding site in their promoter, we assessed if inhibition of ERK signaling abrogated EGR1 expression in fibroblasts. Treatment of fibroblasts with the MEK inhibitor AZD8330 blocked ERK phosphorylation, but not EGFR or STAT1 phosphorylation in response to TGFA (Fig. 6D). Both ERK inhibitor temuterkib and MEK inhibitor AZD8330 blocked TGFA induced expression of EGR1, CTGF and CCL2 (Fig. 6E–G). Thus, EGFR activation by TGFA induces ERK-dependent EGR1 expression, which initiates the pathway leading to the STAT1-dependent fibrotic gene expression profile. Given the prominent role of STAT1 in driving fibrotic gene expression, we sought to investigate its potential for therapeutic inhibition using fludarabine, a well-known STAT1 inhibitor that binds its SH2 domain52. We found no cytotoxicity of fludarabine on cultured fibroblasts in a range of 25–100 μM by LDH release over 24 h (Supplementary Fig. 4). Treatment of HFF with 100 μM fludarabine modestly reduced STAT1 transcription (Fig. 6G) and markedly decreased STAT1 phosphorylation in response to TGFA (Fig. 6H). Moreover, fludarabine treatment led to reduced expression of fibrotic genes CTGF, COL1A1 and TNC (Fig. 6I-K). Thus, fludarabine shows antifibrotic properties by targeting STAT1 in fibroblasts. Activated keratinocytes are the main sources of TGFA in fibrotic skin

High affinity EGFR ligands appeared equally capable at inducing STAT1 phosphorylation, so we wanted to examine which ligands were disease relevant and their cellular sources. Analysis of high affinity EGFR ligands in the human scRNA-Seq inflammatory skin disease atlas showed the most expression of TGFA by differentiated keratinocytes, HBEGF expressed by multiple cell types at a lower level, and low expression of EGF and BTC (Fig. 7A, B). Notably, HBEGF is an immediate early gene activated by tissue digestion53, so its ubiquitous expression is likely artifactual in this data. To gain insight into what is occurring in vivo, we measured EGFR ligand expression by qPCR of skin biopsies after bleomycin injection (Fig. 7C). Tgfa and Ereg were the most upregulated EGFR ligands in the skin in alignment with the human scRNA-Seq inflammatory skin disease atlas (Fig. 7D). As previously described, expression of Ereg increased during the maintenance phase of skin fibrosis26. Expression of Tgfa, by contrast, increased early during mouse skin fibrosis. The time point of increased Tgfa corresponded with increased expression of fibrotic ECM genes Tnc and the fibrotic isoform of Fn1 (FnEDA) (Fig. 7E, F). We considered why TGFA may be upregulated in the epidermis of fibrotic skin disease. During cutaneous inflammation, keratinocytes in the epidermis undergo activation and express stress keratins, such as KRT6 and KRT16, which enables keratinocytes to migrate and activate pathways related to innate immunity, oxidative stress, and interferon signaling54. It remained unclear if keratinocytes are similarly activated in fibroinflammatory skin diseases. During differentiation, keratinocytes express KRT5/14, then KRT1/10, and finally KRT2 from basal to spinous to fully differentiated states, respectively (Fig. 7G). Analysis of TGFA expression in the epidermis showed higher expression in the differentiated layer across healthy and inflamed skin (Fig. 7H). Inflammatory skin diseases showed higher expression of stress keratins KRT6A and KRT16 in each ascending layer of the epidermis (Fig. 7I, J). Corresponding immunofluorescence of KRT16 and TGFA co-staining in healthy and fibroinflammatory skin showed strong KRT16 and TGFA co-localization throughout the epidermis in fibroinflammatory skin diseases (Fig. 7K). These results suggest keratinocyte activation is a shared feature of fibroinflammatory skin diseases that may be driving production of TGFA. Analysis of differentially expressed genes by SSc, HS, and lupus keratinocytes showed 100 shared genes with higher expression than healthy controls, notably stress keratins, the alarmin S100A8, and ISGs (Fig. 7L). Disease unique genes included neutrophilic cytokines and IFNG in HS55,56, IL25 and TNF in lupus57,58, and CGAS and IL6 in SSc47,59, which have been previously associated with each disease. Discussion

Chronic inflammatory skin conditions affect millions of people worldwide and cause billions of dollars in economic burden1–3. Over the last two decades, advances in treatments that target disease specific cytokines have resulted in dramatic improvements in patient care of non-fibrotic inflammatory skin diseases such as atopic dermatitis and psoriasis4–7. Limited treatments are approved for some fibroinflammatory skin conditions; treatment response is suboptimal for most patients60 while other conditions like SSc lack effective skin treatments all together. Therefore, understanding signaling pathways that underlie fibroinflammatory skin diseases may identify new therapeutic avenues in diseases with great unmet medical need. Independent groups have performed scRNA-Seq on different inflammatory skin conditions, but these datasets had not yet been integrated and analyzed as a whole. We aimed to fill this knowledge gap and focus on shared disease drivers of fibroinflammatory disease, the most difficult to treat disease subset.

Interferon signaling has been strongly linked to autoimmune diseases including lupus61 and SSc15,62 as well as associated with inflammatory skin conditions like psoriasis and HS56,63–65. STAT1 is the common signaling molecule downstream of interferon receptors, making it an important mediator to understand its full spectrum of functions. Furthermore, STAT1 is expressed by pathogenic SSc skin fibroblasts11, but its role in fibroblasts remains understudied in fibroinflammatory skin conditions. EGFR is highly associated with fibrosis, cross-talks with interferon signaling during viral infection, and has been reported to also activate STAT1. The mechanism linking EGFR and STAT1 signaling in fibrosis remained unclear. Our findings elucidate a mechanism by which EGFR directly phosphorylates STAT1 to drive fibrosis gene expression profile in fibroblasts of fibroinflammatory skin conditions.

It is noteworthy to mention that profibrotic functions of EGFR-STAT1 signaling in fibroblasts and JAK-STAT signaling are not likely to be mutually exclusive. In separate or the same cell types, both pathways may be activated in parallel in fibroinflammatory skin conditions. Others have shown treatment with ruxolitinib can prevent skin and lung fibrosis in multiple mouse models66. The antifibrotic effect of ruxolitinib was mediated by decrease in pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic markers in M1 and M2 differentiated primary human macrophages45. Altogether, data support a model in which STAT1 functions as a signaling hub downstream of EGFR and interferon receptors in multiple fibrosis relevant cell types including macrophages and fibroblasts.

The body of literature surrounding EGR1 role in regulating fibrosis is limited and conflicting data has been published. EGR1 as a transcription factor can have activating and repressing functions depending on its binding partners, post-translational modifications and cellular context67. It plays a negative role in regulating lung fibrosis in response to TGFA overexpression in mice23 while it was found to positively regulate collagen transcription in skin fibroblasts in response to TGFB68. Similarly, overexpression of EGR1in cultured fibroblasts and cancer cells led to upregulation of a fibrotic gene profile including TGFB1, PAI1 and fibronectin38,69. We have found a link between EGR1 and STAT1, which are coregulated in human fibroinflammatory diseases and mechanistically they modulate fibrotic gene expression. Further work understanding the complexity of the EGR1-STAT1 signaling pathway will be the focus of future studies.

Limitations of our study include heterogeneous sample processing and subject numbers in each inflammatory skin disease dataset. Counterintuitively, higher numbers of distinct datasets in the atlas may provide greater reproducibility when analyzed together, a finding which has been leveraged to identify fibrosis biomarkers70. Consistent enrichment of STAT1 across fibroinflammatory skin diseases highlights the strength of this observation. The inflammatory skin disease atlas could be improved by incorporating additional patient level data. Unlike psoriasis and AD, fibroinflammatory skin conditions are characterized by stages and endotypes of disease. SSc for example, includes edematous, fibrotic and atrophic phases71. HS specimens may derive from individuals with one of multiple disease phenotypes and lesions types with varying degrees of inflammation and fibrosis72–74. This level of patient metadata is not currently available for all the subjects in the atlas. Thus, understanding signaling mechanisms for mild and severe disease subsets is limited. Nonetheless, comparison of multiple inflammatory skin diseases has provided broader understanding of their shared pathogenesis.

The EGFR-STAT1 axis has multiple potential therapeutic targets. Pathway initiation by activated keratinocytes suggests keratinocyte targeted therapies may have benefit. Many case series and uncontrolled trials have analyzed ultraviolet phototherapy for SSc with unclear mechanism75. Dermatologists frequently prescribe phototherapy off-label for SSc treatment which has shown benefit with minimal side effects in the experience of one of the authors (I.D.O.). If phototherapy is capable of modulating keratinocyte activation, this may be a plausible explanation. Of note, UV phototherapy would be contraindicated for patients with lupus because patients have photosensitive disease manifestations. Small molecule EGFR inhibitors have shown therapeutic benefit in mouse models of skin, liver, and kidney fibrosis16,17,76, but have not been tested in clinical trials of fibrosis. Although JAK inhibitors are now widely used for inflammatory diseases (e.g. rheumatoid arthritis, atopic dermatitis, psoriasis, and alopecia areata), direct STAT1 inhibition has not been pursued. We show that genetic and pharmacologic inhibition of STAT1 reduces skin fibrosis through its effects on fibroblasts. Fludarabine showed promising effects on inhibition of STAT1 phosphorylation, but clinical use would be limited by its inhibition of DNA synthesis52. Our mouse data suggests that TGFA induced EGFR-STAT1 signaling may occur early in skin fibrosis development, but our human data includes patients with longer standing disease. It will be important to compare timing of potential therapies with stages of fibroinflammatory skin diseases. In sum, our work here describes an alternative function of EGFR in fibroblasts to drive fibrosis genes through direct phosphorylation of STAT1 and may thus open new therapeutic avenues.

Methods

Study design

This study aimed to discover cellular signals that distinguish fibrotic from non-fibrotic chronic inflammatory skin diseases through analysis of publicly available and newly generated scRNA-Seq of skin samples obtained by multiple independent groups. Data were analyzed by differential gene expression and SCENIC, in which we discovered upregulated STAT1 expression as a signature of fibrotic fibroblasts. The requirement of fibroblast STAT1 in skin fibrosis was assess using conditional knock out mice and STAT1 bone marrow chimeras in the bleomycin model of skin fibrosis. To understand mechanism, we investigated how activation of STAT1 by EGFR could regulate fibrotic gene expression using western blot, proximity ligation assay, qPCR and CUT&RUN. Resulted were validated using genetic and pharmacologic approaches on mouse and human dermal fibroblasts.

Inflammatory skin scRNA-seq atlas

Patient photos were obtained with patient’s consent as part of human studies that were approved by the Yale University Human Investigation Committee. Publicly available scRNA-Seq datasets were downloaded from NCBI gene expression omnibus including AD (GSE153760)24, psoriasis (GSE228421)25, SSc (GSE128169, GSE214008)11,26, GvHD (GSE306357)28, cutaneous lupus erythematosus (GSE186476)27, HS (GSE154775)29, wound repair (GSE154557)30, and their corresponding healthy controls. Dead cells with high mitochondrial DNA content ( > 7.5%) were filtered, yielding 206,281 live cells. Integrative analysis was then performed with Seurat version 5 R toolkit for single cell genomics77,78 and R version 4.4.2 with computer seed set to 4. Healthy and disease samples from each dataset were combined into separate Seurat objects for a total of 10 objects. Data normalization (log normalized using the default scaling factor of 10000), scaling, and PCA analysis with 20 dimensions was performed on each object, followed by integration using the RPCAIntegration method. Cell type annotation and differential gene expression analysis was performed as described in the text. The resulting Seurat object was exported to a loupe file using LoupeR for DEG analysis. Overlap among differentially expressed genes (DEG) was determined using https://www.bioinformatics.org/gvenn.

SCENIC analysis

Analysis of gene regulatory networks active in five SSc samples compared to six healthy controls from our previously study26 was performed on the full set of cells using SCENIC (v1.3.1)37. SCENIC analysis across diseases was performed on the fibroblasts. Because the algorithm uses high computational resources, fibroblasts were randomly downsampled to 500 cells per subset per disease. Genes were filtered for those expressed with value of at least 3 in 1% of cells and detected in at least 1% of cells. Co-expression modules between transcription factor and target genes (regulons) were derived using GENIE3, then RcisTarget was used to identify transcription factor binding motifs over-represented in the gene lists using the hg19 - refseq_r45 - v9 databases, and regulon activity scores were calculated for each cell using AUCell. The resulting cell-type specific regulators were imported into a Seurat object and analyzed using the Seurat v5 toolkit. Ingenuity pathway analysis (QIAGEN IPA) was performed using the list of transcription factors with upregulated regulons to identify the regulatory network inference.

Animal studies

Wild-type C57Bl/6 mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories and Stat1-/- (#012606), Stat1fl (#032054) and Pdgfra-CRE (#013148) mice were purchased from Jackson laboratories. All mice were maintained at the Yale University School of Medicine Animal Resources Center. Mouse experiments were conducted on 7- to 10-week-old females under a protocol approved by the Yale University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol number 2022–10537) and in accordance with the American Association for Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC) guidelines. Bleomycin skin fibrosis model experiments were performed as previously described26. Briefly, animals were injected subcutaneously with a single dose of Bleomycin Sulfate (Sigma B1141000 Batch3) 8 mg/kg on the lower back or PBS using a 31-gauge insulin syringe. Animals were euthanized 21 days later and skin on the lower back was analyzed for histology and collagen content. In vivo EGFRi and JAKi experiments were performed by treating animals with gefitinib (80 mg/kg) or ruxolitinib (90 mg/kg) dissolved in DMSO:Corn oil (1:9) administered by mouth once daily (gefitinib) or twice daily (ruxolitinib) 5 days a week for 3 weeks. For bone marrow transplantation, prior to radiation recipient mice were fed chow supplemented with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for one week and acidified water (2 mL 1 N HCl per 1 L sterile water) for 24 h. On day of transfer, recipient female mice were radiated with a single dose of 10 gray. While recovering, bone marrow was isolated from the femurs and tibias of euthanized male donor mice. 5 h after irradiation, recipient mice were injected intravenously with 3 × 106 bone marrow cells with greater than 95% viability. Immune cell engraftment was monitored weekly for 4 weeks by FACS analysis of peripheral blood.

Cell culture and in vitro experiments

Human foreskin fibroblasts (HFF) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (SCRC-1041) and routinely grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% heat inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 U/ml Penicillin/Streptomycin in a humidified chamber with 5% CO2. Confluent cells grown in 12-well tissue culture plates were incubated overnight in DMEM supplemented with 1% FBS prior to treating cultured cells with EGFR ligands or IFNa2. On day of assay, cells were treated with ligands or IFN diluted in serum free DMEM and then lysed in 150μl Non-Denaturing Lysis Buffer (Tris HCl 20 mM, NaCl 137 nM, NP-40 1% v/v, EDTA 2 mM) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Halt, Thermo Scientific 1861281). Cells were allowed to complete lysis with gentle rotation at 4 °C, scraped and lysates cleared by centrifugation 15 min at 21,000 x g at 4 °C. Protein concentration of supernatants was determined using a detergent compatible protein assay (BioRad 500-01111). Lysates were analyzed by western blot using the antibodies listed in Supplementary Table 1. For in vitro experiments using inhibitors, all small molecules were dissolved in DMSO and then diluted in DMEM to final concentrations while keeping final DMSO concentration lower than 0.001%. All non-treated controls included the equivalent concentration of DMSO to the inhibitor treated wells. Cells were pre-treated with small molecule inhibitors for 2 h (gefinitib, ruxolitinib, deucravacitinib, temuterkib and AZD8330) or 24 h (fludarabine) prior to treatment with TGFA in the continued presence of the inhibitor. For quantitative ERK phosphorylation analysis, cells were cultured, treated and lysates prepared as described above. Then lysates were measured with a pERK/ERK ELISA kit (Invitrogen 85-86013-11) following manufacturer’s instructions. For analyzing transcript levels in fibroblasts, cells were cultured and treated as described above, then lysed in RLT Plus (QIAGEN) supplemented with beta-mercaptoethanol (1:100 dilution). RNA was extracted using QIAGEN RNAeasy plus kit and cDNAs were developed using Maxima H Minus Reverse Transcriptase 10 U/μl supplemented with 0.5 mM dNTPs and 25 ng/μl Oligo d(T)16. Primers were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Sources of all small molecules used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 2. Relative quantification of gene expression using SYBR Green was measured with CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). Gene expression was normalized to the housekeeping gene UBC79 and relative expression was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method80. Heatmaps of gene expression were generated from the cell clusters in the 10x Loupe Browser v5 with MORPHEUS software (https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus).

Proximity ligation assay

HFFs were cultured on PLL-coated glass coverslips (Neuvitro GG-12-PLL) to 50% confluency. On day of assay cells were treated with TGFA 100 ng/ml for 20 min, washed 1x with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized in 0.1% Triton X. Proximity ligation assay was performed using Duolink PLA Fluorescence kit (Sigma Aldrich DUO92101) following manufacturer’s instructions. The antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table 2. Images were taken with a 40x objective in a Nikon confocal microscope. Fluorescent PLA signal was quantified using Fiji.

In vitro kinase assay

The EGFR in vitro kinase assay (BPS Bioscience 40321) was performed by incubating recombinant human STAT1 and recombinant human EGFR kinase in the kinase buffer (provided with the kit) supplemented with 100 μM ATP and 2 mM DTT. The kinase reaction was performed at 30 °C for 40 min. Samples were boiled in SDS-PAGE sample buffer with 2.5% beta-mercaptoethanol and analyzed by western blot.

Statistical analysis

Differential gene expression analysis of scRNA-Seq data was performed using Cell Ranger and Loupe Cell Browser software (10x Genomics), which uses a variant of the negative binomial exact test from sSeq and the asymptotic beta test in edgeR depending on sample size81,82. Statistical analysis and graphs of other data were generated using GraphPad Prism v10. Pairwise comparisons were analyzed using two-tailed Student t test and multiple comparison with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For all graphs, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.
