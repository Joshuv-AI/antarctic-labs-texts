Transient increase in anxiety and depression following rotator cuff repair
Authors: Ayyappan V Nair, Kiran Veerendra, Aravind Rajan, Sreejith Thampy J, Bharath Bharadwaj MS, Prince Shanavas Khan, Bimal A Kumar, Yon-Sik Yoo, Hamdi Nizar Ahamed, Jimmy Joseph Meleppuram, Aditya Mahindru, Nitu Tiwari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients undergoing rotator cuff repair take long periods to recover and are restricted against using their arm initially, which could affect the mental status of these patients. Hence, we sought to assess the anxiety and depression in patients undergoing rotator cuff repair.MethodsA total of 42 consecutive patients who underwent arthroscopic rotator cuff repair and followed up for 12 months after surgery were enrolled. Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale and Montgomery Asberg depression rating scale were used to assess anxiety and depression, The clinical outcomes were assessed using the visual analog scale, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score, and Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (Quick DASH) score at 2 weeks, 4 weeks, 6 weeks, 8 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months.ResultsPatients undergoing rotator cuff repair had a significant increase in anxiety and depression at 3 months following surgery, which returned to pre-operative levels 9 months following surgery. It reduced to less than pre-operative levels at 12 months. Significant correlations between psychological measures and functional outcomes were observed predominantly at 3 and 6 months post-operatively, with stronger associations noted for QuickDASH scores. All the patients had a significant improvement in the QuickDASH score and the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score at final follow-up (P < .01).Discussion/ConclusionFollowing rotator cuff repair, there is a transient increase in anxiety and depressive symptoms. However, these symptoms reduce significantly at 12 months after surgery. Recognition of patients experiencing anxiety and depression and early psychological counseling in them may improve functional outcomes and facilitate complete recovery.Keywords: Rotator cuff repair, Rehabilitation, Anxiety, Depression, Orthopedics, Psychology, Mental health, Outcomes

Rotator cuff tears are among the most common musculoskeletal injuries encountered in clinical practice, particularly affecting the aging population. These injuries can result in substantial pain, loss of function, and a decline in quality of life, often necessitating surgical repair to restore shoulder mechanics and reduce symptoms.18 However, the impact of rotator cuff pathology and subsequent surgical intervention extends beyond physical symptoms, often influencing the psychological well-being of affected individuals.

Emerging literature highlights a significant association between musculoskeletal pain and mental health disorders such as anxiety and depression. Psychological distress has been shown to contribute not only to symptom perception but also to post-operative outcomes in orthopedic patients.2,16 Specifically, in the context of shoulder surgeries, pre-operative and post-operative depression have been identified as predictors of poorer functional recovery and patient dissatisfaction.7

Despite these associations, the mental health trajectories of patients undergoing rotator cuff repair remain underexplored. Furthermore, the early phase of post-operative recovery typically restricts shoulder use, leading to disruptions in daily activities and reduced independence, factors known to exacerbate psychological distress.10 Understanding this psychosocial dimension is vital for optimizing rehabilitation strategies and aligning patient expectations.

To evaluate depression and anxiety in our patient cohort, we employed two validated tools: the Montgomery Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS). The MADRS, a clinician-administered instrument, is specifically designed to assess the severity of depressive symptoms and is sensitive to changes in mood over time.1 The HADS, on the other hand, is a widely used self-reported screening tool that simultaneously evaluates symptoms of anxiety and depression, particularly in physically ill patients, and avoids confounding due to somatic symptoms.19

It is important to distinguish between a formal diagnosis of depression or anxiety and the presence of symptoms indicative of psychological distress. Our study focuses on symptomatology rather than clinical diagnosis, allowing us to assess subthreshold yet functionally significant emotional changes in the post-operative period. Clarifying this distinction is essential for interpreting our findings within the scope of surgical outcomes and patient-centered care.

We hypothesize that patients undergoing rotator cuff repair may experience symptoms of anxiety and/or depression post-operatively, and that these symptoms could be associated with variations in functional recovery. Through this study, we aim to highlight the need for comprehensive pre-operative counseling and integrated psychosocial support during rehabilitation.

Materials and methods

The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology

Guidelines was used to conduct this study. On getting the institutional review board approval, we prospectively enrolled patients undergoing rotator cuff repair surgery between February 2023 and February 2024. (IRB approval number – 05/1-23). All adult patients undergoing arthroscopic rotator cuff repair were eligible to participate in the study. All the 51 patients enrolled consented to participate in the study. All full-thickness rotator cuff tears with or without retraction, underwent an arthroscopic rotator cuff repair, and a double-row repair was performed in all the cases. All the surgeries were performed by a single surgeon and his supervised trainees. Patients were not randomized and consecutive patients who consented for the study were included.

Patients were immobilized for the first six weeks in an arm sling. Only elbow flexion and extension exercises were allowed. Shoulder passive forward elevation and external rotation was started at 6 weeks following surgery. At 8 weeks, patients were started on strengthening exercises so as to achieve full range and power.

We followed up these patients at biweekly intervals for 2 months and at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months following surgery. Initially, 51 patients were enrolled, out of which 9 patients did not follow-up till the end of the study. A total of 42 patients were included. All the patients were asked to fill up the forms in person during their visit to the out-patient department. The patients were ruled out of having clinical depression using the MADRS scale pre-operatively. The primary aim of the study was to determine the prevalence of depression and anxiety symptoms following surgery and its effects on the functional outcome.

To assess anxiety and depression symptoms, participants had to complete HADS12 and the Montgomery and Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS)1 pre-operatively and during regular follow-up visits to the clinic. Patients diagnosed with moderate depression (HADS D > 11) or anxiety (HADS A > 11), or MADRS for depression > 7, were given appropriate treatment in the form of counseling and pharmacotherapy, as deemed necessary by the psychiatrist. Twenty-five patients had to be referred during the entire study period to the psychiatrist.

The impact of this treatment on the functional outcome could not be objectively investigated. Functional outcome was assessed at this time using the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score14 and the Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (QuickDASH) score.6

The correlation between HADS A and HADS D at various time points was assessed using spearman correlation coefficient. Correlation analysis was performed between HADS, MADRS, and QuickDASH and ASES scores at various follow-up visits. Correlation strength was interpreted as: 0.00-0.19 very weak, 0.20-0.39 weak, 0.40-0.59 moderate, 0.60-0.79 strong, and 0.80-1.0 very strong.

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS software (version 29; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics, particularly the mean and standard deviation, were used to analyze the data. A paired t test was performed to compare the functional outcomes and the anxiety and depression scores at the various follow-up time points. Spearman correlation was conducted to analyze correlation. Significance level was defined as 0.05. Graphs were generated with the Matplotlib library (version 3.5.2; Matplotlib Development Team, San Francisco, California, USA)8

Discussion

This research article studied the effect of rotator cuff repair on the patients and found that there was an increase in the anxiety and depression symptoms during the initial phase of rehabilitation following surgery (highest at 3 months). This is in agreement to the previous studies on the topic.12,14,15

The author's previous research on the functional outcome following arthroscopic double-row rotator cuff repair showed improvement in Rowe Score, ASES scores at 6 months and was highest at 12 months.9 However, the correlation with the mental status of these patients had not been studied previously.

The reported prevalence of depression among patients with rotator cuff tears ranges from 12.8% to 26.2% when measured on the HADS.3,4

Another study reported depression symptoms in 3% of patients with shoulder instability and 8% of patients with rotator cuff rupture.11 In our study, we found that pre-operative anxiety or depression was found in 7.1% of the patients.

Pre-operative mental health disorders are known to cause increase in the rate of revision surgeries and post-operative complications.5 A study comparing patients having anxiety and depression pre-operatively and those without found that patients without mental disorders had significant improvements in patient-reported outcome measures. However, those with a pre-operative diagnosis of depression or anxiety had lower functional outcome scores at final follow-up.11

Park et al14 found that anxiety or depression negatively influenced the functional outcomes, specially pain, function, and range of motion. They concluded that an assessment of the pre-operative psychological status is warranted before rotator cuff repair surgery.

There was significant correlation between QuickDASH and HADS A, HADS D, and MADRS at 3 months and 6 months postsurgery in our study, indicating that improvement in function does improve the psychological status of the patients.

London et al13 concluded that patients with pre-operative depression symptoms reported worse outcomes at 3 months after procedures for atraumatic hand conditions; however, both patients with and without depression symptoms equally improved. In this study, patients had an initial decrease in function; however, all the patients improved significantly at the end of 9 months.

Few studies have reported a decrease in the prevalence of depression at 1 year after rotator cuff repair.3,17 In our patient cohort, only 1 patient (2.3%) had mild depression 12 months after surgery. Recognition of patients experiencing anxiety and depression, and early psychological counseling in them, may improve functional outcomes and facilitate complete recovery.

This study is unique in identifying the patient's psychological response to surgery and rehabilitation. It can guide the clinicians to be supportive of the patients and to be prepared in advance to identify and treat such symptoms for an overall improved functional outcome.

The limitations of our study could be a relatively small sample size, as all the patients were from a single center. All the patients were enrolled irrespective of the comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus or hypertension. It is unclear whether the depression and anxiety improved due to the psychological intervention or it was due to the overall improvement in the shoulder function. It was ethically not possible to ascertain the above in this study.

Conclusion

Patients undergoing rotator cuff repair are expected to have major limitations in their daily activities owing to the time required for complete healing and immobilization of the arm in a sling. This can affect the mental well-being of the patients. However, this can likely be tackled by pre-operative education, supportive care, and psychological counseling during the recovery period.
