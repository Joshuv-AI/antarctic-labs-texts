Prevalence of comorbidities and their impact on prognosis among patients with rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis: a multicenter retrospective cohort study in China
Authors: Liping Zou, Zhili Li, Qing Pan, Jiaojie Ma, Peijun Tang, Wanli Kang, Qing Chen, Zhengyu Shi, Xianzhen Tang, Chunhui Guo, Juan Du, Qingdong Zhu, Song Yang, Zhanlin Chang, Zhouli Guo, Shenjie Tang, Guihui Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Comorbidities are a significant factor affecting the prognosis of patients with rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (RR-TB). We evaluated the prevalence of comorbidities and their prognostic effects in a Chinese RR-TB cohort. In this study, we reviewed the clinical data of RR-TB patients who started anti-tuberculosis treatment in China between May 2018 and April 2020 by conducting a multicenter cohort study. A log-binomial regression model was used to analyze the relationship between comorbidities and the treatment outcomes of RR-TB. The burden of comorbidities among RR-TB patients in China was heavy, with 49.45% (670/1355) experiencing at least one comorbidity. The most common comorbidities were diabetes (17.79%), followed by other respiratory diseases (11.14%), other liver and kidney diseases (5.31%), hypertension (5.24%), immunodeficiency (4.94%), viral hepatitis or carriers (3.91%), severe heart disease (2.58%), tumours (1.11%), and chronic kidney disease or renal insufficiency (0.74%). Diabetes (RR = 1.31), severe heart disease (RR = 1.70), tumours (RR = 1.89), hypertension (RR = 1.28), aged ≥ 60 years (RR = 1.64) and 45–59 years (RR = 1.38), ethnic minorities (RR = 1.58), retreatment cases (RR = 1.34), and those not using the bedaquiline regimen (RR = 1.92) significantly increased the risk of unfavorable treatment outcomes. While higher education or above (RR = 0.49), employment (RR = 0.51), and having a normal (R = 0.53) or overweight (R = 0.56) BMI could reduce the risk of unfavorable treatment outcomes. Patients with RR-TB beared a substantial comorbidity burden in China. Notably, the presence of comorbidities such as diabetes, severe cardiac disorders, tumours, and hypertension markedly elevate the risk of adverse treatment outcomes in these patients. It is necessary to strengthen the screening and management of comorbidities to optimize the treatment strategies.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is a global public health problem caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, which seriously threatens human health. According to the latest estimates from Global tuberculosis report 2024, there were 10.8 million tuberculosis cases worldwide in 2023, with an incidence rate of 134/100,000 and 1.25 million deaths due to tuberculosis. Tuberculosis has returned to the leading cause of death from a single infectious agent globally, with the number of deaths almost twice those of HIV/AIDS1. In the field of tuberculosis prevention and control, the emergence of drug-resistant tuberculosis, especially rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (RR-TB), has significantly exacerbated the challenges. Rifampicin, a core drug in TB treatment regimens, plays a critical role in effective therapy. Resistance to it leads to complexity in treatment protocols, long-term treatment, serious side effects, large economic burden, and low quality of life which may affect the prognosis. As a result, RR-TB has become one of the key global challenges in TB control.

TB patients frequently present with comorbidities including human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), diabetes mellitus, mental disorders, silicosis and viral hepatitis, et al.2. A study in Gabon indicated that 46% (104/227) of TB patients had at least one comorbidity or non-communicable disease risk factor (including hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, amd obesity). When HIV was considered, this prevalence rised to 65% (147/227)3. These comorbidities play an important role in the occurrence and development of tuberculosis. They increase the complexity of TB treatment, especially RR-TB treatment. During RR-TB treatment, it is necessary not only to manage drug-drug interactions between anti-TB therapy and treatments for comorbidities but also to address the treatment of the comorbidities themselves. Additionally, comorbidities may also affect the prognosis of RR-TB patients. Previous studies had indicated that various comorbidities such as diabetes, HIV, chronic kidney disease, and liver disease were associated with poorer treatment outcomes for RR-TB4–7. Research also indicated that among TB patients, only 17.3% of deaths were directly attributable to TB, while 82.7% resulted from non-TB-related causes, which underscored the critical impact of comorbidities on mortality8. Comorbidities have become a significant public health challenge for the End TB strategy. To enhance the treatment success rate of RR-TB, managing comorbidities is crucial.

However, current researches on comorbidities in RR-TB and their impact on prognosis remain inadequate. Most studies have only investigated the impact of multiple comorbidities on the prognosis of TB, without distinguishing between drug-resistant and drug-susceptible tuberculosis3,8. Additionally, some studies are limited to single-center, small-sample designs instead of multi-center, large-sample ones6,9. There are also studies that have only examined the impact of a specific disease or common diseases on the prognosis of RR-TB, without systematically investigating the influence of multiple comorbidities4–7. In China, there is also a lack of nationally representative studies that can investigate the prevalence of comorbidities among RR-TB patients and systematically assess the combined impact of multiple comorbidities on the prognosis of a representative RR-TB cohort. The study by Lecai J et al. was a single-center, small-sample study, and only evaluated the impact of chronic hepatitis B or C and diabetes on the prognosis of RR-TB10. Another study by Feng X et al. assessed the impact of comorbidities on drug-resistant tuberculosis, but did not evaluate their impact on the prognosis of RR-TB11. In our study, we reviewed the clinical data of RR-TB patients in China between May 2018 and April 2020 by conducting a multicenter cohort study, in order to investigate the prevalence of comorbidities and their impact on prognosis in RR-TB patients. These findings will carry critical implications for optimizing the treatment strategy and improving prognosis for RR-TB patients.

Methods

Sources

Research design and research object

A multicenter retrospective cohort study was conducted in eight cities in China, including Beijing, Chongqing, Anqing in Anhui Province, Chengdu in Sichuan Province, Harbin in Heilongjiang Province, Nanning in Guangxi Province, Suzhou in Jiangsu Province, and Wuhan in Hubei Province. The study enrolled patients with laboratory-confirmed RR-TB who initiated anti-TB treatment from May 2018 to April 2020, including cases of RR-TB, multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB), pre-extensively drug-resistant TB (Pre-XDR-TB), and extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB). The definitions of RR-TB, MDR-TB, Pre-XDR-TB, and XDR-TB were in accordance with the WHO12. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Laboratory-confirmed RR-TB; and (2) Age of 15 years or older. The exclusion criteria included patients with incomplete medical records. 1355 patients with complete data on comorbidities and treatment outcomes were included in the analysis. The overview of the study population in Fig. 1. Data collection for the retrospective cohort study was performed using a pre-specified, expert-validated case report form (CRF).

Data collection

Demographic information (including gender, age, ethnicity, place of residence, education level, occupation, BCG vaccination history, Body Mass Index (BMI), history of alcoholism and smoking) and disease information (including comorbidities, type, therapeutic regimens, and treatment outcome of RR-TB) were extracted from the CRF. Comorbidities were assessed according to the disease classification provided by the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10). This study focused on comorbidities that have been confirmed by previous research to be potentially related to the treatment outcomes of tuberculosis: diabetes, severe heart disease, tumours, immunodeficiency (including those with AIDS/HIV, autoimmune diseases, solid organ transplant recipients, and others experiencing weakened immunity), viral hepatitis (including carriers), and chronic kidney disease or renal insufficiency. Additionally, other comorbidities believed to be potentially related to the treatment outcomes were recorded in the database for further analysis, including hypertension, other respiratory system diseases (such as respiratory failure, bronchiectasis, pneumonia, hypoxemia, Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD), and pneumothorax), as well as other liver and kidney diseases (including fatty liver, abnormal liver function, liver cysts, kidney cysts, nephritis, and kidney stones). Re-treated TB is defined as Patients who have received irregular or non-standard anti-tuberculosis drug therapy for ≥ 1 month; or Patients with initial treatment failure or relapse.

Treatment outcome

WHO classifies the outcomes of tuberculosis treatment as treatment success (cured or completed treatment), treatment failure, death, loss to follow-up, and not evaluated13. We adopted the same criteria as the WHO for determining the treatment outcomes of patients.

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro–Wilk test was employed to assess the normality of the distribution for continuous data, revealing that none of the continuous data followed a normal distribution. The median (lower quartile, upper quartile) was used to describe the distribution of continuous data, while counts and proportions were utilized for categorical data. Either the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or the chi-square test was conducted for univariate analysis of between-group variation.

The values of the“comorbidity”variable include no comorbidity, diabetes, severe heart disease, tumours, immunodeficiency, viral hepatitis, chronic kidney disease or renal insufficiency, and other conditions as indicated in the CRF. If patients select “other”, they needed to specified the specific disease. We determined the presence and number of comorbidities in patients based on this variable.

We redefined the treatment outcomes as following: “Treatment success” or “favorable outcomes” merged cured and completed treatment, while “unfavorable outcomes” merged treatment failure, death, loss to follow-up, and not evaluated.

A log-binomial regression model was used to analyze the relationship between comorbidities and the treatment outcomes of RR-TB. Variables with P ≤ 0.05 in univariate analysis were included in the model. Our dependent variable in the model was the treatment outcome, which encompassed treatment success and other outcomes, while age, ethnicity, education level, occupation, BMI, type of patient, therapeutic regimens, diabetes, severe heart disease, tumours, immunodeficiency, and hypertension served as explanatory variables.

In this model, we transformed continuous variables into categorical variables, defining age groups as 15–29 years, 30–44 years, 45–59 years, and 60 years or older. BMI was categorized as lean (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2), normal (BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m2), and overweight (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2). Risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and P-values for the occurrence of other treatment outcomes were calculated to assess the risk of adverse tuberculosis outcomes, with a two-sided P-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit chi-square test was employed to evaluate the model’s fit, and all statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.2.2. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Result

The basic characteristics of patients

A total of 1355 patients were included in this study, including Anqing (51 cases, 3.76%), Beijing (168cases, 12.40%), Chengdu (373 cases, 27.53%), Harbin (397 cases, 29.30%), Nanning (27 cases, 1.99%), Suzhou (77 cases, 5.68%), Wuhan (234 cases, 17.27%), Chongqing (28 cases, 2.07%), with a median age of 42 years (Interquartile Range[IQR]:28, 56), and a median BMI of 20.70(IQR: 18.59,22.60)kg/m2. 1064 patients (78.52%) achieved treatment success. The majority of patients were male (947, 69.89%), and over half of the patients resided in urban areas (818, 60.37%) and were unemployed (787, 58.08%). Additionally, 402 cases(29.67%) had a history of smoking, and 265 cases (19.56%) had a history of alcohol abuse. The basic characteristics of the 1355 patients are shown in Table 1; Fig. 2.

Univariate analysis

In the unadjusted analyses, compared to individuals with treatment success, patients with unfavorable outcomes were older (median age: 49 years vs. 39 years, P < 0.01), lower levels of education (secondary education or below: 60.14% vs. 39.37%, P < 0.01), more likely belonging to ethnic minorities (13.06% vs. 4.32%, P < 0.01), lived in rural areas (45.02% vs. 38.16%, P = 0.03), unemployed or engaged in peasants (80.41% vs. 68.79%, P < 0.01), a lower BMI (median BMI: 19.56 vs. 20.96, P < 0.01), a history of smoking (35.74% vs. 28.01%, P = 0.01), a history of alcohol abuse (24.40% vs. 18.23%, P = 0.02), retreatment patients (66.32% vs. 54.23%, P < 0.01). However, fewer patients received treatment with the bedaquiline-contained regimens (9.28% vs. 22.18%, P < 0.01), and a higher proportion had comorbidities (60.14% vs. 46.52%, P < 0.01), including diabetes (21.99% vs. 16.64%, P = 0.03), severe heart disease (7.22% vs. 1.32%, P < 0.01), tumours (3.44% vs. 0.47%, P < 0.01), immunodeficiency (8.59% vs. 3.95%, P < 0.01), hypertension (0.08% vs. 0.05%, P < 0.05), and other liver and kidney diseases (7.56% vs. 4.70%, P = 0.05) (Tables 1 and 2).

Multivariate analysis

In the multivariate analysis, several factors were found to be positively associated with the occurrence of unfavorable outcomes. These factors included older age (45–59 years vs. 15–29 years: 1.38 [1.03–1.86], 60 years and older vs. 15–29 years: 1.64 [1.29–2.10]), belonging to an ethnic minority (1.58 [1.16–2.14]), being a previously treated patient (1.34 [1.07–1.67]), receiving regimens without bedaquiline (bedaquiline-excluded regimens vs. bedaquiline-contained regimens: 1.92 [1.32–2.81]) and having other comorbidities (diabetes: 1.31 [1.02–1.68], severe heart disease: 1.70 [1.28–2.26], tumours: 1.89 [1.37–2.61], hypertension: 1.28 [1.28, 1.28]).

In contrast, a higher education or above (0.49 [0.32–0.76]), being a worker, civil servant, or service staff (0.51 [0.30–0.87]), and having a higher BMI (normal: 0.53 [0.43, 0.64], overweight: 0.56 [0.37, 0.85]) were found to be negatively associated with the occurrence of unfavorable outcomes (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Some studies have explored the prevalence of comorbidities in patients with tuberculosis and analyzed the impact of a certain comorbidity on RR-TB. However, few studies have investigated the prevalence of comorbidities in RR-TB patients and the impact of various specific comorbidities on the prognosis of RR-TB. In our research, we investigated the comorbidities of RR-TB in China through a multicenter retrospective cohort study, and revealed a high comorbidity burden, with 49.45% of RR-TB patients experiencing at least one comorbidity. While most RR-TB patients had only one comorbidity, a considerable proportion also suffered from two or more concurrent comorbidities. Comorbidities posed a significant burden on RR-TB patients, and some comorbidities could adversely affect treatment outcomes. Therefore, it was crucial to prioritize the screening and management of comorbidities in RR-TB patients.

In our study, diabetes was the most common comorbidity in RR-TB patients, accounting for 17.79%, similar to the 17% reported by Kang et al.14. Compared with the non diabetes population, individuals with diabetes had a 1.5 to 2.4 times higher risk of developing TB, and the risk of contracting drug-resistant tuberculosis might further increase. Some studies had reported that the prevalence of MDR-TB among diabetes patients ranges from 10 to 30%15–17. Compared with MDR-TB patients without diabetes, those with diabetes exhibited poorer treatment responses, higher rates of treatment failure, and increased mortality14. Our study also suggested that diabetes was a risk factor for adverse outcomes in RR-TB patients. The underlying mechanisms might involve impaired immune function in DM patients, such as reduced Th1-related cytokine synthesis18, altered innate and type 1 cytokine responses19, as well as exaggerated inflammatory reactions and higher mycobacterial burden in DM patients which led to the delay of microbial clearance rate20. Active control of blood glucose levels in RR-TB patients with diabetes had demonstrated potential to ameliorate treatment responses and reduce relapse rates21. These findings highlighted the critical importance of systematic diabetes screening protocols and intensive glycemic control strategies in the management of this high-risk patient cohort.

In this study, we also found that RR-TB patients with severe heart disease, tumours, and hypertension were more likely to experience adverse treatment outcomes. Previous research had similarly confirmed that cardiac diseases, hypertension, and tumours were associated with increased mortality risks among TB patients22–25. Severe heart disease could impair heart function, leading to circulatory dysfunction, which might affect drug distribution and metabolism. Meanwhile, cardiac insufficiency might limit the use of certain drugs with good anti-TB effects, such as new anti-TB drugs like bedaquiline and delamanid with novel mechanisms26,27. Patients with tumour, who were in an immunosuppressed state and further compromised by treatment-induced physical damage, exhibited increased susceptibility to severe complications and mortality when complicated with MDR-TB.

RR-TB patients with hypertension on long-term antihypertensive therapy might experience drug-drug interactions (DDIs) with anti-TB drugs. Studies had suggested that hospitalized TB patients with comorbidities such as hypertension were at a higher risk of DDIs28. These intertwined comorbidities collectively exacerbated the patient’s condition, making treatment and management more challenging, while significantly compromising prognosis. Prioritizing the screening and management of these comorbidities through timely interventions is crucial to reduce adverse outcomes. Previous studies had also suggested that chronic kidney/river disease, chronic obstructive bronchitis, and the number of comorbidities were associated with adverse outcomes7,9. while our findings demonstrated inconsistencies, likely attributable to variations in included variables and population heterogeneity.

In addition to comorbidities, we also identified several other factors associated with poor outcomes, including older age, belonging to an ethnic minority, being a previously treated patient, receiving regimens without bedaquiline. The impact of age on the prognosis of RR-TB had been confirmed in multiple studies4,6. Aging-related progressive immunosenescence, exacerbated by prevalent multimorbidity, might impair host defenses against Mycobacterium tuberculosis and reduce treatment tolerance, subsequently exerting detrimental effects on patient outcomes4,6. The relatively suboptimal economic conditions, scarce medical resources, and inconvenient transportation in western China might impede timely and effective treatment for RR-TB patients, along with a higher rate of loss to follow-up during treatment. This could be one of the primary drivers of prognostic disparities among ethnic groups29. Moreover, genetic factors might contribute to these disparities. Previous studies had established interethnic variations in genetic polymorphisms that predisposed to tuberculosis30. A history of prior tuberculosis treatment was also associated with adverse outcomes. This correlation can be primarily explained by several critical clinical factors. First, patients undergoing retreatment have a substantially higher likelihood of harboring drug-resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis strains31,32, often resulting from previous non-standardized or incomplete anti-tuberculosis therapies, which increased the complexity of subsequent treatments and prolonged the therapeutic course. Additionally, retreated patients frequently present with more advanced pulmonary pathology, including cavitary lesions33. Pulmonary cavities reduce drug penetration into affected tissues, shelter bacilli from antimicrobial activity, and elevate bacterial loads, all of which contribute to an increased risk of treatment failure and relapse34. Moreover, repeated treatment processes might impose heavier physical and psychological burdens on patients, adversely affecting their quality of life and immune function, thereby further increasing the risk of poor prognosis29. However, in our study, we failed to observe any association between lung cavities and poor prognosis. This discrepancy may be related to factors such as sample size, model variations, and the potency of anti-TB treatment regimens. The choice of treatment regimens and medications directly impacted the therapeutic outcomes of RR-TB patients35. As a novel anti-TB, bedaquiline effectively inhibited the ATP synthase of Mycobacterium tuberculosis and demonstrated significant bactericidal activity against drug-resistant strains. Particularly for patients with severe drug resistance and limited treatment options, incorporating bedaquiline into therapeutic regimens could optimize treatment strategies and improve prognosis36–38. Additionally, studies had suggested that the use of bedaquiline in treatment regimens was a protective factor for successful treatment in re-treated MDR/RR-TB patients, and could promote cavity closure39,40.

In our study, we also made an interesting discovery: although it was theoretically predicted that Pre-XDR-TB and XDR-TB patients would exhibit the poorest treatment outcomes, our analysis failed to demonstrate an independent association between this condition and adverse prognoses.The following factors may explain this observation: Firstly, in our study, the definitions of Pre-XDR-TB and XDR-TB are based on the latest standards of WHO. Less than 20% of the patients were classified as Pre-XDR-TB (19.56%) or XDR patients (0.15%). This limited sample size may have reduced the statistical power to detect a significant association, even if a clinically important difference exists. Secondly, In our cohort, many RR-TB patients with high levels of drug resistance and complex treatment needs received regimens containing new drugs (such as bedaquiline). The improved efficacy of these regimens for highly drug-resistant tuberculosis may have mitigated the additional risk associated with Pre-XDR-TB, narrowing the outcome gap with less drug-resistant RR-TB and MDR-TB.Another study conducted in China also supports this conclusion41.

Our study also found that some factors were inversely associated with poor prognosis. Compared to patients with lower education, unemployment, or below-normal BMI, those with higher education or above, employed occupations (workers, civil servants, and service staff), and normal or above BMI were more likely to achieve successful treatment outcomes, which was similar to the findings of Panford V et al.42. Good treatment adherence was an important factor for improving prognosis, and patients’ educational levels were significantly correlated with adherence43. Generally, individuals with higher education demonstrated better adherence as they possessed a more comprehensive understanding of the disease, stronger capacity to comprehend and accept treatment regimens, and greater initiative in following medical instructions (such as timely drug administration and routine follow-up examinations). Consequently, they were more likely to achieve favorable therapeutic outcomes. Additionally, income levels and economic status also influence treatment adherence44,45. Employed individuals with stable financial resources could afford treatment costs and often benefit from social security systems, enabling access to more comprehensive medical services. This reduced treatment interruptions or non-adherence caused by financial constraints, thus facilitating better disease control. Nutritional status could affect immune function46. BMI served as a crucial indicator for assessing patient’s nutritional status. A higher BMI generally indicated better nutritional reserves, which helped enhance immune function, improved tolerance to anti-TB treatment, and reduced the risk of adverse prognoses. However, it should be noted that an excessively high BMI might also bring other health problems, such as cardiovascular diseases47. Therefore, in future research, it is necessary to further clarify the optimal BMI range and its impact on prognosis.

We explored the comorbidities with RR-TB and their impacts on prognosis through a large-scale multicenter research project in China, overing a wide range of regions and ethnic groups, making the results more representative, which could provide valuable references for clinicians in assessing the prognosis of RR-TB patients and formulating treatment strategies. Nonetheless, there were still certain limitations. Firstly, the retrospective design limited data availability, for example, treatment regimens were only categorized by bedaquiline use, lacking other specifics. Secondly, all hospitals participating in this study were tertiary hospitals, where patients generally had relatively complex conditions and might suffer from more comorbidities. Therefore, the research results could only represent the status of comorbidities in RR-TB patients in tertiary hospitals, with limited representativeness. Moreover, tertiary hospitals boasted high medical standards, and 5 of these were among the first in China to use bedaquiline, a new anti-TB drug. This might result in a relatively higher treatment success rate observed in this study. The results might not be representative of the national situation. In the future, prospective studies can be carried out to collect more key variables such as treatment plans, therapeutic drugs, and treatment courses, to assess their impact on prognosis. At the same time, the sample coverage can be expanded, for instance, by including more community health centers and secondary hospitals, to enhance representativeness. Additionally, further research can be conducted to evaluate the impact of effective management of comorbidities on prognosis.

Conclusion

In summary, our study revealed a substantial comorbidity burden among RR-TB patients in China, with nearly half of these patients suffering from at least one comorbidity. Comorbidities including diabetes, severe heart disease, tumours, and hypertension significantly increased the risk of unfavorable outcomes in RR-TB patients. Based on the comorbidity burden and risk factors of RR-TB patients in China, a stratified and comprehensive intervention strategy should be adopted. clinicians, pharmacists, or other health workers should proactively manage comorbidities (e.g., intensive diabetes control, cardiac monitoring for severe heart disease, integrated oncology-TB care); prioritize individualized regimens containing more effective and drugs like bedaquiline; ensure adherence support for retreatment and elderly patients, and provide culturally tailored care for ethnic minorities; strengthen social support (promoting education/employment, and nutritional support to maintain normal/overweight BMI). Through engaging in multidisciplinary collaboration, optimizing pharmacotherapy, controlling comorbidities, and strengthening social support, the treatment efficacy will be maximized.
