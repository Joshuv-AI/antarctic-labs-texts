In ovo toxico-pathological effects of medicinal plants used against coccidiosis on chicken embryos development and hatchability☆
Authors: Ferdinand G Tchodo, Hervé B Dakpogan, Benjamin Adjei-Mensah, Ombortime N'nanle, Simplice Karou, Wéré Pitala, Kokou Tona, Batomayena Bakoma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTOXICO-pathological effects of herbal plants have always been a major concern, but little information is provided on the toxico-pathological effects of medicinal plants used against coccidiosis. This study aimed to assess the histopathological effects of Carica papaya seeds (CPS), Azadirachta indica leaves (AIL), and Sarcocephalus latifolius root (SLR) used as coccidiostat in traditional poultry farms with various doses using the chick embryo model. A total of 420 Sasso breeder eggs at ED4 of incubation were inoculated with the extract of these plants following a 3×4 factorial design with 3 plant extracts (CPS, AIL and SLR) and 4 inoculation doses (0, 25, 50 and 100 mg/kg egg-weight). From ED6 to ED19, the weights of the albumen and embryo were recorded, and the weights of ED19 embryo organs such as liver, heart, kidney, and lungs were measured. Additionally, histopathological lesions were examined. The results indicated that the presence of various phytoconstituents such as alkaloids, phenolics, flavonoids, tannins, saponins, coumarins, steroids, and triterpenes with statistically significant free-radical-scavenging ability differed among the plant extracts (p < 0.0001). Toxico-pathological examination revealed a dose-dependent slight toxicity (p < 0.0001) of the Azadirachta indica leaves extract compared to the other plants. Additionally, the relative organ weight showed kidney hypertrophy (p = 0.001) and liver hypertrophy (p = 0.0001), as well as dilation of hepatic and cardiac vessels. The conclusion drawn was that chicken embryos are more susceptible to in ovo inoculation with Azadirachta indica leaves compared to Carica papaya seeds and Sarcocephalus latifolius root.Keywords: Toxico-pathology, Antioxidant, Medicinal plant, Coccidiosis, Embryonic development

Introduction

In the poultry industry, over 70 million chicken meat are produced each year and between 1970 and 2020, global poultry meat production increased from 15.1 million tons to 137 million tons. In the OECD/FAO Agricultural Outlook until 2030, an increase in poultry meat production to 153 million tons is projected (OECD/FAO, 2023). This intensive production predisposes poultry to an increase in the incidence of poultry diseases (Qaid et al., 2021).

Among these diseases, coccidiosis stands out as a significant and devastating condition, inflicting substantial economic losses on the poultry industry in the world. It is a widespread intestinal disease caused by intracellular parasites belonging to the phylum Apicomplexa, the family Eimeridae, and the genus Eimeria, posing significant implications for both animal welfare and industry sustainability (Blake et al., 2014). Nine species of Eimeria have been identified and recognized in domestic chickens (Morris et al., 2007). Among these, E. brunetti, E. maxima, E. necatrix, and E. tenella are considered the most pathogenic. E. acervulina, E. mitis, and E. mivati are considered the less pathogenic, while E. praecox and E. hagani are the least pathogenic or non-pathogenic species (Joyner & Long, 2008, Adjei-Mensah & Atuahene, 2023). The disease manifests through the invasion of epithelial tissues within the intestine, resulting in the destruction of the intestinal mucosa by sporulated oocysts. This process suppresses the immune system causing symptoms of different severity (from moderate to severe) leading to high mortalities and notably poor performance and productivity in poultry (Dalloul et al., 2006). The annual losses due to coccidiosis are estimated to be approximately £10.36 billion for anticoccidial drugs (e.g., ionophores and synthetic chemicals) administered in feed or water, based on a global re-calculation (Blake et al., 2020).

The prevalence of coccidiosis among infected birds depends fundamentally on the characteristics of Eimeria biology (Blake et al., 2015), but also from innumerable other geographic and climatic factors (Luu et al., 2013). The factors influencing parasite occurrence include bird genotype (breed), age, and previous exposure history. Thus, poor management of sanitary conditions, housing, and management systems increases Eimeria occurrence, burden, and disease (Gharekhani et al., 2014). All these factors contribute to the parasites' ability to survive as oocysts in the environment (Belli et al., 2006), and their tendency to develop drug resistance poses a significant threat to the secure production of poultry-derived food products (Chapman et al., 2013). Various anticoccidial feed additives; predominantly ionophores antibiotics, anticoccidials, vaccines, and strict management practices (biosecurity) have been developed and used as a preventive method against coccidiosis (Chapman et al., 2005). However, the routine use and misuse of these drugs have led to coccidial parasite drug-resistant strains (Baynes et al., 2016) and are prejudicial to consumer health because of potential drug residue accumulation in poultry products (meat and egg). All these are the main limitations to the use of anticoccidial drugs.

Therefore, alternative and more sustainable methods are constantly sought to prevent coccidiosis in the poultry industry (Shivaramaiah et al., 2014). Ethnoveterinary medicine often provides cheaper options than synthetic drugs, and the products are locally available and more easily accessible (Qasem et al., 2020). This interest in medicinal plants can be explained by the fact that plants are the main source of new bioactive molecules for pharmaceutical industries and are considered harmless (Diallo et al., 2020). In the poultry industry, many plant products have been reported to be in use already in the various regions and countries of Africa for the treatment of poultry diseases. These plants need to be investigated for their efficacy and toxicity and those substances that are proven to be effective with minimal toxicity could be recommended for use in the veterinary medicine industry. For a long time, Carica papaya leaves or seeds (Caricaceae), Azadirachta indica leaves (Meliaceae) and Sarcocephalus latifolius bark or root (Rubiaceae) have been used in traditional poultry farming for coccidiosis treatment (Dakpogan et al., 2018; Rabia et al., 2022; Abbas and Alkheraije, 2023), without considers the dosage and administration length, two important notions, in toxicology (Hayes et al., 2014). In addition, very little research has addressed the evaluation of such medicinal plants and their toxicity on embryo development. Therefore, the present study aimed to carry out basic toxicological studies and establish the safety of the hydroalcoholic extracts of Carica papaya seeds, Azadirachta indica leaves and Sarcocephalus latifolius root, focusing on gross and histopathological lesions using the Chick Embryo Model. In addition, phytochemical screening, antioxidant activity and in ovo effect of these extracts were investigated on hatchability.

Materials and methods

Ethics and Study Site

This research was approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee guidelines of the Regional Center of Excellence on Poultry Sciences, University of Lome (CERSA-UL). The study was carried out at the hatchery of the poultry research unit of CERSA-UL, following the guidelines of the Canadian Council on Animal Care (2009).

Hatching Eggs

The present study was conducted on hatching eggs from a 33-week-old breeder flock of Sasso chicken broiler line (Hendrix Genetics, France). The Sasso broiler breeders were housed in separate floor pens within a hygienic environment characterized by a wooden shavings-covered floor house, adhering to standard husbandry practices in an open housing system. Eggs selected for tests were free from shell defects and exhibited an average egg weight of 56.08 ± 0.92 g. A total of 245 eggs were collected once from the flock, stored for one day at 16°C, were individually numbered, weighed and subjected to incubation at a temperature of 37.5°C and relative humidity of 55%, with the air sac positioned upwards (vertically) and subject to automatic turning at hourly intervals for the first 18 days of incubation (ED18) in a Petersime® incubator (CIDAPE, France).

Plant collection and extract preparation

The entire leaves of Azadirachta indica and Carica papaya seeds were purchased from Badja, Ave district of Togo, while Sarcocephalus latifolius roots were obtained from the Gbossime market in Lome, Togo. Authentication of the plant materials was conducted at the Botany and Ecology Department of the University of Lome. Subsequently, the leaves, seeds, and roots were thoroughly washed and dried under controlled air conditioning conditions (20°C), following which they were ground into a powdered form using an electric grinder. Hydro-ethanolic extract were prepared according to the method described by Bakoma et al. (2013). Briefly, 300 g of each plant powder was soaked in 3L of ethanol/water (70:30) for 72 hours under continuous agitation using the shaker SM-30 CONTROL stirrer (Labortechnik, Switzerland). The mixtures were then filtrated three times with hydrophilic cotton and two times with Whatman filter paper and the filtrate of each plant was evaporated at 45°C using a rotate evaporator BUCHI R-210 (Labortechnik-Materialtechnik, Switzerland).

Qualitative phytochemical screening

The active components of Carica papaya seeds, Azadirachta indica leaves and Sarcocephalus latifolius root extracts were screened in the Pharmaceutical Sciences Research Laboratory, Faculty of Health Sciences, University of Lome, Togo. Standard methods, as previously described by Karumi et al. (2004) and Rania et al. (2019), were employed to ascertain the presence of certain phytochemicals.

Determination of total polyphenol content

The total polyphenol content of the extracts was determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu method (Shetty et al., 1995) with slight modifications. A volume of 1000 μL solution of Folin-Ciocalteu diluted to one-tenth, was added to 200μl of the extract, and the mixture was incubated for 2 min at room temperature. Then, 800 μl Sodium carbonate at 75 g.l−1 was added. The mixture was thoroughly mixed and the absorbance was measured using a spectrophotometer (Visible Spectrophotometer, VWR International Europe bvba, China) at 765 nm after 30 minutes of incubation at room temperature, while protected from light. The calibration curve was constructed using gallic acid standards ranging from 0 to 100 μg.ml-1. Three tests were performed for each extract. The total polyphenol content was expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per gram of dry weight (DW).

Determination of total flavonoids

The method employed was that described by Bakoma et al. (2019). A volume of 2 mL of 2% (w / v) AlCl3 in pure methanol was mixed with an equal volume of extract (1 mg/ml) in methanol. The absorbance was read after 10 min in the dark at 415 nm using a spectrophotometer. Quercetin has been used as a reference substance. three tests’ replicates per extract were performed and the results are expressed in mg of quercetin equivalent (mg EqQ) per mg of extract. The standard range (quercetin 0 to 100 mg /L) was carried out under the same conditions.

In vitro antioxidant assays

DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) free radical scavenging activity: The extracts’ ability to scavenge free radicals was carried out by the 2,2′-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl tests (DPPH) as a stable free radical according to the method described by Bakoma et al. (2019). To determine the antioxidant activity (AAO) of the various extracts, 100 µL of each extract, dissolved in methanol at a concentration of 1 mg/mL, was introduced to 2 mL of a methanolic solution containing DPPH (0.004%). The mixture was vigorously vortexed to ensure homogeneity, and absorbance was read after 30 min in the dark at 517 nm using a spectrophotometer. Three independent tests were conducted for each sample. As a positive control, a solution of a standard antioxidant, quercetin (with concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 mg/mL), was prepared, and its absorbance was assessed under the same conditions as the sample. The results were determined using the equation derived from the calibration curve and were expressed in milligrams of quercetin equivalent per milligram of extract (mg EqQ/mg sample).

Ferric-reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay: A modified method described by Amaeze et al. (2011) was adopted for the ferric-reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay. This method relies on the sample's capacity to reduce the ferric tripyridyltriazine (Fe(III)-TPTZ) complex to ferrous tripyridyltriazine (Fe(II)-TPTZ) at low pH. The formation of Fe(II)-TPTZ results in a strong blue color, which can be read at 593 nm. Specifically, 2 mL of freshly prepared FRAP solution, containing 25 mL of 300 mM acetate buffer at pH 3.6, 2.5 mL of 10 mM 2,4,6-tripyridyls-triazine (TPTZ) in 40 mM HCl, and 2.5 mL of H2O for the standard FRAP, and 20 mM ferric chloride {FeCl3·6H2O} solution for the sample FRAP, was mixed with 100 μL of the extracts. The absorbance was then measured at 593 nm after 30 min in the dark area. The standard curve was linear between 0 and 100 μM FeSO4·7H2O. Results are expressed in μM Fe(II)/g dry plant material and compared with that of ascorbic acid.

Experiment design

On the fourth day of incubation (ED4), eggs underwent candling to identify and remove infertile ones. Eggs displaying evidence of living embryos (indicated by the presence of blood vessels) were randomly allocated to 12 groups according to a 3 × 4 factorial design (Fig. 1). The design involved the use of three different medicinal plants known for their efficacy against coccidiosis (Azadirachta indica leaves (AIL), Carica papaya seeds (CPS), and Sarcocephalus latifolius roots (SLR), each at four concentrations (0, 25, 50, and 100 mg per kg of egg-weight). Before injection, the extracts were diluted in PBS buffer and filtered through Whatman filter paper No. 1 to eliminate any microparticles. Eggs were then punctured with a 0.5 mm thick needle, and the injections were administered using insulin syringes as described in the experimental design (Fig. 1).

Experimental design. Abbreviations: NC: Normal control (without injection), CP: Carica papaya seed, AI: Azadirachta indica leaves, SL: Sarcocephalus latifolius root inoculated at doses of 0, 25, 50 and 100 mg/Kg egg-weight on day 4 of incubation.

After the administration of the plant extracts, the puncture site on the eggshell was sealed using a hypoallergenic adhesive bandage. Eggs were then returned to the incubator and allowed to continue their development. Throughout the incubation period, the viability of the embryos was periodically assessed via candling. On the 18th day of incubation (ED18), meticulously examined for both macroscopic and microscopic lesions. Any eggs exhibiting signs of late mortality during the candling process on ED18 were excluded from further analysis. The remaining eggs were transferred to an egg hatcher (Petersime® incubator, CIDAPE, France), where hatching was facilitated under conditions of 37.4°C temperature and 60% relative humidity.

Embryo and albumen weight measurements

From embryonic day 6 (ED6) to the internal pipping stage (ED19), 15 eggs per group were opened at each embryonic and embryo weights were recorded. Additionally, the remaining albumen from the same eggs was also recorded.

Embryonic organ examination, hatchability, and chick quality assessment

On day 19, embryos were weighed together with the yolk sac. Subsequently, they were euthanized by decapitation, and necropsy examinations were performed on all embryos. Histological examination

In the laboratory, liver and heart section slices were then taken and systematic samples were taken. After dehydration in different ethyl alcohol baths of increasing degree, a 5-μm section of each sample underwent impregnation and coating. Then thin sections were made using the microtome, spread on object slides to be stained with hematoxylin-eosin. The slides were observed and interpreted using an optical microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US, Massachusetts) at X100 magnifications following the procedure of Al-Sabaawy et al. (2021).

Effect of the extracts on the albumen and embryo weight up to ED18 of incubation

The absolute weight of albumen decreased as the incubation duration progressed, as shown in Fig. 3. There was no differential expression of egg albumen utilization among groups of plant extract; however, the albumen weight was significantly reduced in non-inoculated eggs (p = 0.0121) compared to eggs that had been treated with extracts at 25, 50, and 100 mg/kg egg weight, regardless of the plant type. Embryo weight increased with the progression of incubation age. On embryonic day 6 (ED6), embryo weight remained consistent across all extract doses administered. However, the plant extracts notably influenced embryo weight. From ED 12 to ED 18, embryo weights were influenced by the inoculation dose of plant extracts (p < 0.0001). These weights were 25.67g for non-inoculated embryos (0 mg/kg of egg weight) and 23.98, 24.51, and 23.65g for embryos receiving 25, 50, and 100 mg/kg of egg weight, respectively. However, no discernible distinction was observed when considering only the plant species. Nevertheless, a significant interaction between the plant extracts and the doses (P = 0.0003) was also noted. These interactions showed for example that embryos receiving the extract of Sarcocephalus latifolius at 50 mg/kg of egg weight (24.59 g) had a statistically higher weight than those receiving the extract of Azadirachta indica at 100 mg/kg of egg weight (22.33 g).

Effect of hydroethanolic medicinal plant extracts on relative embryo organs weight at internal piping stage (ED19) of incubation

Based on the results presented in Fig. 4, in ovo inoculation revealed that the liver of embryos was affected by plant extracts, whereas the kidney of embryos was significantly impacted by both plant extracts and the doses of inoculation. Specifically, embryos from the group inoculated with Azadirachta indica leaf extracts exhibited liver hypertrophy (p = 0.0001) compared to those treated with other plant extracts. This pattern was also evident with Azadirachta indica leaf extracts (0.238 g) and Carica papaya seed extracts (0.224 g) inoculated at 50 mg/kg egg-weight (0.242 g) and 100 mg/kg egg-weight (0.240 g) in comparison to Sarcocephalus latifolius root extracts (0.214 g) inoculated at 25 mg/kg egg-weight (0.216 g) and 0 mg/kg egg-weight (0.204 g) on fertile eggs on the piping stage (ED19) of incubation. The relative weights of the heart and lungs did not exhibit significant differences between the groups treated with different plant extracts or doses of inoculation.

Effect of hydroethanolic medicinal plant extracts on hatchability, embryo mortality and chick weight of Sasso chicks

The outcomes of in ovo inoculation with medicinal plant extract on embryo mortality, hatchability, and chicks' weight are detailed in Table 3. Overall, hatchability was significantly lower (p < 0.0001) in eggs inoculated with Azadirachta indica (79.20%) compared to those inoculated with Sarcocephalus latifolius (84.02%) and Carica papaya (83.97%). Hatchability in the Azadirachta indica and Carica papaya groups exhibited an inverse relationship with the inoculation dose, unlike the Sarcocephalus latifolius extract, which displayed dose-dependent effects on hatchability and mortality. However, the mortality rate was impacted differently by the dose of Carica papaya extract administered. The lowest embryo mortalities were observed in the groups inoculated with Sarcocephalus latifolius extract at 50 mg/kg egg-weight and Carica papaya extract at 100 and 50 mg/kg egg-weight. An interaction between plant extracts and inoculation dose was noted for hatchability and embryo mortality (p < 0.0001). Chick weights were influenced by both the plant extracts and dose levels administered, as well as the interaction between plant extracts and dose (p < 0.0001). Specifically, the weights of day-old chicks were significantly higher (p < 0.0001) in the Sarcocephalus latifolius extract group (36.80 g) compared to the Azadirachta indica, Carica papaya extract groups and standard Sasso chick weight (36.24 g, 36.23 g and 35.20), respectively.

In ovo inoculation of plant extracts on chick quality

Table 4 presents the impact of in ovo inoculation of SAC extract on chick quality in Sasso chickens. The study revealed that overall chick quality scores were significantly lower (p < 0.001) with Azadirachta indica extract (94.85%) compared to Carica papaya extract (96.25%) and Sarcocephalus latifolius extract (96.94%). The total chick quality scores exhibited an inverse relationship with the dose of Azadirachta indica extract and a dose-dependent pattern with Sarcocephalus latifolius extract. However, the various doses of Carica papaya extract inoculated had differing effects on total chick quality at hatching. This score increased with a concentration of 50 mg/kg Egg-weight but became similar to the low concentration inoculated (25 mg/kg Egg-weight) at 100 mg/kg Egg-weight.

Histopathology

Significant histological alterations associated with in-ovo Sarcocephalus, Azadirachta and Carica (SAC extracts) inoculation were noted in the liver and heart tissues. In Fig. 5, distinct cytoplasmic vacuolization of hepatocytes was evident in liver sections on the 19th day of incubation for groups SL0, AI0, CP0, SL25, SL50, and SL100. Slight cytoplasmic vacuolization of hepatocytes was observed in liver sections of groups AI25, AI50, and CP25. Notably, embryos exposed to AI100, CP50, and CP100 exhibited dilatation of embryonic liver sections and congestion of blood vessels.

Discussion

Currently, herbal remedies are increasingly being integrated into strategies for researching alternative solutions to antibiotics, but their potential side effects necessitate thorough investigation (Mahadavi et al., 2016). Consequently, this study was undertaken to assess the embryotoxic potential of Carica papaya seeds, Azadirachta indica leaves, and Sarcocephalus latifolius root, commonly used against coccidiosis in traditional poultry systems. Our investigation revealed a diverse array of phytoconstituents, including phenols, alkaloids, saponins, flavonoids, tannins, coumarins, triterpenes and steroids except alkaloids tannin and coumarins in Carica papaya seeds. This finding supports the recent study by Onyiche et al., 2021; Ying et al., 2021, who observed the existence of these phytochemicals in these plants. However, Kanadi et al. (2021) observed the existence of tannins in Carica papaya seeds. This could depend on the type of solvent, type of extraction, or climatic conditions of the region from which the plant samples were taken (Gunwantrao et al., 2016). In order to establish a potential correlation between activity and polyphenolic content, we also determined the quantity of phenols and flavonoids in these most active extracts. The results highlighted that hydro-ethanolic extracts from Azadirachta indica leaves, from Sarcocephalus latifolius root and Carica papaya seed are the richest in phenolic compounds, confirming the interest of this kind of secondary metabolites for coccidiosis control by the marked reduction in the oocyst count as reported by Onyiche et al. (2021) and Dakpogan et al. (2018). However, Sarcocephalus latifolius root extract had a higher content compared to the others. It also appears that flavonoid compounds constitute only a small part of the total phenolic content present in Carica papaya seed extract. This result agrees with the work of Sani et al. (2022), who found an insignificant impact on the total phenolic content (TPC) of Carica papaya. Muhamad et al. (2022) and Özcan et al. (2021) also found insignificant TPC of almond (Mentha aquatica and Prunus amydgalus respectively) varieties due to thermal dissociation of TPC during sonication-assisted extraction. The ability of plants to trap free radicals was evaluated. Thus, for the DPPH and FRAP, the hydro-ethanolic extract of Sarcocephalus latifolius roots showed the strongest antioxidant capacity, followed by Carica papaya seeds. This reducing capacity of free radicals in these plants could therefore be mainly due to the high level of phenolic compounds present in them (Kouassi et al., 2020) and to a polar character which can contribute to the potentialisation of power to reduce the free radicals (liu et al., 2015). Several studies have shown that the reducing power of phenolic compounds can be considered as an indicator of potential antioxidant effect (Bougandoura et al., 2012).

According to Amresh et al. (2008), the relative organ weight is an indicator of hypertrophy or atrophy of organs. In this study, the embryo organ relative weight of the heart and lung did not differ among the embryos exposed to plant extracts during embryonic development. However, liver and kidney hypertrophy were observed in embryos inoculated with Azadirachta indica extract in a dose-dependent manner. This result corroborates the report of Wang et al. (2013) who showed that the target organs of neem oil toxicity were the kidneys, liver, and testicles. Braga et al. (2021) also showed that neem extracts/subproducts’ toxicity is dose-dependent. Regarding hatching performance, the results showed that the inoculation of different extracts during embryonic development statistically affected hatchability, mortality, and chick weight at hatching. The Azadirachta indica extract presented low hatch rates and chick weights with a high embryo mortality rate compared to the other extracts. This could be attributed to the low antioxidant capacity observed in this study of Azadirachta indica leaves. It is established that during the late period of incubation, high metabolic activities induce oxidative stress in the embryo, leading to reduced performance and survival of the chick (Kuka et al., 2023). Kuka et al. (2023) and N'nanlé et al. (2017) reported a similarity in hatchability and increased hatch weight of chicks with in ovo administration of Soursop (0.25, 0.50 and 0.75 µg/ml) and Moringa oleifera leaves (0.5, 5 and 50 µg/ml), respectively. It was observed that the overall quality scores of the chicks varied significantly depending on the inoculated plant extract in this present study. The higher score with the Sarcocephalus latifolius root and Carica papaya seed extract also depended on the inoculation dose. Sarcocephalus roots and Carica papaya seed are rich in vitamin E, β-carotene, ascorbic acid (Maisarah et al., 2014; Osama et al., 2017) and several phytochemical compounds that have shown the free-radical-scavenging ability to reduce oxidative stress and improve the chick quality. The result of this study agrees with Kuka et al. (2023), Ngueda et al. (2021) and Araújo et al. (2019) who injected respectively Soursop leaf, cassava leaf and vitamin E and observed a significant increase in the overall quality scores of Noiler, Sasso and Cobb 500 broiler chicks. On the contrary, the low chick quality scores of the chicks inoculated with Azadirachta indica leaf extract could be explained by their low antioxidant capacity.

In our investigation, the histopathological alterations observed during in ovo administration was the dilatation of blood vessels in the liver and heart. These histopathological lesions were seen without any gross abnormality, especially with those embryos treated with Azadirachta indica leaf and Carica papaya seed. This alteration observed could be attributed to the capability of the bioactive compounds in the plants to reduce vascular integrity thereby causing vascular dysfunction and cellular senescence (Arauna et al, 2019). Another hypothesis explaining the induced alteration could be the outcome of certain active chemical compounds present in the plants. This finding is consistent with the studies of Derakhshanfar et al. (2019), who demonstrated that the aqueous extract of Origanum vulgare and Berberis vulgaris could induce apoptosis in embryonic organs and rats with liver cancer. Furthermore, the results point to a possible negative impact on liver development and vascular integrity that may be dose-dependent. Concerns regarding possible circulatory disruptions, which may affect the egestion of waste and the supply of nutrients during embryonic development, are raised by the observed blood vessel congestion in these groups (Awuchi et al., 2020). For example, cytoplasmic vacuolization may suggest metabolic stress or altered lipid metabolism in hepatocytes, which may impact energy metabolism and detoxifying processes necessary for the survival and development of embryos (Malhi et al., 2010). In a similar vein, the observed vascular congestion may impair the exchange of nutrients and oxygen, which could affect the growth and viability of embryos and thus the slight toxicity observed in the Azadirachta indica group. The research on developmental toxicity offers some insight, even though the exact processes causing the observed metabolic alterations need to be further clarified. Research has demonstrated that impaired liver development during embryonic growth can result in aging, aberrant metabolism, and heightened vulnerability to diseases in later life, all of which can have long-term effects on post-hatch performance (Brumbaugh & Friedman, 2014; Oke & Hardy, 2021).

Conclusion

In this research, the toxic effects of hydroalcoholic extracts from Carica papaya seeds, Azadirachta indica leaves, and Sarcocephalus latifolius root used against coccidiosis were evaluated in ovo using gross lesion and histopathological changes. The findings revealed a dose-dependent slight toxicity of the Azadirachta indica leaves extract, characterized by liver and kidney hypertrophy, as well as dilation of hepatic and cardiac vessels. Further studies to investigate the immune status of embryos at hatch would be crucial, mainly to assess whether immune priming against coccidiosis has occurred. Additionally, a follow-up study involving a coccidiosis challenge in older birds could help evaluate whether early immune stimulation translates into improved resistance post-infection.
