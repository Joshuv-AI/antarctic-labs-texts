Congenital left atrial appendage aneurysm in an adolescent: Diagnostic clues and surgical management
Authors: Yingjie Huang, Nouman Ahmad, Xuliang Chen, Lingjin Huang
Published: 2026

Computed tomography image showing an LAAA posterior to the left atrial appendage.

Central Message

Congenital left atrial appendage aneurysm can mimic other cardiac masses and pose diagnostic challenges.

Left atrial appendage aneurysm (LAAA) is a rare condition first reported in 19621 and characterized by abnormal dilation of the left atrial appendage that may result in arrhythmias, thromboembolism, or cardiac dysfunction.2,3 Congenital LAAA accounts for most cases and is thought to arise from developmental weakness of the appendage wall.2 Despite improvements in cardiac imaging, LAAA remains difficult to distinguish from other cardiac or pericardial masses. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges of LAAA and emphasizes the importance of surgical management to prevent serious complications.

Case Presentation

A 17-year-old male patient presented with a 10-year history of intermittent, stabbing chest pain relieved by rest. The delay in diagnosis was attributed to limited access to medical care and a lack of awareness of the significance of his symptoms. Findings of a physical examination and auscultation were unremarkable. Electrocardiography showed a normal sinus rhythm. Radiography of the chest demonstrated localized bulging of the left cardiac border. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed a 50- × 41-mm abnormal cystic structure posterior to the left ventricle (LV), with a left atrial diameter of 34 mm, an ejection fraction of 65%, and mild mitral and tricuspid regurgitation (Figure 1). Computed tomography (CT) confirmed a 56- × 41-mm cystic lesion compressing the LV and causing wall deformation (Figure 2).

Preoperative echocardiography demonstrating a cystic structure located posterior to the left atrial appendage, and mild mitral and tricuspid regurgitation. The mitral valve and other cardiac structures were normal.

Computed tomography confirmed the presence of the aneurysm, showing a cystic structure located posterior to the left atrial appendage, which caused compression of the left ventricle and resulted in left ventricular wall deformation.

Given the mass effect, surgical exploration was planned. Median sternotomy with cardiopulmonary bypass via the ascending aorta and bicaval cannulation was performed. Intraoperatively, a 60- × 40-mm aneurysmal dilation arising from the LAA was identified without adhesion to surrounding structures, confirming an LAAA (Figure 3, Video 1). The aneurysm and most of the LAA were excised, and the LAA stump was closed with continuous sutures. No thrombus or additional intracardiac abnormalities were present.

A, Intraoperative image showing the left atrial appendage aneurysm that was not adherent to the ventricular wall or pericardium. B, Aneurysmal resection and suture closure of the left atrial appendage.

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on day 5. Pathology revealed myocardial tissue and fibrous cyst wall–like tissue with a flat epithelial lining, consistent with congenital aneurysm formation (Figure 4). Follow-up TTE and CT at 1 month after the procedure demonstrated complete removal of the LAAA and resolution of LV compression (Figure 5). At 3 months, the patient remained asymptomatic, with full recovery made. Discussion

The absence of standardized diagnostic criteria for LAAA has contributed to variability in reporting and management. Foale and colleagues4 proposed criteria for congenital LAAA, including origin from a structurally normal left atrium, clear communication with the atrial cavity, an intrapericardial position, and distortion of the LV.

Although many patients are asymptomatic and diagnosed incidentally, LAAA may cause chest pain, palpitations, or dyspnea, typically presenting in early adulthood.2,5 In the present case, chest pain might have been the result of compression of the adjacent coronary artery; however, coronary catheterization and stress test electrocardiogram were not performed because the patient was young and the symptoms were not clearly suggestive of coronary ischemia. Reported complications include atrial tachyarrhythmias, thromboembolism from blood stasis, and heart failure resulting from ventricular compression.2,5 Early recognition is especially important in patients with unexplained atrial fibrillation.3

Multimodality imaging plays a central role in diagnosis.2,3 Although radiographs of the chest may show nonspecific abnormalities, TTE is the usual initial test, with transesophageal echocardiography and cross-sectional imaging (CT/magnetic resonance imaging) providing a detailed evaluation of aneurysm morphology and its relationship to surrounding structures.1,5 Differentiating LAAA from cardiac tumors or pericardial cysts is essential for appropriate management.

Surgical resection is generally recommended because of the risk of thromboembolism, arrhythmias, and progressive cardiac dysfunction. Recent studies indicate excellent outcomes with low recurrence rates after complete resection.1,2 Median sternotomy with or without cardiopulmonary bypass is the standard approach, particularly when thrombus is present.1

Minimally invasive and endoscopic techniques have been reported but require significant surgical expertise.E1 In summary, definitive diagnosis and surgical intervention remain crucial for preventing complications associated with LAAA, even in individuals who are asymptomatic.
