Antimicrobial Functionalization of Surfaces by a Chimeric Adhesive Protein
Authors: Rossana Pitocchi, Anna Pennacchio, Flavia Zuber, Qun Ren, Eugenio Notomista, Silvia Campioni, Gustav Nyström, Paola Giardina, Alessandra Piscitelli
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe main aim of this work is to account for the prevention and control of microbial growth on surfaces of interest in medical technology. Surface modification is often achieved by physiotherapy or chemical treatments that can involve time-consuming steps, hazardous reagents, and harsh conditions. One of the ways to overcome these drawbacks is the use of surface-active proteins such as hydrophobins. They can form stable protein layers on different surfaces, serving as anchoring points for other molecules of interest. The fungal hydrophobin Vmh2, already exploited for its adhesive ability, has been fused with the antimicrobial peptide GKY20, forming the chimeric protein used herein for functionalizing polystyrene (PS) and bacterial cellulose (BC). As a natural biomass, BC has multiple advantages, including biodegradability, low cost, renewability, high purity, and excellent mechanical properties. The chimeric protein has been proven to successfully adhere to both surfaces. A strong decrease in biofilm formation on PS and a good bactericidal effect of BC have been demonstrated. These findings provide evidence of an alternative strategy to obtain functional composites using a green, easy process.Keywords: antimicrobial peptides, chimeric protein, bacterial cellulose, hydrophobin, antibiofilm

Introduction

Microbial contamination of surfaces represents a serious concern both in material science and in medicine; therefore, prevention and control of microbial growth on surfaces are key issues currently addressed by many researchers.1,2 Despite many breakthroughs in science and technology, a significant proportion of implantable medical devices (e.g., heart valves, endovascular stents, implantable meshes, artificial lenses, or cochlear implants) are still susceptible to bacterial contamination. According to Van Epps and Younger,3 around one-fifth of implanted device failures are estimated to be caused by infections.

Wet/humid surfaces, an ideal substrate for planktonic cell adhesion and colonization, are usually involved in wound repair processes, often leading to problems like slow wound healing, secondary infection, and inflammation.4 The antibacterial activity of wound dressings can play an important role in preventing infections. Moreover, current antimicrobial therapy practices and easy access to antibiotics have led to the development of drug-resistant bacteria, making the problem serious from both an economic and health perspective.5 Massive efforts to create new anti-infective systems are urgently needed. Promising candidates are antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), also known as “Host Defense Peptides”, as they are essential components of the innate immune system.6 They exhibit relatively broad antimicrobial activity, even against drug-resistant pathogens and human-pathogenic fungi, and low toxicity toward eukaryotic cells. In addition, AMPs have multiple advantages, including thermal stability, good solubility, and, above all, lack of resistance.7 Mechanisms of action are diverse and dependent on the peptide and the pathogens with which they interact. In general, these mechanisms involve interaction and disruption of the integrity of the lipid membrane, transportation of compounds, and eventually cell lysis. On the other hand, nonmembrane targeting mechanisms can involve the inhibition of metabolic and translational processes.5,8 AMPs have been isolated from a great variety of organisms; The antimicrobial peptide database (APD, http://aps.unmc.edu/AP/)9 lists about 4000 AMPs, among which the majority are isolated from animals. AMPs are widely exploited to coat surfaces of biomedical interest to impart antimicrobial properties. Several studies have been focused, for example, on the prevention of infections of implantable medical devices (e.g., orthopedic prostheses).10−12

A peculiar subclass of AMPs is the so-called “cryptic” AMPs, hidden in the structure of larger proteins, often not involved in host defense, that become active after partial proteolytic degradation of the host protein.13,14 The antimicrobial peptide GKY20, for example, is a cryptic AMP derived from the C-terminal sequence of the human thrombin, and it is directly implicated in host defense.15 The AMP GKY20 shows a wide panel of biological and immunomodulatory functions combined with low toxicity and hemolytic activity. Regarding its mechanism of action, a physiochemical characterization pointed to a carpet-like complex mechanism of action involving electrostatic interactions, peptide conformational changes, lipid segregation, and domain formation as key steps in promoting membrane rupture.16

An emerging biomaterial widely exploitable to develop sustainable, nontoxic, and environmentally friendly products for the biomedical field is bacterial cellulose (BC).17 Multiple species in the genus Komagataeibacter have been described in the literature as efficient BC producers.18−20 The porous structure of BC, together with its high water retention capacity and purity degree, tensile strength, flexibility, and nontoxicity, makes BC a promising material for wound healing and skin tissue applications. Indeed, no adverse reactions were observed either in the short or in the long term when BC was in contact with skin tissues.21 Anyway, BC itself has no antimicrobial or antioxidant activity.22

Functionalization of surfaces of interest without the use of chemicals is one of today’s challenges, particularly in the biomedical/medicine field. Engineered proteins that combine the adhesive capacity of surface-active proteins (e.g., Hydrophobins, HPBs) with biologically active molecules (e.g., enzymes, peptides) represent a green tool in this context.23 HPBs are small cysteine-rich fungal proteins that can self-assemble in highly organized and stable layers at the solid–liquid or air–liquid interfaces, adhering to conventional and nanostructured surfaces.24,25 In particular, the class I HPB Vmh2, isolated from the basidiomycete fungus Pleurotus ostreatus, has already been used for the functionalization of various surfaces such as plastics, silicon, steel, and gold, alone or fused to other relevant proteins/peptides.23,26

In this work, Vmh2-GKY20, the chimeric protein formed by the HPB Vmh2 and the antimicrobial peptide GKY20,27 has been chosen for the development of innovative antimicrobial surfaces. Indeed, the optimized design of the coding sequence (with Vmh2 and GKY20 at the N-terminus and C-terminus, respectively) allows the achievement of a proper amount of chimera. Moreover, the presence of repetitive sequences of proline, serine, and alanine as a spacer guarantees the right exposure of the peptide and, consequently, its ability to bind bacteria.27 Herein, the antimicrobial effect of the immobilized chimera was first tested on polystyrene (PS), a surface already functionalized with other Vmh2-based chimera. Thereafter, Vmh2-GKY20 was immobilized on BC, an innovative material that is widely exploitable in wound healing applications.

Materials and Methods

Production of Vmh2-GKY20

A synthetic gene encoding for the peptide GKY20 in frame with a linker PAS (a repetition of proline serine and alanine) and with Vmh2 from P. ostreatus was designed (445 base pairs long).27 The coding sequence of interest was optimized according to the genetic code of Escherichia coli, synthesized by GenScript, restricted with NcoI and BamHI, and ligated into the corresponding sites of the pET22b (+) vector. The recombinant plasmid was transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3). A recombinant colony was transferred from solid culture to 20 mL of Lauria–Bertani (LB) culture medium (1% tryptone, 0.5% yeast extract, 1% NaCl, 100 μg/mL ampicillin) and incubated overnight at 37 °C. The cultures were diluted 1:100 with LB culture medium (1.2% tryptone, 2.4% yeast extract, 0.9% K2HPO4, 0.3% KH2PO4, 0.4% glycerol, 100 μg/mL ampicillin) and further incubated at 37 °C. The recombinant protein expression was induced using 1 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). The culture was incubated for 3 h at 37 °C. After induction, cells were collected by centrifugation at 7,000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min, resuspended in an appropriate lysis buffer, and treated as described in Sorrentino et al.,26 to obtain the recombinant protein. The protein concentration was evaluated using a Pierce 660 nm protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA) using BSA as the standard protein. Wild-type Vmh2 was purified as previously reported.28 The GKY20 peptide was chemically synthesized by INBIOS s.r.l. (Naples, Italy).

Antimicrobial Assay

Overnight cultures of Staphylococcus epidermidis RP62A grown in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI), Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1, and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 6538, both grown in LB, were diluted to a cell concentration of about 0.05 OD600 nm. Twenty-five microliters of the different cultures and 25 μL of the chimeric protein Vmh2-GKY20 at various concentrations were inoculated in each well of a 96-well plate, and then 50 μL of culture media was added and incubated in a static condition at 37 °C for 24 h. Vmh2 at 100 μg/mL (7 μM) and the antibiotics ampicillin, kanamycin, and vancomycin at 50 μg/mL, respectively, for P. aeruginosa, S. epidermidis, and S. aureus (considering their specific resistance) were used as controls. Cell growth was determined by measuring the absorbance at 600 nm. The data reported were statistically validated using the Student’s t-test comparing the mean absorbance of the treated and untreated samples. The significance of the differences between the mean absorbance values was calculated using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. A p-value of <0.05 was considered significant.

Adhesion of Vmh2-GKY20 on PS Wells

A volume of 100 μL of Vmh2-GKY20 at different concentrations (0.1 | 0.3 μg/μL) in 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, and 0.25 M urea was loaded into wells of a 96-well flat PS plate and incubated overnight (16 h) at room temperature in sterile conditions to let the solutions to evaporate. After the overnight incubation, the wells were washed three times with the same buffer to remove the proteins that were not adhering to the surface. In the case of the double deposition, the solution was not completely evaporated (6 h incubation) and the residual liquid (about 20 μL) was removed, then 100 μL of Vmh2-GKY20 were reloaded and incubated overnight (16 h) until complete evaporation. The wells were then washed as described above, and the immobilization yield was determined as the difference between the amount of deposited protein and that one measured in the residual liquid and/or in the washes. Vmh2 immobilization was performed by a single deposition of 300 μL of protein (0.3 μg/μL) following the above-described procedure.

Biofilm Assay on PS Wells

To analyze biofilm formation, one representative strain for Gram-positive and one for Gram-negative were used (S. epidermidis and P. aeruginosa, respectively).

Biofilm Formation and Quantification

Overnight cultures of S. epidermidis and P. aeruginosa, were diluted to a cell concentration of about 0.05 OD600 nm. One hundred microliters of this culture was added to each well of a 96-well plate, previously functionalized with Vmh2-GYK20 and Vmh2 used as control. As a negative control, the same cultures were incubated in uncoated wells. Quantification of the formed biofilm was performed analogously to what was reported by Sorrentino et al.26 The data reported were statistically validated using the Student’s t-test comparing the mean absorbance of the treated and untreated samples. The significance of the differences between the mean absorbance values was calculated using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. A p-value of <0.05 was considered significant.

Biofilm Thickness and Bacterial Viability Determined by Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy (CLSM)

For CLSM analysis, the S. epidermidis biofilm was investigated due to its easy visualization, using NuncTM Lab-Tek 8-well Chamber Slides. In brief, overnight cultures of RP62A were diluted to a cell concentration of about 0.05 OD600 nm, and 300 μL were inoculated into each well of a chamber slide, previously functionalized. The bacterial culture was incubated at 37 °C for 20 h in wells coated with either Vmh2-GKY20 or Vmh2. As a negative control, the same culture was added to uncoated wells. The biofilm cell viability was determined by the FilmTracer TM LIVE/DEAD Biofilm Viability Kit (Molecular Probes, Invitrogen), as already described by Artini et al.29 the proportions of live and dead bacteria were also quantified using BioFilmAnalyzer software v.1.0 22 by counting fluorescence-specific pixels in digital fluorescence images. Three different images per condition were selected for analysis. The COMSTAT software package30 was used to determine the biomasses (μm3/ μm2) of treated and untreated biofilm samples. For each condition, two independent biofilm samples were used.

Production of BC

Growth of Komagataeibacter medellinensis (strain ID13488) was performed in HS medium (20 g/L glucose, 2 g/L peptone, 5 g/L yeast extract, 2.5 g/L Na2HPO4, pH 4.5) in 15 mL tubes for 7 days at 28 °C, in static conditions. Cellulosic pellicles were visible at the liquid/air interface already after 3–4 days; nevertheless, they were harvested after 7 days, when they reached about 1–2 mm thickness. Cellulosic membranes were removed and washed with water to remove excess culture media. Then, they were first immersed in 0.1 M NaOH at 60 °C for 4 h, washed with water until reaching a neutral pH, and finally sterilized in an autoclave to eliminate any living cells. Upon treatment with alkali, the color of the BC pellicles (BCps) changed from yellow to transparent, and the pellicles so obtained were used for protein functionalization.

BC Functionalization

Functionalization was performed by submerging the pellicles in 2 mL of protein solutions in 15 mL tubes under mild agitation for 72 h (150 rpm, R.T.) to ensure adhesion on both faces of the pellicles, also considering the porous nature of BC. After incubation time, the BCps were rinsed and completely dried overnight, then washed three times with 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8) to remove the unbound proteins. Three protein concentrations were tested: 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 mg/mL, corresponding to 7, 14, and 22 μM, respectively. Control was performed using the AMP GKY20 at the same molar concentrations and Vmh2 at 14 μM. Adhesion yields were estimated by subtraction of the residual amount of proteins in solution after incubation to the initial amount (Bradford assay, BSA as a standard).

Antimicrobial Assay of Functionalized BC

Touch Test and Diffusion Assay

Bacterial colonies of the Gram-positive S. aureus and S. epidermidis and the Gram-negative P. aeruginosa were precultured from an agar plate in 5 mL of 30% Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) supplemented with 0.25% glucose and incubated overnight at 37 °C and 180 rpm. The overnight culture was diluted to 0.1 OD600 nm in 5 mL of fresh TSB and cultivated for around 1.5 h until an exponential growth phase. To obtain the final concentrations of 0.05 OD600 nm for S. aureus and 0.1 OD600 nm for S. epidermidis and P. aeruginosa, the bacterial cultures were diluted in 0.9% NaCl. Therefrom, 100 μL of bacterial suspension was spread on a Plate Count agar plate. For the touch test, functionalized BCps were placed on the agar with the bacterial lawn and allowed to interact for 2 h at 37 °C. Then, the bacterial growth of the touched surfaces was analyzed. For the diffusion test, another set of functionalized BCps was left on the agar plate at 37 °C overnight. The growth inhibition zone caused by the diffusion of the samples was analyzed afterward. Bacterial growth in the touched area (touch test) and the diffusion assay were imaged using a SCAN300 (Interscience).

Quantitative Evaluation

Further experiments to quantify the antimicrobial activity of functionalized BCps were carried out using S. epidermidis, which is used as a reference pathogen. Indeed, its behavior in the presence of Vmh2 and its engineered variant has already been studied in our laboratory.23,27 Colonies from an agar plate were added to 10 mL of BHI and incubated overnight at 37 °C and 180 rpm. One milliliter of the overnight culture was added to 10 mL of fresh BHI and cultivated for around 2 h until the exponential growth phase was reached. The bacterial culture was diluted with fresh media to obtain 0.1 OD600 nm. Two hundred microliters of bacterial suspension was added to 96-multiwell plates in the presence of untreated BCps (as control), and BCps functionalized with 22 μM of Vmh2-GKY20 or free GKY20. Multiwells were incubated under static conditions at 37 °C for 24 h. The optical density of grown bacterial cells was measured. Thereafter, these suspensions were vortexed for 5 min, diluted 108 fold, and spread on BHI-agar dishes. After 12 h incubation at 37 °C, the obtained colonies were counted. The data reported were statistically validated using the Student’s t-test comparing the mean absorbance of the treated and untreated samples. The significance of the differences between the mean absorbance values was calculated using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. A p-value of <0.05 was considered significant.

Results and Discussion

Production and Characterization of Vmh2-GKY20

The chimeric protein Vmh2-GKY20 (∼15 kDa) was designed to link the peptide GKY20 to the C-terminus of the HPB Vmh2 through a PAS spacer. The recombinant protein was found in E. coli inclusion bodies, purified, and refolded by dialysis in the presence of low urea concentration (0.25 M) to ensure its solubility and activity. A production yield of 20 mg/L of culture broth was achieved. To verify that the designed chimeric protein retains both the antimicrobial and adhesion capabilities of the two fusion partners, these properties were evaluated as follows.

Antimicrobial Activity of Vmh2-GKY20

Antimicrobial experiments against different bacterial strains were performed at different concentrations of the chimeric protein to confirm the efficiency of the GKY20 peptide following its fusion with Vmh2. Antimicrobial tests were carried out on the opportunistic pathogen bacteria P. aeruginosa, as Gram-negative, and S. aureus and S. epidermidis, as Gram-positive. Their cell viability was examined in the presence of Vmh2-GKY20, native Vmh2, and antibiotics as controls. Figure 1 shows that cell growth was reduced upon increasing the chimeric protein concentration, and only about 5% of S. epidermidis cell growth remained at the highest protein concentration tested (3.5 μM corresponding to 0.05 mg/mL). In the case of the other two bacteria (P. aeruginosa and S. aureus), a slightly minor inhibition effect was measured. When native Vmh2 was used, no statistically significant effect was observed (Figure 1).

Cell viability of the three bacterial strains tested (S. epidermidis (A), S. aureus (B), P. aeruginosa (C)) in the presence of increasing concentrations of Vmh2-GKY20 (up to 3.5 μM), native Vmh2 (1.75 μM), and antibiotics (12.5 μg/mL). Cell growth percentages are referred to the bacterial cell cultures without any supplements. Each data point represents the mean ± SD of at least three independent experiments; the mean values were compared to the control and considered significant when p < 0.05 (***p < 0.0001, ** p < 0.01, *p < 0.05) according to the Student’s t-test.

The results herein obtained confirmed the preservation of the antimicrobial activity of GKY2031,32 upon fusion with Vmh2, thanks to the presence of the PAS spacer.

Adhesion of Vmh2-GKY20 on PS

Native Vmh2 is known to efficiently adhere to PS, with immobilization yield higher than 90%.33 The adhesive capability of the chimeric protein to PS was tested by depositing the sample onto a 96-multiwell plate. The immobilization process was optimized by looking at the protein concentration, incubation time, and the resulting water evaporation. As demonstrated in Table S1, when different protein quantities were deposited (10, 20, and 30 μg), the immobilization yield was always about 50%. Surface saturation of the adhered chimera was not reached; anyway, higher protein concentrations were not achievable due to its poor solubility. However, the amount of immobilized protein was duplicated by performing a successive deposition (Table S2). Hence, two depositions of the protein were performed in the following experiments. To exclude a contribution to the adhesive capability by the synthetic peptide, it was analogously tested using a solution at the same molar concentration. The peptide was completely removed after washing, as expected.

The good immobilization yield and the antimicrobial activity previously discussed confirm that both functions of the partners of the chimeric protein were conserved.

Antimicrobial and Antibiofilm PS Functionalization

The colonization of different pathogens on medical devices is still an issue that needs to be addressed. An ecofriendly and fast approach to impart antimicrobial/antibiofilm properties to PS is suggested using the chimeric protein GKY20-Vmh2.

To verify the antibiofilm activity of the functionalized PS, we chose one bacterial strain per category as an example. The Gram-positive S. epidermidis and the Gram-negative P. aeruginosa were grown in multiwell plates coated by Vmh2-GKY20 or native Vmh2, and then a crystal violet staining test was carried out to evaluate their biofilm production. The biofilm formation was already reduced by Vmh2 coating, but a remarkable increase in the antibiofilm action was achieved by the chimeric protein. As observed in Figure 2, the higher the amount of the deposited protein, the lower the residual biofilm. These results prove the utility of the chimeric protein in developing versatile antibiofilm surfaces.

Residual biofilm percentages of S. epidermidis (A) and P. aeruginosa (B) incubated on differently functionalized wells, evaluated using the crystal violet assay and referred to as the biofilm formed on bare wells. Each data point represents the mean ± SD of at least three independent experiments; the mean values were compared to the control and considered significant when p < 0.05 (***p < 0.0001) according to the Student’s t-test.

The antibiofilm functionality of coated PS plates was evaluated over time, storing them at two different temperatures (R.T. and 4 °C) within 4 weeks. The residual biofilm assay performed on the coated plates showed only a slight decrease in the antibiofilm activity over time (ca. 10%) at both temperatures, thus confirming the stability of the Vmh2-GKY20 layer (Figure S1).

The inhibition of S. epidermidis biofilm formation was further investigated by CLSM to analyze cell viability, biofilm structure, and biomass distribution through three-dimensional (3D) biofilm images. LIVE/DEAD staining was used to visualize the viability of cells embedded in the biofilm, where green color indicates viable cells and red color indicates damaged cells. In Figure 3a, a significant reduction in biofilm formation was observed in both Vmh2 and Vmh2-GKY20 coated wells, which agrees with the crystal violet results (Figure 2). Vmh2-GKY20 coating led to a more irregular and inhomogeneous biofilm. The quantitative analysis (testing three images from two different samples) showed that coating with the chimeric protein resulted in an effective reduction in total biomass, biofilm thickness, and smoothness when compared to the negative control and slightly different when compared to Vmh2 (Figure 3, panel B). The most significant difference in the activity of the two proteins is the bactericidal action of Vmh2-GKY20, which causes the death of 23% of bacterial cells (compared to 7% for Vmh2). Considering the bactericidal effect of the chimeric protein formed by Vmh2 and the well-known AMP LL37 (14% killed bacteria),26 the results shown are encouraging.

(B) COMSTAT quantitative analysis of three different images per condition: total biomass, average thickness, and roughness coefficient. (C) LIVE/DEAD staining percentage reported as a pie graph. The percentage of dead cells is in red and of live cells in green.

Antimicrobial BC Functionalization

Adhesion of Vmh2-GKY20 on BC

As already discussed in the Section 1, bacterial contaminations represent a challenge for wound dressing materials, among which BC is currently one of the most fascinating. To test whether Vmh2-GKY20 could adhere to BCps, a protocol to ensure immobilization on both sides of the pellicles was set up, also considering the porosity of the material. After prolonged soaking in the protein solution, BCps were dried and extensively washed. The effect of chimeric protein concentration was evaluated at different concentrations (7, 14, and 22 μM, corresponding to 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 mg/mL, respectively). The same protocol was used to functionalize BC with the free peptide and the native Vmh2 (at 14 μM). Adhesion yield to BCps was estimated by measuring the residual protein or peptide amount in the solution (Table 1).

Doubling the initial amount of chimera, the adhered amount doubled, leading to a comparable immobilization yield for all tested conditions without reaching the surface saturation, as already observed in the case of the PS surface. A comparable immobilization yield was achieved when BCps were incubated with native Vmh2. On the other hand, a higher immobilization yield was attained when the free peptide was used. This is not surprising since the peptide, being small and positively charged, could be easily adsorbed and trapped in the highly porous and moderately negatively charged BC material. However, from the SEM analysis images reported in Figure 4, no noticeable change in surface appearance was observed in the case of the peptide, whereas the surface remarkably changed after chimeric protein immobilization. Therefore, although the immobilization yield of the free peptide was higher, the BCp surface was not modified, highlighting how differently the free peptide and Vmh2-GKY20 interact with the cellulose. These findings may suggest that the fusion of the peptide to Vmh2 is functional to its exposure on the BCp surface.

Other SEM micrographs reported in the Supporting Information (Figure S2) also show amyloid-like fibers formed on BCps upon functionalization with Vmh2-GKY20. The ability of Vmh2 to spontaneously assemble into very large fibers has already been proven.34

Antibiofilm and Antimicrobial Activity of Functionalized BCps

Attempts were performed to evaluate the antibiofilm properties of the coated BCps using S. epidermidis. However, technical issues related to the intrinsic characteristics of the BCp material hindered biofilm analysis either by crystal violet or CLSM. On the other hand, the antimicrobial properties of functionalized BCps were analyzed. First, qualitative experiments at the same molar concentration of Vmh2-GKY20 and the free GKY20 were carried out against the three bacterial strains by diffusion tests (overnight interaction). Only BCps functionalized with the chimeric protein showed an inhibition halo in the case of the Gram-positive bacteria (S. epidermidis and S. aureus) (Figures S3, S4), while no clear effect was observed for Gram-negative bacteria (P. aeruginosa). A higher protein concentration (22 μM) not only led to an evident inhibition area but also completely killed S. aureus on the touch surfaces (2 h interaction) (Figure 5). These results demonstrate that the Vmh2-GKY20 functionalized pellicles possess strong bactericidal activity against Gram-positive bacteria, even upon a short interaction with the pathogens.

Antimicrobial activity against S. aureus of BCps functionalized with Vmh2-GKY20. Untreated BCps are reported as a control. DT: diffusion test and TT: touch test.

On the other hand, the free peptide, despite its high immobilization yield, does not impart antimicrobial activity to BCps, probably because of its poor surface exposure. Moreover, the environment in which these tests were performed could further affect the peptide diffusion due to electrostatic interactions with the medium components.35 Pellicles functionalized with native Vmh2 did not display any antimicrobial effect, as expected26 (Figure S5).

Quantitative analyses of the antimicrobial effect of functionalized BCps were carried out in liquid cultures of S. epidermidis. A significant reduction of turbidity (OD600 nm), about 50%, was observed only for bacterial cells grown in the presence of BCps functionalized with Vmh2-GKY20, while a much lower reduction (10%) was observed in the case of GKY20 (Figure 6, panel A). The antimicrobial effect was then confirmed by bacterial colony counts to measure cell viability (Figure 6, panel B, Figure S6).

Conclusions

In this work, the fusion of the antimicrobial peptide GKY20 to the class I HPB Vmh2 has been proven to successfully impart biocidal properties to two different materials of interest for medical applications. Coating PS with Vmh2-GKY20-chimera led to a reduction in the biofilm formation of S. epidermidis, as well as a good bactericidal effect. Moreover, we demonstrated for the first time the adhesion of a fungal hydrophobin onto BC. When the hydrophobin-based chimera, Vmh2-GKY20, was immobilized onto the cellulosic substrate, it maintained antimicrobial activity against the same pathogen. A comparison between Vmh2-GKY20 and peptide-functionalized BCps revealed that the fusion protein provides an advantage in terms of functionality rather than adhesion yield. These results lead to the hypothesis that Vmh2, in the chimeric protein, acts as an anchor for GKY20, ensuring the peptide’s right exposure. Instead, the free peptide, remaining embedded in the cellulose network and thus less exposed, could not carry out its activity. Cellulose, especially BC, is a very highly valuable material in biomedical applications, e.g., for wound healing. Studies on composites that combine their excellent chemophysical properties with antimicrobial/antifouling molecules represent a step forward in the development of new wound dressing materials. Indeed, the increasing concerns about antibiotic-resistant microorganisms are currently pushing toward new strategies in developing alternatives to solve biofilm infections.
