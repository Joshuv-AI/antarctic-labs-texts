Research on the dose calculation of BNCT based on the time-varying boron concentration in pharmacokinetics
Authors: Yao Chen, Qi Zheng, Bo Wang, Heyu Peng, Yunhui Tang, Xinlei Zhang, Yuqi Dai, Zhifeng Li, Jie Li, Sheng Wang, Xiaozhi Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBoron Neutron Capture Therapy (BNCT) is a targeted radiotherapy that utilizes the nuclear reaction of 10B with thermal neutrons to destroy tumor cells while sparing healthy tissue. Its effectiveness relies on accurately modeling boron distribution. Current treatment planning systems use a fixed tumor-to-normal tissue (T/N) boron ratio, ignoring pharmacokinetics. This study improves BNCT dose calculations by integrating time-dependent boron concentrations from pharmacokinetic simulations. Firstly, this research improved the traditional two-compartment pharmacokinetic model to a three-compartment model to better represent boron distribution, accounting for different tumor locations. Two patient cases were simulated, and the comparisons were performed between the dose distributions obtained by the fixed T/N ratio method and the ones obtained by our pharmacokinetic-based approach. Results showed significant discrepancies between the two methods, with the maximum dose deviation in the tumor region reaching 11.386%. The pharmacokinetic-based method provided more accurate and individualized dose calculations. Secondly, the multi-objective optimization using the Basin Hopping algorithm was employed to determine the optimal irradiation time periods. This approach enhanced treatment efficacy by increasing the average dose and maximum dose in the gross tumor volume by ~4% within the same irradiation period, while minimizing damage to normal tissues. The optimized irradiation schedules resulted in improved dose delivery to the tumor while maintaining safe levels for normal tissues. Our findings highlight the importance of integrating pharmacokinetic data into BNCT treatment planning to improve dose accuracy and treatment outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Boron neutron capture therapy (BNCT) is a biological-targeted radiotherapy that, compared to other radiotherapy techniques [1–3], can more effectively kill tumor tissue while ensuring that the surrounding normal tissue remains undamaged. This targeted treatment effect is achieved through a nuclear reaction that occurs when 10B is irradiated with thermal neutrons, resulting in the release of high-energy alpha particles and 7Li nuclei. These reaction products have a very short range, approximately the diameter of a single cell, which enables highly localized destruction of the cancer cells.

The effectiveness of BNCT relies heavily on the distribution and concentration of boron within the tumor and surrounding tissues [4, 5]. The distribution of boron drugs directly impacts dose calculation results in the radiotherapy planning system, as well as the evaluation and effectiveness of subsequent diagnostic and treatment plans. Accurate modeling of these time-dependent and spatial variations is crucial for effective treatment planning and dose delivery in BNCT. Pharmacokinetic studies have demonstrated that boron concentrations in both blood and tumor tissues can change significantly over time following the administration of boron compounds [6–8]. W.S. Kiger III et al. conducted studies using a two-compartment open model to analyse boron concentration changes in the human body, and their results indicated that the proportion of boron concentration in well-perfused to less well-perfused regions varies over time [7]. These temporal variations can lead to discrepancies between the planned and actual delivered doses, potentially affecting the treatment plan’s effectiveness and safety. Additionally, the spatial distribution of boron within different compartments of the body, influenced by factors such as the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and tumor location [9], further complicates accurate dose delivery.

However, existing BNCT treatment planning systems (TPS) such as SERA [10], Tsukuba Plan [11], and THORplan [12] set the tumor boron concentration to normal tissue boron concentration ratio (T/N) as a constant. This fixed ratio simplifies the planning process and allows for straightforward dose calculations. However, this approach fails to account for the dynamic nature of boron pharmacokinetics. The boron drug concentration changes over time, leading to variations in the T/N ratio, which can introduce errors in the dose calculation results.

Recognizing the importance of these dynamic and spatial factors, this study aims to integrate pharmacokinetic data into BNCT dose calculations, providing a more realistic representation of boron concentration dynamics during treatment. By tracking the changes in boron concentration over time and across different compartments, it is possible to refine dose distribution and improve the overall precision of BNCT.

Most existing pharmacokinetic models are based on two-compartment open models, which do not include a compartment representing the tumor region. Kunio Yokoyama et al. [13], using secondary ion mass spectrometer (SIMS) technology, found that there exists a gradient in boron concentration between tumor tissues and normal tissues. This highlights the need to extend pharmacokinetic models to better represent boron distribution in BNCT.

The primary objective of this research is to incorporate time-dependent boron concentration data into BNCT treatment planning to achieve a more accurate and individualized dose distribution. This involves collecting time-dynamic boron concentration data in both blood and tumor tissues and using these data to adjust the T/N ratio throughout the treatment period. Secondly, based on the time-varying boron concentration curves, the aim is to determine an optimal irradiation time for treatment, and multi-objective optimization methods are considered for implementation.

To accomplish these goals, advanced pharmacokinetic modeling techniques are utilized to simulate the temporal and spatial changes in boron distribution. These models are integrated into the treatment planning system, allowing for continuous adjustment of the dose calculation based on real-time boron concentration data. Multi-objective optimization is applied to identify the comparatively excellent irradiation times, ensuring effective and safe treatment.

METHODS AND MATERIALS

Boron pharmacokinetic model

Physiologically based pharmacokinetics is the science of studying the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion processes of drugs within the body. The essential task of boron pharmacokinetics is to reveal the dynamic changes of boron drugs in the body and to provide a theoretical basis for individualized and customized BNCT treatments.

The traditional open two-compartment pharmacokinetic model can only describe the temporal trends of boron drug concentrations in well-perfused and less well-perfused areas. However, in boron neutron capture therapy, tumors specifically absorb boron drugs. Therefore, it is necessary to extend the conventional pharmacokinetic model to make it more suitable for simulating the variations of boron drugs in BNCT.

Given the property of boron drugs to specifically accumulate in tumors, the commonly used open two-compartment model in pharmacokinetics is extended to include a specific compartment representing the tumor region that can specifically absorb boron drugs. Two types of open three-compartment models are established based on the different locations of the tumor. To achieve the global optimal solution for this problem, the Basin Hopping algorithm is employed. Basin Hopping is a global optimization algorithm particularly suitable for nonlinear, multimodal functions [14, 15]. This algorithm combines local optimization with global random jumps to effectively explore the solution space and locate the global optimum. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Patient data and neutron source

In this paper, medical image samples were selected to validate the above proposed methods. Medical image, which is formed as Patients DICOM data, were selected from The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA) for dose calculation [16]. The first patient’s tumor is located in the brain, which is a less well-perfused area, while the second patient’s tumor is located in the neck, a well-perfused area. The DICOM includes structures such as gross tumor volume (GTV), brain, brainstem, lens, optic nerves, eyes and body.

The accelerator-based neutron source used in this study is the XJTU-BNCT ultra-thermal neutron source at Xi'an Jiaotong University's Advanced Neutron Technology Laboratory, with neutron source data obtained from the exit of the beam shaping assembly as calculated by Monte Carlo N-Particle code (MCNP) [17]. The energy spectrum of the neutron source is shown in Fig. 4. As recommended in the IAEA TECDOC Series [18], a field diameter of 10 ~ 15 cm is suggested for head and neck cancer treatments. The energy spectrum of the neutron source is shown in Fig. 4. In this study, a field diameter of 10 cm is used to ensure full coverage of the gross tumor volume (GTV).

Energy spectrum of neutron source.

In BNCT, the dose to normal tissues is produced by various radiation components, including photon dose, nitrogen dose, hydrogen dose, and boron dose [19, 20]. Each of these dose components is weighted according to experimentally determined relative biological effectiveness (RBE) factors, with RBE values of 1.0, 2.5, 2.5, and 3.8, respectively, and then summed. The unit of biologically weighted dose is RBE Gy.

The engine used for dose calculation is NECP-MCX [21], a Monte Carlo-deterministic coupled particle transport calculation software independently developed by the Nuclear Engineering Computational Physics (NECP) Laboratory at Xi'an Jiaotong University, with fully independent intellectual property rights. The software includes features such as automatic variance reduction, neutron-photon coupled transport calculations, transport-burnup-activation-source term coupled calculations, continuous energy nuclear data sensitivity analysis, point kernel integration, and multi-group calculations.

Given the variability of boron pharmacokinetics across patients and time points, pharmacokinetic (PK)-based simulations provide a more dynamic and patient-specific representation of boron concentration over time. In this study, a numerically driven approach was adopted to explore the potential of PK-based modeling in dose planning during the early development stage, where experimental validation is not yet feasible. By incorporating time-resolved boron distribution into dose calculation, the model enables individualized irradiation planning and enhances dose precision to tumors while minimizing exposure to normal tissues. Accordingly, integrating pharmacokinetic modeling into BNCT is not only scientifically justified but also critical to improving treatment accuracy and clinical outcomes.

For Patient 2, employing the fixed T/N ratio also results in higher dose calculation outcomes. It is noteworthy that the maximum dose calculated for the brain and larynx using the fixed T/N ratio shows significant deviations compared to the results obtained using pharmacokinetic corrections (brain: 21.179%, larynx: 10.483%). This is because, when utilizing the fixed T/N ratio, the brain is considered as normal tissue, whereas in pharmacokinetics, due to the presence of the BBB, the brain is treated as a less well-perfused tissue with lower boron concentration. The larynx, being a critical organ located near the infiltrative tumor, benefits from pharmacokinetic corrections as it allows for a more accurate assessment of the dose received, thereby better protecting normal tissues. This occurs because, over time, drug metabolism and excretion cause the drug concentration in each compartment to decrease, which the fixed T/N ratio method fails to account for.

The simulation results from two cases indicate that there are discrepancies between the dose calculations obtained using the fixed T/N ratio and those incorporating pharmacokinetic corrections. The differences in both critical organ dose and tumor dose are correlated with tumor size and depth. By utilizing the time-dependent boron concentrations derived from pharmacokinetic modeling, the dynamic changes in boron distribution over time can be more accurately accounted for. Therefore, to correct dose calculation results and ensure more precise treatment planning, it is essential to employ pharmacokinetic simulations that reflect the time-varying nature of boron concentrations, facilitating personalized treatment for each patient.

CONCLUSION

This study established two three-compartment open pharmacokinetic models suitable for BNCT based on tumor location and conducted pharmacokinetic simulations. Based on the pharmacokinetic simulation results, dose calculations and comparisons were performed between the traditional fixed T/N ratio method for setting boron concentration and the method based on time-varying boron concentration from pharmacokinetics. The calculation results indicated that using the fixed T/N ratio method introduces significant dose deviations (with a maximum deviation of 11.386% in the tumor region). Therefore, it is recommended to use pharmacokinetic simulations to obtain time-varying boron concentration information for more accurate boron concentration settings, correcting the errors introduced by the fixed T/N ratio.

Secondly, the Basin Hopping global multi-objective optimization method was further applied to the pharmacokinetic results to obtain an optimal irradiation time. The irradiation time obtained using the Basin Hopping method, compared to previous calculations, can increase the average dose and maximum dose in the GTV by ~4% within the same irradiation period. Thus, this approach provides a method to determine the optimal neutron irradiation time before boron drug injection, improving treatment effectiveness and reducing irradiation time.

The present study focuses on validating the dual-compartment model's core functionality through two representative clinical cases (intracranial versus neck tumors), intentionally selected to span the clinically relevant perfusion spectrum. Building upon this foundation, future work will expand to cohort validation utilizing the accelerator-based neutron source at the Huzhou Neutron Science Laboratory [24, 25], where individualized pharmacokinetic modeling will be implemented by integrating dynamic 18F-BPA PET imaging data and time-resolved boron concentration monitoring. Concurrently, we plan to advance dose calculation algorithms that account for temporally evolving boron distributions, rigorously test pharmacokinetic models under actual irradiation conditions, and refine boron drug kinetics through iterative experimental optimization. These multidisciplinary efforts aim to establish a precision dosimetry framework for clinical BNCT applications.
