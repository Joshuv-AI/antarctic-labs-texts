Mortality attributable to youth drinking in 194 WHO member countries/territories, 1990–2021
Authors: Dan Tian, Jie Li, David C Schwebel, Cifu Xie, Tongyan Wang, Ruisha Peng, Li Li, Peixia Cheng, Zhenzhen Rao, Peishan Ning, Guoqing Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractYouth drinking is known to be a leading and avoidable risk factor of mortality. However, recent evidence on mortality attributable to youth drinking on a global scale is limited. Using risk-attributable mortality data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, we conducted a longitudinal analysis to examine trends in deaths and mortality attributable to youth drinking among individuals under 20 years across 194 WHO member countries/territories from 1990 to 2021. Mortality differences were assessed by sex, socio-demographic index (SDI) levels, and countries/territories. Average annual percentage changes (AAPCs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) estimated by Joinpoint regression were used to quantify significant mortality changes. A total of 417,198 deaths attributable to youth drinking were reported from 1990 to 2021, primarily from transport injuries (36.1%) and interpersonal violence (17.7%). The number of total deaths attributable to youth drinking declined from 13,346 in 1990 to 10,563 in 2021 (AAPC = -0.73%, 95% CI: -0.77% to -0.68%). Mortality among males was 5.8–7.5 times that of females. Large mortality decreases occurred over time in countries/territories with high (AAPC=-2.71%, 95% CI: -2.78% to -2.67%) and high-middle SDI (AAPC=-2.06%, 95% CI: -2.26%% to -1.87%%), while those with lower SDI levels showed relatively stable or slightly increasing mortality trends. The percent change in mortality varied greatly across the 194 WHO countries/territories from 1990 to 2021, ranging from − 96.8% (Seychelles) to 772.8% (Libya). Increases occurred in 30.4% of countries (59/194), all in low or middle SDI levels. Despite a substantial decline in mortality, therefore, youth drinking remains a global health challenge that disproportionally affect males and young adults in countries with low or middle SDI levels. Introduction

Early initiation of alcohol use can impair brain development and lead to over 200 adverse health outcomes in adulthood1. Youth drinking, defined as alcohol consumption by individuals under the legal drinking age (typically between 18 and 21 years)2, is an important but avoidable risk factor of death and disability3. Despite this, the global alcohol drinking rate was 22.0% among individuals aged 15–19 years in 20194, and the age of onset for alcohol use is tending younger, with some children starting as early as 10 years old5,6. According to estimates from the Global Burden of Disease study group 2021 (GBD 2021), alcohol consumption caused approximately 40,500 deaths among people aged 10–24 years in 20217.

In The Global Alcohol Action Plan 2022–2030, the 75th World Health Assembly prioritized a goal to “effectively implement the global strategy to reduce the harmful use of alcohol”. The plan placed particular emphasis on protecting children and adolescents8,9.

To support implementation of the global alcohol action plan, it is valuable to comprehensively assess recent global burden of disease caused by youth drinking. To date, relevant publications on these topics are largely limited to few developed countries (particularly the United States)10 and a restricted number of harmful outcomes from alcohol use (e.g., road traffic injury)11.

We designed a longitudinal analysis based on risk-attributable mortality data produced by the GBD 2021 study group to examine trends in overall and subgroup deaths and mortality attributable to youth drinking in 194 WHO member countries/territories from 1990 to 2021. Two primary research questions were tested: (a) did global deaths and mortality attributable to youth drinking change over time between 1990 and 2021? and (b) did the changes in number of deaths and mortality attributable to youth drinking differ across sex groups, socio-demographic index (SDI) categories, countries/territories, and cause of death?

Method

Data source

According to recommendations from the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention2 and publicly available GBD 2021 estimates7, this study operationally defined youth drinking as the consumption of alcoholic beverages by individuals under the age of 20 years, which coincides with the legal drinking age in many countries.

Data on the number of deaths and mortality attributable to youth drinking, and their 95% uncertainty intervals (UI), were extracted from the GBD 2021 dataset7 for 194 WHO member countries/territories, covering the period from 1990 (the earliest available data) to 2021. The end date was necessitated by recent changes in GBD data access policies, which limit the free use of the most recent GBD 2023 dataset. However, the two GBD rounds show only minor differences in the number of input data sources to estimate alcohol-attributable mortality (554 for GBD 202112 vs. 561 for GBD 202313 and both datasets use similar methods to derive the estimates14.

The GBD 2021 study estimated deaths and mortality data using data from vital registries, surveys, and literature (primarily via GHDx), generated estimates using ensemble modeling tools (including DisMod-MR 2.1, CODEm, and ST-GPR), and applied rigorous quality control following GATHER guidelines15. Within the GBD framework, mortality estimates offer the greatest robustness and methodological maturity compared to other available data15. Thus, as the most definitive health consequence of youth drinking, mortality provides a clear and comparable metric for quantifying disease burden both across countries and over time and was used as the primary outcome in this study16.

Using Comparative Risk Assessment, GBD 2021 estimated alcohol attributable mortality rates through five steps: (a) modeling global alcohol exposure (g/day ethanol) via ST-GPR to address data gaps, (b) applying continuous dose-response risk curves from MR-BRT meta-regression to quantified harm across consumption levels, (c) defining optimal thresholds for risk calculation using location-, age- and sex-specific theoretical minimum risk exposure levels (TMRELs), (d) calculating population-attributable fractions (PAFs) to translate excess risk into population impact, and (e) combining results with CODEm/DisMod-MR 2.1 mortality estimates to obtain attributable deaths17. Estimates for all metrics were computed as the mean value across 500 Monte Carlo draws, with 95% uncertainty interval (UI) defined by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the distribution17.

Statistical analysis

Number of deaths and mortality attributable to youth drinking were also extracted by sex (male/female), SDI levels (i.e., high SDI, high-middle SDI, middle SDI, low-middle SDI, and low SDI)15, and seven cause of death groups, including: (1) respiratory infections and tuberculosis, (2) substance use disorders, (3) transport injuries, (4) other unintentional injuries, (5) self-harm, (6) interpersonal violence, and (7) other disease (include cardiovascular diseases, diabetes and kidney diseases, digestive diseases, neoplasms, and neurological disorders), as recommended by the GBD 20217,15 and based on International Classification of Diseases 10th revision (ICD-10) codes (Supplemental Table 1).

Area charts and line graphs and were used to visualize overall trends and disparities in youth drinking attributable deaths and mortality across subgroups by sex and SDI of countries/territories from 1990 to 2021. The average annual percentage changes (AAPCs) were estimated by Joinpoint regression, which assess the epidemiological trend over a fixed interval. “AAPC > 0” indicates an increasing trend, while “AAPC < 0” denotes a decreasing trend. “P < 0.05” with two-sided tests was regarded as statistically significant. Results

Overall deaths and mortality attributable to youth drinking

For all years combined, a total of 417,198 youths and young adults were estimated to die from youth drinking across the 194 WHO member countries/territories, with 87.1% of deaths occurring to males and 61.0% to individuals from countries/territories with low and middle SDI levels (Fig. 1). The number of youth-drinking attributable deaths across these 194 countries/territories decreased from 13,346 in 1990 to 10,563 in 2021 (from 11,470 to 9,344 in males and from 1,875 to 1,219 in females). Countries/territories with high and high-middle SDI levels witnessed notable decreases in the number of deaths attributable to youth drinking (−60.3% and − 57.2% respectively), compared to a −11.9% decrease in those with middle SDI and 70.5% and 66.7% increases in those with low-middle and low SDI levels respectively (all Ps < 0.05) (Fig. 1B).

The youth-drinking attributable mortality across the 194 WHO member countries/territories dropped from 0.6 per 100,000 population (95% UI: 0.4 to 0.8) in 1990 to 0.4 per 100,000 population (95% UI: 0.3 to 0.6) in 2021 (Fig. 2A). Both sexes showed similar mortality trends during 1990–2021, but males had notably higher rates than females (5.8–7.5 times). Defining countries/territories by SDI, those with high and high-middle SDI levels generally had the highest youth-drinking attributable mortality rates and experienced the largest mortality declines (−57.1% and − 47.8%; both Ps < 0.05) between 1990 and 2021. In contrast, mortality in those countries/territories in the three lower SDI levels decreased with lesser magnitude (−10.1% for middle SDI and − 20.2% for low SDI) or showed an increase (31.8% for low-middle SDI), all Ps < 0.05 (Fig. 2B). Cause spectrum of youth drinking attributable deaths

Among the seven types of cause of death, transport injuries (36.1%, 95% UI: 25.5% to 41.6%), interpersonal violence (17.7%, 95% UI: 11.7% to 20.3%), and self-harm (13.5%, 95% UI: 5.7% to 17.5%) accounted for the largest proportions of total deaths attributable to youth drinking between 1990 and 2021 (Fig. 3A). This ranking of the top three causes remained consistent through all years during the time period, prompting our presentation of change in total deaths from 1990 to 2021 rather than the annual number of deaths by cause. Top five diseases attributable to youth drinking differed in males and in females (males: transport injuries [37.5%], interpersonal violence [18.8%], self-harm [13.4%], other unintentional injuries [7.9%], and substance use and disorders [7.7%]; females: transport injuries [27.1%], respiratory infections and tuberculosis [14.3%], substance use and disorders [14.3%], self-harm [14.2%], and interpersonal violence [9.9%]).

In low SDI countries/territories, respiratory infections and tuberculosis (28.8%) surpassed transport injuries (25.1%, 95% UI: 21.7% to 25.4%) as the leading cause of youth drinking attributable deaths, while in countries/territories at other SDI levels, transport injuries remained the leading cause, particularly in high SDI (44.4%, 95% UI: 31.7% to 52.3%) and high-middle SDI (39.5%, 95% UI: 26.7% to 48.1%) countries/territories (Fig. 3B).

Variations across countries/territories

Of 417,198 deaths attributable to youth drinking across the 194 countries/territories from 1990 to 2021, 44.3% were in five countries: China (11.1%), Brazil (9.2%), Russian Federation (9.0%), United States (7.8%), and India (7.3%) (Fig. 4A). The mortality attributable to youth drinking for all years combined differed greatly across 194 countries/territories. 138 of 194 countries/territories (71.1%) had a mortality less than 1.0 per 100,000 population, while two possessed a mortality rate exceeding 2.5 per 100,000 population: 3.2 per 100,000 population in Russian Federation and 2.5 per 100,000 population in Venezuela (Fig. 4B).

Between 1990 and 2021, the percent changes in mortality attributable to youth drinking varied greatly across the 194 WHO countries/territories, ranging from − 96.8% to 772.8% (Supplemental Table 3). Alarmingly, 59 of the 194 countries/territories (30.4%) witnessed an increase in mortality attributable to youth drinking, all of them in low, low-middle, middle, or high-middle SDI levels (Fig. 5 and Supplemental Table 3). The six countries/territories that experienced the largest percent increases in mortality were Libya (772.8%), Viet Nam (766.9%), Nepal (408.8%), Iran (394.3%), Cook Islands (317.3%), and Cambodia (314.6%). In contrast, five countries/territories experienced an over 80.0% mortality reduction: Seychelles (−96.8%), Sudan (−95.3%), Bhutan (−86.3%), Brunei Darussalam (−84.5%), and Lebanon (−82.8%).

Transport injuries were the most common cause of mortality attributable to youth drinking for 58.8% countries/territories (114/194) between 1990 and 2021, followed by respiratory infections and tuberculosis (11.9%, 23/194) and interpersonal violence (10.8%, 21/194).

Discussion

This study systematically analyzed epidemiological characteristics and trends in the number of deaths and mortality attributable to youth drinking in 194 WHO member countries/territories from 1990 to 2021. Three key findings emerged. First, youth drinking caused 417,198 deaths across the 194 WHO member countries/territories between 1990 and 2021, primarily due to five major diseases and injuries: transport injuries, interpersonal violence, respiratory infections and tuberculosis, substance use disorders, and self-harm. Both the number of deaths and mortality attributable to youth drinking across the 194 WHO member countries/territories decreased from 1990 to 2021, by 20.9% and 33.3% respectively. Second, males and countries/territories with low and middle SDI had much heavier mortality burden from youth drinking than females and countries with higher SDI. Third, varying mortality trends appeared across the 194 countries/territories, with 59 of 194 countries (30.4%) showing an increasing tendency, all of them having low or middle SDI levels.

The high number of deaths attributable to youth drinking impacts the health of young adults greatly. It may be explained by a range of factors, including poor policies and enforcement to prevent drinking among youths, biological factors related to alcohol metabolism, and impulse regulation in this age group, plus current social norms concerning alcohol consumption among young people. We detail each issue below.

First, many countries have inadequate policies to prevent drinking among young adults, or poor enforcement of the policies that do exist. As of 2016, 11 of 163 countries/territories reporting relevant information to the WHO did not establish any minimum legal drinking age at all18. Among those with existing laws, the minimum legal drinking age varied widely, from 13 to 25 years18. Only 66% of countries with national alcohol policies reported government-funded support for their implementation, such as enforcement, market regulation, and penalties for violations, resulting in large gaps in enforcing minimum legal drinking age laws and restricting access to alcohol use among youths18.

Second, adolescents metabolize alcohol less efficiently than adults due to lower levels and activity of the enzyme alcohol dehydrogenase19. This biological process prolongs exposure to ethanol and its toxic metabolites, increasing risk of acute alcohol poisoning, organ and tissue toxicity, and the development of alcohol dependence. Third, neurodevelopmental immaturity — particularly in the prefrontal cortex — makes adolescents and young adults more susceptible to using alcohol, and to alcohol’s neurotoxic effects. They may not recognize the risks of alcohol, or of consuming large amounts of alcohol. Once consumed, alcohol use may impair judgment and increase even further the innate risk-taking behaviors of youths such as dangerous driving, unprotected sex, and violent acts18–21. These mechanisms account for several of the leading health losses from underage alcohol use among youths, including traffic crashes, violence, and self-harm3,5,20,22.

Fourth, social and traditional media may support cultural norms for youth drinking. In countries like Italy and France, alcohol has long been integrated into social rituals, consequently legitimizing early exposure to and consumption of alcohol23. Meanwhile, contemporary media and advertisements often depict alcohol consumption as appealing and socially advantageous, reinforcing perceptions that drinking is a normative and desirable behavior among young people. This pervasive cultural exposure likely contributes to elevated initiation and alcohol use among youths24.

Encouragingly, both overall youth drinking deaths and mortality declined from 1990 to 2021 globally, and particularly over the last decade. This pattern may be partially attributed to implementation of the Global Strategy to Reduce the Harmful Use of Alcohol9, which recommended that all WHO member countries/territories adopt evidence-based measures to reduce youth drinking. These measures include establishing a higher minimum age to legally purchase alcohol, increasing alcohol taxes, and restricting alcohol advertising, promotion and sponsorship6. As a result, 19 countries established or raised the minimum legal purchase age for alcoholic beverages4. By 2019, 92% of WHO member countries/territories had implemented alcohol excise taxes4, and increased alcohol taxation in 15 European countries was associated with significant reductions in per capita alcohol consumption between 2000 and 20204,25. Further, by 2022, 18% of countries worldwide had implemented bans on digital alcohol marketing, a notable rise from only 8% in 201326.

Large disparities across countries/territories in deaths and mortality rates attributable to youth drinking by sex and SDI are likely due to inconsistent exposures to alcohol. Compared to girls, boys engage more in binge drinking and drinking-related risk-taking behaviors — such as fighting, and delayed health-seeking behavior — that may amplify alcohol-related harms11,27. Higher mortality burden attributable to youth drinking in countries/territories with low and middle SDI might be caused by inadequate alcohol control legislation or enforcement28, under-resourced healthcare systems that limit effective treatment for acute alcohol poisoning and other alcohol-related injuries29, and competing health priorities — such as infectious diseases and malnutrition — that divert resources from alcohol control30,31.

The most common harmful effects of alcohol use in many countries/territories with low SDI levels were lower respiratory infections and tuberculosis, which concords with a previous report32 and could be partially interpreted as the result of underdeveloped economies and public health infrastructure7,33.

Substantial variations in mortality trends across the 194 countries and territories may reflect differences in four factors: legal and police capacity to control alcohol use11,18, timing to implement relevant policies6,9, sociocultural characteristics, and external shocks34–36. First, despite global progress, alcohol control policies remain more established in high-income countries/territories than in low-income countries/territories18. For example, in 2019, 63.0% of high-income countries had national policies concerning alcohol sales and use, compared to 25.0% of low-income countries11. Beer excise taxes are present in 96% of high-income countries/territories, compared to 86% of low-income ones4. In contrast, the absence of a minimum legal drinking age law occurs occasionally in both low- and middle-income countries/territories (13.6%, 9/66) and upper middle- and high- income countries/territories (2.1%, 2/97)4,18.

A second factor that may explain disparities across countries/territories is the timing of legislation to control alcohol use. Seychelles and Bhutan, for example, are the two countries that achieved the largest declines in mortality during our study time period (−96.8% and − 86.3%). Both established a minimum drinking age of 18 in 1990, complemented by high alcohol excise taxes, sales bans (restrictions on operating hours, and on sales in certain contexts such as special events), and improved trauma care after the enactment of the WHO Strategy6,9. In contrast, Viet Nam experienced a nearly 7-fold increase in mortality attributable to youth drinking. Driven by economic development, alcohol production in Viet Nam increased by 50% between 2002 and 201618. The introduction of a minimum legal drinking age law in Vietnam in 2008 was considerable later than that for many countries and might be another reason for the dramatic increase in mortality attributable to youth drinking over our study period6.

Third, cultural norms and external shocks may sharpen country disparities. In Viet Nam, alcohol use is deeply embedded in cultural ceremonies, social interactions, and business practices34, normalizing early and frequent alcohol use among youth and undermining the implementation of regulatory action35. In Libya, which experienced a 772.8% increase in mortality attributable to youth drinking between 1990 and 2021, a prolonged civil conflict since 2011 has significantly disrupted health regulatory and emergency care systems and may have stimulated alcohol use and undermined previously effective alcohol control policies36.

Our findings have important implications for policy. Despite a substantial and encouraging reduction between 1990 and 2021, the global number of deaths attributable to youth drinking still exceeded 10,000 in 2021, underscoring the continued need for prevention efforts. Urgent action is especially needed in countries with high mortality rates attributable to youth drinking and those witnessing large increases in mortality in recent decades. Targeted and intensified interventions, including effective policy measures, should be developed, implemented, and enforced4. Behavioral interventions should be considered also. Interventions might aim to reduce risky behaviors through stricter enforcement of regulations concerning drunk-driving and violence37. In countries with low or middle SDI levels, priority might be given to capacity building and resource allocation to facilitate evidence-based measures, such as high alcohol taxation, enforcement of minimum legal drinking age, and restrictions on alcohol marketing targeted at youth38. Successful experiences should be summarized and shared for tailoring to other countries. Integration of adolescent-specific alcohol control measures with broader community alcohol restrictions may be effective39.

This study examined trends in overall and subgroup deaths and mortality attributable to youth drinking in 194 WHO member countries/territories from 1990 to 2021. Study strengths include use of a large and validated dataset and a comprehensive analysis previously lacking in the literature. We also acknowledge several study limitations. First, GBD estimates involve methodological limitations resulting from low quality or absence of raw data, which may lead to unexpected biases to these results, although the GBD framework incorporates corrections for under-reporting40. Two external validation studies based on real-world data generally match GBD estimates, particularly for cross-national trend analyses41,42. Second, the COVID-19 pandemic may have affected mortality patterns somewhat in 2020 and 2021. Third, comparisons between findings of this study and previous research should be interpreted with caution, as each adopts different age thresholds and definitions to define “youth drinking”43,44.

Conclusion

Between 1990 and 2021, mortality attributable to youth drinking across 194 World Health Organization (WHO) member countries/territories experienced a modest decline. However, an estimated 10,563 deaths occurred in those countries in 2021 and profound disparities remain across genders and countries/territories. To address persistent challenges, acceleration to implement preventative actions outlined in the WHO Global Alcohol Action Plan 2022–2030 is recommended. Key measures include establishing laws dictating higher minimum legal ages for alcohol purchases, increasing alcohol taxes, and regulating alcohol marketing (e.g., restricting alcohol advertising, promotion and sponsorship). These efforts should be supplemented by international efforts to implement effective and culturally-adapted behavioral interventions, especially in resource-limited regions.
