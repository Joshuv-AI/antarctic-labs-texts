Dual Challenges: A Case Report of Hirschsprung’s Disease and Anorectal Malformation in a Child
Authors: Yoshita Gupta, Leela Dhar Agrawal, Dinesh Kumar Upadhyay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHirschsprung’s disease (HD) and anorectal malformations (ARMs) are rare congenital conditions with an incidence of approximately 1 per 5,000 live births. While each condition presents unique challenges, their simultaneous occurrence is rare, complicating diagnosis and surgical management. A 6-year-old female with ARM, previously managed by colostomy and anorectoplasty, continued to experience stool passage issues. Further evaluation of colonic biopsies confirmed the diagnosis of aganglionosis and HD. A modified Duhamel procedure was performed to remove the aganglionic segment and restore bowel function. The postoperative care was uneventful, and the patient was discharged with normal bowel function. This case illustrates the diagnostic complexity of HD in patients with ARM in whom symptom overlap and diagnostic delays are common. Comprehensive evaluation and customized surgical management are essential along with long-term follow-up to monitor complications and improve quality of life.Keywords: Hirschsprung disease, Anorectal malformations, Congenital abnormalities, Colostomy

INTRODUCTION

Hirschsprung’s disease (HD) and anorectal malformation (ARM) each occur at an incidence of approximately 1 per 5,000 live births [1]. HD is a congenital condition resulting from the absence of ganglion cells in a portion of the bowel. Ganglion cells are crucial for coordinating relaxation within the enteric nervous system. In their absence, the aganglionic segment becomes spastic, leading to symptoms caused by functional obstruction of the distal intestine [2]. There is a higher prevalence in males for classical segment cases, whereas females are more likely to have a long-segment form. Approximately 70% of HD cases occur in isolation, while approximately 20% are associated with other congenital anomalies including gastrointestinal abnormalities, heart defects, polydactyly, and craniofacial malformations such as cleft lip or palate [3]. The effects on two or more organs derived from neural crest cells are termed neurocristopathies. These disorders, characterized by abnormalities involving neural crest development, include not only clinical syndromes such as Waardenburg syndrome and congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, but also affect specific cell types and structures derived from neural crest cells. Examples include the adrenal medulla, dorsal root ganglia, parafollicular (C) cells of the thyroid, and the ossicles of the middle ear [4].

This case merits review because of the rarity and diagnostic complexity posed by the overlapping symptoms. Timely recognition of HD in patients with ARM is crucial for optimizing treatment outcomes. Furthermore, this report contributes to the limited literature on the co-occurrence of these two conditions and underscores the importance of a comprehensive diagnostic approach and tailored surgical management in pediatric patients with complex congenital anomalies.

ARMs are common congenital abnormalities that play a significant role in pediatric surgical practice. These defects range from minor misplacement of the anus to more complex malformations such as cloacal exstrophy [5]. The simultaneous occurrence of these two conditions is rare and requires careful surgical intervention. This case emphasizes the importance of early diagnosis and tailored surgical management to achieve optimal outcomes.

CASE REPORT

A 6-year-old girl presented to our hospital with recurrent complaints of impaired stool passage and chronic constipation. At birth, she was diagnosed with ARM in the form of a vestibular fistula with a fistulous opening located within the vestibule at the posterior verge of the vaginal orifice. The patient underwent staged surgical management of ARM at the same hospital. Initially, sigmoid loop colostomy was performed, followed by definitive posterior sagittal anorectoplasty (PSARP) to create a functional anal opening. Three months after PSARP, colostomy closure was performed as part of the staged surgery for ARM.

During routine monthly follow-ups after ARM surgery, the patient experienced constipation, a common complication of surgical intervention. Initially, her condition responded to laxatives; however, over time, constipation persisted despite prolonged treatment. Eventually, she developed palpable hard fecalomas in the lower abdomen, raising concerns about an underlying condition. The exact onset and duration of constipation could not be determined as it was a continuous complaint noted during follow-up.

Given the severity of her symptoms and the failure to respond to conservative management, the medical team considered the possibility of HD. Rectal biopsy confirmed the absence of ganglion cells (aganglionosis). To assess the extent of the disease, laparotomy was performed and multiple biopsies were obtained from different regions of the colon. The descending colon was ganglionated, whereas the rectal and distal colonic segments were aganglionic. Subsequently, a colostomy was created at the ganglionated portion of the descending colon to prepare for definitive surgical intervention.

Six months later, the patient underwent Martin’s modified Duhamel procedure. The surgical method involved resection of the aganglionic segment of the colon up to the site of the ganglionated colostomy (Fig. 1). A small portion of the aganglionic terminal rectum was preserved to prevent injury to neuromuscular structures and pelvic innervation. Appropriate bowel preparation was performed preoperatively and the patient underwent surgery under general anesthesia. The ganglionated colon was mobilized and pulled posteriorly to the retained terminal rectum. A side-to-side anastomosis between the posterior wall of the retained rectum and the anterior wall of the ganglionated colon was performed using a GIA stapler with an 80-mm cartridge, effectively reconstructing the rectum.

During the procedure, the surgical team identified HD, as evidenced by the aganglionic segment of the colon. The resected colonic segment revealed the absence of ganglion cells in the myenteric and submucosal plexuses, which was consistent with aganglionosis. Ganglionated bowel tissue was verified in the pull-through segment, confirming the adequacy of resection.

Postoperative care included nil per os status, nasogastric decompression, and monitoring of vital signs. The patient was maintained on oxygen support and antibiotic prophylaxis, including meropenem, vancomycin, amikacin, and metronidazole, was administered. Isolyte-P was provided for patient fluid and electrolyte balance. The postoperative period was uneventful and the patient was discharged with normal bowel movements.

Four months postoperatively, the patient demonstrated regular bowel movements during follow-up with no significant complications or abnormalities. Regular monitoring revealed a positive outcome, indicating the recovery and restoration of bowel function.

DISCUSSION

The underlying causes of HD and ARM remain unknown. Multiple theories have suggested hereditary, familial, and environmental factors [6,7,8]. These are common congenital conditions observed during pediatric surgery. However, the occurrence of both anomalies together is quite rare, with an incidence of 2.0–3.4% [9]. The association between HD and ARM remains an area of significant clinical interest. A study by Sukawat Watanatittan et al. [10] conducted in Thailand highlighted the improbability of their coexistence as a mere coincidence. Based on their independent epidemiological incidences, the expected rate of the concurrent occurrence of unrelated events was approximately 1 in 8,750,000 live births. However, the observed rate in their study was significantly higher, at 1 in 110,000 live births, which is nearly 80 times the expected frequency [11].

Hence, their coexistence presents a significant diagnostic challenge, as highlighted by Nakamura and Puri [9] in their systematic review. They emphasized that the diagnosis of HD in the presence of ARM is often obscured by overlapping clinical features. Classical symptoms of HD, such as delayed passage of the meconium and abdominal distension, may be masked by underlying ARM, which makes early detection difficult. In their large series, they reported that HD diagnosis was frequently delayed until full correction of the ARM, primarily because of atypical symptomatology and inconclusive radiological findings [9]. This diagnostic delay was reflective of our case, in which the patient was initially diagnosed with ARM; despite undergoing corrective surgery, her symptoms of bowel dysfunction persisted. Rectal biopsy confirmed an association with HD in our case. Multiple colonic biopsies and colostomies of the presumed innervated colon were performed, which further confirmed the presence of HD. The modified Duhamel procedure was performed after 6 months.

Most patients with HD experience positive outcomes without significant complications after treatment. However, a minority (approximately 10%) may continue to suffer from constipation due to retention of the aganglionic native rectum; however, with time, this complaint resolves with the use of laxatives. However, <1% of patients may develop fecal incontinence. The most severe and potentially life-threatening complications include enterocolitis and colonic perforations, which are among the leading causes of mortality and morbidity in the postoperative period [10].

The management of this case, which involved both ARM and HD, underscores the importance of a comprehensive surgical approach and vigilant postoperative care. Surgical intervention typically resolves bowel obstructions. Regular evaluations ensure the timely detection of obstructive symptoms, fecal incontinence, or recurrent enterocolitis, which can significantly affect a patient’s quality of life.

The rarity of this dual pathology and its diagnostic complexity underscore the importance of this report. This case reinforces the significance of multidisciplinary care in the management of congenital anomalies by detailing the challenges and successful resolution of congenital anomalies using a tailored surgical approach. Additionally, this case illustrates the critical role of thorough histopathological evaluation in guiding surgical decisions, particularly when conventional interventions are insufficient.

Conclusion

This case highlights the challenges in managing co-occurring HD and ARM in a pediatric patient. Despite initial surgical interventions, persistent symptoms necessitated further investigation and led to a diagnosis of HD, which was confirmed both intraoperatively and histopathologically. The modified Duhamel procedure effectively addresses the aganglionic segment and restores bowel function. This case emphasizes the importance of thorough diagnostic evaluation of complex presentations and highlights the role of long-term follow-up in monitoring bowel function, managing potential complications, and ensuring an improved quality of life.
