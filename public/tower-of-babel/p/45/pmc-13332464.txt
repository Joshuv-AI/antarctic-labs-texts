APOBEC mutational signatures predict immune checkpoint inhibitor benefit in TMB-low metastatic NSCLC independent of PD-L1 status
Authors: A. Yaacov, A. Grinshpun, R. Gillis, A. Pomerantz, R. Basheer, N. Asna, N. Peled
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background∼60%-70% of patients with metastatic non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) have relatively low tumor mutational burden (TMB), with limited biomarker-guided immunotherapy options beyond programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression. APOBEC (apolipoprotein B mRNA editing enzyme, catalytic polypeptide-like) mutational signatures, defined by characteristic genomic footprints, are associated with higher TMB and a better response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). However, whether APOBEC independently predicts ICI response in TMB-low tumors has not been established.Materials and methodsWe analyzed 857 ICI-treated and 1328 ICI-untreated TMB-low (<10 mut/Mb) patients with metastatic NSCLC from the MSK-CHORD cohort. APOBEC status was classified using MESiCA, a machine-learning algorithm for mutational signature detection from targeted gene panels. Predictive value was established through treatment interaction testing, multivariate adjustment, propensity score weighting, and landmark analyses. External validation included 82 TMB-low patients from published whole-exome sequencing studies.ResultsAPOBEC-positive patients (n = 52, 6.1%) had significantly improved overall survival [median 33.7 versus 17.4 months; hazard ratio (HR) 0.60, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.42-0.85, P = 0.004]. No such benefit was observed in ICI-untreated patients (HR 0.98, P = 0.85), with significant treatment interaction (P = 0.032), confirming a predictive rather than prognostic effect. APOBEC remained independently predictive after adjustment for PD-L1 status and age (adjusted HR 0.57, P = 0.002). The benefit was particularly pronounced in PD-L1-negative ICI-treated patients (HR 0.51, P = 0.002). The effect was robust across propensity score, landmark, and bootstrap analyses. External validation confirmed independent APOBEC benefit (adjusted HR 0.23, P = 0.020).ConclusionsAPOBEC mutational signatures represent a robust predictive biomarker for ICI response in TMB-low metastatic NSCLC, identifying responders particularly among PD-L1-negative patients where biomarker guidance is most needed, detectable from targeted gene panels used in routine clinical practice.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed the treatment of metastatic non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), with anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) agents now established as standard of care in the first-line setting.1, 2, 3 However, only a subset of patients derive durable benefit from immunotherapy, underscoring the critical need for predictive biomarkers to guide treatment selection in metastatic disease.3,4

ICI therapy is mainly guided by PD-L1 expression; yet the association of ICI response and tumor mutational burden (TMB) has been well reported, with the biological rationale that highly mutated tumors generate more neoantigens capable of eliciting anti-tumor immune responses.5, 6, 7 While the accelerated approval of pembrolizumab for TMB-high (≥10 mutations/megabase) solid tumors by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) validated this approach,8,9 TMB was not adopted widely in NSCLC by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network. Moreover, TMB-high status is present in only ∼30%-40% of patients with NSCLC, leaving the majority classified as TMB low.10,11 Similarly, PD-L1 expression, while useful for patient selection, fails to identify all responders and excludes many patients who might benefit.3,4 Therefore, there remains a substantial unmet need for biomarkers that can identify ICI responders independent of PD-L1 within the TMB-low metastatic population.

APOBEC (apolipoprotein B mRNA editing enzyme, catalytic polypeptide-like) cytidine deaminases represent an endogenous source of mutagenesis in cancer.12, 13, 14, 15 The APOBEC3 family enzymes, particularly APOBEC3A and APOBEC3B, induce characteristic C>T and C>G mutations in specific trinucleotide contexts (TCW motifs), generating a distinctive mutational signature detectable through genomic sequencing.14,16, 17, 18 APOBEC-mediated mutagenesis is prevalent across multiple cancer types, including cervical, breast, bladder, and head and neck cancers, with APOBEC signatures detected also in NSCLC in varying proportions and magnitudes of contribution.16, 17, 18

Several biological features suggest that APOBEC signatures may be independently associated with ICI response, including their association with TMB, immunogenic neopeptides, and an immune-infiltrated tumor microenvironment, indicating not only quantitative effects but also qualitative ones.19, 20, 21, 22, 23 Indeed, prior studies have provided preliminary evidence linking APOBEC signatures to immunotherapy outcomes. The strongest evidence to date is in urothelial carcinoma. In an exploratory biomarker analysis of the randomized, phase III CheckMate 274 trial, tumors enriched for APOBEC-associated mutational signatures experienced favorable disease-free survival outcomes with adjuvant nivolumab compared with APOBEC-negative patients, whereas such stratification was not observed with placebo.24 In NSCLC, the data are modest, retrospective, and limited by small sample sizes.25,26 Further, prior APOBEC-ICI associations have been reported predominantly in TMB-high tumors, leaving the question of clinical utility in TMB-low disease unanswered. Overall, whether APOBEC mutagenesis is associated with improved ICI response in NSCLC, specifically independent of TMB and other important factors such as PD-L1 expression, remains unclear.

In this study, we sought to determine whether APOBEC mutational signatures predict ICI response specifically in TMB-low metastatic NSCLC. We leveraged a large discovery cohort from the MSK-CHORD database with both ICI-treated and ICI-untreated comparator groups to establish predictive value through formal interaction testing, assessed independence from PD-L1 status and tobacco mutational signatures through multivariate modeling, evaluated disease control rates (DCRs) as a complementary clinical endpoint, and carried out external validation using whole-exome sequencing data from published studies.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient cohorts

Discovery cohort

We conducted a retrospective analysis of patients with metastatic (stage IV) NSCLC who underwent tumor genomic profiling with the MSK-IMPACT assay, using the MSK-CHORD database.27 The discovery cohort included two groups: (i) ICI-treated patients who received at least one dose of ICI therapy (anti-PD-1, anti-PD-L1, or anti-CTLA-4, alone or in combination), and (ii) an ICI-untreated comparator group of patients who never received ICI therapy. Patients were included if they had (i) histologically confirmed NSCLC, (ii) stage IV (metastatic) disease, (iii) available MSK-IMPACT sequencing data, (iv) available treatment timeline, progression notes, and overall survival (OS) data, and (v) microsatellite stable tumors. Patients with microsatellite instability-high tumors were excluded given the established benefit of ICI in this population.

External validation cohort

External validation was carried out using published whole-exome sequencing (WES) data from two landmark NSCLC immunotherapy studies: Hellmann et al.11 (Cancer Cell 2018, n =75, nivolumab + ipilimumab) and Rizvi et al.28 (Science 2015, n = 34, pembrolizumab). Patient-level data were obtained from published supplementary materials aggregated by Chen et al.26 The validation cohort included TMB-low, ICI-treated patients with stage IV NSCLC. Sample-size feasibility for the validation cohort was framed in terms of the minimum-detectable hazard ratio (MDH) at conventional power via Schoenfeld’s formula.

Molecular profiling

Tumor genomic profiling was carried out using the FDA-authorized MSK-IMPACT assay, a hybridization capture-based next-generation sequencing panel targeting 468-505 cancer-associated genes depending on the panel version. TMB was calculated as the total number of nonsynonymous somatic mutations divided by the coding region coverage of the panel (∼1.2 Mb), expressed as mutations per megabase (mut/Mb). Actionable genomic alterations in key NSCLC genes were evaluated and included activating/hotspot variants in epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) (exon 19 deletions, L858R, etc.), ERBB2 (exon 20 insertions and known hotspots), BRAF (V600E/K/R), MET (exon 14 skipping), and the absence of any ALK, ROS1, or RET fusion.

APOBEC mutational signature classification

APOBEC mutational signature status in the discovery cohort was determined using MESiCA (Mutational Embeddings for Signatures Identification in Cancer Assays), a validated machine-learning algorithm specifically designed for signature detection from targeted gene panel data with low mutation counts.18 Patients were classified as APOBEC-positive if APOBEC-associated signatures (corresponding to COSMIC SBS2 and SBS13) were identified as the dominant mutational process (cosine similarity > 0.6). To assess MESiCA’s invariance to low single nucleotide variant (SNV) counts, we explored its probability scores by different SNV bins. APOBEC-positive call fraction was ∼ invariant across mutation count bins (3.4% at <3 SNVs; 6.7% at 3-5; 5.2% at 6-10; 4.7% at 11-20), and all APOBEC-positive calls in samples with <3 SNVs retained high MESiCA probabilities [median 0.94, interquartile range [IQR] 0.90-0.99], indicating that the classifier remains conservative at low mutation counts (Supplementary Figure S1 and Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108305).

Clinical endpoints

OS was defined as the time from the start of the first MSK-IMPACT sequencing to death from any cause. DCR was defined as the proportion of patients without documented disease progression at prespecified landmark timepoints (90, 120, and 180 days from ICI initiation) from the MSK-CHORD data. A sensitivity analysis re-anchored OS at first ICI administration based on the timeline data from MSK-CHORD.

Statistical analysis

Survival distributions were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared between groups using the log-rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards regression. To formally distinguish predictive from prognostic biomarker function, a Cox model was fitted with APOBEC status, ICI treatment status, and their interaction term. Multivariate Cox models adjusted for PD-L1 status and age. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) analysis used propensity scores estimated from age, sex, smoking status, histology, PD-L1 status, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, and line of therapy. To adjust for ECOG, available within ±90 days of ICI initiation for 84.7% of the cohort, multiple imputation was used (m = 10; Rubin’s rules).29 Propensity score matching used variable-ratio up-to-1:4 nearest-neighbor matching with a 0.2 standard deviation caliper without replacement. E-value analysis quantified robustness to unmeasured confounding. Landmark analyses at 3, 6, and 9 months assessed bias from early deaths and informative early censoring by excluding early deaths and resetting survival time to zero. Restricted mean survival time (RMST) was computed at 36 and 60 months with bootstrap CIs (1000 iterations). Two-sided P values were reported in analyses using the discovery cohort, whereas one-sided P values were reported for the external validation cohort. Analyses were carried out using Python (version 3.10) with lifelines, scipy, pandas, and matplotlib.

Data availability

MSK-CHORD clinical and genomic data are available through the cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics (https://www.cbioportal.org/).30 Validation cohort data were obtained from published supplementary materials. Processed data and analysis code sufficient to reproduce the main findings will be made available upon publication.

Results

Discovery cohort

The discovery cohort comprised 857 TMB-low (<10 mut/Mb) ICI-treated patients with metastatic NSCLC from the MSK-CHORD database (Figure 1A). Among these, 52 patients (6.1%) were classified as APOBEC-dominant based on MESiCA signature analysis (see ‘Materials and Methods’ section). Baseline characteristics were generally balanced between APOBEC-positive and APOBEC-negative groups in the ICI-treated cohort (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108305). The groups were similar with respect to sex distribution (44.2% versus 46.2% male, P > .05), histology (73.1% versus 75.5% adenocarcinoma; 9.6% versus 10.3% squamous, P > 0.05), and PD-L1 positivity (40.4% versus 39.6%, P > 0.05). APOBEC-positive patients were younger (median 64 versus 69 years, P = 0.002). Notably, since MESiCA provides a dominant signature, there is mutual exclusivity with the tobacco mutational signature, which was found in 28.6% of APOBEC-negative patients (P < 0.0001). Deaths occurred in 63.5% of APOBEC-positive and 75.0% of APOBEC-negative patients.

APOBEC predicts ICI benefit in TMB-low metastatic NSCLC

In the ICI-treated TMB-low discovery cohort, APOBEC-positive patients demonstrated significantly improved OS compared with APOBEC-negative patients (Figure 1B). Median OS was 33.7 months in the APOBEC-positive group versus 17.4 months in the APOBEC-negative group, with an HR of 0.60 (95% CI 0.42-0.85, P = 0.004), representing a 40% reduction in the risk of death. To determine whether this association reflected a predictive (ICI-specific) or prognostic (treatment-independent) effect, we examined the ICI-untreated comparator cohort (Figure 1C). APOBEC status was not associated with survival in ICI-untreated patients (HR 0.98, 95% CI 0.77-1.29; P = 0.85). To formally distinguish predictive from prognostic biomarker function, we carried out an interaction test in the combined ICI-treated and ICI-untreated cohorts. The APOBEC-by-treatment interaction was significant (interaction HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.41-0.96, P = 0.032), providing the statistical evidence that APOBEC benefit is specific to ICI-treated patients and not a general prognostic factor. The effect was consistent when excluding first-line targeted therapy, restricting the comparator cohort to chemotherapy only (n = 918; interaction HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.41-0.96; P = 0.032) (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108305).

To corroborate the survival findings with a complementary clinical endpoint, we assessed DCRs at landmark timepoints in the ICI-treated discovery cohort (see ‘Materials and Methods’ section). APOBEC-positive patients demonstrated significantly higher disease control across all assessed timepoints (Figure 1D): at 90 days (23.1% versus 9.3%; OR 2.93, P = 0.011), 120 days (22.0% versus 8.5%; OR 3.03; P = 0.009), and 180 days (21.4% versus 7.6%; OR 3.32; P = 0.006). APOBEC-positive patients were ∼ three times more likely to achieve disease control at each timepoint, confirming that the survival benefit reflects meaningful clinical disease control rather than postprogression survival differences.

APOBEC is independent of PD-L1

To assess whether APOBEC provides independent predictive value beyond established clinical factors, we carried out multivariate Cox regression adjusting for PD-L1 status and age, as PD-L1 is the most widely used biomarker for ICI response and because APOBEC-dominant patients were younger than APOBEC-negative patients in our cohort. APOBEC-positive status remained a significant independent predictor of improved survival (adjusted HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.40-0.81, P = 0.002), with the adjusted effect size larger than the univariate estimate, indicating that confounders, if anything, attenuated the APOBEC signal (Figure 1E). Similar to the univariate analysis, in the ICI-untreated patients, there was no OS association by APOBEC status (Figure 1E). The survival benefit was preserved when OS was re-anchored at ICI initiation: HR 0.69 (95% CI 0.48-0.99, P = 0.042; n = 845; Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108305). The attenuation relative to the sequencing-anchored primary estimate (HR 0.60) reflects that 27.9% of patients were sequenced after their first ICI dose (median seq-to-ICI 11 days, IQR −8 to 120), which mechanically shortens effective follow-up under the ICI anchor. Lastly, since ECOG performance status near ICI treatment (within 90 days) was available for 84.7% of patients, ECOG-adjusted analysis was carried out on the full cohort using multiple imputation (m = 10). The APOBEC-adjusted HR was 0.55 (95% CI 0.39-0.79, P = 0.001).

To assess robustness, we carried out sensitivity analyses examining the APOBEC effect across different analytical thresholds, treatment subtypes, and detection methodologies. Firstly, we evaluated whether the survival benefit was sensitive to the minimum panel SNV count. Across thresholds from ≥1 to ≥5 SNVs, the APOBEC effect remained consistent (HR 0.56-0.61), with significance maintained through ≥4 SNVs (P = 0.009) and marginal at ≥5 SNVs (P = 0.051), likely reflecting reduced power from sample attrition (Figure 2A). Secondly, the survival advantage was preserved regardless of ICI target (heterogeneity P = 0.890) and whether chemotherapy was combined (ICI alone: HR 0.61, P = 0.035; ICI plus chemotherapy: HR 0.54, P = 0.028; heterogeneity P = 0.629) (Figure 2B). Thirdly, we compared MESiCA to SigMA for signature detection.31 The cut-off for SigMA is 5 SNVs. Among samples with ≥5 SNVs (n = 426), concordance was 90.4% (Cohen κ = 0.50), with 100% of MESiCA-positive cases confirmed by SigMA (Supplementary Figure S4A, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108305). Patients detected by MESiCA in the low-SNV stratum (<5 SNVs; n = 28) demonstrated significant improvement (HR 0.55, P = 0.017), whereas SigMA-only cases (n = 41) showed no benefit (HR 1.13, P = 0.500) (Supplementary Figure S4B, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108305). Given the predominance of tobacco-related mutagenesis in lung cancer and the evidence of tobacco-associated mutagenesis and improved ICI response,18,28,32 we investigated whether the APOBEC effect was confounded by tobacco mutational signatures. In our cohort, tobacco mutagenesis itself had no predictive value for ICI response (P = 0.885). Among TMB-low patients with metastatic NSCLC, the tobacco mutational signature was not associated with OS (HR 1.01, 95% CI 0.85-1.21, P = 0.885). In the tobacco-negative subpopulation (n = 627), the APOBEC effect was fully preserved (HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.42-0.85, P = 0.004). Lastly, further adjustment for tobacco signature in the multivariate model did not change the APOBEC estimate (adjusted HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.40-0.81, P = 0.002). These findings demonstrate that APOBEC mutational signatures represent a biologically and statistically distinct predictive biomarker, unconfounded by tobacco-associated mutagenesis.

Clinical subgroup analyses

To assess whether the APOBEC predictive effect was consistent across clinically defined subgroups, we carried out prespecified stratified analyses within the stage IV TMB-low ICI-treated cohort.

When stratified by PD-L1 status, APOBEC-positive patients showed a significant survival benefit in the PD-L1-negative subgroup (HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.33-0.79, P = 0.002; n = 517, APOBEC-positive n = 31, Figure 3A). As expected from our previous analyses, the interaction test was nonsignificant (P = 0.245), indicating that the APOBEC effect did not significantly differ by PD-L1 status. These findings suggest that APOBEC may be particularly informative in the PD-L1-negative population, where biomarker guidance is most needed. Four-way stratification by combined APOBEC and PD-L1 status (Figure 3B) demonstrated that APOBEC-positive/PD-L1-negative patients had the most favorable outcomes relative to the APOBEC-negative/PD-L1-negative reference group (HR 0.50, 95% CI 0.32-0.77, P = 0.002), while APOBEC-negative/PD-L1-positive also showed improved survival (HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.63-0.88, P = 0.001). The APOBEC-positive/PD-L1-positive group showed a similar trend (HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.32-1.05, P = 0.072). The APOBEC benefit was consistent across lines of therapy, with near-identical point estimates in first line (HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.30-1.06, P = 0.077; n = 502, APOBEC-positive n = 22) and second line or later (HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.37-0.88, P = 0.011; n = 355, APOBEC-positive n = 30; Figure 3C). The interaction test confirmed no significant heterogeneity (P = 0.731). Consistently, the APOBEC benefit was observed in both adenocarcinoma (HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.41-0.90, P = 0.014; n = 648, APOBEC-positive n = 38) and squamous cell carcinoma (HR 0.13, 95% CI 0.02-0.96, P = 0.046; n = 88, APOBEC-positive n = 5; Figure 3D). The extreme effect size in squamous histology should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of APOBEC-positive patients (n = 5). The interaction test was nonsignificant (P = 0.124).

Across driver mutations, the APOBEC benefit was preserved in patients wild type for KRAS (HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.43-0.89, P = 0.010), EGFR (HR 0.52, 95% CI 0.33-0.82, P = 0.005), and STK11 (HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.43-0.88, P = 0.008; Figure 3E). No significant interaction was detected for any driver gene (all interaction P > 0.25). However, small APOBEC-positive counts within driver-positive strata preclude firm subgroup conclusions. In a pan-negative subgroup defined by wild-type activating status for EGFR, ALK, ROS1, RET, HER2 (ERBB2), MET, and BRAF (n = 648, APOBEC-positive n =22, 481 events), the multivariate-adjusted HR was 0.55 (95% CI 0.32-0.96, P = 0.035).

Sensitivity and robustness analyses

To address potential confounding from baseline imbalances, we carried out multiple sensitivity analyses (Figure 4). The APOBEC survival benefit was consistent across all modeling approaches: unadjusted (HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.42-0.85, P = 0.005), multivariate-adjusted (HR 0.57; 95% CI 0.40-0.81, P = 0.002), IPTW-weighted (HR 0.64, 95% CI 0.41-0.99, P = 0.046), and doubly robust IPTW plus multivariate (HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.38-0.97, P = 0.036) (Figure 4A). Propensity score matching using variable ratio up-to-1:4 nearest-neighbor matching (observed ratio 1:3.67) confirmed these findings in a balanced cohort of 52 APOBEC-positive and 191 matched APOBEC-negative patients (HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.40-0.86, P = 0.006; Figure 4B). Covariate balance pre- and post-matching is summarized in (Supplementary Table S3 and Figure S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108305). To rule out bias from early deaths and informative early censoring, landmark analyses excluded patients who died before 3, 6, and 9 months (Figure 4C). The APOBEC benefit persisted at all timepoints: 3-month landmark (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.46-0.95, P = 0.024), 6-month landmark (HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.40-0.89, P = 0.011), and 9-month landmark (HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.39-0.91, P = 0.017). E-value analysis demonstrated robustness to unmeasured confounding, with E-values of 2.91 (multivariate) and 2.71 (unadjusted) exceeding the threshold of 2.0 (Figure 4D). To quantify the clinical magnitude of benefit, RMST analysis showed APOBEC-positive patients gained 5.5 additional months within 3 years (24.8 versus 19.3 months; 95% CI of difference: 1.8-9.1) and 8.8 months within 5 years (32.5 versus 23.8 months; 95% CI 2.2-14.4; Figure 4E). External validation

The external validation cohort included 82 TMB-low ICI-treated patients with stage IV NSCLC from two published WES studies (see ‘Materials and Methods’ section). Eleven patients were APOBEC-positive. PD-L1 and TMB distributions were comparable between groups (Figure 5A). Using Schoenfeld’s formula with the observed event structure (n = 60 events), the MDH at 80% two-sided power is 0.35. In the independent WES validation cohort, APOBEC-positive TMB-low patients (n =11) showed improved progression-free survival (PFS) compared with APOBEC-negative patients (n = 71) (HR 0.47, 95% CI 0.19-1.18; one-sided P = 0.049) (Figure 5B). After adjusting for PD-L1 status in multivariate Cox regression, APOBEC positivity was independently associated with superior PFS (adjusted HR 0.23, 95% CI 0.05-0.94, P = 0.020) (Figure 5C). Objective response rates were numerically higher in APOBEC-positive patients (55.6% versus 25.4%, one-sided P = 0.068), with the direction and magnitude consistent with the discovery cohort findings despite limited statistical power in the small validation sample (Figure 5D). Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that APOBEC mutational signatures represent a robust, independently validated predictive biomarker for ICI response in TMB-low metastatic NSCLC. APOBEC-positive patients experienced OS benefit and higher DCR, confirmed as predictive through formal interaction testing, validated in an independent cohort (adjusted HR 0.23, P = 0.020), and independent of PD-L1 status, age, and tobacco mutational signatures. These findings address an unmet need in ∼60%-70% of patients with metastatic NSCLC classified as TMB low who currently lack validated biomarkers to guide ICI treatment selection.

Furthermore, the predictive nature of the APOBEC association as opposed to a general prognostic effect was established through two complementary approaches. Firstly, the survival benefit was completely absent in ICI-untreated patients, excluding the possibility that APOBEC positivity merely identifies patients with a better prognosis regardless of treatment. Secondly, a formal statistical interaction test in the combined ICI-treated and ICI-untreated cohorts confirmed treatment-specific effect modification.

An important practical consideration is that APOBEC signatures were detected from targeted gene panels in our study using MESiCA. This is particularly important, as targeted panels are the ones used in clinics, meaning APOBEC testing could be integrated into existing molecular profiling workflows without additional tissue or sequencing requirements, facilitating clinical implementation. Further validation of detecting APOBEC from targeted panels should be carried out before utilization.

First-line systemic therapy in the ICI-untreated stage IV NSCLC comparator was enriched by targeted therapy (40%), reflecting the driver-positive population. Chemotherapy-based regimens accounted for most other cases, dominated by platinum-based chemotherapy. These regimen patterns suggest, as a hypothesis-generating observation, that APOBEC positivity in a TMB-low PD-L1-negative patient could help tilt the treatment decision toward ICI-based therapy rather than chemotherapy-only strategies; prospective head-to-head testing is warranted.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, this is a retrospective analysis, and prospective validation embedded in ongoing ICI clinical trials will be necessary before clinical implementation. Residual unmeasured confounding cannot be fully excluded despite the converging evidence across modeling strategies, which provides robust but not definitive support for a predictive effect. Furthermore, certain driver-positive strata have very small APOBEC-positive counts and subgroup estimates in these strata should be interpreted with caution. Secondly, the validation cohort was small, limiting statistical power for subgroup analyses, and powered only post hoc, though the consistency of effect direction and magnitude with the discovery cohort supports the validity of our findings. Thirdly, the mutual exclusivity of APOBEC and tobacco signatures reflects the dominant-signature classification approach used by MESiCA, which assigns each sample a single dominant mutational process. Fourth, different PD-L1 thresholds were used across cohorts - tumor proportion score (TPS) ≥1% in the discovery cohort and TPS ≥50% in the validation cohort. Lastly, the discovery cohort was from a single institution, though the MSK-CHORD database includes patients from diverse geographic and demographic backgrounds.

Future directions should include prospective, well-powered validation studies, ideally embedded within ongoing ICI clinical trials in metastatic NSCLC. Integration of APOBEC testing with existing biomarker algorithms (TMB and PD-L1) should be explored to develop composite biomarker strategies that maximize patient selection. The evaluation of APOBEC in the first-line PD-L1-negative or PD-L1-low metastatic setting, where treatment decisions are most uncertain, is a particularly high-priority application. Furthermore, considering the link between APOBEC and mutational burden, further investigation is needed to classify TMB-high tumors into more granular subgroups, as this remains a gap in current research.

Conclusion

We provide discovery and validation evidence that APOBEC mutational signatures represent an independent predictive biomarker for ICI response in TMB-low metastatic NSCLC. APOBEC identifies a qualitatively distinct mutational process whose predictive value is independent of PD-L1 status, age, and tobacco mutagenesis. These findings support the incorporation of APOBEC signature assessment into clinical decision-making algorithms for metastatic lung cancer immunotherapy.
