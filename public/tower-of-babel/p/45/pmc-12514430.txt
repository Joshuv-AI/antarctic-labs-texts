Expanding the Family Tropidoatractidae: Characterization of Two New Tropidoatractus Species (Armophorea: Metopida) From South Korea Based on Morphology and Molecular Phylogeny
Authors: Nanda Dwi Kristanti, Novia Cahyani, Quoc Dung Nguyen, Mann Kyoon Shin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhile oxygen is essential for metabolic processes, many ciliate species thrive in low‐oxygen or even oxygen‐free environments. Anaerobic ciliates have adapted to a broad range of such habitats, yet have long remained understudied compared to their aerobic counterparts. In recent years, however, interest in these lineages has grown, including in the family Tropidoatractidae Rotterová et al. 2018 (Order Metopida), which currently comprises of two genera, Tropidoatractus Levander, 1894 and Palmarella Jankowski, 1975, and five described species. This report presents two novel freshwater species of Tropidoatractidae, identified through a combination of morphological analysis and 18S rRNA gene phylogeny. Tropidoatractus songi n. sp. is identified by its arcuate body and 11–13 oblique rows of cortical granules between ciliary rows. Tropidoatractus rostrum n. sp. possesses an elongated, ellipsoidal body; a prominent, dark, beak‐like anterior end to the preoral dome brim; a macronucleus that is closely associated with rod‐shaped endosymbiotic prokaryotes; and sparse, irregular cortical granules. Both species exhibit a diplostichomonad paroral membrane. Phylogenetic analyses of the 18S rRNA gene confirm their affiliation with Tropidoatractus, revealing previously unrecognized species diversity among anaerobic freshwater ciliates.Keywords: 18S rRNA gene, anaerobes, freshwater ciliates, new species, phylogeny, protargol

Introduction

Oxygen plays a fundamental role in the metabolic processes of most free‐living ciliates, serving as the terminal electron acceptor in the mitochondrial electron transport chain and thereby enabling oxidative phosphorylation, which is a critical process for cellular energy production (Fenchel and Finlay 2008; Graf et al. 2021; Muñoz‐Gómez 2023; Müller et al. 2012). However, many ciliates exhibit specific oxygen preferences and tolerances. Oxygen‐sensitive ciliates are typically classified into three functional groups: obligate anaerobes, microaerophiles, and facultative anaerobes (Bernard and Fenchel 1996; Embley and Martin 1998; Fenchel 2014; Finlay et al. 1986; Hu 2014). Anaerobic ciliates, in particular, inhabit a broad range of anoxic or low‐oxygen environments, including soil, aquatic sediments, and gastrointestinal tracts (Fenchel and Finlay 1990, 1995; Fenchel et al. 1997; Müller 1988; Paul et al. 1990).

Anaerobic ciliates are sporadically distributed across various lineages within the phylum Ciliophora. However, certain classes, namely Armophorea Lynn, 2004, Cariacotrichea Orsi et al., 2012, Muranotrichea Rotterová et al., 2020, Parablepharismea Rotterová et al., 2020, Odontostomatea Fernandes et al., 2018 and Plagiopylea Small and Lynn, 1985, are composed of obligate anaerobic ciliates (Cahyani et al. 2025; Fernandes et al. 2018; Kristanti et al. 2025; Lynn 2004, 2008; Méndez‐Sánchez et al. 2023; Nguyen and Shin 2024; Nguyen et al. 2024; Orsi et al. 2012; Rotterová et al. 2020). Compared to their aerobic counterparts, anaerobic ciliates have been less extensively studied in terms of taxonomy and phylogeny. Nevertheless, they have garnered increasing scientific interest in recent decades, particularly as model organisms for exploring mitochondrion‐related organelles (MROs) and their associations with various prokaryotic symbionts (Beinart, Beaudoin, et al. 2018; Beinart, Rotterová, et al. 2018; Chen et al. 2022; Hao et al. 2025; Lewis et al. 2019; Méndez‐Sánchez et al. 2024; Rotterová et al. 2020, 2022, 2024; Schrecengost et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2024).

Among the free‐living anaerobic ciliates, the order Metopida Jankowski, 1980, which represents the most species‐rich taxon within the class Armophorea, has attracted the most research interest. This order so far consists of three families such as Apometopidae Foissner, 2016, Metopidae Kahl, 1927, and Tropidoatractidae Rotterová et al., 2018 (Foissner 2016; Jankowski 2007; Kahl 1927; Rotterová et al. 2018). Members of Tropidoatractidae are morphologically distinguished from those metopids by several features, including a flattened preoral dome, the presence of interkinetal ridges, sparsely distributed and widely spaced somatic ciliature, a short peristomium, and a reduced adoral zone (Rotterová et al. 2018). In addition, phylogenetic analyses of armophorean ciliates have revealed that Tropidoatractidae form a monophyletic lineage positioned at the base of the Metopida clade (Bourland et al. 2022, 2024; Feng et al. 2024, 2025; Li et al. 2021, 2023, 2025; Méndez‐Sánchez et al. 2022; Pomahač et al. 2024; Rotterová et al. 2018; Zhuang et al. 2022, 2024, 2025).

Levander (1894) first established the genus Tropidoatractus within the Metopidae. With the growing research interest in free‐living anaerobic ciliates, particularly within the class Armophorea, Tropidoatractus Levander, 1894, along with Palmarella Jankowski, 1975, is reclassified into the family Tropidoatractidae (Rotterová et al. 2018). Despite considerable morphological similarities, Tropidoatractus and Palmarella can be distinguished by the presence of the cortical ridges (present vs. absent), habitat preference (freshwater vs. brackish to marine), and differences in the small subunit (SSU) rRNA gene sequence (Jankowski 1964, 2007; Kahl 1927, 1932; Levander 1894; Rotterová et al. 2018). To date, only four valid species of Tropidoatractus have been described. In the course of investigating the species diversity of this poorly studied genus, we discovered two new species, providing both morphological characterization and phylogenetic analyses to determine their taxonomic placement.

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection

Both specimens were collected from the sediment of anaerobic freshwater ponds. Tropidoatractus songi n. sp. was collected from a pond in Cheongnyang‐up, Ulsan, South Korea (35°31′51.3″ N, 129°15′22.6″ E) in September of 2023, and Tropidoatractus rostrum n. sp. was found in a pond in Aewol, Jeju, South Korea (33°28′18.4″ N, 126°25′36.2″ E) in October of 2023. The collected samples were maintained in the original bottles, supplemented with grains to enhance bacterial growth. Samples were kept at room temperature for several weeks, but long‐term cultures could not be established.

Morphological Characterizations

Live cells were observed using a bright‐field microscope at magnifications of 40–200× (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). For higher magnifications, 400–1000×, differential interference contrast (DIC) illumination was used. The ciliature and nuclear apparatus of the cells were revealed using the protargol impregnation technique according to Wilbert (1975). Protargol powder was synthesized in the laboratory following the protocol of Pan et al. (2013). Measurements and quantifications of in vivo cells were performed on uncompressed, free‐moving specimens. Photomicrographs were captured using a CCD camera (AxioCam MRc, Carl Zeiss). Protargol‐impregnated cells were quantified and measured using calibrated Carl Zeiss Axiovision 4.8 software (Carl Zeiss Microscopy LLC) at 1000× magnification. Line drawings were made using Adobe Illustrator 2022.

DNA Extraction, PCR Amplification, and Sequencing

Five to seven cells of each species were isolated using a glass micropipette and washed three times with distilled water. Each sample was then transferred to a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube. Genomic DNA was extracted using the RED Extract‐N‐Amp Tissue PCR Kit (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. SSU rRNA gene sequences were amplified using TaKaRa ExTaq polymerase (TaKaRa Biomedicals, Japan) with universal eukaryotic primers EukA and EukB (Medlin et al. 1988). The amplification conditions followed the protocol of Méndez‐Sánchez et al. (2022). The PCR products were analyzed by 1.2% agarose gel electrophoresis and sequenced using five primers (EukA, EukB, 900F, 900R, 528F) (Alves‐de‐Souza et al. 2011; Lu et al. 2025) on an ABI automated sequencer (Cosmo Genetech, Seoul, South Korea). Sequence assembly and contig generation were performed using Geneious Prime (ver. 2019.0.4).

Phylogeny Analyses

A dataset of 18S rRNA gene sequences was generated, including two newly obtained sequences of Tropidoatractus and 101 sequences retrieved from the GenBank, including four Plagiopylea species as outgroup. Alignments were generated using the MAFFT method in the MAFFT server https://mafft.cbrc.jp/alignment/server/index.html under the G‐INS‐i algorithm and default settings (Katoh et al. 2019). Alignments were carefully edited and trimmed manually using MEGA11 (Tamura et al. 2021). The final dataset comprising 1707 unambiguously aligned characters from the original alignment of 1886 bp is available upon request. Phylogenetic analyses, including Bayesian inference (BI) and maximum likelihood (ML), were performed to determine the phylogenetic positions of the two new species. ML analysis was performed in IQ‐TREE v.2.3.3 (Minh et al. 2020), with the GTR + F + R6 model selected based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Node support was assessed using 1000 bootstrap pseudoreplicates. BI analysis was performed using MrBayes 3.2.6 (Ronquist et al. 2012). The best evolutionary model, the GTR + I + G model, was selected using the AIC in MrModeltest 2.4 (Nylander 2004). Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulations were run for ten million generations, sampling every 100 generations until the mean standard deviation of the split frequencies was less than 0.01. The first 25% of the sampled trees were discarded as burn‐in. Phylogenetic tree topologies were visualized using FigTree v.1.4.4 (Rambaut 2018). Genetic distances were calculated using the Kimura 2‐parameter model in MEGA11 (Kimura 1980; Tamura et al. 2021).

Terminology

The morphological terminology is based on Foissner and Agatha (1999), Lynn (2008), and Rotterová et al. (2018).

Diagnosis

Medium‐sized cells, 70–95 × 35–50 μm in vivo, with an arcuate‐shaped or bow‐like body exhibiting a strongly convex right margin and nearly straight left margin. The cell is widest at the middle and tapers gradually towards the rounded posterior end. The preoral dome is rounded, slightly overhanging the left margin with the minimal torsion. Macronucleus is globular, approximately 24 μm across. Colorless cortical granules are arranged in 11–13 oblique interkinetal rows between somatic kineties. Paroral membrane is of diplostichomonad type.

Type Locality and Habitat

A sediment sample from the freshwater pond in Ulsan (35°31′51.3″ N, 129°15′22.6″ E), South Korea.

Etymology

The species group name songi (L. gen. masc.): Named in honor of Professor Weibo Song (Qingdao, China) for his outstanding contributions to the taxonomy, phylogeny, and systematics of ciliates. The name is a Latinized genitive noun, formed in accordance with ICZN (1999) Article 31.1.2.

Description

Body size 70–95 × 35–50 μm (n = 15), with an average of 84 × 40 μm in vivo. Body size of protargol‐impregnated specimens 70–105 × 40–55 μm (n = 27), 87 × 47 μm on average (Figures 1A,F,G and 2A,D–G). The length to width ratio averages 2.1:1 in vivo (Figures 1A and 2A). Arcuate body, featuring a strongly convex right side, almost straight left side, and dorsoventrally flattened. The posterior end is rounded. Ridges between somatic ciliary rows are inconspicuous. Preoral dome typical of Tropidoatractus, forming a small snout that slightly overhangs the left side of the body with minimal twist (Figures 1A and 2A). An aggregate of dark granules forms a black spot at the anterior extremity of the preoral dome (Figures 1A and 2A,F,G). Macronucleus is single, globular, 15–30 μm across after protargol‐impregnation and located at the same level as the peristome. One globular micronucleus is adjacent to the macronucleus, about 4 μm across in protargol‐impregnated specimens (Figures 1G, 2D,E,J). Contractile vacuole is located terminally (Figures 1A and 2A,C). The colorless cytoplasm contained numerous granules and endosymbiotic prokaryotes. Two types of symbiotic prokaryotes differed in shape and size: type I, rod‐shaped, measuring 5–16 × 1–1.5 μm; and type II, needle‐shaped, 4–6 × 0.3–0.5 μm (Figures 1A,B and 2H). Cortical granules are colorless, arranged in 11–13 oblique rows between somatic kineties, forming a peripheral fringe in optical section (Figures 1B,C and 2B,I). The organism swims moderately while rotating around its longitudinal axis.

All somatic kineties composed of regularly arranged dikinetids, with loose gaps, forming 16–22 longitudinal ciliary rows, including 6–9 preoral dome kineties (Figures 1F,G, 2D,E). Ordinary somatic cilia 10–13 μm long, perizonal cilia slightly longer, 13–16 μm, and caudal cilia inconspicuous, 11–18 μm long (Figures 1A and 2C). Perizonal stripe occupied about 38% of the body length, invariably composed of five rows arranged in false kineties, rows 1–3 closely spaced, whereas rows 3–5 exhibit markedly wider spacing (Figures 1E,F and 2D,K). False kineties composed of 29–44 dikinetids (Figures 1F, 2D,K).

Adoral zone approximately 36% of body length, comprising 18 membranelles on average, and oblique rather than spiral, confined to the ventral side (Figures 1A,F and 2A,D,K). Paroral membrane diplostichomonad, that is, composed of two files of the basal bodies with different lengths: outer row (PM1) 17–23 μm long, while inner row (PM2) distinctly shorter, that is, 8–12 μm long, commences at the proximal end of the adoral zone (Figures 1D,F and 2D,K).

Description

Body size 90–150 × 30–40 μm (n = 10) with an average of 101 × 35 μm in vivo, and 90–160 × 30–70 μm (n = 26) after protargol impregnation, approximately 121 × 45 μm. Length to width ratio averaged 2.9:1 in vivo (Figures 3A,C–E and 4A,D–G). Body elongated ellipsoidal to ovate with a truncated posterior end, preoral dome narrow and flattened, slightly overhanging the left body margin with the brim forming a beak‐like structure; dorsoventrally flattened (Figures 3A,D,E and 4A,D,E). Cortical ridges inconspicuous (Figures 3A,C and 4A,D–G). Single macronucleus, broadly ellipsoidal, about 54 × 26 μm in size after protargol impregnation, usually at mid‐body, closely associated with rod‐shaped endosymbiotic prokaryotes (Figures 3A and 4I). Micronucleus single, globular, about 4 μm across, adjacent to macronucleus, often difficult to observe due to the dense endosymbionts covering the macronucleus (Figures 3A,D,E and 4E,I,J). Contractile vacuole terminal, shaped round to elongated ellipsoid (Figures 3A,C and 4A,F,G). Colorless cortical granules, less than 1 μm, irregularly sparse between somatic kineties (Figures 3B and 4H). Cytoplasm colorless, with a cluster of dark granules at the anterior end, numerous globular granules 2–3 μm across, and several food vacuoles filled with prokaryotes distributed throughout the cytoplasm (Figures 3A and 4A,B,F,G). Swims slowly while rotating around its longitudinal axis.

Ordinary somatic cilia 8–9 μm long, perizonal stripe cilia slightly longer, 10–13 μm long (Figures 3A and 4A). Excluding perizonal stripe rows, 14–20 somatic kineties in total, including 7–12 preoral dome kineties (Figures 3D,E and 4D,E). Approximately four elongated caudal cilia, 11–13 μm long (Figures 3A and 4C). Perizonal stripe composed of invariably five rows arranged in false kineties with densely spaced dikinetids, both basal bodies ciliated, 32 false kineties on average; rows 4 and 5 shorter than other rows at both ends; rows 1–3 closely spaced, separated from rows 4 and 5 by a distinctly wider gap (Figures 3D,H and 4D,K).

The adoral zone comprising 15–20 membranelles begins at mid‐body and extends to the left margin of the ventral surface (Figures 3A,C,D and 4A,D,K). Adoral membranelles are typically rhomboidal, with those in the mid‐portion being widest, about 9 μm long, composed of three or four rows of basal bodies (Figures 3D,G and 4D,K). The paroral membrane is diplostichomonad and commences at the proximal end of the adoral zone, extending anteriorly (Figures 3F and 4D,K). The outer row (PM1) is about 22 μm long, while the inner row (PM2) is approximately half the length of the outer row, averaging 13 μm (Figures 3D,F and 4D,K).

The values in the nodes represent the statistical support of the bootstrap values (maximum likelihood (ML)/posterior probabilities (BI)) and black circles denote full support (100/1.00). The partial SSU rRNA gene sequences of T. songi n. sp. (accession number: PX354651) and T. rostrum n. sp. (accession number: PX354652) are 1714 bp, with 43.9% of guanine‐cytosine (GC) content and 1591 bp, with 43.2% of GC content, respectively. The SSU rRNA gene sequences from all Tropidoatractus species form a robustly supported clade (95/0.89 ML/BI). Our sequence of T. songi forms a sister clade to the Tropidoatractus sp. PP274975 with medium support (94/0.74 ML/BI). Tropidoatractus rostrum groups with T. spinosus MG896717 with full support. Moreover, the family Tropidoatractidae is divided into two strongly supported clades, corresponding to the genera Tropidoatractus and Palmarella (96/0.99 ML/BI). The family occupies a basal position within the order Metopida and is distinctly separated from the Clevelandellida + Metopidae + Apometopidae clade, with full support. These topologies highlight the distinct evolutionary lineage of the family Tropidoatractidae within the order Metopida.

Discussion

Morphological Comparison

The two new species, Tropidoatractus songi and T. rostrum , can be easily distinguished by several notable morphological differences. These include differences in body shape (arcuate vs. elongated ellipsoidal), body size (70–95 × 35–50 μm vs. 90–150 × 30–40 μm), the shape of the preoral dome brim (snout‐like vs. beak‐like), endosymbionts closely associated with the macronucleus (absent vs. present), and the arrangement of cortical granules between somatic kineties (11–13 oblique rows vs. 1–2 irregular rows). Despite these distinctions, both species share a diplostichomonad paroral membrane with the outer row longer than the inner row, a feature not previously reported in other members of the genus (Rotterová et al. 2018).

Tropidoatractus songi can be clearly distinguished from its congeners by its arcuate body shape and globular macronucleus. By contrast, T. levanderi Rotterová et al., 2018 has a body that ranges from broad to narrow ellipsoidal in shape, with an elongated macronucleus. Tropidoatractus ariella Rotterová et al., 2018 has an elongated, ellipsoidal body, with a bilobed macronucleus. Tropidoatractus acuminatus Levander, 1894 has an elongated body with prominent cortical ridges and an ellipsoidal macronucleus, while T. spinosus (Kahl, 1927) Rotterová et al., 2018 has an ellipsoidal body with an acute tail at the posterior end and an ellipsoidal macronucleus (Kahl 1927; Levander 1894; Rotterová et al. 2018). Additionally, T. songi differs in having a notably higher number of somatic kineties (16–22) than T. levanderi (5–14), T. ariella (9–10), T. acuminatus (8–11), and T. spinosus (12–13) (Jankowski 1964; Rotterová et al. 2018).

Tropidoatractus songi can be distinguished from T. levanderi by its larger cell size (70–95 × 35–50 μm vs. 50–70 × 15–30 μm), the number of dome kineties (6–9 vs. 4–5), and false kineties (29–44 vs. 11–16) (Rotterová et al. 2018). Tropidoatractus songi differs from the type species, T. acuminatus , in its number of dome kineties (6–9 vs. 5–6) (Levander 1894; Rotterová et al. 2018). Tropidoatractus songi can also be compared with T. ariella by the number of adoral membranelles (16–20 vs. 5–10), preoral dome kineties (6–9 vs. 2), and false kineties (29–44 vs. 8–10) (Rotterová et al. 2018). Additionally, T. songi can be distinguished from T. spinosus by its number of adoral membranelles (16–20 vs. 11–13) and false kineties (29–44 vs. 16–26) (Kahl 1927; Rotterová et al. 2018).

The beak‐like preoral dome brim is a unique feature that distinguishes T. rostrum from its congeners ( T. spinosus, T. levanderi, T. ariella , and T. acuminatus ) (Kahl 1927; Levander 1894; Rotterová et al. 2018). Tropidoatractus rostrum also has a greater number of somatic kineties (14–20 vs. 12–13, 5–14, 9–10, and 8–10, respectively) and adoral membranelles (15–20 vs. 6–11, 7–14, 5–10, and 10–17, respectively) (Jankowski 1964; Rotterová et al. 2018).

Tropidoatractus rostrum resembles T. spinosus in its elongated body and the presence of rod‐like endosymbionts closely associated with the macronucleus. However, T. rostrum appears plumper and lacks the acute tail posteriorly found in T. spinosus . Additionally, T. rostrum possesses more false kineties (25–38 vs. 14–16) (Rotterová et al. 2018). Compared to T. levanderi , T. rostrum is significantly larger (90–150 × 30–40 μm vs. 50–70 × 15–30 μm), harbors endosymbionts associated with the macronucleus (vs. absent), a broadly ellipsoidal macronucleus (vs. narrow, elongated shape), a higher number of false kineties (25–38 vs. 11–16), and preoral dome kineties (7–12 vs. 4 or 5) (Rotterová et al. 2018).

Tropidoatractus rostrum is clearly distinct from T. ariella in its larger body size (90–150 × 30–40 μm vs. 58–90 × 13–20 μm), lack of staggered perizonal stripe (vs. present), presence of prokaryotes associated with the macronucleus (vs. absent), the number of false kineties (25–38 vs. 8–10), and preoral dome kineties (7–12 vs. 2) (Rotterová et al. 2018). Finally, in contrast to T. acuminatus , T. rostrum lacks a tail‐like posterior end, cortical ridges inconspicuous (vs. conspicuous), and a higher number of preoral dome kineties (7–12 vs. 5 or 6) (Levander 1894; Rotterová et al. 2018).

Molecular Phylogeny

Our analysis supports the polyphyly of Armophorea, with the order Metopida + Clevelandellida clade distinctly separated from the Armophorida, with high support (100/0.61 ML/BI; Figure 5). Two new species, Tropidoatractus songi n. sp. and T. rostrum n. sp., are confidently assigned to Tropidoatractus based on both molecular and morphological evidence. Tropidoatractus songi forms a well‐supported clade with Tropidoatractus sp. PP274975 (ML/BI 94/0.74), whereas T. rostrum clusters with T. spinosus with full support (Figure 5). Despite their close phylogenetic position, both species pairs exhibit considerable genetic divergence: T. songi and PP274975 differ by 0.0507 substitutions per site, corresponding to 79 nucleotide differences (nt), which is comparable to the divergence between T. songi and T. levanderi populations (0.0757–0.0674 substitutions per site; 69–107 nt). Tropidoatractus rostrum and T. spinosus show an even greater divergence of 0.0809 substitutions per site (121 nt) (S1). These molecular distinctions are accompanied by clear morphological differentiation (Table 3). Although morphological data are unavailable for the environmental sequence PP274975, its level of genetic divergence suggests that it represents a distinct species from T. songi .

All available sequences within Tropidoatractidae, including those newly obtained for T. songi and T. rostrum , produced a phylogeny that differs from that in previous studies (Bourland et al. 2024; Feng et al. 2024; Rotterová et al. 2018; Zhuang et al. 2021, 2022, 2024). Marine environmental sequences AY179985, AY256331, and AY256271 cluster within the Palmarella clade. Meanwhile, FJ000246 emerges as a sister lineage to P. salina , which is consistent with the findings of Rotterová et al. (2018). In contrast, the placement of AB252766 remains unresolved. Although this sequence was collected from saline habitat, it does not cluster with other marine taxa. Instead, it branches basally within Tropidoatractus, with medium to strong support (ML/BI 95/0.89). As the salinity of the sampling site was not reported, its affiliation with the predominantly freshwater genus Tropidoatractus remains uncertain (Rotterová et al. 2018; Takishita et al. 2007). These findings highlight the need for further taxonomic and ecological research to clarify species boundaries and environmental preferences within the family Tropidoatractidae. However, as demonstrated by Metopus Claparède & Lachmann, 1858, members of a single genus can exhibit broad ecological tolerance, inhabiting both freshwater and saline environments (Kahl 1927; Li et al. 2025; Zhuang et al. 2022).

Molecular phylogenies and consistent morphological differences clearly distinguish T. songi and T. rostrum from their congeners. The congruence between genetic divergence and morphological traits lends support to their recognition as distinct species within the genus.
