Multifunctional Glycine-Carbon Dots Protect Against Arsenic Hepatotoxicity Through Redox Balance and PI3K/AKT Activation
Authors: Mengqi Wang, Mengxuan Jia, Rongrong Zhang, Caixia Xue, Xue Ma, Mingjun Song, Gang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe theranostic potential of glycine-derived carbon dots (Gly/CDs) against arsenic-induced hepatotoxicity remains largely unexplored. This study aimed to synthesize Gly/CDs via a green, microwave-assisted method and to systematically evaluate their hepatoprotective efficacy and underlying mechanisms in a sodium arsenite (NaAsO2)-induced hepatic injury model.MethodsGly/CDs were synthesized using citric acid, urea, and glycine as precursors. Their physicochemical properties were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), fluorescence spectroscopy, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Following in vivo biocompatibility assessment, a murine model of NaAsO2-induced hepatotoxicity was established to evaluate therapeutic efficacy. Comprehensive biochemical assays, histopathological examinations, and transcriptomic analyses were conducted to assess oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, DNA damage, apoptosis, fibrosis, and the involved molecular pathways.ResultsGly/CDs exhibited excellent biocompatibility and demonstrated significant hepatoprotective effects, including restoration of redox homeostasis, suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and attenuation of hepatocellular DNA damage, apoptosis, and fibrotic remodeling. Transcriptomic profiling suggested the involvement of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway as a key molecular axis associated with the observed therapeutic effects. Overall, Gly/CDs preserved hepatic structure and function under chronic arsenic exposure.ConclusionThis study provides the first comprehensive evidence that Gly/CDs function as biologically active nano-antioxidants capable of mitigating arsenic-induced hepatotoxicity through redox modulation and modulation of PI3K/AKT signaling activity. Given their low toxicity, ease of synthesis, and multifunctional properties, Gly/CDs represent a promising nanotherapeutic platform for applications in redox biology, toxicology, and environmental health.

Introduction

Despite its historical and contemporary utility, arsenic remains one of the most pervasive and hazardous environmental toxins. Even at trace levels, arsenic accumulation poses severe health risks, affecting hundreds of millions of individuals globally.1 A substantial body of evidence from epidemiological and laboratory-based studies has established that acute arsenic poisoning, primarily via ingestion or inhalation, manifests with a spectrum of symptoms, including gastrointestinal distress, neurological dysfunction, hypotension, and characteristic electrocardiographic abnormalities.2 Subacute toxicity of arsenic exposure, typically emerging within one to three weeks post-exposure, is marked by progressive neurotoxicity, manifesting as hematologic dysregulation, sensory and motor deficits, gait disturbances, and, in severe instances, quadriplegia or respiratory failure.3 Extensive toxicological and epidemiological investigations have unequivocally linked chronic arsenic exposure, also referred to as arsenicosis, to a significantly elevated risk of multiple malignancies, including cancers of the skin, lung, bladder, liver, prostate, and kidney.4 Additionally, prolonged arsenic exposure contributes to a diverse array of metabolic and systemic disorders, impacting cardiovascular, neurological, endocrine, hepatic, and reproductive functions, underscoring the role of arsenic as a critical global health hazard.1 Reflecting its ubiquity and high toxicity, arsenic consistently ranks at the top of the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR) Hazardous Substance Priority List (2023).5 Moreover, both the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) and the World Health Organization (WHO) classify arsenic and its compounds as Group 1 carcinogens.6 Arsenic exposure poses severe public health risks, highlighting the need to clarify the molecular mechanisms underlying its toxicity and metabolism. Equally critical are the development of preventive strategies, mitigation technologies, and therapeutic interventions to counteract arsenic-induced pathophysiology and reduce the global burden of arsenic-related diseases.

The liver serves as the primary site for arsenic biotransformation, where arsenic undergoes sequential reductive and oxidative methylation reactions to yield less toxic metabolites, primarily monomethylarsonic acid and dimethylarsinic acid, which are subsequently excreted via the urine.7 Paradoxically, despite its central role in detoxification, the liver is particularly vulnerable to arsenic-induced toxicity.8 Mounting evidence from epidemiological and experimental studies indicates that chronic arsenic exposure disrupts hepatic development, impairs hepatocyte proliferation, and elevates the risk of a wide spectrum of hepatic disorders, including non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), fibrosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma.9–11 Among the diverse mechanisms implicated, oxidative stress has emerged as a key driver of arsenic-mediated hepatotoxicity.12–14 Mechanistically, arsenic exposure induces a pronounced imbalance in cellular redox homeostasis through excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), depletion of endogenous antioxidants, and impairment of mitochondrial and endoplasmic reticulum function. This oxidative insult initiates a cascade of pathological events, including oxidative damage to nucleic acids, lipids, and proteins, activation of pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, DNA strand breaks, and induction of hepatocyte apoptosis. Concomitantly, ROS-mediated signaling promotes the upregulation of inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α, and activates fibrogenic programs that compromise membrane integrity and tissue architecture.15–19 Collectively, persistent oxidative stress acts as both an initiator and amplifier of hepatic injury, promoting a self-sustaining cycle of cellular dysfunction and inflammation that drives progression toward chronic liver disease.

Given the pivotal role of oxidative stress in arsenic-induced hepatotoxicity, antioxidant-based therapeutic strategies have garnered increasing attention in recent years.16,20–22 A variety of candidate antioxidants, ranging from melatonin and natural small-molecule compounds (eg, citicoline, chrysin, sulforaphane) to vitamins, phytochemicals (eg, eugenol, resveratrol), and essential trace elements such as selenium and zinc, have shown partial efficacy in mitigating arsenic-induced hepatic damage in preclinical models.18 Notably, the rapid evolution of nanotheranostic technologies over the past decade has opened new avenues for targeted and multimodal interventions against hepatotoxicity and hepatocellular carcinoma, offering enhanced therapeutic precision and efficacy.23–25 Nevertheless, in light of these advancements, emerging evidence now points to the hepatoprotective effect of carbon dots (CDs)-based nanoparticles.26–28

Since their fortuitous discovery in 2004 and formal classification in 2006, CDs have attracted significant interdisciplinary interest spanning nanomedicine, materials science, and analytical chemistry.29 As a distinctive class of metal-free, quasi-zero-dimensional carbon nanostructures, CDs typically exhibit a suite of remarkable physicochemical properties, including high photoluminescence quantum yield, exceptional biocompatibility, favorable thermal and electrical conductivity, high optical responsiveness, and large surface-area-to-volume ratios.30 These features enable efficient surface modification and facilitate interactions with biological and chemical environments, making CDs highly adaptable to complex systems.31 In particular, the diversity in synthetic precursors, intrinsic crystallinity, and tunable surface modifications of CDs enhances their modularity, allowing precise engineering of properties to meet application-specific demands and offering transformative capabilities for personalized diagnostics and therapeutic strategies.29,32

Among surface modification strategies, functionalization of CDs with amino acids has emerged as a particularly promising approach, integrating the intrinsic optical properties of CDs with the molecular specificity and chemical versatility of amino acids.33,34 This strategy markedly improves biocompatibility and targeting efficiency, while enabling fine control over surface charge, solubility, and molecular recognition-parameters that are critical for advanced diagnostic and therapeutic applications.33,34 Beyond modulating the optical and electronic properties of CDs, amino acids also exhibit strong affinities for metal ions and commonly function as ligands in biological coordination systems, including metalloenzymes and other bioinorganic complexes.35 Furthermore, the structural diversity of amino acid side chains, which differ in polarity, hydrophobicity, and steric configuration, substantially broadens the applicability of amino acid-functionalized CDs across biomedical imaging, environmental monitoring, and energy-related applications.35–37 To date, CDs derived from taurine and cyclic amino acids have been successfully synthesized and applied in anti-cancer therapy, bioimaging, biosensing, and antimicrobial interventions.38–40 Recently, Subrata Pandit et al reported the synthesis of glycine acid-derived CDs (Gly/CDs) via the pyrolysis of citric acid in the presence of glycine under hydrothermal conditions.36 However, the biological antioxidant effects of thermally synthesized Gly/CDs are more likely mediated through regulatory redox pathways, rather than direct chemical radical-scavenging activity, which remains a subject of ongoing discussion.

Against this background, further differentiation among amino acid-derived CDs is warranted to optimize their application in arsenic-induced hepatotoxicity. Compared with previously reported amino acid-derived CDs, particularly those synthesized from sulfur-containing amino acids such as taurine or cysteine, Gly/CDs may offer distinct physicochemical and biological advantages. Glycine lacks reactive sulfur-containing side chains, which may reduce structural heterogeneity and limit unintended redox or metal-coordination reactions under heavy metal stress conditions, including arsenic exposure.41 From a biological perspective, glycine is a central metabolic intermediate directly involved in glutathione biosynthesis and the maintenance of cellular redox homeostasis.42 These characteristics are highly relevant to hepatic physiology and oxidative stress regulation, providing a strong mechanistic rationale for exploring Gly/CDs as a hepatoprotective nano-intervention against arsenic-induced oxidative injury.

In the present study, Gly/CDs were synthesized via a green, microwave-assisted irradiation strategy using citric acid as the carbon source, urea as the nitrogen source, and glycine as the functional dopant. The resulting Gly/CDs were systematically characterized with respect to their physicochemical properties and in vivo biocompatibility. Their hepatoprotective efficacy was subsequently evaluated in a murine model of sodium arsenite-induced liver injury. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that Gly/CDs exert pronounced protective effects against arsenic-induced hepatotoxicity, highlighting their potential as a biocompatible nano-antioxidant platform. These results not only broaden the functional scope of amino acid-derived carbon nanomaterials but also provide a translationally relevant foundation for their application in nanomedicine and environmental toxicology.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals

All chemicals used in this study were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany), Aladdin (Shanghai, China), and Macklin (Shanghai, China), unless otherwise specified.

Synthesis and Characterization of Gly/CDs

Gly/CDs were synthesized via a microwave-assisted pyrolysis method. Briefly, 3.0 g citric acid (C805019, Heowns, Tianjin, China) was employed as the carbon source, while 3.0 g urea (U820349, Heowns, Tianjin, China) served as nitrogen precursors, and 1.2 g glycine (G0099, Heowns, Tianjin, China) served as the dopant. The reagents were dissolved in 10.0 mL deionized water in a high-form beaker and stirred vigorously until complete dissolution. The resulting homogeneous mixture was subjected to microwave irradiation at 750 W for 10 minutes.43–46 This process led to the formation of a black, foamy solid indicative of CDs formation. Upon cooling, the upper fluffy layer was collected and ground into fine powder using an agate mortar.

For characterization, the morphology and structure of Gly/CDs were analyzed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL JEM-2100F). The X-ray diffraction (XRD) of Gly/CDs was obtained by a X-ray-diffractometer-Malvern Panalytical X’Pert3 Powder (Panalytical, Netherlands). The surface functional groups of Gly/CDs were then identified by Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy using a ThermoFisher Scientific instrument equipped with a DTGS KBr detector. In addition, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) of Gly/CDs was performed with a Thermo Fisher ESCALAB 250Xi system using Al Kα radiation (hν = 1486.6 eV, spot size: 500 μm). All binding energies were referenced to the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV from adventitious carbon. Photoluminescence (PL) properties of the powdered Gly/CDs were investigated using a steady-state fluorescence spectrometer (Edinburgh Instruments, UK), with both excitation and emission slits set to 5 nm.

Biocompatibility Evaluation of Gly/CDs

To evaluate the biocompatibility of Gly/CDs, twenty-four male C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old) were obtained from SiPeiFu Biotechnology (Beijing, China) and maintained under standard laboratory conditions (20–22 °C, 60 ± 10% relative humidity, and a 12-h light/dark cycle). Mice had free access to a standard rodent chow and water. All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Inner Mongolia Medical University (Approval No. 2024/513/05) and complied with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

After a one-week acclimation period, mice were randomly assigned to two experimental groups (n = 12 per group): the negative control (NC) group received 0.2 mL of normal saline orally, while the Gly/CDs group received Gly/CDs at a dose of 1.0 g/Kg body weight via oral gavage.47,48 The corresponding treatments were administered once daily for 7 or 14 consecutive days. On days 7 and 14, six mice from each group were weighed, and blood samples were collected. Subsequently, animals were euthanized via cervical dislocation, and the hepatic and renal tissues were harvested, weighed, and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (G2161, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for histopathological evaluation. Organ indices were calculated using the formula: Organ index (%) = (Organ weight/Body weight) × 100.49 Biochemical evaluation of hepatic and kidney function was conducted by measuring the serum levels of blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (CRE), uric acid (UA), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) using an automated biochemical analyzer.49

For histological assessment to evaluate the biocompatibility of Gly/CDs, formalin-fixed hepatic and renal tissues were embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained using Hematoxylin & eosin staining kit (H&E, G1120, Solarbio, Beijing, China), Masson’s trichrome staining kit (G1345, Solarbio, Beijing, China), and Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining kit (G1281, Solarbio, Beijing, China).49

Hepatoprotective Evaluation of Gly/CDs

To assess the hepatoprotective effects of Gly/CDs, twenty-four male C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old) were utilized. After one week of acclimation, animals were randomly assigned to the following experimental groups (n = 6 per group), as outlined below: NC group (received intragastric administration of normal saline), NaAsO2 group (intragastrically administered 5.0 mg/kg body weight of NaAsO2, S7400, Sigma-Aldrich, Shanghai, China), and Gly/CDs group (intragastrically administered with 5.0 mg/Kg body weight of NaAsO2 and 1.0 g/Kg body weight of Gly/CDs). In addition, 6 mice were intragastrically administered with 5.0 mg/Kg body weight of NaAsO2 and 1.0 g/kg body weight of Gly, with the experimental group accepted as the positive Gly group. According to the experimental design, all animals received the corresponding treatments daily for four consecutive weeks. The dosages and treatment durations for NaAsO2, Gly/CDs, and Gly applied in the present study were selected based on previous studies with small modifications to optimize consistency and efficacy.2,47,48,50

Glucose Metabolism Analysis

Prior to sacrifice, mice were fasted for 8 hours with free access to water. Fasting blood glucose (FBG) was measured via tail vein sampling using an AutoCoding glucometer. For the glucose tolerance test (GTT), mice received an intraperitoneal injection of D-glucose at a dose of 2.0 g/Kg body weight (prepared as a 20% solution in deionized water). Blood glucose levels were measured at 0, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 minutes post-injection using tail vein sampling.51

Sample Collection and Tissue Processing

Twenty-four hours after the final treatment administration, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of tribromoethanol (T831042, Macklin, Shanghai, China).52 Body weight was recorded, and blood samples were collected for hepatic function (ALT, AST, and ALP) and serum biochemical analysis. Mice were then euthanized by cervical dislocation, and the hepatic tissues were promptly excised, weighed, and assessed for organ index calculation. The left lateral lobe of hepatic tissues was dissected and fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for histopathology, while the median lobe was dissected, harvested, and stored at −80 °C for biochemical and transcriptomic analyses.

Histopathological Examination

Formalin-fixed hepatic sections (3 μm thickness) of the NC, NaAsO2, Gly/CDs, and Gly groups were prepared and subjected to H&E staining, Masson’s trichrome staining, PAS staining, and ALP staining (GP2090, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) to assess the hepatic pathology.49

Immunohistochemical (IHC) Staining

IHC staining was conducted to examine proteins associated with oxidative stress (KEAP1, NRF2, and HO-1), inflammation (IL-1β, IL-10, and NF-κB), DNA damage (γ-H2A.X, RAD51, and p53), apoptosis (BAX and BCL-2), and fibrosis (E-CADHRIN, VIMENTIN, α-SMA, and TGF-β1).53 According to the department’s protocols, formalin-fixed hepatic sections (3 μm thickness) of the NC, NaAsO2, Gly/CDs, and Gly groups were prepared, dewaxed, rehydrated, subjected to antigen retrieval, and blocked with 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA, SW3015, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Sections were incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies, followed by secondary antibodies at 25 °C for 30 minutes. Visualization was achieved using DAB (DA1010, Solarbio, Beijing, China), and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Quantification of staining intensity was performed with ImageJ.49 Details of antibodies are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

TUNEL Staining

Apoptotic cells in hepatic tissues of the NC, NaAsO2, Gly/CDs, and Gly groups were identified using the terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) staining.53 After rehydration and proteinase K digestion, the sections were incubated with TdT reaction solution (G1502, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) at 37 °C for 60 minutes. Followed by DAPI counterstaining, TUNEL-positive cells were quantified via ImageJ.53

Arsenic Determination in Hepatic Tissues and Serum

Hepatic and serum arsenic levels were determined by atomic fluorescence spectrometry following a modified digestion protocol.54 Briefly, hepatic tissues of the NC, NaAsO2, Gly/CDs, and Gly groups were homogenized and digested with a mixture of nitric acid and hydrogen peroxide at 120 °C for 1.5 hours. Blood serum samples were similarly digested under the same conditions. After cooling, all digested samples were diluted to a final volume of 10 mL with deionized water. Arsenic content was then analyzed using hydride generation-atomic fluorescence spectrometry, conducted by Qingdao Science Innovation Quality Testing Co., Ltd.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Serum levels of glucose metabolism (insulin), inflammatory markers (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α), and fibrosis-related proteins [hyaluronic acid (HA), type III procollagen (PC-III), and laminin (LN)] were quantified using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits, following the manufacturer’s protocols. Corresponding inflammatory markers (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α) and fibrosis-related proteins, including HA, PC-III, and LN, were also measured in hepatic tissue homogenates using the same assay platform under identical procedural conditions.55–57 All measurements were performed in triplicate, and absorbance values were recorded using a microplate reader to ensure consistency and accuracy.

Determination of Antioxidant-Related Enzyme Activity and Oxidative Stress Markers

After tissue homogenization, the oxidative stress in hepatic tissues of the NC, NaAsO2, Gly/CDs, and Gly groups was assessed by quantifying the activities of key antioxidant indicators, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), catalase (CAT), malondialdehyde (MDA), lipid peroxide (LPO), and nitric oxide (NO). Assays were performed in triplicate using commercial kits according to the manufacturer’s protocols (BC0175 for SOD, BC1195 for GPx, BC0205 for CAT, BC0025 for MDA, BC5240 for LPO, BC1470 for NO, Solarbio, Beijing, China).58

RNA Extraction and Reverse Transcription-Quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from mouse liver tissues using TRIzol® reagent (79306, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of the isolated RNA were assessed spectrophotometrically prior to downstream analyses. For cDNA synthesis, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (RR047A, Takara, Dalian, China), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was subsequently performed using the SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ Kit (RR820A, Takara, Dalian, China) on a Roche LightCycler 480 II real-time PCR system (Roche Diagnostics), with a total reaction volume of 20 μL. Gene-specific forward and reverse primers were designed using PrimerBank (version 2.1; https://pga.mgh.harvard.edu/primerbank/) and synthesized by Sango Biotech Co., Ltd.

The thermocycling conditions for qPCR were as follows: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s, annealing at 60 °C for 15s, and extension at 72 °C for 10s. Fluorescence signals were collected during the annealing-extension phase. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the internal reference gene for normalization. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The specific primer sequences used for reverse transcription and real-time PCR analyses are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Transcriptomic Analysis

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the hepatoprotective effects of Gly/CDs against NaAsO2 toxicity, transcriptome profiling was conducted. Total RNA was isolated from the hepatic tissues of NC, NaAsO2, and Gly/CDs groups. RNA sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2, with thresholds set at a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and absolute fold change ≥ 2. Functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, were performed to identify significantly enriched biological processes and signaling pathways.49

Western Blot

Western blot was employed to validate the effects of Gly/CDs on protein expression levels associated with PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in hepatic tissues.49 Proteins were extracted from the hepatic tissues of the NC, NaAsO2, and Gly/CDs groups using RIPA buffer (R0010, Solarbio, Beijing, China) and quantified by a BCA assay (PC0020, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Equal amounts of protein were resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE gels (P1200, Solarbio, Beijing, China) and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore, Shanghai, China). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk (D8340, Solarbio, Beijing, China) at room temperature for 2 hours and incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies. After washing, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 hour. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (W1001, Promega, Shanghai, China) and captured using a chemiluminescence imaging system. The full list of primary and secondary antibodies is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis and data visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 9.0) and SPSS (Version 19.0). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. One-way ANOVA followed by Least Significant Difference (LSD) post hoc analysis was used to compare three or more groups with normally distributed data. For non-normal data, the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests was applied. Two-group comparisons were analyzed using either the Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U-test, depending on data distribution. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characterization of Gly/CDs

As shown in Figure 1A, TEM analysis showed the presence of dispersed, quasi-spherical nanoscale features with sizes ranging from approximately 3–8 nm, which are consistent with previously reported CDs synthesized via microwave-assisted or other thermal carbonization routes.59–61 Due to the ultrasmall size and carbon-based composition, the contrast of individual dots is relatively weak, which is commonly observed for CDs prepared through similar routes. XRD spectrum (Figure 1B) showed broad peaks centered at 2θ ≈ 20.01° and 26.15°, corresponding to amorphous turbostratic carbon and disordered graphitic (002) planes, respectively. The interlayer spacing (~0.34–0.36 nm) exceeds that of crystalline graphite, indicating partial graphitization and nanoscale disorder consistent with CDs. The results of XPS (Figure 1C) demonstrated a high carbon content (60.75%) and significant incorporation of nitrogen (18.71%) and oxygen (20.54%), confirming successful heteroatom doping. These elements are known to influence redox activity and electronic interactions, providing potential biological relevance. In addition, the FTIR analysis (Figure 1D) revealed abundant surface functionalities, including hydroxyl, amino, carbonyl, and ether groups, which are favorable for colloidal stability and biological interactions. Moreover, the PL spectra (Figure 1E) showed strong, excitation-dependent emission peaks at 382 nm and 447 nm under 330 nm and 365 nm excitation, respectively, indicative of quantum confinement and surface state contributions. Hepatoprotective Effects of Gly/CDs Against NaAsO2-Induced Hepatic Injury

Given the central role of the liver in maintaining systemic glucose homeostasis, and the well-documented association between arsenic-induced hepatotoxicity, insulin resistance, and impaired glucose metabolism, glucose metabolism parameters were assessed as functional indicators of hepatic metabolic dysfunction. Based on the connections between the NaAsO2 exposure and impaired glucose tolerance, the hepatoprotective potential of Gly/CDs against NaAsO2-induced metabolic dysfunction was analyzed by assessing the FBG, serum insulin level, and GTT performance. After an 8-hour fast, the FBG level in the NaAsO2 group was significantly elevated compared to the NC group (Figure 3A, P<0.05), while the serum insulin concentrations were markedly reduced in the NaAsO2 group (Figure 3B, P<0.05), indicating pronounced impairment in glycemic regulation. GTT analysis further revealed persistent hyperglycemia in the NaAsO2 group (Figure 3C and D, P<0.05), remaining above baseline throughout the test, thereby confirming the induction of glucose intolerance. In contrast, treatment with either Gly/CDs or Gly led to a substantial reduction in FBG and partial restoration of insulin levels. Discussion

Arsenic, the 20th most abundant naturally occurring element in the Earth’s crust and the 12th most prevalent metalloid in the human body, exists in diverse chemical forms, including gaseous states, inorganic species such as arsenite and arsenate, and organic compounds like monomethylarsonic acid, dimethylarsinic acid, and trimethylarsine oxide.62,63 These multifaceted chemical forms, coupled with the high environmental mobility and persistence of arsenic, enable the widespread distribution of arsenic across terrestrial, aquatic, and atmospheric systems. Both geogenic sources (eg, volcanic activity and mineral weathering) and anthropogenic inputs (eg, mining, industrial emissions, and pesticides) contribute to this global dispersion, resulting in notable ecological consequences and increased human exposure risks.18

Historically, arsenic compounds have been employed in traditional medicinal systems across ancient Chinese, Indian, and Greco-Roman civilizations to treat various ailments, including respiratory disorders, dermatological conditions, and infectious diseases, with such applications documented in classical pharmacopoeias.18 However, due to its recognized toxicity, the use of arsenic in mainstream medicine declined during the modern era. Nonetheless, scientific interest was rekindled in the 1970s, when Chinese researchers reported the remarkable efficacy of arsenic trioxide (ATO), the active component of a traditional Chinese remedy, in inducing complete remission in patients with acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL).18 This pivotal discovery led to the eventual FDA approval of ATO in 2000 for the treatment of relapsed or refractory APL.7 This therapeutic breakthrough not only revolutionized the clinical management of APL but also reignited global interest in arsenic-based compounds as promising agents for antimicrobial, antiviral, antiparasitic, and anticancer therapies.64,65 In parallel with its re-emergence in medicine, arsenic continues to be widely used in various non-therapeutic industries, including agriculture, animal husbandry, cosmetics, metallurgy, and semiconductor manufacturing.64,66

However, the intensification of anthropogenic activities has markedly elevated environmental arsenic concentrations, aggravating the global burden of arsenicosis and drawing heightened attention from toxicologists and public health agencies.64,65 Among organs susceptible to arsenic toxicity, the liver is particularly at risk during chronic exposure. As the primary site for xenobiotic metabolism and systemic detoxification, the liver undergoes a spectrum of pathological alterations in response to arsenic insult. These include oxidative stress, dysregulated cellular proliferation and differentiation, apoptosis, autophagy, lipid metabolic disturbances, and persistent inflammation, all of which synergistically compromise hepatic function and drive disease progression.67

Notably, advances in redox biology have positioned oxidative stress as a central mechanism in arsenic-induced hepatic injury. Arsenic exposure leads to excessive generation of ROS, depletion of endogenous antioxidants, and impairment of mitochondrial function. These redox disturbances initiate a range of cellular damages, including DNA oxidation, protein modification, and lipid peroxidation, which together compromise hepatocellular viability and homeostasis. Modulating redox homeostasis has not only deepened our mechanistic understanding of arsenic-induced hepatotoxicity but has also informed the development of public health and environmental strategies aimed at mitigating arsenic-related risks.

While a variety of natural compounds, such as α-lipoic acid,67 baicalein,68 mitoquinone,69 melatonin,70 and curcumin,71 as well as various herbal extracts72–74 have shown hepatoprotective potential against arsenic-induced hepatotoxicity through antioxidant mechanisms, emerging attention has shifted toward engineered nanomaterials with intrinsic redox-modulating properties.75–77 Unlike conventional antioxidants, emerging nano-antioxidants exhibit potent hepatoprotective activity and may operate through distinct redox-regulating mechanisms, offering new opportunities for both mechanistic studies and translational applications.75–77 Consistent with these former publications, our findings in the current study demonstrate that Gly/CDs treatment markedly alleviates NaAsO2-induced hepatic impairments, as reflected by the suppression of excessive ROS accumulation and lipid peroxidation, together with the restoration of endogenous antioxidant capacity, including GSH levels and antioxidant enzyme activities. These coordinated biochemical improvements support the notion that Gly/CDs confer hepatoprotection primarily through the restoration of hepatic redox homeostasis.

CDs, an emerging class of metal-free, quasi-zero-dimensional nanomaterials with diameters typically below 10 nm, were first discovered serendipitously in 2004 during the purification of arc-synthesized carbon nanotubes.78 Since then, CDs have garnered considerable scientific interest due to their unique photophysical properties, tunable structures, and remarkable functional versatility. Their facile and cost-effective synthesis, coupled with intrinsic biocompatibility, low toxicity, and environmental sustainability, distinguishes them from conventional nanomaterials and positions them as highly promising candidates for diverse applications, including biomedicine, optoelectronics, photocatalysis, environmental sensing, and light-emitting devices.79,80 In preclinical studies, CDs have also demonstrated significant potential as platforms for gene delivery, drug nanocarriers, diagnostic probes, and advanced cancer therapies, such as photodynamic and photothermal treatments.81,82 Notably, a recent study demonstrates that CDs confer arsenic tolerance in Cicer arietinum L. by modulating arsenic uptake, enhancing ROS detoxification, and upregulating defense-related gene expression,83 underscoring the emerging role of CDs as multifunctional nanomaterials capable of mitigating arsenic-induced toxicity.

Despite these promising advances, conventional CD synthesis methods often involve harsh chemicals, high energy inputs, and complex procedures, posing limitations for biomedical translation.84 In response, green synthesis strategies have been developed, employing natural or renewable carbon sources, including a diverse array of biological materials, such as fruits, spices, animal products, microorganisms, herbal medicines, food waste, and beverages, as well as small biomolecules such as vitamins, proteins, amino acids, and carbohydrates.84 These environmentally benign precursors not only reduce toxicity and production costs but also significantly enhance the biocompatibility and functionalization of the resulting CDs, thereby improving environmental sustainability and enabling precise customization for targeted biomedical applications.79

Among these green synthesis strategies for CDs, the applications of amino acids have emerged as particularly effective in enhancing their chemical, electronic, and optical properties when compared to other ligands.35 Amino acids, owing to their abundance, renewability, biocompatibility, and low cost, represent ideal candidates for the sustainable synthesis of functionalized CDs. The presence of multiple reactive groups, most notably amino and hydroxyl moieties, facilitates efficient cleavage, cross-linking, and carbonization during high-temperature synthesis processes, thereby enabling the formation of structurally robust and chemically versatile nanostructures.85 Beyond contributing to improved electronic and photoluminescent behavior, amino acids also exhibit high affinity for metal cations, functioning as natural ligands in bioinorganic systems and metalloenzyme coordination.35 Moreover, their diverse side-chain functionalities, ranging in polarity, charge, and hydrophobicity, offer additional tunability for tailoring CDs toward specific applications across healthcare, environmental remediation, and energy technologies.35–37 Among these simplest amino acids, Gly, a fundamental metabolic intermediate and multifunctional biochemical precursor, is integral to the biosynthesis of essential compounds such as collagen, GSH, creatine, and purine nucleotides.86 Accounting for approximately 11.5% of the free amino acid pool in plasma and contributing up to 20% of the nitrogen content in body proteins, Gly is pivotal in maintaining nitrogen homeostasis and supporting a broad spectrum of biosynthetic pathways.86 Compelling evidence from a wide range of biological models, including in vitro cell cultures, in vivo animal studies, and clinical investigations, demonstrates diverse physiological roles of Gly, notably as an antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and cytoprotective agent.87 These effects are largely mediated through the enhancement of GSH synthesis, restoration of metabolic homeostasis, improvement of insulin sensitivity, attenuation of systemic inflammation, and stabilization of cellular membranes, collectively contributing to the mitigation of metabolic disorders.87 Notably, previous studies have confirmed the regulatory effects of Gly on antioxidant enzyme systems in response to arsenic toxicity, which in turn reduces the arsenic accumulation in Oryza sativa L.88 partly consistent with the results of the current research. However, the application of Gly/CDs to mitigate arsenic-related hepatic injury remains largely underexplored.

To bridge this knowledge gap and investigate the biomedical potential of Gly/CDs, we developed a rapid, microwave-assisted synthesis method using citric acid as the carbon source, urea as the nitrogen donor, and Gly as a functional dopant. This green and scalable approach yields biocompatible CDs that integrate Gly’s intrinsic redox-modulating properties with the unique structural and physicochemical advantages of CDs. Our findings demonstrate that Gly/CDs effectively mitigate NaAsO2-induced hepatotoxicity through multiple, interrelated mechanisms. Specifically, Gly/CDs attenuate oxidative stress by restoring hepatic redox homeostasis. In addition, Gly/CDs suppress hepatic inflammation by downregulating key proinflammatory signaling pathways, thereby limiting inflammatory cytokine expression. Furthermore, Gly/CDs exert protective effects against NaAsO2-triggered hepatocellular DNA damage, apoptosis, and fibrotic remodeling, suggesting their capacity to preserve hepatic architecture and function under chronic arsenic stress. Collectively, these results highlight Gly/CDs as a novel, multifunctional, and cost-effective nano-antioxidant platform with substantial potential for therapeutic development in the fields of toxicology, redox medicine, and hepatic disease management.

It is important to clarify that, although Gly/CDs are referred to as “nano-antioxidants” in this study, this terminology does not imply that they function as classical chemical antioxidants capable of directly scavenging free radicals. Given the thermal nature of the microwave-assisted synthesis process, labile reducible functional groups present in precursor molecules may be partially lost during carbonization. Accordingly, the antioxidant effects observed herein are more appropriately interpreted as biological and regulatory outcomes, mediated through modulation of endogenous antioxidant enzyme systems and redox-sensitive signaling pathways. This interpretation is also consistent with accumulating evidence indicating that CDs synthesized via thermal or pyrolytic routes predominantly alleviate oxidative stress through regulation of endogenous antioxidant systems and stress-responsive signaling pathways.89–93

Mechanistically, transcriptomic profiling revealed a strong association between Gly/CDs treatment and activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in NaAsO2-exposed hepatic tissue, suggesting its potential involvement in Gly/CDs-mediated hepatoprotection. This evolutionarily conserved pathway is a central regulator of diverse cellular processes, including survival, proliferation, differentiation, autophagy, apoptosis, metabolic homeostasis, and adaptive stress responses, by integrating extracellular signals and engaging in complex cross-talk with other signaling networks.94 Dysregulation of the PI3K/AKT axis has been widely implicated in the pathogenesis of numerous cancers, cardiovascular disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, and metabolic syndrome.94,95 Consistent with the former report,96 our data demonstrate that the NaAsO2 exposure inhibits the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, contributing to NaAsO2-induced hepatotoxicity. Notably, we report for the first time that Gly/CDs regulates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in NaAsO2-exposed hepatic tissue, suggesting a novel mechanistic basis for their therapeutic action. While these findings position Gly/CDs as a promising nanoplatform for mitigating NaAsO2-related hepatic injury, several important limitations and unresolved questions remain to be addressed before Gly/CDs can be advanced toward broader translational application. Notably, although the present study highlights the efficacy of Gly/CDs in mitigating NaAsO2-induced hepatotoxicity, a systematic comparative evaluation against CDs derived from other amino acids remains lacking. Given that amino acid-derived CDs may differ substantially in surface chemistry, redox reactivity, metal-binding behavior, and biological interactions depending on side-chain composition, direct head-to-head comparisons between Gly/CDs and sulfur-containing or other functional amino acid-based CDs will be essential to delineate their relative physicochemical properties and biological performance. Such comparative analyses would provide deeper insight into structure-function relationships and help clarify whether the observed hepatoprotective effects observed here are uniquely attributable to glycine-derived features or instead reflect more general properties shared among amino acid–functionalized carbon nanomaterials.

From a translational perspective, although the present study demonstrates favorable in vivo biocompatibility of Gly/CDs, further studies incorporating direct chemical radical-scavenging assays and in vivo biodistribution tracking following oral administration would be valuable to more comprehensively elucidate the functional properties, tissue distribution, and translational potential of Gly/CDs, particularly under chronic arsenic exposure conditions that more closely recapitulate real-world environmental scenarios.

Furthermore, although the redox-modulating capacity of Gly/CDs likely contributes to their therapeutic efficacy, the precise molecular targets and signaling nodes affected by Gly/CDs remain to be fully delineated. Moreover, although transcriptomic and protein-level analyses consistently indicate an association between Gly/CDs treatment and modulation of PI3K/AKT signaling, the present study does not directly interrogate pathway dependence, and thus the precise causal contribution of this pathway to Gly/CDs-mediated hepatoprotection warrants further investigation. Beyond these mechanistic considerations, the intrinsic multifunctionality of CDs offers additional opportunities for further development. For example, the inherent fluorescence of CDs could be exploited for real-time bioimaging of arsenic accumulation and oxidative stress in hepatic tissues, enabling integrated diagnostic and therapeutic applications. Additionally, the tunable surface chemistry of Gly/CDs provides a versatile platform for further functionalization, including the targeted delivery of hepatoprotective agents or co-delivery of synergistic antioxidants. From an environmental health perspective, the potential of Gly/CDs to chelate heavy metals or modulate detoxification enzyme activity also suggests possible utility in water purification and soil remediation.

In summary, our study presents compelling evidence supporting the use of Gly/CDs as a redox-active nanotherapeutic strategy for NaAsO2-induced hepatotoxicity. However, realizing their full clinical and environmental potential will require a concerted interdisciplinary approach integrating nanotechnology, toxicology, pharmacology, and environmental science to optimize functionality, ensure biosafety, and facilitate scalable implementation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study presents robust experimental evidence that Gly/CDs, synthesized via a green and scalable microwave-assisted method, exert potent hepatoprotective effects against NaAsO2-induced toxicity. Specifically, the present study demonstrates that Gly/CDs exhibit excellent biocompatibility and exert antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, accompanied by attenuation of hepatocellular apoptosis and fibrotic remodeling. Mechanistically, transcriptomic analysis revealed that these protective effects are closely associated with alterations in PI3K/AKT signaling, highlighting a potential regulatory role of this pathway in Gly/CDs-mediated hepatoprotection. Collectively, these findings define the experimentally validated biological functions of Gly/CDs as a redox-active nanoplatform for hepatic protection.

On the basis of these validated findings, the unique optical and surface properties of Gly/CDs suggest potential applications in bioimaging, targeted nanotherapeutic delivery, and environmental detoxification. Nonetheless, comprehensive in vivo validation and deeper mechanistic investigations are warranted to facilitate clinical translation and broaden their applicability. Taken together, this work lays a solid foundation for the continued development of amino acid-derived nanomaterials as next-generation therapeutic agents in toxicology, nanomedicine, and environmental health.
