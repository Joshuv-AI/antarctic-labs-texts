Pure Chitosan Microfluidic Spinning Affords Modular Core‐Sheath Fibers and Hand‐Crafted Scaffolds with Enhanced Fibroblast Compatibility
Authors: Ishneet Kaur, Alejandro Forigua, Hatem M. Titi, Yonatan Morocz, David Juncker, Christopher Moraes, Audrey Moores
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMicrofluidic‐spun hydrogel fibers are appealing for tissue engineering and cell transplantation applications, as they give access to fiber‐shaped tissues that mimic blood vessels, muscle fibers, or neural networks in vivo. Alginates are overwhelmingly employed as their base material, as they allow for simple processing, despite their poor cell adherence and weak cell‐matrix interactions. Alginates also require crosslinking, and thus can leach ions and lose integrity under physiological conditions. To overcome these limitations, we are reporting herein the first synthesis of pure chitosan fibers by microfluidic wet spinning, avoiding the use of crosslinking agents, and achieving excellent cell viability of 85%. These fibers exhibit higher mechanical strength (695 MPa) than alginate counterparts (2‐4 MPa). Our system can also accommodate a core of chitin nanocrystals to modulate mechanical properties or serve as a reservoir of bioactive molecules, such as methylene blue. As chitosan is a natural biopolymer, this work addresses the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (UN SDGs) 6 and 14. The resulting pure chitosan and chitin/chitosan composite fibers exhibit high processability and can be woven into a variety of structures. Finally, these microstructured chitosan fibers have the potential to be used as templates to create fiber‐shaped tissues or to develop into live building blocks for the assembly of very complex artificial tissues.

Introduction

Tissue engineering is an important field of research concerned with the formation of biologically active scaffolds with relevance to making implants, evaluating medication, or studying cell biology [1]. In tissue engineering, it is essential to have access to materials that can be processed reproducibly, feature both strength and flexibility matching those of biological tissues, be shaped into 3D scaffolds able to replicate the structure of organs, and be fully biocompatible to allow cell culturing [2, 3]. Hydrogel fibers have been extensively demonstrated as a powerful means to achieve some of these functions [1]. They can be processed into matrix materials, on which cells can easily polarize, stretch, aggregate, and migrate [1]. Specifically, 3D structures are achieved via the assembly of hydrogel fibers, which possess important attributes such as high length to diameter ratio (50‐1000), micron‐scale sizes (1centimeter to 100 centimeters), flexibility, and lightweight, making them highly suitable for biomedical applications [4, 5]. By folding, binding, and weaving these microfibers, one can simulate the physical microenvironment essential for achieving 3D cell culturing [6]. Melt‐spinning [7], electrospinning [8], draw spinning [9], solution blow spinning [10], direct writing [11], and coaxial electrospinning [12] are techniques developed to produce fiber materials.

Yet single/monolith fibers have limitations for targeted release, since biomolecules may easily phase‐separate, migrate toward the fiber surface, and get released in an uncontrolled fashion [13, 14]. In this context, core‐sheath structures offer a solution, with the sheath serving as a release control layer. Coaxial electrospinning is one of the most popular methods [15] to fabricate core–shell fibers [12]. However, these kinds of spinning techniques require physical solidification processes, limiting the type, material, and structure of the fabricated fibers [16]. In addition, only nanoscale fibers can be produced using electrospinning. On the other end, microfluidic wet spinning (MWS), which integrates microfluidics with wet spinning, has emerged as a solution to this limitation. The polymeric solution is injected into the microchannels of the microfluidic chip, where the produced fiber solidifies in a coagulation bath. This ultimately allows for precise control over fiber diameter, and for the introduction of a hierarchical organization within the fiber, including core‐sheath structures [17]. The formation of core–shell structures is prevalent in biological and geological systems. These types of composite structures enable the fine‐tuning of mechanical, chemical, and physical properties. Amorphous sheaths wrapped around a crystalline core are found in many biological structures [18], such as bone, where an amorphous calcium phosphate layer surrounds the apatite crystalline nanoplates [19]. A similar kind of structure is exhibited during the biomineralization of tooth enamel and nacre [20, 21]. These structures are important for formulating high‐strength flexible fibers that can be used for various biomedical applications, such as tissue engineering.

While microfluidic‐assisted synthesis has undergone numerous developments, it remains largely limited to the use of alginates as the primary constituent of the fibers, typically cross‐linked with calcium salts, either in their pure form or as composites. However, it has been extensively reported that cell adhesiveness, being one of the most crucial properties for cell growth on the fiber surface, cannot be achieved on the calcium alginate surfaces, which feature poor mechanical strength [22], propensity to Ca2+ leaching, fibrous structure loss in physiological environments, and lack of bioactivity [23, 24]. To remedy the latter point, cells are often grown on alginate fiberscoated with fibronectin, laminin or polylysine [25, 23], though these coatings add an extra processing step and are unstable and transient under cell culture conditions. Another strategy is the covalent linking of calcium alginate with peptides of various sequences (e.g. VAPG, RGD, KQAGDV), but again, these are expensive, and the process is long and complex [26, 27, 28] (Table 1, row 1). Finally, while they easily lose their structures during application, their molecular degradation remains poor, making them unusable in many applications. Besides alginates [29, 30, 31, 32] (Table 1, row 2), researchers have reported the use of GelMA [23], PCL [33, 34], PLGA [35], and collagen [36] to make hydrogels, but none combine good biocompatibility with mechanical strength.

Therefore, there is a pressing need to research new materials able to be processed by MWS without the need for a crosslinker, with better mechanical properties as well as cell compatibility. Chitosan is a biopolymer produced from crustacean waste, which is biocompatible, mucoadhesive, biodegradable, and non‐toxic [37]. It possesses ion adsorption and complex‐forming capabilities. Because of its many different chemical, physical, and biological characteristics, chitosan is an extremely adaptable substance [38]. While it had already been used in conjunction with alginates to make microfibers, it has never been reported in its pure form, in the absence of substances used for cross‐linking that can cause leaching as depicted in Table 1, third and fourth rows [39, 40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47]. Relying on chitosan's great flexibility and processability, we sought to use it in MWS in its pure form, thereby preventing the use of cross‐linkers for both the core and the sheath (Table 1, row 5). Achieving this goal can open new avenues, including the possibility of loading the core or the sheath with functional materials, such as biomolecules, as well as the growth of cells on the fiber's surface.

Herein, we report the first MWS synthesis of chitosan hydrogel as a biomaterial for the generation of on‐the‐fly core‐sheath fibers, which can then be further utilized for a variety of constructs, including coils and mesh‐like structures. We were able to formulate chitosan so that it could be processed by MWS and form core sheath structures, subsequently coagulated in a base bath. When using pure chitosan solutions in both inlets, we produced pure chitosan microfibers with diameters of 200–250 µm with an excellent Young's modulus of 365 MPa. To mimic the core–shell structures found in nature, we used oxidized chitin nanocrystals (Ox‐ChNCs), blended with chitosan, in the core and pure chitosan in the sheath, taking advantage of electrostatic interactions between the carboxylate moiety (COO−) of Ox‐ChNCs and the NH3 + of chitosan to prepare flexible and tough chitosan composite microfibers. Ox‐ChNCs were added to enhance the strength of the fiber. These displayed a Young's modulus of 0.996 MPa for the wet fibers and 695 MPa for the dried ones, a higher mechanical strength than alginate fibers, with excellent processability. We also demonstrated that these fibers could effectively encapsulate a biomolecule, such as methylene blue in the core or the sheath in a controllable fashion. Finally, we assessed them in HS‐5 bone marrow‐derived fibroblast cell culture and showed that these new fibers have excellent cell adhesion, while comparable alginate ones were completely inefficient. All resulting materials could be woven into gauzes and could be easily coiled to make tubular architectures. From a biomaterials standpoint, this work addressed a key challenge of existing microfluidic fiber systems, which have been dominated by alginate‐based materials with limited mechanical stiffness. Tissues such as major blood vessels and cortical bone exhibit Young's moduli spanning 105–109 Pa [3]. Chitosan, with a comparatively high modulus (∼107 Pa), provided a mechanically relevant alternative. Thus, preparing chitosan in a crosslinker‐free environment holds significant potential for creating the tissues in vitro. For in vivo implantation, chitosan can provide rigidity and stability to engineered microstissue [48]. This novel microfiber synthetic method opens new avenues for the development of simple microfluidic fibers with improved properties from a readily available biomass waste.

Results and Discussions

Microfluidic Fabrication of Core‐Sheath Chitosan Microfibers

For this method, we used a PDMS microfluidic chip fabricated via a templating method. To ensure homogeneous hydrodynamic pressure exerted by the sheath to induce hydrodynamic focusing, the channel walls should have high shape fidelity [49]. In brief, two stainless steel dispense 20 g needles were positioned perpendicular to a 22 g syringe needle inserted into an 18 g needle to make a right angle (Figure 1a), to yield a flow‐focused junction. This cross was laid on top of a layer of partially cured Sylgard 184 (Dow Silicones) (1:10 v/v cross‐linker to elastomer ratio). Another layer of PDMS was poured to completely cover the needles. The whole construct was degassed in a desiccator attached to a vacuum pump. The device was fully cured for 15 min in a 150°C oven before being trimmed to expose the four needle ends. The middle 18 g needle was carefully removed, leaving a hollow tube around the 22 g needle (Video S1). The 22 g needle was inserted into an 18 g needle because we wanted to create an outlet capable of holding solutions from both the sheath and core together so they could flow side by side in a parallel (laminar) fashion. Additionally, the 22 g needle was inserted directly into the core instead of threading the micromedical tubing into the core inlet—to ensure the core was properly covered by the sheath. A syringe pump controlled the flow of fluid injected into the microdevice through both the core and sheath inlets. The chitosan solution was delivered into the microchannels of the chip via micromedical tubing. Chitosan, being viscous when dissolved in aqueous acidic media (pH 3–4), required distinct concentrations in the core and the sheath channels in order to handle the fiber contents without clogging the micromedical tube and microfluidic chip channels to make homogeneous core sheath structures. Additionally, the concentrations of Ox‐ChNCs were considered, as their addition to the chitosan core solution altered the viscosity overall. To determine which concentration yielded the toughest and easiest‐to‐process core sheath fibers, three different concentrations were examined. Hence, 2 (w/v)% for the sheath and 1 (w/v)% for the core were identified as the most appropriate concentrations for pure chitosan fibers. While 2 (w/v)% chitosan for the sheath and 1% chitosan for the core alongside 1, 1.5, and 2 (w/v)% Ox‐ChNCs were chosen to form chitosan composite fibers. At the outlet of the chip, chitosan solution was flowed into a 1:1 (v/v) ethanol and 1 M sodium hydroxide solution (pH antisolvent system), where it immediately solidified into chitosan microfibers, maintaining the morphology molded by the cylindrical channel of the microfluidic device (Figure 1b). There are two main processes for the solidification of fibers: physical and chemical methods [16]. Physical solidification includes the solvent exchange method, ionic crosslinking, non‐solvent‐induced phase separation, and solvent evaporation [49]. In this work, the coagulation of chitosan was based on the solvent exchange method. When the spinning solution (precursor solvent) came into contact with the coagulation bath (non‐solvent), interfacial solvent exchange enabled fiber solidification [50]. Chitosan was dissolved in an aqueous acetic acid solution, which acted as the solvent system, while the NaOH and ethanol mixture acted as the non‐solvent system. Diffusion proceeded until equilibrium was reached, after which the diffusion process effectively stopped. In our system, NaOH deprotonated the NH3 + groups of chitosan in the spinning solution (pH 3–4), while ethanol acted as a dehydrating agent. The coagulation bath, with a pH of 10–11, neutralized the fibers and rendered them insoluble, thereby inducing fiber precipitation.

Schematic illustration of the microfluidic wet spinning setup. (a) The microfluidic chip. (b) The microfluidic wet spinning setup with coagulation bath (c) A zoom‐in on the chip junction for the production of Chs‐Chs‐MF (d) A zoom‐in on the chip junction for the production of Chs‐ChNC‐MF.

Compared to other solidification methods, the solvent exchange method exhibited a wide material scope and was suitable for a variety of synthetic and natural polymer processing [16]. This solidification is induced by interfacial solvent exchange between the filaments and the bath components [50], triggering physical crosslinking, which consist of noncovalent interactions. This kind of crosslinking relies on the network formation by self‐assembly induced by the change in temperature or pH. In reported MWS systems, chemical crosslinking with divalent cations such as Ca2+ is common, but suffers from the issue that these crosslinkers easily leach under physiological conditions.

This process gave us access to core‐sheath fibers of approximately 200–300 µm in diameter, generated in a continuous fashion, reaching lengths of several tens of centimeters. We optimized the formation of fibers employing different concentrations and flow rates.

We first sought to make pure chitosan core sheath microfibers, which we refer to as Chs‐Chs‐MF (Video S2). After optimization, we found that a chitosan solution of 2 (w/v)% for the sheath and a 1 (w/v)% chitosan solution for the core, afforded good results (Figure 1c). Alternatively, we used a solution of oxidized chitin nanocrystals (Ox‐ChNCs) of varied concentrations as 1, 1.5, and 2 (w/v)%, chosen as a material with high rigidity, to form the core of the fiber with chitosan as sheath and achieve the composite fiber Chs‐ChNC‐MF (Figure 1d; Video S3). Ox‐ChNCs were prepared using a methodology previously reported in our lab [51]. To showcase the continuous and controllable nature of our process, we provide videos as supplementary material.

Infrared and Nuclear Magnetic Resonance Characterization of Spun Microfibers

We used infrared (IR) and solid‐state nuclear magnetic resonance (ssNMR) to characterize Chs‐Chs‐MF and Chs‐ChNC‐MF. The fibers were lyophilized and ground into a powder, which was then used for analysis. As observed from the IR spectrum in (Figure 2a), both Chs‐Chs‐MF and Chs‐ChNC‐MF exhibited IR peaks at 1551 and 1645 cm−1 corresponding to amide II and amide I peaks, respectively. The IR analysis also confirmed the presence of Ox‐ChNCs in Chs‐ChNC‐MF, as evidenced by the presence of a carbonyl stretching peak at 1743cm−1 as Ox‐ChNCs contain carboxylic acids from their synthesis. The ssNMR confirmed the presence of characteristic peaks of chitosan for both Chs‐Chs‐MF and Chs‐ChNC‐MF, for instance, peaks at 105 and 22 ppm corresponding to C1 and CH3 carbons, respectively. Both microfibers also featured a peak around 175 ppm, which was weaker for Chs‐Chs‐MF but stronger in Chs‐ChNC‐MF (Figure 2b). This peak represents the signal of residual amide in chitosan and overlaps with the signal of the carboxylic group in Chs‐ChNC‐MF. The degree of acetylation was calculated by using the integrated areas of C1, CH3, and the acetamide carbonyl peaks (SI Method 1). From the calculations, we observed that the degree of acetylation for Chs‐Chs‐MF was 18 ± 1.55%, and for Chs‐ChNC‐MF, it was 23 ± 0.33%. The higher value of acetylation corresponds to the presence of nanocrystals in the fibers. Therefore, this validates our experimental setup for loading crystals into the core of the fiber.

Microfiber Characterization by Scanning Electron Microscopy

We used scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to analyze the cross‐section and surface morphology of Chs‐Chs‐MF and Chs‐ChNC‐MF. Two drying methods were tried, namely hang‐drying (samples were dried while hanging on either side of a petri dish) and surface drying (the fibers were allowed to sit on the surface of a petri dish). For the second protocol, the fibers displayed flattened, uneven surfaces at the periphery of the fibers (see Figure S1 for details). Thus, the hang‐dry method was chosen for all the experiments thereafter. Overall, the SEM analysis revealed that the hang‐dried fibers displayed a regular and circular cross‐section. Chs‐Chs‐MF and Chs‐ChNC‐MF featured a uniform and smooth outer surface (Figure 3a,d) and a fiber cross‐section varying from 200 to 250 µm. The interesting difference lies in observing the cross‐section of two different microfibers, where we observed that the Chs‐Chs‐MF have a fairly smooth cross‐section without a clear distinction between the core and the sheath (Figure 3b,c) because of their similar contents, chitosan 2(w/v)% and chitosan 1(w/v)% in the core, which makes it hard to distinguish between the two.

Chs‐ChNC‐MF SEM images (Figure 3d–f) showed distinct cross‐sectional characteristics. These fibers were created with a core flow rate of 200 µL/min and a 1.5 (w/v)% Ox‐ChNC concentration. From the outside, the fibers had a smooth surface, suggesting that the oxidized crystals were securely contained within the core. We focused on a particular area of the core that showed the existence of needle‐shaped particles or crystals. Similar patterns were observed in the core of another fiber that we inspected (Figure 3g–i). The surfaces of both types of fibers were smooth, supporting our hypothesis that the core and sheath phases are well‐separated and flow in a parallel fashion in the chip, and maintain their structure in the coagulation bath (Video S3). We also carefully analyzed the sheath portion of the Chs‐ChNC‐MF (see Figure S2) to find that it was similar to the pure chitosan cross‐section, thereby confirming that nanocrystals are not significantly diffusing to the sheath. In addition, we also observed that when the fibers were incubated in the wet state, the core and the sheath were clearly visible as separate domains (Figure S3).

X‐ray Diffraction Analysis of Synthesized Microfibers

SEM revealed the morphology and structural differences of Chs‐Chs‐MF and Chs‐ChNC‐MF. We turned to X‐ray diffraction (XRD) analysis to understand their crystalline properties. For this, fibers were air dried and analyzed as it is or freeze‐dried (FD) and further ground into a powder. (Practical Grade) PG chitosan was used as a reference (Figure 4a), and features two reflections at 2θ = 10.4° and 2θ = 19.8°, attributed to (020) and (110) planes, respectively [52, 53, 54]. The stability of the innate structure of chitosan is attributed to inter‐ and intramolecular hydrogen bonding [55]. The chitosan chains feature an orthorhombic unit cell with the space group P212121 [56]. FD samples both featured the characteristic chitosan peaks, with broader signals indicating amorphization, likely caused by the grinding. We mounted the hang‐dried fibers in a parallel fashion. For both Chs‐Chs‐MF and Chs‐ChNC‐MF, we saw a diminished reflection at the (020) plane and a new arising reflection at 11.8° (Figure 4b). Additionally, we used the deconvolution method built into the Fityk software [57] to determine the fiber's crystallinity. The crystallinity of the fibers placed parallel to one another on the holder was high, reaching 65% and 64% for Chs‐Chs‐MF and Chs‐ChNC‐MF, respectively (Figure 4a), a jump of over 20% as compared to 41% for PG chitosan. This clearly indicates that the microfluidics process induced order in both produced fibers. Interestingly, there was no improvement in crystallinity induced by the introduction of already crystalline Ox‐ChNCs. This suggests that shear forces incurred during the MWS process caused important crystallization of chitosan, on par with that of Ox‐ChNCs itself. For freeze‐dried Chs‐Chs‐MF and Chs‐ChNC‐MF, the crystallinity was much lower, as a result of the grinding of the fibers, and ranged from 47% to 56% (Figure 4b). These values remain higher than the 41% for PG chitosan, although marginally for Chs‐Chs‐MF. This suggests that Ox‐ChNCs were able to withstand mortar and pestle grinding, preserving part of the crystallinity of the system, while Chs‐Chs‐MF were almost entirely amorphized by grinding.

Synthesis of Hollow and Biomolecule‐Loaded Fibers

To demonstrate the versatility of our system, we fabricated hollow chitosan fibers, designated as Chs‐oil‐MF (Figure 6a), using silicone oil as the core (Video S4). This system also allowed us to study the effect of flow rates on the MF inner (core) and outer (core + sheath) diameters. Oil allows a clear visualization of the boundary between the core and sheath, as seen in Figure 6b. We explored the impact of flow rate on the core‐sheath fiber structure, using flow rates of 600, 700, 800, and 900 µL/min for both the phases (core and sheath) while keeping the other one constant. To determine the fiber diameters, we placed these fibers under a polarized optical microscope (Figure 6c) and then performed the measurements. As the flow rate of the sheath increased from 600 to 900 µL/min, keeping the core flow rate constant at 700 µL/min, both the outer and inner diameters of the fiber decrease, from 1738 ± 72 to 1445 ± 48 µm for the former and from 1398 ± 77 to 788 ± 131 µm for the latter (Figure 6d). This effect is likely due to increased shear in both core and sheath, causing a stretching and thinning of the core and the fiber envelop. Also, as the sheath flow rate increases, the sheath thickness increases, as more material is injected in the sheath vs the core. The opposite effect was seen for the core flow rate. An increase in core flow rates from 600 to 900 µL/min, keeping the sheath flow rate constant at 800 µL/min, resulted in an increase in the fiber's outer and inner diameters from 1610 ± 102 to 1701 ± 70 µm and 1256 ± 61 to 1254 ± 146 µm, respectively (Figure 6e). This time, the difference between the adjacent values was minimal. This may be due to the rapid gelation of chitosan in the coagulation bath before the core flow can influence the hollow structure to a considerable degree. This work demonstrates the highly modular nature of this process. By carefully adjusting the flow rates, the key characteristics of the microfibers can be finely tailored to meet the requirements of specific applications.

Thus, after testing the possibility of creating hollow microfibers, we found it would be relevant to show that chitosan microfibers can act as a reservoir of bioactive molecules. To demonstrate this, we loaded methylene blue (MB), as a model molecule, into microfibers, through incorporation into the core (Figure 6f,h). Specifically, the core solution was composed of MB and chitosan, with a sheath of pure chitosan, to form Chs‐MB‐MF. We observed a complete encapsulation of the dye in these fibers, as the release of MB into the coagulation bath during the fiber processing was negligible (Video S5). After synthesis, the fiber was exposed to an acidic solution, causing controlled release of MB into the solution. The MB cumulative release profile over the span of three days was plotted. This system exhibited a robust and steady release profile over 3 days, reaching nearly 60% of the total release within the first 24 h (Figure 6g). As a means to showcase the versatility of this approach, we also demonstrated that MB could be selectively incorporated into the sheath only, with a pure chitosan core, thus forming MB‐Chs‐MF (Figure S5).

Mechanical Characteristics and Processability of Microstructured Chitosan Fibers

Fibrous structures offer the significant advantage of being highly processable, allowing them to be knitted or woven (by hand or with machines like 3D printers) into a variety of 2D or 3D patterns or motifs [62]. We wanted to examine how our pure chitosan and chitosan composite microfibers could be processed in such a fashion. By hand‐wrapping or knitting chitosan fibers, various microarchitectural motifs (such as braids, coiled fibers, knots, or mats/gauze) were created. When the fibers were knotted, braided, interwoven, or tightened, they did not break, demonstrating a good degree of flexibility (SI Videos S6–S9).

One of the most fundamental prerequisite characteristics for generating artificial scaffolds for cell development is their mechanical properties. We thus acquired stress–strain curves for all samples, in their dry state first (Figure 7). Chs‐Chs‐MF demonstrated a Young's modulus of 365 MPa with a tensile strength of 67.3 MPa. When Ox‐ChNCs were introduced, the mechanical properties were altered. The Young's modulus increased from 510 MPa for 1 (w/v)% loading to 695 MPa for 1.5 (w/v)% loading, and decreased to 494 MPa for 2 (w/v)% loading, while the tensile strengthchanged from 54.6 to 82.2 and 50.1 MPa for loadings of 1, 1.5, and 2 (w/v)%, respectively. The 2 (w/v)% loading resulted in a brittle fiber, and lowering the loading led to a higher strain ratio in the fiber. This mechanical strength was far higher than that of alginate fibers, which have Young's moduli ranging between 200 and 400 kPa [63], and synthetic hydrogels with Young's moduli of 2 to 4 MPa that are available in the literature [64, 23]. Because of this, chitosan has an advantage as a scaffolding material for creating both hard and medium‐strength tissues.

Stress–strain curves for dry Chs‐Chs‐MF at a flow rate of 800 µL/min for the sheath and 200 µL/min for the core and dry Chs‐ChNC‐MF with the varying concentration of Ox‐ChNCs from 1(w/v)% to 1.5 (w/v)%, and 2 (w/v)%.

To determine fiber flexibility, we performed three‐point bending measurements described in Figures S6 and S7. Notably, Chs‐Chs‐MF featured an elastic modulus of 1465 ± 68 MPa, and it regained its original horizontal shape within a few minutes after the bending tests, demonstrating their excellent flexibility and shape recovery behavior. The addition of Ox‐ChNCs altered this flexibility with 1 and 2 (w/v)% having a lower elastic modulus, while 1.5 (w/v)% had the most flexibility with an elastic modulus of 1881± 170. This technique is, however, not well adapted to this type of sample, and this result has to be considered preliminary.

We also performed tensile strength measurements for the fibers in their wet state. In the following, we call the fibers Chs‐Chs‐MF(w) and Chs‐ChNC‐MF(w) to ensure a clear differentiation with the dry forms. On average, the strength of the wet fibers was 10 times smaller than that of the dry fibers. We observed that Chs‐Chs‐MF(w) exhibited the highest stretchability, followed by the 1.5 (w/v)% Chs‐ChNC‐MF(w), reaching approximately 80% and 70% strain, respectively.

The Chs‐Chs‐MF(w) demonstrated a Young's modulus of 0.606 ± 0.357 MPa. Upon introducing Ox‐ChNCs into the fiber core, noticeable variations in mechanical properties were observed. The Young's modulus decreased from 0.525 ± 0.227 MPa for 1 (w/v)% loading to 0.281 ± 0.087 MPa for 1.5 (w/v)% loading and increased again to 0.996 ± 0.181 MPa for 2 (w/v)% loading. A continuous increase in the tensile strength from 0.126 ± 0.063 to 0.191 ± 0.083 and 0.416 ± 0.146 MPa for 1, 1.5, and 2 (w/v)% loadings, respectively, was measured. Hence, the 1 (w/v)% Chs‐ChNC‐MF showed the highest brittleness, followed by the 2 (w/v)% Chs‐ChNC‐MF (Figure S8).

Water Absorption and Water Vapor Transmission Rates

Water absorption is a key property of hydrogels. In order to run these tests, we wove our MF into 1×1 cm gauzes and exposed them to two sets of conditions. We immersed them for 8 h in 25 mL of DI water. This experiment was carried out at two different temperatures: 20°C and 37°C, simulating ambient and physiological temperature, respectively. Results are presented in Figure 8a. In the immersion test, Chs‐Chs‐MF swelled more than Chs‐ChNC‐MF at both temperatures, reaching 728% and 688% swelling at 20°C and a remarkable 961% and 723% for 37°C for Chs‐Chs‐MF and Chs‐ChNC‐MF, respectively. Chs‐Chs‐MF always swelled more than Chs‐ChNC‐MF, which was expected because Ox‐ChNCs are crystalline and thus have less flexibility than chitosan strands [65]. Also, these crystals can fill the pores of chitosan gauzes, thereby leaving fewer spaces available for the water uptake. For Chs‐Chs‐MF, an increase in temperature resulted in increased swelling for both immersion and vapor exposure. For Chs‐ChNC‐MF, however, the trends are less evident. In the immersion experiment, higher temperatures resulted in more swelling. It is possible that crystals, which we know from SEM, are migrating to the surface, are interacting more at higher temperature, and limiting water uptake. Next, we examined Water Vapor Transfer Rates (WVTR) to evaluate the breathability of our fibers. The WVTR was carried out by taking 1×1 cm gauzes and wrapping them around the head of a 2‐dram vial filled with 5 mL of water and placing it at rest for 24 h in an air‐tight container (Figure S9), keeping the relative humidity at 50%. Similar to the water uptake experiment, two temperatures (20°C and 37°C) were chosen. The results showed that the rates ranged from 669 to 802 gm−2day−1 for Chs‐Chs‐MF at 20°C and 37°C, respectively. However, a diminished rate was observed for Chs‐ChNC‐MF, varying from 699 gm−2day−1 at 20°C to 634 gm−2day−1 at 37°C (Figure 8b). These results suggest that an increase in breathability can be achieved by augmenting the temperature, making the material compatible with the performance of classic epidermal sensors, which fall within the range of 800–1200 gm−2day−1 [66]. Hence, this enables us to determine compatibility with skin having a WVTR of 200–700 gm−2day−1 [66, 67].

Biocompatibility and Cellular Response

To evaluate the biocompatibility of the chitosan fibers, HS‐5 bone marrow‐derived fibroblast cells were cultured on Chs‐Chs‐MF and Chs‐ChNC‐MF and directly compared to tissue culture plastic (TCP) and alginate fibers. Live/dead (green/red) staining was performed on day 9 to assess cell viability across these substrates, as shown in Figure 9.

Live/dead (green/red) viability analysis. (a) Chs‐Chs‐MF, (b) Chs‐ChNC‐MF (c) Tissue Culture Plastic (TCP), and (d) Alginate fibers. Data presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's post‐hoc test (n = 3; ** p < 0.01).

Fluorescence microscopy images revealed clear differences in cell attachment and survival among the tested materials. Cells seeded on Chs‐Chs‐MF (Figure 9a) exhibited a high density of viable, well‐spread fibroblasts, indicating strong adhesion and biocompatibility. In contrast, Chs‐ChNC‐MF (Figure 9b) supported cell growth but showed a greater proportion of dead cells, suggesting moderate cytocompatibility. TCP (Figure 9c) displayed nearly complete cell confluency with minimal cell death, as expected for a standard cell culture substrate. Notably, alginate fibers (Figure 9d) failed to retain viable cells, reinforcing their limitations in supporting cell‐matrix interactions unless modified for this purpose [24].

Quantitative cell viability assessment confirmed these observations, with Chs‐Chs‐MF fibers supporting approximately 85% viability, which was lower than TCP (>95%) but still substantially higher than Chs‐ChNC‐MF (∼65%). The reduced viability on Chs‐ChNC‐MF fibers may be attributed to differences in surface chemistry or mechanical properties [68, 69]. However, our physicochemical characterization, alongside the viability results presented here, suggests that nanoscale structural variations introduced by chitosan nanocrystals increase local polymer packing and stiffness [70, 71]. While these changes enhance mechanical stability, they can also alter surface roughness and reduce protein adsorption, potentially hindering focal‐adhesion formation and cell spreading, consistent with previous work showing that nanoscale substrate topography can disrupt integrin clustering and, in turn, reduce fibroblast proliferation and viability [72]. In contrast, alginate fibers exhibited no measurable viability, consistent with their poor adhesive properties (as previously discussed). Statistical analysis (p < 0.01) confirmed significant differences between groups, further emphasizing the superior performance of pure chitosan‐based substrates (Chs‐Chs‐MF) over Chs‐ChNC‐MF and alginate fibers.

Further assessment of cell adhesion and morphology at fiber junctions and in parallel fiber arrangements was conducted to investigate how structural organization influences cellular responses (Figure 10). Brightfield imaging highlights the fiber edges and contact regions (Figure 10a), and fluorescence microscopy revealing widespread cell adhesion across these structures. Hoechst‐stained nuclei (Figure 10b) confirm high cell density, while actin staining (Figure 10c) shows well‐aligned cytoskeletal organization on chitosan fibers, consistent with curvature‐ and topology‐guided fibroblast alignment reported for fibrous and patterned hydrogel substrates [73, 74], where local geometry and surface continuity promote cell elongation and stress fiber alignment, and with our previous work demonstrating that aligned or fibrous topographies can guide fibroblast morphology and cytoskeletal organization [75, 76]. Lastly, the merged images (Figure 10d) show continuous staining along adjacent fibers and at junctions, indicating that fibroblasts extend and organize across neighboring fiber surfaces. These observations suggest that our fabricated chitosan fibers can support interconnected cellular networks, which are important for tissue‐like organization.

(b) Hoechst‐stained nuclei (DAPI, blue) confirm high cell density on Chs‐Chs‐MF and Chs‐ChNC‐MF. (c) Actin staining (FITC, green) reveals well‐spread cytoskeletal organization on chitosan fibers, while cells on Chs‐ChNC‐MF exhibit a less pronounced cytoskeletal structure. (d) Merged images of DAPI and FITC channels show continuous cell coverage along fiber surfaces and contact regions between adjacent fibers.

Overall, the results presented here demonstrate the potential of our crosslinker‐free, microfluidic‐spun chitosan fibers as scaffolding materials for cell culture and tissue engineering applications. The fibers exhibit strong biocompatibility and appear to promote cell organization, attachment, and proliferation, unlike alginate‐based fibers, which typically require surface modification to support adhesion. While the incorporation of chitosan nanocrystals (Chs‐ChNC‐MF) improved the fibers’ structural integrity, the reduced cell viability compared to Chs‐Chs‐MF suggests that further optimization may be required to balance mechanical reinforcement with cellular compatibility. Finally, future studies should confirm these organizational behaviors by assessing markers of cell–cell and cell‐matrix interactions to further support the material's positioning as a viable scaffold for tissue engineering applications.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we used microfluidic wet spinning to successfully fabricate chitosan core‐sheath microfibers. We can generate fibers with excellent accuracy, control, and reproducibility using this simple and practical method. We demonstrated that chitosan can be processed without a crosslinker to produce incredibly resilient and strong microfibers in both dry and wet states reaching as high as 695 MPa and 0.996 ± 0.181 MPa, respectively. Additionally, we characterized the different fibers using SEM, which further helped us to visualize clustered nanocrystals. Quantitative characterization indicated the presence of a carbonyl stretching peak at 1743cm−1 and 23 ± 0.33% of acetylation in the Chs‐ChNC‐MF, which strengthened our argument for the loading of crystals in the core of the fiber. The preferred orientation was evaluated using WAXS and XRD, which enabled us to verify the role of shear forces in the crystal alignment. Using core‐sheath wet spinning, we tackled the problem of spinning viscous chitosan and molded it into 3D structures like gauzes and coiled fibers. In addition to molded constructs, we also demonstrated the preparation of hollow microfibers. Wherein, by varying the flow rates of the sheath or core, we could readily modify the diameter of the fibers. The versatility of the system was expanded to load the microfibers with bioactive molecules and showed a sustained release profiles. The cytocompatibility of fiber surfaces was then investigated using these structures. We showed that our microfiber surface was highly suitable and that HS‐5 bone marrow‐derived fibroblast cells could adhere and proliferate without any further processing or coating. Yet a full in vitro proof of concept for drug delivery in the presence of cells would be a valuable experiment as an extension of this work. Hence, these architectures offer a research avenue where these fibers could be utilized for bottom‐up tissue engineering for the purpose of creating highly functional macroscopic tissue assembly. This novel system offers the possibility to produce precise fibers with excellent biocompatibility without any cross‐linking agent.

Experimental Section

Materials

Practical grade (PG) chitosan (high molecular weight), Practical grade (PG) chitin, Ammonium persulfate(APS), acetic acid, sodium hydroxide, and ethanol were purchased from Sigma Aldrich, Oakville, Ontario, Canada. Air‐Tite HSW Soft‐Ject Disposable Syringes were purchased from Fisher Scientific. 18, 20, and 22G hypodermic needles were purchased from Avantor Scientific Products and Services (VWR International), Mississauga, Ontario, Canada. PDMS kit (SYLGARD 184) containing silicone elastomer and a base from Krayden Canada.

Manufacture of Core‐Sheath Microfluidic Spinning System

A poly(dimethylsiloxane) (PDMS)‐based device developed by Gursoy and colleagues was replicated. In brief, two stainless steel dispense 20 g needles were positioned perpendicular to a 22 g syringe needle inserted into an 18 g needle to make a cross. This cross was laid on top of a layer of partially cured Sylgard 184 (Dow Silicones) (1:10 v/v cross‐linker to elastomer ratio). Another layer of PDMS was poured to completely cover the needles, which were then degassed with a desiccator attached to a vacuum pump. The device was fully cured for 15 min in a 150°C oven before being trimmed to expose the four needle ends. The middle 18 g needle was carefully removed, leaving a hollow tube around the 22 g needle. Using syringe pumps (KDS syringe pump, series 100), a core flow of chitosan solutions in buffer was driven through the central needle at a rate of 700 µL/min. A sheath flow of chitosan (pH 3.4) was pumped at 900 µL/min. The fibers were gathered in a glass‐bottomed Petri plate located at the device's terminus.

Fabrication of Flow Focused Chs‐Chs‐MF

Two solutions (1 and 2 (w/v)%) of chitosan were prepared in a 5 (v/v)% aqueous acetic acid solution. Both solutions were stirred at 45°C for a duration of 5 to 6 h. Once the chitosan was fully dissolved, the mixture was centrifuged to eliminate any air bubbles. Subsequently, the 2 (w/v)% chitosan solution was introduced into the sheath inlet, while the 1 (w/v)% chitosan solution was fed into the core inlet. The resultant fibers were collected through the outlet which was immersed in a coagulation bath, composed of a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of 1 M aqueous NaOH and ethanol. This particular solution serves as a pH antisolvent system, facilitating the solidification of the fibers. The fibers were then incubated in distilled water until further use.

Fabrication of Flow‐Focused Chs‐ChNC‐MF

Ox‐ChNCs were produced from chitin following a reported method [51]. A 2 (w/v)% solution of chitosan was prepared in a 5 (v/v)% aqueous acetic acid solution and employed as the sheath solution. A blend of 1 (w/v)% chitosan with 1 (w/v)%, 1.5 (w/v)%, or 2 (w/v)% Ox‐ChNCs were utilized as the core solution. Full solubilization and centrifugation were performed as for chitosan 2 (w/v)% preparation. However, the 1 (w/v)% of chitosan solutions with different concentrations of Ox‐ChNCs was prepared and kept in a desiccator for 4 h to remove air bubbles. Again, the fibers were collected in a 1:1 (v/v) of 1 m NaOH and ethanol coagulation bath and washed with distilled water.

Fabrication of Flow‐Focused Chs‐MB‐MF

10 mg of methylene blue was added to a 1 (w/v)% chitosan solution, which served as the core of the microfiber. A similar 2 (w/v)% chitosan solution was used as the sheath component. The solutions were allowed to pass through the microfluidic chip at an 800 µL/min sheath flow rate and a 600 µL/min core flow rate. These fibers were then collected in a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of 1 M NaOH and ethanol as the coagulation bath. After 5 min of coagulation, we transferred the fibers into a distilled water bath. Then, we began taking readings by cutting and dissolving a 2 cm length of fiber into a vial containing aqueous acetic acid solution (pH 4–5).

ATR‐FTIR Spectroscopy

Attenuated total reflectance ATR‐FTIR spectra were acquired from dried microfibers using a Bruker Alpha II equipped with a monolithic diamond ATR accessory (wavenumber range 500–4000 cm−1, 24 scans, resolution 4 cm−1) at each step to monitor the reactions. Data were gathered and processed using OPUS software (7.5), which averaged the obtained spectra and removed noise signals.

Powder X‐Ray Diffraction

The powder XRD was obtained with a Bruker D8 Advanced powder diffractometer. There were two approaches to obtain the diffractograms. The first one involved placing hang dried fibers, and the second was freezing dry the wet fibers and grinding them with a mortar and pestle. The crystallinity was tested from 2θ running from 5° to 50° on a zero‐background plate using a Bruker D8 Advantage X‐Ray Diffractometer equipped with a Cu‐Kɑ (λ = 1.5418 Ǻ) source, LinxEye detector, and a Ni filter, operating at 40 kV and 40 mA. Note that for crystallinity calculations, we took into account the instrumental broadening βinstrument which was subtracted from the βexperiment during the calculation of full width at half maxima (FWHM, βsample 2 = βexperiment 2 – βinstrument [2]).

Wide Angle X‐Ray Scattering (WAXS)

To obtain the degree of orientation of Ox‐ChNCs instilled in the core with the changing sheath flow rates we did Small‐angle X‐ray scattering: Anton Paar SAXSpoint 2.0. Fibers measuring 2.5 cm in length were cut and put on the sample holder of WAXS. The graphs were processed using SAXSAnalysis software.

Sem

The digital images from the SEM were taken using the FEI Quanta 450 Environmental Scanning Electron Microscope (FE‐ESEM). Fibers measuring 0.5 cm in length were cut and coated with platinum to a thickness of 4 nm for 1 min using Leica Microsystems EM ACE600 High‐Resolution Sputter Coater with Room Temperature Stage. Later, the fibers were planted on the SEM sample holder to get pictures of the fiber diameter and cross‐section.

13C Magic Angle Spinning Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (MAS‐NMR)

NMR spectra were recorded on a Varian VNMRS operating at 400 MHz for the solid‐state 13C acquisition using a 4 mm double‐resonance Varian Chemagnetics T3 probe. A contact time of 1000 µs and a recycle delay of 3 s were used to acquire quantitative spectra. Five hundred scans were acquired of each sample for a total time of 1.5 h.

Water Absorption Tests

1 cm x 1 cm mats were made of Chs‐Chs‐MF and Chs‐ChNC‐MF fibers. The dry weights of each sample were measured utilizing an ultramicrobalance. Immersion tests were done with the addition of in a 50 mL beaker containing 25 mL of DI water and allowed to sit for 8 h, at temperatures of 20°C and 37°C. The sample's surplus water was blotted out the next day, and the ultramicrobalance was used to measure each sample's wet weight. The swelling ratio was measured using Equation (2) (2) Swellingratio%=Weq−W0/W0∗100 where Weq is the equivalent weight of swelled fiber, and W0 is the initial weight.

An 1×1 cm gauze of pure chitosan and chitosan composite fibers was cut and wrapped around the mouth of a 2‐dram vial filled with 5 mL of water. Both the vials were kept in an air‐tight Tupperware. One was at rest on the shelf of the lab at 20°C, and the other being was in the oven at 37°C. The weights of the vials were recorded, and they were stored at two different temperatures: human body temperature (37°C) and room temperature (20°C), and relative humidity of 50%. Tensile Strength Tests

Samples of varied 3 ‐5 mm in lengths were gathered and glued with cyanoacrylate glue (Henkel, LePage superglue) at the opposite ends (proximal and distal) of a window cut out of waterproof paper. Samples were examined using a tensile mechanical tester (CellScale UStretch) with a 4.5 N load cell and 1.5 N load cell, as well as video extensometry with a camera equipped with a 1/2 in. monochrome CCD sensor to get a resolution of 1280 pixels by 960 pixels. Samples were loaded into the tester's grips and stretched at a rate of 2 mm/min, with a data output frequency of 5 Hz. The location of the glue spots and force were assessed concurrently using Ustretch software (ver. 10.76), which is based on an image‐tracking engine with a template‐matching method.

Fiber Preparation for Cell Culture

Fibers were hydrated in Milli‐Q water overnight to allow full swelling of the hydrogel. Once swollen, they were secured at the bottom of a 6‐well plate using a thin layer of petroleum jelly (Vaseline, Unilever) at the bottom of the tissue culture plastic and sterilized under UV light (255 nm) for 1 h. Following sterilization, fibers were transferred to a biosafety cabinet (BSC) under sterile conditions and washed twice with 2 mL of phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, Sigma) containing 1(v/v)% antibiotic‐antimycotic (anti‐anti, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Finally, the fibers were incubated overnight at 37°C in 2 mL of Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10 (w/v)% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cytiva) and 1 (v/v)% anti‐anti.

Cell Culture on Fibers

HS‐5 bone marrow‐derived fibroblast cells were seeded onto the fibers at a density of 100,000 cells per well in 3 mL of DMEM supplemented with 10 (w/v)% FBS and 1 (v/v)% anti‐anti on day 0. The culture medium was changed on days 2 and 6. On day 9, cell viability (live/dead) was assessed using calcein AM (Life Technologies) and ethidium homodimer‐1 (EthD‐1, Life Technologies). Calcein AM (2 µM in culture medium) was used to label live cells, while EthD‐1 (4 µM in culture medium) stained dead cells. Live/dead stains were applied directly to the culture and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. For cytoskeletal and nuclear staining, cells were fixed in 4 (w/v)% paraformaldehyde (Sigma) for 30 min at room temperature, rinsed twice with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.1 (w/v)% Triton X‐100 (Sigma) in PBS for 10 min. After two PBS washes, F‐actin was stained using phalloidin‐FITC (Acti‐stain 488 phalloidin, Cytoskeleton Inc.) diluted to 5 µg/mL in PBS with 1 (w/v)% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma). Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (Sigma) at a final concentration of 2 µg/mL, incubated at 37°C for 30 min, followed by three PBS washes.

Bright‐field and fluorescence images were obtained using an Evos Invitrogen M7000 inverted microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Live/dead cell counts were quantified using FIJI (NIH) image analysis software. A parallel control culture was maintained under identical conditions using alginate fibers and direct plating onto tissue culture plastic.
