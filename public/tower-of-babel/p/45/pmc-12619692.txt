In Vitro Diagnostics for SARS‐CoV‐2 Infection Authorized in the United States and Approved in Japan
Authors: Mayumi Idei, Kiyoshi Asada, Mototsugu Tanaka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn vitro diagnostics (IVDs) for severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2) played a critical role in the early detection and management of infection, yet regional disparities in their availability remain underexamined. We retrospectively analyzed molecular and antigen IVDs for SARS‐CoV‐2 authorized by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and approved by the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA)/Ministry of Health, Labour, and Welfare (MHLW) in Japan between 2020 and 2023, using publicly available data. The FDA granted Emergency Use Authorization (EUA) to 373 IVDs (302 molecular and 71 antigen), while the PMDA/MHLW approved 153 IVDs (55 molecular and 98 antigen) under the Priority Review system within the regular approval framework. In the United States (US), authorizations surged in early 2020, whereas in Japan, approvals increased more gradually until mid‐2021. Real‐time polymerase chain reaction and lateral flow immunoassay were the predominant methods for molecular and antigen tests, respectively, in both countries. In Japan, 71% of prescription‐only IVDs were developed by domestic companies, compared with 79% in the US. In summary, a greater number of IVDs for SARS‐CoV‐2 were authorized in the US than were approved in Japan, particularly early in the pandemic. The EUA system in the US facilitated the rapid authorization of IVDs. Japan's Priority Review system enabled rapid approvals, while the newly introduced Emergency Approval system—although not applied to IVDs for SARS‐CoV‐2—may enable more timely approval and distribution of IVDs in future public health emergencies.

Introduction

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19), caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2), was first reported in December 2019 [1, 2]. It rapidly spread worldwide, leading the World Health Organization to declare a pandemic in March 2020. Although the designation of COVID‐19 as a public health emergency of international concern was lifted in May 2023 [3], it remains an important infectious disease.

Diagnostic testing for COVID‐19 has been critical in detecting infections, facilitating early treatment, and preventing transmission [4, 5]. Several types of in vitro diagnostics (IVDs) for SARS‐CoV‐2 have been developed, including molecular, antigen, and antibody tests, while SARS‐CoV‐2 infection is diagnosed using molecular and antigen tests [4, 6, 7]. In the early phase of the pandemic, many countries struggled with limited testing capacity due to insufficient laboratory infrastructure, shortages of trained personnel, and a shortage of test kits [8, 9, 10]. Although the performance of IVDs for SARS‐CoV‐2 has been widely studied [11, 12], regional differences in their availability remain underexamined. In the United States (US), Emergency Use Authorization (EUA) was applied to expedite the availability of IVDs for SARS‐CoV‐2, whereas Japan applied the Priority Review system.

This study aimed to compare the authorization and approval status for IVDs for SARS‐CoV‐2 and their regulatory frameworks in the US and Japan.

Materials and Methods

We conducted a retrospective analysis of molecular and antigen IVDs for SARS‐CoV‐2 infection granted EUA by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) or approved by the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA)/Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) in Japan between 2020 and 2023. In Japan, antibody tests for SARS‐CoV‐2 are not subject to regulatory approval because they are not used diagnostically; therefore, this study focused on molecular and antigen tests.

We examined the following factors: number of authorizations/approvals, test types, regulatory frameworks, and nationality of the companies that developed the products. Company nationality was classified as the US, Japan, European Union (EU), or other. For subsidiaries, the nationality of the parent company was assigned. For each test, we calculated the time difference between the EUA issuance date in the US and the approval date in Japan.

All data were obtained from publicly available sources, including the FDA, PMDA/MHLW, and corporate websites. Descriptive statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro 18 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Because this study used only publicly available data without patient information, ethical approval and informed consent were not required.

Results

Overview of IVDs for SARS‐CoV‐2 Authorized in the US and Approved in Japan

Between 2020 and 2023, 373 IVDs for SARS‐CoV‐2 were granted EUA in the US, and 153 were approved in Japan. Of these, 85% in the US and 87% in Japan were prescription‐only IVDs (Table 1). In the US, 81% of IVDs were molecular tests, whereas in Japan, 64% were antigen tests. The number of molecular tests authorized in the US was approximately 5.5 times higher than in Japan, while Japan approved more antigen tests. Notably, nearly half of the antigen tests authorized in the US were over‐the‐counter (OTC) products, whereas Japan approved roughly twice as many prescription‐only antigen tests as the US. Real‐time polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) and lateral flow immunoassays were the predominant methods for molecular and antigen tests in both countries (Table 1). In Japan, 70% of prescription‐only IVDs were developed domestically, with most of the remainder originating from the US and EU. In the US, 79% of prescription‐only IVDs were developed by US companies, with none from Japan (Tables 1 and S1).

Trends in IVDs for SARS‐CoV‐2 Authorized in the US and Approved in Japan

The first IVDs for SARS‐CoV‐2 authorized in the US (February 2020) and approved in Japan (March 2020) were molecular tests. Antigen tests followed in both countries in May 2020. IVDs available without a prescription were first authorized in the US nearly 2 years before their approval in Japan (Figure 1).

Authorizations in the US increased rapidly by mid‐2020, whereas approvals in Japan increased more gradually by mid‐2021. The first authorization in the US for each test type was as follows: molecular tests—prescription‐only in February 2020, DTC in May 2020, and OTC in March 2021; antigen tests—prescription‐only in May 2020, and OTC in December 2020. The first approval in Japan for each test type was as follows: molecular tests—prescription‐only in March 2020; antigen tests—prescription‐only in May 2020 and OTC in August 2022. Quarterly trends showed that authorizations in the US increased rapidly by mid‐2020, whereas approvals in Japan increased more gradually by mid‐2021 (Figure 1). In the US, molecular test authorizations peaked in the first half of 2020, with many foreign‐developed products authorized early in the pandemic. In Japan, molecular test approvals remained relatively low and consistent. For antigen tests, both countries reached a peak in the first quarter of 2021. The US authorized numerous foreign‐developed and OTC products, whereas Japan's OTC tests were approved considerably later (Figure 1).

Regulatory Pathways in the US and Japan

In Japan, all prescription‐only IVDs for SARS‐CoV‐2 were approved under the Priority Review system within the regular approval framework, which was formally applied to these IVDs in April 2020. Although the Emergency Approval system was introduced in May 2022, it was not applied to these IVDs. In contrast, the US implemented the EUA system for IVDs for SARS‐CoV‐2 in February 2020.

The median (interquartile range) review time for prescription‐only IVDs in Japan was 69 (35–115) days for molecular tests and 84 (61–138) days for antigen tests. Comparable review times in the US could not be determined because submission dates were not publicly available.

For non‐prescription IVDs, EUA in the US covered both OTC and direct‐to‐consumer (DTC) tests, whereas in Japan only OTC tests were approved because DTC tests are not permitted under the regulatory framework.

A total of 20 molecular and 8 antigen tests (including one OTC) were both authorized in the US and approved in Japan. For these prescription‐only IVDs, the median time from US authorization to Japanese approval was 192 (95–484) days for molecular tests and 138 (81–224) days for antigen tests. The corresponding median review times in Japan were 71 (35–114) days and 69 (55–119) days, respectively.

Discussion

This study showed that the number of IVDs for SARS‐CoV‐2 granted EUA in the US exceeded those approved in Japan, particularly in the early phase of the pandemic. Molecular tests were more common in the US, whereas Japan approved more antigen tests. In both countries, RT‐PCR and lateral flow immunoassay were the predominant methods for molecular and antigen tests, respectively.

Nucleic acid amplification tests, considered the gold standard for SARS‐CoV‐2 detection [4, 6, 7], were prioritized early in the pandemic, followed by the widespread use of rapid antigen tests to expand testing capacity [4, 13, 14]. Consistent with this, molecular tests were authorized or approved earlier than antigen tests in both the US and Japan (Figure 1). The limited development of molecular tests for SARS‐CoV‐2 in Japan may reflect the historically restricted use of molecular diagnostics for infectious diseases, influenced by limited insurance coverage and high costs, as well as the need for device‐specific instrumentation [15]. In contrast, Japan's familiarity with antigen tests for infectious diseases likely facilitated their development. In the US, few domestic prescription‐only antigen tests were authorized; however, many OTC and foreign‐developed products were rapidly authorized. The early, strategic authorization of OTC tests likely improved public access to testing [13]. Conversely, Japan approved its first antigen OTC test considerably later than the US, potentially limiting early access to self‐testing.

Japan has traditionally used the Special Approval for Emergency system to rapidly approve products already approved for sale overseas during emergencies, based on confirmed efficacy and safety [16]. To strengthen responses to infectious disease threats, Japan introduced the Emergency Approval system in May 2022. This system allows expedited approval of products when urgent use is needed and no appropriate alternatives are available, based on a reasonable presumption of efficacy and the absence of significant safety concerns [17]. Regulatory procedures, such as Good Manufacturing Practice inspections, can be simplified, but post‐marketing commitments, including additional studies, may be required. Approval can be revoked after the emergency is resolved. However, this system was not applied to IVDs for SARS‐CoV‐2 because 90 tests had already been approved. Its practical role, particularly for diagnostics, remains to be clarified in future public health emergencies.

All prescription‐only IVDs in Japan were approved under the Priority Review system within the regular approval framework. This system shortens review times for products such as those designated as orphan, SAKIGAKE (a system promoting the early practical application of innovative products first developed in Japan), or otherwise recognized for their clinical usefulness and disease seriousness [18]. In practice, almost all IVDs were rapidly approved under relatively flexible conditions, with post‐marketing commitments for clinical performance and product stability [19, 20]. Although approval criteria were not disclosed, approximately half of the molecular tests were approved based on clinical performance studies involving only 10 positive and 15 negative samples. The ability to adapt regulatory requirements in a public health crisis, even within an existing framework, likely contributed to faster review times than traditionally expected.

In the US, EUA—distinct from full regulatory approval—permits temporary use of medical products during a public health emergency, and is revoked when the EUA declaration ends [21]. EUAs require less evidence than full FDA approval, based on a reasonable presumption of efficacy. Submissions for molecular and antigen tests were required to include performance data from at least 30 positive and 30 negative samples and meet specified performance thresholds [22]. The EUA's rapid implementation and clear requirements may have encouraged early developer engagement and accelerated IVD development.

While regulatory approaches differed, both countries made requirements more flexible and shortened review times to approximately 2–3 months in the US (report) [23] and in Japan (our analysis) during the pandemic, compared with pre‐pandemic benchmarks of 5 months in the US and 7 months in Japan [24, 25]. Japan employed a structured process within its existing regulatory framework rather than an emergency‐oriented system, and the approval requirements were not publicly disclosed. This may have posed barriers for foreign developers unfamiliar with the system, potentially contributing to fewer foreign‐developed IVDs approved in Japan than in the US.

The COVID‐19 pandemic highlighted the importance of not only robust testing infrastructure but also clear and anticipatory regulatory frameworks. For Japan, rapid implementation of the Emergency Approval system with clear requirements and review timelines may help test development and approval in future pandemics. Proactive communication from regulatory agencies may promote early developer engagement and facilitate timely test development.

Limitations of this study include its focus on regulatory approval and authorization status based on publicly available data, without examining actual test utilization, distribution, and testing capacity. Moreover, the effectiveness of Japan's Emergency Approval system could not be assessed because it was not applied for IVDs during the pandemic. Future research should evaluate its use in future public health emergencies, including these factors.

In conclusion, the number of IVDs for SARS‐CoV‐2 granted EUA in the US exceeded those approved in Japan, particularly in the early phase of the pandemic. The EUA system in the US facilitated rapid authorization of IVDs for SARS‐CoV‐2. Japan's Priority Review system enabled rapid approvals during the pandemic, while the newly introduced Emergency Approval system may enhance responsiveness in future pandemics.
