Serious adverse events and mortality among adults with non-surgically treated distal radius fractures- a nationwide population-based cohort study of 184,446 Danish patients
Authors: Laura Kärnä, Merete N Madsen, Antti P Launonen, Tina Senholt, Aleksi Reito, Helle K Østergaard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDistal radius fractures (DRFs) are usually considered low-risk injuries. However, data on serious adverse events (SAE) and mortality in large population-based cohorts of non-surgically treated DRFs are limited. We aimed to examine 30-day risk of SAEs with associated factors and mortality up to one year after non-surgical treatment of DRFs.MethodsThis is a retrospective population-based cohort study using the Danish National Patient Registry. All adults (≥ 18 years) with a first-time, non-surgically treated DRF between 1998 and 2018 were included. SAEs (stroke, myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, respiratory tract infection, acute renal failure) within 30 days and mortality up to 1-year post-fracture were assessed. Association between age, sex, cancer, and major osteoporotic fractures (MoF) and the occurrence of SAEs and mortality were analyzed.ResultsA total of 184,446 non-surgically treated DRFs were identified, with median age of 64 years. SAEs were generally uncommon. Death was the most frequent but occurred in only 0.7% of patients within 30 days. Male sex, concurrent MoF, and cancer were significantly associated with a higher risk of SAEs and death. Patients with concurrent MoF had a 5-fold increased risk of 30-day mortality (OR 5.3, CI 4.6–6.2).ConclusionShort-term SAEs were rare after non-surgical treatment for DRF. The presence of concurrent MoF in nonoperatively treated patients with DRF is associated with an elevated risk of early SAEs and death. These findings suggest the need for increased clinical attention in selected high-risk subgroups.Trial registrationNot applicable, this study is an observational retrospective cohort; no prospective registration was required.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-10037-2.Keywords: Distal radius fracture, Non-surgical treatment, Mortality, Serious adverse events

Background

Distal radius fractures (DRFs) are among the most common injuries in the adult population, particularly affecting older individuals [1–4]. The majority of patients are treated nonoperatively [2, 4]. In older patients, nonoperative management of DRFs has been shown to yield outcomes comparable to surgical intervention, with no clear advantage of surgery over conservative treatment [5–7].

DRFs are usually considered as low-risk fractures. Previous research on DRFs has primarily focused on fracture-related complications and functional recovery. Comprehensive data examining the incidence and factors associated for these severe adverse outcomes in large, population-based cohorts remain limited [8]. Serious adverse events (SAEs) such as stroke, myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, and death are critical outcomes influencing patient prognosis and are mainly studied in other osteoporotic fractures [9–13].

DRFs are categorized as major osteoporotic fractures (MoFs), alongside hip, proximal humerus, and spine fractures, due to their strong association with osteoporosis [14–16]. Concurrent upper extremity fractures with hip fractures have been shown to increase both mortality and hospital length of stay compared to hip fractures only [17]. Additionally, women who sustain a DRF face an elevated risk of subsequent hip fractures (RR 1.43), reflecting a potential cascade toward adverse outcomes commonly associated with hip fracture recovery [18]. Whether the presence of other MoFs contributes to excess mortality in DRF patients, independent of confounding factors, remains unclear [16]. Literature reports of both increased and decreased overall mortality rates in patients with DRF compared to the background population [19–21]. Evidence is limited to report SAEs, including mortality, especially for the non-surgically treated group.

This study aimed to evaluate (1) mortality and associated factors up to one year follow-up and (2) the 30-day risk and associated factors to SAEs other than death in patients with non-surgically treated DRFs.

Methods

This is a retrospective population-based cohort study, using data from the Danish National Patient Registry (DNPR). DNPR contains comprehensive information from all public and private hospitals in Denmark. The registry provides detailed information on diagnoses, surgical procedures, treatments, complications, hospital admissions, as well as visits to outpatient specialist clinics and emergency departments. Most DRFs are diagnosed and managed in public hospitals, and all radiographically confirmed fractures—including those treated in private hospitals—must be recorded in the DNPR [22]. Based on the International Classification of Diseases 10th edition (ICD-10), all adults (≥ 18 years) with DRF (S52.5 and S52.6*) were identified in the DNPR. The participants’ first DRF was included. The patients entered the cohort at their fracture date and were, per default, assigned as non-operatively managed. If any DRF-related surgical procedures were performed within 30 days after the time of fracture (considered initial treatment), the patients were excluded.

After identifying individuals with DRF in the DNPR, their records were linked to the Civil Registration System (CRS) through the unique 10-digit personal identification number assigned to all Danish residents at birth or upon immigration since 1968. This linkage enabled individual-level integration of data across multiple national registries. The CRS provides essential demographic information, including date of birth, age, sex, and vital status [23].

Data were accessed through the Secure Research Platform provided by the Danish Health Data Authority [24]. The study was pre-registered at Central Denmark Region whereas data access and approval were obtained by the Danish Data Protection Agency (reference no. 680317, case no. 1-16-02-100-20). The platform hosts duplicate of most of the Danish Health Data Authority’s registers, including the DNPR and CRS. Data was de-identified to ensure that no data were directly identifiable. Data cleaning, preprocessing, and all statistical analyses were conducted using the Stata software package (version 18, StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Data is presented according to the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data (RECORD) statement [25].

Variables

Information on sex and age at the time of fracture, as well as any cancer recorded during the same episode, was extracted. A One-year look-back period was used to gather information on ischaemic heart disease, diabetes mellitus, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, along with additional comorbidities included in the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). The CCI was calculated using a validated coding algorithm [26].

Each DRF was linked to any concurrent fractures or intracranial injuries recorded during the same episode as the DRF. Concurrent fractures were classified according to anatomical region: head, neck, thoracic, lumbopelvic, other upper arm (excluding DRF), proximal humerus, other forearm (excluding DRF), hand, hip, other femoral, knee or ankle, and foot fractures. For subsequent subgroup analyses, hip fractures, proximal humerus fractures, and spinal fractures were categorized as MoFs. The ICD-10 codes used to identify MoFs in the DNPR are well validated and have demonstrated high reliability for epidemiological research [27]. A complete ICD-10 code list for all included variables is available in supplementary material 1 and 2.

The SAEs included stroke, respiratory tract infection, myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolus, acute renal failure, and death. Each included DRF was linked to the first episode of each SAE in a set of pre-defined SAEs, occurring within 30 days after the index date of fracture. Additionally, death within 90 days and one year after the fracture was recorded.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of DRF patients, divided by sex, were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), or as numbers with percentages (%). SAEs, occurring within 30 days post-fracture, were presented as cumulative incidence with 95% confidence interval (95% CI), assuming a normal approximation to the Poisson distribution for random count data. In addition, the incidence of death within 30 days was stratified by sex, age, and presence of MoFs.

Three multivariable logistic regression models were used to analyse the risk of mortality and the SAEs specified above. Model 1, the primary analysis, included age as a continuous variable, sex, the interaction between age and sex, MoF (as total) and cancer. Model 2 applied the same covariate structure but restricted the sample to patients older than 64 years. Model 3 mirrored Model 2 but used restricted cubic splines for age to account for potential non-linear associations between age and the log-odds of the outcomes.

For all models and across all outcomes, predicted probabilities were visualized using predictive graphs. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported for all outcomes under Models 1 and 2, and for mortality under Model 3. Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with corresponding 95% CIs, reported for the same model–outcome combinations.

One-year survival following fracture was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. All participants were followed from the index date until the earliest of death or the end of follow-up; individuals alive at one year were administratively censored at 365 days. The mortality outcome was defined as all-cause mortality.

Results

Baseline

A total of 184,446 non-surgically treated DRFs were identified in DNPR between 1998 and 2018. A detailed description of the data management process is provided in Fig. 1.

Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. Most patients with non-surgically treated DRFs were female (74%). The overall median age was 64 years, with females being older compared to males (67 vs. 51 years). The prevalence of concurrent cancer was similarly low in both sexes, affecting 0.1% of patients in each group.

Mortality

Death was the most common SAE, with 0.7% of patients dying within the first 30 days. Crude survival rate within one year after non-surgically managed DRF, divided by sex, is presented in Fig. 2. Among patients aged 60 years and older with non-surgically treated DRFs, the 30-day mortality rate was higher in those with concurrent MOFs, reaching 6.8% in females and 8.4% in males, compared to 0.9% and 1.3% in those without MOFs.

Crude survival rate within 1 year, divided by sex

At all ages, males had a higher predicted mortality than females across the 30-, 90-, and 365-day time points, as shown in Fig. 3. The presence of concurrent MoF was associated with an increased risk of 30-day mortality (OR 5.3; 95% CI 4.6 to 6.1), although its effect attenuated over time, declining to an OR of 2.5 (95% CI 2.3 to 2.7) at one year. Concurrent cancer was a consistent predictor of mortality at every time point (Table 3).

Across all time points, Model 1 (all patients) showed higher ORs than Model 2 (patients aged > 64 years). The differences were most pronounced for male gender, while estimates for age were largely similar between models and MoF showed only minor attenuation in Model 2 (Table 3) This pattern is consistent with a stronger effect of male sex on mortality at younger ages. Discrimination was also higher in Model 1 than in Model 2 (e.g., AUC 0.86 vs. 0.77 at 30 days), consistent with the greater heterogeneity in age and mortality risk captured when all adult patients are included.

Predicted mortality graphs by sex and age among patients aged over 64 years is presented in Supplemental Material 3. In Model 3, which modelled age using a restricted cubic spline (3 knots at Harrell’s recommended positions), each additional year of age was associated with higher odds of mortality at all timepoints, while cancer and MoF remained strong independent predictors. Model discrimination was good at 30 days and declined modestly over longer follow-up, and allowing for non-linearity in age did not materially alter the conclusions of the primary analysis (Supplemental material 3).

Serious adverse events other than death within 30 days

Within 30 days of non-surgical treatment for DRF, SAEs were rare (0.03% to 0.72%) (Table 4). Males demonstrated slightly higher predicted risks for all SAEs compared to females (Fig. 4). Increasing age, the presence of concurrent cancer, and concurrent MoF were all associated with an elevated risk of SAEs. Notably, patients with a concurrent MoF, acute renal failure had the highest SAE odds. (Table 5) Across all SAE outcomes, Model 1 and Model 2 showed broadly similar patterns, although estimates for male gender differed between models and were accompanied by wide CIs. Age estimates were consistent between models across all outcomes. For most other variables, Model 2 showed lower or comparable ORs than Model 1. Discussion

In this large, population-based cohort study of more than 180,000 adults with non-surgically treated DRFs, SAEs within 30 days were rare but clinically relevant. The most frequent SAE at 30-days was death, occurring in 0.7% of patients. Across all time points, males consistently demonstrated a higher predicted mortality than females. Similar sex-related patterns have been reported in studies of other MoFs, such as hip and proximal humerus fractures, where men show a higher risk of mortality [11, 13]. Male sex, concurrent MoF, and cancer were the strongest predictors of 30-day mortality in our study. Association with concurrent cancer remained significant throughout the 1-year study period. DRFs appear to be associated with lower mortality compared to other MoFs, despite being classified within the same fragility fracture spectrum [13, 28].

In a recent nationwide Danish study conducted in a broadly comparable population, distal radius fracture was associated with higher mortality than in age- and sex-matched controls, particularly in the short term [29]. Because previous studies have not reported the risk of other SAEs following DRFs; this study provides additional knowledge into the potentially contributing factors. Our findings support previous evidence with other MoFs, that SAEs can be sentinel events for more serious outcomes in certain subgroups, particularly older adults and those with comorbid conditions [15, 30]. In addition to a higher predicted risk of death, males also had higher predicted risks in every SAE. The presence of a concurrent MoF increased the risk of SAEs, suggesting a need to prioritize these patients for closer follow-up and targeted preventive measures. Although the overall short-term mortality was low, its presence within DRF may signal underlying frailty. This is consistent with prior literature demonstrating increased risk of subsequent osteoporotic fractures and elevated mortality among patients with MoF [18, 31, 32].

The ORs for male sex were markedly higher in Model 1 than in Model 2 (e.g., OR 8.8 vs. 2.0 at 30 days), indicating a stronger effect of male sex at younger ages, whereas among older patients competing risks appear to dominate and narrow the sex difference. CIs widened considerably at the extremes of the age distribution and for rare adverse events such as pulmonary embolus and acute renal failure, reflecting small numbers of patients and events and limiting the clinical utility of individual-level predictions in these groups. In Model 3, the spline-based predictions at older ages were higher than those from the linear specification in Model 2, likely because a linear term on the log-odds scale cannot capture a steeper-than-log-linear rise in mortality risk in the very old; restricted cubic splines relax this constraint and allow the curve to bend upward in the tails, providing a more realistic representation of the age–risk relationship.

A key strength of this study lies in the use of the DNPR, which offers comprehensive, nationwide, and longitudinal data with validated diagnostic and procedural coding. The ICD-10 codes used to identify MoFs in the DNPR are well validated and have demonstrated high reliability for epidemiological research. The large cohort size allowed for precise estimation of even uncommon outcomes and facilitated detailed subgroup analyses by age and sex. Furthermore, the population-based design minimized selection bias, by including all patients in Denmark who had a first-time, non-surgically treated DRF over the 21-year period [22].

Due to the observational nature of our study, we refrained from making conclusive statements regarding causality between the occurrence of SAEs and non-surgical treatment. Patients selected for non-surgical treatment may differ from those receiving surgical care in ways that are not captured by our data. As such, the observed association between non-surgical treatment and SAEs should not be interpreted as a direct consequence of the treatment itself. Our findings reflect the risk of SAEs among patients selected for non-surgical management, which may be influenced by factors such as frailty or comorbidities, factors that are likely to contribute to the decision to avoid surgery. Although our target population was patients assigned to nonoperative treatment, cohort entry at the time of fracture meant that patients who might otherwise have undergone surgery but died before it could be performed were not excluded. This may have introduced a selection bias in the estimated 30-day mortality, although the effect is likely minimal because most surgeries are performed within the first week. A limitation of the available data is that only a one-year look-back period could be applied to identify baseline comorbidities, as this was the structure of the data extract available. Omission was an a priori decision based on data validity rather than variable selection on statistical ground. This likely underestimates the prevalence of long-standing chronic conditions such as ischaemic heart disease, diabetes mellitus, and COPD, and because these variables were therefore omitted from the adjusted analyses, residual confounding cannot be excluded.

Additionally, we lacked information on potential confounders, including socioeconomic status, detailed past medical history, and health status beyond one year. These unmeasured variables may have influenced both treatment selection and the likelihood of adverse outcomes. Adjusting for cancer status based only on diagnoses recorded concurrently with the fracture episode may have led to bidirectional misclassification. Patients with prior cancer in remission may have been classified as cancer-free, whereas some patients with active cancer may have been missed because of underreporting during acute fracture admissions. As cancer was a strong predictor of mortality at all time points, the reported odds ratios should be interpreted with caution, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. An additional limitation is that only SAEs leading to hospitalization were captured. Although most of the reported SAEs are associated with hospital admission, conditions like respiratory tract infections may not be. Consequently, the number of SAEs in this study may be underestimated. While our results highlight the clinical outcomes in a non-surgically managed population, they do not allow for causal inference regarding the safety or efficacy of non-surgical treatment.

In cohorts of older adults, DRF may function more as a sentinel marker of underlying frailty rather than as a direct physiological cause of death; because registry data typically lack comprehensive measures of frailty and functional status, residual confounding is likely, and our findings should therefore be interpreted as an association rather than evidence that DRFs increase mortality. Despite the low absolute risk of SAEs in the population with non-surgically treated DRFs, the pronounced increase in risk among certain subgroups suggests that management strategies should extend beyond fracture care alone. Identifying patients with elevated risk profiles could inform clinical decision-making, including consideration for geriatric or multidisciplinary assessment, and closer monitoring in the weeks following injury. Since non-surgical management is the golden standard treatment for DRFs in older adults, our findings provide important reference values for future studies involving this specific population.

Conclusion

Short-term SAEs were rare after non-surgical treatment for DRF. Male sex and the presence of concurrent MoF in nonoperatively treated patients with DRF were associated with an elevated risk of SAEs, including death. These findings suggest the need for increased clinical attention in selected high-risk subgroups of adults with this fracture.
