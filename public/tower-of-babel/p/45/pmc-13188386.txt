Age-specific TSH reference limits and early levothyroxine effects in postmenopausal women with subclinical hypothyroidism: a prospective cohort study in Vietnam
Authors: Han Thi Ngoc Nguyen, Huong Thi Thanh Nguyen, Phan Ngoc Vu Dang, Hoa Thai Nguyen, Chau Thi Kim Doan, An Viet Tran, Nga Bich Vu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveSubclinical hypothyroidism (SCH) in postmenopausal women is challenging to diagnose due to age-related shifts in thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) and a lack of population-specific reference limits. Evidence on levothyroxine responsiveness in this group is also limited. This study aimed to establish age-specific TSH reference limits in postmenopausal women and to evaluate the six-month effects of low-dose levothyroxine on thyroid-related quality of life and thyroid function.MethodsAge-specific TSH reference limits were derived from 754 euthyroid postmenopausal women. A separate cohort of 27 women with subclinical hypothyroidism was prospectively followed for six months during levothyroxine therapy initiated with conservative dosing and adjusted according to TSH response. Thyroid function and ThyPRO scores were assessed at baseline, 3 months, and 6 months.ResultsThe 97·5th percentile upper TSH limits were 3.43 mU/L (ages 50–64) and 3.57 mU/L (≥65 years), both lower than values reported in international cohorts. Levothyroxine therapy produced a clinically meaningful 6.5-point improvement in ThyPRO scores. Median TSH decreased from 7.30 mU/L (IQR 5.56–12.21) at baseline to 2.14 mU/L (IQR 1.43–2.92) at 6 months, with modest increases in free thyroxine. Anthropometric and cardiometabolic parameters did not show meaningful changes over the 6-month follow-up.ConclusionsPostmenopausal women in this population exhibited lower age-specific TSH limits than those reported internationally, supporting a ≥ 4.5 mU/L diagnostic threshold for subclinical hypothyroidism. Low-dose levothyroxine improved thyroid-related quality of life without short-term metabolic effects.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Menopause represents a critical transition in women’s aging, accompanied by cardiometabolic alterations, sarcopenia, and changes in bone turnover, domains in which thyroid function plays an important regulatory role [1]. Emerging evidence suggests a hormonal interplay between thyroid function and menopausal hormone therapy in shaping cardiometabolic risk [2]. Thyroid disorders are common in postmenopausal women, and subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH) is particularly prevalent in this group [3, 4]. Diagnosing SCH, however, remains challenging. Age-related shifts in thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) distributions increase the risk of misclassification near the upper reference limit [5], while symptoms often overlap with nonspecific menopausal complaints.

TSH reference limits vary considerably across population studies, ranging from approximately 4.0 mU/L in Dutch cohorts to >6.0 mU/L in the Whickham Survey and nearly 7.0 mU/L in Korean data [6–8]. Such heterogeneity reflects differences in iodine intake, demographic structure, and assay characteristics, underscoring the need for population-specific reference intervals. Management of SCH is similarly debated: evidence for symptomatic or cardiometabolic benefits of levothyroxine remains inconsistent, guideline thresholds for treatment initiation differ across ATA, ETA, and NICE recommendations, and concerns about overtreatment are heightened in older women with comorbidities [9–11].

In Vietnam, data on SCH in postmenopausal women are scarce, and age-specific TSH reference limits have not been defined. Evidence on levothyroxine responsiveness, including patient-reported outcomes, is also lacking. Therefore, this study aimed to establish age-specific TSH reference limits in postmenopausal women and to evaluate the six-month effects of levothyroxine therapy on thyroid-related quality of life and thyroid function, as well as secondary metabolic outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study comprised two related but independent components conducted at Hanoi Medical University Hospital, a tertiary referral center in northern Vietnam, from March 2024 to July 2025: (1) establishment of population-specific reference limits for serum thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), and (2) a prospective evaluation of the short-term effects of levothyroxine (LT4) therapy in women with subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH).

These two components were based on related but independent cohorts. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval (Approval No. 1481/GCN-HMUIRB). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Population for TSH reference range

A reference cohort of 754 postmenopausal women aged 50–80 years was identified from the hospital’s endocrine screening program. Eligible women had no evidence of thyroid dysfunction, as determined by TSH and FT4 measurements. Women with a current or past history of thyroid disorders, including hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, clinically significant thyroiditis, thyroid cancer, prior thyroid surgery, or radioiodine treatment, were excluded. Those taking medications or supplements known to interfere with thyroid function (e.g., glucocorticoids, thyroid medications, estrogens, androgens, lithium, and biotin) were also excluded. Thyroid antibody status was not uniformly available; therefore, the reference population reflects a pragmatically defined, clinically screened euthyroid cohort.

Menopause was defined as at least 12 consecutive months of amenorrhea, according to the criteria of the European Menopause Society [12].

Population for screening and identification of SCH

A separate cohort of 735 postmenopausal women aged 50–80 years presenting either for evaluation of possible thyroid-related symptoms or for routine annual health examinations was screened to classify thyroid dysfunction and identify candidates for LT4 evaluation.

SCH was defined as persistently elevated TSH above the age-specific upper reference limit on two measurements 3–6 months apart, with FT4 within the normal range [9, 10]. Prospective LT4 treatment evaluation

Treatment criteria

LT4 therapy was offered based on guideline-derived thresholds [1, 9–11]. Treatment was initiated when TSH was ≥ 10 mU/L, or when TSH was 4–10 mU/L accompanied by hypothyroid symptoms, cardiovascular risk factors, positive TPOAb, or a progressive rise in TSH. Women aged ≥ 70 years were assessed more conservatively, but the overall treatment criteria remained the same across age groups.

Levothyroxine initiation protocol

Participants aged < 70 years with a TSH level ≥ 10 mU/L, as well as those aged ≥ 70 years without cardiovascular disease (CVD) and weighing > 50 kg with TSH ≥ 10 mU/L, were started on 50 µg/day. A lower starting dose of 25 µg/day was used for individuals aged ≥ 70 years, those with CVD, or those ≥ 70 years with body weight < 50 kg when TSH was ≥ 10 mU/L, and also for all participants aged < 70 years whose baseline TSH was < 10 mU/L. The lowest starting dose of 12.5 µg/day was reserved for participants aged ≥ 70 years with CVD or with body weight < 50 kg who had TSH < 10 mU/L. In this study, low-dose levothyroxine refers to a conservative initiation and titration strategy for subclinical hypothyroidism, using doses substantially lower than conventional full replacement therapy for overt hypothyroidism. This tiered approach aimed to minimize overtreatment in older adults while providing adequate hormone replacement where indicated.

Dose titration was guided by serum TSH at each follow-up visit. When TSH remained ≥ 10 mU/L, levothyroxine doses were increased in stepwise increments of 25 µg/day, with additional escalation using weekly cumulative dosing when a more pronounced reduction in TSH was required. When TSH remained between 7 and 10 mU/L, the daily dose was increased by a standard increment of 25 µg. For TSH levels between 4 and 6 mU/L, minor upward adjustments were performed using weekly cumulative dosing. TSH values not falling within predefined adjustment ranges were managed using individualized titration guided by clinical context and prior TSH trends. When TSH was within or close to the target range (2.5–4 mU/L), the current dose was maintained. If TSH fell below 0.4–0.5 mU/L, the dose was reduced by 25 µg/day. Further fine-tuning reductions (e.g., removing 1–2 weekly doses) were considered when TSH decreased to <0.2–0.3 mU/L to minimize overtreatment. Levothyroxine was discontinued if TSH dropped below 0.1 mU/L or remained < 0.4 mU/L on two consecutive assessments.

All participants received levothyroxine from a single manufacturer (Merck, Germany) to ensure formulation consistency. Follow-up visits were scheduled at 4–6 weeks for baseline TSH ≥ 10 mU/L and at 12 weeks for baseline TSH < 10 mU/L, followed by evaluations at 3 and 6 months.

Adverse events were systematically monitored throughout the study, assessed for severity and treatment-relatedness.

Laboratory methods

Serum TSH, FT4, thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb), and thyroglobulin antibody (TgAb) were measured using standardized chemiluminescent immunoassays. Reference intervals were 0.35–4.94 mU/L for TSH and 9.01–19.05 pmol/L for FT4. Normal ranges were < 34 IU/mL for TPOAb and <115 IU/mL for TgAb.

Serum lipid levels were measured using enzymatic colorimetric assays. Internal and external quality-control procedures were applied throughout testing.

Quality of life assessment

Thyroid-related quality of life was assessed using the 85-item Thyroid-Related Patient-Reported Outcome (ThyPRO) questionnaire, a validated thyroid-specific instrument originally developed and psychometrically evaluated by Watt et al. (2009) [13, 14]. The full-length ThyPRO yields 13 scale scores conceptually grouped into four domains (physical, mental, well-being, and social function), as well as a single item measuring the overall impact of thyroid disease on quality of life (QoL).

All items are rated on a 0–4 Likert scale and linearly transformed to a 0–100 metric, with higher scores indicating poorer quality of life. Scale scores were calculated when ≥ 50% of items were completed; missing items were not imputed.

For parsimony and to provide a clinically interpretable summary measure, the primary QoL outcome in this study was the ThyPRO composite QoL score, derived from the corresponding items within the administered 85-item ThyPRO, following the approach described by Watt et al. (2015) [15]. This composite score is based on seven well-being and functioning scales (tiredness, cognitive complaints, anxiety, depressivity, emotional susceptibility, impaired daily life, and impaired social life) together with the single overall QoL impact item.

Previous studies have demonstrated acceptable internal consistency for the ThyPRO scales, with Cronbach’s α > 0.77 [16, 17]. The translated Vietnamese version used in this study followed forward–backward translation and cognitive debriefing procedures, and the instrument’s content and structure were preserved without modification; no additional or revised items were introduced.

Statistical analysis

TSH distributions were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q–Q plots. Because values were right-skewed, reference limits were estimated using nonparametric percentile methods in accordance with CLSI EP28–A3, with the upper limit defined as the 97.5th percentile. Participants were stratified into two age groups (50–64 and ≥65 years), consistent with commonly used epidemiologic age categories, to maintain adequate sample size within each stratum and ensure stable nonparametric percentile estimation. Kernel density plots were used to illustrate differences across age groups.

Changes in QoL, TSH, and FT4 from baseline to 3 and 6 months were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Paired bootstrap resampling (B = 5000) was applied to improve robustness; results are reported as median (IQR). Weight, BMI, blood pressure, pulse, and lipid parameters were summarized as mean ± SD and compared using paired t-tests with bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals (B = 5000). Cumulative levothyroxine dose, average daily dose, weight-adjusted dose, and relative dose (%) were summarized descriptively as median (IQR) due to skewed distributions. Adverse events were documented at each visit and summarized descriptively. Because of the minimal number of events, no hypothesis testing or confidence intervals were generated.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and p-values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Population-specific TSH reference limits

Population-based reference limits for serum TSH were established using data from 754 postmenopausal women without thyroid disease. TSH demonstrated a right-skewed, non-Gaussian distribution in both age groups. Quality of life and thyroid function

Quality-of-life scores improved significantly. Median ThyPRO composite QoL scores decreased from 38.5 (IQR 33.5–47.8) at baseline to 35.0 (IQR 30.0–44.0) at 3 months and 32.0 (IQR 28.5–45.0) at 6 months (both p < 0.001 vs. baseline). No significant difference was observed between 3 and 6 months (p = 0.126), indicating that most QoL improvement occurred within the first 3 months and subsequently remained stable (Fig. 3A). Given the construction of the composite score, this improvement primarily reflects changes in domains related to mental, well-being, and social function.

Serum TSH declined progressively over follow-up (Fig. 3B). The median TSH decreased from 7.30 mU/L (IQR 5.56–12.21) at baseline to 5.31 mU/L (IQR 4.09–6.77) at 3 months, and 2.14 mU/L (IQR 1.43–2.92) at 6 months. A significant reduction from baseline was observed at both time points (p < 0.001), as well as from 3 to 6 months (p < 0.001). By 6 months, TSH values had fallen into the low-normal physiological range without evidence of suppression.

FT4 levels increased modestly. No significant change was observed at 3 months (p = 0.180), whereas FT4 increased significantly at 6 months (p = 0.017) (Fig. 3C).

The median cumulative levothyroxine dose over 6 months was 6300 μg (IQR 5380–8350). The median average daily dose was 39.1 μg/day (IQR 29.2–50.0), corresponding to 0.76 μg/kg/day (IQR 0.62–0.92). The relative dose (percentage of full replacement dose, 1.6 μg/kg/day) was 47.6% (IQR 38.5–57.2).

One participant (3.7%) experienced new-onset premature beats during follow-up. No other adverse events or treatment-related complications were reported. Given the low event rate, no inferential analysis was performed.

Discussion

This study provides two principal findings. First, age-specific upper TSH limits in postmenopausal women were lower than those commonly reported in international cohorts. Second, low-dose levothyroxine therapy produced clinically meaningful improvements in thyroid-related quality of life and expected biochemical responses without short-term metabolic effects.

Population-specific TSH reference limits

In this rigorously screened cohort of 754 postmenopausal women without thyroid disease, we identified age-specific upper TSH limits of 3.43 mU/L (50–64 years) and 3.57 mU/L (≥65 years) using the CLSI EP28–A3 nonparametric method. These values are notably lower than those reported in major population-based studies, where percentiles frequently exceed 5–7 mU/L in older adults [7, 18–20]. This variability highlights the absence of a universal physiological upper threshold and reinforces the need for population-specific intervals.

The threshold of ≥4.5 mU/L remains widely used in epidemiologic research and clinical practice as a diagnostic criterion for subclinical hypothyroidism [21, 22]. Because this cut-off lies above the 97.5th percentile in our population, it is unlikely to result in overdiagnosis, and upward adjustment of diagnostic thresholds does not appear necessary in this setting. Differences in TSH distributions may reflect iodine exposure patterns, the strict inclusion of women without clinical or ultrasonographic thyroid abnormalities, assay variability, or genetic and environmental influences [23–25].

Effects of levothyroxine therapy

Levothyroxine therapy resulted in clear biochemical improvement, accompanied by modest but clinically meaningful gains in thyroid-related quality of life. TSH declined to a median of 2.1 mU/L, and FT4 rose slightly but remained within the physiological range. Treatment was well tolerated, and LT4 requirements were relatively low (median 0.76 µg/kg/day), consistent with age-related reductions in hormone requirement among postmenopausal women.

QoL improved rapidly by 3 months and stabilised thereafter, consistent with the expected timeline of symptom resolution following biochemical correction. The 6-month reduction in ThyPRO score exceeded the established minimal meaningful change [26], indicating clinically meaningful benefit. Differences from prior meta-analyses [27] likely reflect methodological factors: the use of generic QoL instruments with limited sensitivity to thyroid-specific symptoms [16, 17] and the inclusion of heterogeneous populations with borderline TSH elevations. In contrast, our cohort consisted largely of symptomatic, antibody-positive women with persistent TSH elevation, a subgroup more likely to respond to LT4.

Titration strategy may also explain divergent findings. Trials such as TRUST employed fixed-dose increments and a wide therapeutic target, resulting in limited biochemical change and no QoL benefit [28]. Our individualized, weekly-cumulative adjustments achieved greater TSH reduction while avoiding overtreatment, potentially enabling a greater symptomatic response.

The observed TSH decline, slightly below but within the prespecified 2.5–4.0 mU/L range, is consistent with the nonlinear TSH-FT4 relationship, in which small FT4 increments yield disproportionately significant TSH reductions. This target was selected to balance efficacy with the risk of overtreatment while remaining consistent with the lower age-specific TSH limits identified in our population.

Anthropometric, hemodynamic, and lipid parameters remained unchanged, consistent with evidence that metabolic responses to levothyroxine in subclinical hypothyroidism are modest and may require more extended observation to detect.

The low LT4 requirement aligns with physiological features of older adults, including reduced lean body mass and increased sensitivity to thyroid hormone [29].

Strengths of this study include the large, rigorously screened reference cohort; the use of standardized, guideline-based nonparametric methods for TSH estimation; the application of a validated thyroid-specific quality-of-life instrument; and the detailed characterization of dose titration. Limitations include the modest size of the treated cohort, which limited statistical power to detect small-to-moderate changes in lipid and hemodynamic measures; single-center recruitment; incomplete antibody data in the reference population; and the absence of long-term cardiovascular or skeletal outcomes requiring extended follow-up.

Conclusions

In this population, age-specific TSH upper reference limits were relatively low. A conservative diagnostic threshold for subclinical hypothyroidism of ≥4.5 mU/L may help avoid overdiagnosis. Low-dose levothyroxine, requiring only modest dosing, was well tolerated and produced clinically meaningful improvements in thyroid-related quality of life, particularly among symptomatic and antibody-positive women, while metabolic parameters remained unchanged; however, small effects cannot be excluded. Further studies are needed to clarify long-term cardiometabolic and skeletal outcomes and to refine treatment indications in older women with subclinical hypothyroidism.
