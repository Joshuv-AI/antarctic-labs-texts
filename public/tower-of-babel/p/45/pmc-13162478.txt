Optimising pulmonary tuberculosis diagnosis in adults: diagnostic usefulness of stool gene xpert alone and in combination with sputum gene xpert at a referral centre in Northeastern Nigeria
Authors: Ibrahim Adamu, Nura Umaru, Adamu Mohammed Sani, Ali Adamu Abdullahi, Jabir Hamza Adamu, Haruna Adamu, Ibrahim Mahmood Maigari, Abdulwahab Aliyu, Yusuf Bara Jibrin, Idris Mohammed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposePulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) continues to be a major public health burden, a situation exacerbated by significant diagnostic gaps. Conventional testing relies heavily on sputum samples, but many patients are unable to produce adequate and qualitative sputum. This limitation leaves a number of cases undiagnosed. Microbiological diagnosis of pulmonary tuberculosis relies mainly on smear microscopy and Xpert MTB/RIF on sputum samples, but obtaining good-quality sputum samples may be difficult. Stool GeneXpert, a molecular diagnostic test, has shown potential as an alternative specimen for diagnosing PTB. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of stool GeneXpert, both as a standalone test and in combination with sputum GeneXpert, for diagnosing PTB in adults in Northeastern Nigeria.MethodsThis retrospective descriptive study was conducted at the Federal Teaching Hospital, Gombe, in Northeastern Nigeria, and included data collected between January 2023 and October 2024. Adult patients suspected of having PTB and tested with sputum and/or stool GeneXpert were included. Data were collected from medical records, including demographic details, HIV status, diagnostic test results, and patient weight. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated for stool GeneXpert and the combination of stool and sputum GeneXpert. McNemar test, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and logistic regression were used to evaluate diagnostic performance and identify factors influencing diagnostic outcomes.ResultsA total of 318 patients were included. The diagnostic yield of stool GeneXpert alone was 72.1%, higher than sputum GeneXpert at 64.3%. The combined stool and sputum GeneXpert testing achieved a diagnostic yield of 83.7%, with 100% sensitivity and 100% NPV. The McNemar test showed no significant difference between stool and sputum GeneXpert results (p = 0.164). However, combining both samples significantly improved detection (p = 0.000). ROC analysis demonstrated a modest discriminatory ability for stool GeneXpert (AUC = 0.621), which improved moderately when combined with sputum GeneXpert (AUC = 0.728).ConclusionStool GeneXpert, particularly when combined with sputum GeneXpert, offers a promising alternative for diagnosing PTB, especially when sputum collection is difficult. The combined testing approach enhances diagnostic yield and may improve early detection and treatment outcomes for PTB in resource-limited settings. Further studies are warranted to confirm these findings and explore the broader applicability of stool GeneXpert.

Introduction

Tuberculosis is one of the major causes of morbidity and mortality globally, and it is among the most common causes of mortality worldwide [1]. The World Health Organization Global Tuberculosis Report 2025 estimated that 10.7 million people developed tuberculosis (TB) worldwide (95% uncertainty interval [UI]: 9.9–11.5 million), with approximately 1.23 million deaths attributed to the disease (95% UI: 1.13–1.33 million). The global TB incidence rate was estimated at 131 cases per 100,000 population per year (95% UI: 122–141), with a case fatality rate of approximately 11.5%. TB remains among the top ten causes of death worldwide and continues to be the leading cause of death from a single infectious agent [2]. More than half of the global tuberculosis burden originates from eight countries, most of which are low- and middle-income nations, including Nigeria [2]. Low- and middle-income countries account for approximately 80% of the global tuberculosis (TB) burden, with Africa contributing about 23%. Nigeria remains among the high-burden countries, ranking sixth globally and first in Africa in terms of TB burden contributing to 4.8% of global tuberculosis burden [2, 3]. World Health Organization reported 161 new and relapse tuberculosis cases per 100, 000 people in Nigeria [4]. Timely diagnosis and treatment of TB are essential to improve TB-related morbidity and mortality. Despite its significance, tuberculosis remains underdiagnosed. For instance, the National Tuberculosis, Leprosy, and Buruli Ulcer Control Program, Nigeria reported 207,785 tuberculosis cases, with over 50% of the estimated cases remaining undetected [5].

The diagnosis of pulmonary tuberculosis relies on the analysis of sputum samples; however, its accuracy is highly dependent on the quality and quantity of the sputum provided [6]. Sputum-based diagnosis of Pulmonary Tuberculosis, the current standard, faces significant challenges, especially in children, people living with HIV (PLHIV), and other patients who are unable to produce adequate and qualitative sputum samples [7–9]. Alternatively, stool may be a valuable alternative for the diagnosis of pulmonary tuberculosis, as sputum containing Mycobacterium tuberculosis may be coughed up, swallowed, and pass through the gastrointestinal system to be excreted in the stool [10]. Stool samples have been found to be useful in the diagnosis of tuberculosis, and this is critically important in a population where sputum collection is challenging [11]. Due to the low sensitivity of sputum microscopy and the longer turnaround time of sputum culture, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends the use of a molecular diagnostic test (GeneXpert) for diagnosing tuberculosis. GeneXpert MTB/RIF, a molecular diagnostic tool, has revolutionized TB diagnosis by providing rapid, sensitive, and specific detection of Mycobacterium tuberculosis and rifampicin resistance [9]. Previous studies on the diagnostic utility of stool GeneXpert were mostly in the paediatric population, which revealed sensitivity ranging from 60 to 83% [12–14]. Notably, a recent systematic review by Sultana et al. on the use of stool specimens for adult pulmonary TB diagnosis concluded that there is currently insufficient evidence to determine its diagnostic accuracy, citing a paucity of studies and substantial heterogeneity among existing ones [15]. This study, therefore aimed to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of stool GeneXpert in adult patients with presumptive pulmonary tuberculosis in northeastern Nigeria and to assess the novel strategy of combining sputum and stool samples to improve overall case detection and diagnostic yield.

Methodology

Study design

This retrospective descriptive study was conducted at the Federal Teaching Hospital, Gombe, Northeastern Nigeria. Data were obtained from health records at the tuberculosis clinic, covering the period from January 2023 to October 2024.

Study population

This study included adult patients (≥ 18 years) who were clinically suspected of having pulmonary tuberculosis and underwent GeneXpert diagnostic testing on sputum and/or stool samples between January 2023 and October 2024 at the Federal Teaching Hospital, Gombe. Participants were identified from the hospital’s tuberculosis (TB) registry to ensure accurate and comprehensive data collection. Inclusion criteria were adults with clinical suspicion of pulmonary TB who had at least one GeneXpert test result, whether from sputum or stool samples. In this setting, some adult patients are unable to produce adequate sputum samples; in such cases, stool specimens are occasionally collected and tested using GeneXpert as an alternative diagnostic specimen. Although not recommended by WHO for routine diagnosis in adults, this approach is sometimes used locally to improve case detection in patients unable to expectorate sputum. Patients were excluded if they had incomplete medical records or if no GeneXpert test result was available.

Data collection

Medical records were reviewed to extract relevant information, including age, sex, HIV status, type of sample tested (sputum, stool, or both), GeneXpert MTB/RIF results (MTB detected or not detected), and patient weight. A pre-designed data extraction form was used to ensure consistency, and all information was entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet stored securely on an encrypted computer.

Both sputum and stool samples were obtained on the same day, and all samples were collected prior to the initiation of anti-tuberculosis treatment. Laboratory protocols and conditions remained unchanged throughout the study period. All specimens were processed using the Xpert MTB/RIF assay in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions and the hospital’s standard operating procedures. While stool testing in adults is not a WHO-recommended standard, the hospital followed a uniform internal protocol to ensure consistency and comparability. For the purposes of this study, sputum GeneXpert served as the reference (gold) standard, and stool GeneXpert was evaluated as the index test.

Sample size

The sample size included all eligible presumptive pulmonary tuberculosis patients with at least sputum or stool GeneXpert testing during the study period.

Statistical analysis

Data was analysed using SPSS software version 22. Descriptive Statistics were used to summarise the baseline characteristics of the participants. For categorical variables (e.g., gender, and diagnostic method), frequencies and percentages were used. For numerical variables (age and weight), the mean, standard deviation, median, and interquartile range were used depending on the distribution of the variables. For diagnostic performance analysis, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated for stool GeneXpert and the combination of sputum and stool GeneXpert. Kappa (κ) statistic was used to evaluate the agreement between stool GeneXpert and sputum GeneXpert. The diagnostic accuracy of stool GeneXpert was compared to sputum GeneXpert using McNemar’s test for paired nominal data. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the diagnostic performance of each test, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to assess the overall diagnostic ability. Logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with better diagnostic outcomes, focusing on identifying patient characteristics that predict benefit from the stool GeneXpert alone and the combined diagnostic approach.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of Federal Teach- ing Hospital Gombe. As this is a retrospective study, no informed consent was required from patients; however, all patient data was anonymized to protect confidentiality. There were no direct risks or interventions for the study participants as the research involved analyzing existing records.

During the study period, a total of 447 suspected tuberculosis (TB) cases were identified. Of these, 69 were excluded because they were suspected to have extra-pulmonary TB, 11 had no GeneXpert results, and 49 were under 18 years of age. Comparison of diagnostic yields of sputum, stool, and combined GeneXpert testing for pulmonary tuberculosis

Of the 318 eligible participants, 53.1% (169/318) were confirmed positive for pulmonary tuberculosis, while the remaining 46.9% (149/318) tested negative. A subset of 129 (40.6%) participants underwent concurrent sputum and stool GeneXpert testing and were included in the comparative analysis.

The diagnostic yield of each test alone and in combination within this subset is detailed in Table 2. The sputum GeneXpert test identified 83 PTB cases, yielding a detection rate of 64.3% (83/129). The stool GeneXpert test identified 93 cases, yielding a higher detection rate of 72.1% (93/129). A significant number of cases (n = 67) were positive by both tests.

The combination of both sputum and stool tests—where a positive result from either test was considered diagnostic for PTB—significantly increased the overall detection. This combined approach identified 108 PTB cases, increasing the composite diagnostic yield to 83.7% (108/129) Fig. 2.

Diagnostic performance measures of the combination of sputum and stool GeneXpert compared to sputum GeneXpert alone

The McNemar test showed no statistically significant difference in detection rates between stool and sputum GeneXpert results (p = 0.164). However, combining stool and sputum samples significantly improved detection rates for pulmonary tuberculosis compared to sputum samples alone (p = 0.000). The performance of stool GeneXpert compared to sputum GeneXpert in detecting Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) was assessed using a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. The area under the curve (AUC) for stool GeneXpert was 0.621 (95% CI: 0.517–0.725), indicating that stool GeneXpert has a modest ability to distinguish between MTB-positive and MTB-negative cases when compared to sputum GeneXpert. The ROC curve for the combination of stool and sputum GeneXpert compared to sputum GeneXpert alone yielded a higher AUC of 0.728 (95% CI: 0.627–0.829). This indicates a moderate improvement in discriminatory ability when both stool and sputum samples are used together, compared to sputum alone. The agreement between stool GeneXpert alone and sputum GeneXpert reference standard was fair (κ = 0.254). However, when using a combination of stool and sputum GeneXpert as the index test, agreement with the reference standard improved to moderate (κ = 0.519). This suggests that a diagnostic strategy incorporating both specimen types significantly enhances case detection compared to stool testing alone.

Discussion

Delayed detection of Mycobacterium tuberculosis due to poor-quality samples can adversely influence treatment outcomes. While previous studies have mainly evaluated stool-based diagnostics for paediatric pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB), a systematic review of adult patients could not conclude on the diagnostic accuracy of stool tests for PTB due to a lack of sufficient data [15]. This study was designed to determine the utility of stool GeneXpert as an alternative or adjunct to sputum-based testing in adults. Our findings provide crucial evidence from a high-burden Nigerian setting on the potential of stool GeneXpert as an alternative or adjunct to sputum-based testing in adults.

In the present study, stool GeneXpert demonstrated a diagnostic yield of 72.1% and a sensitivity of 80.7%. This sensitivity falls within the upper range (69.7% to 100%) of PCR-based assays, including Xpert, reported in the Sultana et al. review [16], reinforcing that stool is a sensitive specimen for molecular testing in adults. However, we observed a lower specificity (43.5%) compared to the high specificities (69.8% to 100%) noted in the systematic review. This discrepancy is critically important and likely attributable to our use of the sputum GeneXpert as the reference standard, whereas many studies in the review used the more definitive sputum culture. This premise is supported by the findings of Mazidur Rahman et al. [17], who reported a markedly higher sensitivity of 94.8% (95% CI: 88.5–97.8) when stool GeneXpert was benchmarked against sputum culture, underscoring the significant impact of the reference standard on performance estimates.

The modest discriminatory power of the standalone stool test was further evidenced by a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) area under the curve (AUC) of 0.621. Crucially, the agreement between stool and sputum GeneXpert was fair (κ = 0.254), indicating that stool testing alone is an inconsistent substitute for the sputum-based reference standard. These results are comparable to those reported by Ngadaya et al. [18].

Given the high sensitivity but suboptimal specificity and agreement of stool GeneXpert observed here, we investigated whether combining stool and sputum GeneXpert testing could enhance overall diagnostic utility. This combined approach yielded a synergistic effect, with the detection rate improving significantly to 83.7% (p < 0.001). The combination achieved 100% sensitivity and NPV, enabling the identification of all true-positive PTB cases in our cohort. This transformative improvement was corroborated by a substantial increase in inter-test agreement (κ = 0.519) and a higher ROC AUC of 0.728, reflecting a moderate and robust discriminatory ability. This is particularly valuable in high-burden settings and among patients who cannot produce adequate sputum. However, the specificity remained low at 45.7%, reflecting a persistent risk of false positives. While combining tests enhances case detection, caution is warranted to avoid overdiagnosis. The PPV of 76.9% supports the utility of this combined approach for confirming PTB in high-prevalence settings, while the 100% NPV underscores its reliability in ruling out the disease when both tests are negative.

Our findings align with and contextualise previous research. A systematic review by Segala et al. [19] reported a pooled sensitivity of 68% (95% CI: 61%-75%) and specificity of 99% (95% CI: 98%-99%) for stool GeneXpert, primarily in paediatric studies. Our observed sensitivity of 80.7% is higher than this pooled estimate, which may reflect population differences or our specific reference standard. The higher specificity in the review likely stems from the use of more composite and stringent reference standards. Similarly, Oladipo et al. [20] reported superior accuracy using Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra on stool samples (sensitivity: 96%; specificity: 83%). The key distinction is their use of the more advanced Ultra cartridge, which is designed to detect lower bacterial loads [13], suggesting that technological advancements could further improve stool-based diagnosis. Furthermore, Moussa et al. [21] reported a comparable sensitivity of 80.56% in children but a much higher specificity of 99.36%, a discrepancy likely attributable to their use of culture on Lowenstein-Jensen medium as a reference standard and the different study population.

In conclusion, these findings collectively emphasise the evolving role of stool-based diagnostics for PTB. While stool GeneXpert demonstrates limited agreement with sputum testing as a standalone assay, its integration with sputum GeneXpert yields a synergistic diagnostic strategy with perfect sensitivity and significantly improved overall agreement. This positions stool testing not as a replacement for sputum, but as a valuable adjunct that substantially enhances case detection and reliably excludes disease when both tests are negative. Further studies incorporating advanced diagnostic tools like Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra and standardised culture-based reference standards are warranted to refine the utility and specificity of stool-based diagnostics in diverse adult populations.

Implications for research and practice

This study highlights Stool GeneXpert as a feasible and acceptable diagnostic tool for PTB in adults. While its standalone use shows limitations, combining stool with sputum GeneXpert testing significantly improves case detection and ensures reliable exclusion of disease when both tests are negative. This combined strategy has the potential to reduce diagnostic delays, enable earlier initiation of treatment, and curb community transmission, especially in high-burden, resource-constrained settings.

For practice, these findings support the consideration of stool GeneXpert as an adjunct in national tuberculosis diagnostic algorithms, particularly for patients unable to produce adequate sputum. For research, further studies are needed to validate this approach using advanced platforms such as Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra and culture-based reference standards, as well as to explore cost-effectiveness, scalability, and long-term impact on TB control.

Strengths and limitations

This study presents several strengths. The inclusion of a large sample size (318 presumptive PTB cases) and the use of validated diagnostic methods (GeneXpert) provide robust and reliable data. The study also compares stool GeneXpert and a combination of stool and sputum GeneXpert with sputum GeneXpert alone, filling a gap in the existing literature by directly assessing the contribution of stool samples to the diagnostic yield of PTB in adults. However, some limitations should be noted. First, the number of paired samples was restricted (129 out of 318 eligible cases), which may have affected the robustness of comparisons. Second, the diagnostic evaluation was limited to sputum and stool GeneXpert tests; culture, which is considered the gold standard, and chest X-rays were not available for analysis, as culture was not routinely performed at our centre during the study period. This may have limited a more comprehensive assessment of PTB prevalence and detection. Finally, the absence of significant associations with demographic and clinical factors warrants further investigation to explore patient-specific influences on diagnostic outcomes.

Future research prospects

Future studies should be multi-centre with larger sample sizes to validate stool GeneXpert performance across diverse settings. Evaluating its accuracy against culture and radiological assessments, testing advanced tools like Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra, and assessing cost-effectiveness will provide stronger evidence. In addition, incorporating key clinical indicators—such as previous TB history, cough and its duration, fever, weight loss, sputum production status, ARV use among HIV-positive participants, and chest X-ray findings—would enable a more comprehensive evaluation of diagnostic outcomes.

Conclusion

This study indicates that stool GeneXpert, particularly as an adjunct to sputum testing, could be a useful diagnostic tool for pulmonary TB (PTB) in scenarios where sputum collection is challenging. The combined testing approach demonstrated an encouraging increase in diagnostic yield in our cohort, which suggests a potential to improve early case detection in resource-limited settings. However, the generalisability of our findings is limited by the single-center design. Therefore, we recommend that future research should focus on multi-center studies to validate the performance of stool GeneXpert.
