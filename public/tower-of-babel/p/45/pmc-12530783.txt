Giant cervicothoracic extradural meningioma involving the brachial plexus: A case report
Authors: Carlo Mandelli, Cinzia Mura, Pietro Mortini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Spinal meningiomas account for 25% of spinal tumors, with the majority being intradural neoplasms. Extradural spinal meningiomas, however, represent only 2–3.5% of cases. These tumors tend to be more aggressive, often invading neural structures, particularly the brachial plexus. This results in faster progression, higher recurrence rates, and challenges in achieving complete resection.Case Description: A 52-year-old male presented with right-hand muscle hypotrophy and motor deficits. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a large extradural mass from C7 to D3, compressing neural structures. Surgical resection was performed, and histopathology confirmed a World Health Organization Grade I meningothelial meningioma. Postoperative follow-up showed significant neurological improvement, and control MRI confirmed optimal decompression of the spinal cord and neural structures.Conclusion: Extradural spinal meningiomas are rare and challenging to treat due to their invasive nature. Surgical resection with decompression, combined with adjuvant therapies like radiotherapy, is crucial for managing residual disease and improving long-term outcomes. A multidisciplinary approach is essential to optimize patient recovery and quality of life.

INTRODUCTION

Spinal meningiomas account for approximately 25% of all spinal tumors and are most commonly intradural extramedullary lesions, originating from the meningothelial cells of the arachnoid membrane.[1, 12] They are classified as intradural and extradural lesions. Intradural meningiomas represent the majority of spinal meningiomas, while extradural ones are rare, accounting for only 2–3.5% of cases.[3,4,6,8,11,12] The thoracic spine is the most affected region (approximately 80%), followed by the cervical (16%) and lumbar (4%) segments.[4,12] Extradural meningiomas can grow along the dura mater, as in the “en-plaque” subtype, and extend through the intervertebral foramen into adjacent soft tissues. In the cervicothoracic region, they may involve the brachial plexus and form a dumbbell-shaped mass resembling a schwannoma.[10,12] Compared to their intradural counterparts, extradural meningiomas tend to exhibit more aggressive behavior, with faster clinical progression and a higher rate of postoperative recurrence. Due to their infiltrative nature, they often require careful differentiation from malignant neoplasms.[10,12]

CASE PRESENTATION

Clinical findings

We present the case of a 52-year-old man with a 4-year history of non-specific tremors affecting the left side of his body and paresthesias in the right C8 dermatome.

Neurological examination revealed hypotrophy of the first dorsal interosseous muscle and other intrinsic hand muscles on the right side. A motor deficit was noted in finger abduction of the right hand, graded 4/5 on the Medical Research Council Scale. The patient, a professional barber, reported difficulty performing his job.

His medical history includes a thymic biopsy performed at a young age, which resulted in a diagnosis of large B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma, treated with chemotherapy and radiotherapy (36 Gy in 20 fractions).

Diagnosis assessment

A chest computed tomography (CT) scan revealed the presence of a solid expansive lesion with epidural intracanal extension from C7 to D3, involving the vertebral canal and affecting the upper thoracic region and the area of the brachial plexus.

Subsequent cervicothoracic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed the presence of an epidural mass at the C7–D3 level, measuring 5.4 × 4.4 × 7 cm. The mass was isointense to the spinal cord on T1-weighted images and slightly hyperintense on T2-weighted images, with heterogeneous contrast enhancement and extrapleural extension into the right apical lung region. The lesion extended into the foraminal space at the D1 level, causing leftward displacement of the dural sac and anterior displacement of the C8 and D1 nerve roots [Figure 1].

Following a multidisciplinary evaluation by the thoracic surgeons, an fludeoxyglucose-positron emission tomography (PET) scan was performed, revealing an area of solid tissue with radiotracer uptake in the right extrapleural paravertebral apex.

Electromyography of all four limbs revealed a marked chronic axonal radiculopathy at the right C8–T1 level, with signs of active denervation in the first dorsal interosseous and extensor indicis proprius muscles.

Then, the patient underwent a subsequent neurosurgical evaluation. Considering the clinical and radiological findings, surgical intervention was indicated.

Therapeutic intervention

The patient subsequently underwent surgical treatment involving laminectomy at D1, D2, and D3, excision of the epidural lesion from C7 to D3, and placement of transpedicular screws on the left side at D1, D2, and D3.

In the operating room, the patient was placed in the prone position with the head secured using a Mayfield headrest. Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring was conducted with neurophysiologists present, and CT-guided neuronavigation was employed. A targeted skin incision was made at the upper cervicothoracic level. Skeletonization was performed bilaterally from C7 to D4. Using the surgical microscope and the drill, an extended laminectomy of D1, D2, and D3 was performed, more extensively on the right side [Figure 2].

Anatomical illustration. The intracanal extradural lesion extends from C7 to D3, with extrapleural extension into the right apical lung region and infiltration of the brachial plexus. To expose the lesion, a laminectomy was performed at D1, D2, and D3, with a wider opening on the right side.

A violaceous-colored neoplastic mass was visualized, spreading along the anterolateral dural surface on the right and extending laterally into the neural foramen [Figure 3]. The right transverse process of D2, the facet joint, and the pedicle were removed. The lesion was also observed on the right extraforaminal side. The tumor, which was highly vascular, was debulked and detached from the dura mater, decompressing the neural structures, specifically, the spinal cord and the right C8, D1, and D2 nerve roots, which were enveloped by the mass [Figures 4 and 5]. Lateral tumor resection continued as far as safely possible, followed by meticulous hemostasis. Using the neuronavigation system, transpedicular screws were placed on the left side at D1, D2, and D3. Final neurophysiological monitoring results were optimal. A postoperative CT scan performed in the operating room confirmed satisfactory decompression of the spinal cord and nerve roots, with a residual lateral component tightly adherent to and inseparable from the nerve root.

During hospitalization in our department, the patient showed progressive improvement in grip strength of the right hand as well as enhanced sensory perception.

Histopathological diagnosis

On histopathological examination, two fragments of gray-reddish tissue and multiple fragments of gray-brownish tissue were observed. Microscopically, a solid-growing meningothelial mesenchymal neoplasm was identified, infiltrating the dura mater and bone tissue. The neoplastic cells exhibited moderate cytological atypia, with an average of 1 mitosis per 10 high-power fields and no evidence of necrosis. The histological diagnosis was consistent with a meningothelial meningioma infiltrating the dura mater and bone tissue (the World Health Organization [WHO] Grade 1).

Follow-up

As part of the early postoperative follow-up, an MRI of the cervicothoracic spine was performed, confirming successful surgical removal of the intracanal lesion and optimal spinal cord decompression. As expected, a residual portion of the lesion was identified in the right extracanalar and extraforaminal region at the D1–D2 level, anteriorly. No signal alterations were observed in the spinal cord [Figure 6]. The patient was then discharged from our department with a recommendation to begin physical therapy to help restore function in the right hand. A follow-up MRI of the brachial plexus was also advised. Further treatment will be planned based on the results of the histological examination.

On follow-up MRI of the brachial plexus performed 2 months later, there was a clear improvement in the visualization of the perimedullary cerebrospinal fluid spaces and regression of the compressive effects on the corresponding spinal cord segment. A thin residual intracanal tissue component persisted, with anterior and right lateral distribution, without causing any spinal cord compression, along with residual tissue at the paravertebral extrapleural right apical pulmonary region.

Adjuvant treatment

Following the histopathological examination, which confirmed the diagnosis of a WHO grade I meningothelial meningioma infiltrating the dura, and the postoperative MRI, a radiotherapy evaluation was conducted. Given the residual disease, the indication for adjuvant treatment was established. Considering the patient’s prior history of mediastinal radiotherapy, the patient was evaluated by radiation oncologists in Trento and subsequently underwent proton therapy. A dose of 50.4 GyRBE in 28 fractions (1.8 Gy per fraction) was prescribed, and the treatment was carried out without complications. Target definition was performed through co-registration of non-contrast CT images, MRI with and without contrast, and PET.

DISCUSSION

Spinal meningiomas are most commonly intradural extramedullary tumors, typically characterized by a benign nature and slow progression. Extradural spinal meningiomas, conversely, are rare, and represent a greater diagnostic challenge.[3,4,6,8,11,12] For this reason, our case report adds meaningful value to the current literature.

Compared to intradural forms, extradural meningiomas tend to grow more rapidly and often exhibit an infiltrative pattern, frequently involving the intervertebral foramina, and potentially invading the nerve plexus. Due to their more aggressive behavior, these lesions are frequently associated with neurological deficits, as observed in our patient.

The preoperative imaging diagnosis of extradural spinal meningiomas remains challenging, particularly in differentiating them from other spinal lesions. In our case, MRI revealed a large extradural cervicothoracic lesion extending from C7 to D3, involving the intervertebral foramina, adjacent soft tissues, and the brachial plexus. The lesion appeared isointense to the spinal cord on T1-weighted images and slightly hyperintense on T2-weighted images, with heterogenous contrast enhancement after gadolinium administration [Figure 1].

As reported in the literature, calcifications are radiographically visible in only 1–4.6% of extradural meningiomas and are more commonly seen in the en-plaque subtype. One MRI feature that can assist in the differential diagnosis is the “Dural tail” sign on post-contrast sequences. This finding can be especially useful in distinguishing extradural meningiomas from other conditions that should be considered in the differential diagnosis, such as metastatic tumors, sarcomas, nerve sheath tumors (including schwannomas and plexiform neurofibromas), and lymphomas.[10] Specifically, schwannomas commonly develop along nerve structures. They have a soft, elastic consistency and may contain cystic or necrotic areas. Meningiomas otherwise are lesion firmly attached to the dura mater and typically exhibit a rounded, lobulated appearance. They have a more solid and compact consistency. On MRI, schwannomas usually appear as well-circumscribed lesions that are slightly hyperintense compared to meningiomas on T2-weighted sequences, and show heterogeneous contrast enhancement.[5,7,13]

As reported in the literature, diagnostic biopsy is not recommended in patients with lesions that are radiologically suspicious for schwannomas. Biopsy often causes fibrosis and adhesions, making future surgical removal much more difficult. It carries a significant risk of neurological injury, potentially resulting in pain, as well as motor and sensory deficits. Moreover, the analysis of a small or inadequate tissue sample may lead to incorrect histopathological interpretation and a false diagnosis of benignity, thereby compromising subsequent therapeutic planning.[2,9]

Given the difficulty in making a definitive preoperative diagnosis, histopathological examination remains crucial, as it provides diagnostic confirmation and directs subsequent treatment. Although most extradural meningiomas are classified as WHO Grade I tumors, they tend to have a higher recurrence rate than their intradural counterparts, due to their more aggressive and invasive biological behavior.[12]

From a surgical perspective, extradural meningiomas present significant challenges due to their often infiltrative growth pattern and close association with neural and vascular structures.

From a surgical point of view, meningiomas are often more tightly adherent to neural structures than schwannomas, making them difficult to dissect. Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring plays an important role throughout the procedure.

In our case, following a multidisciplinary evaluation, the patient underwent a laminectomy from D1 to D3 [Figure 2], with resection of the epidural lesion and placement of transpedicular screws on the left side for stabilization. Intraoperatively, a highly vascular, extradural tumor was encountered, demonstrating adherence to surrounding tissues and partial encasement of neural elements, especially in the more lateral part where the cleavage plane was lost, which limited the extent of resection [Figures 3 and 4]. A subtotal resection was therefore performed, with successful decompression of the spinal cord and nerve roots [Figure 5]. The first step is to proximally identify the root, following it in the medio-lateral direction to preserve it.

Neurophysiological monitoring was essential throughout the procedure, ensuring the preservation of neural function and allowing real-time assessment of surgical impact. Postoperative MRI confirmed complete removal of the intracanal portion of the lesion and effective decompression of the neural structures [Figure 6]. Clinically, the patient showed progressive improvement in the right-hand grip strength and sensory function in the early postoperative phase.

As widely reported in the literature, extradural meningiomas are associated with a less favorable prognosis compared to their intradural counterparts.[4,12] This is largely due to the difficulty in achieving gross total resection, given their aggressive growth, tendency to infiltrate adjacent tissues, and frequent involvement of critical neurovascular structures. These factors often necessitate adjuvant therapies, to reduce the risk of recurrence.

Due to the poor cleavage plane between the lesion and the surrounding neural structures, which must be meticulously preserved, it is often preferable to leave a small residual portion of the tumor adherent to these critical areas. This residual tumor can subsequently be managed with adjuvant radiotherapy. Long-term survival in these patients has been shown to improve when postoperative radiotherapy is combined with surgical resection. Furthermore, tumor control and overall survival may be enhanced with the administration of higher radiation doses.[11] In our patient’s case, due to a history of prior mediastinal radiation, the radiotherapist recommended proton therapy. The patient had previously undergone mediastinal radiation therapy at a young age (36 Gy in 20 fractions). The patient was therefore treated with proton therapy, with a prescribed dose of 50.4 GyRBE in 28 fractions (1.8 Gy per fraction). The treatment was carried out without complications.

CONCLUSION

Extradural spinal meningiomas, though rare, present considerable diagnostic and therapeutic challenges due to their invasive nature and tendency to affect critical neural structures. In this case, surgical resection allowed for the decompression of the affected structures, leading to an improvement in neurological function. Adjuvant therapies, such as radiotherapy, are crucial for managing residual disease and reducing the risk of recurrence. Effective multidisciplinary management, including careful surgical planning and the appropriate use of adjuvant treatments, can significantly improve long-term patient outcomes and quality of life.
