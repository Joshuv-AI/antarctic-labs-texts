Tetrahydropalmatine has analgesic role in mouse model of bone cancer pain by inactivating the TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway in dorsal root ganglia
Authors: Guangda Zheng, Linghan Meng, Lu Shang, Juanxia Ren, Dongtao Li, Yanju Bao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTreatment of bone cancer pain (BCP) remains a challenge. The current paper was to research the analgesic effect of Tetrahydropalmatine (THP) on BCP and the related mechanisms.MethodsMouse model of BCP was constructed by injecting E0771 breast cancer cells into the tibia. Behavioral test was performed to research the effect of THP on pain nociception of BCP mice. Tibia and dorsal root ganglia (DRG) damage was evaluated by HE and Nissl staining. The construction of BCP cell models was conducted by co-culture DRG neurons with E0771 breast cancer cells. The effect of THP on the viability and apoptosis of BCP cell models was monitored by CCK-8 and Tunel assays. TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway activity in DRG was detected by qRT-PCR and Western blot. Pomalidomide (PMA), exogenous TNF-α protein and Capsaicin were utilized to treat BCP mouse model and cell models to explore whether THP exerted analgesic effect via inactivating the TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway.ResultsTHP attenuated pain nociception, relieved tibia destruction, and mitigated inflammation, neuronal death and neuronal excitotoxicity in DRG of BCP mice. It enhanced the viability, but suppressed the apoptosis of BCP cell models. The activated TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway in BCP mouse model and cell models was abrogated by THP treatment. PMA and THP had additive effect, which combination attenuated pain nociception, tibia and DRG damage of BCP mice, and apoptosis of BCP cell models. The pain relief and the TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway inactivation induced by THP in BCP mice was abolished by exogenous TNF-α protein or Capsaicin.ConclusionTHP exerted the analgesic role in BCP might be through inactivating the TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway in DRG. It may be an effective drug for relieving BCP in patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-03972-y.Keywords: THP, BCP, Tibia destruction, DRG damage, TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1

Introduction

Bone cancer pain (BCP) is a refractory pain with a high prevalence in patients with advanced cancer [1]. Occasional breakthrough pain and spontaneously persisting pain are the major clinical manifestations of BCP [2]. BCP is the major source of pain in advanced cancer patients; besides, it is the most important cause of serious decline in life quality and self-confidence in cancer survivors [3]. Based on the current state of medicine, complete relief of BCP is still impossible; the clinical treatment goal of BCP remains to reduce the pain degree of patients as much as possible so that the life quality of patients can reach an acceptable state [4]. Increasingly potent analgesics have been used for the relief of BCP, such as non-opioids, opioids and bisphosphonates; however, approximately one-third of BCP patients are ineffective in pain therapy and suffer from serious adverse side effects [2, 5]. Hence, more effective drugs need to be discovered to alleviate BCP.

The pathogenesis of BCP is a complex process involving inflammation, bone destruction and neuropathic factors. Dorsal root ganglia (DRG) neurons are first-order neurons in the somatosensory conduction pathway, which are capable of transducing and integrating stimuli and signals from the periphery to the central nervous system [6]. DRG neurons are the basis for the transmission and propagation of pain signals, and the excitability of DRG neurons are related to chronic nociception, inflammatory pain and neuropathic pain [7, 8]. Recently, the abnormal or dysfunction DRG neurons have been researched to be associated with the induction and maintenance of BCP [9]. The loss of DRG neurons may be a contributing factor to persistent hyperalgesia, including BCP [2]. Therefore, DRG neurons are considered to be a novel target for the relief of BCP.

Tetrahydropalmatine (THP) is the main active ingredient of traditional Chinese medicinal herb yanhusuo, possessing prominent anti-inflammatory and analgesic properties [10]. It has shown favorable analgesic effect in alleviating chronic pain [11]. Moreover, THP has been demonstrated to have well neuroprotective and anti-tumor effects [12]. It is well known that some common chemotherapeutic drugs, such as oxaliplatin, can cause neuropathic pain in cancer patients; however, this neuropathic pain induced by chemotherapeutic drugs usually responds poorly to common analgesics; interestingly, THP has been discovered to be effective in relieving neuropathic pain induced by oxaliplatin in mice [13]. A recent study have demonstrated the effectiveness of THP in relieving BCP, as it relieved mechanical and thermal nociception in BCP rats; the analgesic effect of THP in BCP rats might be related to its inhibition on the activation of microglia and the expression of pro-inflammatory factor tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) [14]. However, to date, more exhaustive studies on the mitigating effects of THP on BCP have not yet emerged.

Recently, THP has been researched to alleviate oxaliplatin-induced DRG neuronal toxicity without compromising the anti-tumor efficacy of oxaliplatin [15]. Another report has indicated that THP was was able to alter the excitability of rat DRG neurons in response to acid stimulation, which in turn alleviated acidosis-induced pain in vivo [16]. According to these above previous findings, we speculated that THP might exert analgesic effect in BCP by acting on DRG neurons. Thus, in this paper, we established in vivo and in vitro BCP models, and investigated the analgesic effect of THP on BCP and the underlying mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Establishment of BCP mouse model and treatment

Female Balb/c mice [17] (n = 63, 8 weeks old, weight 20–25 g) were commercially supplied by Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, China), and housed in a room free of specific pathogens at 22 °C. The day and Night cycle was 12-h. All mice had free access to diet and water throughout the experiment. Animal study had been approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of MDKN Biotech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Animal welfare was in accordance with the “Guiding Opinions on the Good Treatment of Laboratory Animals” issued by the Ministry of Science and Technology of the People’s Republic of China.

The construction of BCP mouse model was done according to the following operations: mice were deeply anesthetized by inhalation of 2% isoflurane; when mice did not respond to head and Limb stimuli, the hair on the right hind Limb was shaved and 75% ethanol was utilized to disinfect the skin. Then a longitudinal incision was made in the skin in order to expose the tibia; a total of 10 µL E0771 breast cancer cells (4 × 106 cells/mL) (Yubo Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) were slowly injected into the intramedullary space of the right tibia by using a syringe [18, 19]. The syringe was slowly withdrawn after 1 min of injection, and then the injection site was sealed with bone wax rapidly. The skin incision was closed and disinfected by 75% ethanol. The whole surgery was finished on an insulating blanket at 37 °C. After surgery, mice were returned to their cages after they had awakened.

The 63 mice were randomly divided into seven groups with nine mice per group. The treatment of mice in each group was as follows: mice of the Sham group were without any treatment; those of the BCP group were only subjected to the construction of BCP model; those of the BCP + THP 20 mg/kg group and the BCP + THP 40 mg/kg group were firstly subjected to the construction of BCP model, and then administered intragastrically with THP at doses of 20 mg/kg and 40 mg/kg daily (dissolved in 2 mL normal saline), respectively; for mice of the BCP + THP + PMA group, they firstly underwent the construction of BCP model, and then administered intragastrically with THP at a dose of 40 mg/kg and pomalidomide (PMA) at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg daily; in terms of the BCP + THP + TNF-α group, mice were administered intragastrically with THP (40 mg/kg) and injected intraperitoneally with exogenous TNF-α protein (5 mg/kg) (MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) daily after the construction of BCP; mice of the BCP + THP + Capsaicin group were administered intragastrically with THP (40 mg/kg) and injected intraperitoneally with Capsaicin (TRPV1 agonist) (6.5 mg/kg) (MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) daily after BCP modeling. Mice were treated under the relevant conditions for 21 consecutive days. THP was purchased from Biolab Technology (Beijing, China), and PMA was supplied by Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China).

On postoperative day 21, the right hind limbs of all mice were subjected to micro-computed tomography (micro-CT). Then all mice were deeply anesthetized by inhalation of 5% isoflurane. The right hind limb and DRG (L4-L6) of each mouse were collected.

Behavioral tests

The behavioral tests of mice were performed before surgery, on postoperative day 3, 7, 14 and 21. Mechanical nociception of mice was assessed by testing paw mechanical withdrawal threshold. To acclimatize, mice were individually placed in plastic boxes with a wire mesh floor for 30 min. After that, monofilament was placed perpendicular to the mid-surface of the right plantar surface of each mouse, and sufficient force was applied to bend the monofilament slightly for 6–8 s. The force applied to the monofilament was from 0 g to 20 g until the right paw was retracted. Von Frey hairs were utilized to stimulate the right paws of mice. A positive reaction was defined as a sharp retraction of the right paws or a retreat of the right paws when the hair was pulled out. If the right paw had no response, a higher-force hair was applied. Each trial was performed three times and the interval between trials was 5 min.

Thermal nociception of mice was evaluated by detecting paw withdrawal thermal latency via a hot plate analgesiometer. Before testing, mice were acclimatized to the environment for 30 min. During the hot plate test, mice were placed in a hot plate (at 52 ± 0.5°) for 30 s. Mice were immediately removed from the hot plate if they exhibited escape behavior. Each trial was performed three times with a 10-min interval between trials.

The evaluation of movement-evoked pain was implemented by assessing the Limb use score of the right hind Limb. Briefly, mice were placed on a smooth plastic table and allowed to move freely. The size of the plastic table was 50 cm in length and 50 cm in width. The scoring criteria of the Limb use score was as follows: 0 point, the right hind Limb could not be used completely; 1 point, the right hind Limb could be used partial; 2 points, the right hind Limb was clearly Limping; 3 points, the right hind Limb was slightly Limping; 4 points, the right hind limb could be used normally.

micro-CT

The right hind Limbs of mice underwent micro-CT on postoperative day 21 via using a micro-CT scanner (Skyscan 1172, Bruker microCT, Kontich, Belgium). The scan parameters were as follows: current, 80 µA; voltage, 80 kVp; exposure time, 3000 ms; rotation angle of increment, 0.4°; total rotation angle, 360°. The three-dimension reconstruction was finished by the CTAn software (Skyscan, Kontich, Belgium).

Hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining and Nissl staining

Tibia and DRG of each mouse was immobilized in 4% polyformaldehyde (Biolab Technology, Beijing, China). After dehydrated by gradient ethanol and xylene, the tibia was paraffin-embedded and cut into sections with a thickness of 4 μm. The sections were baked for 2 h at 60℃, followed by being dewaxed by xylene and rehydrated by gradient ethanol. Hematoxylin solution (Solarbio Technology, Beijing, China) staining was performed on the sections for 5 min, and then the sections were immersed into eosin solution (Solarbio Technology, Beijing, China) for 1 min staining. Afterwards, the sections were washed by tap water, dehydrated by gradient ethanol, treated by xylene, and disclosed in neutral resin. The pathological changes of the tibia and DRG were observed under an inverted microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

For Nissl staining of DRG, the DRG sections were stained by Nissl solution (Solarbio Technology, Beijing, China) for 10 min after being rehydrated. Then the DRG sections were washed, dehydrated, disclosed, and observed under an inverted microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The number of survival neurons (neurons with positive Nissl staining) was counted.

Immunofluorescence staining

The rehydrated DRG sections were immersed into 0.25% Triton X-100 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 20 min, and then blocked in 5% normal goat serum (Solarbio Technology, Beijing, China) for 1 h at room temperature. For the detection of c-Fos protein, the DRG sections were firstly incubated with rabbit anti-c-Fos primary antibody (1:100, ab209794, Abcam, Shanghai, China) overnight at 4 °C, and then with Alexa Fluor 594-labeled goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:200, SY0673, Biolab Technology, Beijing, China) for 1 h at room temperature. To detect the expression of TNF-α protein, the DRG sections were incubated with rabbit anti-TNF-α primary antibody (1:100, ABP52624, AmyJet Scientific, Wuhan, China) at first, and then Alexa Fluor 488-labeled goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:200, SY0675, Biolab Technology, Beijing, China). Thereafter, 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Solarbio Technology, Beijing, China) was recruited for the nuclear staining. After dried and sealed in neutral resin, the fluorescence intensity of c-Fos and TNF-α were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and then qualified by the Image J software (Image J, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Immunohistochemistry

The rehydrated DRG sections were immersed into 3% H2O2 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 10 min, and then boiled in citric acid buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 3 min. After being blocked with 5% normal goat serum, the DRG sections were probed with rabbit anti-uPA (1:100, ABP55603, AmyJet Scientific, Wuhan, China) and rabbit anti-TNF-α (1:100, ABP52624, AmyJet Scientific, Wuhan, China) overnight at 4 °C. In the subsequent, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:200, A21020, AmyJet Scientific, Wuhan, China) was utilized to react with the DRG sections for 1 h at room temperature. The DRG sections were stained with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) solution (Biolab Technology, Beijing, China) for 5 min, and then with Hematoxylin solution for 1 min. The DRG sections were routinely dried and disclosed before being observed under an inverted microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Isolation and identification of primary mouse DRG neurons

This work isolated primary mouse DRG neurons from new born Balb/c mice (n = 10, Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology, Beijing, China). Briefly, the skin and tissue on the back of new born mice were incised to expose the spine and both sides of the spine. The spinal canal was opened and the DRG (L4-L6) was carefully peeled off. The DRG was clipped and transferred to a centrifuge tube. Equal volumes of 0.25% trypsin (Solarbio Technology, Beijing, China) and 0.1% type IV collagenase (Solarbio Technology, Beijing, China) were added into the centrifuge tube for 30 min incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO2. In the subsequent, Neurobasal/B27 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China) was added into the centrifuge tube. The centrifuge tube was centrifuged at 100 × g for 5 min in order to harvest the cell sediment. The cell sediment was resuspended into Neurobasal/B27 medium and then inoculated into sterile culture flasks for 8 h of culture at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After 8 h of culture, DRG neurons had grown adherently. Then the medium in the culture flasks was replaced by fresh Neurobasal/B27 medium. DRG neurons were continued to be cultured for 5 days. On the 1, 3 and 5 days of culture, DRG neurons were observed under an inverted microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

After 5 days of culture, DRG neurons were identified by immunofluorescence staining of neuron-specific markers, including NeuN and MAP2. In short, after being immobilized in 4% for 15 min and blocked in 5% normal goat serum for 30 min, DRG neurons were probed overnight by mouse anti-NeuN (1:200, ab104224, Abcam, Shanghai, China) and rabbit anti-MMP2 (1:200, ab235167, Abcam, Shanghai, China) primary antibodies at 4 °C. Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse (1:200, SY0683, Biolab Technology, Beijing, China) and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (1:200, SY0673, Biolab Technology, Beijing, China) secondary antibodies were employed to treat DRG neurons for 2 h at room temperature. After nuclear staining by DAPI, DRG neurons were sealed in neutral resin and placed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) for observation.

Determination of safe doses of THP on mouse DRG neurons

Mouse DRG neurons were plated into 96-well plates with 5 × 103 cells per well, and culture for 24 and 48 h in Neurobasal/B27 medium containing different concentrations of THP (1, 5, 10, 25, 50, 75 and 100 µg/mL, respectively) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. At each time point, cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay was employed to detect the viability of DRG neurons. Mouse DRG neurons cultured in normal conditions were used as control.

Construction of BCP cell models and treatment of THP and PMA

The construction of BCP cell models were performed by co-culturing DRG neurons with E0771 breast cancer cells in a non-contact form. In detail, a total of 4 × 106 DRG neurons were inoculated into the lower chambers of the transwell chambers (5 μm pore size) (Corning, Wiesbaden, Germany), while 2 × 104 E0771 breast cancer cells were seeded into the upper chambers. Neurobasal/B27 medium were filled the upper and lower chambers. The transwell chambers were kept at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 48 h. For THP treatment, Neurobasal/B27 medium containing different concentrations of THP (1, 5, 10 and 25 µg/mL, respectively) was filled the upper and lower chambers. For THP and PMA treatment, Neurobasal/B27 medium containing THP (25 µg/mL) and PMA (10 µg/mL) was filled the upper and lower chambers. Mouse DRG neurons cultured in normal conditions were used as control.

Treatment of DRG neurons by exogenous TNF-α protein and TRPV1 agonist capsaicin

Mouse DRG neurons were plated into the 6-well plates (1 × 106 cells/well), and treated by 1 mL Neurobasal/B27 medium containing THP (25 µg/mL) and exogenous TNF-α protein (10 ng/mL) (MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Additionally, Capsaicin (TRPV1 agonist) (50 µM) (MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) combined with THP (25 µg/mL) were adopted to treat mouse DRG neurons. After 48 h of treatment, mouse DRG neurons were gathered for Western blot analysis.

CCK-8 assay

DRG neurons cultured under the relevant conditions for 24 and 48 h were treated by 10 µL CCK-8 solution (Solarbio Technology, Beijing, China) for 2 h at 37 °C. Then the optical density (OD) values of wells were monitored by a microplate reader (Biotek, Winooski, VT, USA). Five repetitive wells were set up for each group.

Tunel assay

The apoptosis of DRG neurons were detected by Tunel assay. Briefly, DRG neurons were cultured for 48 h under the relevant conditions. Then Tunel staining was implemented by using a One Step TUNEL Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) in strict according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DAPI was used for the nuclear staining. The apoptosis of DRG neurons were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and the apoptosis rate was calculated.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

On postoperative day 21, the blood samples of mice were harvested through abdominal aorta, followed by being centrifuged for 10 min at 300 × g. The serum was collected. DRG (L4-L6) tissues of mice were gathered and homogenized in the lysate (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Additionally, DRG neurons cultured under the relevant conditions for 48 h were harvested and lysed in cell lysate for 30 min. The lysate mixtures of DRG tissues and DRG neurons were centrifuged for 15 min at 12,000 × g and 4 °C, and the supernatant was harvested. The levels of uPA and TNF-α in the serum samples and the supernatant samples were tested by applying a TNF-α assay kit (Xinyu Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and an uPA assay kit (Zeye Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Besides, the levels of neuronal excitotoxicity factors, including glutamate (Glu), aspartate (Asp) and 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT), in the supernatant samples of DRG tissues were assessed by Glu assay kit, Asp assay kit, and 5-HT assay kit (Jianglai Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). The testing procedure was in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Real-time quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

This work extracted total RNA from DRG of BCP mice and DRG neurons (cultured for 48 h under the relevant conditions) via using a Total RNA Extraction Kit (Solarbio Technology, Beijing, China). Using a Reverse transcription kit (Lianmai Biological Engineering, Shanghai, China), the total RNA sample was reverse transcribed to generate complementary DNA (cDNA). Thereafter, Applied Biosystems 7900 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) combined with SYBR Premix Ex Taq (TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan) was employed for the amplification of cDNA. The expression of uPA, TNF-α, PAR2 and TRPV1 was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH).

Western blot

DRG of mice were added into radio-immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Solarbio Technology, Beijing, China) and homogenized for 30 min on ice. Besides, DRG neurons cultured for 48 h under the relevant conditions were collected and lysed in cell lysate for 30 min. The mixture was centrifuged for 15 min at 12,000 × g and 4 °C. The supernatant was harvested and the concentration of total proteins in the supernatant was tested by a BCA assay kit (Boster Biological Engineering, Wuhan, China). Each total protein sample with a weight of 30 µg was collected, followed by being separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Afterwards, the proteins were transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Boster Biological Engineering, Wuhan, China) for 1 h blockage in 5% skimmed milk. The proteins were then subjected to the incubation by primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, including rabbit anti-TNF-α (1:1000, ABP52624, AmyJet Scientific, Wuhan, China), rabbit anti-urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA) (1:1000, ABP55603, AmyJet Scientific, Wuhan, China), rabbit anti-protease-activated receptor-2 (PAR2) (1:1000, ab180953, Abcam, Shanghai, China), rabbit anti-transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) (1:1000, ab305299, Abcam, Shanghai, China), and rabbit anti-GAPDH (1:1000, ab9485, Abcam, Shanghai, China). The PVDF membranes were washed three times with Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 (Boster Biological Engineering, Wuhan, China). The proteins were reacted with HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:2000, A21020, AmyJet Scientific, Wuhan, China) for 2 h at room temperature. The protein blots were visualized by treating the PVDF membranes with enhanced chemiluminescent reagent (Boster Biological Engineering, Wuhan, China). Image J software (Image J, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) was utilized for the qualification of protein blots. GAPDH was utilized as the internal reference.

Statistical analysis

To rule out experimental chance, all experiments in this study were repeated independently three times. Data were displayed as mean ± standard deviation, and the analysis of data was implemented by GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data comparison for more than two groups was carried out by one-way analysis of variance following Tukey’s post-hoc test. P < 0.05 was regarded as the threshold for differences to be statistically significant.

Results

THP treatment alleviated pain behavior in BCP mice

To evaluate whether THP treatment could relieve pain-related behaviors, this study investigated the response of mice to mechanical and thermal stimuli. Further, movement-evoked pain was assessed by monitoring the use of the right hind Limb. From postoperative days 7–21, mice of the BCP group exhibited lower paw withdrawal threshold, paw withdrawal latency and limb use score than the Sham group (P < 0.01); at the same time, THP treatment effectively elevated paw withdrawal threshold, paw withdrawal latency and limb use score in BCP mice (BCP + THP 20 mg/kg group vs. BCP group; BCP + THP 40 mg/kg group vs. BCP group) (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01) (Fig. 1A and C).

THP treatment relieved pain behavior in BCP mice (A) THP treatment relieved mechanical allodynia in BCP mice. (B) THP treatment induced thermal hyperalgesia in BCP mice. (C) THP treatment promoted the use of the right hind limb (i.e., the limb of BCP model) in BCP mice. (D-F) Intra-group comparisons of paw withdrawal threshold, paw withdrawal latency and limb use score in mice of each group. * P < 0.05. ** P < 0.01

Intra-group comparisons were also analyzed. As time increased, the paw withdrawal threshold, paw withdrawal latency and limb use score of mice in the Sham group were changed not obvious. However, as the time lengthens, mice of the BCP showed a decrease in paw withdrawal threshold (day 3 vs. day 0; day 7 vs. day 3; day 14 vs. day 7), paw withdrawal latency (day 3 vs. day 0; day 7 vs. day 3; day 14 vs. day 7), and limb use score (day 3 vs. day 0; day 7 vs. day 3) (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01). By 20 mg/kg THP treatment (BCP + THP 20 mg/kg group), the reduced paw withdrawal threshold (day 3 vs. day 0; day 7 vs. day 3), paw withdrawal latency (day 3 vs. day 0; day 7 vs. day 3; day 14 vs. day 7; day 21 vs. day 14), and limb use score (day 3 vs. day 0) occurred in mice with BCP (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01). Similarly, BCP mice that treated by 40 mg/kg THP treatment (BCP + THP 40 mg/kg group) had the declined reduced paw withdrawal threshold (day 3 vs. day 0; day 7 vs. day 3; day 14 vs. day 7) and paw withdrawal latency (day 7 vs. day 3; day 14 vs. day 7) with time increased, but the limb use score was not obviously changed (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01) (Fig. 1D and F). These behavioral findings suggested that THP treatment could mitigate pain behavior in BCP.

THP treatment mitigated inflammation and bone destruction in BCP mice

On postoperative day 21, we used micro-CT combined with three-dimension reconstruction technique to monitor the bone destruction of mice. As shown in Fig. 2A, obvious destruction of the cortical and trabecular bone around the tibia was observed in mice of the BCP group, as relative to the Sham group; interestingly, THP treatment could alleviate these destruction phenomena in BCP mice (BCP + THP 20 mg/kg group vs. BCP group; BCP + THP 40 mg/kg group vs. BCP group). HE staining of tibia sections exhibited that, neither inflammatory infiltration nor bone destruction were monitored in mice of the Sham group; however, obvious inflammatory infiltration, as well as bone destruction, was displayed in tibia sections of BCP group; by THP treatment, the attenuated inflammatory infiltration, as well as the relieved bone destruction, occurred in tibia sections of BCP mice (Fig. 2B). These phenomena indicated that THP treatment could relieve inflammatory infiltration and bone destruction in BCP.

THP treatment alleviated inflammation and bone destruction in BCP mice (A) Micro-CT combined with three-dimension reconstruction technique showed that, THP treatment relieved lesions in the cortical and trabecular bone around the tibia on postoperative day 21 in BCP mice. (B) HE staining presented the relieved inflammatory infiltration and bone destruction in tibia sections of BCP mice by THP treatment

THP treatment protected DRG from damage in BCP mice

DRG (L4-L6) samples of mice in each group were harvested on postoperative day 21. Its pathological changes and survival neurons were observed by HE staining (Fig. 3A) and Nissl staining (Fig. 3B and C), respectively. Mice of the Sham group showed normal DRG (without inflammatory infiltration) and more survival neurons; as compared to the Sham group, severe inflammatory infiltration and less survival neurons were discovered in DRG of the BCP group (P < 0.01); in contrast, the inflammatory infiltration induced by BCP could be effectively alleviated, and the number of survival neurons was markedly increased by THP treatment (BCP + THP 20 mg/kg group vs. BCP group; BCP + THP 40 mg/kg group vs. BCP group) (P < 0.01). Immunofluorescence staining of DRG sections was implemented to research the effect of THP on the expression of c-Fos and TNF-α. As displayed in Fig. 3D and G, a significant increment in immunofluorescence intensity of c-Fos and TNF-α was found in DRG of mice in the BCP group, when matched to the Sham group (P < 0.01); conversely, in comparison to the BCP group, much reduced immunofluorescence intensity of c-Fos and TNF-α was observed in DRG sections of the THP-treated BCP mice (BCP + THP 20 mg/kg group, and BCP + THP 40 mg/kg group) (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01). The impact of THP on DRG neuronal excitability was appraised by assessing the levels of neuronal excitotoxicity factors, including Glu, Asp and 5-HT. In contrast to the Sham group, mice of the BCP group expressed higher Glu and Asp, but lower 5-HT in DRG (P < 0.01). Conversely, the THP-treated BCP mice (BCP + THP 20 mg/kg group, and BCP + THP 40 mg/kg group) showed a much reduction in Glu and Asp levels, as well as an elevation in 5-HT level in DRG, when referred to the BCP group (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01) (Figure S2). Thus, THP treatment was suggested to protect DRG from damage in BCP.

(B-C) Nissl staining presented that THP treatment was conducive to improve neuronal survival in DRG of BCP mice. (D-G) Immunofluorescence staining displayed that THP treatment reduced the expression of c-Fos and TNF-α in DRG of BCP mice. * P < 0.05. ** P < 0.01

THP treatment suppressed the activity of the TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway in DRG of BCP mice

The expression of uPA and TNF-α in DRG and the levels of serum uPA and TNF-α was detected by immunohistochemistry (Fig. 4A and B) and ELISA (Fig. 4C and D), respectively. Obviously, the intensified uPA and TNF-α staining in DRG, as well as the increased serum uPA and TNF-α levels, was shown in BCP mice (BCP group vs. Sham group) (P < 0.01); conversely, THP treatment weakened the expression of uPA and TNF-α in DRG and the levels of serum uPA and TNF-α, comparatively (BCP + THP 20 mg/kg group vs. BCP group; BCP + THP 40 mg/kg group vs. BCP group) (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01). By qRT-PCR (Fig. 4E and H) and Western blot (Fig. 4I and M), mice of the BCP group expressed higher uPA, TNF-α, PAR2 and TRPV1 at both mRNA and protein levels, when relative to the Sham group (P < 0.01); intriguingly, THP treatment prominently suppressed the expression of these genes in DRG of BCP mice (BCP + THP 20 mg/kg group vs. BCP group; BCP + THP 40 mg/kg group vs. BCP group) (P < 0.01). All of these data implied that THP treatment could inhibit the activity of the uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway in DRG of BCP mice.

THP treatment protected mouse DRG neurons from BCP-induced damage

We successfully isolated mouse DRG neurons, as these DRG neurons were capable of expressing neuronal markers, including NeuN and MAP2 (Fig. 5A). To explore the safe and effective doses of THP, we treated DRG neurons with different concentrations of THP, and then performed CCK-8 assay to evaluate the viability of DRG neurons. THP at concentrations of 1, 5, 10, and 25 µg/mL was not biotoxic to DRG neurons (Fig. 5B). Therefore, in the following, this study established BCP cell models by co-culturing DRG neurons with E0771 breast cancer cells in a non-contact form, and then treated BCP cell models with THP at concentrations of 1, 5, 10, and 25 µg/mL. As shown in Fig. 5C, the significantly suppressed viability was found in BCP cell models (Model group vs. Control group) (P < 0.01); however, THP treatment at concentrations of 5, 10, and 25 µg/mL pronouncedly enhanced the viability of BCP cell models (5 µg/mL THP group vs. Model group; 10 µg/mL THP group vs. Model group; 25 µg/mL THP group vs. Model group) (P < 0.01). Thus, THP at concentrations of 5, 10, and 25 µg/mL was effective in treating BCP cell models. In the subsequent expriments, this study selected 5 and 25 µg/mL of THP to treat BCP cell models.

THP protected mouse DRG neurons from BCP-induced damage (A) Mouse DRG neurons were successfully isolated, as they were capable of expressing neuronal markers, including NeuN and MAP2. (B) CCK-8 assay revealed that THP at concentrations of 1, 5, 10, and 25 µg/mL was not biotoxic to normal DRG neurons. (C) THP at concentrations of 5, 10, and 25 µg/mL was effective in treating BCP cell models as demonstrated by CCK-8 assay. (D) THP at concentrations of 5 and 25 µg/mL enhanced the viability of BCP cell models as revealed by CCK-8 assay. (E-F) Tunel assay suggested that THP at concentrations of 5 and 25 µg/mL suppressed the apoptosis of BCP cell models. (G-H) ELISA implied that THP at concentrations of 5 and 25 µg/mL inhibited the expression of uPA and TNF-α in BCP cell models. * P < 0.05. ** P < 0.01

CCK-8 assay (Fig. 5D), Tunel assay (Fig. 5E and F), and ELISA (Fig. 5G and H) of DRG neurons in each group was implemented, in order to investigate the effect THP on the viability, apoptosis, and expression of uPA and TNF-α, respectively. BCP cell models exhibited lower viability, but higher apoptosis rate and expression of uPA and TNF-α, comparatively (Model group vs. Control group) (P < 0.01); instead, THP treatment distinctly enhanced viability, but decreased apoptosis rate and expression of uPA and TNF-α in BCP cell models (THP 5 µg/mL group vs. Model group; THP 25 µg/mL group vs. Model group) (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01). Hence, these findings illustrated that THP could protect mouse DRG neurons from BCP-induced damage.

THP treatment inactivated the TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway in BCP-induced DRG neurons

The activity of the TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway in mouse DRG neurons of each group was evaluated by detecting the expression of mRNAs and proteins for TNF-α, uPA, PAR2 and TRPV1 via qRT-PCR (Fig. 6A and D) and Western blot (Fig. 6E and I). It could discovered that, compared with normal DRG neurons (Control group), BCP cell models (Model group) showed higher expression of mRNAs and proteins for TNF-α, uPA, PAR2 and TRPV1 (P < 0.01); reversely, THP treatment remarkably suppressed the expression of these genes in BCP cell models (THP 5 µg/mL group vs. Model group; THP 25 µg/mL group vs. Model group) (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01). Thereby, THP treatment could inhibit the activity of the TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway in BCP cell models.

THP treatment inactivated the TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway in BCP-induced DRG neurons (A-D) THP treatment suppressed the expression of mRNAs for TNF-α, uPA, PAR2 and TRPV1 in BCP cell models, as proved by qRT-PCR. (E-I) Western blot indicated that THP treatment blocked the expression of proteins for TNF-α, uPA, PAR2 and TRPV1 in BCP cell models. * P < 0.05. ** P < 0.01

To further explore whether THP could inhibit TNF-α and its downstream uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathways, this work treated DRG neurons (without cancer cell treatment) with THP and exogenous TNF-α protein or Capsaicin (TRPV1 agonist). As shown by Western blot, DRG neurons that only treated by THP (THP group) expressed lower proteins for TNF-α, uPA, PAR2 and TRPV1 than control DRG neurons (Control group) (P < 0.01). Instead, as referred to the THP group, DRG neurons treated by THP and exogenous TNF-α protein (THP + TNF-α group) exhibited a much elevation in the expression of TNF-α, uPA, PAR2 and TRPV1 proteins (P < 0.01). Capsaicin effectively stimulated the expression of TRPV1 protein, as DRG neurons of the THP + Capsaicin group expressed higher TRPV1 protein than the THP group (P < 0.01). When matched to the THP + TNF-α group, DRG neurons of the THP + Capsaicin group displayed a much reduction in TNF-α, uPA and PAR2 protein expression (P < 0.01), but an increase in TRPV1 protein expression (P < 0.05) (Figure S1 A-E). Hence, THP was verified to inhibit TNF-α and its downstream uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathways in DRG neurons.

THP treatment might protect mouse DRG neurons from BCP-induced damage via inactivating the TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway

To verify whether THP mitigating BCP through inactivating the TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway, this work used PMA (an inhibitor of TNF-α) to treat BCP cell models. CCK-8 assay (Fig. 7A) and Tunel assay (Fig. 7B and C) exhibited the attenuated viability and the enhanced apoptosis of BCP cell models (Model group vs. Control group) (P < 0.01); THP treatment (25 µg/mL) effectively enhanced the viability and weakened the apoptosis of BCP cell models (THP group vs. Model group) (P < 0.01); besides, PMA and THP had additive effect, which combination enhanced the viability and weakened the apoptosis of BCP cell models (THP + PMA group vs. THP group) (P < 0.01).

(B-C) By Tunel assay, THP and PMA had additive effect, which combination suppressed the apoptosis of BCP cell models. (D-E) By ELISA, THP and PMA had additive effect, which combination reduced the levels of TNF-α and uPA in BCP cell models. (F-J) Based on Western blot, THP and PMA had additive effect, which combination suppressed the expression of proteins for TNF-α, uPA, PAR2 and TRPV1 in BCP cell models. * P < 0.05. ** P < 0.01

Subsequently, the levels of TNF-α and uPA, as well as the expression of proteins for TNF-α, uPA, PAR2 and TRPV1, in mouse DRG neurons of each group were detected by ELISA (Fig. 7D and E) and Western blot (Fig. 7F and J), respectively. Obvious increment in TNF-α and uPA levels and expression of proteins for TNF-α, uPA, PAR2 and TRPV1 were discovered in BCP cell models, comparatively (Model group vs. Control group) (P < 0.01); conversely, THP treatment reduced the levels of TNF-α and uPA, as well as the expression of proteins for TNF-α, uPA, PAR2 and TRPV1, in BCP cell models (THP group vs. Model group) (P < 0.01); interestingly, PMA and THP had additive effect, which combination reduced the levels of TNF-α and uPA and the expression of proteins for TNF-α, uPA, PAR2 and TRPV1 in BCP cell models (THP + PMA group vs. THP group) (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01). Therefore, THP was suggested to protect mouse DRG neurons from BCP-induced damage by inactivating the TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway.

PMA and THP combination alleviated the pain behavior in BCP mice

In this part, we treated BCP mice with both THP (40 mg/kg) and PMA. As shown in Fig. 8A and C, paw withdrawal threshold, paw withdrawal latency and limb use score were all significantly decreased in BCP mice (BCP group), as matched to the Sham group (P < 0.01); comparatively, THP treatment increased paw withdrawal threshold, paw withdrawal latency and limb use score (BCP + THP group vs. BCP group) (P < 0.01); moreover, PMA and THP had additive effect, which combination elevated paw withdrawal threshold, paw withdrawal latency and limb use score (BCP + THP + PMA group vs. BCP + THP group).

(B) THP and PMA had additive effect, which combination attenuated thermal hyperalgesia in BCP mice. (C) THP and PMA had additive effect, which combination promoted the use of the right hind limb (i.e., the limb of BCP model) in BCP mice. (D-F) Intra-group comparisons of paw withdrawal threshold, paw withdrawal latency and limb use score in mice of each group. * P < 0.05. ** P < 0.01

By intra-group comparisons, similar paw withdrawal threshold, paw withdrawal latency and limb use score were found in mice of the Sham group with time increased. Mice with BCP (BCP group) displayed the declined paw withdrawal threshold (day 3 vs. day 0; day 7 vs. day 3; day 14 vs. day 7), paw withdrawal latency (day 3 vs. day 0; day 7 vs. day 3; day 14 vs. day 7) and limb use score (day 3 vs. day 0; day 7 vs. day 3; day 14 vs. day 7), as time increased (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01). In term of mice in the BCP + THP group, they exhibited a decrease in paw withdrawal threshold (day 3 vs. day 0; day 7 vs. day 3), paw withdrawal latency (day 3 vs. day 0; day 7 vs. day 3; day 14 vs. day 7) and limb use score (day 3 vs. day 0) (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01). Simultaneously, mice of the BCP + THP + PMA group had the reduced paw withdrawal threshold (day 3 vs. day 0) and paw withdrawal latency (day 14 vs. day 7) (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01), whereas the changes in limb use score was not obvious (Fig. 8D and F). Thus, it was suggested that THP treatment might relieve pain behavior in BCP mice via inactivating the TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway.

PMA and THP combination mitigated bone destruction and DRG damage in BCP mice

This work performed HE staining (Fig. 9A) on tibia sections, and then implemented micro-CT combined with three-dimension reconstruction technique (Fig. 9B) to observe the bone destruction of mice. Mice of the Sham group showed normal bone tissues and structure; however, seriously inflammatory infiltration and destruction of the cortical and trabecular bone around the tibia were discovered in BCP mice (BCP group); THP treatment mitigated the inflammatory infiltration and destruction of the cortical and trabecular bone around the tibia in BCP mice, comparatively (BCP + THP group vs. BCP group); intriguingly, PMA and THP had additive effect, which combination relieved the inflammatory infiltration and destruction of the cortical and trabecular bone around the tibia in BCP mice (BCP + THP + PMA group vs. BCP + THP group).

Additionally, DRG (L4-L6) samples were collected from mice. Nissl staining (Fig. 9C and D) and HE staining (Fig. 9E) were carried out to research the survival neurons and pathological changes of DRG. Mice of the Sham group showed more survival neurons and normal DRG (without inflammatory infiltration); undoubtedly, less survival neurons and severe inflammatory infiltration were found in DRG of BCP mice, comparatively (BCP group vs. Sham group) (P < 0.01); THP treatment facilitated the survival of neurons, and relieved inflammatory infiltration in DRG of BCP mice (BCP + THP group vs. BCP group) (P < 0.01); further, PMA and THP had additive effect, which combination increased the survival of neurons and mitigated inflammatory infiltration in DRG of BCP mice (BCP + THP + PMA group vs. BCP + THP group) (P < 0.05). All of these signs revealed that PMA treatment might relieve bone destruction and DRG damage in BCP mice by inactivating the TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway.

THP might relieve pain behavior in BCP mice by inactivating the TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway

THP combined with exogenous TNF-α protein or Capsaicin was utilized to treat mice with BCP. Behavioral tests showed a decrease in paw withdrawal threshold, paw withdrawal latency and limb use score of BCP mice (BCP group), as compared to mice in the Sham group (P < 0.01); THP treatment dramatically increased paw withdrawal threshold, paw withdrawal latency and limb use score of BCP mice, comparatively (BCP + THP group vs. BCP group) (P < 0.01); however, the combination of THP and exogenous TNF-α protein treatment as well the combination of THP and Capsaicin treatment resulted in a much reduction in paw withdrawal threshold, paw withdrawal latency and limb use score of mice with BCP (BCP + THP + TNF-α group vs. BCP + THP group; BCP + THP + Capsaicin group vs. BCP + THP group) (P < 0.01) (Figure S3 A-C).

According to intra-group comparisons, mice of the Sham group exhibited similar paw withdrawal threshold, paw withdrawal latency and limb use score as time increased. However, a gradual decrease in paw withdrawal threshold (day 3 vs. day 0; day 7 vs. day 3; day 14 vs. day 7), paw withdrawal latency (day 3 vs. day 0; day 7 vs. day 3; day 14 vs. day 7) and limb use score (day 3 vs. day 0; day 7 vs. day 3; day 14 vs. day 7; day 21 vs. day 14) was found in mice with BCP (BCP group) over time (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01). Mice of the BCP + THP group also showed a decline in paw withdrawal threshold (day 3 vs. day 0; day 7 vs. day 3; day 14 vs. day 7) and paw withdrawal latency (day 7 vs. day 3; day 14 vs. day 7; day 21 vs. day 14) (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01), whereas the limb use score was not obviously changed as time increased. Regarding mice in the BCP + THP + TNF-α group, remarkable decline in paw withdrawal threshold (day 3 vs. day 0; day 7 vs. day 3; day 14 vs. day 7; day 21 vs. day 14), paw withdrawal latency (day 3 vs. day 0; day 7 vs. day 3; day 14 vs. day 7; day 21 vs. day 14) and limb use score (day 3 vs. day 0; day 21 vs. day 14) was discovered (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01). Similar trends were observed in paw withdrawal threshold (day 3 vs. day 0; day 7 vs. day 3; day 14 vs. day 7), paw withdrawal latency (day 3 vs. day 0; day 7 vs. day 3; day 14 vs. day 7; day 21 vs. day 14) and limb use score (day 14 vs. day 7) of mice in the BCP + THP + Capsaicin group (P < 0.01) (Figure S3 D-F).

Western blot of DRG samples of mice was recruited to monitor the expression of TNF-α, uPA, PAR2 and TRPV1 proteins. TNF-α, uPA, PAR2 and TRPV1 proteins showed a distinct elevation in DRG of BCP mice (BCP group vs. Sham group) (P < 0.01), which was reduced by THP treatment (BCP + THP group vs. BCP group) (P < 0.01). Conversely, the suppression of TNF-α, uPA, PAR2 and TRPV1 proteins by THP was eliminated by exogenous TNF-α protein treatment (the BCP + THP + TNF-α group vs. the BCP + THP group) (P < 0.01). Besides, mice of the BCP + THP + Capsaicin group expressed higher TRPV1 protein in DRG region, as relative to that of the BCP + THP group (P < 0.01). Simultaneously, in contrast to mice in the BCP + THP + TNF-α group, those in the BCP + THP + Capsaicin group expressed lower proteins for TNF-α, uPA and PAR2, but higher TRPV1 protein (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01) (Figure S3 G). Collectively, all of these data was indicative that THP might relieve pain behavior in BCP mice by inactivating the TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway.

Discussion

BCP seriously affects the Life quality of patients with advanced cancer. However, relief of BCP remains a challenge to be overcome, Due to the lack of effective analgesic drugs in the clinic. THP has been utilized in the clinic for more than 40 years in China because of its excellent analgesic effect. The analgesic effect of THP has been illustrated in some chronic pain, inflammatory pain, and neuropathic pain [11, 20, 21]. Recently, THP has been reported to exert the palliative effect on BCP through inhibiting the activity of microglial cells and suppressing the expression proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-18 [14]. Thus, this study established BCP mouse model and treated it with THP. THP was revealed to have well analgesic effect, as it relieves mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia of BCP mice. Pathological tests indicated that THP attenuated tibia destruction, as well as inflammation and death of DRG neurons. Besides, we constructed BCP cell models by co-culture DRG neurons with E0771 breast cancer cells in a non-contact form. THP treatment was researched to enhance the viability but reduce the apoptosis of the BCP cell models. Hence, THP was demonstrated to have favorable analgesic effect on BCP. Notably, this was the first time that THP might be through inactivating the TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway in DRG neurons to exertanalgesic effect on BCP.

BCP is a unique pain state that exhibits features of both inflammatory pain and neuropathic pain [22]. Bone is the preferred site of metastasis for breast cancer; the invasion of tumor cells can lead to the destruction of bone tissue, which in turn induces severe pain via inducing central sensitization [23]. In animal models of BCP, tibial destruction and severe inflammatory responses are common pathologies [23, 24]. Similarly, in this study, bone destruction and inflammatory infiltration was discovered in tibia of BCP mice. Importantly, THP treatment relieved tibial destruction and inflammatory response. THP has been reported to improve pain symptoms through its anti-inflammatory effects [25]. As far as we know, this was the first time that THP was revealed to attenuate tibial destruction and inflammation in BCP.

As primary sensory neurons, DRG neurons underlie the transduction of nociceptive signals, including inflammatory pain and neuropathic pain [7]. It has been researched that the damage and excitability of DRG neurons, as well as the aberrant gene expression in DRG neurons, are important factors in BCP induction [26–28]. In this paper, THP was shown to ease the inflammatory response in DRG neurons of BCP mice. The large amount of Glu and Asp are important mechanism leading to neuronal excitability, while 5-HT had neuroprotective properties by attenuating neuronal excitability [29–31]. This paper suggested the suppression role of THP on DRG neuronal excitability, as it decreased Glu and Asp levels but increased 5-HT level in DRG of BCP mice. Besides, THP treatment was conducive to the survival of DRG neurons in BCP mice. In vitro data also suggested that THP was effective in enhancing the viability and suppressing the apoptosis of DRG neurons co-cultured with breast cancer cells. Thereby, THP was indicated to be effective in reducing the damage of DRG neurons in BCP. Existing data have shown that THP can relieve pain by acting on DRG neurons [15, 16, 32]. This study initially revealed that THP exerted its analgesic effect by reducing the damage of DRG neurons in BCP. c-Fos is regarded as a marker of pain conduction; its activation in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord has been found to be involved in BCP, while the down-regulation of c-Fos can relieves nociception in animals with BCP [33]. This paper revealed the overexpressed c-Fos in DRG neurons of BCP mice, whereas THP treatment repressed the expression of it. This discovery suggested the inhibitory effect of THP on pain transmission in BCP mice.

TNF-α is a key pro-inflammatory mediator, which has been widely recognized to be involved in persistent pain state [34]. Recently, TNF-α has been researched to be up-regulated in DRG neurons of BCP rats, and considered to be a specific signaling pathway resulting in neuropathic pain in BCP [35]. In BCP, the over-activated neurons in nervous system can facilitate the release of multiple proinflammatory cytokines (including TNF-α) to trigger persistent pain [36]. In this research, the abnormally over-expressed TNF-α was investigated in BCP mice and BCP cell models, and THP treatment reduced the expression of TNF-α. The suppression effect of THP on TNF-α has been revealed in BCP [14], which is consistent to our findings. uPA is highly expressed in tumors, especially in tumors prone to bone metastasis, and its expression is positively regulated by TNF-α; however, uPA can activate PAR2 by converting plasminogen to plasminase [37–40]. Subsequently, PAR2 enhances the excitability of DRG neurons by activating TRPV1; TRPV1 is expressed on pain receptors, which can trigger neuronal excitability, neuropathic pain, inflammatory pain, and even BCP [41]. In BCP animal, PAR2 has been demonstrated to be up-regulated in DRG neurons, and can specifically contribute to the hyperalgesia [42–44]. The activation of PAR2/TRPV1 pathway has been revealed to promote the pain sensitivity in BCP animals [41, 45]. In this research, the over-activated TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway in DRG neurons induced by BCP was suppressed by THP treatment. Notably, THP and PMA had additive effect, which combination alleviated BCP and inhibited the TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway activity. More importantly, the pain relief and the inactivation of the TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway that induced by THP treatment in BCP mice was eliminated by treatment of exogenous TNF-α protein or Capsaicin. Thus, all of the findings implied that THP might exert its analgesic role in BCP by inactivating the TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway in DRG neurons.

This work has limitation. Neurons in the spinal cord and brain play a crucial role in BCP. It should be better to study the effect of THP on neurons in spinal cord and brain. However, due to experimental limitations, these experiments cannot be performed at present, which will be the focus of our future research.

Conclusion

This article suggested that THP exerted favorable analgesic effect on BCP. It attenuated pain nociception of BCP mice, as evidenced by its alleviation on mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia of BCP mice. Besides, THP treatment relieved tibia destruction and DRG neuronal damage in BCP mice. Initially, THP was firstly implied to relieve BCP by inactivating the TNF-α/uPA/PAR2/TRPV1 pathway in DRG neurons. Thus, THP was proposed to be an effective agent for the treatment of BCP.
