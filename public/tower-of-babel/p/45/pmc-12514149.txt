Modifying effects of PM2.5 and O3 on heat-related cardiorespiratory hospitalizations in Brazil
Authors: Weeberb J. Requia, Heresh Amini, Paulo Hilario Nascimento Saldiva
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Extreme heat is a critical public health threat, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. While its direct health impacts are well documented, less is known about how air pollution modifies heat-related risks. We analyzed daily hospital admissions for circulatory and respiratory diseases in Brazil (2008–2018), combined with high-resolution temperature and pollution (PM₂.₅ and O₃) data. Using generalized additive models and random-effects meta-analysis, we found that PM₂.₅ consistently amplified the effect of heat on respiratory admissions, with a 20.1% increase in risk under high PM₂.₅ conditions (95% CI 4.8–35.4%). In contrast, O₃ showed heterogeneous effects, including a national-level protective association at high concentrations (− 7.6%, 95% CI − 9.6 to − 5.6%). Regional analyses revealed stronger interactions in the North and Southeast. These findings indicate that PM₂.₅ exacerbates, and O₃ variably modifies, heat-related hospitalizations in Brazil, underscoring the need for regionally tailored adaptation and air quality policies.

Introduction

Climate change is increasingly recognized as a critical public health issue, with extreme heat events posing significant risks to human health. Rising global temperatures have been linked to increased morbidity and mortality, particularly due to cardiorespiratory diseases1,2. Heat exposure places physiological stress on the body, triggering cardiovascular strain, systemic inflammation, and respiratory complications, especially in vulnerable populations such as the elderly, children, and those with preexisting conditions3,4. However, while the direct impacts of extreme heat on health are well established, the role of air pollution as a modifier of these effects remains an area of growing concern.

Air pollution, a major environmental health hazard in urban settings, exacerbates cardiorespiratory conditions through systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and impaired pulmonary and cardiovascular function5,6. Studies have shown that exposure to pollutants such as particulate matter (PM2.5) and ozone (O3) can independently increase hospital admissions and mortality rates due to respiratory and cardiovascular diseases7–9. When combined with extreme heat, these pollutants may further amplify health risks, leading to synergistic or multiplicative effects on morbidity and mortality10–13. Despite these known interactions, research examining the modifying effects of air pollution on heat-related health outcomes remains limited, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where environmental and socioeconomic vulnerabilities are heightened. Most previous studies have been conducted in high-income settings, often focusing on mortality outcomes or single-pollutant models, and providing limited insight into the heterogeneous conditions of countries like Brazil.

Brazil, with its diverse climate and densely populated urban areas, presents a unique case for studying these interactions. Many Brazilian cities experience high levels of both air pollution and extreme heat, creating an environment conducive to adverse health effects. Prior research in Latin America has suggested that heat-related hospitalizations and mortality may be more pronounced in regions with poor air quality, yet comprehensive studies quantifying this interaction in Brazil remain scarce14–18. To our knowledge, there are no previous studies that have systematically assessed how pollution modifies heat-related hospitalizations across the entire country in Brazil. Our study addresses this gap by conducting the first nationwide analysis of modification effects, using high-resolution data on temperature, PM₂.₅, and ozone linked to over 23 million hospital admissions from 2008 to 2018. By assessing differences across Brazil’s five macroregions, we capture geographic variability in climate, pollution sources, and population vulnerability that has been largely overlooked. We hypothesized that PM₂.₅ would amplify the effects of heat on hospital admissions, whereas ozone, due to its distinct mechanisms of action, would interact with heat in a different and more heterogeneous manner across regions.

Methods

Hospital admission data

We analyzed hospital admissions for circulatory and respiratory diseases in Brazil from 2008 to 2018. The hospitalization data were obtained from publicly available open-access database managed by the Brazilian Ministry of Health, which records all admissions in public hospitals and a subset from private healthcare facilities (https://pcdas.icict.fiocruz.br/). This dataset covers approximately 80% of hospitalizations nationwide. Coverage is nearly complete for public hospitals, while private hospitals are included only when admissions are reimbursed by the Unified Health System (SUS). As such, individuals who rely exclusively on private healthcare may be underrepresented. Given that the Brazilian SUS provides care for the majority of the population, including the most socioeconomically vulnerable groups who are also at greater risk from environmental exposures, we believe that the dataset provides a highly relevant basis for assessing population-level impacts.

To identify relevant cases, we selected hospital admissions where the primary diagnosis corresponded to circulatory or respiratory diseases. According to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), circulatory diseases are classified under Chapter IX (ICD-10: I00–I99), while respiratory diseases are categorized under Chapter X (ICD-10: J00–J99). The study period extended from January 1, 2008, to December 31, 2018.

Each hospitalization record included the date of admission and the patient’s municipality of residence. For analytical purposes, hospitalization counts were aggregated at the municipality level on a daily basis, allowing for the construction of a time series of daily hospital admissions for each geographic unit throughout the study period.

Ambient temperature data

We sourced daily mean surface temperature data (°C) from the Copernicus ERA5-Land dataset (https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/cdsapp#!/dataset), which offers weather information at a spatial resolution of approximately 0.1° (~ 9 km at the equator). To align this data with the study’s geographic framework, we applied a zonal mean (area-based) approach to aggregate temperature values for each Brazilian municipality. This method ensures that temperature estimates correspond to the specific geographic boundaries of each municipality, accurately capturing local variations in climate conditions. Population-weighting was not applied, as consistent high-resolution gridded population data were unavailable for the full study period.

Air pollution data

We retrieved daily air pollution data from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF—https://www.ecmwf.int/), focusing on particulate matter (PM₂.₅, μg/m3) and ozone (O₃, ppb). This dataset has a spatial resolution of 0.125° (approximately 12.5 km) and a temporal resolution of 6 h. To ensure consistency with the study’s geographic and temporal framework, we applied the same spatial and temporal aggregation approach used for temperature data, allowing for accurate integration of pollution estimates at the municipal level. Ground-based monitoring data for PM₂.₅ were not used because monitoring stations in Brazil are sparse, concentrated in a few major cities, and lack consistent national coverage across the study period. The use of ECMWF reanalysis ensures complete spatial and temporal coverage for all municipalities.

Statistical analysis

The analysis was conducted in two sequential stages. In the first stage, we applied generalized additive models (GAMs) with a quasi-Poisson distribution to investigate how air pollution modifies the impact of heat on hospitalizations. The quasi-Poisson approach was chosen to account for potential overdispersion in the data. To provide a more detailed assessment, the models were stratified by geographical region, health outcome (all-cause hospital admission, circulatory hospital admission, and respiratory hospital admission), and air pollutant (PM₂.₅ and O₃). The analysis focused exclusively on the warm season in Brazil, defined as the period from December to March.

To control for potential confounding factors, each model incorporated day of the week as a categorical variable and adjusted for seasonal trends using cyclic cubic splines with four degrees of freedom. This degree of freedom were selected based on minimization of the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). This spline-based approach provided the flexibility needed to capture nonlinear seasonal variations over the study period. Additionally, air temperature and pollutant concentrations were included as two-day moving averages, incorporating exposure from both the current and previous day to better capture short-term effects on hospital admission risk.

The interaction between ambient temperature and air pollution was explicitly modeled to examine whether different pollution levels modified the association between heat and hospital admission. Also based on AIC, a tensor smooth function with three degrees of freedom was applied to allow for non-linear interactions, ensuring that the complex dynamics between these environmental stressors were appropriately captured.

To assess the temperature-admisson relationship under varying pollution conditions, we evaluated hospital admission risk at three exposure levels of air pollution—low, medium, and high—defined using the 25th and 75th percentiles of pollutant concentrations within each region: low (< 25th percentile), medium (25th–75th percentile), and high (> 75th percentile). We adopted these percentile-based categories of pollutant exposure for three reasons: (i) this approach facilitates comparability across regions with different absolute pollutant levels, (ii) it enhances interpretability for communication to policymakers and non-technical audiences, and (iii) it avoids instability in estimates at extreme values.The effect of heat on hospital admission was quantified by estimating the percentage change in hospital admission risk associated with an increase in temperature from the 75th to the 99th percentile of the local temperature distribution during the warm season. Bootstrap resampling was used to derive 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), ensuring statistical robustness and reliable uncertainty estimates.

In the second stage of the analysis, we aimed to derive a national-level estimate of the modifying effect of air pollution on the temperature-admission relationship. To achieve this, we employed a random-effects meta-regression model, which allowed us to account for variations across different regions and assess whether specific contextual factors influenced the interaction between temperature and air pollution. In this meta-regression, we included population size, gross domestic product (GDP) per capita, and green space availability as explanatory variables to explore their potential influence on regional differences in temperature-related hospital admission risks. GDP per capita was obtained from the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE), while green space availability was estimated using municipal-level normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) from MODIS satellite data. We assessed multicollinearity among covariates using pairwise correlations and variance inflation factors (VIFs), all of which were < 2, suggesting no collinearity concerns.

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The hospital admission data analyzed in this study were obtained from a publicly available open-access database managed by the Brazilian Ministry of Health. This dataset consists of aggregated, anonymized information and does not contain any individual-level patient data. Therefore, ethical approval from an institutional and/or licensing committee and informed consent from individual subjects were not required for this study.

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we conducted three distinct sensitivity analyses, each designed to assess the impact of alternative methodological choices on our primary results.

In the first sensitivity analysis, we modified the exposure assessment by applying a three-day moving average for both temperature and air pollution levels, instead of the two-day moving average used in the main analysis. This adjustment aimed to capture a wider temporal exposure window, allowing for a more comprehensive evaluation of cumulative effects on hospital admission. By incorporating an additional day, we sought to better account for potential delayed health effects associated with extreme heat and pollution exposure.

For the second sensitivity analysis, we redefined the thresholds for low and high air pollution levels, setting them at the 5th and 95th percentiles, rather than the 25th and 75th percentiles used in the primary analysis. This modification was introduced to examine how the temperature-admission relationship behaves under more extreme pollution conditions, offering insights into whether interactions between heat and air pollution remain consistent across a wider range of air quality scenarios. By shifting the reference percentiles, we aimed to improve the generalizability of our findings to broader pollution exposure conditions.

The third sensitivity analysis extended the study period to cover the entire year, instead of restricting the analysis to the warm season (December–March). This change allowed us to explore how the interaction between temperature and air pollution affects hospital admission across different seasons, acknowledging that fluctuations in temperature and pollution can impact health outcomes throughout the year. This approach was particularly relevant given that some regions in Brazil experience persistent warm conditions beyond the designated summer months. By including all months in the analysis, we aimed to assess whether the temperature-pollution interactions observed during the summer held consistently across diverse climatic conditions and seasonal patterns.

The analysis revealed clear regional variations in heat-related hospital admissions, which differed by pollutant type, outcome, and exposure level.

National meta-analysis: pooled estimates of the modifying effects of air pollution

The national-level meta-analysis using a random-effects model revealed differential modifying effects of PM₂.₅ and O₃ on the association between heat and hospital admissions due to cardiorespiratory diseases in Brazil. The pooled estimates indicated that the impact of heat on respiratory hospital admissions was significantly amplified at moderate and high levels of PM₂.₅. Specifically, at high PM₂.₅ concentrations, there was a 20.1% (95% CI 4.8 to 35.4%) increase in respiratory hospital admissions associated with heath exposure (i.e., percent change in admissions when temperature increased from the 75th to the 99th percentile of the local distribution during the warm season), while at medium levels, the risk rose by 19.0% (95% CI 2.9–35.1%). In contrast, at low PM₂.₅ levels, the effect was small and not statistically significant (0.9%, 95% CI −4.1 to 6.0%) (Fig. 3).Fig. 3National (results from meta-analysis) percent changes (95%CI) in the hospital admissions risk (respiratory and circulatory) associated with heat at low (blue color), medium (green color), and high levels (red color) of air pollutants (O3 and PM2.5).

For O₃, the pattern was notably different. High levels of O₃ exposure were associated with a significant reduction in heat-related respiratory admissions, with a pooled estimate of −7.6% (95% CI −9.6 to -5.6%). A similar inverse relationship was observed at medium O₃ levels, with a −4.1% change (95% CI −6.3 to −1.9%). At low O₃ levels, however, the effect was not significant, with an estimated 9.8% increase (95% CI −6.9 to 26.4%) (Fig. 3).

Regarding circulatory admissions, the results showed a similar, though less conclusive, trend with PM₂.₅. At high PM₂.₅ levels, the estimated increase in risk was 15.3% (95% CI −3.3 to 33.8%), and at medium levels, the estimate was 15.5% (95% CI −7.1 to 38.1%). However, both estimates were statistically non-significant due to wide confidence intervals. At low PM₂.₅ levels, no meaningful association was observed (–1.1%, 95% CI −8.2 to 6.0%). In contrast, ozone exposure appeared to consistently reduce the impact of heat on circulatory hospitalizations. At high O₃ levels, the pooled estimate was –14.5% (95% CI −22.3 to −6.8%), while medium levels yielded a –10.5% change (95% CI –15.9 to –5.2%). The estimate at low ozone levels was 15.6% (95% CI –9.6% to 40.7%) and not statistically significant (Fig. 3).

Together, these findings suggest that PM₂.₅ tends to amplify the health burden of extreme heat—particularly for respiratory outcomes—while O₃ exposure may reduce or modify this relationship. These national estimates align with the region-specific analyses and highlight the complex interplay between air pollution and temperature in shaping public health risks.

The sensitivity analyses largely confirmed the robustness of the primary findings related to the modifying effects of air pollution on heat-related cardiorespiratory hospital admissions. Across multiple scenarios—expanding the analysis to the full year, altering exposure windows, and adjusting percentile thresholds—most of the primary estimates remained directionally consistent, with some variations in magnitude and statistical significance. In the North and Midwest, the positive associations between heat and circulatory risk under low and high O₃ and PM₂.₅ levels remained consistent across all alternative models, including the use of a 3-day moving average, year-round data, and different pollutant percentiles. The Southeast also showed stable positive associations, especially under high PM₂.₅, with little change across sensitivity scenarios. Discussion

This study reveals that the interaction between heat and air pollution on cardiorespiratory hospital admissions varies substantially across Brazil’s regions, pollutants, and health outcomes. These regional disparities likely reflect Brazil’s climatic, environmental, demographic, and socioeconomic heterogeneity. For example, In the North region, the combination of persistently high temperatures and widespread biomass burning, particularly during the dry season, contributes to elevated baseline levels of both heat stress19 and PM₂.₅ exposure20. Previous studies have shown that the dry season in Amazonia is characterized by the co-occurrence of high temperatures, severe drought, and intense fire activity, leading to sharp increases in PM₂.₅ concentrations21. This aligns with our finding of substantial increases in circulatory admissions under both low ozone (76.2%) and high PM₂.₅ (51.5%) conditions. The North also showed a significant rise in respiratory admissions under high PM₂.₅ exposure (40.6%), further supporting the impact of pollutant accumulation from biomass combustion in rural and forested areas. In contrast, the Midwest, which shares climatic similarities with the North but has lower population density, also exhibited increased circulatory admissions under low O₃ (59.5%) and respiratory effects under high PM₂.₅ (9.3%), though to a lesser extent, suggesting a shared exposure burden, albeit moderated by demographic or infrastructure factors.

In the Southeast, Brazil’s most urbanized and industrialized region, exposure is predominantly driven by vehicular emissions and industrial pollutants, which generate complex mixtures of ozone precursors and particulates22–25. This is consistent with our observation of the strongest respiratory response to high PM₂.₅ (75.5%) and the only significant increase in respiratory admissions under high O₃ (11.3%). Interestingly, despite this pollutant burden, the Southeast exhibited a null effect for circulatory outcomes under high O₃, and a positive association under high PM₂.₅ (67.7%), which may reflect contrasting effects of specific pollutants on different organ systems. The Northeast and South regions, by contrast, displayed protective or null effects in most scenarios. These areas are characterized by moderate climatic conditions, relatively lower industrial activity, and potentially greater vegetation cover in urban areas, all of which could buffer the impact of extreme heat or pollution spikes. In particular, the negative associations under high ozone in the Northeast and South may indicate lower pollution reactivity or effective public health adaptations in these regions.

The divergent health effects observed under PM₂.₅ and O₃ exposure are consistent with their distinct physicochemical and biological properties. PM₂.₅, a complex mixture of fine particles derived from combustion sources26, penetrates deep into the lungs and enters the bloodstream, leading to oxidative stress, systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and exacerbation of both respiratory and cardiovascular diseases27. During heat events, thermally driven increases in heart rate and vasodilation may amplify PM₂.₅’s systemic effects28,29. In contrast, O₃ is a reactive gas formed through photochemical reactions involving sunlight and precursors like nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds30,31. O₃ primarily irritates the airways and induces local respiratory inflammation, but its systemic cardiovascular effects are more limited and inconsistent32,33. Moreover, ozone levels often peak during sunny, dry weather, which may coincide with increased outdoor exposure but also behavioral adaptations like staying indoors or reduced physical exertion, potentially reducing exposure.

Notably, the differences between respiratory and circulatory outcomes are likely rooted in distinct pathophysiological mechanisms. The respiratory system is directly exposed to ambient air pollutants, making it more immediately responsive to inhaled irritants like ozone and fine particulates 34–36. Heat can also dehydrate mucosal linings and impair lung function, compounding the respiratory burden of pollutants. By contrast, cardiovascular outcomes may result from indirect systemic effects, such as increased blood viscosity, endothelial dysfunction, and autonomic imbalance triggered by heat or pollution-induced inflammation37–39. These indirect pathways may manifest over longer time scales or require more cumulative exposure, which may explain the weaker and less consistent associations for circulatory admissions in this study.

At the national level, the meta-analysis revealed a compelling pattern: PM₂.₅ tends to amplify the adverse health effects of heat, particularly for respiratory outcomes, whereas inverse associations were observed for ozone. These patterns may reflect confounding influences, behavioral adaptations during periods of elevated ozone (e.g., reduced outdoor activity), or non-linear atmospheric interactions that alter pollutant dynamics under high-temperature conditions.. This suggests that particulate pollution exacerbates thermally induced physiological stress40,41, while ozone—though a known respiratory irritant—may interact with heat through more complex and potentially adaptive or behavioral pathways42,43. These findings are aligned with recent international studies that report similar pollutant-specific differences in health outcomes during heatwaves44.

This study has several strengths. First, it is among the first comprehensive assessments in Brazil to investigate how air pollution modifies the association between heat and hospital admissions for both respiratory and circulatory diseases, across multiple geographic regions. By leveraging over a decade of nationally representative hospitalization data and integrating it with high-resolution environmental exposure estimates for temperature, PM₂.₅, and ozone, the study achieves both scale and precision rarely seen in environmental health research in low- and middle-income countries. Methodologically, the study employs advanced statistical modeling using generalized additive models that capture complex, nonlinear relationships and allow for interaction terms, providing a robust framework for disentangling the joint effects of heat and pollution. The stratification by region and pollutant, combined with a random-effects meta-analysis, enables both localized insights and generalizable national-level estimates, which are critical for informing scalable interventions. Furthermore, by including both PM₂.₅ and O₃, the study goes beyond single-pollutant models and offers a nuanced understanding of pollutant-specific pathways, an important consideration given the divergent toxicological profiles and atmospheric behaviors of these pollutants.

However, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, exposure misclassification may have occurred due to the use of satellite-derived pollution estimates at the municipal level, which may not fully capture individual-level exposures. This could lead to non-differential measurement error, potentially attenuating the estimated associations. Second, the study uses administrative hospitalization data, which, while comprehensive, lacks granular clinical details. We were unable to control for individual comorbidities, medication use, occupational exposure, or socioeconomic status, all of which may influence susceptibility to heat or pollution. This limitation introduces the risk of residual confounding, especially in heterogeneous urban settings. Third, the analysis is restricted to hospital admissions and does not account for non-hospitalized morbidity, such as outpatient visits or subclinical exacerbations of disease, which could result in an underestimation of the total health burden. Fourth, while we adjusted for temporal trends, day of the week, and seasonality, the study cannot fully account for unmeasured time-varying confounders, such as infectious disease outbreaks (e.g., influenza), changes in healthcare-seeking behavior, or policy interventions, all of which could influence hospital admission patterns. Another limitation is that temperature was aggregated using area-based zonal means without population-weighting. This may introduce some exposure misclassification, especially in municipalities with heterogeneous population distributions, and could attenuate estimated associations. In addition,, while we explored effect modification by air pollution levels, we did not account for lagged or cumulative exposure patterns, nor did we assess co-pollutant interactions or indoor exposure variability, which are increasingly recognized as critical components in environmental epidemiology. Finaly, ECMWF reanalysis data may underestimate urban-scale pollution levels due to spatial smoothing, potentially attenuating associations in areas with high local pollutant concentrations.

Conclusions

This study provides compelling evidence that PM₂.₅ and ozone are associated with modifications in the relationship between heat exposure and cardiorespiratory hospital admissions in Brazil. Our results highlight the importance of regional context in shaping observed associations between climate and health in Brazil..

Specifically, PM₂.₅ consistently amplified the associations of heat, particularly for respiratory diseases, with the strongest associations observed in the North, Southeast, and Midwest regions. These findings reflect the influence of both biomass burning and urban-industrial pollution sources, which increase particulate concentrations during hot periods. In contrast, ozone exhibited a more complex role, with some regions—such as the Southeast—showing increased respiratory admissions, while other areas experienced reduced or null associations. For circulatory outcomes, interactions were more variable and often non-significant, reinforcing the notion that biological susceptibility differs between respiratory and cardiovascular systems.

Our results underscore the importance of regional context in understanding climate-health relationships in Brazil. Environmental factors such as pollution source profiles, urbanization levels, vegetation cover, and public infrastructure likely interact to shape local vulnerability. These findings highlight the urgent need for integrated public health strategies that account for both climate change and air pollution—particularly in urban centers and regions exposed to biomass burning.

These findings also have important policy implications. Adaptation strategies should integrate heat–health action plans with air quality management, recognizing that PM₂.₅ and ozone can modify heat-related hospital admissions. Regionally tailored interventions are particularly important in Brazil, where sources of pollution and climatic conditions vary widely across the North, Southeast, and other regions. Policymakers should prioritize combined approaches—such as improving air quality standards, strengthening wildfire prevention and control, and implementing heat-health early warning systems—to reduce the joint burden of climate and air pollution on public health.
