Host perceptions of an undergraduate semester abroad: strengthening partnerships in global health education
Authors: Myriam Vuckovic, Joseph Mulinge, Stephen Ledet, Margaret Baker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesAs global health education becomes more embedded in university curricula, the availability of experiential learning opportunities in this field has expanded as well. These programmes offer students valuable exposure to diverse cultural perspectives and help develop essential competencies in global health. However, concerns have arisen regarding the persistence of colonial-era dynamics, power imbalances and the potential negative impacts on host organisations in low- and middle-income countries. Much of the existing literature on this topic centres on short-term medical student experiences and is focused on the perspectives of the sending organisations. This study aims to fill this gap by examining a semester-long undergraduate global health experiential learning programme through the perspectives of host organisation staff.DesignThis qualitative case study draws on interviews with 18 key informants from host organisations across seven countries. The research design, data collection and analysis were developed using an analytical framework based on recommendations made by previous studies.SettingHost organisations across seven countries participated in the study. Interviews were conducted remotely via Zoom. The interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed, coded in Dedoose software and analysed for emerging patterns and themes.ParticipantsEighteen key informants from host organisations were interviewed, representing seven countries — Tanzania, Ghana, Mexico, India, Bangladesh, Australia and the Philippines — and a range of institutions including research institutes, government agencies and UN bodies.ResultsParticipants generally viewed their engagement in experiential learning courses positively. They emphasised the importance of their role in mentoring American and other foreign global health students, building their research skills and cultural competency, and providing valuable insights into power dynamics, local health systems and the wider context in which global health operates. Beyond financial remuneration, participants emphasised multiple non-financial benefits for host organisations. The study identified three key themes in developing strong programmes: partnership, communication and cultural competence. These findings reinforce the value of longer-term programmes and offer actionable recommendations for their continued evolution in global health education.Keywords: PUBLIC HEALTH, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH, Health Equity, EDUCATION & TRAINING (see Medical Education & Training)

Introduction

According to a 2015 Gallup study, college graduates who had experiential learning experiences while in college were twice as likely to be deeply engaged in their careers and more likely to be thriving in life.1 As more universities have begun to include global health education in their curricula, the number of programmes offering global health experiential learning opportunities has also increased. Among these approaches, international experiential learning has become established as an effective way for students to gain cultural competency by interacting with other global health scholars abroad and through immersion in new cultural environments. This exposes students to a wide range of perspectives on learning, research and the evolving concept of global health. They learn to translate abstract concepts into real experiences. Studies on short-term experiences in global health have found that students gained skills in ‘culturally appropriate communication’, ‘creative approaches to resources’ and a better receptiveness to new ideas and the importance of advanced planning.2 3 This approach is based on classic experiential learning theory (ELT), described in Kolb’s experiential learning cycle.4 According to ELT, individuals gain knowledge through grasping and transforming their experiences. Grasping is done through concrete experience and abstract conceptualisation, while transforming is accomplished through reflective observation and active experimentation.5 According to ELT, these actions are part of a four-step learning process where concrete experiences lead to reflections, which become abstract concepts that are transformed into implications that can be tested through action.4 In short, experience leads to new knowledge and learning. These perspectives are supported and expanded on by many other scholars, as discussed by Vuckovic et al. (2021).

At the same time, recent criticism has called universities under question for continuing to work within a framework of outdated relationships formed during the age of colonialism and supporting a lopsided system that benefits higher-income countries (HICs) over lower- and middle-income countries (LMICs).6,10 One concern is that students from universities in HICs are ‘dropped’ into global health institutions, often situated in LMICs, sometimes with little consideration of the impact that this is having on the recipient organisations.11 12 A systematic review of short-term medical global health immersions published in 2021 found that a lack of cross-cultural communication, power imbalances and language differences were primary drivers of host concerns.13 This has led to the coining of phrases like ‘parachuting’ and ‘medical tourism’.

In response, several papers published in recent years have called for a re-imagining of global health experiential learning programmes, emphasising more equitable partnerships, transparency in programme goals and costs, and enhanced trainee preparation in both technical skills and cultural competency. One of the earliest frameworks to guide global health educators and universities emerged in 2010, when the Working Group on Ethics Guidelines for Global Health Training (WEIGHT) developed recommendations based on existing literature and member experiences. These guidelines outline best practices for both sending and host institutions, stressing the importance of structured, long-term programmes that provide mutual benefit.14

Subsequent studies have built on these principles from different perspectives. Decamp et al15 examine these issues through a medical ethics lens, while Shah et al16 propose a framework categorising interventions at the individual, programme and societal levels. Lu et al13 further expand on these discussions through the above-mentioned systematic review focusing on LMIC host perceptions of short-term medical immersions. Their analysis identifies several best-practice themes, including the need for formal partnerships, clear learning objectives, mutual benefits (such as bidirectional exchanges and host institution compensation), consistent communication, collaborative planning and mandatory pre-departure training centred on sociocultural awareness and cultural humility. The Advocacy for Global Health Partnerships (AGHP), founded in 2017 to promote ethical standards in global health partnerships, ‘to maximise the value and minimise the harm of short-term engagements in global health’,17(p1) launched the Brocher Declaration in 2020, emphasising mutual partnership, local empowerment, sustainability and accountability. In 2022, AGHP expanded its mission and established its secretariat at the Uganda National Academy of Sciences, fostering shared learning and collaboration through its community of practice.

Beyond ethical frameworks and best-practice recommendations, a growing body of literature addresses experiential learning in the context of decolonising global health and shaping the future of global health education. Scholars such as Adams et al,18 Hartman et al,19 Eichbaum et al,20 Velin et al21 and Krentel et al22 advocate for strategies to strengthen and sustain partnerships, including redistributing power to LMIC institutions, embedding ethical, anti-racist and fair-trade principles into global health education, addressing the environmental impact of experiential learning and promoting equitable access and opportunities. Additionally, Binagwaho et al,23 in their 2022 BMJ opinion piece, emphasise the necessity of formal agreements that fully acknowledge and compensate LMIC institutions for their contributions.

Other studies have explored the perspectives of global health practitioners and institutions. John et al24 base their analysis of global health partnerships on conversations with 14 global health researchers from Kenya, Uganda and the USA with over a decade of experience, identifying four essential factors for successful collaborations: mutual respect and benefit, trust, clear communication and well-defined partner roles and expectations. Similarly, another study synthesises insights from 42 senior representatives across four East African universities on what constitutes a strong international partnership. Their findings highlight the importance of supporting educational development — particularly PhD training, new programme creation and international trainee experiences that align with institutional strategies. While financial and research support remain valuable, educational capacity-building was deemed the highest priority, along with reliability and reciprocity in partnerships.25

Together, these works contribute to a more nuanced understanding of how global health experiential learning can be restructured to promote ethical, equitable and sustainable collaborations. We have summarised recommendations under four categories: recognition of host organisations as partners in the design and implementation of the programme, focusing on building cultural competency, ensuring services are compensated and building on strong inter-institutional partnerships. This framework is shown and explained in box 1.

Core components of equitable partnerships in global health education

Strong focus on building cultural competency:

Ensuring students are thoroughly prepared is essential; they should have a solid understanding of the host site’s healthcare system, including the roles of local and visiting researchers, as well as the relevant language and cultural norms. Students should also be well-informed about the typical research or work conditions, standards of practice, potential ethical challenges and strategies for addressing these challenges. Recommendations include having sending programmes systematically solicit feedback from both students and host organisations regarding cross-cultural communication issues. This feedback should be actively used to refine and improve collaboration. Furthermore, visiting students, faculty and staff should actively promote and model cultural humility across all levels of the partnership from institutional policies to individual interactions, fostering a respectful and mutually beneficial environment.10 17 18

Programme provides appropriate compensation:

This element recognises that partnerships need to be based on an agreement that acknowledges the contributions of host institutions and fully compensates them for the training provided and the use of their resources.10 20 22

The programme is based in the context of strong institutional partnerships:

Programmes should be built upon a bilateral institutional partnership that provides equitable educational experiences for all participants, ensuring mutual capacity building for both institutions. Research findings should be shared with fairness and respect, with co-ownership of data and equal authorship rights in co-generated publications. To maintain transparency and accountability, the programme should ensure that research findings are disseminated to all relevant stakeholders, including the communities involved in the research. Additionally, the programme should provide LMIC partners with access to the sending institution’s academic resources, such as libraries, curricula and online learning platforms, to foster a truly collaborative and enriching educational environment.10 15 17 20 23

An increasing number of global health programmes at the graduate and undergraduate levels now integrate experiential learning into their curricula, with comparatively less research focusing on these programmes. Most of the existing literature on this topic, summarised above, centres on the short-term experiences of medical students, with most perspectives being presented from the viewpoint of the sending institution. This study seeks to fill that gap by studying the case of a long-running undergraduate global health programme that explores the lived experiences of people working with host organisations. This study complements the study published by Vuckovic et al26 that used the same case to explore student perspectives.

Methods

This study employed a qualitative case study design, taking a phenomenological approach27 to understand the lived experiences of individuals at host organisations who supervise undergraduate students. The case study design, as described by Yin (2018), is a well-established qualitative methodology suited for examining a bounded programme in depth.28

We examine the lived experiences of individuals at host organisations who supervise U.S.-based undergraduate global health students. Specific research questions are:

How do the host organisations perceive the experiential learning programme’s value to students’ learning?

How do the host organisations perceive the experiential learning programme’s value to students’ learning?

What are host organisation priorities and preferences related to experiential learning programmes?

What are host organisation priorities and preferences related to experiential learning programmes?

The case we selected for this study is Georgetown University’s Department of Global Health’s experiential learning course (see description of the programme in box 2). The case was selected for several reasons: it was known to the authors, it had been in operation for over a decade and the preceptors involved are experienced professionals from multiple countries and world regions with experience of this programme and several others.

Description of Georgetown University’s senior year Experiential Learning Programme in Global Health

The experiential learning programme is a semester-long (14 weeks) placement required for all seniors in the undergraduate Bachelor of Science in Global Health programme at Georgetown University in Washington, DC.

The programme’s learning objectives include strengthening research skills, an awareness of global health inequities, developing cultural competency and fostering teamwork and other professional skills. Students engage in tasks such as data analysis and literature reviews, contributing to ongoing projects aligned with host priorities. They contribute to active research studies, conduct independent studies under the guidance of local preceptors, or engage in public health activities led by the host organisation.

Students are placed with research or policy organisations, not in direct clinical settings, and work on public health topics under the guidance of local preceptors. Since the early 2000s, more than 350 students have participated. Placements span both high- and low-income countries like Tanzania, Ghana, Mexico, Bangladesh, Cameroon, Japan and Australia, as well as more recently domestic sites—including Alaska and West Virginia—ensuring exposure to diverse health systems and populations.

Preceptors are affiliated to Georgetown University and provide regular mentorship, academic guidance and logistical support, including orientation to the local professional and cultural context.

Participants in this study are lead preceptors and other supervisors at the institutes hosting Georgetown undergraduate students. Guided by department administrative records, all 34 preceptors who had supervised a student at least once from 2012 to 2022 were selected. They were contacted by the lead researcher (MV) by email and invited to be a part of the study. JM then followed up at least twice. A semi-structured interview guide was used to direct the interviews (online supplemental 1), designed based on the framework derived from the literature review that informs this study and is presented in box 1. After being trained on the guide by MCB, JM set-up and conducted the interview on Zoom from March to May 2023. Interviews were recorded with the participants’ permission and transcribed with the support of the Happy Scribe automatic transcription software. A total of 18 participants were finally interviewed.

Transcriptions were coded both deductively (using themes in the Framework) and inductively—allowing for additional themes to be identified by the participants. Inductive codes were developed by the three researchers working together, reading through the transcripts and discussing and deciding on new codes that should be added. Coding was done by the three researchers in Dedoose. To enhance consistency, the research team developed a shared data dictionary and jointly coded the initial transcripts, refining definitions through discussion until consensus was reached. Following this calibration, codes were divided among researchers, with each individual responsible for applying their assigned codes across all transcripts, so that any given code was applied consistently by a single researcher.

The lead researcher (MV) read all coded segments and wrote a thick description of the data retrieved under each code. Thick description, following Geertz’s conceptualisation and as described by Hennink et al. (2020), involves describing not only actions but also the social and cultural context that gives it meaning.27 In our analysis, thick description entailed producing detailed summaries that considered the breadth, depth, nuance and context of coded data, supported by quotations to illustrate meaning. These descriptions were subsequently used to develop and refine themes. MV and MCB met weekly during this phase to discuss in detail the interpretation of findings, jointly developed inductive sub-themes under each of the four pillars and together edited the material for length.

Study procedures were reviewed and approved by the Georgetown University’s Institutional Review Board (STUDY00005897). Participants provided written consent and engaged in member-checking to validate findings. Data were stored in password-protected files, available for access only to the three investigators (MV, MCB and JM).

Reflexivity statement

MCB had worked as programme director for 1 year at the time of data collection, and both MCB and MV worked for the Department of Global Health for several years. Although this was not a programme evaluation, this potentially biased us to hear positive comments more strongly. Potential biases were addressed by having JM conduct the interviews and review all preliminary results and interpretations. JM is a professional nurse with prior qualitative research experience from Kenya, conducting his master’s at Georgetown University at the time. When MV and MCB were working on the analysis, they sought to maintain awareness by regularly asking and discussing how they might be biasing the findings. Finally, an early summary of key points was presented and discussed at a preceptor’s meeting, and a draft of the manuscript was sent to all participants for comment and approval (a process known as member checking).

Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness was supported through use of a shared data dictionary, joint coding of initial transcripts, division of codes to ensure consistency, regular team discussions during thematic analysis, and participant member-checking of findings.

Results

A total of 18 people were interviewed, representing four world regions, with 17 supporting students in low- or middle-income countries. Ten were from Africa (3 Tanzania, 7 Ghana), three from the Pacific Region (1 Australia, 2 Philippines), three from South Asia (2 Bangladesh, 1 India) and two from Latin America (Mexico). 16 were nationals of these countries; 10 were male and 8 female. Most worked in research and education organisations and two worked with the UN. All were mid- or senior-level professionals. They had been involved with the programme from 1 to nearly 20 years, and several had experience with other, similar domestic or international experiential learning programmes. Participating organisations included national research institutes (Ghana, India, Mexico, Tanzania), universities and training institutes (Australia, Ghana, Bangladesh) and UN agencies (Philippines). Study findings from the thematic analysis are presented below, organised by the framework’s four headings (box 1).

Host Organisations are recognised thought partners in the design and delivery of the programme

Perspectives on Programme Learning Objectives

Participants highlighted academic, professional and cultural learning goals. Academically, learning research skills was seen as a priority, with most respondents emphasising the importance of students experiencing the entire research cycle, from proposal development to reporting. Many stressed the value of field research to understand the time required for primary data collection, the people and communities behind the data and the importance of building trust. Understanding context and culture was also seen as crucial:

Getting used to developing-country health systems and seeing the deficiencies and difficulties… I think going forward in their future careers, it will open their minds because they are coming from a background of advancements. (Participant #15, Africa Region)

Studying health topics ‘in situ’ was viewed as a chance for host organisations to guide interpretation, provide contextual understanding and offer constructive critique:

When they analyze everything, they present it during the scientific meeting for input… so that she’ll interpret her results, considering the context, society, and beliefs to avoid misinterpretation. (Participant #6, Africa Region)

Participants also emphasised professional skills such as cross-cultural communication, working with community gatekeepers, navigating large organisations and managing ambiguity. Personal growth objectives included overcoming culture shock, stepping outside comfort zones, exploring host cultures and embracing new experiences.

Roles related to Co-Design

A senior preceptor from Ghana, one of the University’s first partner sites, recalled co-designing the original programme with the Chair of the GU Department of International Health at the time:

So basically, both institutions had to come up with a plan on what the students were supposed to do. I had been hosting [Masters] students from … Ghana and from … Amsterdam previously… So, what I did was to adjust the program that I had for these students so that the undergraduate students could have a similar experience, but not to the extent that the master’s students were having. (Participant #11, Africa Region)

The programme has since expanded to include over 35 preceptors, some serving for over a decade and others for shorter periods. Several preceptors noted adapting the programme over time to enhance learning and meet their own needs, including changing site locations, shifting to secondary data analysis to address ethical approval timelines and increasing community engagement opportunities.

Participants described research topic selection as primarily guided by the host organisation, influenced by students’ methodological preferences, alignment with the institution’s research agenda, available data and feasibility of ethical approval. Approaches varied: some provided a list of topics before students arrived, while others determined topics through discussions after arrival. Participants emphasised balancing students’ passions with local relevance.

Roles related to Co-delivery

Participants’ roles on the programme included preceptors, supervisors or programme coordinators, often assigned by their superiors. Their responsibilities fell into three main categories: supervising students’ research, providing mentorship and managing logistics.

The level of academic supervision varied based on expertise and the student’s background. Many participants described providing frequent feedback, including helping students develop research questions, methodologies and ethical submissions, as well as guiding analysis and recommendations.

Mentorship extended to career advice and cultural adjustment, with a focus on developing soft skills like teamwork and cross-cultural communication. Particularly with undergraduates, many saw their role as akin to that of a family member:

I felt they are more like a niece than a student… It’s more than just a job or technical. It’s the whole aspect of them being in an international post. (Participant #14, Pacific Region)

Participants also managed logistics, such as visas, transportation, housing, health and safety orientations and financial aspects. Preceptors are on call 24/7 for emergencies, reflecting the significant amount of time and effort required for their roles.

Some frustrations were expressed about times their autonomy was curtailed, such as when communication had bypassed them, if they weren’t fully briefed on academic deliverables, or when the university’s rules concerning students’ safety, such as not allowing them to drive a car, made it harder to immerse them in day-to-day life and put more onus on the host organisation to find alternatives.

Communication as a facilitator

Two-way communication emerged as a strong theme and critical to facilitating collaborative planning and implementation. Communication frequency varied across participants, with senior preceptors often having more contact with Georgetown University than assigned researchers. Overall, respondents felt regular communication was important and had improved in recent years, facilitated by tools like WhatsApp and Zoom.

Challenges have arisen in the past from misunderstandings about assignment quality or scope, often linked to unclear expectations. Written assignment guidelines and the student handbook were valued by many as they clearly outlined academic deliverables and roles, helping to align expectations and reduce miscommunication. Participants stressed the need for syllabi with due dates. One respondent also recommended a routine tripartite meeting between the programme, the preceptor and the students, shortly after the students’ arrival to address any challenges collaboratively.

Several participants valued opportunities to visit GU during biennial preceptor meetings, which allowed them to meet faculty, visit the campus, exchange ideas with peers and strengthen relationships. One preceptor, who spent 3 months as a guest researcher, suggested making this opportunity more widely available. Participants expressed a desire for more frequent scholarly exchanges and deeper institutional collaboration.

Strong focus on building cultural competency

Most participants felt that undergraduate students were well-prepared academically and received practical advice on what to bring, how to stay safe from diseases like malaria, navigating traffic and what to expect regarding housing. Participants praised students’ academic ability, work ethic, enthusiasm, critical thinking, creativity, independence and openness to learning.

Adapting to a new, often very different cultural environment was highlighted as both a joy and a challenge. Participants observed that most students adjusted well, demonstrating openness to cultural learning and respectful behaviour, though culture shock was common early on as they adapted to accommodation, internet connectivity, local infrastructure and food. Several participants mentioned dress as an area for adjustment:

Okay, so some would come to the office wearing flip flops, but our staff are not allowed to do that… You should dress properly as if you are going to an office in your country… The weather here is very warm … really very hot at times. But they still needed to know that. (Participant #15, Africa Region)

Perspectives on cultural adaptation sometimes reveal cultural differences in values. One notable example is individual agency and efficiency versus collective harmony and relationship-building. In Australia, where values are similar to the United States, students’ independence was praised:

One of the things I do really like … they don’t seem to be going, oh, I couldn’t possibly do that. They teach themselves how to do things… They come with a lot of humility.

While in Asia, Africa and the Americas these same attitudes could at times run counter-cultural:

The difficulty was in terms of not work, but more in terms of trying to fit in here, living here, doing things on their own. Very much independence… and not really allowing us or giving us the opportunity to deal with the situation. They were taking decisions on their own, which we found a bit frustrating at one point. (Participant #18, Asia Region)

Pre-departure video conferences between students, preceptors, supervisors and Georgetown University faculty were seen as very helpful for relationship building, discussing research topics, site logistics and managing expectations. Additional suggestions included more extensive pre-departure training, conversations with past students or students from the host country, curated reading lists and longer Q&A sessions with preceptors. Participants noted that students, often young, were also influenced by their parents’ anxieties:

You could tell that some of them were scared. For some of them it wasn’t so much the student who was scared, it was the family or the parents who were scared. (Participant #11, Africa Region)

On-site orientations varied, with some sites offering multi-day sessions on geography, security, health and culture. Others provided detailed handbooks with practical information and assigned peer mentors. Many participants personally showed students around town, took them shopping or hosted dinners to ease their adjustment. Some host organisations had students rotate through departments, shadow in hospitals and visit local health directorates.

Regular, open communication with mentors and colleagues was critical to building trust. Many participants described regular meetings that covered research and personal adjustment:

Daily she will come and meet… She is very much approachable. I am very much approachable. I told her that if you have any problem, please come and discuss with me. So, she would come and discuss it. That is not a problem. This is a small institute. (Participant #4, Asia Region)

Participants very much appreciated reading students’ blogs, which offered insights into their experiences:

Every time I read their blog, every week, the same blog, I just laugh. And I said, that is good. (Participant #17, Africa Region)

Overall, participants viewed challenges as manageable and integral to cross-cultural learning:

There can be challenges, which is okay, which is part of the process. It’s fine… Georgetown is doing this is really great, sending students like this with full trust in host organization… they’re really providing this opportunity to students to actually explore at this age, at this stage. (Participant #18, Asia Region)

The programme provides appropriate compensation

Knowledge of the institutional agreement and payments differed by site and depended on whether the participant was in a preceptor role and an official signatory of the agreement. One participant who was familiar with the agreement observed that having a formal agreement between their organisation and Georgetown University shows how the sending organisation is accountable, makes their role clear and helps to align their expectations of students.

But this Georgetown one [agreement] looks formal. They are accountable to many things. So, I think that is the best one. So, I like it, especially… when the direction we had [is] clear. (Participant #17, Africa Region)

Overall participants reported that the funding received was enough to cover their expenses—with strong preference expressed for receiving funds up front, before the expenses are incurred locally.

Many participants reported that they personally did not receive any monetary compensation for their mentorship, which is regarded by their organisation as part of their work. Others, however, reported that receiving personal compensation would be an added incentive.

Several participants also talked about other non-financial benefits they received, as described in the following section.

Programme is based in the context of strong institutional partnerships

Many participants noted that they valued the long-lasting nature of the partnership. For participants from three countries, the partnership started more than 15 years ago. A couple of preceptors moved jobs during this time and continued receiving students, establishing a new inter-institutional agreement with their new workplace. In other situations, preceptors have left the organisation and someone else at that same organisation took over the preceptor role.

Benefits of the partnership for the host organisation

Participants noted the direct benefits of being named Global Affiliated Faculty at Georgetown University and accessing the online library system, as well as several other opportunities resulting from hosting students.

They also shared examples of students contributing to their organisation’s work and offering learning opportunities for staff. These included conducting literature reviews to stay updated, analysing data they hadn’t had time to process, assisting with manuscript preparation for international journals using their English skills, and teaching new technical skills like GIS. Participants also valued the fresh perspectives and insights students brought, often uncovering issues preceptors hadn’t considered. One preceptor emphasised how an outsider’s viewpoint can prove valuable in this context:

I would say the ability for the students to even identify health problems is one of the good advantages. Because we are there, sometimes we don't see the problems because we are insiders. (Participant #17, Africa Region)

Several preceptors pointed out how having an international partnership with a well-known American university added to their organisation’s global network and reputation. This was described by one preceptor as follows:

It also puts us as an institution on the… global map that we have this collaboration. Georgetown University is not a small university. And so, to have some of the students come around to spend time with us, to do some learning is a good thing for us. I don't want us to lose that. (Participant #5, Africa Region)

Preceptors frequently emphasised the value of relationships, highlighting the personal connections and friendships formed with Georgetown faculty and staff, some lasting many years. The pre-Covid-19 pandemic bi-annual preceptor meetings at Georgetown were seen as valuable for strengthening partnerships, exchanging ideas and gaining insight into GU and the students’ home environment. Participants also appreciated the friendships built with students and among students and colleagues at their organisations, citing examples of mutual cultural learning on topics like the U.S. education and healthcare systems. Some expressed hope that students would later become “ambassadors” for their host organisations and countries and wished for opportunities to follow their careers:

It makes us happy, really happy when we know that they have got the job of their dreams or they are going with their studies the way they are. So I think that’s amazing! (Participant #1, Americas Region)

Ownership and dissemination of findings from students’ work

All preceptors emphasised the importance of students presenting to the local research team, noting that their studies often informed future research. Receiving a copy of the final written document was also highlighted as critical. Several participants stressed the importance of sharing results with the host organisation’s partners, emphasising their relevance to policy and practice development:

…most of the analysis data sets that they worked (on)… were actually used to negotiate with the Ministries of Health… Situational reports were used for many, many documents as well. And of course, even for our internal meetings… they were absolutely useful. (Participant #13, Pacific Region)

Some preceptors suggested dissemination should extend beyond presentations and reports, such as preparing report summaries or publishing in peer-reviewed journals — a norm at some sites but a wish at others. While some saw publishing as a ‘good idea,’ others strongly advocated for it:

When students come and then invest time and collect rich data, and they end up in thesis documents and are not published, it’s sad and a waste to an extent. (Participant #8, Africa Region)

However, some noted that undergraduate studies often fell short of publication standards, though this could be mitigated:

What we are planning to do, that we will write the paper. One of our seniors [scientists] will do more advanced analysis. (Participant #10, Asia Region)

If they are undergrads and they start with something, sometimes it’s not weighty enough for publication… Sometimes we’ve had to add information if it needs to be published. (Participant #11, Africa Region)

Challenges included preparing work for publication after the semester ended, given students’ academic obligations and preceptors’ workloads. Ownership of studies was also discussed, with most noting that data belonged to the organisations where it was collected. Participants stressed the need for host organisation permission and co-authorship when publishing. Preceptors also agreed that data from district health authorities or ministries belonged to those authorities, not the students or host institutions.

Opportunities to build on the partnership

The partnership was widely viewed as positive, with participants eager to strengthen it further. Some suggested that, alongside an onsite preceptor, students should also collaborate with a US-based faculty member specialising in their study area, someone they could work closely with and discuss:

Do we agree on this? And do we think the student should do something differently? Is the analysis plan okay? Is this the standard of Georgetown? (Preceptor #11, Africa Region)

Many preceptors expressed interest in increased collaboration with Georgetown faculty on research projects and publications beyond the students’ work. While joint proposals had been attempted in the past, these efforts were not sustained.

I would really suggest that apart from just having students come over, it will be great to have a project or a grant written proposal or research that could actually accommodate maybe like a five-year, ten-year continuous program… that could accommodate students of different levels. That could also accommodate students from [here] as well.” (Participant #3, Africa Region)

Several participants hoped for expanded student and faculty exchanges, enabling more LMIC students and researchers to visit Georgetown or contribute to its educational programmes. A participant who had spent several months at Georgetown highly recommended the experience, but participants based in Africa and Asia also emphasised the need for travel subsidies and scholarships to facilitate exchanges. One preceptor proposed joint graduate-level teaching or degree programmes. Many encouraged GU faculty to visit host institutions to deepen their understanding of local research, build stronger relationships with preceptors and staff, and better appreciate students’ activities and experiences.

Discussion

This study contributes to the academic literature on global health education, specifically on the topic of experiential learning opportunities for students. We use a qualitative case study approach that includes interviews of 18 mid-level and senior staff from host organisations from seven countries and four world regions, with the aim of understanding the lived experiences of staff based at the host organisations—a previously under-represented perspective in the literature. Furthermore, with our focus on a semester-long research programme for undergraduate global health students, this study also serves to broaden existing research, which has most often centred on shorter programmes and graduate-level medical students.

An important question raised in the literature is whether it is necessary for students to go abroad to develop competencies in global health. Scholars have argued that meaningful experiential learning can occur in underserved settings within high-income countries.10 We acknowledge this perspective and recognise that global health education is not inherently tied to geography. At the same time, our findings suggest that reciprocal partnerships across borders can provide mutual benefits: capacity exchange, institutional collaboration and long-standing professional networks that extend beyond student learning alone.

Participants in this study highlighted the benefits of experiential learning programmes for the students. They perceived Georgetown’s programme to be building a culturally competent workforce in global health, able to reflect on their own power and privilege and informed by a deeper understanding of health systems and the reality of resource limitations.

Participants also provided in-depth insights into what makes experiences like these of value and meaningful for the host organisation. Beyond financial remuneration, participants provided many examples of personal and institutional benefits. Students had contributed to host organisations by conducting literature reviews, analysing data to inform reports and policy documents, assisting with manuscript preparation and sharing technical skills such as GIS. Many respondents also appreciated the fresh perspectives students brought, often surfacing overlooked issues. Hosting students was perceived to enhance institutional visibility, expand global networks and foster long-term professional relationships. These were seen as meaningful returns that strengthened both individual careers and institutional capacity.

This study also highlights the broader value of host engagement. Student contributions supported local research, informed situational reports for ministries of health and bolstered analytic and writing capacity within institutions. Hosts described these efforts as useful for policy development and organisational learning. Simultaneously, the experience promoted students’ cultural humility, adaptability and professional growth. By centring host perspectives, our analysis demonstrates how equitably structured programmes can enhance the functioning of host institutions while equipping students with the skills needed for ethical and effective global health practice. Overall, the results of this study align with existing literature on the key components of effective experiential learning programmes in global health, as summarised in the introduction and illustrated in the framework presented in box 1. Three key themes emerged especially strongly: the importance of partnership, communication and cultural competence, and these are discussed in turn below.

Partnership

Partners emphasised the need for genuine collaboration with sending organisations that balanced the sharing of agency as well as responsibility which speaks to the complexity of developing equitable partnerships in global health. This emphasis on a bidirectional partnership that gives autonomy to host institutions resonates with recent scholarship advocating for equitable and decolonised approaches to global health, highlighting the need to reimagine and reform global health education by prioritising local agency, mutual respect and shared decision-making.18 20 22 23

Communication

One of the main findings of our study was that regular, two-way communication is fundamental to establishing strong and effective partnerships. Our partners emphasised that most academic and logistical challenges can be resolved through consistent communication among local supervisors, students and the sending institution. This finding aligns with established organisational development principles, which emphasise that regular, two-way communication is essential for effective collaboration, building trust and fostering mutual understanding. Our study also underscores the growing role of technology, with tools like Zoom and WhatsApp enabling more frequent and accessible interactions, helping address challenges and promoting shared responsibility.29,31 In the context of decolonising global health, regular communication ensures that host partners’ perspectives and priorities are heard, fostering agency, mutual accountability and more equitable partnerships.32

Cultural competency

Our study highlights challenges related to differences in cultural values. These are not unique to experiential learning programmes but reflective of working in global health and indeed any work environment that includes international collaboration. While prior literature emphasised the role of sending institutions in preparing students to understand their host sites’ contexts, participants from host countries in this study also emphasised their role in helping students adjust and noted that they too are learning about the students' cultural background. While such challenges are unlikely to disappear entirely, acknowledging and addressing them is vital for fostering mutual understanding, solidarity and respect.33 34

Study Limitations

Although participants represent eight institutes in seven countries and four world regions, many who receive students from multiple universities, their perspectives have all been shaped in part by their participation in one experiential learning programme organised by Georgetown University. There is a need for more case studies of other programmes.

Conclusion

This study reinforces the value of longer-term undergraduate experiential learning programmes in global health and offers actionable insights into their effective implementation. By building mutually beneficial partnerships, prioritising strong communication, addressing cultural competency and recognising the contributions of host country partners, these field-based programmes can continue to evolve and make meaningful contributions to global health education.

Future research should further explore the perspectives of host organisations and examine student experiences at various stages of their careers and across different disciplines. Additionally, there is a need to deepen our understanding of how to measure the effectiveness and equity of experiential learning programmes, as well as the role of technological advancements in strengthening these partnerships.
