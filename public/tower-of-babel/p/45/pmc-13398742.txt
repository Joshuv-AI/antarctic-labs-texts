Long‐term outcomes of haploidentical hematopoietic stem cell transplantation with antithymocyte globulin–based myeloablative conditioning in pediatric refractory or relapsed non‐Hodgkin lymphoma
Authors: Chenguang Jia, Jie Zheng, Meng Yuan, Hongbo He, Yibo Fan, Bin Wang, Guanghua Zhu, Jun Yang, Yanhui Luo, Lixiao Cai, Pan Zhang, Yanlong Duan, Maoquan Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImportanceThe prognosis of pediatric refractory/relapsed non‐Hodgkin lymphoma (R/R NHL) remains poor, with limited evidence guiding optimal treatment. Haploidentical hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (haplo‐HSCT) is a promising strategy for this high‐risk population. However, the optimal conditioning regimen and long‐term outcomes of haplo‐HSCT have not been fully elucidated.ObjectiveTo evaluate the long‐term efficacy and safety of antithymocyte globulin (ATG)‐based myeloablative haplo‐HSCT in pediatric patients with R/R NHL.MethodsPediatric patients with R/R NHL who underwent ATG‐based myeloablative haplo‐HSCT at Beijing Children's Hospital between March 2015 and December 2021 were retrospectively enrolled. Overall survival (OS) and progression‐free survival (PFS) were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log‐rank test.ResultsA total of 29 pediatric R/R NHL patients were enrolled, including 21 with lymphoblastic lymphoma and 8 with mature T‐cell and natural killer–cell lymphoma. The 5‐year OS, PFS, cumulative incidence of relapse, and non‐relapse mortality were 79.3%, 72.4%, 10.0%, and 17.0%, respectively. At a median follow‐up of 5.5 years, the cumulative incidences of Grade III–IV acute graft‐versus‐host disease (GVHD) and chronic GVHD were 13.8% (4/29) and 44.8% (13/29; predominantly mild), respectively. Thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) developed in 12 patients and was associated with significantly inferior prognosis (P = 0.001).InterpretationATG‐based myeloablative haplo‐HSCT is a safe and effective treatment for pediatric R/R NHL, offering a viable alternative for patients lacking matched donors or requiring urgent intervention. Early detection and timely treatment of TMA may reduce mortality and improve survival outcomes.Keywords: Haploidentical, Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, Long term, Lymphoma, Outcome, Pediatrics

INTRODUCTION

Non‐Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) is the fifth most common malignancy in children, accounting for 7% of pediatric cancers in developed regions. 1 In contrast to adult NHL, pediatric NHL is predominantly high‐grade with aggressive behavior. The most common subtypes are lymphoblastic lymphoma (LBL), anaplastic large cell lymphoma, diffuse large B‐Cell lymphoma and mature T/NK (natural killer)‐cell neoplasms. 2 Modern intensive, short‐course, multiagent chemotherapy regimens have achieved long‐term survival rates exceeding 80% for newly diagnosed pediatric NHL. However, outcomes for refractory or relapsed non‐Hodgkin lymphoma (R/R NHL) remain dismal, with long‐term overall survival (OS) rates of 34% ± 2%. 3 Most treatment failures result from chemoresistance and rapid disease progression, highlighting the urgent need for effective salvage approaches.

Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo‐HSCT) offers a potential curative modality through cytoreductive therapy and graft‐versus‐lymphoma (GVL) effects. Nevertheless, the lack of suitable unrelated donors and concerns about transplantation‐related morbidity have historically limited its use in pediatric R/R NHL. Haploidentical HSCT (haplo‐HSCT) has recently emerged as a feasible choice, providing nearly universal donor availability and potent GVL activity. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Advances in graft manipulation and immune modulation, such as antithymocyte globulin (ATG) and posttransplant cyclophosphamide (PTCy), have significantly improved engraftment while reducing graft‐versus‐host disease (GVHD) and non‐relapse mortality (NRM).

Despite these developments, data on haplo‐HSCT specifically for pediatric R/R NHL remain scarce, particularly in the context of ATG‐based myeloablative conditioning (MAC) regimens. Most available evidence is derived from small, heterogeneous cohorts that combine adults and children. Consequently, the duration of remission, incidence of relapse, and survival outcomes following ATG‐based haplo‐HSCT in pediatric R/R NHL remain largely undefined.

To address this gap, we conducted a retrospective study evaluating the long‐term outcomes of pediatric R/R lymphoblastic and mature T/NK‐cell lymphomas who underwent ATG‐based MAC haplo‐HSCT at our institution, focusing on survival rates, relapse incidence, toxicity, and the occurrence of GVHD.

METHODS

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Children's Hospital (BCH) ([2025]‐E‐085‐R.). Written informed consent was waived for this retrospective study.

Patients and treatment

All pediatric patients with R/R NHL who underwent haplo‐HSCT between March 2015 and December 2021 in Beijing Children's hospital were included. Patients with extramedullary lymphoma or CNS3 (central nervous system stage 3) disease received total body irradiation (TBI)‐based regimen, which consisted of TBI at a total dose of 12 Gy administered from Day −8 to −6, fludarabine (30 mg·m−2·d−1, i.v., Days −6 to −2), and cyclophosphamide (30 mg·kg−1·d−1, i.v., Days −5 to −2). For patients with LBL, a modified busulfan/cyclophosphamide‐based regimen was administered, consisting of cytarabine (2 g·m−2, i.v., q12h, Days −10 to −9), busulfan (0.8–1.2 mg·kg−1, i.v., q6h, Days −8 to −6), cyclophosphamide (1.8 g·m−2·d−1, i.v., Days −5 to −4), and semustine (250 mg·m−2, Day −3). For patients with hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), modified fludarabine/busulfan based regimen was used, consisting of fludarabine (30 mg·m−2, qd, i.v., Days −10 to −6), VP16 (100–300 mg·m−2, qd, i.v., Days −10 to −8), busulfan (0.8–1.2 mg·kg−1, i.v. q6h, Days −8 to −6), and cyclophosphamide (30 mg·kg−1·d−1, i.v., Days −5 to −2). All patients in this study received rabbit ATG (2.5 mg·kg−1·d−1, Days −5 to −2), cyclosporine A, mycophenolate mofetil, and short‐course methotrexate for GVHD prophylaxis. Patients who relapsed or showed increased measurable residual disease or decreased donor chimerism post‐HSCT were candidates for therapeutic donor lymphocyte infusions (DLIs), whereas prophylactic DLI was administered based on clinicians’ individual experience, usually to patients who carried high‐risk genes or showed no chronic GVHD (cGVHD) after haplo‐HSCT.

Compound sulfamethoxazole was given to prevent Pneumocystis carinii pneumonia before the conditional regimen. Patients who received broad‐spectrum antibiotic treatment also received caspofungin to prevent fungal infections. Acyclovir was administered to prevent herpes virus infections. After stem cell infusion, cytomegalovirus (CMV) and Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) DNA copies were monitored weekly. If bacterial infection occurs before stable stem cell implantation, broad‐spectrum antibiotics are used immediately and then adjusted according to microbiological assay results. If CMV infection occurred, antiviral drugs, such as ganciclovir or foscarnet sodium, were administered until viral DNA copies returned to normal.

Definitions and data collection

For consistency, the nomenclature of the 2016 edition of the WHO classification was used. 8 Refractory NHL was defined as partial remission (PR) or less after interim assessment of first‐line therapy; or failure to achieve bone marrow (BM) remission after the first course of frontline chemotherapy in patients with BM infiltration at diagnosis; presence of high‐risk gene mutation with chemotherapy intolerance; or mature T/NK‐cell neoplasm with concurrent hemophagocytotic lymphohistocytosis (HLH).

The day of neutrophil engraftment was defined as the first day of three consecutive days with an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) ≥ 0.5 × 109 L−1, and the day of platelet engraftment was defined as the first day of seven consecutive days with a platelet count ≥ 20 × 109 L−1. Primary engraftment failure was defined as ANC < 0.5 × 109 L−1 or platelet count < 20 × 109 L−1 on 28 days. Secondary engraftment failure was defined as a recurrent decline in ANC to < 0.5 × 109 L−1 for at least 7 days.

Acute GVHD (aGVHD) and cGVHD were evaluated using the modified Glucksberg grading system 9 and Chinese consensus on the diagnosis and management of cGVHD, 7 respectively. Thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) was diagnosed when at least four of the following criteria were present concurrently: (1) lactate dehydrogenase above the age‐specific normal range; (2) de novo thrombocytopenia or increased transfusion requirements; (3) schistocytes on blood smear; (4) de novo anemia or increased transfusion requirements; (5) hypertension for age; (6) proteinuria or random urine protein/creatinine ratio ≥1 mg/mg−1; and (7) elevated plasma sC5b‐9 levels. Complete remission (CR), PR, stable disease, or progressive disease were determined using the Lugano classification. 10 OS was defined as the time between transplantation and death from any cause or the time of the last follow‐up. Progression‐free survival (PFS) was defined as the day from transplantation to the occurrence of the first event (including death from any cause, treatment failure, and relapse) or from transplantation to the last follow‐up. NRM was defined as death during continuous remission.

Baseline characteristics, treatment details, and outcome data were extracted from the electronic health records. Cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR), NRM and its cause, and prognostic factor were analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the disease and transplant characteristics. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing), SPSS 28.0 (IBM), and GraphPad Prism version 9.0.0 (GraphPad Software). Data were shown as median (Q1, Q3). Group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the Chi‐square or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. OS and PFS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log‐rank test. CIR was calculated using the Fine‐Gray model, treating death as a competing risk. NRM was similarly estimated, with relapse treated as a competing risk. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

A total of 16 males (55.2%) and 13 females (44.8%) without a prior history of HSCT were included in this study. The median age at HSCT was 95 (54, 129) months. Diagnoses included LBL in 21 patients (14 T‐cell LBL and 7 B‐cell LBL) and mature T/NK neoplasms in 8 patients (5 EBV+ T‐cell and NK‐cell lymphomas [EBV‐TNKL], 2 peripheral T‐cell lymphoma not otherwise specified [PTCL‐NOS], and 1 extranodal NK/T‐cell lymphoma). All patients were at an advanced stage at initial diagnosis according to the international pediatric NHL staging system (stage III, n = 6; stage IV, n = 23). Twenty‐three patients had BM involvement, and 10 had central nervous system involvement. Notably, all LBL cases in this study exhibited involvement of ≥3 extramedullary organs and multiple lymph node regions, with nine patients presenting with large mediastinal tumors (tumor mass ≥10 cm in diameter). Five cases of mature T/NK neoplasms presented with HLH at initial diagnosis. The primary indication for transplantation was refractory disease (86.2%, 25/29). Four patients experiencing relapse (1 with HLH). Among the refractory cases, 7 patients had high‐risk genetic profiles (4 with PR, 3 with chemotherapy intolerance); 18 patients achieved PR at interim restaging (including 4 with high‐risk gene mutations). At the start of the conditioning therapy, all patients were in CR. Patient characteristics were summarized in Table 1.

Stem cell transfusion and engraftment

The median dose of infused mononuclear cells was 8.84 (6.25, 14.22) × 108 kg−1, and the median dose of CD34+ cells was 9.80 (8.57, 10.00) × 106 kg−1. Twenty‐eight patients (96.6%) were successfully engrafted. One patient experienced primary platelet engraftment failure and died 78 days post‐HSCT. The median time to neutrophil engraftment was 12 (11, 16) days, and platelet engraftment was 13 (12, 21) days (Table 1). Complete donor chimerism was confirmed in all the engrafted patients.

GVHD

Nineteen of the 29 (65.5%) patients had aGVHD (15 Grade I–II; 4 Grade III–IV). Thirteen patients (44.8%) developed cGVHD, including 8 mild, 4 moderate, and 1 severe case. No significant difference in the cumulative incidences of Grade III–IV aGVHD and cGVHD was observed according to histologic subtype (Figure 1). The severe cGVHD case was diagnosed as bronchiolitis obliterans, with FEV1 reduced to 42.3% (Table S1).

Infections

Transplantation‐related infections were observed in 19 patients (65.5%). CMV viremia was found in 15 patients. One patient developed CMV pneumonia and was successfully treated with cidofovir and foscarnet sodium; the remaining 14 patients with CMV viremia achieved remission after treatment with ganciclovir and/or foscarnet sodium. EB viremia was observed in one patient, BK virus‐associated hemorrhaged cystitis in five, and bacteremia in three. Mixed infections caused by two of more of the above pathogens were observed in five patients. Additionally, five patients were diagnosed with Clostridium difficile colitis secondary to dysbiosis (Table S1).

Transplantation‐associated TMA

TMA was observed in 12 of the 29 patients (6 T‐LBL; 2 B‐LBL; 3 EBV‐TNKL and 1 PTCL‐NOS). Univariate analysis linked TMA to infectious triggers, CMV viremia (P = 0.035), and bacteremia (P = 0.019), rather than histology (P = 0.683), conditioning (P = 0.703), or GVHD (P = 0.449 for aGVHD, P = 0.710 for cGVHD). In multivariate logistic regression analysis adjusting for CMV infection, bacteremia, and viremia, no factor emerged as an independent predictor of TMA (Table S2).

Outcomes

The median follow‐up time for the 23 alive patients in the cohort was 66 months (range: 41–140 months). The 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year OS rates were 82.8% (95% CI: 69.1%–96.5%), 79.3% (95% CI: 64.6%–94.0%), and 79.3% (95% CI: 64.6%–94.0%), respectively (Figure 2A). The 5‐year OS rate for T‐LBL was 71.4% (95% CI: 47.7%–95.1%), comparable to that of B‐LBL (71.4%, 95% CI: 37.9%–100.0%), and slightly lower than that of mature T/NK neoplasm in which all patients remained alive (Figure 2B). PFS rate was 75.9% (95% CI: 60.4%–91.4%), 72.4% (95% CI: 56.1%–88.7%), and 72.4% (95% CI: 56.1%–88.7%) at 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year, respectively (Figure 2C). The 5‐year PFS for T‐LBL was 71.4% (47.7%–95.1%), which was also slightly lower than mature T/NK neoplasm (87.5%, 95% CI: 64.6%–100.0%) but higher than B‐LBL (57.1%, 95% CI: 34.2%–80.0%) (Figure 2D). Patients with cGVHD demonstrated superior 5‐year OS (92.9%, 95% CI: 79.4%–100.0%) and PFS (92.9%, 95% CI: 79.4%–100.0%) compared with patients without cGVHD ([66.7%, 95% CI: 42.8%–90.6%] and [53.3%, 95% CI: 28.0%–78.6%]), respectively (Figure 2E,F). TMA was strongly associated with inferior prognosis. Patients with TMA showed a 5‐year OS of 50.0% (95% CI: 21.8%–78.2%) compared with 100.0% in those without TMA (P = 0.001; Figure 2G). Similarly, the 5‐year PFS was 50.0% (95% CI: 21.8%–78.2%) in the TMA group versus 88.2% (95% CI: 72.9%–100.0%) in the non‐TMA group (P = 0.001; Figure 2H). Stage IV disease, CMV infection, and bacteremia were associated with inferior prognosis too, but the differences did not reach statistical significance (Figure S1). No significant differences in 5‐year OS or PFS were observed by histologic subtype (Figure S2).

Relapse and death

Disease relapse occurred in three patients at 4, 6, and 9 months post‐HSCT, respectively. The patient P8 (B‐LBL, stage IV with MLL::AF9, more detail in Table S1), who relapsed in the BM at +6 months, chose to discontinue treatment and subsequently died. P10 (B‐LBL) experienced extramedullary recurrence 9 months after the first HSCT. CAR‐T therapy induced remission for 20 months before disease recurrence. Benefiting from a second HSCT, P10 remained in remission for 3 years. P12 relapsed with a nasal vestibule nodule and achieved remission for more than 5 years after combined therapy involving surgery, chemotherapy, and DLI. Six patients (P1, P2, P8, P11, P20, and P25) succumbed. Among them, five cases were attributed to NRM, including TMA, respiratory failure, and interstitial pneumonia. The other one died of relapse (P8).

The 5‐year CIR and NRM were 10.0% and 17.0%, respectively (Figure 3A,B). No significant differences in either CIR or NRM were observed according to histologic subtypes (Figure 3C,D). No significant differences were found in CIR according to TMA (Figure 3E). Regarding NRM, a significant difference was found between patients with and without TMA (P = 0.004, Figure 3F). Given the limited number of patients who underwent transplantation in CR2, we are unable to compare relapse rates between patients in CR1 and CR2.

DISCUSSION

The main subtypes of pediatric R/R NHL are LBL, mature aggressive B‐cell lymphma, anaplastic large cell lymphoma and mature T/NK neoplasm. 11 This study focused exclusively on pediatric LBL and mature T/NK neoplasms, both of which present significant therapeutic challenges. Historical data indicate a 3‐year OS of 17%–58% for R/R LBL following allo‐HSCT, 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 whereas the survival of mature T/NK neoplasms is highly subtype‐dependent. Non‐anaplastic PTCL subtypes (e.g., extranodal NK/T‐cell lymphoma, hepatosplenic T‐cell lymphoma, and EBV+ TNKL) exhibit particularly poor responses to conventional therapies, with reported 5‐year OS rates of 34%–63% even after sequential HSCT. 16 , 17 Against this backdrop, this study demonstrated a 5‐year OS of 79.3% in pediatric R/R LBL and mature T/NK neoplasms using an ATG‐based MAC regimen with haploidentical donors, alongside acceptable NRM (17.0%), relapse incidence (10.0%), and manageable GVHD.

The survival advantage observed in this cohort may be attributed to several factors. First, haplo‐HSCT exhibits potent GVL effects and guarantees sufficient DLI, which reduces the risk of recurrence. 18 In addition, haplo‐HSCT offers the advantage of rapid donor accessibility, benefiting patients requiring urgent therapy. However, historical concerns regarding higher rates of transplant‐related mortality and GVHD have limited its widespread usage, and modern T‐cell replete/deplete strategies, such as rabbit ATG, PTCy, and αβ+ T‐cell depletion, have mitigated these risks. 4 , 5 , 19 ATG and PTCy are commonly used as GVHD prophylaxis for malignancy. Previous studies using these two protocols have yielded inconsistent results. 20 In a multicenter retrospective dataset, PTCy showed better outcomes than ATG in terms of NRM and survival. 20 However, the Chinese Bone Marrow Transplantation Registry Group retrospective cohort showed better engraftment, PFS, and OS and lower incidence of NRM for the G‐CSF/ATG strategy compared to PTCy. 21 Our protocol utilized unmanipulated stem cells with ATG, leveraging its dual immunomodulatory properties: T‐cell‐depleting role and functional interference with other immune effectors. 19 This approach yielded high engraftment rates (96.6%) and acceptable Grade III–IV aGVHD (13.8%) in our cohort, aligned with the study employing a similar ATG‐based MAC. 22 Second, the use of hybrid peripheral blood stem cell (PBSC)/BM grafts mobilized with G‐CSF likely contributed to these favorable outcomes. PBSC provided adequate CD34+ cell infusion, ensuring rapid and robust engraftment and immune reconstitution, while BM‐derived mesenchymal stromal cells may mitigate GVHD severity and increase engraftment rates. 23 Third, in this cohort, patients who developed cGVHD had higher OS and PFS than those without cGVHD. Given that only one case of severe cGVHD occurred in this cohort, these findings are consistent with previous reports suggesting that mild‐to‐moderate cGVHD may enhance GVL effects, resulting in lower relapse rates. 22 Finally, disease status at transplantation was confirmed to be a strong prognostic indicator. 24 The related high PFS and OS in this study might be attributed to the fact that predominantly CR1 patients, although they were refractory at the time of the initial chemotherapy. Although DLI has been demonstrated to reduce relapse risk, its application in this study was determined selectively based on disease status and clinicians’ individual experiences, which complicates the assessment of its true impact on OS.

Despite promising survival outcomes observed in this cohort, several adverse events warrant attention. The infection rate was notably high (19/29, 65.5%), especially CMV viremia (15/29, 51.7%), consistent with previous studies using 10 mg·kg−1 ATG for GVHD prophylaxis. 25 Therefore, an elevated infection risk is hypothesized to correlate with ATG dose‐dependent immunosuppression and delayed immune reconstitution. ATG dosage reduction (e.g., 5–7.5 mg·kg−1) combined with or not with low‐dose post‐cyclophosphamide, 26 , 27 or letermovir‐based CMV prophylaxis, 28 or risk‐stratified infection surveillance may mitigate this risk. 29 Furthermore, the incidence of TMA occurred in this cohort (41.4%) exceeds that reported in previous studies (10%–25%). 30 TMA was strongly associated with inferior survival in this cohort (P = 0.001). As univariate analysis linked TMA to infectious triggers, enhanced infection prophylaxis and preemptive TMA treatment may help reduce TMA‐related mortality. 31

The management of pediatric R/R NHL remains a high‑stakes clinical challenge, as outcomes with conventional salvage therapies are generally poor. 3 However, the therapeutic landscape has been transformed by the rapid development of non‑transplant salvage modalities, including chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)‐T‑cell and CAR‐NK‐cell therapies, bispecific antibodies, and antibody–drug conjugates. 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 Broader application is currently limited by scientific, clinical, and logistical challenges. 34 Moreover, because most target surface antigens such as CD19, CD20, CD22, CD3, or CD30, not all patients with R/R pediatric NHL are eligible for or can benefit from them. Our findings suggest that ATG‐based MAC haplo‑HSCT may present a crucial curative consolidation strategy, particularly for patients without a matched donor or those requiring urgent intervention. Integrating novel agents with haplo‐HSCT in a combinatorial or sequential manner may thus offer greater clinical benefit, capitalizing on the complementary strengths of each modality.

This study has several inherent limitations. The retrospective design, limited sample size (n = 29), and single‐center setting limit the generalizability of our findings. Subgroup analyses (e.g., CR1 vs. CR2, TBI‐based conditioning and DLI application) were limited by insufficient statistical power and the absence of randomization. Prospective multicenter randomized controlled trials are therefore warranted to validate these findings.

In conclusion, ATG‐based MAC haplo‐HSCT represents an effective salvage therapy for pediatric patients with R/R LBL and mature T/NK neoplasm, offering favorable survival outcomes with acceptable toxicity. It may serve as a feasible regimen in resource‐limited and urgent settings. Future efforts should prioritize TMA prevention and explore hybrid GVHD prophylaxis strategies (e.g., ATG/PTCy combinations, ATG dose reduction, and letermovir‐based CMV prophylaxis) to reduce transplantation‐related complications.
