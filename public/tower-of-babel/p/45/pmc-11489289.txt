Risk factors for postoperative complications in patients undergoing cytoreductive surgery combined with hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy: A meta-analysis and systematic review
Authors: Dengzhuo Chen, Yongli Ma, Jinghui Li, Liang Wen, Guosheng Zhang, Chengzhi Huang, Xueqing Yao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCytoreductive surgery (CRS) combined with hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) is one of the most effective treatments for intraperitoneal malignancies. However, current research on risk factors for postoperative complications needs to be more consistent. This study aimed to conduct a meta-analysis of the risk factors for postoperative complications in CRS + HIPEC patients.MethodsStudies meeting the inclusion criteria were screened by searching the Embase, PubMed, Cochrane and Web of Science databases. RevMan and STATA software were used to analyze the data extracted from the included articles.ResultsA total of 15 articles with 4021 patients were included in the meta-analysis. The results revealed that sex, elevated peritoneal cancer index, prolonged duration of surgery and smoking habits were risk factors for postoperative complications in CRS + HIPEC patients. In contrast, BMI, eGFR, age, history of preoperative chemotherapy, history of preoperative surgery, and history of neoadjuvant therapy had no significant effect on postoperative complications in the CRS + HIPEC group. The effects of diabetes, hypertension, preoperative albumin level, tumor location and chemotherapy regimen on the occurrence of complications need to be further investigated.ConclusionsWe identified several risk factors for postoperative complications after CRS + HIPEC, which should help clinicians minimize the incidence of postoperative complications and make more beneficial decisions for cancer patients who need treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00384-024-04741-5.Keywords: CRS, HIPEC, Meta-analysis, Postoperative complication, Risk factor

Introduction

Cytoreductive surgery (CRS) and hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) have been used as the first-line treatments for patients with primary peritoneal tumors or peritoneal metastases from other organs, such as the gastrointestinal tract and ovaries [1–5]. Cell reduction surgery is used to remove visible tumors, and hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) is used to kill or suppress remaining cancer cells in the body. This treatment has greatly improved the prognosis and survival of patients [6, 7]. In patients with peritoneal metastases, it is the only treatment strategy that has been shown to significantly improve 5-year survival [8, 9]. In addition, studies have shown that preventive cytoreductive surgery (CRS) and hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) also have good effects on high-risk patients with indeterminate peritoneal metastases [10]. However, patients who received CRS + HIPEC were more likely to experience grade 3 or higher adverse events compared with those who received CRS alone (34 of 131 patients [26%] vs. 20 of 130 patients [15%]; p = 0.035) [13]. In addition, this radical treatment has high morbidity and mortality rates, there are still many controversies and doubts regarding the treatment in different countries, and not all people can undergo this treatment [10–12]. Although CRS + HIPEC improves patient prognosis, it is also associated with dangerous complications; therefore, special attention should be given to the occurrence of postoperative complications, and measures should be taken to prevent them.

Common postoperative complications of CRS and HIPEC are classified into infectious and noninfectious complications, including abdominal infection, pancreatitis, anastomotic leakage, deep vein thrombosis, and acute kidney injury [14]. These complications are often associated with radical resection of peritoneal and abdominal organs and the toxicity of chemotherapeutic drugs. Due to the surgical safety and efficacy of CRS combined with HIPEC, CRS is widely used in clinical practice for patients with peritoneal metastases [2]. However, with the increase in the number of patients undergoing surgery, reports of postoperative complications are increasing every year. In recent years, the risk factors after CRS + HIPEC surgery have been extensively analyzed and described, mainly through univariate and multifactorial analyses. It has been reported that the postoperative complications of CRS + HIPEC are related to sex, age, BMI, PCI, operative duration, whether preoperative chemotherapy is performed, and the chemotherapy regimen, but the specific mechanism is still unexplained. Although the risk factors related to postoperative complications of CRS + HIPEC have been reported in numerous studies, the sample size is relatively small, and the results of some risk factor studies are controversial [16, 18–23]. For example, Akash et al. and Tayfun et al. reported that smoking was strongly associated with the development of postoperative complications in CRS + HIPEC patients [15, 28]. Naciye et al. reported that smoking was not a risk factor for postoperative complications [25]. In addition, a study by Akash et al. revealed that sex was also a risk factor for postoperative complications [15]. In contrast, sex was not strongly associated with risk factors in the studies by Amihai et al. and Konstantions et al. [16, 21]. In addition, some studies have shown that intraoperative blood loss, intraoperative blood transfusion, and previous surgical history are risk factors, although most of the literature does not consider them as risk factors.

Therefore, the aims of our study were the following: (1) to further clarify the risk factors for postoperative complications of CRS + HIPEC by aggregating the results of published studies for meta-analysis and (2) to encourage clinicians to make clinical decisions that are more beneficial to patients.

Materials and methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines and has been registered with PROSPERO (registration number CRD42024506573). The PRISMA checklist for this meta-analysis can be found in Table S1 in the attached file.

Search strategy

Two researchers systematically searched the PubMed, Embase, Cochrane and Web of Science databases for literature related to risk factors for postoperative complications of CRS + HIPEC (up to 20 January 2024). To ensure that the search results were as complete and scientific as possible, we used the following search terms: cytoreductive surgery, cytoreductive surgical procedures, debulking surgical procedures, hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy, HIPEC, intraperitoneal hyperthermic chemotherapy, intraperitoneal hyperthermic chemotherapies, risk factors, and complications (the search strategy can be viewed in the supplementary file).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All studies included in this meta-analysis met the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) original English articles; (2) studies related to the occurrence of postoperative complications of HIPEC + CRS or the relationship between complications and prognosis; (3) randomized controlled trials (RCTs), case control studies or cohort studies; and (4) studies that described definitions of CRS and HIPEC and the complete baseline data of patients.

The exclusion criteria included the following: (1) meeting reports, systematic reviews, letters, meeting abstracts, case reports and animal studies; (2) studies with poor quality, incomplete and unreliable data; and (3) incomplete articles and full texts that were not available.

Data collection and analysis

Two researchers independently screened the articles and extracted the data. By reading the title and abstract, they excluded studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria. After the preliminary screening, they read the full text one by one and finally selected all the included literature. If there were differences or disagreements between the two investigators, a third investigator (a specialist in gastrointestinal surgery) was added to the discussion to help make a decision. Baseline data on the population, postoperative complications, treatment options and prognosis were extracted via specially designed standard tables.

Bias and quality assessment

The modified Newcastle‒Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used to assess the bias and quality of the included studies, with an NOS rating of 9 stars: 1 to 3 stars as low quality; 4 to 6 as medium quality; and 7 to 9 as high quality. The NOS quality scores for the articles included in this study can be found in the appendix (Table S2).

Preoperative and intraoperative factors

The risk factors for postoperative complications in CRS + HIPEC patients include preoperative and intraoperative factors. Preoperative factors included age, sex, BMI, smoking history, diabetes, hypertension, PCI, previous surgery and chemotherapy, and primary tumor origin.

Intraoperative risk factors include operative time, intraoperative blood transfusion and blood loss, chemotherapy regimen, abdominal temperature, lymph node metastasis, pathological diagnosis, and ascites volume.

Statistical analysis

Review Manager 5.4 and Stata 17 software were used to analyze the main outcome measures in all included articles. The odds ratio (OR) and 95% CI were used to summarize BMI, PCI, age, operative time, smoking history, operative history, chemotherapy history, sex and other data. The Cochrane Q test and I2 measures were used to assess the heterogeneity of the pooled data. If I2 ≥ 50% indicated significant heterogeneity, a random effects model was used. If I2 < 50%, heterogeneity was not significant, and the fixed effects model was used. Egger's test and Begg's funnel plot were used to assess publication bias in the included studies. A sensitivity analysis was performed for the primary outcomes, including only high-quality studies with NOS scores ≥ 6. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Given that the included studies were from different regions and countries, for sex as a potential risk factor, we divided the included studies into European, American and Asian groups for subgroup analysis to explore the influence of regional factors on the results.

Results

A total of 357 articles were retrieved from various databases via search strategies (Fig. 1). Endnote 20.0 software was used to search the titles and read the abstracts, initially excluding duplicates (n = 52) and papers that did not meet the inclusion criteria (n = 243). After preliminary screening, 62 papers were retained. After the full texts were read, 15 studies with a total of 4021 patients were ultimately included, including 12 retrospective studies and 3 prospective studies (Table 1). All included studies were of high quality, and the NOS frequency score was basically 7 or above, indicating that the information provided by the included papers was comprehensive and detailed (Table S2).

Sex

A total of 10 studies [15, 17–19, 22, 25–29] were included in the meta-analysis and analyzed via a fixed effects model (I2 = 0%, P = 0.84). The results revealed that the diamond shape after the combined effect size did not intersect the null line and that sex was one of the risk factors for postoperative complications. The difference between the complication group and the noncomplication group was statistically significant, with the proportion of women being greater than that of men (OR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.60–0.97, p = 0.03) (Fig. 2A).

Age

A total of 9 studies reported detailed age information, 5 of which [17, 18, 20, 27, 28] reported age information as the mean ± standard deviation. Continuous variables and random effects models (I2 = 99%, P < 0.001) were used for the meta-analysis. The results revealed a diamond shape after the combined effect size intersected the null line, and increasing age did not affect the occurrence of postoperative complications (MD = 10.47, 95% CI: -8.16–29.09, p = 0.27) (Fig. 2 B). Due to the high degree of heterogeneity, we excluded the included articles one by one and found that after excluding Eduarda et al.’s study, the heterogeneity tended to be stable (I2 = 0%, P = 0.52); however, the final result remained unchanged (MD = 0.52, 95% CI: -1.94–2.98, p = 0.68).

The other four studies [19, 22, 24, 26] reported age information of ≥ 60 years and < 60 years, which were analyzed via binary variables and a fixed effects model (I2 = 0%, P = 0.83). The results suggested that "age < 60 years" or "age > 60 years" was not a risk factor for postoperative complications (OR = 1.04, 95% CI = 0.76–1.42, p = 0.83) (Fig. 2C).

BMI

A total of five articles [17, 18, 20, 27–29] reported detailed BMI data and were tested for heterogeneity (I2 = 63%, P = 0.02). A random effects model revealed no significant difference between the two groups (MD = -0.30, 95% CI: -1.62–1.01, p = 0.65) (Fig. 2D).

Smoking

Smoking was assessed in three studies[15, 25, 28] with 590 participants. A heterogeneity test was performed (I2 = 0%, p = 0.42), and a fixed effects model was used for the meta-analysis. The results revealed that a history of smoking was a risk factor for postoperative complications, and the results were statistically significant (OR = 3.38, 95% CI: 2.07–5.53, p < 0.00001) (Fig. 2E).

Peritoneal cancer index

A total of four studies [17, 18, 22, 28] mentioned the Peritoneal Cancer Index (PCI). After the heterogeneity test (I2 = 0%, p = 0.82), a fixed effects model was used for summary analysis. The results revealed that the peritoneal cancer index was a statistically significant risk factor for postoperative complications (MD = 1.69, 95% CI: 0.18–3.20, p = 0.03) (Fig. 2F).

Other preoperative risk factors

Other preoperative risk factors included primary tumor site, previous surgery, previous chemotherapy and previous neoadjuvant therapy. We performed a summary analysis of these results, which are shown in Table 3.

Three of the studies[22, 25, 28] mentioned the patients' previous surgical history, and the heterogeneity test (I2 = 0%, P = 0.77) adopted by the fixed-effects model clearly revealed that previous surgical history did not increase the incidence of postoperative complications (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 0.71–1.51, p = 0.84) (Fig. 3A). Patient chemotherapy history was recorded in 6 studies[17–19, 22, 23, 25], and a heterogeneity test (I2 = 0%, P = 0.77) was adopted with a fixed effects model; the results were not statistically significant (OR = 1.23, 95% CI: 0.91–1.68, p = 0.18) (Fig. 3B). Neoadjuvant therapy was included in three trials, and the heterogeneity test (I2 = 0%, P = 0.94) revealed no statistical significance (OR = 1.20, 95% CI = 0.81–1.77, p = 0.37) (Fig. 3C). Currently, neoadjuvant therapy is not considered to have an effect on the incidence of postoperative complications.

A total of 11 studies reported the primary tumor sites of patients in detail, and the most common primary sites were colorectal cancer (27%) and the appendix (20.8%), followed by the ovary (18.9%), peritoneum (7.8%), and stomach (5.4%). An independent meta-analysis was performed for different tumor sites to investigate whether different primary tumor sites were risk factors for postoperative complications, and the results are shown in Table 3.

Intraoperative risk factors

The 15 included studies reported intraoperative risk factors such as operative time, intraoperative blood transfusion and blood loss, chemotherapy protocol, abdominal temperature, lymph node metastasis, pathological diagnosis, and abdominal water flow. A meta-analysis was performed on the studies with detailed data. Heterogeneity tests were performed to determine whether a fixed effects model or a random effects model should be used. Finally, a meta-analysis was performed on the two factors of operative time and chemotherapy regimens, among which the results of operative time were statistically significant; the results are shown in Table 3.

Operative time

Two studies recorded the operative time of patients. After the heterogeneity test (I2 = 80%, p = 0.03), a random effects model was applied, and the results revealed that operative time was one of the risk factors for postoperative complications. The difference between the complication and noncomplication groups was statistically significant (MD = 133.93, 95% CI: 51.43–216.43, p = 0.001) (Fig. 3D), and prolonged operative times increased the possibility of postoperative complications.

Chemotherapy regimens

A total of 3 studies reported patients' chemotherapy regimens, which were divided into mitomycin and cisplatin regimens, and we performed a meta-analysis of each. The results revealed that the heterogeneity of the mitomycin regimen (I2 = 70%, p = 0.07) was not statistically significant according to the random effects model (OR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.14–1.96, p = 0.33) (Fig. 3E). After the heterogeneity test of the cisplatin regimen (I2 = 88%, p = 0.0003), a random effects model was adopted, and the results revealed that there was no statistically significant difference between the complication and noncomplication groups (OR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.14–6.04, p = 0.94) (Fig. 3F). Therefore, currently, differences in chemotherapy regimens cannot be considered risk factors for postoperative complications.

Subgroup analysis

Given that the populations included were from different regions, we performed a subgroup analysis of sex factors to determine whether there was greater heterogeneity between different regions. The results revealed no significant difference between the European (p = 0.76) and American (p = 0.96) subgroups, and the results were not statistically significant. In the Asian subgroup (p = 0.02), the results suggested that the incidence of postoperative complications was greater among women than men and that sex was one of the risk factors for postoperative complications, which was consistent with the original conclusion (Appendix Fig. S2) based on both sides of the funnel plot (Fig. 4A). In addition, Egger’s test and Begg’s test were also conducted, and the results of Egger’s test were p = 0.846 (p > 0.05) (Fig. 4B, 4C), indicating that there was no significant publication bias in our included studies. We used the one-by-one exclusion method for sensitivity analysis of the age-included studies, and the results after one-by-one exclusion were all within the 95% CI of the effect size, indicating that the results of our meta-analysis were relatively stable (Fig. 4D). The results of the Begg test can be found in Appendix Fig. S3.

Discussion

The perioperative mortality of CRS combined with HIPEC is approximately 4%, while the incidence of common postoperative complications is as high as 40%[35–37]. In addition, studies have shown that high postoperative morbidity and mortality are highly correlated with the extent of CRS surgery [33]. CRS combined with HIPEC is currently the most recommended treatment method for abdominal tumors (primary or metastatic) [1, 30, 31], which has the ability to effectively cure and control the disease and can significantly improve the prognosis and survival of patients [32]. With the continuous improvement in CRS and HIPEC, the survival rate of patients with peritoneal cancer (primary or metastatic) has increased from 10% to more than 40–50% [40–42]. However, CRS has unavoidable problems, such as long operative times and greater physical trauma to patients. In addition, when patients undergo HIPEC, changes in abdominal temperature, fluid flow and drug infusion time may lead to changes in various biochemical indicators and vital signs, resulting in a series of complications [34]. In the course of CRS + HIPEC treatment, the patient experienced hyperthermic abdominal perfusion, invasive abdominal surgery, and the toxicity of chemotherapy drugs caused by pain. Therefore, to ensure the survival and prognosis of patients, the prevention and treatment of postoperative complications in CRS + HIPEC patients are particularly important. Our study aimed to explore the risk factors for postoperative complications associated with CRS + HIPEC and improve the survival prognosis of patients. In addition, the definition of complications in this study involves changes in the structure and function of tissues and organisms caused by various pathogenic factors, which cause abnormal pathological conditions in the body. These risk factors are precisely what can cause complications. Therefore, this study assessed the preoperative and intraoperative factors.

The results of the meta-analysis revealed that preoperative risk factors such as sex, smoking history, BMI and the PCI increased the incidence of postoperative complications (p < 0.05), whereas preoperative treatment methods such as age, preoperative chemotherapy, radiotherapy and neoadjuvant therapy did not affect the incidence of postoperative complications. However, the results of our study contradict the results of multiple articles, which may be caused by various confounding factors or may be related to different inclusion populations and sample sizes.

For example, in our study, sex was one of the risk factors for postoperative complications, and the incidence of postoperative complications among women was significantly greater than that among men, which is completely different from the results of Dusan et al. [17]. In addition, in the study of Eduarda et al. [18], the incidence rate among males was as high as 65.2%, which was significantly higher than that among females. Different results caught our attention. We conducted a meta-analysis by summarizing the data of the included studies to exclude various confounding factors and factors leading to bias as much as possible. For example, because the population included in the study came from all regions of the world, we classified the different regions for subgroup analysis. The results suggest that sex is not a risk factor in European and American populations, whereas it is in Asian populations. These differences may be due to differences in genetics, dietary habits, or local health care.

In our study, Akash[15] et al. reported that smoking was a risk factor for postoperative complications (p = 0.043), and Tayfun et al. [28] reported that smoking was closely related to the occurrence of postoperative complications (p < 0.001). A study by Simkens et al. [38] also revealed that a patient's smoking history before surgery was highly correlated with the occurrence of postoperative complications. Smoking changes the hormone secretion level, vascular status and neural activity of patients, so it is recommended that patients, especially male patients, quit smoking from 4–8 weeks before surgery to 4 weeks after surgery [39]. Therefore, our study revealed that patients' smoking habits before surgery lead to an increased incidence of postoperative complications, and smoking is an important risk factor.

Regarding BMI, our results revealed that obesity did not differ significantly between the complication and noncomplication groups (p > 0.05), but other studies have shown that obesity is a risk factor for surgical patients. Therefore, there is currently much controversy. As a public health problem that cannot be ignored, obesity has been widely recognized. A BMI > 30 kg/m2 is defined as obesity, which is associated with a variety of inflammatory and physiological changes, and these changes lead to a variety of diseases, such as hypertension, coronary heart disease, diabetes, and osteoarthritis [43]. Notably, obesity is also highly correlated with the occurrence of cancer [44, 45]. Given the occurrence of various comorbidities, patients with higher BMIs may face more negative surgical effects in the perioperative period. Some studies have shown that obesity increases the risk of complications in the perioperative period [46–48]. However, several articles examining the relationship between obesity and surgical outcomes have reached the opposite conclusion [49, 50]. For example, in the study of Makino et al. [51], through comprehensive retrieval and analysis of all articles on the relationship between obesity and postoperative complications from 1983 to 2010, a total of 33 studies met the criteria and concluded that there was no causal relationship between obesity and postoperative complications. Obese patients often have some preexisting underlying diseases, which affect the general condition of patients before surgery, and there may be differences in surgical methods and anatomical difficulties during surgery; however, there is no direct evidence that obesity has an impact on intraoperative bleeding, perioperative complications, and mortality. However, obese patients are often more prone to underlying diseases, which can lead to reduced tolerance after surgery. However, underweight patients are five times more likely to die after major surgery because of insufficient energy reserves and low albumin levels [52, 53]. Therefore, maintaining a healthy weight may be more conducive to the recovery of surgical patients after surgery.

The peritoneal cancer index (PCI) score proposed by Jacquet et al. [54] is currently the most widely used score prediction system. By evaluating the tumor burden of cancer patients, the feasibility of reducing the number of cells and the prognosis of patients can be assessed [55]. PCI scores are widely used to evaluate peritoneal metastases in gastrointestinal cancer, peritoneal mesothelioma, ovarian cancer, and primary peritoneal cancer. Laparoscopic surgery, open surgery, or imaging examinations are used to assign grades or add scores to assess the specific situation of a patient [56, 57]. The results of our meta-analysis suggested that PCI scores were significantly higher in the postoperative complication group than in the uncomplicated group, i.e., patients with higher preoperative PCI scores were more likely to have postoperative complications. In the study by Tayfun et al. [28], there was no significant difference in the PCI between the two groups. The different results may be due to different PCI scoring methods, errors in data recording, and insufficient sample sizes. However, many studies have shown that the PCI can be used to evaluate the preoperative risk index and postoperative prognosis of patients. Therefore, the PCI score should be routinely determined before surgery to evaluate the feasibility of surgery for patients with peritoneal metastatic cancer, and the prognosis of patients should be assessed by the PCI score.

In our study, we conducted a meta-analysis of two intraoperative risk factors, including the duration of surgery and the chemotherapy regimen used during surgery. There was a significant difference in the operative time between the two groups (p < 0.05), and the operative time in the complication group was significantly longer than that in the noncomplication group. With increasing operative time, the risk of postoperative complications also increases. A prolonged operative time leads to long-term exposure of various diseased organs, prolonged stretching and squeezing of tissues during resection of the diseased site, massive bleeding caused by invasive surgery, long-term use of anesthetic drugs, and long-term stress in patients, all of which lead to reduced tolerance of patients in the perioperative period. Many studies have reported that a long operative time is a risk factor for surgical complications; however, there is currently no unified quantitative standard, and ensuring that the operative time recorded in various studies is accurate is impossible. In addition, different surgical methods and different chemotherapy drugs also have different effects on the prognosis of patients, which has been clearly demonstrated by several studies [58, 59]. Therefore, medical staff should fully evaluate various physiological indicators of surgical patients before surgery, select the most appropriate surgical method according to the patient's condition to control the operative time, select the best chemotherapy program according to the patient's specific conditions, minimize damage to non-diseased tissues during surgery, and ensure the safety and stability of surgical incisions and sutures. Reasonable postoperative perioperative management is recommended to protect patients as much as possible from unnecessary complications after CRS + HIPEC. Moreover, there is an urgent need for scientific researchers and medical professionals worldwide to discuss and develop a uniform quantification of surgical time guidelines.

With the widespread use of ERAS in the clinic, a rapid rehabilitation surgical pathway during the perioperative period can improve the efficiency of functional rehabilitation of the body, shorten the length of hospital stay, and reduce postoperative adverse events. Therefore, whether the application of prehabilitation and ERAS can reduce complications after CRS + HIPEC also warrants further study by researchers. In addition, due to the lack of support from primary data, this study was unable to analyze the number of sutures and the number of anastomoses, which are two important prognostic factors. However, it has been reported in the relevant literature that there is no correlation between the number of anastomoses and the occurrence of AL (p = 0.401) [60]. In addition, it has also been mentioned in the conclusion of related literature that the possible prognostic variables of morbidity are the extent of peritoneal malignancy and the number of suture lines [61]. Therefore, whether these two common but important prognostic factors have an impact on the occurrence of postoperative complications needs to be further investigated, and this will be a new direction for our future research.

Limitations

To date, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to examine the risk factors for postoperative complications in CRS + HIPEC patients. However, there are several limitations in this study. (1) Among the 15 studies included, 12 were retrospective studies, and 3 were prospective studies; thus, the influence of bias effects on the results could not be excluded. (2) Some of the included studies did not describe the intraoperative chemotherapy regimen in detail, which may have resulted in significantly different results from the actual clinical results. (3) There is no uniform quantitative standard for the data included in the study, such as the PCI and operative time, and large subjectivity cannot guarantee scientific and rational data. (4) None of the included articles were graded for complications, and the inclusion of minor complications diluted the data.

Conclusion

The results of this study revealed that smoking, sex and the PCI were the preoperative risk factors for postoperative complications after CRS + HIPEC, whereas the operative time was an intraoperative risk factor. Clinicians can refer to the results of this study to develop personalized programs for patients' specific conditions before and during surgery to prevent complications. More large-sample, multicenter prospective studies and the development of uniform quantitative criteria should be conducted in the future to increase the reliability of the results to facilitate a more comprehensive study of postoperative risk factors.
