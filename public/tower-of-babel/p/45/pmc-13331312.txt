What's New in Emergencies Trauma and Shock - The Helicopter Paradox in Trauma Care: Does Faster Transport Necessarily Improve Survival?
Authors: Bikram Gupta, Alisha Chaudhury, Steve Kamm
Published: 2026

Trauma remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, accounting for nearly five million deaths annually and representing a substantial burden on healthcare systems, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).[1] The concept of the “golden hour” has profoundly influenced trauma care for decades, emphasizing the importance of rapid access to definitive treatment following injury.[2] Consequently, helicopter emergency medical services (HEMS) have become an integral component of many modern trauma systems, offering rapid transport, access to remote locations, and advanced prehospital interventions.[3]

Despite these theoretical advantages, the true survival benefit of HEMS remains controversial. In this issue of the journal, Anvari et al. present a systematic review and meta-analysis comparing HEMS and ground emergency medical services (GEMS) for trauma patients.[4] Their analysis included more than three million patients across 29 studies and reported that HEMS transport was associated with higher crude inhospital mortality compared with GEMS (odds ratio: 1.27, 95% confidence interval: 1.05–1.53). Interestingly, no significant differences were observed in day-1 or 30-day mortality. Furthermore, HEMS patients demonstrated significantly higher injury severity scores (ISS), and substantial regional variation was observed, with increased mortality associated with HEMS primarily in North American studies.[4]

At first glance, these findings seem counterintuitive. If helicopter transport provides faster access to trauma centers and advanced prehospital capabilities, why would mortality be higher? The answer lies in the complexity of trauma systems and the methodological challenges inherent in evaluating prehospital interventions.

THE CHALLENGE OF SELECTION BIAS

One of the most important considerations in interpreting HEMS literature is selection bias. Helicopters are rarely dispatched for average trauma patients. Instead, they are preferentially activated for patients with severe physiological derangement, major multisystem trauma, prolonged transport distances, difficult terrain, or anticipated need for specialized trauma services. Consequently, crude mortality comparisons may involve fundamentally different patient populations.

The current meta-analysis demonstrates this clearly. Patients transported by helicopter had significantly higher ISS scores than those transported by ground ambulance.[4] This finding suggests that HEMS is frequently utilized for patients at inherently greater risk of death. Therefore, higher crude mortality among HEMS patients may not reflect harm from helicopter transport but rather the underlying severity of injury.

Several previous investigations support this interpretation. Aiolfi et al. demonstrated higher crude mortality among HEMS-transported patients with severe traumatic brain injury; however, after adjustment for injury severity and patient characteristics, helicopter transport was associated with improved survival.[5] Similar observations were reported by Michaels et al. using a large national trauma database and by Beaumont et al. in a comparative cohort study from England.[67] These studies highlight the critical distinction between crude and risk-adjusted outcomes and underscore the importance of accounting for confounding factors when evaluating transport modalities.

SPEED ALONE DOES NOT DETERMINE OUTCOME

The assumption that faster transport necessarily improves survival may oversimplify trauma care. Although reducing delays remains important, outcomes depend on many factors beyond transport time alone. These include prehospital assessment, airway management, hemorrhage control, triage accuracy, crew expertise, receiving hospital capability, and the overall maturity of the trauma system.[8]

Indeed, several studies have suggested that the survival benefit associated with HEMS may be attributable less to the aircraft itself and more to the specialized personnel and advanced interventions that accompany helicopter transport.[3] Physician-staffed HEMS systems, common in parts of Europe and Asia, may provide advanced airway management, critical care interventions, and earlier specialist decision-making that influence outcomes independently of transport speed.[910]

The findings of Anvari et al., therefore, reinforce an important principle: helicopters should not be viewed merely as faster ambulances. Their effectiveness depends on how they are integrated into broader trauma systems and on whether they provide capabilities beyond transportation.[4]

THE SIGNIFICANCE OF HETEROGENEITY

Perhaps the most striking methodological observation in this review is the extraordinarily high heterogeneity among included studies, with I2 values approaching 99%.[4] Such heterogeneity reflects substantial variation in patient populations, dispatch criteria, trauma mechanisms, crew composition, transport distances, trauma center accessibility, and healthcare infrastructure.

This heterogeneity limits the ability to draw universal conclusions regarding the superiority of one transport modality over another. Rather than indicating that HEMS is ineffective, the findings may suggest that the benefit of helicopter transport is highly context dependent.

For example, helicopter activation criteria vary markedly across countries and even within the same country. Some systems reserve HEMS for the most severely injured patients, whereas others use broader dispatch criteria. Crew composition also varies considerably, from paramedic-based teams to physician-led critical care units. These differences inevitably affect outcomes and complicate direct comparisons.

Consequently, the question may not be whether helicopter transport is superior to ground transport, but rather under what circumstances helicopter transport provides meaningful clinical benefit.

LESSONS FROM REGIONAL DIFFERENCES

The regional analysis presented by Anvari et al. offers important insights into this question. While North American studies demonstrated increased crude mortality among HEMS patients, studies from Asia, Europe, and the Middle East did not show significant differences.[4]

These findings likely reflect differences in trauma-system organization rather than differences in transport modality itself. In Japan, for example, physician-staffed helicopter services are closely integrated into organized regional trauma systems. Nationwide studies have demonstrated improved outcomes among selected patients with major trauma and traumatic brain injury transported by helicopter.[1112] Similarly, European physician-led HEMS programs have reported survival benefits among severely injured patients.[13]

The contrast between these systems and certain North American models suggests that organizational structure, crew expertise, and patient selection may be more important determinants of success than aircraft availability alone.

THE COST-EFFECTIVENESS QUESTION

The debate surrounding HEMS cannot ignore economic considerations. Helicopter transport is one of the most resource-intensive components of trauma care. Individual flights may cost tens of thousands of dollars, and concerns about overtriage and unnecessary utilization remain widespread.[1415]

Cost-effectiveness analyses have yielded inconsistent results, largely because outcomes vary with patient selection and trauma-system characteristics. Consequently, indiscriminate expansion of HEMS programs may not be the most efficient use of limited healthcare resources.

This issue is especially relevant in LMICs, where investments in trauma care often compete with other pressing healthcare priorities. For many healthcare systems, strengthening ground ambulance services, improving access to trauma centers, enhancing triage protocols, and developing integrated trauma networks may yield greater population-level benefits than the widespread deployment of helicopters.

IMPLICATIONS FOR INDIA AND OTHER LOW- AND MIDDLE-INCOME COUNTRIES

The findings of this meta-analysis are particularly relevant to countries such as India, where trauma remains a major public health challenge. Geographic diversity, variable emergency medical services infrastructure, traffic congestion, and disparities in access to trauma centers all complicate prehospital care.

While HEMS may play an important role in remote areas, mountainous terrain, disaster response, and interfacility transfers of critically injured patients, its widespread implementation is unlikely to be feasible or cost-effective in many regions. Instead, policymakers should focus on strengthening trauma systems as a whole to ensure that patients receive timely access to appropriate care, regardless of transport modality.

The ultimate objective is not to maximize helicopter utilization but to optimize trauma-system performance.

CONCLUSION

The systematic review by Anvari et al. contributes thoughtful insights and some evidence to the ongoing debate regarding when to “call for the helicopter.”[4] We have all known for some time that requesting a helicopter to transport a trauma patient requires deliberate thought and consideration of multiple factors. Certainly, ISS scores are important, but patient preference, weather, and funding also play important roles. If there is little difference in time saved when calculating the transport times between GEMS and HEMS, what is the point, other than dramatically increased costs and putting the patient and crew at significant risk? It is certainly a known occurrence to read in the news about trauma helicopters crashing, taking the lives of all on board. Was this HEMS transport really necessary compared to GEMS?

In rural areas and areas underserved by legitimate trauma centers, HEMS certainly plays a role in bringing the patient quickly to a center where they have the best chance of survival and morbidity reduction. Differences in morbidity and mortality can certainly be attributed to HEMS flight time and the level of expertise in caring for the patient during the HEMS flight. As noted, it would be rare indeed to have a physician in a helicopter in the USA, whereas in other countries it is the norm. The right patient, transported by the right team to the right facility based on the originating and accepting hospital physicians, after robust collaboration over the phone, is typically the most important factor in ensuring we are neither overutilizing nor underutilizing HEMS. In many cases, and not entirely clear, this confounding factor was considered in the study, which is how weather can take an obvious case of a necessary HEMS transport and relegate it to GEMS. In other words, the subjectiveness of the weather and the pilot always outweighs the objective desires of the physicians. Is the helicopter pilot in one case more risk-averse than in another?

Future research should ideally be prospective and double-blinded, although this might be ethically challenging, especially in the United States. If one considers the cost involved per year of life saved, whether the transport was HEMS or GEMS, that would be another way to evaluate. However, it is difficult to assign an agreed-upon value to human suffering, injury, or death.
