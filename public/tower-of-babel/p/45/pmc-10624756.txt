Continued Opioid Use and Adverse Events Following Provision of Opioids for Musculoskeletal Pain in the Emergency Department: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Qiuzhe Chen, Chris G. Maher, Christopher S. Han, Christina Abdel Shaheed, Chung-Wei Christine Lin, Eileen M. Rogan, Gustavo C. Machado
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prevalence of continued opioid use or serious adverse events (SAEs) following opioid therapy in the emergency department (ED) for musculoskeletal pain is unclear. The aim of this review was to examine the prevalence of continued opioid use and serious adverse events (SAEs) following the provision of opioids for musculoskeletal pain in the emergency department (ED) or at discharge.MethodsRecords were searched from MEDLINE, EMBASE and CINAHL from inception to 7 October 2022. We included randomised controlled trials and observational studies enrolling adult patients with musculoskeletal pain who were administered and/or prescribed opioids in the ED. Continued opioid use and opioid misuse data after day 4 since ED discharge were extracted. Adverse events were coded using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), and those rated as grades 3–4 (severe or life-threatening) and grade 5 (death) were considered SAEs. Risk of bias was assessed using the Quality in Prognosis Studies (QUIPS) tool.ResultsSeventy-two studies were included. Among opioid-naïve patients who received an opioid prescription, 6.8–7.0% reported recent opioid use at 3–12 months after discharge, 4.4% filled ≥ 5 opioid prescriptions and 3.1% filled > 90-day supply of opioids within 6 months. The prevalence of SAEs was 0.02% [95% confidence interval (CI) 0, 0.2%] in the ED and 0.1% (95% CI 0, 1.5%) within 2 days. One study observed 42.9% of patients misused opioids within 30 days after discharge.ConclusionsAround 7% of opioid-naïve patients with musculoskeletal pain receiving opioid therapy continue opioid use at 3–12 months after ED discharge. SAEs following ED administration of an opioid were uncommon; however, studies only monitored patients for 2 days.Protocol Registration10.31219/osf.io/w4z3u.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40265-023-01941-1.

Introduction

Musculoskeletal pain is a common reason for emergency department (ED) visits [1–3]. In the USA, musculoskeletal disorders were the principal reason for more than nine million ED visits in 2020 [1]. Evidence shows that non-opioid therapies, such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), are at least as effective as opioid therapy for some musculoskeletal pain conditions [4, 5]. Clinical practice guidelines [5–7] discourage routine use of opioid analgesics for the management of musculoskeletal pain. If opioid therapy is required for opioid-naïve patients with acute pain, the guidelines suggest that immediate-release opioids should be prescribed at the lowest effective dose for the shortest time possible.

Opioids are commonly used to manage musculoskeletal pain in the ED despite recommendations from clinical guidelines and research evidence for judicious use. For instance, among patients presenting to the ED in the USA with low back pain, over one-quarter represents patients with any musculoskeletal condition [1], with more than half of them prescribed and/or administered opioids [8, 9]. In Australian EDs, more than two-thirds of patients with low back pain are administered opioid analgesics [10–12]. The estimated percentage of opioid-naïve patients with low back pain prescribed an opioid in ED is about 85% in Canadian EDs [13] and 54% in the USA [14]. With tens of millions of patients visiting EDs worldwide with musculoskeletal pain each year, a large number of opioid-naïve patients have been unnecessarily exposed to opioid analgesics during the ED visit.

Opioid therapy for musculoskeletal pain carries potential for considerable risks. Opioid prescription initiated for acute musculoskeletal pain may lead to long-term opioid use, which increases the risk of serious harms, such as opioid use disorder and opioid overdose [5, 15, 16]. Opioid therapy may also cause adverse events affecting all body systems, ranging from vomiting and nausea to life-threatening serious adverse events (SAEs) such as fatal respiratory depression [5, 17, 18]. The risk of adverse events is significantly higher in people aged 60 years or above [19], and in those on medium- or long-term opioid therapy [20].

A recent systematic review of randomised controlled trials showed uncertain evidence of SAEs following the provision of opioid therapy for musculoskeletal pain in the ED or at discharge and found no trial examining long-term opioid use after discharge [21]. We therefore aimed to review both randomised controlled trials and observational studies to examine the prevalence of adverse outcomes, including continued opioid use, SAEs and opioid overdose, following the provision of opioid therapy in the ED or at discharge for the management of musculoskeletal pain.

Methods

This systematic review is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) [22]. The protocol of this review was uploaded to OSF Preprints on 28 September 2022 (preprint https://doi.org/10.31219/osf.io/w4z3u). Deviations from the protocol are summarised in eTable 1.

Data Sources and Searches

We searched in MEDLINE (via Ovid), EMBASE (via Ovid), and CINAHL (via EBSCO) from inception to 7 October 2022. The search strategy included three key domains: musculoskeletal pain and disorders, emergency care and opioid analgesics. Search strings were developed on the basis of terms used in previous systematic reviews [17, 23, 24]. Search terms used in MEDLINE via Ovid and search results are shown in eTable 2.

Study Selection

We included both randomised controlled trials and observational studies published in peer-reviewed journals. Eligible studies needed to enrol patients who were administered opioid analgesics in the ED and/or received an opioid prescription at discharge. Patients could be administered and/or prescribed opioid analgesic(s) alone or combined with a non-opioid medicine (e.g. oxycodone plus acetaminophen). Reviews, qualitative studies, grey literature, conference abstracts and protocols were excluded.

We included studies enrolling adult patients (≥ 18 years) presenting to the ED for musculoskeletal pain of any duration. Patients with or without previous or recent history of opioid use and current opioid users were all included. Studies evaluating musculoskeletal and other conditions were included if patients with musculoskeletal pain represented over 75% of the cohort or the results for patients with musculoskeletal pain were presented separately. We excluded studies in which patients received opioid analgesics for pregnancy- or cancer-related pain, examined local sedation and analgesia for an operative procedure in the ED (e.g. reduction for shoulder dislocation), or enrolled patients presenting to the ED for opioid-related adverse events or substance use disorder.

Outcome Measures

We considered studies evaluating continued use of any opioid analgesic beyond day 4 post ED discharge, which was likely to be a continuation of opioid administration in the ED or prescription provided at discharge [25]. We stratified the included definitions of continued opioid use into three categories: (i) any recent opioid use—defined by the use of any opioid analgesic or any opioid prescription filled within a short-term before or after the specific timepoint of follow-up; (ii) the total number of opioid prescriptions filled—defined by a certain number of opioid prescriptions filled in different phases of the follow-up period or a minimal number of prescriptions filled in total; and (iii) the duration of opioid supply—the cumulative amount of opioid use measured by the duration of opioid supply in the follow-up period.

Secondary outcomes were SAEs and opioid overdose. We included adverse events observed after opioid administration in the ED and/or prescription provided at discharge. Adverse events to opioid therapy during hospitalisation were excluded. According to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) [26], two reviewers (Q.C. and C.H.) independently rated all adverse events reported in included studies at grade 1–2 (i.e. mild or moderate), grade 3–4 (i.e. severe or life-threatening) and grade 5 (i.e. death). Grade 3–4 and grade 5 adverse events were designated as SAEs [27, 28]. A third reviewer (G.M.) was consulted to resolve disagreements.

Included adverse events were classified as grade 1–2 if information to determine severity was insufficient (e.g. only the total count of patients with any adverse event were reported). Adverse events which led to change of drug use or withdrawal were referred to as SAEs if they required hospitalisation or urgent medical treatment. To examine the consistency of SAEs rated on the basis of the CTCAE criteria versus the reporting of SAEs in included studies, we also extracted data on SAEs as defined in study methods and/or reported in the results of included studies. If the term SAEs was not used by the authors, synonyms referring to a similar definition were accepted, such as major, severe or significant adverse effects and life-threatening effect or complication.

Opioid overdose was measured by the excessive amount of opioid use at any time up to the timepoint of follow-up after opioid therapy in the ED or at discharge [29]. Studies measuring outcomes with a synonymous definition but referring to other terms, such as opioid misuse or opioid poisoning, were also included. Any fatal or non-fatal injury or events related to excessive opioid use will also be included if reported.

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

We developed a checklist based on eligibility criteria. Two reviewers (Q.C. and C.H.) independently screened titles and abstracts of all retrieved records and reviewed the full-text copy of potentially eligible records using Covidence (https://www.covidence.org); the reason for exclusion was recorded. We performed backward citation tracking of included studies to identify additional eligible studies. All disagreements were resolved by discussion to gain consensus.

We developed an Excel data extraction sheet guided by the PRISMA 2020 checklist [22] and extracted data on study design and setting, characteristics of patients, history of opioid therapy, musculoskeletal condition, length of follow-up, the number of patients who received opioid therapy and completed outcome measures, and opioid therapy in the ED and/or at discharge (including type of opioid analgesic, dose, route, form, amount of opioid supply and rescue opioid analgesic). For studies evaluating continued opioid use and opioid overdose, we extracted the definition of continued opioid use or opioid overdose and the number of patients who fulfilled the definition. For studies where any adverse event was reported, we extracted the total number of adverse events, the number of patients with each adverse event and the number of patients with SAEs (if reported). Two reviewers (Q.C. and C.H.) modified the data extraction sheet by discussion and independently extracted data from included studies, with conflicts resolved by consensus.

This review focussed on the clinical course of opioid therapy for musculoskeletal pain in the ED or at discharge; and therefore, we chose to use the Quality in Prognosis Studies (QUIPS) tool to assess the risk of bias of included studies. As neither prognostic nor confounding factors were investigated in this review, we adapted the QUIPS tool to four domains: selection bias, attrition bias, outcome measurement and bias related to the statistical analysis and presentation of results [30]. Each domain was assessed as being at low, moderate, high, or unsure risk of bias. Studies scored at high/unsure risk of bias in any two domains or high/unsure in any domain and moderate in any other two domains were scored as an overall high risk of bias. Two reviewers (Q.C. and C.H.) independently assessed risk of bias and resolved discrepancies by discussion.

Data Synthesis and Analysis

We used bubble charts to present the percentage of patients with continued opioid use at different lengths of follow-up. Data on continued opioid use defined by any recent opioid use, the total number of prescriptions filled and the duration of opioid supply were separately presented. The minimal number of prescriptions filled as defined in the study was also used to stratify data. Data from the same study but measured at multiple lengths of follow-up were independently analysed.

The observational period for SAEs after opioid administration was dichotomised as during ED visit (i.e. ≤ 6 h after opioid administration) and after discharge (i.e., > 6 h). Data on SAEs were analysed in four patient groups: (i) patients who were administered opioids in the ED and observed during ED visit; (ii) patients who were administered opioids in the ED and followed up after discharge; (iii) patients who received an opioid prescription at discharge; and (iv) patients who received both opioid administration and prescription. The total number of patients and SAEs reported in included studies were summed by four patient groups.

We used the random effects model of proportional meta-analysis to estimate the prevalence of SAEs, as graded according to the CTCAE criteria, in patients who were administered opioids in the ED. We calculated the sum of SAEs if the study monitored adverse events at multiple timepoints within 6 h after opioid administration, whereas data were separately analysed if the study monitored adverse events both within 6 h and after discharge. Subgroup meta-analysis stratified by study design was conducted if pooled studies included more than two cohort studies and two randomised trials. We also calculated the total number of SAEs as defined in study methods and/or specified in the results of included studies if reported. Data were analysed using RStudio (version 1.4).

Results

In total, we identified 6658 records from three databases and included 73 reports from 72 studies in the review (Fig. 1). Two reports [31, 32] used data from the same cohort study, and we chose to analyse the report [32] which were based on a larger sample. Of the 72 included studies, 13 [13, 14, 32–42] measured continued opioid use after ED discharge, 59 [33, 43–100] reported adverse events related to opioid therapy in the ED or after discharge, and one [101] investigated opioid misuse. Risk of Bias

Seven of the 72 included studies [56, 59, 60, 62, 71, 80, 87] were scored at a high overall risk of bias (eTable 3); all investigated opioid-related adverse events. More than one-third of included studies (25 studies) [35, 36, 45, 47, 51, 53, 56, 57, 59, 62, 63, 67, 71, 73, 75, 77, 80, 85, 87, 88, 90, 92, 94, 95, 101] were considered as having high or unsure risk of attribution bias due to the lack of reporting of loss to follow-up in randomised controlled trials or data completion rate not reported in observational studies.

Continued Opioid Use

All 13 studies [13, 14, 32–42] reported continued opioid use in patients who received an opioid prescription at ED discharge, and two [14, 35] of them also observed patients who were administered opioids during ED visit and those who received both (eTable 4). The 13 studies included one randomised trial [33] and 12 observational studies [13, 14, 32, 34–42]. Six studies [13, 33–37] defined continued opioid use by any recent opioid use, eight [13, 14, 32, 38–42] counted the total number of opioid prescriptions filled, and one [14] summed the duration of opioid supply. Two studies [13, 14] reported alternative definitions in two categories.

Any Recent Opioid Use

One study [36] observed 139 opioid-naïve patients with musculoskeletal pain who received an opioid prescription at ED discharge and found 74 patients continued using opioids on day 4 (53.2%) and 27 (19.4%) on day 9 post discharge (Fig. 2). Another study [35] reported that 22 out of 56 patients (39.3%) who were administered opioids in the ED and/or prescribed at discharge continued using opioids in the 4th week after discharge. At 3 months, one cohort study [37] found that 8.7% of patients (27 out of 309) used opioids in the last 2 weeks, whereas one randomised trial [33] in which 108 patients were provided a 10-day supply of opioids at ED discharge reported that two patients (1.9%) used opioids in the 3 days prior to the 3-month follow-up survey. Two studies [13, 34] reported the percentage of any recent opioid use after 6 months in opioid-naïve patients who received an opioid prescription at ED discharge. The prevalence was 6.8% at 6 months (359 out of 5252 patients, two studies) and 6.9% at 12 months (267 out of 3882 patients, one study). One of the four studies [34] also reported a seven times higher prevalence of recent opioid use at 6 months in previous or current opioid users (268 out of 1313 patients, 20.4%) compared with opioid-naïve patients (35 out of 1229 patients, 2.8%).Fig. 2Continued opioid use in opioid-naïve patients with musculoskeletal pain who received an opioid prescription at ED discharge. The Total Number of Prescriptions Filled

One study [32] found that 93.7% of patients (374 out of 399) who were prescribed an opioid at discharge had filled at least one opioid prescription by day 14, and another study [41] reported 80.9% (938 out of 1159 patients) by day 100. The pooled percentage of opioid-naïve patients who received an opioid prescription at discharge and filled ≥ 2 prescriptions in total after discharge (Fig. 3) was 8.0% on day 14 (32 out of 399 patients; one study) [32], 20.8% at 3 months (838 out of 4023 patients; one study) [13], and 17.8–25.9% at 6 months (1206 out of 6687 patients; two studies) [38, 42]. Within 6 months after ED discharge, 4.6–6.9% of patients (632 out of 10,486; two studies) had filled ≥ 3 opioid prescriptions [13, 38], 3.0% (191 out of 6463 patients; one study) filled ≥ 4 prescriptions [38], 1.4–6.1% (919 out of 20,965 patients; two studies) filled ≥ 5 prescriptions [14, 38], and 1.8% (4 out of 224 patients; one study) filled ≥ 6 prescriptions [42]. The Duration of Opioid Supply

One study reported that 445 out of 14,502 opioid-naïve patients (3.1%) with back pain who were administered an opioid in the ED and/or received an opioid prescription at discharge filled more than a 90-day opioid supply within 6 months after the index ED visit [14].

Adverse Events

Of the 59 included studies where adverse events were reported, 49 [33, 44–53, 55, 56, 58–61, 63–72, 74–79, 81, 82, 84–88, 90–93, 95, 96, 98–100] were randomised trials and 10 [43, 54, 57, 62, 73, 80, 83, 89, 94, 97] were observational studies (eTable 5). We identified a total of 108 unique types of adverse events (e.g. vomiting, dyspnoea, fatigue), including 90 rated as grade 1–2 (i.e. mild to moderate), 17 grade 3–4 (i.e. severe to life-threatening) and one grade 5 (i.e. death) (eTable 6).

In total, 47 studies monitored adverse events after opioid administration (Fig. 4). Forty-five studies [43–87] observed 3811 patients for ≤ 6 h (i.e. during the ED visit) and reported in total 1276 adverse events, including six SAEs. The six SAEs included one patient with hypoxia below 88% within 30 min of opioid administration [60], one patient with significant respiratory depression requiring naloxone in 1 h [70], one admission due to extended hypotension for 5 h [57], and four admissions related to severe dizziness, drowsiness and/or vomiting [79]. The pooled estimated rate of SAEs within 6 h was 0.02% [95% confidence interval (CI) 0, 0.2%]. Post hoc subgroup analyses stratified by study design (eFig. 1) showed an estimated rate of 0.02% (95% CI 0, 0.2%) in 38 randomised trials [44–53, 55, 56, 58–61, 63–72, 74–79, 81, 82, 84–87] and 0.03% (95% CI 0, 0.5%) in seven cohort studies [43, 54, 57, 62, 73, 80, 83]. Another four studies [56, 69, 92, 100] followed up 170 patients for 24–48 h after opioid administration (i.e. after discharge) and reported 67 adverse events. The only SAE identified was one patient who developed an acute state of confusion within 24 h [56]. Seven studies [33, 89–91, 97–99] monitored 966 patients in total who received opioid prescription at discharge for 1–30 days and reported 497 adverse events. Five studies [88, 93–96] enrolled 587 patients who received both opioid administration and prescription and reported 385 adverse events in a total at 4–9 days after discharge. No SAE was identified in these 12 studies according to the CTCAE criteria. Three out of 91 patients (3.3%) with low back pain who received opioid prescription at discharge re-visited the ED within 30 days for adverse events; but adverse events were not described in the study [89].

Of the 59 studies, four [61, 76, 82, 85] specified a list of examples of SAEs in the study methods, (eTable 7); none (569 patients) observed any patient with SAEs as defined. Among other studies where SAEs were not defined in the methods, 16 studies [43, 56, 57, 60, 63, 64, 66, 70, 79, 83, 87, 92, 93, 95, 97, 99] reported in the results a total of 19 out of 1555 patients who experienced SAEs. One study [93] followed up 59 patients for 1 week after opioid therapy in the ED and found 10 of them had experienced severe adverse drug events which precluded work or daily activities, were prolonged, led to drug discontinuation or required medical evaluation or treatment. Another study [97] reported that 3 out of 101 patients who received a prescription at ED discharge discontinued opioid analgesics within 14 days due to significant side effects, but no example of significant side effects was provided.

Opioid Misuse

One observational study [101] evaluating opioid misuse (eTable 8) reported that, among patients that received an opioid prescription at ED discharge, 9 out of 20 patients (45.0%) with back pain and 9 out of 22 patients (40.9%) with extremity pain either self-escalated the prescribed dose, used prescribed opioids not for pain management or obtained non-prescribed opioids at any time in 30 days post discharge. This study, however, did not measure any subsequent injury or other outcomes, such as opioid-related poisoning, hospitalisations or deaths.

Discussion

This review examined the prevalence of continued opioid use, SAEs and opioid misuse following the provision of opioid therapy for musculoskeletal pain in the ED or at discharge. The percentage of opioid-naïve patients who received an opioid prescription at discharge and had recent opioid use remained stable at around 7% from 3 to 12 months after discharge. There were 4.4% of opioid-naïve patients who filled ≥ 5 opioid prescriptions and 3.1% who filled an over 90-day supply of opioids in 6 months. The pooled estimate of SAEs following opioid administration in the ED was low at 0.02% (95% CI 0, 0.2%) during ED visit (i.e. within 6 h) and 0.1% (95% CI 0, 1.5%) within 2 days after discharge.

A strength of this study was that we included both randomised controlled trials and cohort studies. We used the CTCAE criteria to grade all included adverse events which standardised the wide variation in the classification of severity of adverse events used by included studies. We also classified the studies’ definitions of continued opioid use into three categories and analysed the prevalence by the length of follow-up. However, despite being categorised, definitions within each category still varied. The grading of included adverse events using the CTCAE criteria was at times limited by the lack of description of adverse events in included studies. Additionally, we extracted very limited data on excessive opioid use from only one study, and no data on opioid-related injury or events were identified. Although the study reported a high rate of opioid misuse (42.9%), the small sample size compromised its representativeness.

Continued opioid use for musculoskeletal conditions has been evaluated in previous reviews. A review [15] reported that 134 out of 786 patients with acute low back pain (17.0%) who filled the initial opioid prescription had ongoing opioid use beyond 3 months. The two included studies, however, defined long-term opioid use as receiving ≥ 5 opioid prescriptions between 30 and 730 days postonset and having medical bills for ≥ 3 opioid prescriptions, respectively. Other reviews [102–104] investigating continued opioid use after surgical operation for musculoskeletal disorders also included widely varying definitions, but none categorised extracted data by definitions before pooling and presenting the data. Past or current substance use disorder has been identified as a risk factor of prolonged opioid use after initial prescription for acute musculoskeletal pain [16], which were consistent with the significantly higher risk of continued opioid use we found in previous or current opioid users compared to opioid-naïve patients.

The heterogeneous and lax definitions of continued opioid use chosen by included studies are worth rethinking. Our review included 23 different definitions that varied by length of follow-up, the cumulative opioid supply, consistency of opioid use, and the number of prescriptions filled. This heterogeneity in definitions has also been raised in previous reviews [105, 106]. Evidence has shown that the duration and dose of opioid therapy in the first month are critical predictors of continued use for one year or longer [107, 108], but these parameters were not measured in any of our included studies. Instead, measurement of any recent opioid use at a specific timepoint and the total number of prescriptions filled may flag patients who had intermittent opioid use and were essentially at a low risk of harms [106]. This extended but intermittent opioid use may partially explain the estimate of only 4.4% of opioid-naïve patients having filled ≥ 5 prescriptions in total from discharge up to 6 months after, whereas around 7% still had recent opioid use at 6 months post discharge.

In contrast to previous reviews, we used the CTCAE criteria to re-classify all reported adverse events and defined the prevalence rate as the percentage of patients with SAEs among all included patients. The estimated prevalence of SAEs reported in previous reviews of randomised trials was 2.7% in patients with low back pain [17] and 6.3% in those with chronic non-cancer pain who received ≥ 2 weeks of opioid therapy [18]. Both reviews adopted the definition of SAEs used by included studies. To understand the relatively low prevalence of SAEs estimated by our review, we investigated the included studies which reported the highest prevalence of SAEs in each of the two previous reviews. One study [109] enrolled 328 patients receiving oxycodone and found that only 2 of the 11 reported SAEs were related to opioid analgesics. The two patients had an incident of dizziness and dehydration, respectively, which were not SAEs as rated by the CTCAE criteria. The other study [110] pooled the total number of SAEs (88 SAEs in 379 patients) rather than the total number of patients with any SAE (48 patients). Among the 48 patients with SAEs, 3 experienced events that were related to opioid analgesics and 2 of them experienced SAEs (confusional state, cholecystitis acute and cholelithiasis) that adhered to the CTCAE criteria (2 out of 379, 0.5%). In addition, using the standardised CTCAE criteria, our review was able to include and analyse studies where SAEs were not explicitly defined in study methods and/or reported in the results. Therefore, our comprehensive method of extracting and grading opioid-related SAEs suppressed the prevalence rate.

The low-estimated risk of opioid-related SAEs found in our review can also be attributed to the short length of follow-up after opioid therapy administration. Four opioid-related hospitalisations were reported in studies monitoring for 5–6 h after opioid administration, whereas 37 out of 47 included studies which measured adverse events after opioid administration only monitored patients for ≤ 2 h and might have overlooked opioid-related SAEs that occurred afterwards. Similarly, no included study measured any adverse events later than 1 month after receiving an opioid prescription at discharge, even though a significant percentage of patients continued using opioid analgesics and refilling prescriptions up to 12 months and had ongoing risk of opioid-related harms [20, 111]. None of the 13 included studies that reported on continued opioid use provided data on long-term SAEs or other opioid-related harms. There is a clear need for extending the length of follow-up to monitor SAEs that occurred after 2 h following opioid administration and to investigate harms associated with continued use of opioids for up to 12 months after opioid initiation in the ED.

Conclusions

This review reveals that around 7% of opioid-naïve patients with musculoskeletal pain who received opioid prescription at ED discharge continued using opioid analgesics 3–12 months after discharge. There were 4.4% of opioid-naïve patients who filled ≥ 5 prescriptions and over 3% who filled more than a 90-day supply of opioids in 6 months. SAEs immediately following opioid administration in the ED or within 2 days after discharge are rare. Definitions of continued opioid use varied significantly across included studies. Future studies should choose wisely the definition of continued opioid use following opioid initiation in the ED and extend the length of observational period.
