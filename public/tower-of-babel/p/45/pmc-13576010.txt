RESET as a Primary Care Redesign: Differences in Burnout, Fulfillment, and Workplace Experience in a Large Integrated System
Authors: Stacy Fletcher, Andrew T Muck, Holly Finertie, Mable Chan, Jeffrey Keyes, Amanjot S Sethi, Thea Giboney, Julie A Schmittdiel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBurnout among primary care physicians is widespread and associated with reduced quality of care. System-level interventions that simplify workflows, clarify priorities, and restore autonomy may help address contributors to burnout. RESET (Reward Efficiency. Set Priorities. Empower Teams.) is a physician-designed primary care redesign that links results with meaningful flexibility in how physicians practice. This study compared well-being and workplace perceptions between physicians participating in RESET and physicians in traditional practice models.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analyzed 2024 Joy and Meaning in Medicine and Physician Opinion Survey data from primary care physicians in a large integrated delivery system. Physicians were categorized into RESET or control groups, with RESET physicians further classified by duration on the program. Independent 2-sample t tests and analysis of variance tests compared mean scores across groups.ResultsRESET physicians reported significantly higher scores in 32 of the 35 survey questions (P < .05) across 5 survey domains of interest: including Joy and Meaning in Medicine, quality of care and patient care experience, health and wellness, implementing change, and overall practice experience. Physicians with longer duration on the RESET program demonstrated higher scores across all domains.ConclusionParticipation in RESET was associated with lower burnout and higher professional fulfillment, with the most favorable ratings among physicians with longer duration in RESET. These findings suggest that a system-level redesign that clarifies priorities, reduces operational friction, and restores autonomy may improve primary care physicians’ work experience. Longitudinal studies are warranted to evaluate effects on patient outcomes, utilization, and costs.

Introduction

Physician burnout has become a global crisis, particularly within primary care, the segment of the health system most closely linked to access, continuity, and long-term patient outcomes. Prevalence estimates a range from 13.5% to more than 60%, with most studies reporting rates above 50% and consistently higher among primary care physicians. 1–5 Contributors include excessive workload, administrative burden from electronic health records, staffing shortages, and a persistent misalignment between physicians’ professional values and organizational demands. 6–8 This misalignment, described by Agarwal and colleagues as professional dissonance, erodes the sense of purpose and identity central to medical practice. 1

The downstream consequences of burnout are substantial. Burnout is associated with lower quality of care, increased medical errors, and reduced patient satisfaction. 9 Physicians experiencing burnout demonstrate decreased productivity, higher turnover, and greater intent to leave practice entirely, worsening workforce shortages and undermining continuity of care. 10 Burnout also carries substantial financial consequences. The US health care system spends an estimated 4.6 billion dollars annually due to physician burnout, including nearly 1 billion dollars attributable to primary care physician turnover alone.¹⁰ Each 1-point increase in burnout on a validated 7-point scale corresponds to a 30% to 50% increase in the likelihood of reducing professional work effort within 2 years. 11 These findings underscore that burnout is not merely an individual wellness issue; it is a system-level problem with measurable impacts on access, quality, and affordability.

Evidence increasingly shows that organizational and system-level interventions are more effective than individual-level approaches in reducing burnout and improving professional well-being. 2,12–14 Workflow redesign, reductions in low-value administrative work, improved staffing and team functioning, and increased autonomy all demonstrate durable, meaningful improvements. Leadership behavior is also influential. Transparent communication, alignment of values, and attention to physician flexibility and autonomy are associated with higher engagement and lower burnout. 11,13

Despite this growing recognition of the need for system-level solutions, few large-scale redesigns have been rigorously evaluated within integrated health care systems. To address this gap, The Permanente Medical Group implemented RESET (Reward Efficiency. Set Priorities. Empower Teams.), a primary care redesign created to improve professional fulfillment, efficiency, and sustainability by linking measurable results with earned professional autonomy. RESET was designed to reduce inefficiency, restore clarity, and realign daily clinical work with physician purpose and organizational goals.

The Joy and Meaning in Medicine (JAMM) measure and the Physician Opinion Survey (POS), administered annually across The Permanente Medical Group, provided an opportunity to evaluate RESET at scale. These surveys assess drivers of burnout, fulfillment, leadership, teamwork, and perceived control over work. In 2024, RESET had been implemented across dozens of clinical sites, and survey data were available for both RESET and control physicians.

The goal of this study is to examine differences in drivers of burnout, professional fulfillment, and workplace experience between RESET and control physicians, using 2024 JAMM measure and POS data.

Methods

STUDY SETTING AND DESIGN

Kaiser Permanente Northern California is a large integrated health care delivery system serving more than 4.5 million members. This cross-sectional analysis included all primary care physicians in adult and family medicine (AFM) departments employed by The Permanente Medical Group as of November 2024. Physicians in residency or with panel sizes < 100 or > 3000 were excluded. Physicians who initiated RESET on or before the 2024 survey administration date were assigned to the RESET group, while all others were assigned to the control group. The RESET group was further divided into early implementation and sustained implementation based on whether the physician had reached “Summit” (program completion) at the time of the survey.

EXPOSURE: THE RESET PROGRAM

RESET is a physician-designed model of care that redefines the relationship between leadership and frontline clinicians. It functions as a mutual handshake: frontline physicians commit to delivering measurable results, and leaders commit to granting meaningful autonomy. RESET rewards efficiency with flexibility, sets clear priorities through a transparent scorecard, and empowers teams by streamlining workflows and removing operational friction.

RESET aligns everyone around key outcomes that matter most in primary care: access, quality, affordability, and patient satisfaction. When teams achieve results, they earn greater control over scheduling, panel management, and daily practice design, supported by reliable data, peer comparison, and coaching. Management shifts from oversight to partnership, offering leaders reliability and physicians greater professional control. RESET thus operates both as a care delivery model and as a behavioral operating system that aligns physician, patient, and organizational success.

Central to RESET is the frontline-facing RESET Scorecard, which distills hundreds of competing priorities into a balanced set of measures and countermeasures that define high performance. Its design draws on behavioral science, including principles of autonomy, mastery, and purpose popularized by Daniel Pink’s Drive, 15 and incorporates clear-goal frameworks shown to increase engagement in organizational psychology research. The STEP (Strategic Targets that Elevate Performance) Logic methodology ensures goals rise gradually with improvement, supporting sustainable progress.

FOUNDATION FOR RESET

Traditional health care models often rely on compliance, standardization, and external pressure, all of which have been associated with reduced autonomy, diminished engagement, and higher burnout among physicians. 8 RESET reverses this pattern by replacing control with clarity and micromanagement with measurable freedom. The model activates intrinsic drivers of performance, such as autonomy, mastery, purpose, and connection, consistent with self-determination theory 16 and motivation science.

RESET promotes transparent data, simplified expectations, and psychological safety. Physicians gain visibility into their performance, receive coaching that strengthens practice habits, and operate in an environment where testing and iteration are encouraged. The earned-autonomy structure creates a self-reinforcing loop in which success generates additional flexibility and engagement. Balanced measures and countermeasures protect against gaming or unintended consequences, aligning individual, patient, and organizational success and creating the “win win win” at the center of the model.

PROGRAM STRUCTURE AND IMPLEMENTATION

RESET is structured around stable physician–medical assistant dyads that jointly own their patient panels. The dyads are grouped into small teams called “islands,” which train together, build shared habits, and support one another through coordinated coverage and peer learning while maintaining individual accountability for their own panels. These micro–practice units operate with the agility, ownership mindset, and accountability of private practice while benefiting from the infrastructure of a large integrated system.

Implementation follows a structured curriculum known as The Climb. Teams begin at Kickoff, learning foundational demand-management and access strategies. At Basecamp, they integrate quality, affordability, and patient experience measures. Upon meeting scorecard expectations consistently, teams advance to Summit, unlocking additional flexibility and rewards. Across program history, physicians require an average of 297.4 days (9.8 months) to reach Summit.

RESET implementation intentionally incorporates play, celebration, and peer mentorship based on research indicating that fun and positive social connection can improve engagement, resilience, and team cohesion. Faculty known as Implementation Leads guide teams through change management, storytelling, and hands-on support. RESET is an opt-in model, and more than 93% of eligible physicians and medical assistants have chosen to participate since 2020.

RESET’s spread follows a standardized implementation with clearly defined nonnegotiables that safeguard fidelity and trust. Consistency across sites protects the core design from local variation that can dilute impact, a phenomenon widely described in implementation science literature. RESET continues to evolve through close partnership with frontline teams and leaders, with updates made thoughtfully and transparently to preserve balance, protect integrity, and maintain trust.

CORE DESIGN PRINCIPLES

The RESET model is grounded in 7 behavioral design principles that guide implementation and sustain results:

1. Trust as the Operating Currency

RESET begins with the belief that physicians want to do meaningful work and will meet high expectations when supported and respected.

2. Transparency With Psychological Safety

Data visibility promotes learning and shared accountability only because psychological safety is established first.

3. Clarity Through Simplicity

RESET focuses attention on the measures that frontline teams can meaningfully influence. When everything is important, nothing is; clarity becomes the discipline that sustains focus.

4. Behavioral Design for Sustainable Performance

STEP Logic, countermeasures, and earned rewards make progress self-reinforcing and balanced.

5. Earned Autonomy as the Reward for Mastery

Physicians who achieve results gain meaningful control over how they practice.

6. Joy Through Play and Community

Humor, celebration, and belonging are intentionally woven into the experience, consistent with research showing that positive emotion strengthens engagement and learning.

7. Fidelity Over Local Variation

RESET scales through disciplined implementation with defined nonnegotiables that protect integrity and outcomes.

Together, these principles form a disciplined, trust-based system that brings out the best in teams. RESET began with a simple question: can high performance and high satisfaction coexist at scale? Historically, these were treated as opposing forces, but RESET has shown they can rise together.

STUDY OUTCOMES: JAMM MEASURE AND POS SURVEYS

Physicians’ professional satisfaction and workplace well-being have meaningful implications for quality of care, clinician retention, and the long-term stability of health systems. For this reason, supporting physician well-being has been a longstanding strategic priority for The Permanente Medical Group. Although organizational resources and structural initiatives are essential, sustained improvement requires systematic identification of day-to-day factors that shape physicians’ work experience. To guide these efforts, The Permanente Medical Group uses 2 complementary survey instruments: the JAMM measure and the POS.

The JAMM measure is a 15-item instrument scored on a 5-level Likert scale from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” Items assess core determinants of professional fulfillment, well-being, and burnout, including camaraderie, clerical burden, psychological safety, recognition, and workload. The instrument was heuristically developed following extensive literature review and stakeholder consultation and is designed to provide actionable insights for operational leaders. Since 2019, it has been administered annually to all The Permanente Medical Group physicians, generating more than 37,000 responses. JAMM results are reviewed by senior leadership and used to inform both clinic-level improvement efforts and broader organizational strategies. Neither the JAMM measure nor the POS has been published previously in the peer-reviewed literature; however, both instruments have been used for many years within The Permanente Medical Group for strategic assessment, operational planning, and evaluation of physician experience.

The POS is a 32-item instrument with 3 open-response questions, sponsored by The Permanente Medical Group Board of Directors and administered annually to all career-track physicians. Although individual items have evolved over its more than 25-year history, the survey consistently measures perceptions of organizational performance, professional satisfaction, and the practice environment. POS results provide executive leadership with a strategic, system-level view of physician perspectives, complementing the more operational insights captured by the JAMM measure.

Both JAMM and POS surveys were administered between November 11 and December 15, 2024. Survey responses were included for any AFM physician who completed at least 1 question on either instrument during the survey window.

Across both surveys, 50 total question-level responses were available for analysis (see Supplemental Table S1). For this study, the authors focused on 5 survey domains (35 total items and 3 free responses) hypothesized to be most sensitive to RESET participation:

COVARIATES

Physician demographic and practice characteristics were obtained from The Permanente Medical Group’s human resources database. Age, sex, race and ethnicity, physician degree, and tenure with Kaiser Permanente Northern California were defined as of November 11, 2024. Risk-adjusted panel size was calculated using an internally validated regression model that predicts monthly AFM utilization for all adult Kaiser Permanente Northern California members and is used operationally to support panel management and staffing strategy. Panel size values for this analysis reflect the most recent calculation available before survey administration (November 1, 2024).

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Physician characteristics by survey response status and RESET participation were summarized as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and as means with standard deviations for continuous variables. Differences between groups were assessed using χ2 tests for categorical variables and 2-sample t tests for continuous variables.

For each survey question, unadjusted mean scores were calculated by RESET status, and differences were evaluated using 2-sample t tests or analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate. Survey questions were then aggregated into 5 predefined topic domains (JAMM measure, quality of care and patient care experience, health and wellness, implementing change, and overall practice experience), and mean domain scores were compared across 3 groups: control, early implementation, and sustained implementation. The authors used ANOVA to assess overall group differences and applied Tukey’s honestly significant difference tests for pairwise comparisons. 17 As a sensitivity analysis, the authors ran linear regression models for all survey outcomes, adjusting for risk-adjusted panel size and RESET status. Because the analysis involved multiple hypothesis testing across 35 individual survey questions, statistical significance was evaluated using both a conventional alpha threshold of 0.05 and the sequential Holm–Bonferroni correction to reduce the possibility of Type I error. 18

Qualitative analysis of open-ended responses was performed by first identifying RESET-related comments using Microsoft Copilot, followed by manual review to categorize remarks as positive, neutral, or negative. When possible, comments were mapped to the 5 primary survey domains.

The Kaiser Permanente Northern California Research Determination Committee reviewed this study and determined the project does not meet the regulatory definition of research involving human participants per 45 CFR 46.102(d) (July 3, 2025). This cross-sectional study follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1.

Among RESET physicians, 287 (45.4%) provided at least 1 open-ended comment. Of these, 119 (41.5%) mentioned RESET directly. Most RESET-related comments (81.5%) were positive, fewer than 5 comments (3.4%) were negative, and the remainder were neutral. Positive themes included transformation, improved balance, enhanced teamwork, and support for day-to-day practice. Among the positive comments that could be mapped to survey domains, 22.7% related to overall practice experience, 16.5% to quality of care and patient experience, 11.3% to implementing change, 11.3% to JAMM, and 6.2% to health and wellness; 32% did not map cleanly to a single domain.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional analysis of more than 1100 primary care physicians in a large integrated health care system, participation in RESET was associated with significantly higher scores across nearly all measures of professional well-being, perceived influence over work, teamwork, openness to change, and overall practice experience when compared with physicians practicing in traditional models of care. These findings add empirical support to the growing evidence that system-level redesigns, rather than individual-focused wellness strategies, offer the greatest potential to address burnout and enhance professional fulfillment in primary care. 2,12–14

Notably, RESET physicians reported higher satisfaction despite having substantially larger risk-adjusted panel sizes than their peers, a pattern that contrasts with conventional associations between rising workload and lower well-being. 10 This suggests that the benefits observed are not attributable to workload reduction but instead reflect differences in work design, culture, autonomy, and clarity. Physicians who had completed the program (“sustained implementers”) reported the highest scores across nearly all domains, indicating that familiarity with and maturity within the model may further strengthen its impact on professional satisfaction.

These results align with well-established behavioral and organizational theory. RESET’s structure operationalizes several elements shown to promote engagement and reduce burnout, including autonomy, clear expectations, psychological safety, mastery, and purpose. 15,16,19 Traditional health care models often rely on compliance-oriented processes and top-down oversight, which can contribute to disengagement by eroding agency and professional identity. 1,8,11 In contrast, RESET’s design replaces prescriptive control with transparent goals, balanced measurement, and earned autonomy, enabling physicians to practice with greater clarity, ownership, and alignment with professional values.

The small team “island” structure may also help shape these outcomes. Prior research has demonstrated that psychological safety, meaningful relationships, and team cohesion enhance engagement and contribute to lower burnout. 13 RESET’s emphasis on trust, partnership, and shared accountability, in combination with its intentionally positive and community-building implementation strategy, may help create these conditions. This interpretation is supported by qualitative findings: among physicians who mentioned RESET in open-ended survey comments, more than 80% expressed positive sentiments, often describing improved balance, a greater sense of control, and enhanced practice experience.

The program’s high rate of voluntary adoption (more than 93% of eligible primary care physicians and medical assistants) suggests strong perceived value among frontline teams. Voluntary participation, combined with a standardized implementation structure and clearly defined nonnegotiables, likely helped protect fidelity and contributed to consistent outcomes across diverse practice settings. The finding that sustained implementers scored highest further supports the interpretation that duration on the model itself, rather than baseline enthusiasm, is contributing to improved professional experience.

Although RESET was not designed primarily as a burnout intervention, its alignment with known drivers of fulfillment and engagement positions it as a promising system-level redesign that can complement existing wellness initiatives. Its significant associations with JAMM domains, quality and patient experience, health and wellness, implementing change, and overall practice experience suggest that the model influences well-being and may also support operational reliability and readiness for change, 2 factors critical to high-performing health systems.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, RESET is a voluntary program, and physicians who choose to participate may differ in motivation, openness to change, or engagement compared with those who do not enroll. This introduces the possibility of selection bias. However, this concern is reduced by the high voluntary participation rate, which exceeds 90%, and by the finding that physicians who had completed the program reported higher scores than those still in earlier stages. These patterns suggest that the experience of the program itself is contributing to the observed improvements.

Second, RESET physicians had substantially larger risk-adjusted panel sizes than control physicians. Larger panels are typically associated with higher burnout and lower satisfaction, which strengthens the likelihood that RESET’s structural and cultural features, rather than baseline workload differences, explain the higher scores seen in the program group. Nonetheless, unmeasured differences in clinical practice or team dynamics could contribute to the associations observed.

Third, the analysis relies on cross-sectional survey data, which limits the ability to infer causal relationships. The findings describe associations between RESET participation and measures of well-being, but they cannot determine the direction or magnitude of causal effects. Future longitudinal analyses will be needed to evaluate trajectories of well-being before, during, and after RESET implementation.

Fourth, JAMM and POS are internal organizational surveys rather than externally validated research instruments. Although both surveys have been used for many years within The Permanente Medical Group for operational and strategic assessment, the absence of prior peer-reviewed validation may limit direct comparability with other research settings.

Finally, qualitative interpretations from open-ended comments may not capture the full range of physician experiences. Comments reflect the views of those who elected to respond, and positive or negative sentiments may be overrepresented.

Conclusions

RESET is a primary care redesign associated with significantly higher levels of professional fulfillment, perceived influence over work, teamwork, and overall practice experience among primary care physicians. These results suggest that system-level redesigns that emphasize clarity, psychological safety, balanced measurement, and earned autonomy may offer a promising approach to improving physician well-being at scale. Although RESET was not created as a burnout intervention, its alignment with known drivers of engagement and satisfaction positions it as a potentially valuable strategy for health systems seeking to strengthen workforce stability and performance.

Future research should examine whether improvements in professional experience translate into downstream effects on patient outcomes, access, utilization, and cost. Longitudinal and mixed-methods studies will be important to evaluate the durability of RESET’s effects and to identify which components most strongly predict sustained well-being. Although RESET has been implemented only in primary care to date, exploration of its principles in additional clinical specialties may provide insights into adaptability and scalability across a broader range of practice environments.
