The brain structure and immunometabolic mechanisms underlying the association between frailty and dementia
Authors: Wenjuan Chen, Jiahao Min, Penghao Xu, Chenjie Xu, Wei Chen, Zhi Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysical frailty, characterized by heightened sensitivity to stressors and decreased physiological reserves, is an established risk factor for incident dementia among older adults. However, the biological mechanisms underlying this association remain incompletely elucidated. This study aimed to dissect the potential roles of systemic inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and accelerated brain aging in the association between physical frailty and dementia.MethodsThis study included 452,516 participants from the UK Biobank who were free of dementia at baseline and had data available for all five components of frailty, with the majority also providing inflammatory, metabolic, neuroimaging and genetic information. Cox proportional hazard models were utilized to examine the longitudinal association between frailty and dementia. Causal mediation analyses were performed to quantify the potential mediating effects of composite scores for inflammation and metabolism, as well as accelerated brain aging (quantified as the brain age gap) on the observed frailty-dementia association.ResultsOver an average follow-up period of 13.7 years, 7,777 individuals were diagnosed with dementia. Compared to non-frail individuals, frailty status was significantly associated with an increased dementia risk (adjusted hazard ratio 2.21; 95% confidence interval 2.02–2.42), with an interaction effect between frailty and polygenetic risk score (P-value = 0.033). Inflammation and metabolic composite scores were separately constructed using weighted combinations of relevant biomarkers. Mediation analyses revealed that the inflammatory score and metabolic score significantly mediated 4% and 13% of the frailty-dementia association, respectively. Moreover, accelerated brain aging, estimated using machine learning applied to neuroimaging data, mediated a substantial proportion (20%) of the association between frailty and dementia.ConclusionThe positive association between frailty and dementia is mediated through multiple biological mechanisms pathways, including inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and accelerated brain aging. These findings underscore the importance of frailty-oriented preventive strategies against dementia.Keywords: Frailty, Dementia, Inflammation, Metabolism, Brain aging

Introduction

Dementia is the leading cause of disability and death worldwide, accounting for 36.3 million disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) and 1.9 million deaths in 2021 [1]. The Lancet Commission’s 2024 report suggested that approximately 45% of dementia cases could be prevented or delayed by addressing a variety of modifiable risk factors [2], and physical frailty has been identified as one of the significant risk factors for the development of dementia [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Growing evidence has thoroughly established the significant associations of frailty status with cognitive impairments and a higher risk of dementia [[7], [8], [9]]. However, research investigating the underlying mechanisms remain scarce. Elucidating how frailty contributes to dementia onset could enhance understanding of the involved pathophysiology and inform preventive strategies.

Potential mechanisms underlying the nexus between physical frailty and dementia include chronic inflammation, metabolic dysfunction, and structural brain changes. A meta-analysis revealed that frailty is associated with elevated levels of inflammatory markers (e.g., leukocytes, tissue necrosis factor alpha [TNF-α], and serum C-reactive protein [CRP]) [10,11], which are also established risk factors for cognitive decline and incident dementia [12]. Regarding metabolic factors, a metabolomics study identified 15 out of 131 metabolites related to antioxidation, muscle or nitrogen metabolism, and amino acids as frailty markers [13]. Specific metabolites may serve as potential intermediaries linking frailty and dementia. But limited research has directly examined whether these factors mediate the relationship between frailty and dementia. Additionally, existing evidence showed that brain structural changes are key characteristics in patients with both status of physical frailty and dementia, probably suggesting a shared structural basis underlie both conditions [14,15]. However, most previous studies have assessed these associations from a single aspect, such as specific brain structures, rather than adopting a comprehensive perspective.

In this study, leveraging data from the UK Biobank, we aimed to dissect the biological and neurological mechanisms underlying the association between physical frailty and dementia (Fig. 1). We first evaluated the longitudinal associations between physical frailty and dementia and the interaction effect of genetic predisposition and frailty on dementia risk. Furthermore, we separately explored the extent to which inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and accelerated brain aging mediate the association between frailty and dementia.

Overview of the study.

Methods

Study design and population

The present study utilized data from the UK Biobank, a large-scale prospective cohort encompassing over 500,000 participants aged 37–73 years at baseline assessments conducted between 2006 and 2010 across 22 England, Scotland, and Wales centers. The UK Biobank collected multi-dimensional information on participants through a series of interviews and questionnaires covering lifestyle factors and health conditions, physical measurements, biological samples, brain imaging, and genotyping. All participants provided informed consent, and the study received ethical approval from the North West Multicenter Research Ethics Committee (Reference number: 21/NW/0157). We first included 501,753 participants with available frailty information and without pre-existing dementia at baseline. Then, participants with any missing data of covariates were excluded, leaving 452,516 individuals in the primary analyses. For subsequent mechanism analyses, complete datasets were used to examine the specific associations and underlying mechanisms between frailty and dementia. The Supplementary Fig. S1 presented the flowchart of the study and the sample size utilized in each analysis.

Frailty assessment

We assessed the physical frailty using five criteria: weight loss, exhaustion, low physical activity, slow walking speed, and low grip strength, which was originally described by Fried et al. [16]. To fit the available data within the UK Biobank, modified definitions of these criteria were adopted in this study. Specifically, the definitions for weight loss, exhaustion, slow walking speed, and low grip strength were based on the work of Hanlon and colleagues [5]. For the component of low physical activity, we used the data from the International Physical Activity Questionnaire short form, following the definition created by Fanny and colleagues [7]. Detailed information on these criteria could be found in Supplementary Table S1. Each frailty criterion was treated as a binary variable in the analyses for the associations between individual frailty components and dementia risk. Consistent with previous literature, we classified participants as non-frailty if they did not encounter any criteria, pre-frailty if they met one or two criteria, and frailty if they met three or more criteria. In the primary analysis, participants with missing data for all five frailty criteria were excluded. Considering that classifying frailty status as missing only when all five frailty components were absent could introduce potential bias, we performed a sensitivity analysis. In this analysis, the variable of frailty status was considered as missing if data for any of single component was absent.

Dementia diagnoses

We used the datasets of “first occurrences” (category ID: 1712) in the UK Biobank which included data from self-report, hospital admissions and death registries to identify participants with dementia. Specifically, dementia cases were defined as all-cause dementia, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), vascular dementia, dementia classified as a medical condition elsewhere, and unspecified dementia (Supplementary Table S2). Follow-up assessments for participants spanned from the baseline assessment date until the time of dementia diagnosis, death, or the end of the follow-up period (April 1, 2023), whichever occurred first.

Polygenic risk score for dementia

We utilized a pre-existing, validated polygenic risk score (PRS) for dementia (Field ID: 26206) from the Standard PRS set in the UK Biobank [17,18]. This set, comprising calculations across all individuals in the UK Biobank for 28 diseases and 8 quantitative traits, was developed by a meta-analysis of multiple external genome-wide association study (GWAS) sources by Thompson et al. [17]. Detailed information can be found online [17]. Participants were assigned to a polygenic risk group based on their PRS, categorizing them into low (first tertile), intermediate (second tertile), and high (third tertile) polygenic risk levels.

Neuroimaging data and brain age prediction

The UK Biobank employed a standard Siemens Skyra 3T scanner with a standard Siemens 32-channel RF receive head coil to obtain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data at a resolution of 1 × 1 × 1 mm and a field of view of 208 × 256 × 256. Detailed imaging protocols are described elsewhere (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/showcase/docs/brain_mri.pdf). This study utilized volumetric data for white matter, grey matter, and hippocampus from processed T1-weighted images, along with white matter hyperintensity (WMH) volumes derived from T1 and T2-FLAIR data (Field IDs: 25007, 25005, 25781, 25019, and 25020).

Furthermore, the brain age gap (BAG) was calculated for use in subsequent mediation analysis. Firstly, the brain age was predicted for each participant using a Light Gradient-Boosting Machine (LightGBM) algorithm [19]. This model was trained on valid T1-weighted neuroimaging data from 45,719 participants, utilizing 1425 distinct imaging-derived phenotypes (IDPs) as features. We then computed the BAG by subtracting chronological age from predicted brain age, where a higher value indicates a greater degree of accelerated brain aging. Detailed methods concerning feature selection, model development, performance assessment, and interpretation are available in our prior work [20]. The subsequent analyses involving these neuroimaging features and BAG were conducted on data from 41,732 of the 452,516 total participants included in the main study.

Inflammatory markers

Data on inflammatory markers were obtained from blood count (category ID: 100081) and blood biochemistry (category ID: 17518). Detailed descriptions of the haematology data processing and biochemistry assay procedures are available at https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/ukb/docs/haematology.pdf and https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/ukb/docs/biomarker_issues.pdf, respectively. For this study, we selected baseline data on neutrophil, monocyte, lymphocytes counts and percentage in leukocytes, total leukocyte counts, platelet counts, as well as CRP levels, all selected as indicators of inflammation. Data for these inflammatory marker analyses were available 419,427 of the 452,516 included participants.

NMR metabolic markers

Plasma samples were collected at baseline (2006–2010), while the analysis for plasma metabolite profiling was conducted at two distinct phases: phase 1 from June 2019 to April 2020, and phase 2 from April 2020 to June 2022. NMR metabolic biomarkers (category ID: 220) were measured in randomly selected EDTA plasma samples using a high-throughput NMR-based metabolic biomarker profiling platform developed by Nightingale Health Ltd. Further information can be found at https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/label.cgi?id=220. This study encompassed a total of 247 metabolic markers, and categorized into 16 classes such as “amino acids”, “apolipoproteins”, “cholesterol”, and “inflammation” for subsequent analyses. NMR metabolic marker data were available for 229,196 of the study participants.

Covariates

Covariates in this study consisted of age (in years), sex (female or male), ethnicity (white or others), Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI, an area-based measure of socioeconomic deprivation) [21], education attainment (college/university or others), body mass index (BMI), smoking status (never, former, or current), alcohol intake frequency (daily or almost daily, three or four times a week, once or twice a week, one to three times a month, special occasions only, or never), diet score [22,23], sleep score [24], histories of cardiovascular disease (CVD), diabetes, and cancer (yes and no). Additionally, the APOE ε4 allele carrier status (yes or no), a well-established risk factor for the development of AD, was also included. All covariate data were collected at baseline.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive characteristics of participants were presented based on their frailty and dementia status as mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables, and number and percentage for categorical variables.

We examined the longitudinal association of frailty status, genetic risk categories, and the combination of genetic risk categories and frailty status (grouped into nine categories with low genetic risk and non-frailty as reference) with the incident risk of dementia through Cox proportional regression models. The association between each of the five components of the frailty phenotype and dementia incidence were assessed by separately including individual one component of frailty as exposure variable in the models. Furthermore, we incorporated restricted cubic splines (RCS) to explore the potential non-linear association between frailty score and dementia. The results of these survival analyses are presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All models were adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, BMI, TDI, education attainment, sleep score, diet score, smoking status, alcohol consumption frequency, and histories of CVD, diabetes, and cancer, as well as APOE ε4 allele carrier status. Participants with any missing data on exposures, outcomes, or covariates were omitted from the analyses. To minimize the potential impact of reverse causality, we conducted lagged sensitivity analyses by excluding participants who developed dementia within the first 3 and 5 years of follow-up, respectively.

Linear regression models were employed to evaluate the associations of inflammatory markers and neuroimaging features (volumes of white matter, grey matter, hippocampus, and WMH) with the frailty score, controlling for the same covariates used in the survival analyses. The variables of inflammatory markers and neuroimaging features were standardized before analyses. Based on the beta coefficients for inflammatory markers significantly associated with frailty, a composite inflammatory score was constructed by multiplying each significant coefficient by its corresponding value of marker for subsequent mediation analyses. Additionally, to validate the robustness of the inflammatory score, we calculated the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) as the ratio of neutrophil to lymphocyte counts, and the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), calculated as the product of platelet and neutrophil counts divided by the lymphocyte count [25,26].

Metabolomic data were standardized using Z-score transformation prior to analysis. To identify metabolites and metabolic signatures associated with frailty score, a set of 247 candidate metabolites were firstly put in the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) linear regression model [27,28]. The aforementioned covariates were always included in the model as unpenalized terms to control for confounding. The optimum lambda parameter was determined via 10-fold cross-validation (CV), selecting the largest lambda value within one standard error of the minimum CV error to yield a parsimonious model. Then, we calculated a metabolic score by summing the products of the LASSO derived beta-coefficients and the corresponding standardized metabolites values, representing the combined effects of the selected metabolites. The LASSO analyses were undertaken using the “glmnet” package in R.

Mediation effects of the inflammatory score, metabolic score, and BAG on the association between the frailty score and dementia risk were evaluated within a counterfactual framework using the “CMAverse” package in R [29]. Specifically, a regression-based approach was used to compute direct, indirect, and total effect and to estimate the proportion mediated by each mediator. The same covariates as previously described were included in these models. The 95% CIs were calculated using bootstrapping with 1000 replicates. Sensitivity analyses for the inflammatory mediation were conducted by substituting the composite inflammatory score with the established NLR and SII within the same framework.

All the analyses were conducted using STATA 16 statistical software (Stata Corp LLP, college station, TX) and R software (version 4.1.3). The statistical significance was set as P < 0.05 (two-sided test).

Results

Descriptive observations showed that those identified with pre-frailty or frailty demonstrated a higher tendency to be older and female, current smokers, and were more likely to have lower education attainment, a lower sleep score, and more instances of CVD, diabetes, and cancer. The association between physical frailty and dementia

During a mean 13.7-year follow-up period (SD: 2.1 years), 7,777 individuals were diagnosed with dementia. This study found significant associations between individual frailty components, frailty score, frailty status, and combinations with PRS and dementia risk. In terms of each frailty component, low physical activity, weight loss, low grip strength, exhaustion, and slow walking speed were separately associated with the risk of dementia (Fig. 2a). Multivariable-adjusted RCS regression models showed a significant linear association between increased frailty score and the dementia risk (P for non-linearity = 0.414) with an HR of 1.28 (1.25–1.31) per increment in score (Fig. 2d). For frailty status, individuals with pre-frailty (HR: 1.34 [95% CI: 1.28–1.41]) and frailty (2.21 [95% CI: 2.02–2.42]) exhibited a higher risk of dementia compared to non-frailty individuals (Fig. 2b). A monotonic increase in dementia risk was observed for the combined categories of genetic risk for dementia and frailty status (Fig. 2c). Specifically, compared to those with non-frailty and the lowest tertile of PRS, participants with frailty and high PRS (HR: 4.09 [95% CI: 3.54–4.73]) were associated with the most significant adverse effect on dementia. Moreover, a statistically significant interaction was found between PRS and frailty status in relation to dementia risk (P for interaction = 0.033), which suggested that higher levels of genetic risk may amplify the association between frailty status and dementia risk. The additional analysis, which individuals with complete data on all components of frailty, concurred with the primary analysis, with an HR of 1.29 (1.21–1.36) for pre-frailty individuals and an HR of 2.23 (2.01–2.48) for frailty individuals (Supplementary Table S4). After excluding dementia cases occurring within the first 3 and 5 years of follow-up, the association between frailty and dementia risk remains consistent (Supplementary Table S8).

The mediating effect of inflammation on frailty-dementia association

Linear regression analysis revealed that eight out of nine inflammatory markers, except for lymphocyte count, were significantly associated with the frailty score (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table S5). Most of these markers showed a positive association with the frailty score compared to their lowest levels, with CRP exhibiting the strongest effect (beta [95% CI] = 0.058 [0.055, 0.061]). Conversely, higher percentages of monocytes and lymphocytes were associated with lower frailty scores. In the mediation analysis, an inflammatory score derived from these eight significant markers mediated only 4% of the association between frailty and dementia (Table 2). Regarding the decomposed effects, the HR (95% CI) for the direct, indirect, and the total effects were 3.27 (2.91, 3.65), 1.03 (1.02, 1.04), and 3.36 (2.98, 3.73), respectively.

The mediating effect of metabolic dysregulation on frailty-dementia association

To identify metabolic signatures of frailty, we applied LASSO-linear regression models to 247 metabolites. The results indicated that 46 metabolic markers were associated with frailty score (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Table S6). Of these metabolites, 21 markers exhibited positive correlations with the frailty score, with glycoprotein acetyls, sphingomyelins, phospholipids to total lipids in very large VLDL percentage, free cholesterol to total lipids in very small VLDL percentage, and isoleucine being the top five significant markers. In addition, linoleic acid to total fatty acids percentage showed the strongest negative association with frailty. The metabolic score for frailty was calculated as the weighted sum of the 46 selected metabolites, using weights corresponding to the coefficients from the LASSO regression. Mediation analysis showed that the metabolic score significantly mediated 13% of the association between frailty and dementia. The HRs (95% CI) for the direct, indirect, and total effects were 3.26 (2.74, 3.84), 1.11 (1.07, 1.14), and 3.60 (3.04, 4.24), respectively (Table 2).

The mediating effect of neuroimaging features on frailty-dementia association

After multivariable adjustment, a higher frailty score was associated with greater white matter hyperintensity volume (beta = 0.018, P = 0.002; Fig. 3b and Supplementary Table S7) and smaller hippocampus volume (beta = −0.036, P < 0.001). Following these findings, we subsequently examined whether the association between frailty and dementia was mediated by brain age gap (BAG). Using data from 41,732 participants, we found that BAG mediated a substantial proportion (20%) of the association between frailty and dementia, evidenced by a significant indirect effect (HR = 1.16 [1.08, 1.27]). The HRs (95% CI) of the total effect and direct effect of frailty on dementia did not reach statistical significance, potentially due to the limited number of dementia cases and sample size (106/41,732).

Discussion

Using large-scale and multidimensional data, this study systematically investigated the associations between physical frailty and incident dementia, along with the potential underlying mechanism. Our findings demonstrate a significant positive association between higher levels of frailty and increased dementia risk. Specifically, an interaction effect was identified between frailty and the polygenetic risk for dementia. Furthermore, we found that inflammatory markers, metabolites, and brain aging significantly mediated the observed frailty-dementia association, with estimated proportions ranging from 4% to 20%. Collectively, these findings highlight the contribution of inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and neurological mechanisms to the link between physical frailty and dementia.

Our study identified significant associations between frailty and multiple systemic inflammatory markers. Notably, the CRP demonstrated the strongest correlation, which has also been discovered with the incidence of dementia [30]. The observed positive associations between leukocyte counts (including white blood cell, neutrophil, and monocyte) and frailty score are consistent with the concept of inflammaging, defined as the chronic physiological stimulation of the innate immune system that occurs in later life [31]. Further analysis revealed that a composite inflammatory score, derived from these significantly associated markers, exerted a statistically significant mediating effects on the association between frailty and dementia, although the proportion of mediation was small (4%). Previous studies have reported that peripheral proinflammatory markers, such as IL-6, are frequently elevated in frailty populations [11,32] and the important role of inflammation in the pathogenesis of dementia [33]. It should be noted that these studies solely focused on the relationship of inflammation with frailty status or the risk of dementia, and found several inflammatory markers to be shared contributing factors between frailty and dementia. However, our study provided empirical evidence for inflammation as a partial mediator connecting frailty to dementia, which underscored that more attention should be paid on inflammatory processes in clinical practice for individuals with frailty status.

The investigation on the potential impact of metabolism indicated that metabolic dysregulation could be potential mediators underlying the linkage between frailty and dementia. Based on LASSO linear regression, we identified 46 metabolites significantly associated with frailty score. These metabolites were predominantly enriched pathways pertaining to lipid, lipoprotein, and amino acid metabolism. Notably, several of these markers, including leucine, isoleucine, and creatine, were overlapped with a previous study [13]. Among the positively associated metabolites, glycoprotein acetyls — an NMR-derived composite marker reflecting the glycosylation of circulating acute-phase proteins — exhibited the strongest association with frailty, and has been linked to systemic chronic inflammation and increased risk of a wide spectrum of incident diseases [34]. Isoleucine, a branched-chain amino acid, has been implicated in insulin resistance and may contribute to neurodegeneration, as Mendelian randomization evidence suggests a causal link between genetically elevated isoleucine levels and increased Alzheimer's disease risk [35]. Conversely, linoleic acid showed the strongest negative association with frailty. As an essential polyunsaturated fatty acid with anti-inflammatory properties, lower linoleic acid levels have been prospectively associated with increased dementia risk [36,37]. The mediation analysis revealed that the metabolic score accounted for a significant proportion (13%) of the observed association between frailty and dementia. According to previous findings, pervasive metabolic dysregulation in metabolism such as lipid and glucose metabolism have been linked to both frailty and dementia, suggesting a shared metabolic foundation [38,39]. By quantifying metabolic alterations through the derived metabolic score, we captured the overall metabolic dysfunction for participants and evidenced the critical role of metabolic signature in linking frailty and dementia.

The current study demonstrated that elevated frailty score was significantly associated with increased WMH volume and reduced hippocampal volume, consistent with prior evidence highlighting the detrimental effects of frailty on brain structures [40,41]. This consistent evidence underscored the potential involvement of frailty in accelerated brain aging to increase the risk of dementia [42,43]. In addition to the link between frailty and brain structures investigated in this study, prior research has extensively explored the association of certain brain structures with dementia risk given the change of brain structures might contribute to the development of dementia [[44], [45], [46]]. For example, a study of MR analysis has revealed suggestive causal links between decreased surface area of the temporal pole and decreased thickness of cuneus and a higher Alzheimer’s disease risk [44]. Further, investigation in a cohort study has shown that larger cerebrospinal fluid volumes and the presence of white matter lesions were associated with an increased dementia risk [45]. Consequently, these neurostructural alterations are considered a plausible mechanism underlying the association between frailty and dementia [47]. These observations necessitate targeted investigations elucidating the mediating role of global brain health in the frailty-dementia relationship. In the current analysis, we found that the BAG mediated 20% of the association between frailty and dementia risk, providing support for the hypothesis that accelerated brain aging constitutes a critical mechanism in the nexus between frailty and dementia. Given the limited dementia cases in our neuroimaging analysis, further research with greater sample size is needed to validate this finding.

Several limitations should be taken into consideration. First, four of the five components used to define frailty phenotype were derived from self-reported data rather than objective physical measurements, raising concerns about recall bias and participant misclassification. Second, frailty, mediators, and all covariates were collected at baseline. The concurrent measurement of frailty and mediators limits our ability to definitively establish the temporal sequence strictly required for causal mediation, although our lagged analyses suggest that the main frailty-dementia association is robust against reverse causality. Furthermore, reliance on a single baseline assessment could introduce temporal bias due to the fluctuations of certain covariates over time; however, the Supplementary Fig. S2 suggests that the impact of this bias is minimal for most covariates. Furthermore, the possibility of residual or unmeasured confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. Third, inflammatory markers involved in the mediation analyses were limited to those available in the UK Biobank, which does not include key pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 or TNF-α. More comprehensive analyses on a complete parameter set of inflammation in the future is needed. Fourth, the study may suffer from a “healthy volunteer” selection bias, as participants from the UK Biobank are likely to have higher socio-demographic status and healthier lifestyles compared to the general UK population [48]. Future research involving more representative and diverse community-based cohorts is warranted to validate these results. Fifth, the neuroimaging subset was generally healthier than the main cohort, with fewer dementia cases, also reflecting the healthy volunteer effect (Supplementary Table S10). This limited statistical power may explain the non-significant total and direct effects in this subgroup, and the brain aging mediation estimate should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the current study facilitated a thorough investigation of the potential mechanism, involving inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and accelerated brain aging underlying the association between physical frailty and dementia. These findings not only underscore the potential role of frailty on progression of dementia but also offer novel insights into preventive strategies against dementia.
