Gonioscopy-assisted transluminal trabeculotomy with phacoemulsification in high myopia versus non-high myopia with open-angle glaucoma—A comparative study
Authors: Devendra Maheshwari, Nimrita Gyanchand Nagdev, Madhavi R Pillai, Ramarghya Chakraborty, Mohammed Sithiq Uduman, Syril Dorairaj, Ramakrishnan Rengappa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To compare the safety and efficacy of gonioscopy-assisted transluminal trabeculotomy (GATT) combined with phacoemulsification in eyes with high myopia versus non-high myopia with open-angle glaucoma.Methods:This retrospective comparative study included 76 eyes with open-angle glaucoma. Eyes were stratified into high myopia (Group 1; axial length ≥26.5 mm, n = 36) and non-high myopia (Group 2; axial length <26.5 mm, n = 40). Baseline parameters including intraocular pressure (IOP), best-corrected visual acuity, and number of anti-glaucoma medications (AGMs) were compared at 2 weeks, 1 month, and 3, 6, and 12 months. Surgical success was defined as IOP ≤21, ≤18, or ≤15 mmHg with a ≥20%, ≥25%, or ≥30% reduction from baseline, respectively. Postoperative complications, secondary interventions, and surgical risk factors were analyzed.Results:The mean (SD) IOP (mmHg) decreased from 20.56 ± 4.71 to 13.32 ± 5.02 in Group 1 and 19.33 ± 4.15 to 12.94 ± 3.48 in Group 2 at 12 months, with no significant difference between groups (P = 0.92). AGM reduced from 2.11 ± 0.89 to 0.36 ± 0.95 in Group 1 and 1.98 ± 0.89 to 0.06 ± 0.24 in Group 2 at 12 months (P = 0.07). There was no significant difference in success rate between groups by all criteria at the end of 12 months (P = 0.77, 0.54, 0.14). Transient hyphema and IOP spikes were common complications. Five eyes required anterior chamber wash (3 in Group 1, 2 in Group 2).Conclusion:GATT with phacoemulsification is a safe and effective procedure in high myopia with open-angle glaucoma, with comparable outcomes to non-high myopia eyes at 12 months.

Myopia is a well-established risk factor for primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG).[1234] Several large population-based studies have shown that the prevalence of POAG increases as myopia progresses, with a stronger association observed in eyes with high myopia.[56789] The Blue Mountains Eye Study highlighted that the risk of glaucoma is doubled in patients with low myopia (−1.0 D to −3.0 D) and threefold in those with moderate to high myopia (−3.0 D or worse).[6] The Singapore Malay Eye Study and the Los Angeles Latino Eye Study reported that a higher incidence of glaucoma was associated with increasing axial myopia.[78] One meta-analysis found that the risk of glaucoma increased by about 20% with every 1.0 D rise in myopia, with a disproportionately greater risk in patients with extreme myopia, indicating a nonlinear association.[9] Myopic eyes have a thinner sclera and longer axial lengths, supporting the mechanical hypothesis.[10]

Trabeculectomy is the gold standard filtration procedure, but it is often associated with vision-threatening complications, especially hypotony-related problems in myopic patients due to the presence of thin sclera and low rigidity.[111213] Minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS) has become increasingly popular among glaucoma surgeons due to its favorable safety profile.[14] Gonioscopy-assisted transluminal trabeculotomy (GATT), one of the most economical and versatile MIGS, has shown promising outcomes in various spectrums of glaucoma.[15161718]

As high myopia alters the anatomy of the aqueous outflow pathway, its impact on the efficacy of GATT remains uncertain. Therefore, this study compares postoperative outcomes between high myopia and non-high myopia eyes with open-angle glaucoma.

However, the literature is sparse regarding the comparison of outcomes of MIGS in open-angle glaucoma in high myopia versus non-high myopia eyes.[192021]

Our study aims to evaluate the surgical outcomes of combined GATT with phacoemulsification in high myopia and non-high myopia with open-angle glaucoma.

Methods

This was a retrospective and comparative study which included patients who underwent combined GATT with phacoemulsification at the glaucoma department of a tertiary eye care center between June 1, 2023 and September 30, 2024. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written and informed consent was obtained from all patients before surgery.

All surgeries were performed by a single experienced surgeon (DM).

Patients with open-angle glaucoma, glaucomatous optic disc damage, and significant cataract and eyes classified as high myopia (axial length 26.5 mm and spherical equivalent -6 D) or non-high myopia (axial length <26.5 mm and spherical equivalent -6 D), with a minimum follow-up of 12 months, were included. Patients with a history of any prior ocular/refractive surgery, secondary open-angle glaucoma, and retinal degeneration and those who were on anticoagulants were excluded.

All preoperative details including IOP measurements by Goldmann applanation tonometry, best corrected visual acuity (BCVA), axial length measurement by Zeiss IOL master 700, undilated gonioscopy, dilated fundus examination by 90-D, indirect ophthalmoscopy for peripheral retinal degenerations, fundus photographs, optical coherence tomography by ZEISS CIRRUS 6000, and visual field analysis by Zeiss Humphrey visual field analyzer 863 (24-2 Swedish Interactive Threshold Algorithm) were retrieved from electronic medical records.

Surgical procedure

In all cases, phacoemulsification with implantation of foldable intraocular lens was performed first, followed by GATT. No intracameral miotics (pilocarpine or carbachol) were used intraoperatively. A 1 mm paracentesis incision was made supero-nasally or infero-nasally, depending on the laterality of the eye. After tilting the microscope 30 degrees toward the surgeon and rotating the patient’s head 30-35 degrees away from the surgeon, high-density viscoelastic (1.6% sodium hyaluronate, Aurogel plus-Aurolab, India) was used to dock a Swan Jacob lens over the cornea for viewing the nasal angle. With a 20-gauge microvitreoretinal knife (V-Lance Knife, Alcon Laboratories Inc, Texas, USA), a 1–2 mm incision was given at a nasal angle to expose the Schlemm’s canal. The tip of 6-0 polypropylene suture was blunted with the help of Bovie’s deluxe low-temperature cautery (Bovie Medical Corporation, FL, USA). It was then inserted via the goniotomy site into the Schlemm’s canal with 25-gauge Grieshaber MaxGrip microsurgical forceps (Alcon Grieshaber AG, Switzerland). The tip of the polypropylene suture appeared at the other site after circumnavigating the Schlemm’s canal (360 degrees). At the end of the procedure, 1.4% sodium hyaluronate (Aurogel, Aurolab, India) was injected into the anterior chamber (25-30%) to avoid blood reflux and reduce the incidence of postoperative hyphema.

Postoperative visits and surgical success

All patients were evaluated for IOP, BCVA, and AGM and for any complications or interventions at 2 weeks and 1, 3, 6, and 12 months.

The postoperative regimen consisted of a tapering dose of Prednisolone Acetate Ophthalmic Suspension 1% (Pred Forte, Allergan, India) eye drops for 4 weeks and ofloxacin 0.3% (Auroflox; Aurolab, India) eye drops four times a day for 10 days. Topical pilocarpine was given twice daily for 2 months.

Surgical success

The surgical success following combined GATT with phacoemulsification was defined by using three criteria. Complete success was defined as IOP >6 and ≤21 mmHg and more than 20% reduction (criteria 1), IOP >6 and ≤18 mmHg and more than 25% reduction (criteria 2), and IOP >6 and ≤15 mmHg and more than 30% reduction from baseline (criteria 3). Qualified success was defined as achieving treatment success along with antiglaucoma medication. Failure was defined as an inability to meet the success criteria or if an eye required any additional secondary intervention for IOP control.

Hyphema was classified as micro hyphema (layered or flared blood ≤1 mm) and macro hyphema (layered blood > 1 mm). IOP spikes were defined as an increase in IOP of 30 mmHg within a month following surgery.

Statistical analysis and sample size calculation

Statistical analysis was conducted using STATA statistical software (version 17.0, StataCorp, USA). The distribution of the continuous variables was assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Box–Whisker plots. Descriptive statistics for continuous variables were reported as mean and standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were summarized using frequencies (n), and percentages. Differences in demographic and baseline characteristics between study groups were evaluated with independent t-test for normally distributed data and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for skewed data. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using Chi-square test.

A mixed-effect regression model was used to compare postoperative measures of IOP, AGM, and BCVA with preoperative values over the follow-up period. In this model, the follow-up period was considered a fixed effect, and each patient’s identification number was treated as a random effect. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to visualize the cumulative probability of success between surgical procedures, and the log-rank test was used to compare the success curves between the groups. Cox-proportional hazards regression was used to identify the predictors of surgical failure, with the strength of association reported as a hazard ratio (HR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI). A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Complete 360-degree circumferential trabeculotomy using GATT was achieved in all cases, and no cases were abandoned due to poor intraoperative visibility or other factors.

In Groups 1 and 2, IOP spikes were noted in 5 and 4 eyes, respectively. Micro hyphemas occurred in 9 and 7 eyes, and macro hyphemas in 6 and 4 eyes, respectively. Five eyes (3 in Group 1 and 2 in Group 2) required AC wash for nonresolving hyphemas during the first postoperative week, with BCVA reduced to hand movements close to face (HMCTF). The remaining cases of macro hyphemas (3 in Group 1 and 2 in Group 2) resolved with conservative management within 7–10 days using frequent topical steroids and eyedrop homatropine hydrobromide (2% w/v) twice daily. Following resolution of hyphema, BCVA improved to 6/6. IOP spikes were transient and treated successfully with anti-glaucoma medications.

Based on multivariable Cox proportional hazard analysis for success criteria 1, 2, and 3, none of the variables, including age, gender, HFA MD, preoperative intraocular pressure, number of anti-glaucoma medications, and study group, were found to have a statistically significant association with the outcome.

Discussion

High myopic eyes with open-angle glaucoma tend to show a faster rate of disease progression, likely due to structural weakness of the optic nerve and thinning of the scleral wall. Early surgical intervention is therefore important to prevent irreversible visual field loss.[22]

Trabeculectomy in these eyes is associated with an increased risk of both early and late vision-threatening complications owing to the structural fragility that predisposes to hypotony, choroidal effusion, and bleb-related complications.[21] These challenges have prompted surgeons to consider safer alternatives, particularly in the form of minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS).[14] Various MIGS procedures, including GATT, Kahook dual goniotomy, microlot trabeculotomy, and implants such as XEN 45, have been explored for the management of open-angle glaucoma in these anatomically challenging eyes.[192021222324]

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed the outcomes of combined GATT with phacoemulsification in high myopia and non-high myopia eyes associated with open-angle glaucoma.

In our study, at 2 weeks and 1 month postoperatively, non-high myopia eyes (Group 2) demonstrated a significantly greater reduction in IOP compared with high myopia eyes (Group 1) (P = 0.001 and 0.04, respectively). Beyond 3 months, however, the intergroup difference was no longer statistically significant (P > 0.05). This pattern suggests that high myopia eyes may show a slower IOP response to surgery, but the long-term IOP-lowering efficacy becomes comparable between the two groups. At 12 months, IOP reduction was similar in both groups (Group 1 = 13.32 ± 5.02 mmHg and Group 2 = 12.94 ± 3.48 mmHg, P = 0.92). In a retrospective comparative study, Goto et al.[21] evaluated outcomes of microhook trabeculotomy combined with phacoemulsification in eyes with long and normal axial lengths. Although eyes with longer axial lengths showed a delayed initial IOP reduction, both groups achieved comparable control by 6 months (long AXL: 14.37 ± 3.10 mmHg; normal axial length: 14.18 ± 3.73 mmHg). In a retrospective interventional case series, Yoshida et al.[20] assessed the long-term outcomes of standalone Kahook dual blade goniotomy in eyes with and without high myopia. Both groups showed similar patterns of IOP reduction up to 36 months, with no significant intergroup differences (high myopia: 14.5 ± 3.7 mmHg; non-high myopia: 15.2 ± 2.7 mmHg).

The non-high myopia group required fewer medications, with a marginal statistically significant intergroup difference up to 6 months. At 12 months, high myopia eyes required slightly more medication, although the difference was not statistically significant (0.36 ± 0.95 vs 0.06 ± 0.24, P = 0.07). Similarly, Yoshida et al.[20] observed a greater reduction in medication use in non-myopic eyes (4.4 ± 1.1 to 3.1 ± 1.7) compared with myopic eyes (4.5 ± 0.6 to 2.1 ± 2.0). In line with our findings, Goto et al.[21] reported that high myopia eyes required more medications (3.3 ± 1.3 to 2.43 ± 0.81) than non-high myopia eyes (3.0 ± 1.4 to 1.95 ± 0.42).

Both groups showed significant improvement in BCVA at 12 months (P < 0.01), with non-high myopia eyes demonstrating better visual acuity than high myopia eyes. This may be due to myopia-related retinal changes associated with axial elongation.

The overall success rates were comparable between high myopia and non-high myopia groups across all criteria (P = 0.77, 0.55, 0.14). Although, the non-high myopia group demonstrated marginally higher success rates using stringent criteria (61.3% vs. 43.5% at 12 months), the difference was not significant. Yoshida et al.[20] reported a lower success rate in high myopia eyes compared with non-high myopia eyes (45% vs 65%) following Kahook dual blade ab interno trabeculotomy and identified pathological myopia as a significant risk factor for surgical failure. The lower success in high myopic eyes may result from axial elongation-related changes, including thinning of the trabecular meshwork, enlargement of Schlemm’s canal, and tissue stretching that hinder aqueous outflow.[25262728] Altered collagen structure and elevated inflammatory mediators may further contribute to postoperative scarring.[29] Akgun et al.[19] reported a 24-month success rate of 81.8% in eyes with a long axial length undergoing GATT or trabeculectomy, which was comparable to the high myopia group (84.0%) of our study.

The patients who had IOP spikes were treated with anti-glaucoma medications. Three patients in Group 1 (high myopia) and two patients in Group 2 (non-high myopia) required an anterior chamber washout for nonresolving hyphema within the first postoperative week.

The limitations of our study include the relatively small sample size, retrospective design, and shorter duration of follow-up.

This study evaluates the surgical outcomes of combined GATT with phacoemulsification in high myopia versus non-high myopia eyes with open-angle glaucoma in an Indian population.

Conclusion

GATT combined with phacoemulsification effectively reduced IOP and medication burden in both high myopia and non-high myopia eyes at 12 months. The procedure maintained a favorable safety profile in anatomically long eyes.
