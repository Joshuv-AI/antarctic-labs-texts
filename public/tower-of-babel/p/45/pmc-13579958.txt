Long-term survival probability and predictors of mortality among adult patients living with HIV initiating combination antiretroviral therapy in Oman: an ambidirectional cohort study, 1992–2024
Authors: Zainab M. Al-Zadjali, Faryal Khamis, Amal Malehi, Sanjay Jaju, Sulaiman Dawood AlSabei, Ibtisam Khalifa Al-Maskari, Ruhina Aimaq, Jalila Al-Naamani, Kouthar Al-Alawi, Yahya M. Al-Farsi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) remains a major global public health challenge despite substantial advances in combination antiretroviral therapy (cART). Evidence on long-term survival and mortality predictors among adult patients living with HIV in Oman remains limited. This study evaluated long-term survival probability and predictors of mortality among adults living with HIV initiating cART in Oman over 32 years.MethodsAn ambidirectional cohort study was conducted using data from the HIV/AIDS registry at the national tertiary referral hospital, the Royal Hospital in Oman, covering the period from January 1992 to December 2024. A total of 549 adult patients living with HIV who initiated cART were included in the study. Clinical and laboratory data were extracted from electronic medical records and analyzed using descriptive statistics, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression.ResultsAmong 549 adult patients living with HIV, 99 deaths occurred during follow-up, including 83 AIDS-related and 16 non-AIDS-related deaths. The overall mortality incidence rate was 1.5 deaths per 1,000 person-months (95% CI: 1.21–1.80). Median survival was not reached during follow-up because the cumulative survival probability remained above 50%. In the adjusted Cox model, participants aged 18–27 years (AHR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.20–0.67) and 28–37 years (AHR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.21–0.64) had significantly lower mortality hazards compared with those aged > 47 years. Patients diagnosed at WHO clinical stage 1 (AHR = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.07–0.32), stage 2 (AHR = 0.24, 95% CI: 0.07–0.78), and stage 3 (AHR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.27–0.67) had lower mortality risk than those diagnosed at stage 4. Hemoglobin ≤ 10 g/dL was independently associated with increased mortality (AHR = 1.75, 95% CI: 1.09–2.81). Although crude analyses showed higher mortality among males, gender was not independently associated with mortality after adjustment for confounders. Timing of cART initiation was also not independently associated with mortality after adjustment.ConclusionLong-term survival probability among adult patients living with HIV in Oman was favorable, with a median survival of over 32 years of follow-up. Younger age at diagnosis, earlier WHO clinical stage, and higher hemoglobin levels were independently associated with lower mortality risk. These findings highlight the importance of early HIV diagnosis and sustained clinical monitoring to improve long-term outcomes.

Background

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection continues to pose a major global public health challenge. Since the beginning of the epidemic in 1981, approximately 91.4 million people (range: 73.4 to 116.4 million) have been infected with the virus, and around 44.1 million (37.6 to 53.4 million) have died from AIDS-related illnesses [1]. Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS) is the advanced stage of HIV infection characterized by severe immune suppression due to progressive loss of Cluster of Differentiation 4 (CD4+) T-lymphocytes. It is defined by a CD4 cell count of less than 200 cells/mm³ and/or the presence of AIDS-defining opportunistic infections or conditions [2]. HIV/AIDS remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [3]. According to the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS), approximately 1.3 million people (1.0–1.7 million) acquired HIV globally in 2024, underscoring the ongoing challenge of HIV transmission worldwide [1].

The introduction of combined antiretroviral therapy (cART), has transformed HIV from a fatal diagnosis to a manageable chronic condition [4]. As a result, people living with HIV (PLWH) now often experience life expectancies that approach those of individuals without HIV [4]. Notably, about 31.6 million individuals (27.8–32.9 million) were receiving cART in 2024 [5].

Survival among PLWH is typically defined as the time from initiation of cART to outcomes such as mortality, loss to follow-up, or retention in care. Survival outcomes vary significantly by region and are influenced by a range of individual-level factors [6]. The global burden of HIV-related mortality rose steadily between 1990 and 2005, peaking at 1.9 million deaths in 2005, before declining to 680,000 in 2021 and further to an estimated 630,000 deaths in both 2023 and 2024 [5, 7, 8]. During this period, the HIV-related mortality rate in the United States and its territories was 1.6 per 100 persons aged 13 and older with diagnosed HIV, with the highest rates observed in Southern states [9]. In Ethiopia, the HIV-related mortality rate was reported at 4.2 per 100 person-years (equivalent to 3.5 deaths per 1,000 person-months), with major risk factors including older age, poor adherence to cART, tuberculosis (TB) co-infection, non-disclosure of HIV status, being bedridden at presentation, and severe anemia [6]. Similarly, a study in Indonesia identified male sex, age ≥ 45 years, advanced World Health Organization (WHO) clinical stage (III/IV), low CD4 count (≤ 200 cells/mm³), poor treatment adherence, TB co-infection, and anemia as significant predictors of mortality [3].

Contrary to the global trend, the burden of HIV/AIDS-related mortality has increased in parts of the Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMR) [10]. In the Middle East and North Africa (MENA), mortality peaked at 11,000 deaths in 2013, before falling to 7,900 by 2020 [11]. In 2021, Saudi Arabia recorded over 200 HIV-related deaths, while Qatar and United Arab Emirates (UAE) each reported fewer than 100, while reliable mortality data for Kuwait remain limited [7, 8]. These regional variations highlight the persistent inequities in access to prevention, diagnosis, and care across global contexts, despite overall improvements in HIV control.

In Oman, approximately 2,800 people were living with HIV in 2023 [4]. A local retrospective study reported an average survival time of 8.5 years (102 months) among PLWH, with 44% (689 of 1,566) surviving beyond eight years [11]. The study also highlighted a sex disparity in mortality: by December 2018, 40.3% of diagnosed females had died, compared to 52.1% (1,152 of 2,212) of males. Despite these insights, the factors influencing long-term survival among HIV-positive individuals receiving cART in Oman remain poorly understood. Existing research has largely focused on prevalence and treatment outcomes, relying primarily on cross-sectional designs that limit causal inference [11, 12].

Given these gaps, this study aimed to explore survival outcomes and identify predictors of mortality among adults living with HIV at the national tertiary referral center, Royal Hospital. This study offers valuable insights for national HIV care programs by enhancing the understanding of long-term survival patterns and informing targeted interventions aimed at reducing mortality. The findings may support evidence-based decision-making to optimize care delivery and resource allocation in Oman.

Methods

Study aim

This study aimed to examine long-term survival and identify predictors of mortality among adults living with HIV who initiated combination antiretroviral therapy (cART) in Oman over 32 years (1992–2024).

Study design

This ambidirectional cohort study utilized data from the national HIV/AIDS registry maintained at the Royal Hospital, Oman. The retrospective component included adults diagnosed with HIV and followed between 1 January 1992 and 26 August 2024 using routinely recorded registry and electronic medical record data. The prospective component consisted of continued outcome ascertainment and clinical follow-up between 27 August and 31 December 2024 through the hospital electronic medical record system (Shifa3+). Both components contributed to a unified survival analysis covering the overall observation period from January 1992 to December 2024.

Study setting

The study was conducted at the Royal Hospital in Muscat, Oman, the national tertiary referral center for HIV care. The hospital provides specialized management of complex HIV cases under infectious disease specialists and maintains a national registry for clinical follow-up and treatment monitoring. The facility has a large inpatient capacity and serves as a referral center for HIV care across the country [13].

Participants (inclusion and exclusion criteria)

The study population consisted of adults aged ≥ 18 years diagnosed with HIV infection and managed at the Royal Hospital who initiated cART during the study period. HIV infection was confirmed according to the national HIV diagnostic algorithm, including confirmatory testing procedures used during the respective calendar period. Participants were included if they had sufficient baseline clinical and laboratory information required for survival analysis, including key indicators such as CD4 cell count, viral load, and hemoglobin level when available in the medical record. Patients with missing essential outcome data (date of diagnosis, survival status, or follow-up information) were excluded from survival analysis. Categories such as “unknown/not done” reported in selected variables reflected incomplete historical documentation for specific co-infection assessments or behavioral variables and were retained to avoid unnecessary case exclusion and preserve cohort representativeness.

Data sources and data collection

Data were extracted from the Al-Shifa 3 + electronic health record system using a structured Excel-based checklist. Retrospective data for patients diagnosed between 1992 and 26 August 2024 were retrieved from the electronic registry. From 27 August 2024 onward, prospective follow-up data were collected to update clinical outcomes and treatment information. The study relied entirely on routinely recorded hospital registry data, with no direct participant contact or interviews conducted.

Study variables

The study collected sociodemographic, clinical, and laboratory variables. Socio-demographic variables included age at diagnosis, sex, marital status, and employment status. Clinical variables included probable mode of HIV transmission, comorbidities, WHO clinical stage at diagnosis, timing of cART initiation, and co-infections including tuberculosis, hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C virus (HCV IgG), and syphilis. WHO clinical stage at diagnosis was classified according to the World Health Organization HIV clinical staging guidelines as Stage 1 (asymptomatic), Stage 2 (mild symptoms), Stage 3 (advanced symptoms), and Stage 4 (severe symptoms/AIDS) [14]. Laboratory variables included baseline CD4 cell count, viral load, and hemoglobin level, with 10 g/dL used as the cut-off value.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality among adults living with HIV receiving cART at the Royal Hospital, Oman. Mortality was conceptualized as a time-to-event outcome rather than a simple binary variable. The event of interest was death from any cause during follow-up, while participants who remained alive at the end of the study period or were lost to follow-up were treated as censored observations. Death events were identified through hospital medical records and routinely updated clinical information in the Al-Shifa 3 + electronic system (Shifa3+).Time-to-event was defined as the duration from the date of HIV diagnosis to the date of death or censoring [15]. For censored individuals, follow-up time was calculated from the date of diagnosis to either the last known clinical contact or 31 December 2024, whichever came first. Survival analysis methods, including Cox proportional hazards regression, were applied to evaluate factors associated with mortality while appropriately accounting for censoring in the data [15].

Sample size calculation

This study utilized registry data from the national HIV referral center, all eligible adults meeting the inclusion criteria during the study period were included in the analysis. Although an initial minimum sample size estimation was performed based on prior HIV survival studies, the final analysis was effectively a census of eligible registry records available between 1992 and 2024. The required sample size was calculated using Open Epi version 3.01 employing a two-sided hypothesis test with a 95% confidence level, 80% statistical power, and a 5% level of significance, in accordance with the methodological recommendations of [16]. The calculation was based on estimates derived from a previous study conducted by [17], which included 1,245 patients with documented CD4 cell count measurements between 1984 and 2018. In that study, 59.3% of the non-exposed group had CD4 counts > 200 cells/mm³, whereas 40.7% of the exposed group had CD4 counts < 200 cells/mm³. Using these proportions, the minimum estimated sample size required to detect a statistically significant difference between the two groups was 224 participants (112 participants per group). To account for potential incomplete or missing data, an additional 10% contingency was incorporated, resulting in a final estimated sample size of 246 participants. Nevertheless, all eligible records meeting the predefined inclusion criteria during the study period were included in the analysis, yielding a final sample of 549 participants. This sample substantially exceeded the minimum required sample size, thereby enhancing the statistical precision and robustness of the study findings.

Sampling method

Consecutive inclusion of all eligible adults recorded in the national HIV registry at the Royal Hospital between 1992 and 2024 was performed. Since the Royal Hospital functions as the national tertiary referral center for HIV care in Oman, the registry captures patients from across the country, enhancing national representativeness.

Data statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using International Business Machines (IBM) Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) Statistics (Version 27). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and group differences were assessed using chi-square tests [18]. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was used to estimate survival probabilities and time to mortality among adult patients living with HIV [19]. Mean survival time was estimated using Kaplan–Meier analysis in SPSS, defined as the area under the survival curve up to the last observed event time. Mortality incidence rates were calculated per 1,000 person-months of follow-up. Survival time was measured in months from the date of HIV diagnosis to the date of mortality or death, or censoring [20]. Participants alive at the end of follow-up or lost to follow-up were treated as right-censored observations at their last clinical visit [21–23]. Cox proportional hazards regression was applied to identify independent predictors of mortality while adjusting for potential confounders [15]. All variables of clinical relevance and those supported by previous literature were first examined as crude (univariable) hazard ratios. To avoid overfitting given the number of deaths (n = 99). Results were reported as crude hazard ratios (HRs) and adjusted hazard ratios (AHRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and a p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using log-minus-log survival plots, which showed no major violation of the assumption, although a borderline signal was observed for haemoglobin, whose estimate should therefore be interpreted with some caution. Survival probabilities were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test.

Results

All analyses were conducted within a time-to-event survival framework. Descriptive analyses, including chi-square tests, were used to summarize baseline characteristics according to mortality and censoring status. Survival outcomes were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis with log-rank tests, while Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to identify factors associated with mortality among people living with HIV/AIDS in Oman.

Socio-demographic characteristics of adult patients living with HIV

Table 1 presents the socio-demographic characteristics of 549 adults living with HIV included in the cohort, categorized by survival status at the end of follow-up (deceased n = 99; censored n = 450). A higher proportion of deaths was observed among males (80.8%) compared with females (19.2%). Unadjusted group differences were statistically significant (p = 0.005). Differences in the distribution of deaths were observed across age groups (p = 0.002), with the highest proportion of deaths occurring among individuals aged 28–37 years (27.3%, n = 27), followed by those aged 38–47 years (26.3%, n = 26). With respect to the year of diagnosis, 45.5% (n = 45) of deaths occurred among individuals diagnosed in 2008 or earlier, compared with 21.6% among censored participants (p = 0.001).Marital status also showed differences in the distribution of deaths, with married individuals accounting for 48.5% (n = 48) of deaths compared with 53.1% among censored participants (p = 0.003). Similarly, unemployed individuals represented 55.6% (n = 55) of deaths compared with 44.2% among censored participants (p = 0.001).

Clinical characteristics of adult patients living with HIV

Table 2 summarizes the clinical characteristics of the 549 adults living with HIV included in the cohort, comparing those who died during follow-up (n = 99) with those who were censored (n = 450). The mode of HIV transmission varied between groups, with sexual transmission accounting for 40.4% (n = 40) of deaths and unknown transmission accounting for 48.5% (n = 48) of deaths (p = 0.001). The most common reason for HIV testing among those who died was presentation with HIV-related symptoms (85.9%, n = 85), compared with 48.7% among censored individuals (p = 0.001). WHO clinical stage at diagnosis differed across groups, with 60.6% (n = 60) of deaths occurring among individuals diagnosed at stage 4, compared with 23.3% among censored participants (p = 0.001). Higher proportions of some comorbid conditions were observed among deceased patients in the descriptive analysis.

Comorbidity burden differed significantly between groups (p = 0.001). Among individuals who died, 32.3% (n = 32) had one comorbidity and 17.2% (n = 17) had two comorbidities, compared with 26.9% and 11.6% among censored participants, respectively. In contrast, the absence of comorbidities was more common among censored individuals (55.1%) than among those who died (32.3%), indicating that increasing comorbidity burden was associated with mortality. Most participants-initiated cART on time (90.3%, n = 496). However, a higher proportion of deaths occurred among those with deferred initiation (22.2%, n = 22) compared with censored individuals (6.9%). Tuberculosis regarding timing of cART initiation, we clarify that this variable was defined based on the interval between HIV diagnosis and initiation of antiretroviral therapy, with “timely” and “deferred (active TB)” categories defined according to clinical records. Regarding hemoglobin levels, 44.4% (n = 44) of deaths occurred among individuals with hemoglobin ≤ 10 g/dL compared with 18.7% among censored participants, while 55.6% (n = 55) of deaths occurred among those with hemoglobin > 10 g/dL, reflecting the larger proportion of this subgroup in the cohort. In the multivariable Cox model, hemoglobin ≤ 10 g/dL was independently associated with a higher mortality hazard.

Co-infection among adult patients living with HIV

Table 3 demonstrates that coinfections were significantly linked to mortality rates among HIV/AIDS patients. Specifically, deaths were more frequently observed among patients without TB infection (57.6%, n = 57) compared to those with active TB (23.2%, n = 23). Regarding syphilis infection, deaths were more frequently observed among individuals without documented infection (74.7%, n = 74) (p = 0.001). Similarly, for HCV infection, mortality was more prevalent among those without the infection (73.7%, n = 73) compared to those with positive HCV status (17.2%, n = 17).

Behavioral risk factors among adult patients living with HIV

Table 4 suggests that there was no significant association between behavioral risk factors and mortality. Deaths occurred more frequently among individuals who did not engage in risky behaviors compared to those who were smokers, alcohol users, injection drug users, or individuals who had been imprisoned, with frequencies of n = 23 (23.2%), n = 18 (18.2%), n = 7 (7.1%), and n = 4 (4.0%), respectively.

Discussion

This study aimed to assess predictors of mortality among adult patients living with HIV receiving cART at the Royal Hospital, Oman, between 1992 and 2024. Over the 32-year study period, a total of 99 deaths were recorded among adults on cART, including 19 females and 80 males. Accordingly, after presenting the adjusted Cox regression findings, a statement was added in the discussion section to clearly indicate that older age, advanced WHO clinical stage, and low hemoglobin level at diagnosis were the only statistically significant independent predictors of mortality among adults living with HIV in Oman.

Incidence rate of mortality among adult patients living with HIV

The overall mortality incidence rate in our study was 1.5 deaths per 1,000 person-months (95%CI: [1.2, 1.8]) of follow-up of 65,803 person-months. In contrast, a study from British Columbia, Canada, reported 3,401 deaths over 562,150 person-years (equivalent to 6,745.8 units of 1,000 person-months), yielding a much lower incidence rate of approximately 6 deaths per 1,000 person-years (equivalent to 0.5 deaths per 1,000 person-months) [24]. Additionally, in Ethiopia, a study showed that the overall incidence rate of mortality is 4.6 per 1,000 person-years (which is equivalent to approximately 0.38 deaths per 1,000 person-months) [25]. This suggests that the mortality rate observed in our study is substantially higher than that reported in the Ethiopian context and Canada. This difference may be due to variations in patient characteristics, healthcare systems, and timing of cART initiation.

Incidence rate of mortality among adult patients living with HIV based on gender

The mortality incidence rate among males was 1.8 deaths per 1,000 person-months, which was higher than among females (0.8 deaths per 1,000 person-months). This represents a crude (unadjusted) difference in mortality incidence rates between genders. This disparity may be related to differences in healthcare engagement, clinical presentation, and treatment adherence following HIV diagnosis. Previous studies have suggested that males may present with more advanced disease, delayed healthcare utilization, and poorer adherence to cART, which may contribute to increased mortality risk [26, 27]. Additionally, behavioral factors such as substance use and delayed care-seeking behavior may also contribute to poorer outcomes among males. However, it is important to note that while crude incidence rates and unadjusted analyses suggested higher mortality among males, gender was not statistically significant in the adjusted Cox proportional hazards model in this study. This indicates that the observed crude difference may be influenced by confounding factors rather than representing an independent effect of gender. This finding aligns with a study conducted in Indonesia, which showed that mortality is higher among males than females [3]. Similarly, another study done in Northwest Ethiopia also showed that mortality is AHR = 2.4 times higher among males than females [28]. However, in contrast, another study in Ethiopia showed that mortality is higher among females, N = 172 (63.0%), than males, N = 101 (37.0%) [25].

Age at diagnosis and mortality among adult patients living with HIV

Consistent with the findings of this study, age at diagnosis was significantly associated with mortality among PLWH. The incidence rate of mortality was higher among individuals aged > 47 years (3.7/1000 person-months, 95% CI: [2.2, 5.1]) compared to younger age groups. Similarly, patients diagnosed at younger ages (18–27 and 28–37 years) had a lower risk of mortality than those in older age categories. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting higher mortality risk among older PLWH [6, 25, 29, 30] .

In the United States, for example, 30% of deaths among PLWH occurred in individuals aged 55–64 years in 2023 [9]. The increased mortality risk in older individuals may be explained by weaker immune response, higher prevalence of comorbid conditions, and reduced response to antiretroviral therapy compared to younger patients [31]. In addition, age-related immune deterioration, including thymic involution and immunosenescence, contributes to accelerated HIV disease progression and increased mortality risk [32–34]. The European Study in Infections among Adults (EuroSIDA), which followed PLWH from 2001 to 2020, also reported that although overall mortality declined over time due to improved cART coverage and care, age remained a strong independent predictor of mortality [35].

Association of WHO clinical staging with mortality among HIV/AIDS adult patients

WHO clinical stage at diagnosis was another strong predictor of mortality. Patients who were asymptomatic (stage 1) or had mild (stage 2) to advanced symptoms (stage 3) at the time of diagnosis had significantly better survival outcomes than those diagnosed at a severe stage. These results emphasize the value of early diagnosis and regular screening programs to identify HIV infections before they progress. Consistent with a previous study conducted in Ethiopia, which showed that individuals with WHO clinical stages 1, 2, and 3 had a better survival time than those with stage 4 [36]. A systematic review and meta-analysis in sub-Saharan Africa also showed that the WHO clinical staging ([3–4]) is related to a high mortality rate [37]. Starting cART immediately after HIV diagnosis can significantly reduce HIV-related morbidity and mortality. Additionally, those with an advanced stage need close monitoring during the early treatment phase to assess cART response and detect potential immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome [36].

Association of low hemoglobin level with mortality among HIV/AIDS adult patients

Our study highlighted that hemoglobin levels less than 10 g/dL were associated with a higher risk of mortality. This association may be attributed to anemia, a common complication among PLWH that often indicates an advanced stage of the disease [38]. Consistent with our findings, a study conducted in Ethiopia revealed that severe anemia increases the risk of mortality among PLWH by 4.57 times compared to those with normal hemoglobin levels [39]. Furthermore, research in Indonesia demonstrated that the likelihood of mortality is 2.308 times higher for individuals with hemoglobin levels below 10 g/dL [3]. This study provides one of the longest follow-up cohorts of people living with HIV in the region.

Limitations and bias considerations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Key variables such as stigma, mental health status, education, income, and treatment literacy were not available in the registry, limiting assessment of important social and behavioral determinants of mortality. In addition, missing clinical and follow-up data for a small number of patients may have introduced some degree of bias. Given the 32-year study period, changes in HIV diagnostic practices, treatment protocols, and clinical management over time may have influenced data consistency and survival estimates. Mortality may also have been underestimated among patients lost to follow-up, as some may have died outside the health system. Selection bias may have occurred due to inclusion based on the availability of baseline clinical and laboratory data, which may have resulted in the exclusion of some individuals with incomplete records. Information bias and misclassification are also possible due to reliance on routinely collected electronic medical records, although the use of standardized WHO clinical staging and laboratory thresholds likely reduced these risks [14]. Finally, although time-to-event methods were applied using Kaplan-Meier and Cox regression analyses, residual confounding, immortal time bias, and unmeasured factors cannot be excluded due to the observational nature of the study. To reduce the risk of overfitting and unstable estimates arising from sparse categories, the multivariable model was deliberately limited to a small set of pre-specified predictors; consequently, variables examined only in the univariable analysis (including employment, calendar period, comorbidities, co-infections, and timing of cART initiation) were not mutually adjusted, and their crude associations should be interpreted accordingly [40].

Conclusion

The mortality incidence rate among adults living with HIV initiating combination antiretroviral therapy in Oman was 1.5 deaths per 1,000 person-months. Over a 32-year follow-up period, median survival was not reached, indicating sustained long-term survival in the cohort. Multivariable analysis identified younger age at diagnosis, earlier WHO clinical stage, and higher hemoglobin levels as independent predictors of lower mortality risk, while gender and timing of cART initiation were not significant. These findings emphasize the importance of early HIV diagnosis, prompt initiation of treatment, and continuous clinical monitoring. Integrating routine assessment and management of anemia within HIV care services may further improve survival outcomes among people living with HIV in Oman.
