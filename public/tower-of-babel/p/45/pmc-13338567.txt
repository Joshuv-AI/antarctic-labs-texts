Turnaround Time and Patients' Perceptions of Clinical Laboratory Services at a Tertiary Hospital in Pakistan: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Ali Abdul Basit, Muhammad Usman, Nour Fakih, Laiba Akram, Ayesha Nighat, Zia Ur Rehman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsTurnaround time (TAT) is a key metric of laboratory performance. However, delays occurring outside the intra‐laboratory phases are often overlooked. This study aimed to assess intra‐laboratory TAT and patients' perceptions of laboratory service quality in a public tertiary hospital in Pakistan.MethodsA cross‐sectional survey was conducted between July and December 2024 at Holy Family Hospital, enrolling 300 adult patients using non‐probability quota sampling. Data were collected using a bilingual, validated questionnaire that included a 21‐item patient perception scale. Intra‐laboratory TAT (from sample collection to report verification) was analyzed using non‐parametric statistical tests and generalized linear models.ResultsThe median TAT was 115 min (IQR: 90–145), significantly shorter in morning shifts and for baseline, biochemistry, and special chemistry tests (p < 0.001). The median perception score was 60 (IQR: 54–66.75), with only 37.67% of patients considered satisfied. Satisfaction was significantly correlated with test type and day of the week and was negatively associated with TAT (ρ = –0.307, p < 0.001).ConclusionMore than 60% of patients were dissatisfied, primarily due to delayed reporting, inadequate communication, and staff‐related concerns. Establishing standardized TAT benchmarks, optimizing staffing, and improving patient communication may enhance service quality and patient satisfaction in high‐volume laboratory settings.Keywords: Health service quality, laboratory services, Pakistan, patient satisfaction, turnaround time

Introduction

The laboratory plays a pivotal role in healthcare delivery by providing clinicians with essential diagnostic information that informs treatment planning. It also supports the ongoing monitoring of therapeutic efficacy, ensuring that patients receive the most appropriate and effective care [1]. The quality of laboratory services is typically assessed using four primary indicators: timeliness, accuracy, precision, and cost‐effectiveness [2].

Historically, laboratories have prioritized analytical and technical dimensions of quality, such as accuracy and reliability, while paying relatively little attention to the prompt release of test results. This traditional focus contrasts with clinical practice, where clinicians often prioritize the speed of result release over marginal gains in analytical accuracy to ensure timely patient management [3].

Among quality indicators, timeliness is arguably the most critical. Turnaround time (TAT) is the standard metric used to evaluate timeliness, although its definition varies depending on the scope of measurement and laboratory setting [2]. For instance, Lundberg defines the total testing cycle, or “brain‐to‐brain” TAT (also referred to as therapeutic TAT), as encompassing nine stages: ordering, collection, identification, transportation, preparation, analysis, reporting, interpretation, and clinical action [4]. However, many laboratories contend that TAT should be limited to intra‐laboratory processes, citing their inability to control external delays [5]. This perspective substantially underestimates the true duration of the testing cycle, given that non‐analytical delays account for approximately 96% of total turnaround time [6].

Even within intra‐laboratory processes, there is variability in how TAT is measured, including whether it begins at sample receipt, registration, or collection, and whether it ends with analysis completion, result verification, or report dissemination [7]. To address this variability, TAT is commonly categorized into three phases: pre‐analytical (test ordering, sample collection, and transport), analytical (sample processing and analysis), and post‐analytical (report generation, verification, and result entry) [3]. Intra‐laboratory TAT encompasses the full analytical phase and selected aspects of both the pre‐analytical and post‐analytical phases [8].

As with its definition, there is considerable disagreement among healthcare providers and patients regarding what constitutes an acceptable TAT. Approximately 80% of laboratories report receiving complaints related to delayed reporting, yet no universally accepted benchmarks exist for optimal TAT across different test categories [9]. A Q‐Probes study by the College of American Pathologists on cardiac biomarker testing found that physicians expected a median TAT of 37.5 min (interquartile range: 31–45 min), whereas laboratory personnel estimated 60 min. Actual performance, however, lagged behind both expectations, with a median TAT of 91 min (74–105 min) [10]. Similarly, a survey in the Democratic Republic of Congo reported that 96% of 330 attending physicians believed laboratory results should be available within 120 min, while only 4% found a 180‐min TAT acceptable [11]. TAT expectations also vary by test type, with ≤ 60 min considered acceptable for hematological tests and ≤ 90 min for clinical chemistry assays [12]. A study conducted at Aga Khan University Hospital in Pakistan defined an optimal TAT as less than or equal to 2 h [13].

Extended turnaround times can be a major source of dissatisfaction for both clinicians and patients. Contributing factors include test complexity, high sample volumes, transportation delays, staff shortages, inadequate training, infrastructure limitations, power outages, and administrative inefficiencies [1, 12]. In high‐acuity settings such as emergency departments and operating rooms, prolonged TATs can negatively impact clinical decision‐making and outcomes [14]. Reducing TAT has been shown to significantly shorten patient stays in emergency departments [15], although evidence of a similar impact on inpatient length of stay remains inconclusive [16]. Regardless of outcome‐related evidence, both clinicians and patients consistently express a preference for faster laboratory reporting [14].

Patients' perceptions of laboratory services are shaped by multiple factors, including the professionalism of staff, the availability of information, the laboratory's cleanliness and accessibility, the availability of required tests, TAT, and even the presence of basic amenities such as restrooms [17]. A study conducted in India found that extended turnaround times were a major contributor to patient dissatisfaction with laboratory services [18].

Despite the clinical significance of TAT, few studies in Pakistan, particularly in public‐sector tertiary care hospitals, have comprehensively examined both operational turnaround time metrics and patients' perceptions of laboratory services. In low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) such as Pakistan, resource limitations, infrastructural challenges, and systemic inefficiencies may further compromise laboratory performance. This study, therefore, aims to evaluate the turnaround times of various laboratory tests and explore their association with demographic characteristics, healthcare operational variables, and patients' perceptions, using data collected from the pathology laboratory at Holy Family Hospital, a tertiary care facility in Rawalpindi. The findings may support future quality improvement strategies and contribute to the development of national benchmarks for laboratory efficiency.

Methodology

Study Design and Setting

This cross‐sectional study was conducted between July 1 and December 31, 2024, at the pathology laboratory of Holy Family Hospital, a public tertiary care hospital in Rawalpindi, Pakistan. The hospital receives over 3,300 patient visits daily and provides a wide range of laboratory services, including biochemical, microbiological, histopathological, and baseline tests [19].

Study Population and Eligibility Criteria

The study included adult patients (≥ 18 years) who underwent at least one laboratory test during the study period and were able to understand either Urdu or English.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Rawalpindi Medical University (Approval ID: 439/IREF/RMU/2023). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Participant anonymity, voluntary participation, and the right to withdraw at any time without consequences were ensured.

Data Collection Tools and Procedure

Data were collected using a structured, self‐administered, validated questionnaire available in both English and Urdu. Translation was performed by a certified linguist to ensure linguistic accuracy and consistency. A pilot test was conducted with 20 participants to assess clarity and reliability; these responses were excluded from the final analysis.

The questionnaire comprised three sections:

1.Demographic characteristics: gender, age, residence, marital status, education, and occupation.

2.Healthcare operational factors: type of visit, day of the week, shift of the day, and sample type.

3.Turnaround time (TAT) and patient perceptions: TAT was defined as the interval from sample receipt to report availability, including sample labeling, transportation, processing, analysis, and verification.

For illiterate participants, trained research assistants administered the questionnaire by reading the items aloud using a neutral tone and without providing additional explanation.

Patient Perception Scale

Patient perceptions were measured using a 21‐item scale adapted from Khalid et al. [21], originally derived from a validated Ethiopian tool [22]. Items were rated on a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = very dissatisfied, 5 = very satisfied). The scale demonstrated good internal consistency in the present study (Cronbach's α = 0.856).

It should be noted that this instrument was designed to assess overall patient satisfaction with laboratory services rather than TAT perception specifically. Although certain items, such as waiting time for services and provision of information about report time, capture aspects indirectly related to TAT, the scale does not include dedicated subscales for the independent measurement of TAT perception.

Data Management and Statistical Analysis

Data were cleaned using Microsoft Excel and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0. Statistical analyses were conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for Reporting of Statistics for Clinical Research by Assel et al. (2019) [23] and SAMPL reporting recommendations. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Total patient perception scores were calculated by summing responses to the 21 items (possible range: 21–105), with scores ≥ 63 (≥ 50% of the maximum possible score) considered satisfactory. Continuous variables, including turnaround time (TAT) and perception scores, were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs).

Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, which indicated non‐normal distributions for both TAT and perception scores (p < 0.001). Accordingly, non‐parametric statistical tests were used. The Mann–Whitney U test was applied for comparisons involving binary variables, and the Kruskal–Wallis H test was used for variables with more than two categories, with effect sizes reported as r or ε2, respectively. When appropriate, Dunn's test with Bonferroni correction was used for post hoc pairwise comparisons. The association between turnaround time and total perception score was assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (ρ). To adjust for potential confounders, generalized linear models (GLMs) with a gamma distribution and log link function were fitted. All statistical analyses were prespecified and confirmatory; no exploratory analyses or post hoc subgroup analyses were conducted. All tests were two‐sided, and a p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Of the 320 questionnaires distributed, 300 were returned with complete and valid responses, yielding a response rate of 93.75% (300/320). The sample comprised equal numbers of male and female participants (150/300 each, 50.0%). The most frequent age group was 51–60 years (94/300, 31.3%), followed by those aged over 60 years (73/300, 24.3%). Most respondents were married (214/300, 71.3%) and resided in rural areas (177/300, 59.0%).

Regarding educational attainment, 125/300 (41.7%) had primary education or less, 127/300 (42.3%) had completed secondary education, 27/300 (9.0%) were undergraduates, and 21/300 (7.0%) were postgraduates. The most common occupations were self‐employed individuals (89/300, 29.7%), students (63/300, 21.0%), and employed individuals (58/300, 19.3%).

Two‐thirds of participants were first‐time visitors (202/300, 67.3%), and most attended during the morning shift (183/300, 61.0%). The most frequently requested test categories were baseline tests (144/300, 48.0%), followed by biochemistry (61/300, 20.3%), special chemistry (52/300, 17.3%), microbiology (23/300, 7.7%), and histopathology (20/300, 6.7%) (Table 1).

Item‐level analysis indicated that 12 of the 21 items had cumulative scores below 900, reflecting dissatisfaction. Turnaround Time (Tat)

Turnaround time (TAT) ranged from 35 min to over 3 days, with a median of 115 min (IQR: 90–145 min).

Bivariate analyses demonstrated that TAT differed significantly by shift of the day and type of sample. Samples processed during the morning shift had shorter turnaround times compared with those processed during the evening shift (median: 110 vs. 125 min; IQR: 90–135 vs. 96–165 min; Mann–Whitney U = 8875, p = 0.012, r = −0.1443).

TAT also varied significantly across test categories (Kruskal–Wallis H = 143.314, p < 0.001, ε2 = 0.4723). Baseline tests had the shortest turnaround time (median: 100 min; IQR: 78–120), followed by biochemistry (110 min; IQR: 90–130) and special chemistry tests (140 min; IQR: 112.5–155). Substantially longer turnaround times were observed for microbiology (median: 6,450 min; IQR: 6,450–7,275; approximately 4.5 days) and histopathology tests (median: 6,580 min; IQR: 6,580–7,350; approximately 4.6 days) (Table 3).

Pairwise Comparisons of Sample Types

Post hoc pairwise comparisons using Dunn's test with Bonferroni correction confirmed significant differences in TAT and patient perception scores across most sample types.

For TAT, baseline tests (median: 100 min) had significantly shorter turnaround times compared with special chemistry (median: 140 min; adjusted p < 0.001), microbiology (median: 6,450 min; adjusted p < 0.001), and histopathology tests (median: 6,580 min; adjusted p < 0.001). Biochemistry tests also demonstrated significantly shorter TATs compared with microbiology and histopathology (all adjusted p < 0.001). No statistically significant difference in TAT was observed between microbiology and histopathology (adjusted p = 0.959).

Similarly, for patient perception scores, participants undergoing baseline, biochemistry, or special chemistry tests reported significantly higher satisfaction compared with those undergoing microbiology or histopathology tests (all adjusted p < 0.001). No significant difference in perception scores was observed between microbiology and histopathology (adjusted p = 0.939) (Table 5).

Discussion

Turnaround time (TAT) is a key indicator of laboratory performance, with shorter TATs widely regarded as desirable by both clinicians and patients. However, the definition of TAT varies across stakeholders: laboratories typically focus on intra‐laboratory TAT, defined as the interval from sample receipt to report verification, whereas clinicians are primarily concerned with total or therapeutic TAT, which extends from test ordering to treatment initiation [7]. Patients' perceptions similarly reflect the total testing experience, even though only intra‐laboratory TAT is routinely documented on laboratory reports. Accordingly, the present study measured receipt‐to‐verification intra‐laboratory TAT, consistent with prior studies [2, 7, 13, 14, 24]. This approach captures the full analytical phase and selected components of the pre‐ and post‐analytical phases while excluding delays outside the laboratory's direct control [8].

Turnaround time is influenced by multiple factors, including infrastructure, administrative workflows, equipment availability, staffing levels, workload, and test urgency [1, 12]. International guidelines recommend that laboratories establish context‐specific TAT benchmarks and communicate them clearly to users [25, 26]. As no formal internal benchmarks were available at the study site, our findings were interpreted in comparison with regional and international data.

The median intra‐laboratory TAT observed in this study was 115 min (IQR: 90–145), which is comparable to findings from Iran reporting a median TAT of approximately 2 h (1.5–2.8 h) for chemistry and hematology tests [27]. Consistent with previous reports, chemistry tests in our study required longer processing times than baseline tests, reflecting greater analytical complexity [27]. Similarly, the median TAT for baseline tests (100 min) was comparable to the 90‐min routine TAT reported in a U.S. study [28].

For biochemistry tests, the observed median TAT of 110 min was shorter than the 5.5‐h average reported by Goswami et al. [29], potentially reflecting differences in laboratory workflow efficiency, prioritization protocols, and staffing patterns. In contrast, a study from Nepal reported a mean biochemistry TAT of 79 min; however, that analysis did not distinguish between routine and stat testing, which may explain the lower estimates [30]. In Pakistan, a 2‐h threshold is commonly regarded as an acceptable benchmark for stat laboratory tests [13], placing the present findings within an acceptable operational range.

In contrast, microbiology and histopathology tests demonstrated substantially longer turnaround times, with median TATs of approximately 4.5 days and 4.6 days, respectively. These prolonged durations reflect the inherent complexity of culture‐based microbiological testing and histopathological tissue processing. Comparable studies from Nigeria and the United States have reported that most histopathology results are finalized within 2–5 days [31, 32]. Similarly, studies from India indicate that microbiology turnaround times frequently exceed 48 h, although targeted interventions such as rapid antimicrobial testing can substantially reduce delays [33]. These findings confirm that TAT varies considerably across test categories and laboratory contexts, underscoring the need for locally defined, test‐specific benchmarks that account for available resources, workforce capacity, and clinical demand [18].

A significant difference in TAT was also observed between morning and evening shifts, with shorter turnaround times during morning hours. This finding contrasts with previous studies reporting longer morning TATs due to higher patient volumes [13, 26] and may reflect more optimal staffing levels and resource allocation during earlier shifts at the study site.

Patient perception scores in the present study had a median of 60 (range: 28–95), comparable to the median score reported by Khalid et al. (59; range: 32–100) [21]. However, only 37.67% of participants were classified as satisfied, compared with 56.2% in Khalid's study. This difference likely reflects variation in satisfaction classification criteria, as we defined satisfaction using a fixed threshold (≥ 63), whereas Khalid et al. employed a median‐based percentage approach [21]. This highlights the importance of standardized and validated criteria for assessing patient satisfaction across settings.

Although laboratory‐specific satisfaction in the present study (37.67%) was substantially lower than the overall hospital satisfaction rate (82.53%) previously reported at Holy Family Hospital [34], the findings suggest that this discrepancy is more likely attributable to objective laboratory performance factors rather than generalized institutional dissatisfaction. Despite the absence of a dedicated TAT subscale in the perception instrument, multiple findings support laboratory‐specific concerns, including a moderate negative correlation between TAT and satisfaction (ρ = −0.307, p < 0.001) and significantly lower satisfaction scores for microbiology and histopathology tests—the test categories with the longest turnaround times. Low ratings for laboratory‐specific items such as procedural explanations, waiting time, and staff competence further reinforce this interpretation.

Overall satisfaction in this study was lower than that reported in studies from Ethiopia (48.3%), Lahore (62%), and Karachi (65.7%) [35, 36, 37]. Common drivers of dissatisfaction included inadequate procedural explanations, prolonged turnaround times, and perceived staff incompetence. Previous evidence indicates that clear and empathetic communication significantly improves patient satisfaction [38]. Supporting this, an Ethiopian study ranked turnaround time as the second most important contributor to dissatisfaction (after toilet cleanliness) [35], while an Indian study identified it as the leading cause [18].

Taken together, these findings emphasize the importance of a patient‐centered laboratory care model grounded in timely reporting, skilled personnel, and transparent communication to restore public trust and improve the quality of care delivery.

Strengths and Limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first study in Pakistan to simultaneously assess intra‐laboratory TAT and patient perceptions using a culturally adapted and validated satisfaction scale in a high‐volume tertiary care setting. Additional strengths include the integration of both objective performance metrics (TAT) and subjective patient‐reported outcomes, as well as the use of a structured, bilingual, pilot‐tested questionnaire, which helped minimize misinterpretation and response bias.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single tertiary care center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the use of non‐probability quota sampling may have introduced selection bias. In addition, the study assessed only intra‐laboratory TAT; delays occurring during the pre‐analytical and post‐analytical phases were beyond the scope of the present analysis. We did not independently assess overall hospital satisfaction to account for pre‐existing patient sentiments toward the institution. Consequently, it is not possible to fully disentangle whether the observed low laboratory satisfaction reflects laboratory‐specific service deficiencies or ascertainment bias, whereby patients with unfavorable prior perceptions of the hospital may systematically rate all services lower. Furthermore, characteristics of the 20 non‐responders were not analyzed; if dissatisfied patients were more likely to participate, the degree of dissatisfaction may have been overestimated.

Although the 21‐item perception scale demonstrated good internal consistency, it was designed to assess overall laboratory service satisfaction rather than turnaround time perception specifically. Items related to waiting time and communication regarding report availability therefore provide only indirect indicators of TAT‐related satisfaction, which may have limited the precision with which the influence of TAT on patient satisfaction could be quantified. Finally, age was analyzed using categorical groupings, which assumes homogeneity within categories. Continuous modeling of age may have revealed more nuanced associations; however, no significant age‐related effects were observed in the present analysis.

Future Directions

Future research should adopt multicenter study designs to enhance generalizability and evaluate turnaround time across all phases of laboratory testing, including pre‐analytical, analytical, and post‐analytical processes. In addition, interventional studies examining the impact of laboratory automation, digital reporting systems, optimized staffing models, and structured staff–patient communication strategies on both service efficiency and patient satisfaction are warranted. Finally, the development and validation of a TAT‐specific patient perception instrument would allow more precise evaluation of the relationship between turnaround time and patient satisfaction in laboratory settings.

Conclusion

This study identifies important gaps in laboratory efficiency and patient satisfaction within a high‐volume tertiary care setting. Although intra‐laboratory turnaround times were generally acceptable by international standards, overall patient satisfaction remained low, primarily due to communication deficiencies, prolonged perceived waiting times, and concerns regarding staff competence. The observed negative association between turnaround time and patient perception underscores the central role of timely reporting in shaping patient experience. Establishing context‐specific TAT benchmarks, strengthening staffing during evening shifts, and improving staff–patient communication should be prioritized. Investments in workflow optimization and laboratory automation may further reduce delays, enhance patient trust, and improve the overall quality of laboratory services.
