Integrative analysis of yield and physiological responses of wheat genotypes under drought and heat stress across diverse environments
Authors: H M Mamrutha, Gopalareddy Krishnappa, Rinki Khobra, Yogesh Kumar, Vinayaka, Amaresh, T Lakshmi Pathy, Vanita Pandey, Suma Biradar, Chandra Kant Singh, K J Yashavanthakumar, Jang Bahadur Singh, Deepak Kumar Baranwal, Renu Munjal, Satish Kumar Singh, Hanif Khan, O P Ahlawat, Ratan Tiwari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDrought and heat stress increasingly threaten global wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) production, posing major challenges to food security and sustainable agriculture. Identifying genotypes that combine high yield potential with physiological resilience across diverse environments is essential for developing climate-smart wheat cultivars. The objective of this study was to identify high-yielding and physiologically resilient wheat genotypes with stable performance across diverse environments under drought and heat stress using multivariate and multi-trait analytical approaches.MethodsForty-nine wheat genotypes comprising 43 promising breeding lines (G1–G43) and six released checks (G44–G49) were evaluated across eight locations representing distinct mega-environments during the 2022–23 Rabi season. Trials were conducted under normal (N), drought (D), and late-sown heat (H) conditions. Grain yield, biomass, and key physiological traits were analyzed using multivariate approaches and multi-trait indices to assess genotype performance, stability, and adaptability under drought and heat stress conditions.ResultsHighly significant (p ≤ 0.001) effects of genotype, environment, and genotype × environment (G×E) interaction were observed for all traits. Mean grain yield declined from 6.5 t ha⁻¹ under normal conditions to 4.5 t ha⁻¹ (–31%) under drought and 5.0 t ha⁻¹ (–23%) under heat stress. Genotypes G7 (6.23 t ha⁻¹), G13 (6.15 t ha⁻¹), G8 (5.93 t ha⁻¹), G9 (5.92 t ha⁻¹), G4 (5.91 t ha⁻¹), and G36 (5.88 t ha⁻¹) outperformed the best check G47 (5.80 t ha⁻¹) by 1.5–7.2%. Biomass ranged from 17.2 to 17.5 t ha⁻¹ under normal conditions, with reductions of 17–30% under stress. Physiological traits showed declines of 11–28% in Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and 8–12% in chlorophyll content, along with increases of 14–23% in canopy temperature. Genotype-specific advantages included higher chlorophyll content and grain number in G13, superior biomass and kernel weight in G7 and G8, and lower canopy temperature in G9. Multi-trait indices consistently ranked these genotypes among the most stable across environments. The Genotype + Genotype × Environment (GGE) biplot analysis identified three mega-environments, with E18 (Indore-H) being both highly discriminative and representative.ConclusionsThe integration of multivariate and multi-trait analyses effectively enabled the identification of high-yielding and physiologically resilient wheat genotypes under drought and heat stress. Genotypes G7, G8, G9, and G13 demonstrated stable and superior performance across environments, making them promising candidates for developing climate-resilient wheat ideotypes. This integrative approach provides a robust framework for sustainable wheat improvement under future climate scenarios.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09090-9.

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is one of the most widely cultivated cereal crops and plays a central role in food and nutritional security. Its production supports the livelihoods of millions of smallholder farmers and contributes significantly to economic stability. However, sustaining wheat productivity has become increasingly challenging due to a narrowing genetic base, declining soil fertility under intensive cropping systems, and the intensification of biotic and abiotic stresses [1, 2]. These challenges are further exacerbated by climate change, which has altered rainfall patterns, increased the frequency of extreme temperatures, and intensified stress events during critical growth stages.

India is the second-largest producer of wheat, with production reaching approximately 117.94 million tonnes during 2024–25, contributing significantly to food security [3]. Overall, the country achieved a record foodgrain production of 357.73 million tonnes, highlighting its strong agricultural base [3]. Wheat cultivation is predominantly concentrated in the Indo-Gangetic Plains, which represent the major production mega-environments [4]. Over the past decades, substantial genetic gains have been achieved globally through the development and adoption of high-yielding and stress-adapted cultivars. However, the rate of genetic gain in wheat has been estimated at approximately 1% per year, which is insufficient to match the increasing global demand projected at ~ 1.7% annually [5]. Despite advances in breeding technologies, recent evidence indicates that wheat productivity is increasingly constrained by climate variability, particularly rising temperatures and erratic rainfall patterns [6, 7]. Terminal heat stress during the grain-filling stage has emerged as a critical limitation, especially under late-sown conditions prevalent in several mega-environments, leading to reduced grain weight and yield [8]. These challenges underscore the urgent need to develop climate-resilient wheat genotypes capable of sustaining productivity under stress conditions.

Among abiotic factors, terminal heat and drought are considered the most severe constraints for wheat productivity, particularly when they coincide with reproductive and grain-filling stages, which are crucial determinants of yield and grain quality [9, 10]. Terminal heat stress during grain filling accelerates plant senescence, disrupts enzymatic activities involved in carbohydrate metabolism, impairs starch deposition, and reduces photosynthetic efficiency, leading to decreases in grain weight, size, and overall quality [11–13]. Under field conditions, terminal heat can reduce wheat grain yield by 10–30%, with losses exceeding 60% in highly vulnerable environments, particularly in hot, dry regions, and areas with abrupt late-season temperature increase with inadequate irrigation [14]. Similarly, drought stress restricts water uptake, reduces stomatal conductance, and limits nutrient assimilation, resulting in shortened grain-filling duration, reduced biomass accumulation, and decreased harvest index [15, 16]. Global assessments indicate that drought contributes to approximately 8% annual wheat yield losses, with severe episodes causing reductions of up to 40% depending on intensity, timing, and duration [17]. The concurrence of terminal heat and drought often produces synergistic effects, resulting in compounded reductions in grain number, grain weight, and overall productivity [18]. Meta-analyses further suggest that each 1 °C increase in mean temperature during grain filling period could lower global wheat yields by ~ 6%, underscoring the urgent need for adaptation strategies to maintain yield stability under changing climatic conditions [19].

Wheat responses to these stresses involve complex physiological, biochemical, and molecular mechanisms. Physiologically, drought and heat affect water relations, stomatal regulation, photosynthetic rate, and assimilate partitioning, leading to reductions in spike fertility, grain set, and kernel weight [20]. Biochemically, stress conditions elevate reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which can damage cellular structures, proteins, and nucleic acids, triggering oxidative stress [21]. Plants mitigate such damage through the induction of antioxidant enzymes, osmolyte accumulation, and maintenance of cellular turgor. At the molecular level, several stress-responsive genes have been identified. For drought tolerance, transcription factors such as TaWRKY1 and TaWRKY33 regulate antioxidant defense systems [22], while TaP5CS promotes proline accumulation for osmotic adjustment [23], and TaVQ14 modulates ROS and calcium signaling pathways [24]. Heat tolerance is mediated in part by TaHsfA2e-5D, which regulates heat shock proteins and antioxidant enzymes, stabilizing cellular function under elevated temperatures [11]. Quantitative trait loci (QTLs) associated with canopy temperature depression, grain filling, and yield stability under stress have been identified and serve as valuable targets for marker-assisted selection and genomic prediction [25].

Despite these advances, progress remains constrained by the narrow genetic base of modern wheat cultivars, largely due to directional selection for high yield under optimal conditions. This bottleneck limits the availability of alleles conferring tolerance to abiotic stresses [2, 26]. Landraces, primitive species, and wild relatives represent untapped reservoirs of adaptive alleles that can be introgressed into elite cultivars through conventional and molecular breeding strategies, including marker-assisted selection, genomic selection, and genome editing [27]. Studies demonstrate that landraces exhibit superior tolerance to heat and drought, often possessing unique allelic variation for root traits, phenology, osmotic adjustment, and yield stability under stress conditions [28]. However, systematic incorporation of such genetic resources into breeding programs remains limited, highlighting the need for more integrated pre-breeding efforts.

Identifying wheat genotypes with stable performance across diverse environments requires multi-environment trials (METs), which enable the assessment of genotype × environment interactions (GEI) influencing yield and adaptability [29]. Given the complexity of GEI, reliance on single-trait or single-environment analyses is often insufficient. Therefore, the integration of multivariate and multi-trait approaches provides a more robust framework for evaluating genotypic performance, stability, and stress resilience across varying agro-ecological conditions [30, 31].

The present study was designed to evaluate a diverse set of 43 promising breeding lines with improved genetic backgrounds (G1 to G43) along with six checks (G44 to G49) across eight representative wheat-growing locations during the 2022–23 cropping season. The study aimed to assess the drought and heat tolerance potential of these advanced promising lines using multivariate approaches, identify stable and high-performing genotypes, and provide insights for breeding climate-resilient wheat cultivars suitable for stress-prone agro-ecologies.

Materials and methods

Experimental Site

The experimental material was planted at eight locations namely, Dharwad (15.4934°N, 74.9816°E), Pune (18.1067°N, 74.3512°E), Indore (21.5031°N, 70.4415°E), Junagadh (21°30’N, 70°30’E), Karnal (29.7050°N, 76.9924°E), Hisar (29.1509°N, 75.6977°E), Pusa in Bihar (25.9800°N, 85.6700°E), and Sabour (25.1667°N, 87.3167°E) (Fig. 1), which are considered as hot spot locations for abiotic stress screening [32], which are spread over four major wheat growing zones of India, as many of these testing sites represent the global mega-environments (MEs) [33]. For grain yield, all eight testing sites data were used, whereas for growth and yield contributing traits, data from all eight sites except Sabour were used. Similarly, for physiological traits, Pune, Karnal, Hisar, and Sabour data were used. The global spring wheat area was divided into six MEs; Karnal location typically represents ME1, and Dharwad represents ME4 [33]. Therefore, the testing sites Karnal and Hisar in the North western plains zone may fall in ME1. Similarly, testing sites in the peninsular zone, including Dharwad and Pune, belong to ME4.

Experimental material

The experimental material consisted of 49 genotypes comprising 43 promising breeding lines with improved genetic backgrounds (G1 to G43) along with six checks (G44 to G49) (Table 1). The trial was conducted in a 7 × 7 simple lattice design (7 blocks × 7 plots) under three treatments drought (D), irrigated (N), and late-sown heat (H) conditions, with a plot size of 1.5 m in length and 3 rows. The irrigated condition data were considered as control treatment and late sown condition data as heat stress treatment. The field screening guidelines for drought and heat stress were followed as per the guidelines of ICAR-IIWBR, 2025 [34]. Except for pre-sowing irrigation, no irrigation was given for drought treatment, while the recommended 5–6 irrigations were provided for irrigated and late sown treatments. The trial was sown under irrigated conditions between 10 and 15 November and late sown under irrigated conditions between 10 and 15 December 2022–23 to expose the reproductive growth stage to terminal heat stress condition. The condition-specific package of practices was followed as per recommendations in all study locations. Physiological traits

Leaf chlorophyll content, which is an indirect indicator of photosynthetic efficiency, was measured using a chlorophyll meter (SPAD-502 Plus, Konica Minolta) to record chlorophyll content index (CCI) readings. For each treatment, three biological replicates were taken. Canopy Temperature (CT) was measured during 12.00 and 14.00 h with a hand-held infrared thermometer HTC MT4 [35]. Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) was recorded using an optical handheld Green Seeker sensor (Trimble Industries, Inc.) [35]. The CCI, CT, and NDVI were recorded at 21 days after anthesis.

Growth and yield traits

The plant height (PH) was recorded using a scale from the soil surface to the tip of the spike, excluding awns in three biological replicates. Productive Tillers/metre (PTPM) was recorded in each genotype in both replications. The grain filling duration (GFD) was calculated as the difference in duration between days to maturity and days to anthesis. After harvesting, the biomass per plot (BM) was recorded using a weighing balance. The harvested plants were threshed, and grain yield/plot (GY) was recorded. The Harvest Index (HI) was derived by dividing the grain yield by the biomass. Thousand-grain weight (TGW) was obtained by counting thousand grains using a seed counter and taking their weight. Grain number per spike (GNPS) was estimated by taking the total grain numbers of 5 representative spikes, and the total was divided by five.

Weather parameters

Temporal analysis of meteorological parameters across eight locations during the crop growth period revealed significant spatial and seasonal variability in both temperature and rainfall (Fig. 2). Maximum temperatures were consistently highest in Junagadh, ranging from 32.1 °C in October to 35.4 °C in March, while minimum temperatures at this site remained above 14 °C throughout the season. In contrast, northern locations such as Pusa, Karnal, and Hisar experienced a sharp decline in minimum temperatures during winter, with values reaching as low as 4.2 °C in January at Hisar. Maximum temperatures in these regions ranged from 22 to 28 °C during the same period. Southern and western sites like Dharwad and Pune exhibited relatively stable thermal regimes, with maximum temperatures ranging from 28.5 °C to 31.2 °C, and minimum rarely falling below 12 °C.

Rainfall was highly skewed towards October, with Sabour receiving the highest rainfall of 136 mm, followed by Junagadh (84 mm). Beyond October, rainfall events were sparse; most stations recorded less than 10 mm/month, except for isolated events in January (e.g., Karnal: 12 mm) and March (e.g., Hisar: 18 mm). Sabour and Junagadh displayed the most pronounced rainfall peaks, indicating a strong influence of the retreating monsoon. These data highlight significant agroclimatic heterogeneity across locations, with implications for crop sowing windows, phenological development, and the design of location-specific agronomic interventions during the Rabi season. The study locations experienced high temperature and drought stress under late sown and rainfed conditions, respectively.

Data preprocessing

Prior to statistical analysis, raw data underwent a rigorous preprocessing phase that included data cleaning and trait-wise quality checks. Datapoints were verified and appropriately treated to ensure consistency and accuracy in the dataset. Data integrity was validated for each trait across locations and conditions to confirm readiness for downstream analyses.

Correlation and K-means cluster analysis

The linear association between the traits is examined by the correlation analysis. Its expression is given by:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:r=\frac{n\left(\sum\:Y\widehat{Y}\right)-\left(\sum\:Y\right)\left(\sum\:\widehat{Y}\right)}{\sqrt{\left[n\sum\:{Y}^{2}-{\left(\sum\:Y\right)}^{2}\right]\left[n\sum\:{\widehat{Y}}^{2}-{\left(\sum\:\widehat{Y}\right)}^{2}\right]}}$$\end{document}

Further, K-means clustering is an unsupervised machine learning technique used to partition observations into K clusters, where each observation belongs to the cluster with the nearest mean [55]. Standardization (z-score) was applied, as the variables in the dataset may have different scales. To determine the optimal number of clusters (K), the Elbow Method was used by plotting the total within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS) against K and identifying the “elbow” point [56]. Each data point was then assigned to the cluster whose centroid was closest, typically using Euclidean distance:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}D\left({x}_{i},\:{c}_{j}\right)&=\sqrt{\sum\:_{k=1}^{p}{\left({x}_{ik}-{c}_{jk}\right)}^{2}};\:{c}_{j}\\&=\frac{1}{{n}_{j}}\sum\:_{i\in\:{c}_{j}}{x}_{i}\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Identifying a meaningful number of clusters helped distinguish groups of high-performing or stable genotypes.

The AMMI model is effective for detecting genotype × environment (season) interaction (GEI) and identifying stable genotypes. The additive main effects of genotypes and locations are analyzed using univariate ANOVA, followed by the fitting of G×E interaction by PCA based on the AMMI model. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mu\:$$\end{document} = experimental mean trait value; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{g}}_{i}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{s}_{j}$$\end{document} are the ith genotype and jth environmental mean deviation from the experimental mean trait value respectively. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{k}$$\end{document} = square root of the Eigen value of the kth Interaction Principal Component (IPC) axis. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\alpha\:}_{ik}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\gamma\:}_{ij}$$\end{document} are the interaction IPC scores for kth IPC of the ith genotype and jth environment, respectively. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\epsilon\:}_{ij}$$\end{document} is the residual effect. Results

Pooled ANOVA

The pooled analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed highly significant differences (p ≤ 0.001) for all the studied traits across environments (E), genotypes (G), and genotype × environment (GEI) under normal (N), drought (D), and heat (H) stress conditions (Table 3). Notably, the mean sum of squares for the environment was markedly higher than those for genotype and GEI, indicating strong environmental influence on trait expression. The significant GEI suggests differential genotype responses across locations, emphasizing the importance of multi-environment evaluation for identifying stable and high-performing genotypes under varying abiotic stress conditions. The Tukey’s HSD test identified the top five performing genotypes for each trait under N, D, and H conditions, highlighting genotypic variation and adaptability to stress environments. For GY, Tukey’s HSD identified DBW432 as the best common genotype across the conditions and environments. The other common genotypes across conditions and environments were: HI1674 (GFD), HI1670 (PTPM), UASD22-5 and DBW432 (BM), UASD-22-2, HI1668, and NEHDT-22-2 (TKW), GW-2017-803 and PI3787 (GNPS), and BBP-MABB-50 (CCI). These common genotypes consistently appeared across conditions, indicating their stability and broad adaptability. For multi-traits, the genotypes such as G7 (GY, PH, BM, TKW), G13 (GY, BM, GNPS), G8 (GY, BM, NDVI), G9 (GY and CCI), and G4 (GY and HI) consistently ranked high, confirming their adaptive advantage through yield components and physiological efficiency. The coefficient of variation (CV%) ranged from 2.78% (CT_N) to 14.78% (GY_D), reflecting varying levels of phenotypic variability across traits and environments. Across the pooled ANOVA, the environment (E) accounted for the largest proportion of total variation for most traits, reflecting the strong influence of location and stress regime on phenotypic expression. For grain yield, E explained approximately 62–75% of the total sum of squares, while G and G \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\times\:$$\end{document} E contributed nearly 5–8% and 11–18%, respectively. The predominance of environmental variance underscores the substantial agroclimatic heterogeneity among the eight test locations and three conditions. The relatively high G × E proportion confirms that genotype rankings were not consistent across environments, necessitating stability analyses and multi-trait indices for reliable genotype selection [53, 79]. This variation partitioning aligns with patterns observed in large-scale multi-environment wheat trials [54, 55]. Here, the “normal” (N) condition refers to timely-sown, fully irrigated plots representing the non-stress control, while drought (D) denotes rainfed conditions without supplemental irrigation, and heat (H) corresponds to late-sown irrigated conditions exposing the crop to terminal heat stress during reproductive and grain-filling stages.

Association analysis across traits and conditions

The correlation analysis (Fig. 5) revealed several statistically significant (p ≤ 0.05, 0.01, 0.001) positive and negative associations among agro-morphological, physiological, and yield-related traits across normal, drought, and heat conditions. GY showed significant positive correlations with BM, PTPM, GNPS, TKW, and NDVI across all environments, suggesting these traits as key contributors to yield under both optimal and stress conditions. Similarly, BM exhibited strong positive correlations with PTPM, GNPS, and NDVI, indicating their collective role in overall plant productivity. PTPM also correlated positively with GNPS, NDVI, and TKW across conditions. NDVI was significantly positively associated with GNPS, TKW, and CCI, particularly under stress, reflecting its relevance as a proxy for photosynthetic efficiency and biomass. CCI showed significant positive correlations with NDVI, GFD, and TKW in several conditions, while GFD also had positive associations with PH, CCI, and TKW in some environments. Strong intra-trait correlations were observed across environments for the same traits (e.g., GY_N with GY_D and GY_H), indicating genotypic consistency across conditions. Under normal conditions, grain yield (GY_N) exhibited significant and positive correlations with grain yield under both drought (GY_D) and heat (GY_H), indicating stable genotypic performance across environments. Among the other traits, GY_N showed significant positive associations with chlorophyll content index under heat (CCI_H), normalized difference vegetation index under drought (NDVI_D), plant height under normal and drought conditions (PH_N and PH_D), productive tillers per metre under normal, drought, and heat conditions (PTPM_N, PTPM_D, and PTPM_H), biomass under normal, drought, and heat conditions (BM_N, BM_D, and BM_H), thousand kernel weight under normal, drought, and heat conditions (TKW_N, TKW_D, and TKW_H), as well as grain number per spike under drought and heat conditions (GNPS_D and GNPS_H). Notably, a significant negative correlation was observed between GY_N and grain filling duration under heat stress (GFD_H), suggesting that prolonged grain filling under heat may adversely affect yield potential under normal conditions, possibly due to differential physiological responses across environments.

In contrast, negative correlations were evident between CT and key traits such as GY, BM, PTPM, GNPS, NDVI, and CCI, particularly under drought and heat stress, highlighting the adverse impact of elevated canopy temperatures on plant productivity. CT also showed a negative correlation with TKW and GFD in multiple stress environments, as well as with NDVI, indicating an inverse relationship between CT and plant vigour. Additionally, GFD was significantly negatively associated with BM, PH, GY, NDVI, CCI, and GNPS, suggesting a possible trade-off between prolonged grain development and resource use efficiency. The correlation matrix (Fig. 5) encompasses 33 trait-condition combinations, wherein diagonal blocks representing within-condition correlations and off-diagonal blocks showing cross-condition associations. The colour gradient and significance annotations (NS, *, **, ***) facilitate identification of key relationships. Presenting all trait-condition combinations in a single heatmap preserves the ability to detect cross-environment consistency of trait associations, essential for identifying robust selection targets [46–81].

The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) together explained a substantial proportion of the total variation, effectively separating genotypes based on their trait profiles and environmental responses. Trait vectors such as GY, BM, PTPM, GNPS, and TKW under all three conditions (N, D, H) clustered closely and pointed in a similar direction, indicating strong positive correlations among them and their collective contribution to overall productivity. Similarly, NDVI and HI aligned with these yield-related traits, suggesting their relevance as indicators of plant vigour and resource use efficiency. In contrast, CT was oriented in the opposite direction to yield components, reinforcing its negative association with productivity under stress.

The optimal number of clusters (K = 4) was determined using the Elbow Method, which plots total within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS) against successive values of K (K = 2 to 10). A clear inflection point was observed at K = 4, beyond which the marginal decrease in WCSS was minimal, indicating that four clusters captured the predominant grouping structure. Estimation of stress indices and final ranking of genotypes

To evaluate genotype performance under drought and heat stress, a comprehensive suite of stress indices was employed (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). These included the TOL, STI, SSPI, YI, YSI, RSI, MP, GMP, HM, MRP, and PYR. Each of these indices captures different aspects of genotype resilience and productivity under abiotic stress. To consolidate this complex data, a Weighted Average Stress Index (WASI) was derived by aggregating genotype rankings across the individual stress indices. Weights were potentially assigned to each index based on their importance, thus generating a single composite score that reflects the overall stress performance of each genotype.

The WASI-based ranking under drought stress (Figs. 8a and 9) revealed that DBW358, DBW418, DBW432, DBW353, and NIAW3170 were among the top-performing genotypes with the lowest WASI values, indicating superior drought tolerance and yield stability. These genotypes consistently ranked high across multiple individual stress indices. Conversely, HI1672, UASD-22-5, BBP-MABB-50, UASD-22-2, and DBW429 were the poorest performers under drought stress, displaying higher WASI values and hence more susceptibility. Similarly, under heat stress (Figs. 8b and 9), DBW353, DBW432, BBP-MABB-41, P14111, and P13787 emerged as the most heat-tolerant genotypes with the lowest WASI ranks, demonstrating stable and higher performance across the stress indices.

MGIDI and MTSI Analysis

The multi-trait genotype-ideotype distance index (MGIDI) analysis for GY and related traits across N, D, and H conditions, and locations identified genotypes G16, G7, G33, G36, G25, G29, G2, G5, G14, G44, G22, G23, and G42 as superior, indicating their proximity to the ideal genotype profile in terms of performance across multiple traits (Fig. 10a). Concurrently, the multi-trait stability index (MTSI) analysis revealed genotypes G16, G29, G22, G25, G18, G36, and G26 as highly stable across environments (Fig. 10b). The overlap of genotypes such as G16, G29, G22, G25, and G36 in both MGIDI and MTSI highlights their dual advantage of high performance and consistent expression of traits under variable stress conditions and across multiple locations.

It is important to note, however, that the selection of G16, G29, G22, and G25 by MGIDI and MTSI reflects their multi-trait balance rather than superiority in grain yield under stress conditions specifically. MGIDI and MTSI optimise simultaneously across seven traits: grain yield (GY), grain filling duration (GFD), productive tillers per meter (PTPM), thousand kernel weight (TKW), grain number per spike (GNPS), biomass (BM), and harvest index (HI), weighting all traits equally irrespective of their economic priority. Consequently, G16 (DTS 116) achieved high MGIDI and MTSI ranks primarily because of its consistently long grain filling duration, ranked top for GFD under normal and heat conditions in Tukey’s HSD, a trait that confers multi-trait ideotype proximity but does not necessarily translate into superior grain yield under terminal heat stress. Similarly, G29 (HI1675) and G25 (HI1670) showed advantages in productive tillers per metre, while G22 (GW512) benefited from a high harvest index under drought; none of these genotypes ranked among the top performers for grain yield specifically under drought or heat stress. These genotypes represent valuable germplasm for trait-specific pre-breeding programmes targeting extended grain filling duration and high tiller productivity, respectively. In contrast, the final elite recommendation of G7, G8, G9, G13, and G36 is grounded in their convergent superiority for grain yield under stress across multiple independent analytical approaches, including Tukey’s HSD for GY, weighted average stress index (WASI), GGE biplot winning-genotype analysis, and AMMI stability indices.

Discussion

The present investigation highlights the importance of multi-environment evaluation in identifying wheat genotypes with stable performance under drought and heat stress. Assessing advanced lines across diverse mega-environments captured wide variation in stress responses and ensured reliable genotype selection under contrasting conditions [88–90]. By integrating yield traits, physiological parameters, and multivariate analyses, this study adopted a holistic framework consistent with recent advances in climate-resilient wheat breeding [81, 91]. The combined use of univariate, multivariate, and stress index–based analyses proved effective in dissecting complex GEI, enabling the clustering of genotypes with superior resilience and stability [92]. Such integrative evaluations are vital for accelerating the development of climate-resilient cultivars capable of sustaining productivity in stress-prone agro-ecologies [93].

Genotypic performance under normal vis-à-vis stress

The observed reductions in yield, yield components, and physiological traits under stress highlight the differential impact of drought and heat on wheat performance. Drought primarily constrained biomass accumulation, plant height, grain number, chlorophyll content, and canopy greenness, indicating limitations in assimilate production, whereas heat stress more strongly affected grain filling duration and kernel weight while increasing canopy temperature. These contrasting responses align with earlier findings that drought limits source capacity, while heat accelerates phenological development and shortens the grain-filling period [81]. The overall yield reduction (~ 20–30%) observed across stress environments is consistent with global reports [94], emphasizing the vulnerability of wheat production systems to climatic stresses. The presence of genotypic variation, particularly in maintaining grain filling under heat stress (e.g., G16, G28), suggests opportunities for targeted trait-based selection to enhance resilience.

The significant genotype × environment interaction (GEI) observed across traits confirms that wheat performance is highly environment-dependent [95]. The occurrence of crossover interactions, where genotype rankings varied across environments, further highlights the complexity of breeding for stability under stress conditions [96]. Such variability reinforces the importance of multi-environment trials for identifying genotypes with broad adaptation [97, 98]. Consistent with previous studies [99, 100], both genotype and GEI contributed significantly to variation in yield-related traits. The strong environmental influence observed in this study reflects the increasing impact of climate-driven stresses, particularly drought and heat, on trait expression [89], underscoring the need for robust evaluation frameworks.

The relationships between yield and associated traits provide important insights into the physiological basis of stress adaptation. Grain yield showed positive associations with biomass, productive tillers, grain number, kernel weight, and NDVI, confirming their role as key determinants of productivity under both optimal and stress conditions [101]. Biomass reflects source strength [102], while grain number and tiller productivity contribute to sink capacity. Kernel weight represents grain filling efficiency, which is particularly sensitive to terminal stress [81, 103]. NDVI effectively captured canopy health and photosynthetic activity [104]. In contrast, canopy temperature showed a negative association with yield, supporting its use as an indicator of stress susceptibility [105, 106]. Lower canopy temperature, or higher canopy temperature depression, reflects improved transpirational cooling and has been widely recognized as a reliable trait for identifying stress-resilient genotypes [107–109]. These findings reinforce the importance of incorporating physiological traits alongside agronomic parameters in breeding programs [110].

At the genotype level, differential trait combinations contributed to performance under stress. G13 exhibited a favorable balance of yield and physiological efficiency, while G8 demonstrated strong biomass accumulation and canopy persistence. G9 and G37 combined high yield with improved chlorophyll retention and lower canopy temperature, indicating enhanced stress tolerance. Notably, G7 achieved the highest yield across environments despite moderate physiological expression, suggesting the presence of stable genetic mechanisms conferring broad adaptability. These observations support the concept of general resilience in wheat improvement [111] and highlight the value of combining physiological indicators with agronomic traits to identify superior genotypes [112, 113].

Stability analyses further reinforced the complexity of genotype performance across environments, with all classical models confirming the presence of significant GEI and crossover interactions. Rather than focusing solely on rankings, these analyses highlight the importance of identifying both broadly adapted and specifically adapted genotypes. For instance, while some genotypes exhibited high stability across environments, others demonstrated superior performance within specific mega-environments, reflecting the inherent trade-off between yield potential and stability [114, 115]. The delineation of three major mega-environments through GGE biplot analysis underscores the spatial variability in stress patterns and the need for environment-specific breeding strategies. The identification of representative and discriminative test locations, particularly the prominence of E18 (Indore-H), provides valuable insights for optimizing future multi-environment trials and enhancing selection efficiency [79, 114, 116].

Importantly, the convergence of results across multiple analytical approaches highlights the value of integrating diverse statistical frameworks. While AMMI and GGE biplots primarily capture yield stability and adaptability, indices such as WASI, MGIDI, and MTSI extend the evaluation to multi-trait performance and stress resilience. The variation in genotype rankings across methods reflects their differing statistical emphases, yet their complementarity offers a more holistic understanding of genotype behavior. This reinforces the notion that no single method is sufficient to capture the multidimensional nature of genotype performance under complex stress conditions [116, 117]. Specifically, MGIDI and MTSI assign equal importance to grain yield and associated physiological and yield component traits across all evaluated characteristics, meaning genotypes with exceptional performance in traits such as grain filling duration (GFD), productive tillers per metre (PTPM), or harvest index (HI) may achieve high rankings despite comparatively lower grain yield under stress conditions. Accordingly, G16, G29, G22, and G25 are recognized here as valuable for trait-specific pre-breeding, particularly for extended grain filling duration and high tiller productivity, but are distinguished from the final elite recommendation based on their comparatively lower grain yield performance under stress. Modern multi-trait indices such as MGIDI and MTSI provided additional insights by integrating multiple traits into unified selection criteria, enabling the identification of genotypes with both productivity and stability advantages. The overlap of selected genotypes across these approaches indicates the robustness of these selections and supports their potential for breeding applications. Integrating these indices through composite approaches such as WASI enhances selection accuracy by accounting for multiple dimensions of performance, thereby reducing the risk of selecting genotypes with narrow adaptation. Such integrative frameworks are increasingly recognized as essential tools for improving genetic gain under variable and stress-prone environments [62, 116, 117]. Together, classical and modern analytical approaches provide a comprehensive strategy for identifying elite, broadly adapted genotypes and advancing breeding under climate variability [54, 118, 119].

Breeding implications

The differential responses of wheat genotypes to drought and heat stress highlight the need for trait-based and integrative breeding strategies to achieve climate resilience. Traits such as higher biomass, improved chlorophyll retention, and lower canopy temperature were consistently associated with superior performance, indicating their importance in conferring stress tolerance and yield stability. The contrasting sensitivity of traits under drought and heat stress further suggests that different physiological mechanisms govern adaptation to these stresses. While drought primarily affected traits linked to water acquisition and photosynthetic capacity, heat stress predominantly influenced phenology and grain filling processes.

These findings emphasize that breeding for broad adaptation will likely require the pyramiding of complementary tolerance mechanisms rather than reliance on single traits. Advances in high-throughput phenotyping and genomic selection offer promising avenues to accelerate the development of multi-stress–tolerant ideotypes. The consistent performance of genotypes such as G7, G13, G8, G9, G4, and G36 across stress environments highlights their potential as valuable donor parents for developing climate-resilient cultivars. In addition, the identification and registration of genotypes with superior stress tolerance in national genetic resources further strengthen the breeding pipeline by providing reliable sources of adaptive traits.

The present study was conducted during a single growing season (2022–23), which provides a detailed assessment of genotype performance across diverse environments but does not capture temporal variability. In multi-environment trials, genotype × environment interactions are influenced by both spatial variation across locations and year-to-year variation. Nevertheless, the inclusion of eight geographically diverse locations representing major wheat-growing mega-environments offers substantial spatial variability, enabling reliable identification of genotypes with broad adaptation under drought and heat stress. The consistent performance of selected genotypes across locations and analytical approaches indicates their strong potential under the tested conditions.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that integrating multi-environment trials across eight diverse locations with physiological trait evaluation and multivariate stability analyses provides a robust framework for identifying wheat genotypes resilient to drought and heat stress. Genotypes G7 and G13 consistently combined high grain yield with stability across environments, while G9 and G37 maintained superior physiological performance, including favourable canopy temperature and chlorophyll retention, under stress conditions. In contrast, G16 highlighted the trade-off between yield potential and stability, emphasizing the complexity of genotype response under combined stresses. Multi-trait indices (MGIDI and MTSI), which simultaneously evaluate proximity to an ideotype and stability across seven traits (GY, GFD, PTPM, TKW, GNPS, BM, and HI) with equal weighting, additionally identified G16, G29, G22, G25, and G36 as superior for combined multi-trait performance and stability, with particular strengths in grain filling duration, productive tillers, and harvest index. While these genotypes represent valuable germplasm for trait-specific pre-breeding, the integrated final recommendation is based on convergent superiority for grain yield under stress across multiple independent analyses: G7 (DBW353) consistently achieved the highest grain yield under heat stress, the lowest WASI composite rank for heat tolerance, and was the winning genotype across the largest GGE mega-environment (ME1, anchored by E18, Indore under late-sown heat stress), with superior biomass and kernel weight further supporting its selection; G13 (DBW432) led under drought in Tukey’s HSD and was the winning genotype in GGE ME2, also being MGIDI-selected; G8 (DBW358) showed strong drought performance with superior biomass accumulation and high NDVI retention under stress; G9 (DBW418) combined high grain yield with superior chlorophyll retention and lower canopy temperature, reflecting robust physiological stress buffering; and G36 (P13787) bridged multi-trait and yield-centric frameworks, being MGIDI-selected and ranking consistently high in grain number per spike. The combined application of classical approaches with modern multi-trait indices strengthened the reliability of genotype selection, with G7, G8, G9, G13, and G36 repeatedly identified as superior across analytical approaches. This consistency indicates that, these genotypes possess broad adaptation and stable performance across contrasting environments rather than condition-specific advantages. The findings demonstrate that selection based solely on grain yield or single-environment evaluation would overlook genotypes with strong physiological buffering capacity under stress. Instead, integrating yield with physiological traits such as NDVI, canopy temperature, and chlorophyll content provides a more accurate basis for identifying resilient genotypes. The consistently superior genotypes identified in this study represent candidates for use as donor parents in multi-stress breeding programs targeting drought and heat-prone environments. Furthermore, the identification of stable test environments (e.g., E18; Indore-H) provides practical guidance for optimizing future multi-location trials and improving selection efficiency. Furthermore, G16 and G29, identified by MGIDI and MTSI for their multi-trait advantages in grain filling duration and productive tiller productivity, respectively, are specifically recommended for trait-based pre-breeding programmes targeting these component traits in climate-resilient wheat improvement. This study establishes that combining multi-environment evaluation with multi-trait and multivariate analytical approaches is essential for reliably identifying climate-resilient wheat genotypes capable of maintaining productivity under increasing drought and heat stress conditions.
