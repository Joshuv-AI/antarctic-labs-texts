Clinical impact of mean pulmonary arterial pressure after balloon pulmonary angioplasty for inoperable chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension
Authors: Ryo Takano, Tatsuo Aoki, Shinya Fujisaki, Mitsumasa Akao, Hiroyuki Endo, Naruhiro Nishi, Hiroya Hayashi, Akiyuki Kotoku, Hiroki Horinouchi, Takatoyo Kiko, Ryotaro Asano, Jin Ueda, Akihiro Tsuji, Kenichi Tsujita, Teruo Noguchi, Tetsuya Fukuda, Takeshi Ogo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBalloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA) improves haemodynamics in patients with inoperable chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH). Previous studies on BPA have set the treatment objective to achieve a mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) of <30 mm Hg. However, the clinical impact of mPAP after BPA remains unclear. This study aimed to stratify patients according to their mPAP after BPA and evaluate its association with clinical status and long-term outcomes.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 304 patients with inoperable CTEPH (median age, 72 (61 to 79) years) who underwent BPA and follow-up right heart catheterisation. Patients were categorised by mPAP after BPA: ≤20, >20–<30 and ≥30 mm Hg groups.ResultsThe WHO functional classification, 6 min walk distance and right ventricular ejection fraction were significantly better in the group with a lower mPAP (trend test p=0.007, p<0.001 and p=0.002, respectively). Additionally, the proportions of patients who required pulmonary vasodilators or oxygen therapy were significantly lower in the group with a lower mPAP (trend test p<0.001 and p<0.001, respectively). Across all multivariable models, the mPAP ≥30 mm Hg group had a significantly poorer prognosis compared with the mPAP ≤20 mm Hg group. In contrast, no significant difference was observed between the mPAP ≤20 mm Hg and >20–<30 mm Hg groups.ConclusionsIn patients with CTEPH after BPA, an mPAP of <30 mm Hg was associated with a favourable prognosis, and patients with an mPAP of ≤20 mm Hg presented with better symptoms, exercise capacity, right ventricular function and more withdrawal from oxygen therapy and pulmonary vasodilators. The BPA treatment goal may require individual adaptation.Keywords: Hypertension, Pulmonary; Cardiac Catheterization; Outcome Assessment, Health Care

Introduction

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) is a rare disease characterised by pulmonary artery stenosis or occlusion due to organised thrombus and secondary microvasculopathy.1,3 Treatment options for CTEPH, which include pulmonary endarterectomy, balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA) and pulmonary vasodilators, are recommended considering patients’ anatomical lesions and conditions.4,8 Among them, BPA, first reported by Feinstein et al in 20019 and subsequently refined, has been reported to improve clinical parameters and prognosis in patients with inoperable CTEPH.10,15

BPA requires repeated treatment procedures, and its goal is determined by expert experience. Most previous studies on BPA used a mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) of <30 mm Hg as a treatment goal.1316,18 However, the clinical impact of an mPAP of <30 mm Hg after BPA remains unknown. The latest European Society of Cardiology/European Respiratory Society guidelines published in 2022 updated the definition of pulmonary hypertension to an mPAP of >20 mm Hg.4 5 A recent report on pulmonary arterial hypertension showed that even an mPAP of 21–24 mm Hg can affect long-term prognosis.19 Even in patients with CTEPH, an mPAP of <20 mm Hg after BPA may have better outcomes. However, no studies have investigated the outcomes of patients with CTEPH according to the mPAP after BPA.

In patients with CTEPH, mPAP levels after BPA may influence clinical outcomes and long-term prognosis, and may help define treatment goals. However, a resting mPAP level after BPA has not been established as a treatment goal for CTEPH. Therefore, this study stratified patients by mPAP after BPA and evaluated its association with clinical status and long-term outcomes.

Methods

Study population

We retrospectively reviewed the records of 357 consecutive patients with inoperable CTEPH who underwent BPA without pulmonary endarterectomy at our institution between February 2011 and December 2022. Patients were diagnosed with CTEPH based on standard diagnostic criteria with reference to imaging findings (ventilation-perfusion scan, CT or pulmonary angiography) and haemodynamic criteria (mPAP of ≥25 mm Hg). They were deemed inoperable due to anatomically distal, surgically inaccessible lesions (n=239, 79%), intolerance to surgery due to severe comorbidities (n=45, 15%) or refusal of surgery despite surgical indications (n=20, 7%), as determined in a multidisciplinary conference that included cardiologists, radiologists and surgeons. Among the 357 patients, 53 were excluded because they did not undergo follow-up right heart catheterisation (RHC). A total of 1690 BPA sessions (median 5 (4–6) procedures per patient) were performed in the remaining 304 patients.

In this study, the 304 patients who completed BPA and underwent follow-up RHC after BPA were stratified by their mPAP at the first follow-up RHC after BPA into the following three groups: mPAP of ≤20, >20–<30 and ≥30 mm Hg groups.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Cerebral and Cardiovascular Center (approval number: R20075-3). The requirement for written informed consent was waived, and data were collected in accordance with the standards set by the Institutional Review Board of the National Cerebral and Cardiovascular Center.

BPA procedure

BPA was performed as described previously.11 20 21 Target vessels were determined using preoperative contrast-enhanced CT and pulmonary angiography. BPA was performed through the right femoral or internal jugular vein using a 6F long sheath (BRITE TIP sheath introducer; Cordis, New Brunswick, New Jersey). We selectively introduced a 6F multipurpose guiding catheter (Mach1 Peripheral MP; Boston Scientific, Natick, Massachusetts) or a 6F Ikari Left catheter (Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) with a soft-tipped 0.035-inch wire (Radifocus Guide Wire M; Terumo) into the target vessel. The target lesions were dilated with a balloon (Ikazuchi; Kaneka, Osaka, Japan) using a 0.014-inch wire (B-Pahm; Japan Lifeline, Tokyo, Japan; or Cruise; Asahi Intec, Tokyo, Japan; Agosal XS 0.8; Asahi Intec; or Chevalier universal; Cordis/Johnson & Johnson, New Brunswick, New Jersey). The appropriate balloon size was selected based on the targeted vessel diameter measured using CT and angiographic findings.

Data collection

We retrospectively collected the following data from medical records: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), WHO functional classification (WHO-FC), 6 min walk distance (6MWD), plasma brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) level, medication, oxygen therapy, haemodynamic parameters obtained at the follow-up RHC performed 3 months after the last BPA session, and right ventricular function assessed by cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging.

Haemodynamic evaluation included mPAP, pulmonary arterial wedge pressure, right atrial pressure, cardiac output (CO), mixed venous oxygen saturation and arterial oxygen saturation (SaO₂) in the supine position using the Swan–Ganz catheter method. CO was determined using the indirect Fick method, and the cardiac index (CI) was calculated using CO and body surface area.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as medians with IQRs. The mPAP after BPA was categorised into three groups (mPAP of ≤20, >20–<30 and ≥30 mm Hg groups) and treated as an ordinal variable. Comparisons among the three groups were evaluated using the Kruskal-Wallis test for age and BMI or Fisher’s exact test for sex. Trends across the three groups for WHO-FC, 6MWD, BNP, the proportion of patients treated with pulmonary vasodilators or oxygen therapy, haemodynamics and data on CMR images were evaluated using the Cochran-Armitage and Jonckheere-Terpstra tests for categorical and continuous variables, respectively, as appropriate. Unadjusted survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared among the mPAP of ≤20, >20–<30 and ≥30 mm Hg groups using the log-rank test. Cox regression analysis was used to evaluate mortality risk across the three groups, expressed as HRs with 95% CIs. Four multivariable models were constructed, all adjusted using baseline data. Model 1 was adjusted for age at baseline and sex. Model 2 was adjusted for age at baseline, sex and the change in mPAP from baseline to follow-up. Model 3 was adjusted for age at baseline, sex, mPAP at baseline and the change in mPAP from baseline to follow-up. Model 4 was adjusted for age at baseline, sex, mPAP at baseline and the use of any pulmonary vasodilators at baseline. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP V.18.0 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina).

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Clinical parameters in each group

The clinical parameters of each group are presented in table 2. The WHO-FC score was significantly better in the group with a lower mPAP after BPA than in the other groups (p=0.007, figure 2A). Of the 304 patients, 274 were evaluated for the 6MWD. The 6MWD was significantly better in the group with a lower mPAP after BPA than in the other groups (p<0.001, figure 2B). No significant trend was observed for plasma BNP levels across the three groups (p=0.059, figure 2C). Right ventricular function on CMR images was evaluated in 250 patients. Although the right ventricular ejection fraction (RVEF) was significantly better in the group with a lower mPAP after BPA (p=0.007, figure 2D), no significant trends were observed for the right ventricular end-diastolic volume index and right ventricular stroke volume index across the three groups.

Withdrawal from treatment in each group

A total of 192 patients received pulmonary vasodilators before the first BPA session. Pulmonary vasodilators were continued in 86 patients after the follow-up RHC, and their discontinuation was considered at the discretion of each physician based on a favourable clinical course and the patient’s desires. The proportion of patients who did not require pulmonary vasodilators was higher in the group with a lower mPAP after BPA than in the other groups: 89%, 61% and 19% in the groups with an mPAP of ≤20, >20–<30 and ≥30 mm Hg, respectively (p<0.001, figure 3A).

Withdrawal from treatment in each group. Comparisons among the three groups of withdrawal from treatment, including (A) oxygen therapy and (B) pulmonary vasodilators, are shown. mPAP, mean pulmonary arterial pressure.

Oxygen therapy was administered to 187 patients before the first BPA session. The SaO2 at follow-up RHC was significantly higher in the group with a lower mPAP after BPA than in the other groups (p<0.001, table 2). In total, 74 patients required oxygen therapy after the follow-up RHC. The discontinuation of oxygen therapy was considered at the discretion of each physician based on the lowest peripheral oxygen saturation of ≥90% during exercise. Furthermore, the proportion of patients who did not require oxygen therapy was significantly higher in the group with a lower mPAP after BPA than in the other groups: 88%, 70% and 24% in the groups with an mPAP of ≤20, >20–<30 and ≥30 mm Hg, respectively (p<0.001, figure 3B).

Prognosis in each group

A total of 13 deaths were recorded (four, four and five in the groups with an mPAP of ≤20, >20–<30 and ≥30 mm Hg, respectively) during the follow-up: five died from pneumonia and acute respiratory failure, one died from suicide and seven died suddenly from an unknown cause. The unadjusted survival curves for all patients are shown in figure 4A. The estimated 1-year, 3-year and 5-year survival rates were 99%, 98% and 95%, respectively. Additionally, the log-rank test suggested a significant difference in the prognosis among the three groups (p<0.001), and an unadjusted survival curve for each of the three groups indicated that patients in the group with an mPAP of ≥30 mm Hg had worse long-term survival than those in the groups with an mPAP of ≤20 and >20–<30 mm Hg (figure 4B). The estimated 1-year, 3-year and 5-year survival rates were 99%, 98% and 97%, respectively, in the group with an mPAP of ≤20 mm Hg; 99%, 99% and 96% in the group with an mPAP of >20–<30 mm Hg; and 100%, 91% and 78% in the group with an mPAP of ≥30 mm Hg. As shown in table 3, the results of Cox regression analyses using model 1 (adjusted for age at baseline and sex), model 2 (adjusted for age at baseline, sex, and the change in mPAP from baseline to follow-up), model 3 (adjusted for age at baseline, sex, mPAP at baseline, and the change in mPAP from baseline to follow-up), and model 4 (adjusted for age at baseline, sex, mPAP at baseline, and the use of any pulmonary vasodilators at baseline) consistently suggested that the group with an mPAP of ≥30 mm Hg had significantly worse long-term survival than the group with an mPAP of ≤20 mm Hg. No difference in prognosis was found between the groups with an mPAP of ≤20 and >20–<30 mm Hg.

Discussion

This study investigated the effects of mPAP after BPA on clinical outcomes and long-term prognosis. We compared clinical parameters, withdrawal from pulmonary vasodilators or oxygen therapy and prognosis across three groups: mPAP≤20, >20–<30 and ≥30 mm Hg. Key findings were: (1) patients with mPAP ≤20 mm Hg showed greater improvements in symptoms, exercise tolerance, plasma BNP levels and RVEF. (2) Fewer patients in this group continued treatment with pulmonary vasodilators and oxygen therapy. (3) Although patients with mPAP <30 mm Hg had a favourable long-term prognosis, outcomes were similar between the mPAP ≤20 mm Hg and >20–<30 mm Hg groups.

In this study, the overall prognosis after BPA was favourable, although a small number of patients did not achieve the general treatment goal of an mPAP of <30 mm Hg. The group with an mPAP of ≥30 mm Hg had a significantly poorer prognosis than the other groups, which is similar to the findings of previous reports describing the natural history of CTEPH.22 Patients who achieved an mPAP of <25 mm Hg after BPA were reportedly less likely to experience recurrent pulmonary hypertension and had a better prognosis.21 In the current study, prognosis was similar between groups with an mPAP of ≤20 mm Hg and >20–<30 mm Hg. This suggests that mild pulmonary hypertension after BPA is acceptable for selected patients—such as older individuals with reduced activity and those unable to undergo frequent treatment due to psychiatric or other disorders—when the primary goal is to improve prognosis rather than to relieve symptoms or improve exercise tolerance. However, since the results may not have been fully adjusted for patient background, the findings should be interpreted cautiously. Although the median observation period was 44 months, it might still be insufficient for assessing the long-term prognosis of patients with mild pulmonary hypertension.

The group with normalised haemodynamics after BPA (a group with an mPAP of ≤20 mm Hg) had milder symptoms, better exercise tolerance, lower plasma BNP levels and a higher RVEF. This suggests that BPA aimed at lowering mPAP leads to improved clinical parameters, including symptoms, exercise tolerance and right ventricular function. The decrease in mPAP after BPA may have reduced afterload in the right heart system and contributed to the improvements in symptoms and exercise tolerance. Although no trend in the CI, considered a factor contributing to exercise tolerance, was found across the three groups, the heart rate was lower in the group with a lower mPAP than in the other groups. On CMR images, no trends in right ventricular end-diastolic volume index or right ventricular stroke volume index were found across the three groups; however, RVEF was better in the lower mPAP group than in the other groups, reflecting a decrease in afterload. However, further research is required to determine whether this favourable RVEF and low resting heart rate are beneficial during exercise. Some reports indicate that improvement in resting mPAP alone is insufficient to improve symptoms and exercise tolerance.23 A previous report suggested that although resting mPAP did not correlate with 6MWD, peak oxygen consumption in exercise testing correlated with 6MWD.24 However, the present study did not evaluate exercise-induced pulmonary hypertension, and it may be insufficient to evaluate symptoms and exercise tolerance based on resting haemodynamic assessment alone.

Fewer patients with lower mPAP required oxygen therapy or pulmonary vasodilators. We speculate that improved oxygenation and haemodynamics in this group prompted treatment withdrawal. Given that oxygen therapy restricts daily life, achieving an mPAP of ≤20 mm Hg—and thus reducing treatment reliance—may enhance the quality of life. However, as treatment withdrawal was at each physician’s discretion, the findings may not be generalisable. In Japan, pulmonary vasodilators, including riociguat25 and selexipag,26 are used to treat microvasculopathy in patients with CTEPH. A recent report suggests that pulmonary vasodilators after BPA are effective, particularly in older patients,27 and further studies are warranted to determine the criteria for the continuation or discontinuation of pulmonary vasodilators after BPA.

This study explored the clinical impact of mPAP after BPA and suggested goals for mPAP after BPA. Normalisation of mPAP after BPA is required to improve clinical parameters, such as symptoms and exercise tolerance. A previous report has shown that additional BPA after BPA treatment can be safely performed and can improve symptoms and 6MWD.28 In cases of mild pulmonary hypertension—defined as an mPAP >20–<30 mm Hg with lesions suitable for additional BPA—further BPA may be considered to improve symptoms and exercise tolerance. Further studies are needed to assess the haemodynamic benefits of adding pulmonary vasodilators in patients without BPA-treatable lesions. Moreover, depending on the patient’s daily activities and treatment tolerability, mild pulmonary hypertension with an mPAP >20–<30 mm Hg after BPA may be acceptable solely to improve prognosis. Consequently, BPA treatment should be tailored to the individual patient’s condition.

Study limitations

This study has some limitations. First, this was a single-centre retrospective study with few cases, limiting the generalisability of its findings; thus, prospective multicentre studies are needed. Second, although the findings suggest an association between mPAP after BPA and clinical parameters as well as long-term prognosis, establishing a causal relationship between BPA-induced mPAP reduction and these outcomes was hindered by inadequate adjustment for patient background and other factors. Moreover, as an exploratory study, robust causal inferences were inherently challenging. Third, this study included only patients who underwent follow-up RHC, and 53 patients were excluded for this reason. Among these excluded patients, 11 died during the follow-up period, raising the possibility that selection bias cannot be completely ruled out. In the analytic cohort (n=304), pneumonia and unknown cause were frequent among the 13 deaths; by contrast, in the excluded group (n=53), of the 11 deaths only one was due to pneumonia and two were of unknown cause. The remaining causes included malignancy (n=4), ischaemic heart disease (n=1), senility (n=1), cellulitis (n=1) and intestinal obstruction (n=1). This distribution suggests that deaths in the excluded cohort were not predominantly attributable to CTEPH, and comorbidities may have limited the feasibility of follow-up RHC. Fourth, CMR images were performed as close as possible to the first follow-up RHC, with a median interval of 0 months (IQR 0–2). However, the variability in timing between CMR and RHC may have led to inconsistencies in the assessment of right ventricular function relative to haemodynamic status, potentially introducing measurement bias. Finally, the criteria for withdrawing oxygen therapy and pulmonary vasodilators were unclear and based on each physician’s discretion.

Conclusions

In patients with CTEPH after BPA, an mPAP of <30 mm Hg was associated with a favourable prognosis, and patients with an mPAP of ≤20 mm Hg presented with better symptoms, exercise capacity, right ventricular function and more withdrawal from oxygen therapy and pulmonary vasodilators. The BPA treatment goal may require individual adaptation.
