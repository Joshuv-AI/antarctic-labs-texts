Agmatine and N-hydroxypipecolic acid synergistically enhance salt tolerance in wheat through antioxidant defense, proline metabolism, and photosynthetic protection
Authors: Ghulam Murtaza, Oybek Mamarakhimov, Khasan Musaev, Mamlakat Djabbarova, Lola Sanayeva, Negmurod Rashidov, Khayrullo Ibragimov, Sajad Ali, Mohammed S. Alotaibi, Aziza Huseynova, Nazih Y. Rebouh, Sajid Ullah, Rashid Iqbal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSalt stress severely limits wheat growth by disturbing photosynthesis, osmotic balance, and cellular redox homeostasis. This study evaluated whether agmatine and N-hydroxy pipecolic acid, applied alone or in combination, could improve wheat tolerance under 200 mM NaCl stress. Wheat plants were grown under greenhouse conditions and treated with agmatine at 100 µM, N-hydroxypipecolic acid at 0.01 µM, or their combined application. Salt stress reduced growth, leaf area, leaf relative water content, chlorophyll content, gas exchange, Rubisco activity, and biomass accumulation while increasing hydrogen peroxide, lipid peroxidation, and electrolyte leakage. Both compounds improved wheat performance under salinity, but their combined application produced the strongest response across the measured growth, photosynthetic, osmotic, and antioxidant traits. The combined treatment improved root and shoot growth, maintained higher chlorophyll content and Rubisco activity, supported gas exchange, and increased soluble sugar accumulation. It also reduced oxidative injury by strengthening catalase, peroxidase, superoxide dismutase, and glutathione reductase activities. In addition, the combined treatment increased proline accumulation and activated key enzymes involved in proline metabolism, indicating improved osmotic adjustment under salt stress. These findings suggest that agmatine and N-hydroxy pipecolic acid act through complementary physiological and biochemical pathways to improve wheat salt tolerance. The combined treatment may offer a useful foliar strategy for improving wheat performance under saline conditions, although field validation and dose optimization are needed before practical recommendation.

Introduction

Soil salinity severely limits sustainable wheat production by disrupting water uptake, ion balance, photosynthesis, and redox stability. 1 , 2 Nearly 1.4 billion hectares of land are already salt-affected, while another one billion hectares remain at risk due to climate pressure and poor land and water management. 3 Wheat is highly sensitive to salinity, 4 which reduces growth, 5 leaf expansion, 6 carbon fixation, 7 and grain formation. 8 , 9 Excess sodium and chloride cause osmotic stress, 10 , 11 ionic toxicity, 12 metabolic damage, 13 and biomass loss. 14 , 15 Although wheat activates morphological, physiological, biochemical, and molecular defenses, these responses often fail under severe stress. 16 , 17 Salinity also lowers stomatal conductance, chlorophyll stability, Rubisco activity, and photosynthesis while increasing reactive oxygen species such as hydrogen peroxide. 18 , 19 As a result, improving photosynthetic capacity and antioxidant defense is essential for reducing salt damage in wheat. 20 , 21

Plant biostimulants and defense-related metabolites have gained attention as practical tools to enhance stress resilience without imposing major changes in crop management. 22 , 23 Agmatine, a decarboxylated arginine derivative and polyamine precursor, 24 , 25 may improve stress tolerance by regulating osmotic adjustment, antioxidant activity, membrane stability, nitrogen metabolism, and stress signaling. Through its connection with polyamine metabolism, agmatine can influence cellular protection, reactive oxygen species detoxification, and carbon allocation under adverse environments. 26 , 27 N-hydroxy pipecolic acid is an emerging defense signal associated with systemic acquired resistance and stress memory. 28 Although it has mainly been studied in plant immunity, its defense priming function suggests a wider role in preparing plants for abiotic stress by activating protective metabolism before severe injury develops. 29-31 These two compounds may therefore act through different but complementary routes, with agmatine supporting metabolic adjustment and N-hydroxy pipecolic acid strengthening defense readiness.

Despite increasing interest in plant priming agents, the combined role of agmatine and N-hydroxy pipecolic acid in wheat salt tolerance remains largely unexplored. Most previous studies have examined single regulators or focused on isolated physiological responses. A major knowledge gap remains in the understanding of whether metabolic protection and defense priming can act together to reprogram wheat responses to salinity across photosynthetic, osmotic, and antioxidant pathways. This gap is important because salt tolerance depends not on a single trait but on the coordinated maintenance of water status, chlorophyll function, Rubisco activity, soluble sugar accumulation, proline metabolism, and reactive oxygen species scavenging. A combined strategy could therefore provide stronger protection than either compound alone. This study is important for wheat production because salinity is a major constraint in arid and semi-arid regions, including salt-affected wheat-growing areas, where low-cost foliar strategies may help improve crop performance under stress. The central hypothesis was that co-application of these compounds would produce a synergistic protective effect by integrating photosynthetic preservation, osmotic adjustment, proline metabolic activation, and antioxidant defense. A second hypothesis was that this combined treatment would reduce salt-induced oxidative injury more effectively than individual treatments by lowering hydrogen peroxide accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and electrolyte leakage while increasing catalase, peroxidase, superoxide dismutase, and glutathione reductase activities. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate how agmatine and N-hydroxy pipecolic acid, applied individually and in combination, regulate wheat performance under salt stress. The specific objectives were to assess their effects on growth traits, leaf water status, chlorophyll content, gas exchange, Rubisco activity, soluble sugar accumulation, oxidative stress biomarkers, antioxidant enzyme activities, and proline metabolism. This study is novel because it evaluated agmatine and N-hydroxy pipecolic acid together as complementary foliar regulators of wheat salt tolerance. Rather than focusing on a single stress marker, it links growth recovery with photosynthetic protection, osmotic adjustment, antioxidant defense, and proline metabolism. Because molecular markers were not measured, the study interprets the combined treatment through physiological and biochemical evidence.

Materials and methods

Plant material, biostimulant preparation, and salt stress treatment

Wheat seeds of cultivar MH 21 were obtained from the Wheat Research Institute, Faisalabad, Pakistan. Healthy and uniform seeds were selected and surface sterilized with 1% sodium hypochlorite solution for 10 min. The seeds were then rinsed several times with deionized water to remove residual sterilant.

Agmatine and N-hydroxy pipecolic acid were used as foliar-applied biostimulants. Agmatine was applied at 100 µM, while N-hydroxy pipecolic acid was applied at 0.01 µM. The agmatine concentration was selected as a low and physiologically safe foliar dose based on previous evidence that exogenous agmatine improves plant performance under salinity through antioxidant defense, osmotic adjustment, and membrane protection. The N-hydroxy pipecolic acid concentration was selected as a low dose priming treatment because N-hydroxy pipecolic acid functions as a defense-related signal, and low concentrations can stimulate plant responses, whereas high concentrations may inhibit growth. Therefore, 0.01 µM was used to activate defense priming without causing growth suppression.

Stock solutions of agmatine and N-hydroxy pipecolic acid were prepared separately. The required amount of each compound was first dissolved in 5 mL of ethanol and then diluted to 100 mL with deionized water. Working solutions of agmatine at 100 µM and N-hydroxy pipecolic acid at 0.01 µM were prepared by diluting the stock solutions with deionized water immediately before foliar application. The final ethanol concentration in the spray solution was kept equal across all the treatments. Control plants and salt-stressed plants without biostimulant treatment received the same carrier solution containing ethanol and deionized water but without agmatine or N-hydroxy pipecolic acid. This solvent control was maintained to ensure that plant responses resulted from the applied compounds rather than ethanol. Salt stress was imposed using sodium chloride. A 1 M NaCl stock solution was prepared by dissolving the required quantity of NaCl in deionized water and making the final volume to 100 mL. The stock was diluted with water to obtain the required 200 mM NaCl treatment. The concentration of 200 mM NaCl was selected as a severe salinity level because it produces clear reductions in wheat growth, photosynthesis, water status, and redox stability while allowing plants to survive for physiological and biochemical assessment.

Experimental framework and treatment allocation

The pot experiment was conducted in the greenhouse of the Faculty of Agriculture and Environment, The Islamia University of Bahawalpur, Bahawalpur, Pakistan. The experimental site is located in an arid to semi-arid region where high temperatures, low rainfall, and saline soil conditions are common. Wheat plants were grown in plastic pots containing 8 kg of air-dried and sieved soil. Before sowing, the soil was analyzed for basic physicochemical properties. The soil was sandy loam in texture, with a pH of 7.82, electrical conductivity of 1.31 dS m−1, organic matter 0.68%, available nitrogen 47.6 mg kg−1, available phosphorus 8.9 mg kg−1, and available potassium 156 mg kg−1. All pots received the same basal fertilizer dose equivalent to the recommended wheat nutrient requirement, using urea, diammonium phosphate, and sulfate of potash as sources of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium. Phosphorus and potassium were applied before sowing, while nitrogen was applied in two equal splits, at sowing and after seedling establishment. Irrigation was applied with tap water according to the plant requirements to maintain adequate soil moisture, and all the pots received equal water volumes to avoid moisture-based variation among the treatments. Pots had drainage holes to prevent waterlogging and excessive salt accumulation. After emergence, the seedlings were thinned to maintain three uniform plants per pot. The pots were arranged in a completely randomized design and repositioned regularly inside the greenhouse to reduce positional effects. The greenhouse temperature ranged from 25 °C to 32 °C during the day and 16 °C to 22 °C at night, with a relative humidity ranging from 55% to 65%. The experimental design included 8 treatment units, each comprising 5 replication pots, with 3 plants in each pot. Every measurement was conducted in triplicate for all treatments.

The experimental treatment groups were arranged as: Group-1: control treatment (without NHP and agmatine, and salt stress); Group-2: foliar use of NHP (0.01 µM) for 5 d, commenced 30 d after sowing (following salt exposure); Group-3: Foliar use of agmatine (100 μM) for 5 d, commencing 30 d after sowing (following salt exposure); Group-4: entailed a mix of NHP and agmatine regimens; Group-5: Commenced at 15 d post-sowing by incorporating 200 mM of NaCl into the soil for 5 d consecutively; Group-6: integrated salt exposure with NHP intervention (Group-2 + Group-5); Group-7: integrated salt exposure with agmatine intervention (Group-3 + Group-5); Group-8: integrated salt exposure with agmatine and NHP intervention (Group-4 + Group-5). The eight treatments were established using a two-factor structure consisting of salinity conditions and foliar treatment. The salinity factor included non-stressed and NaCl-stressed plants, while the foliar treatment factor included no biostimulant, N-hydroxy pipecolic acid, agmatine, and their combined application. This design allowed us to evaluate the independent effects of salinity, the individual effects of both compounds, and the possible combined protective effects of N-hydroxy pipecolic acid and agmatine under salt stress. The experiment included eight treatment combinations, with five biological replicates per treatment. Each replicate consisted of one pot containing three uniform wheat plants. For laboratory-based physiological and biochemical assays, measurements were conducted in triplicate as technical repeats, and the mean value of the technical repeats was used for each biological replicate.

All foliar applications were made via a sprayer, dispensing 1 mL per spray applied and 1 mL per spray, provided three times to each plant. Every plant was maintained until 60 d post-sowing, and samples were collected at 65 d post-sowing to assess growth characteristics, photosynthetic performance, oxidative stress markers, and the activity of enzymes associated with antioxidant defense mechanisms and proline metabolism. NaCl stress was imposed at 15 d after sowing because the seedlings were fully established and actively growing at this stage, making them suitable for evaluating early salinity responses without causing complete mortality. Foliar applications of agmatine and N-hydroxy pipecolic acid were started at 30 d after sowing, after the onset of salt stress, because the plants had developed sufficient leaf area for uniform spray application and absorption. This timing allowed assessment of the protective and recovery effects of these compounds under an already established salt stress condition.

Growth parameters assessment

Plant specimens from all the treatments were meticulously extracted with the adjacent soil and delicately rinsed in the beaker containing potable water to eliminate adherent soil particles. Cleansed specimens were dried via paper towels. The root and shoot lengths were quantified utilizing a measurement scale. Similar plants were subsequently positioned in the oven at 65 °C for 72 h for desiccation; after that, the plants' dry weights were determined.

Leaf area was measured at the selected growth stage using the linear measurement method. Five representative plants were randomly selected from each treatment. For each plant, the length of each fully expanded leaf was measured from the ligule to the leaf tip, and the maximum leaf width was measured at the widest point using a ruler. The leaf area was calculated as: Leafarea=leaflength×maximumleafwidth0.75.

The leaf area of all measured leaves was summed to obtain the leaf area per plant. The mean leaf area was then calculated for each treatment and expressed in cm2 per plant.

The leaf relative water content was measured using fresh, turgid, and dry leaf weights. Fully expanded wheat leaves were collected from randomly selected plants and immediately weighed to record their fresh weight. The leaves were then floated in distilled water for 4 h at room temperature under low light. After rehydration, the leaf surfaces were gently blotted dry and weighed to record the turgid weight. The same samples were oven dried at 70 °C until constant weight and weighed again to record the dry weight.

The leaf relative water content was calculated using the following formula: RWC=[(freshweight−dryweight)÷(turgidweight−dryweight)]×100.

Chlorophyll dynamics and photosynthetic performance

The chlorophyll content was measured in the fully expanded flag leaves of randomly selected wheat plants using a SPAD chlorophyll meter (SPAD-502Plus). Three readings were taken from the upper, middle, and lower portions of each leaf, avoiding the midrib, and the mean value was recorded as the SPAD chlorophyll content.

Photosynthetic traits were measured on the same type of leaf using a portable photosynthesis system between 9:00 and 11:00 am on clear days. The net photosynthetic rate, stomatal conductance, transpiration rate, and intercellular CO₂ concentration were recorded after the leaves stabilized in the chamber. Measurements were taken from five representative plants per plot, and the mean value was used for statistical analysis. 32

Stress biomarkers assessment

Stress biomarkers were measured using fresh wheat leaf samples collected from randomly selected plants in each plot. The leaf samples were immediately placed on ice and processed for lipid peroxidation, electrolyte leakage, and hydrogen peroxide content.

Lipid peroxidation was estimated as the malondialdehyde content using the thiobarbituric acid method. Fresh leaf tissue was homogenized in 0.1% trichloroacetic acid and centrifuged. The supernatant was mixed with 0.5% thiobarbituric acid prepared in 20% trichloroacetic acid, heated at 95 °C for 30 min, cooled quickly, and centrifuged again. The absorbance was recorded at 532 and 600 nm. The malondialdehyde content was calculated after correcting nonspecific absorbance and expressed as nmol g−1 fresh weight. 33

Electrolyte leakage was measured using leaf discs from fully expanded leaves. The discs were washed with distilled water and placed in tubes containing distilled water. The initial electrical conductivity was recorded after incubation at room temperature. The samples were then boiled to release total electrolytes and cooled, and the final conductivity was recorded. Electrolyte leakage was calculated as the initial conductivity divided by the final conductivity multiplied by 100 and expressed as a percentage. 34

The hydrogen peroxide content was measured by homogenizing fresh leaf tissue in chilled trichloroacetic acid, followed by centrifugation. The supernatant was mixed with potassium phosphate buffer and potassium iodide, and the absorbance was recorded at 390 nm. The hydrogen peroxide concentration was calculated using a standard curve and expressed as μmol g−1 fresh weight. 35

Biochemical traits assessment

Fresh wheat leaf samples were collected from randomly selected plants, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at 80 °C until analysis. The soluble sugar content was determined using the phenol-sulfuric acid method. The leaf extract was reacted with phenol and concentrated sulfuric acid, and the absorbance was recorded at 490 nm. The soluble sugar content was calculated from a glucose standard curve and expressed as mg g−1 fresh weight. 36

For the antioxidant enzyme assays, fresh leaf tissue was homogenized in chilled phosphate buffer and centrifuged at 4 °C. The supernatant was used as the enzyme extract. Catalase activity was measured from the decrease in absorbance at 240 nm due to hydrogen peroxide decomposition. 37 Peroxidase activity was determined by guaiacol oxidation at 470 nm. 38 Superoxide dismutase activity was assayed by measuring its ability to inhibit nitro blue tetrazolium reduction at 560 nm. 39

Glutathione reductase activity was measured using fresh wheat leaf tissue. The leaf samples were homogenized in chilled phosphate buffer containing EDTA and polyvinylpyrrolidone and then centrifuged at 4 °C. The supernatant was used as the enzyme extract. The reaction mixture contained phosphate buffer, EDTA, oxidized glutathione, NADPH, and enzyme extract. The decrease in absorbance was recorded at 340 nm for 3 min because of NADPH oxidation. Glutathione reductase activity was calculated using the extinction coefficient of NADPH and expressed as units mg−1 protein. 40

Proline content was measured using the acid ninhydrin method. Fresh leaf tissue was extracted in sulfosalicylic acid, reacted with acid ninhydrin and glacial acetic acid, and heated in a water bath. The chromophore was extracted with toluene, and the absorbance was recorded at 520 nm. The proline content was calculated from a standard curve and expressed as μmol g−1 fresh weight. 41

Rubisco activity and enzymes related to proline metabolism were measured from freshly prepared leaf enzyme extracts. Rubisco activity was determined by monitoring NADH oxidation at 340 nm in a coupled enzyme assay. Pyrroline 5-carboxylate synthase and delta ornithine aminotransferase activities were assayed by measuring pyrroline 5-carboxylate formation colorimetrically. Proline dehydrogenase activity was measured by following the reduction of an electron acceptor spectrophotometrically. All enzyme activities were expressed as units mg−1 protein. 42

Statistical analyses

All the data were statistically analyzed using SPSS software version 17.0. The experiment followed a completely randomized design with eight treatment combinations and five biological replicates per treatment. Each pot was considered one experimental unit, and the mean values were used for statistical analysis. The data were first checked for normality and homogeneity of variance. One-way analysis of variance was performed to determine significant differences among treatments. When the treatment effects were significant, mean comparisons were carried out using the least significant difference test at p ≤ 0.05. The results are presented as mean ± standard error. Different letters in the figures indicate statistically significant differences among treatments. Each pot was considered one biological replicate for statistical analysis. Technical triplicate measurements were averaged before analysis and were not treated as independent replicates. The data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance, and the treatment means were compared using the LSD test at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

Growth-related parameters

Growth performance was evaluated by measuring root length, shoot length, leaf area, plant dry mass, and leaf relative water content. Salt stress significantly reduced all the measured growth traits compared with the untreated control, as indicated in Figure 1. Under salt stress, foliar application of N-hydroxy pipecolic acid and agmatine improved growth traits compared with those in the salt-only treatment, Group 5. The combined application of N-hydroxy pipecolic acid and agmatine produced the strongest recovery response among the salt-stressed treatments. Compared with the salt-only treatment, the combined treatment increased the root length by 39.11%, shoot length by 29.38%, leaf area by 39.11%, and plant dry mass by 40.23%. These increases were statistically significant at p ≤ 0.05 according to the LSD test. Individual applications of N-hydroxy pipecolic acid or agmatine also improved growth, but their effects were smaller than those of the combined treatment. The improved growth under the combined treatment may be associated with better leaf water status, stronger antioxidant protection, improved osmotic adjustment, and maintenance of photosynthetic capacity under salinity. Agmatine may support salt tolerance through polyamine-related osmotic regulation and redox protection, whereas N-hydroxy-pipecolic acid may contribute through defense priming.

Physiological parameters assessment

All the assessed photosynthesis variables, such as the net photosynthesis rate, stomatal conductivity, internal CO2 level, Rubisco activity, and SPAD chlorophyll levels, were severely inhibited under salinity (Figure 2a–e). Salt treatment resulted in a 34.29% drop in the Rubisco level and a 39.46% reduction in the net photosynthesis rate relative to control crops. The NHP and agmatine significantly enhanced these variables under salt stress. Although separate treatments with NHP or agmatine slightly mitigated the negative impacts of saline stress, their combination treatment considerably maintained and even improved the photosynthetic efficiency. Significantly, stressed plants with both NHP and agmatine demonstrated complete recovery in net photosynthesis rate, Rubisco activity, and chlorophyll level, with measurements exceeding those of the controls. These results underscore the dual function of NHP and agmatine in safeguarding and augmenting the photosynthetic apparatus under saline circumstances.

Biomarkers of stress

Multiple indicators, such as lipid peroxidation, hydrogen peroxide, and electrolyte leakage concentration, were assessed to measure the stress generated by salinity. All these metrics demonstrated a substantial rise in salt stress (Figure 3a–c). Nonetheless, the concomitant application of NHP and agmatine also increased lipid peroxidation relative to that of the controls. In contrast, under salt exposure, the lipid peroxidation, hydrogen peroxide, and electrolyte leakage concentration diminished when crops were administered the NHP and agmatine were combined. The soluble sugar level was consistently enhanced in each treatment relative to controls. Foliar administration of NHP markedly elevated the soluble sugar concentration, with the maximum concentration recorded in plants under salt exposure and supplemented with the NHP + agmatine combination, demonstrating a 48.02% increase compared to control crops (Figure 3d).

Proline metabolism status

Proline metabolism encompasses proline dehydrogenase, δ-OAT, and P5CS levels in addition to proline levels. Figure 4 b–d illustrates that the activity of every enzyme related to proline metabolism was elevated in each treatment relative to controls. Individual treatments with NHP and agmatine also increased enzyme capabilities; however, the combo of NHP and agmatine yielded superior enzymatic activity concentrations compared to each treatment singly. The most elevated enzyme activity was recorded in plants subjected to salt exposure and amended using a combination of NHP and agmatine. In response to salt exposure, plants administered with NHP and agmatine exhibited a significant increase in proline buildup (Figure 4a). While both amendments boosted proline amounts, NHP elicited a more pronounced increase compared to agmatine. A 65.2% rise, the largest boost, was noted in plants exposed to salinity and concurrently supplemented with NHP and agmatine. This increase was statistically significant at p ≤ 0.05 according to the LSD test, as indicated by different letters in Figure 4.

Discussion

Plants respond to salinity through coordinated morphological, physiological, biochemical, and redox adjustments. 43 , 44 In the present study, NaCl stress clearly disturbed wheat growth and cellular stability, as shown by reduced growth performance, lower leaf relative water content, impaired photosynthetic traits, and increased oxidative injury. The rise in electrolyte leakage, lipid peroxidation, and hydrogen peroxide indicates that salinity disrupted membrane integrity and redox balance. In contrast, foliar application of agmatine and N-hydroxy pipecolic acid reduced these stress markers, with the combined treatment showing the strongest protective effect. This response suggests that both compounds improved salt tolerance by limiting oxidative injury and maintaining membrane stability.

The increase in lipid peroxidation under salinity reflects oxidative damage to membrane lipids. Elevated TBARS levels are widely recognized as a marker of membrane degradation under oxidative stress. 45 , 46 Under salt stress, excessive reactive oxygen species can weaken membrane structure and may trigger cellular damage through ionic imbalance and calcium-mediated signaling. 47 , 48 Salinity also impairs electron transport in chloroplasts and mitochondria, which increases hydrogen peroxide accumulation in leaves. 49 , 50 Our results support this mechanism because salt-stressed plants accumulated more H₂O₂, whereas agmatine and N-pipecolic acid markedly reduced H₂O₂ levels (Figure 3c). This reduction likely resulted from improved redox regulation. Agmatine may have reduced oxidative pressure by improving osmotic adjustment, membrane protection, and antioxidant enzyme activity, including superoxide dismutase, catalase, ascorbate peroxidase, and glutathione reductase. 51 , 52 N-hydroxy pipecolic acid likely worked as a defense priming signal that improved stress perception and activated protective responses before severe injury developed. 53 , 54 Therefore, the decline in H₂O₂ under the combined treatment can be explained by both lower reactive oxygen species generation and faster H₂O₂ detoxification.

Reactive oxygen species are continuously produced during normal plant metabolism. 55 Under favorable conditions, plants control them through a strong antioxidant system. 56 , 57 Under salinity, however, reactive oxygen species production can exceed the plant scavenging capacity, leading to lipid peroxidation, H₂O₂ accumulation, electrolyte leakage, and cellular dysfunction. 58 , 59 In this study, higher activities of catalase, peroxidase, superoxide dismutase, and glutathione reductase under agmatine and N-hydroxy pipecolic acid treatments indicate activation of an adaptive antioxidant defense system. 60 Superoxide dismutase converts superoxide radicals into H₂O₂, while catalase and peroxidase detoxify H₂O₂ into water and oxygen. 61 Glutathione reductase supports the ascorbate glutathione cycle by maintaining reduced glutathione, which helps sustain cellular redox balance. The strongest enzyme activities under the combined treatment suggest that agmatine and N-hydroxy pipecolic acid acted through complementary pathways. Agmatine likely supported metabolic protection and osmotic balance, while N-hydroxy pipecolic acid strengthened defense priming. Together, they allowed wheat plants to remove reactive oxygen species more efficiently under salt stress.

Proline metabolism also contributed to the improved salt tolerance observed in treated plants. Proline functions as an osmoprotectant, redox buffer, membrane stabilizer, and antioxidant under plant stress conditions. 62 , 63 Its metabolism is regulated by key enzymes, including δ OAT, ProDH, P5CR, and P5CS. In the present study, salt-stressed plants treated with agmatine and N-hydroxy pipecolic acid showed higher proline accumulation and increased activities of δ OAT, ProDH, P5CR, and P5CS compared with untreated plants (Figure 4a–d). This indicates that the combined treatment did not simply increase the proline content but activated coordinated proline turnover. P5CS and P5CR likely enhanced the glutamate pathway of proline biosynthesis, while δ OAT supported the ornithine pathway. The increase in ProDH suggests active proline cycling, which can support energy supply and redox balance during stress recovery. Thus, agmatine and N-hydroxy pipecolic acid improved osmotic adjustment by promoting both proline synthesis and controlled proline utilization.

Photosynthesis was another major target of salt stress in this study. Photosynthesis is highly sensitive to salinity because salt stress reduces stomatal conductance, damages chlorophyll, limits carbon assimilation, and increases redox pressure in chloroplasts. 64 , 65 In the present study, salinity reduced the chlorophyll content, net photosynthetic rate, Rubisco activity, and stomatal conductance. The application of agmatine and N-hydroxy pipecolic acid restored these traits, especially when both compounds were applied together (Figure 2 a–e). The positive relationship between Rubisco activity and the net photosynthetic rate (Figure 6) supports the view that improved carbon fixation contributed to better photosynthetic performance. This recovery may reflect improved chloroplast protection, stronger antioxidant defense, and better regulation of stomatal behavior. Salt stress can damage chloroplast structure, including thylakoid organization, which reduces light-harvesting capacity. 66 , 67 The higher chlorophyll content and Rubisco activity in treated plants suggest that agmatine and N-hydroxy pipecolic acid helped preserve chloroplast function under salinity. The Rubisco activity under the salt plus N-hydroxy pipecolic acid plus agmatine treatment exceeded the control level, suggesting a compensatory stimulation of the carbon fixation capacity under the combined treatment. This response may be explained by the coordinated protective effects of both compounds. Agmatine likely improved osmotic adjustment, membrane stability, antioxidant enzyme activity, and chloroplast protection, while N-hydroxy pipecolic acid likely primed defense signaling and reduced oxidative injury. These combined effects may have lowered the reactive oxygen species-mediated inhibition of Rubisco and preserved chloroplast function under salinity. The improvement in photosynthetic performance under the combined treatment may be explained by the coordinated protection of stomatal and biochemical processes. Salt stress restricts photosynthesis by lowering the leaf water status, inducing stomatal closure, damaging chlorophyll, and inhibiting Rubisco activity. Agmatine may reduce these effects by supporting osmotic adjustment, membrane stability, antioxidant enzyme activity, and polyamine-related redox protection. These responses can help preserve chloroplast function and maintain carbon fixation under salinity. N-hydroxy pipecolic acid may contribute through defense priming, which can prepare plants to activate protective metabolism faster under stress. The combined treatment therefore likely reduced the oxidative pressure on chloroplasts, supported stomatal conductance, maintained intercellular carbon dioxide availability, and protected Rubisco activity. This explains why salt-treated plants receiving both compounds showed stronger photosynthetic recovery than plants receiving either compound alone. The improved leaf relative water content and soluble sugar accumulation may have further supported photosynthetic enzyme activity. Thus, the observed increase in Rubisco activity likely reflects treatment-induced recovery and metabolic priming rather than a direct effect of salinity alone.

Pearson correlation coefficients were computed to illustrate relationship between (a) the Rubisco level and the net photosynthesis rate and chlorophyll content and the plant leaf area (b) in the wheat treatment. All values represent the average of five replications (n = 5).

The improvement in soluble sugar content and CO₂ assimilation further supports the role of these compounds in maintaining carbon metabolism under stress. Under salinity, reduced stomatal conductance, impaired chlorophyll stability, low Rubisco activity, and disrupted electron transport limit sugar production. Agmatine may counter these effects by improving osmotic balance, protecting membranes, reducing oxidative damage, and supporting antioxidant defense. These processes help maintain chloroplast function and gas exchange. As a result, treated plants can assimilate more CO₂ and convert fixed carbon into soluble sugars. These sugars serve as energy reserves, osmoprotectants, and signaling molecules. Therefore, agmatine appears to support stress tolerance by preserving photosynthetic efficiency and directing sugar accumulation toward osmotic adjustment.

Overall, the combined application of agmatine and N-hydroxy pipecolic acid produced a stronger response than either compound alone because it integrated several protective mechanisms. Agmatine appears to support mainly osmotic adjustment, membrane stability, antioxidant activity, and carbon metabolism. N-hydroxy pipecolic acid likely acts through defense priming and improved stress signaling. Their combined application reduced oxidative injury, strengthened antioxidant enzyme activity, activated proline metabolism, maintained the leaf water status, and preserved photosynthetic carbon fixation. These coordinated responses explain the improved growth and physiological performance of wheat under salinity.

Although the imposed NaCl level represents a severe salinity condition for wheat, it was useful for identifying the protective capacity of agmatine and N-hydroxy pipecolic acid under strong salt stress. The observed recovery in terms of growth, photosynthetic performance, osmotic adjustment, and antioxidant defense suggests that these compounds may improve wheat salt tolerance. However, further field-based studies under moderate and naturally variable saline conditions are required to confirm their practical agronomic value. In addition, although the selected concentrations improved wheat performance under salt stress, this study did not include a full concentration response analysis. Future studies should evaluate a wider range of doses across growth stages, wheat genotypes, seasons, and field environments to determine the optimum application strategy. The main contribution of this study is that it identifies a coordinated protective response induced by the combined application of agmatine and N -hydroxy pipecolic acid in wheat under salinity. The combined treatment improved growth and physiological performance by linking four major tolerance processes: photosynthetic maintenance, osmotic adjustment, proline metabolic activation, and antioxidant defense. This is important because salt tolerance in wheat depends on the integration of several traits rather than improvement in a single parameter. These results suggest that combining a metabolic regulator with a defense priming molecule may offer a useful strategy for improving wheat resilience under salt stress. However, field validation is still required to confirm its practical value under natural saline soil conditions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the combined foliar application of agmatine and N-hydroxy pipecolic acid effectively improved wheat tolerance to salt stress by coordinating growth recovery, photosynthetic protection, osmotic adjustment, and antioxidant defense. Salt stress severely restricted root and shoot growth, leaf area, relative water content, chlorophyll content, gas exchange, Rubisco activity, and biomass accumulation while increasing the levels of oxidative stress markers such as hydrogen peroxide, lipid peroxidation, and electrolyte leakage. However, agmatine and N-hydroxy pipecolic acid markedly reduced these stress injuries, with the combined treatment showing strongest protective effect. The improvement was closely associated with enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities, including catalase, peroxidase, superoxide dismutase, and glutathione reductase, which strengthened reactive oxygen species detoxification and helped maintain the cellular redox balance. The combined treatment also increased proline accumulation and activated key enzymes of proline metabolism, including P5CS, P5CR, δ OAT, and ProDH, indicating improved osmotic protection and active proline turnover under salinity. In parallel, treated plants maintained higher the chlorophyll content, Rubisco activity, CO₂ assimilation, soluble sugar levels, and leaf water status, suggesting better preservation of photosynthetic carbon metabolism. Overall, the co-application of agmatine and N-hydroxy pipecolic acid appeared to act through complementary physiological and biochemical responses, allowing wheat plants to sustain growth and physiological performance under salt stress. These findings support the potential use of this combined treatment as a potential foliar strategy that requires field validation to improve wheat resilience in saline environments.
