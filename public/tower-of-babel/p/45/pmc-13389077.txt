Investigation of feasibility and diagnostic capability of ultrasound-guided photoacoustic tomography for breast surgical margins
Authors: Yonggeng Goh, Ghayathri Balasundaram, Hui Min Tan, Thomas Choudary Putti, Renzhe Bi, Mikael Hartman, Shaik Ahmad Buhari, Celene Wei Qi Ng, Su Ann Lui, Serene Si Ning Goh, Wei Qi Leong, Eric Fang, Malini Olivo, Swee Tian Quek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe explored the feasibility and preliminary diagnostic capability of ultrasound-guided photoacoustic (US-PA) tomography for breast cancer margin assessment by analysing PA derived lipid, collagen, and hemoglobin distribution patterns. Fifty-three ex-vivo specimens from breast-conserving surgeries were imaged using a handheld US-PA imaging probe illuminated between 700 and 1100 nm across radial margins. Photoacoustic patterns were analysed and assessed for margin involvement using a proprietary algorithm and compared with standard of care against histopathology as the gold standard. US-PA imaging demonstrated 95.45% sensitivity and 76.33% specificity for margin assessment with a positive predictive value (PPV) of 34.43.% and negative predictive value (NPV) of 99.23%. Clinical standard of care showed 40.91% sensitivity, 88.17% specificity, 31.03% PPV and 91.98% NPV. Analysis of false positives revealed that 75% occurred within 5 mm of the margin, with 35% within 2 mm. Overall, US-PA imaging demonstrated superior sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV compared to standard approaches, supporting its potential to reduce missed positive margins. The high false positive rate, primarily due to close margins and tissue compression effects, reflects a conservative classification strategy. Detection of biochemical markers, particularly collagen highlights potential of US-PA in enhancing margin assessment accuracy, although further refinement is needed to improve specificity.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Medical research, Oncology

Introduction

Accurate assessment of tumor margins is crucial for effective surgical treatment and improved patient outcomes in breast cancer management. Approximately, one in five women undergoing breast conserving surgery needs a second operation1–3, mostly due to positive or close margins found on the traditional margin evaluation method of histopathological examination that is time-intensive. To address this limitation, various intraoperative margin techniques have been adopted clinically as standard of care practice, including gross inspection, frozen section analysis, specimen mammography, intraoperative ultrasound and imprint cytology4. Each method has its own advantages and limitations, and selection often depends on lesion type, resource availability, and surgeon preference. Recent advancements in imaging technologies have introduced new possibilities for non-invasive margin assessment, potentially offering precise and real-time feedback to guide surgical decisions5 making this an actively evolving field.

One promising technology is photoacoustic (PA) imaging that has rapidly advanced over the past decade for several biomedical applications. PA imaging combines the strengths of ultrasound and photoacoustic imaging to provide detailed insights into tissue composition6. It is non-ionizing in nature, cost-effective, and easily deployable while having sufficient axial, lateral, and temporal resolutions for resolving various tissues7,8. In recent years, the integration of PA imaging with ultrasound has gained significant traction due to its ease of implementation and the ability to generate co-registered structural and functional images. This cross-referencing capability enhances diagnostic accuracy and is seen as a key factor supporting clinical translatability. For these reasons, US-PA has been increasingly explored for breast cancer margin assessment, with several research groups demonstrating its potential in this context, including prior work by our group7,9,10 as well as studies reported by Li et al.11 and Kosik et al.12,13.

In our previous study10, we explored the utility of US guided PA imaging for ex vivo assessment of tumor margins in breast cancer specimens post-lumpectomy. Focusing on two biomarkers—lipid and hemoglobin—we analyzed 15 lumpectomy specimens and evaluated 90 margins. The study demonstrated the probe’s potential for accurate margin assessment based on the distribution patterns of these biomarkers. Building on these encouraging results, in this study, we have extended our investigation to include a larger cohort of 53 excised breast conservative surgery specimens, allowing for a more comprehensive evaluation in comparison with standard of care practices followed by histopathology analysis. Notably, in this exploratory study, we have incorporated a third biomarker, collagen, alongside lipid and hemoglobin, to enhance the depth of our analysis. Our observation that the presence of fibroglandular tissue (FGT) at the margins can be mistaken for positive margins on US-PA due to the similarities in the photoacoustic patterns of positive margins and FGT9, underscored the importance of incorporating additional biomarkers, such as collagen, to better account for the structural composition of breast tissue. Collagen as a marker in breast cancer assessment has been established previously6,14,15. By integrating collagen into the assessment, we aim to refine our understanding of the US-PA’s capability to delineate tumor margins with greater precision. Herein, our goal is to offer deeper insights into the probe’s accuracy in comparison to standard of care and its potential to improve intraoperative margin assessment, ultimately contributing to more effective surgical outcomes and enhanced patient care.

Results

Characteristics of patient cohort

An overview of the baseline characteristics of the patients, size and histopathology of the breast tumors are given in Table 1. A total of 53 participants were included in the study, with a mean age of 57 years (range: 30–78 years). Breast density assessment showed that most participants had heterogeneously dense breasts (Category C, 28/53, 52.8%), followed by scattered fibroglandular tissue (Category B, 12/53, 22.6%), extremely dense breasts (Category D, 12/53, 22.6%), and entirely fatty breasts (Category A, 1/53, 1.9%). While lower false negative rate suggest that US-PA is more reliable in ensuring complete tumor excision, a higher false positive rate may potentially lead to an increased number of unnecessary re-excisions. Therefore, we further analysed the false positives based on the distance of the residual tumor from the margins. This detailed analysis revealed that 14 cases (35%) were within 2 mm of the margins. These 14 cases were also deemed positive on routine clinical investigations/standard of care and are likely inevitable false positives due to the extremely close distances. These incidences cannot be avoided as the current guidelines for DCIS margin acceptance require 2 mm or more clearance. Therefore, lesions within 2 mm must be called positive. 16 margins (40%) turned out to be false positive cases where residual tumors were observed between 2 mm and 5 mm from the margins. These are likely due to tissue compression during imaging, making them appear closer to the margin than they are. The remaining 10 false positive cases (25%) had cancerous cells at distance greater than 5 mm from the margins. A representative image of the false positive is presented in Fig. 4. These are primarily attributed to severe fibrosis, that can result from post-biopsy changes or normal/benign fibrotic conditions of the breast. Discussion

Herein, we reported the results of US-PA imaging for assessment of surgical margins in BCS specimens against standard of care. Notably, the performance of standard of care modality is lower than the previously published results, particularly in terms of sensitivity. This difference may partly reflect the heterogeneous mix of conventional intraoperative margin assessment methods used for comparison in our cohort, which can introduce variability in sensitivity across specimens. While we noted a sensitivity of 40.91%, a meta-analysis by Christiansen et al reported a pooled sensitivity of 72% (95% CI: 52–86%) for conventional intraoperative margin assessment techniques. Our specificity was slightly higher at 88.17% compared with 78% (55–91%) reported in the same study while the overall accuracy was similar to that reported in their study at 80%16. In another study using high frequency ultrasound sensitivities and specificities of 100% and 74% for DCs, 100% and 80% for ILCs17 were observed. While in general, the sensitivity performance of standard of care/routine clinical studies was on the lower side, it could be very specific to this pool of samples and the clinical workflow in which multiple assessment approaches are used depending on the surgeons’ preference and case characteristics.

In contrast, US-PA imaging demonstrated remarkable sensitivity, consistently achieving near 100% in both our current study and our previous pilot investigation10. The high sensitivity was maintained even as we expanded from 15 to 53 specimens and introduced collagen as a third biomarker alongside hemoglobin and lipid. The ability to identify all positive margins is crucial for reducing the risk of incomplete tumor removal and subsequent re-operations. Notably, the negative predictive value remained at an impressive 99.23%, providing surgeons with high confidence when a margin is declared negative.

However, the expansion of our study revealed some challenges. While sensitivity remained high, we observed a decrease in specificity from 97.7% in the pilot study to 76.33% in the current investigation. This shift likely reflects the increased complexity introduced by a larger, more diverse sample set and the addition of the collagen as a biomarker in margin assessment. The PPV also decreased from 66.7 to 40.34%, indicating a higher rate of false positives in the expanded study. Approximately 75% of false positive cases in our study were attributed to extremely close margins, often within 5 mm of the tumor edge. This high proportion of close-margin false positives aligns with the current clinical approach to margin assessment. As noted in recent literature, the definition of a “positive” margin has evolved, with current guidelines considering margins <2.0 mm as close or positive for DCIS18. Our results reflect this conservative approach, where clinicians tend to err on the side of caution when dealing with close margins. Nevertheless, there must be a balance between prioritizing sensitivity against specificity, and this balance must be interpreted within the context of patient-specific anatomy. In women with smaller breast volume, even limited additional excision may have a disproportionate impact on cosmetic outcomes, potentially limiting the acceptability of a higher false positive rate. This means that the clinical utility of the technique may vary depending on breast size and the tolerance for volume loss. This highlights the importance of individualized surgical decision-making when integrating US-PA into margin assessment workflows.

The challenge of accurately assessing close margins is compounded by tissue compression during imaging, which can make lesions appear nearer to the margin than they are. This phenomenon is well-recognized in breast imaging and contributes to the complexity of intraoperative margin assessment19. The remaining 25% of false positives in our study, primarily attributed to severe fibrosis, highlight an area where further research and technological advancements could significantly improve margin assessment accuracy. Fibrotic changes, whether due to post-biopsy alterations or benign breast conditions, can mimic tumor tissue on imaging, leading to false positive results20. This observation is consistent with our previous findings, where no significant differences were detected in the photoacoustic-derived collagen intensity between fibrotic and tumor-associated collagen6. This overlap in signal characteristics presents a key limitation of the current approach and underscores the need for more sophisticated methods to reliably distinguish benign fibrotic changes from true tumor involvement.

Moreover, this study was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. The patient population, clinical practices, and operational workflows may not fully represent those of other institutions. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, and further multi-center studies with larger and more diverse cohorts are warranted to validate and extend these findings. Though a sample size of 53 specimens and 119 margins has been the largest in evaluating margins using US-PA, we also acknowledge that evaluating performance across molecular subtypes remains an important area for further investigation. Such analyses may help refine the clinical applicability and define specific use-cases for the platform. Future studies in larger, prospectively stratified cohorts will be required to adequately assess subtype-specific performance with sufficient statistical power.

Looking ahead, the integration of quantitative methods and machine learning algorithms into imaging protocols shows promise for enhancing the specificity of margin assessment. Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of computer-aided delineation of tumor boundaries in ultrasound images, with one investigation reporting a sensitivity of 96% and a specificity of 76% for predicting positive and close margins19. Such advancements could help reduce false positives associated with fibrosis while maintaining the high sensitivity crucial for detecting true positive margins.

Also, each specimen scan takes ~20 min, with an additional 5–10 min for post-processing and 10–15 min for review, making intraoperative use challenging. To address this, we propose three improvements: (1) developing an automated sample stage to cut imaging time by ~25%; (2) refining and automating post-processing algorithms; and (3) building a decision-support algorithm using a growing database of annotated US-PA images. These efforts aim to reduce total turnaround time to under 30 min, enhancing intraoperative feasibility and clinical workflow integration.

Despite these challenges, the overall performance of US-PA imaging remains promising. The technology’s ability to maintain near perfect sensitivity while providing a multi-biomarker assessment offers a more comprehensive evaluation of margin status than conventional methods. This could potentially lead to more informed decision-making during surgery, ultimately improving outcomes for breast cancer patients undergoing BCS.

To conclude, while our clinical ultrasound results were lower than some published studies, US-PA imaging has demonstrated significant potential for improving margin assessment in BCS. Its consistent ability to identify positive margins with high confidence, coupled with the provision of multi-biomarker information, positions US-PA as a promising tool for enhancing the accuracy of breast cancer surgery and potentially reducing the need for repeat procedures. As we continue to refine this technology and expand its application, US-PA imaging may play a crucial role in improving outcomes for breast cancer patients undergoing conservative surgery.

Methods

Patient recruitment

All research involving human research participants were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was performed with full institutional ethics approval and informed patient consent in accordance with local research governance requirements in Singapore (Ethics application Ref # NHG DSRB 2017/00805). Between August 2020 and September 2021, women over 18 years of age with histologically confirmed breast cancer who were referred for breast-conserving surgery (BCS) at the Department of Surgery, National University Hospital, Singapore, were screened for eligibility. Patients scheduled for total mastectomy or deemed unsuitable for surgery (e.g., due to distant metastases) or those that underwent neo-adjuvant chemotherapy before BCS were excluded. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to recruitment. In total, 53 women were enrolled; data from the first four participants were used for training purposes, and the remaining 49 were included in the final analysis. Equipment and imaging protocol

Following lumpectomy, excised specimens were oriented by the operating surgeons using silk sutures (Fig. S1) and rinsed with saline to remove surface blood. For this ex vivo study, all samples were obtained within 10 min of surgery and imaged promptly to preserve blood-related signals.

Ultrasound-photoacoustic (US-PA) imaging was performed using the MSOT inVision 512-ECHO system (iThera Medical GmbH, Munich, Germany) integrated with a custom-designed handheld two-dimensional probe. The probe comprised a curved array of 256 detector elements (125° angular coverage) mounted on a spherical surface (40 mm radius) with a central frequency of 5 MHz. The transducers had a central frequency of ~5 MHz with a − 6 dB bandwidth of ≥55%, an element pitch of ~0.735 mm, and ~0.1 mm inter-element spacing.

Specimens were positioned on a movable platform permitting translation and rotation. The probe, mounted on a computer-controlled stage, was brought into contact with the thickest region of the tissue and systematically advanced across the sample in 1 mm increments until full coverage was achieved. Samples were supported on a silicone bed within an imaging chamber filled with heavy water (D₂O) to ensure efficient acoustic coupling (Fig. S1). Tumor location was guided by tissue clips or markers visible on ultrasound.

Illumination was delivered through a fiber-optic bundle coupled to a wavelength-tunable optical parametric oscillator (660–1300 nm; 10 Hz; 80 mJ per pulse at 730 nm). Excitation was performed at multiple wavelengths (700, 730, 760, 800, 850, 930, 970, 1000, 1030, 1064, and 1100 nm) to capture signals from key breast tissue chromophores, including oxyhemoglobin, deoxyhemoglobin, lipids, and collagen. The applied fluence remained below 20 mJ/cm², consistent with ANSI safety standards for nanosecond near-infrared lasers.

Acquired acoustic data were digitized and reconstructed offline using a back-projection algorithm to generate two-dimensional cross-sectional images with an in-plane resolution of 150 μm and an effective field of view of 25 × 25 mm. Images represented the average of five frames per wavelength, with real-time previews displayed during acquisition.

Upon completion of imaging, specimens were fixed in formalin and submitted for histopathological assessment.

Image reconstruction and analysis

Images at each wavelength were reconstructed offline using the default back-projection algorithm in ViewMSOT 3.8, with cut-off frequencies set between 0.5 kHz and 6.5 MHz. Spectral unmixing of hemoglobin (Hb), oxyhemoglobin (HbO₂), lipids, and collagen was performed using the software’s standard linear regression algorithm. For collagen and lipid, the full wavelength range (700–1100 nm) was utilized, whereas Hb and HbO₂ were analyzed using a restricted range (700–850 nm), which provides greater accuracy by avoiding interference from water absorption at higher wavelengths. Chromophore maps were color-coded as follows: lipids in green, collagen in magenta, and total hemoglobin (HbT= Hb+HbO2) in red.

During the training phase, US-PA images from the first four specimens were reviewed by two experienced readers—YG, a breast radiologist with six years of PA experience, and GB, with eight years in clinical PA imaging—who had access to conventional imaging and histopathology results to refine acquisition and processing methods.

For each specimen, optoacoustic distribution maps were subsequently generated. The resulting patterns or signatures were evaluated and assigned scores based on predefined criteria (Fig. 6) arrived based on training specimen and earlier work7. Simply put, the continuity of the lipid layer at the specimen margin was examined initially. A continuous lipid layer without disruption was interpreted as indicative of clear margins.

If disruption of the lipid layer was observed, further evaluation of collagen and hemoglobin patterns was performed.

Three criteria were subsequently assessed and scored:

Collagen intensity at the margin: If the collagen signal intensity at the margin was greater than or equal to that observed at the tumor bed, a score of +1 was assigned.

Collagen intensity at the margin: If the collagen signal intensity at the margin was greater than or equal to that observed at the tumor bed, a score of +1 was assigned.

Hemoglobin intensity at the margin: If the hemoglobin signal intensity at the margin was greater than or equal to that at the tumor bed, a score of +1 was assigned.

Hemoglobin intensity at the margin: If the hemoglobin signal intensity at the margin was greater than or equal to that at the tumor bed, a score of +1 was assigned.

Spatial continuity of tumor-associated signals: If collagen or hemoglobin signals at the margin demonstrated direct spatial continuity with the tumor or tumor bed, a score of +1 was assigned.

The individual scores were then summed to obtain a final margin score.

If the total score of the margins was >=2, the margin was assessed as positive and if <2, it was assessed as negative.

As such, this scoring framework integrates lipid integrity, stromal remodeling, and vascular signatures to classify margins as positive or negative.

As part of training, US-PA images from the first 4 participants were assessed by consensus of three readers who were unblinded to histopathologic findings of the assessed margins: YG (breast radiologist with 6 years’ experience in PA), GB (eight years’ experience in clinical PA imaging) and MO (eleven years’ experience in clinical PA imaging). Subsequently, US-PA images for the remaining 49 participants were assessed blinded to histopathologic findings, and independently by YG and GB, with any discordant readings resolved by MO. In this study, the chromophore signals are currently evaluated visually and qualitatively with hope to develop a quantitative assessment method in the future.

With the knowledge of optoacoustic patterns/signatures for margin assessment, a total of 191 radial margins were then analysed in excised specimens of 53 study participants. Anterior and posterior margins of US-PA were excluded as these surfaces represent anatomical boundaries beyond which no additional breast parenchyma is typically available for further surgical clearance. As such, they were not clinically relevant as involved/positive anterior and posterior margins does not necessitate re-excisions/change in management21,22.

Standard of care/routine clinical investigations

At our institution, intraoperative margin assessment is performed according to the surgeon’s routine clinical practice and may vary depending on lesion characteristics, breast density, imaging visibility of the tumor, and surgeon preference. Standard-of-care modalities include specimen radiography (X-ray), intraoperative ultrasound (US), and clinical palpation of the excised specimen. These techniques may be used individually or in combination during surgery to guide the surgeon’s decision regarding the adequacy of margin clearance.

For the purposes of this study, the results of standard-of-care margin assessment were retrospectively reviewed from the operative and surgical notes recorded at the time of the procedure. A positive intraoperative margin assessment was defined as any instance in which the surgeon elected to perform additional tissue excision (“shave” or re-excision) from the surgical cavity based on intraoperative evaluation. Conversely, a negative intraoperative margin assessment was defined as cases in which the surgeon determined that no additional tissue removal was required following margin evaluation.

This definition reflects the clinical decision-making outcome of intraoperative assessment, as the standard-of-care modalities are primarily used to inform the surgeon’s decision to excise additional tissue during the procedure.

Statistical analysis

Results from margin assessments using US-PA were compared to histopathologic findings in a categorical format (i.e., positive/negative), with cross-tabulation performed for all assessed margins.

Similarly, results from standard of care/routine clinical assessments were compared to histopathologic findings in a categorical format with cross-tabulation performed.

Overall sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value of US-PA and standard of care for diagnosis of margins were performed using the SPSS software (Version 22; PASW Statistics, Chicago, Ill). Kappa statistics were derived for overall agreement of US-PA with histopathology, and for inter-observer variation in assessment of US-PA images between YG and GB. As this was primarily a pilot proof-of-concept study, no sample size calculation was performed.
