OPENing the door for patient and public involvement in medical research in Singapore
Authors: Ai Ling Sim-Devadas, Sherry Bee Siang Soon, Ellil Mathiyan Lakshmanan, Ritu Jain, Sanjay H Chotirmall, Jo-Anne Manski-Nankervis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Plain language summaryPatient and public involvement (PPI) in research in Singapore is developing. In the last 10 years, there has been strong growth in patient advocacy and support groups and increasing interest in contributing to research. In recognition of the positive benefits of PPI to generate high quality research that matters to patients and the community, the Lee Kong Chian School of Medicine launched the Office of Patient Engagement, otherwise known as OPEN. OPEN has developed training workshops and networking opportunities for researchers and patients, developed the Patient Voices community and held its first symposium in November 2024. OPEN is collaborating with the Singapore Clinical Research Institute, healthcare clusters and Universities across Singapore with the aim of expanding training, developing culturally appropriate PPI guidance and undertaking research to understand how to best implement PPI in the Asian setting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40900-025-00789-x.

Patient and Public Involvement (PPI) in medical research has been an increasing area of focus in many countries and healthcare systems. It aims to promote research of higher quality, ensuring relevance, applicability to end users and impact. Systematic reviews have consistently identified key benefits of PPI for patients and members of the public and researchers, ranging from increased confidence and self-worth for the former to fresh insights and identification of barriers to research participation for the latter [1–3]. These benefits remain to be fully realised in much of the Western Pacific region.

Singapore is a hub for research and innovation in Asia with strong clinical care, research, networks and collaborations resulting from strong national investment and Research, Innovation and Enterprise (RIE) planning [4]. PPI, however, remains in early development and opportunistic due to lack of a formal structure in research settings and more traditional patient-doctor relationship [5, 6]. Significant advances in patient advocacy have occurred in the last decade, evidenced by the growth of more than 100 patient support groups, implementation of Patient and Family Advisory Councils across the three public healthcare clusters [5] and contributions to health technology assessment (HTA) [7]. This will be further strengthened by the 2024 WHO resolution urging all member states to ensure “engagement of individuals, families, communities and civil society” to improve health outcomes [8].

The barriers to PPI are well articulated, and include patient difficulty connecting with researchers, lack of knowledge of research and research concepts and researchers struggling to operationalize PPI [9, 10]. Prior research conducted in Singapore suggest that considering social and professional hierarchies and cultural factors are important to incorporate PPI successfully into medical research [11]. Community members have suggested that the “kampung spirit” (community support and inclusion), increasing education about research, incentives and awareness combined to a willingness to adhere to research principles and support non-local researchers by providing local context can facilitate PPI involvement [6]. To date, an absence of dedicated structures and frameworks to support and bridge the gap between patient partners and researchers has stymied PPI growth. With increased growth in patient advocacy and PPI in health services in Singapore and across the ASEAN region in recent years, a clear opportunity exists to put in place formalized PPI infrastructure to build it into routine medical research practices.

In recognition of the significant benefits that PPI brings to research, coupled with an understanding of the supports required to achieve these, the Lee Kong Chian School of Medicine (LKCMedicine), Nanyang Technological University, Singapore launched the Office of Patient Engagement (OPEN) in April 2024. This whole of medical school approach to PPI is the first of its kind nationally, set-up to put in place sustainable infrastructure for PPI. This includes embedding PPI into processes and school ethos, implementing a patient partner gratitude framework, providing support for researchers to integrate PPI into their research projects through consultancy and facilitating access to patient partners and patient organisations.

Since its launch, OPEN has developed workshops for researchers and patients on PPI and best practice and launched OPEN Voices, a community with over 50 pioneer patients and caregivers to partner on research activities, share their lived experience and champion PPI in medical research. OPEN held a national symposium in November 2024 (see Fig. 1, supplementary files 1 and 2) with associated workshops, incorporated PPI into several grant proposals, worked with researchers to integrate PPI into their projects and supported patient partners to contribute to more than 10 research projects on subjects such as respiratory health and artificial intelligence. Our Patient Partners are essential members of the OPEN governance structure and Taskforce that guide the development of OPEN and the implementation of its activities.

OPEN has benefitted from strategic advice of a steering committee that includes international experts in PPI, patient partners, researchers and educators. This has been helpful in informing strategies to respond to challenges that are experienced broadly in the PPI community. For example, specific challenges related to OPEN Voices have included a number of our patient partners becoming unwell, limiting their participation; the challenges of arranging meetings to fit the requirements of a diverse range of participants; the identified need to engage people based on specific research and education activities to create meaningful involvement; and a need for targeted recruitment to identify younger participants.

The initial 50 members of OPEN Voices consisted of experienced patient advocates, people with experience in healthcare improvement activities and those with no prior experience. We identified early that, regardless of previous experience, training for patient partners and researchers was important to introduce PPI, explain the difference between PPI engagement and involvement in research as a participant, and how to develop an appropriate environment and relationships to facilitate effective PPI. These offerings continue to evolve based on feedback. For example, the PREP workshop has undergone several improvements. These include increased teaching within the PREP Workshop by patient partners, introduction of pre-reading and interactive quizzes, improved communication of different research methods in the research module, and an improved framework to develop narratives for shared lived experience.

Communication channels have been developed to facilitate communication in the OPEN Voices community. However, there were different expectations. Whilst some members used the Whatsapp channel to share personal updates and broader opportunities, others wanted the channel to be restricted to updates specifically related to OPEN activities. To address this, we developed separate channels so members could choose how they wanted to engage with the community.

Other decision points have been more contentious. Payment has been a component of PPI in countries such as the United Kingdom and Canada where there is strong recommendation for payment, including suggested rates [12, 13]. Such guidance does not currently exist in Singapore, and patient partners in healthcare initiatives do not necessarily receive payment for their contributions but rather contribute as volunteers. Tensions that needed to be considered included acknowledgement of a lack of funding availability compared to payments offered by pharmaceutical companies, payment vs. volunteerism, gifts vs. payments, and concern from some patient partners on payments resulting in additional administration relating to reporting requirements due to additional income. The resulting framework to acknowledge the contribution of patient partners was developed in consultation with researchers and patient partners and was positioned as a gratitude framework, acknowledging these tensions. The gratitude framework includes recommendations on transport support and financial reimbursement for different levels of engagement. It is now being utilised internally at LKCMedicine as well as in funding applications, but will need to be reviewed and evolve as PPI initiatives continue to evolve.

There are currently no funding requirements in place in Singapore to motivate researchers to incorporate PPI in their programmes. As such, the researchers that are currently engaging with OPEN are “early adopters” that are receptive to PPI and see benefits for how PPI can enrich their work. For these researchers, we have received feedback that they have valued the support and guidance from OPEN, who have been active in assisting them to implement PPI in their projects. We anticipate that the experiences of the “early adopters”, together with patient partners they are working with, will influence expansion of PPI into other research programmes, facilitated by the ongoing strong senior leadership support for PPI in LKCMedicine.

Future initiatives will include an expanded training and support offering and development of localised frameworks for PPI. We have commenced research to describe and evaluate the impact of PPI on research programs, research outcomes and health systems and to identify areas of conflict and consensus amongst stakeholders regarding the roles and operationalisation of PPI. This will provide critical information to understand the supports and infrastructure required to facilitate effective PPI, how research was shaped by the involvement of patient partners, and any challenges that were experienced by both researchers and patient partners that may need to be addressed both by OPEN and in local PPI guidance.

Through OPEN, we have commenced the journey to elevate the patient voice within the school and support the research and education community as they progressively embrace PPI. Growing PPI in research beyond the School, OPEN is partnering with the Singapore Clinical Research Institute (SCRI), leveraging their broad capabilities and central role in supporting and coordinating clinical trials activities in Singapore. We aim to develop PPI in research and clinical trials, demystify clinical trials for the broader community, and realise the benefits of enhanced trial recruitment and participation. Together we will further optimise the relevance and impact of research in Singapore while informing and driving the growing PPI interest across the Western Pacific region.
