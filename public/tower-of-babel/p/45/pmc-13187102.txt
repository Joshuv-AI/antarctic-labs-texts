Exogenous lactate administration alleviates post-cardiac arrest brain injury by coordinating metabolic reprogramming and pathological mitophagy suppression
Authors: Chenghao Wu, Wenbin Zhang, Yao Lin, Minjie Lin, Taiwen Rao, Ziwei Chen, Xiaodan Zhang, Jiefeng Xu, Mao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe brain energy crisis following cardiac arrest and cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CA/CPR) is a major contributor to poor neurological outcomes. While lactate is traditionally viewed as a metabolic byproduct, its potential as an alternative metabolic substrate and as a signaling molecule remains controversial. This study investigates whether exogenous lactate administration (ELA) mitigates post-cardiac arrest brain injury (PCABI) and explores the underlying mechanisms.MethodsA rat model of CA/CPR and HT22 neurons subjected to oxygen-glucose deprivation/reoxygenation were employed. Multi-omics profiling, metabolic flux analysis, immunofluorescence, transmission electron microscopy, co-immunoprecipitation, and gene knockdown, were employed to dissect the underlying mechanisms.ResultsELA significantly improved 5-day survival, neurological function, and neuronal integrity in CA/CPR rats. Metabolomic and proteomic analyses revealed that ELA reprogrammed energy metabolism by alleviating the post-ischemic glycolytic surge, enhancing electron transport chain activity, and restoring the ATP/ADP ratio. In vitro, ELA’s neuroprotection was dependent on its cellular uptake and metabolic utilization, improving mitochondrial function, and reducing oxidative stress. Transcriptomic and ultrastructural analyses identified that ELA potently suppressed pathological mitophagy. Mechanistically, ELA promoted FOXO3A lactylation, thereby preventing its nuclear translocation and promoting its cytoplasmic retention and degradation. Consequently, the mitophagy receptor BNIP3 expression was downregulated, thereby inhibiting excessive mitophagy. The anti-mitophagic effect of ELA was abrogated following genetic ablation of Crebbp, an enzyme reported with lactyltransferase capability.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that ELA alleviates PCABI by dually reprogramming energy metabolism and suppressing pathological mitophagy. These results position lactate not only as a metabolite intermediate but also as a potential therapeutic agent and signaling molecule in PCABI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06190-5.Keywords: Exogenous lactate administration, Post-cardiac arrest brain injury, Energy crisis, Metabolic reprogramming, Lactylation, Pathological mitophagy

Introduction

Cardiac arrest (CA) remains a leading cause of death and neurological disability worldwide [1], with over 500,000 out-of-hospital CA cases reported annually [2]. Although significant advancements in cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) have improved rates of return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), approximately one-third of out-of-hospital CA patients achieve ROSC by hospital admission [3]. Nevertheless, subsequent post-cardiac arrest brain injury (PCABI) continues to drive poor outcomes [4, 5], accounting for up to 70% of in-hospital death and leaving many survivors with persistent neurological deficits [6, 7]. As no direct treatment currently exists for PCABI [8], there is an urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies targeting its underlying pathophysiology.

A cornerstone of PCABI is the profound cerebral energy crisis following global ischemia-reperfusion injury [9, 10]. After ROSC, the brain exhibits severe metabolic dysfunction characterized by aberrant glycolysis, impaired oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial failure, and catastrophic depletion of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) [10, 11]. This inability to meet the immense energetic demands of neurons ultimately results in cell death and neurological deficits. Conventional approaches have largely focused on improving cerebral blood flow or mitigating excitotoxicity [9]; however, interventions directly addressing the fundamental issue of energy crisis remain limited.

Lactate, historically regarded as a metabolic waste product and a biomarker of hypoxia, has undergone substantial re-evaluation in recent years [12]. Compelling evidence now identifies lactate as a preferred aerobic energy substrate for neurons, particularly under stressful conditions, where it may support cognitive function and recovery [13–15]. Beyond its metabolic role, accumulating evidence suggests that lactate also functions as a signaling molecule [16]. It can signal through its specific receptor G protein-coupled receptor 81 (GPR81) [17] or to be transported into cells via monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs) [18]. More recently, lactate-derived lysine lactylation (Kla) has been identified as a novel epigenetic modification regulating gene expression and cellular processes [19].

These discoveries raise the intriguing possibility that lactate influences neuropathological pathways through mechanisms extending beyond classical metabolism. One such critical pathway is pathological mitophagy, a dysregulated process that depletes functional mitochondria and exacerbates the post-ischemic energy crisis [20]. Whether and how lactate modulates this destructive process is largely unknown, although it has been proposed to link glycolysis and autophagy via lactylation [21]. Emerging studies have shown promise in improving outcomes across various models of acute brain injury, including traumatic brain injury (TBI), subarachnoid hemorrhage, and ischemic stroke, regarding the neuroprotective potential of exogenous lactate administration (ELA) [22]. However, in the context of CA, clinical investigations have thus far focused primarily on blood lactate levels and lactate clearance solely as prognostic markers [23–28], as no study has directly examined the physiological role of lactate in PCABI or therapeutic potential of ELA in this setting.

Based on this dual potential of lactate as both a metabolic substrate and a signaling molecule, we hypothesized that ELA mitigates PCABI via two coordinated mechanisms: (1) reprogramming cerebral energy metabolism to alleviate the acute energy deficit, and (2) suppressing pathological mitophagy through a lactylation-dependent mechanism to preserve mitochondrial integrity. To test this hypothesis, we employed a rat model of CA/CPR and an in vitro neuronal model of oxygen-glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R). Using multi-omics profiling, functional assays, and mechanistic studies, we show that ELA robustly improved survival and neurological recovery by restoring energy homeostasis via both metabolic reprogramming and inhibition pathological mitophagy through FOXO3A lactylation.

Materials and methods

Animals and ethical statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (No. 2024-062) and followed the National Guidelines for Animal Experimentation. Male adult Sprague–Dawley rats (400–420 g; Charles River Laboratories, Zhejiang, China) were housed on a 12-hour light and dark cycle with free access to water and food.

CA/CPR model in rats and treatments

The CA/CPR model was established as previously described [29]. Briefly, rats were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (45 mg/kg, i.p.), followed by tracheal intubation with a 14G catheter. The right femoral artery and vein were cannulated with PE-50 tubing for hemodynamic monitoring and drug administration. After a 10-min stabilization, asphyxia-CA was induced by occluding the endotracheal tube following intravenous injection of vecuronium (2 mg/kg), confirmed by the absence of arterial pulse and a mean arterial pressure (MAP) of < 25 mmHg. After 7-min untreated CA, CPR was promptly initiated by pure oxygen ventilation, chest compressions, and initial intravenous epinephrine (20 µg/kg). Subsequently, epinephrine doses were administered every 2 min until ROSC was achieved. ROSC was defined as the restoration of supraventricular rhythm with a MAP > 60 mmHg maintained for at least 10 min. After ROSC, rats were randomly assigned to the Vehicle or Lactate group using a random number table. At 10-min post-ROSC, rats received intraperitoneal L-lactic acid (pre-adjusted to pH 6.8 and iso-osmotic, 0.35 g/kg; Selleck) or an equivalent volume of normal saline (Fig. S1A, B), with dosage based on the previous study and the dose conversion principles [30]. At 4-h post-ROSC, catheters were removed, incisions were sutured, and rats were individually housed with ad libitum food and water.

Monitoring and sample collection

Electrocardiogram, MAP and rectal temperature were continuously monitored throughout the procedure. Blood samples were collected at baseline and 1, 4, 24 and 72 h post-ROSC. Samples were centrifuged, and the resulting serum was stored at -80℃. Rats were euthanized at 24–72 h post-ROSC, and the frontal cortex and hippocampus were harvested for subsequent experiments. (Fig. S1B, C)

Blood gas analysis

Arterial blood samples (0.2 mL) were collected from the femoral artery at baseline and at 10 and 30 min after ROSC. Parameters including pH, arterial partial pressure of oxygen, arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide, glucose, lactate, and electrolytes were measured using a blood gas and chemistry analyzer (i15, EDAN, China).

Neurologic deficit score (NDS)

Neurological function was assessed at 1-, 3- and 5-day post-ROSC using a validated NDS [31]. The NDS ranged from 0 (normal) to 500 (brain death) and was independently scored by two investigators blinded to the treatment groups. The evaluation integrated seven components: level of consciousness, respiration, cornea reflex, carinal reflex, auditory reflex, motor sensory function, and behavior.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

To quantify the serum concentrations of neuron-specific enolase (NSE), neurofilament light chain (NfL) and S100 protein b (S100b) of rats, ELISA kits (JHN85945 & JHN85110 & JHN85873, Jinhengnuo, China) were used following the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were coded and analyzed by researchers who were unaware of the experimental grouping.

Histological examination

Fresh hippocampus and frontal cortex were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin overnight, dehydrated, and paraffin-embedded. Section (5-µm) were prepared for hematoxylin–eosin (H&E), and immunofluorescence (IF) staining. Sections were coded and analyzed by an investigator blinded to the experimental groups.

For H&E staining, sections were briefly processed as follows: dewaxed, stained, dehydrated, and mounted. Viable neurons, identified by a visible nucleus and an intact cytoplasm, were counted in each field under an optical microscope (BX3, Olympus). Neurons exhibiting eosinophilic cytoplasm, vacuolation, shrinkage, or nuclear pyknosis were excluded [32].

For double-labeling IF staining with Tyramide Signal Amplification (TSA), dewaxed sections underwent antigen retrieval (EDTA buffer; pH 9.0), permeabilization (0.2% Triton X-100), and blocking (Blocking Buffer; ab126587, Abcam). In the first round, sections were incubated at 4℃ overnight with either rabbit anti-LC3B (ab192890, Abcam) or rabbit anti-FOXO3A (#12829, CST), before incubating with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (K1223, APExBIO) at room temperature for 1 h. Afterwards, sections were incubated for 10 min at room temperature with either iF594-TSA (G1242, Servicebio) for LC3B detection or iF647-TSA (G1232, Servicebio) for FOXO3A detection. In the second round, after antibody elution (citrate buffer; pH 6.0) and re-blocking, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated rabbit anti-NeuN (ab190195, Abcam) overnight at 4℃. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescent signaling was observed under a fluorescence microscope (DMi8, Leica).

Targeted metabolomics and proteomics

Fresh hippocampus was harvested at 24 h post-resuscitation for integrated targeted metabolomic and proteomic profiling.

For targeted metabolomics, an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system (Waters ACQUITY H-ClassD) coupled to a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (MS; Sciex QTRAP 6500+) was used in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. Raw MS data were processed using the MultiQuant 3.0.3 software (Sciex) for peak integration and peak area extraction.

For targeted proteomics, hippocampal proteins were extracted, quantified, and digested with trypsin. Desalted peptides were analyzed by nanoLC-MS/MS (Q-Exactive HF-X) operating in parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) mode with high-resolution MS2 acquisition (30,000 resolution, normalized collision energy: 27 eV). PRM data were processed using SpectroDive software (Biognosys). Peptide identifications were validated based on precursor and fragment ion matching, with FDR < 1%. Quantification was performed using the sum of fragment ion areas for each peptide.

OGD/R modeling and treatments

HT-22 mouse hippocampal neuronal cells (ATCC) were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (Procell, China) at 37℃/5% CO2. The OGD/R method was used to model cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury [33]. To induce OGD/R, cells were switched to glucose-free DMEM (MeilunBio, China) and transferred to a hypoxic chamber (95% N2/5% CO2) for 6 h. Subsequently, cells were reoxygenated for 24 h in normal culture medium containing the respective treatments. Control cells were maintained in normoxic conditions throughout.

Cell viability

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; APExBIO). Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates and pre-cultured for 24 h. Following experimental treatments, cells were incubated with fresh medium containing 10% (v/v) CCK-8 reagent at 37℃ for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a SpectraMax ABS Plus absorbance reader (Molecular Devices, USA).

RNA-sequencing and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT–PCR)

The total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using TRIzol (ThermoFisher) and quantified by NanoDrop One (ThermoFisher). The RNA libraries were sequenced on the illumina Novaseq™ 6000 platform by LC Bio Technology CO.,Ltd (Hangzhou, China), with subsequent data processing performed using their online platform (https://www.omicstudio.cn/analysis).

For qPCR validation, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA. qRT–PCR was then performed on an ABI QuantStudio 5 system using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The primers (Tsingke, China) used in this study are listed in Table S1. The results were represented as fold changes of the threshold cycle (Ct) value relative to controls using the 2−ΔΔCt method following normalization to β-actin.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM)

Cell cultures were digested with Accutase (Gibco) and harvested by centrifugation. The cell pellets were gently resuspended and fixed in electron microscope fixative solution (G1102, Servicebio) overnight at 4℃, then post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide. After dehydration, samples were infiltrated with resin and polymerized at 60℃ for 48 h. Ultrathin Sect. (70 μm) were prepared using an ultramicrotome (EM UC7, Leica) and observed with a TEM (HT7800, Hitachi).

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm)

HT22 cells were seeded on 6-well plates and pre-incubated for 24 h. ΔΨm was assessed using JC-10 dye (YEASEN, China). The JC-10 working solution was prepared mixing 5 µL of 200 × JC-10, 0.2 mL of 5 × staining buffer, 0.8 mL ultrapure water, and a 2 µL Hoechst 33,342 (#C0030; Solarbio). After respective treatments, cells were incubated with 1 mL culture medium and 1 mL JC-10 working solution at 37 °C for 30 min. Following two washes, the cells were imaged under a fluorescence microscope (IX83, Leica).

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) assessment by flow cytometry

Intracellular ROS were detected using 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA; Beyotime). Briefly, cells were incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA in serum-free DMEM at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 40 min. After washing twice with PBS, the cells were resuspended in PBS containing 10% FBS and analyzed immediately by flow cytometry (Beckman) using the FITC channel.

Mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate (OCR) measurement

HT22 neurons were seeded at an equal density of 10,000 cells/well in XFe96 cell culture microplates and subjected to OGD. During reoxygenation, neurons were treated with lactate or vehicle as specified. After treatment, neurons were washed and incubated with 10 mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate and 2 mM L-glutamine-supplemented XF base medium minimal DMEM (Agilent, USA) at 37℃ in a CO2-free incubator for 1 h for the detection of mitochondrial OCR using the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (103015-100, Agilent) with seahorse XFe96 (Agilent). The OCR values were normalized to the cell number each well. Results were analyzed using Wave 2.6.1 software.

Construction of stable HT-22 cell lines with Crebbp knockdown

The knockdown of Crebbp was performed using a lentiviral vector (pLV-U6-shRNA-CMV-EGFP-T2A-puro; KEL Biotech, China). Transduction was performed when the cell density reached 70–80%, with an empty vector used as the control group. The medium was replaced with fresh culture medium after 4 h of transduction, followed by the addition of selective medium containing 5 µg/mL puromycin (Beyotime) for stable cell screening after 48 h. The selective medium was changed every 2 days until the 6th day to establish a stable polyclonal cell pool. For clone purification, cells were serially diluted and seeded into 96-well plates pre-filled with 150 µL of complete medium per well to achieve approximately one cell per well. Refer to Table S1 for specific sequence details.

Western blotting

Total protein was meticulously extracted using the RIPA lysis buffer, which included 0.1mM PMSF and protease inhibitor cocktail. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE using an 4–20% acrylamide gel and transferred to PVDF membranes. Antibodies used in this study include rabbit anti-β-actin (#4970, CST), anti-FOXO3A (#12829, CST), anti-BNIP3 (ab109362, Abcam), anti-CBP (#7389, CST), and anti-LC3A/B (#12741, CST). The detailed procedures were performed as previously described [29]. The bands representing the targeted proteins were subsequently exposed using the ChemiDoc imager (Bio-Rad).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

This assay was performed according to our previous study [34], with slight modification. Briefly, cells were lysed in a moderate lysis buffer containing 1% Triton X-100, 1% protease inhibitor cocktail, 0.1 mM PMSF, 3 µM trichostatin A, and 50 mM nicotinamide. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected and quantified. Equal amounts of protein were immunoprecipitated with rabbit anti-FOXO3A (#12829, CST) overnight at 4℃, followed by incubation with protein A/G magnetic beads (Beyotime) for 20 min at room temperature. The immunocomplexes were washed five times after magnetic separation, resuspended in loading buffer, and denatured at 100 °C. Subsequently, samples were analyzed by western blot using antibodies against FOXO3A (#12829, CST) and pan-specific anti-lactyllysine (pan-Kla; PTM-1401RM, PTM Bio). Whole-cell lysates (input) and normal IgG were used as controls.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10.2.3 Software (GraphPad, USA). Normality and homogeneity of variances were assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and Brown-Forsythe test, respectively. Based on these assessments, the appropriate tests were selected: unpaired t-test or one-way ANOVA (parametric, equal variances); Welch’s t-test or Welch’s ANOVA (parametric, unequal variances); Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test (non-parametric). Survival curves were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method with log-rank testing. Data are expressed as the mean ± SD (normal) or median with IQR (non-normal). p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

ELA improves the survival and neurological outcome of rats after CA/CPR

A total of 129 rats were used, with 12 rats serving as the sham operation group (Sham group) and 117 rats undergoing CA/CPR modeling. Of the latter, 62 achieved ROSC and were subsequently divided into Vehicle and Lactate groups (Fig. S1C).

The first phase included survival, neurological function assessment, and physiological parameter monitoring. Lactate treatment significantly improved 5-day cumulative survival compared with Vehicle (Fig. 1A). NDS scores were also significantly lower in the Lactate group at 1, 3, and 5 days post-resuscitation (Fig. 1B). Both experimental groups exhibited weight loss post-resuscitation, however, Lactate-treated rats recovered faster and had higher body weights than Vehicle controls from day 2 to day 5 post-resuscitation (Fig. S2A). Heart rates in both the Vehicle and Lactate groups were significantly lower than the sham group at 20 min, but higher values from 40 to 240 min post-ROSC. Notably, heart rate in the Lactate group was significantly lower than in the Vehicle group at 40–60 min and significantly higher at 120–240 min post-ROSC (Fig. S2B). For MAP, Vehicle rats exhibited significantly higher MAP than the sham group from 20 to 60 min but significantly lower MAP from 120 to 240 min post-ROSC. In contrast, Lactate treatment maintained higher MAP than Vehicle at 80 min and 160–240 min post-ROSC (Fig. S2C). As summarized in Supplementary Table S2, other physiological parameters including arterial pH and electrolytes were consistent between the vehicle and lactate-treated groups. Due to the pH-adjusted (6.8) and sodium-matched preparation, lactate administration did not exacerbate post-resuscitation acidosis or induce electrolyte imbalances.

The second phase involved blood and tissue sample collection at predetermined time points for subsequent analyses (Fig. S1C). Serum biomarkers showed that Lactate treatment reduced NfL at 1- and 3-day post-ROSC (Fig. 1C) and significantly lowered S100b and NSE at 1 h, 4 h, 1 d, and 3 d post-ROSC compared with Vehicle (Fig. 1D, E). Serum lactate in Vehicle group peaked at 10 min and returned to baseline within 1 h post-ROSC, whereas Lactate group exhibited significantly higher lactate levels than Vehicle group from 30 min to 4 h post-ROSC before normalizing by 1- and 3-day post-resuscitation (Fig. 1F). Histological examination of the hippocampus and cortex by H&E staining at 3 days post-resuscitation revealed extensive neuronal cell death and disorganization in both hippocampal and cortical regions of Vehicle rats, while Lactate rats exhibited significantly greater neuronal preservation and structural integrity (Fig. 1G).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that post-resuscitation administration of exogenous lactate significantly improves survival and ameliorates neurological and histopathological outcomes after CA.

ELA reprograms hippocampal energy metabolism and alleviates the post-resuscitation energy crisis

To elucidate on the metabolic basis of neuroprotection, we performed targeted metabolomic and proteomic profiling of hippocampus 24 h after resuscitation (Figs. 2 and 3). Principal component analysis (PCA) showed clear separation among sham, vehicle, and lactate groups (Fig. 2A), with metabolites clustering into glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (GNG), tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, amino acids (AAs), pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), and purine/pyrimidine categories (Fig. 2B).

Following CA/CPR, glycolytic and gluconeogenic intermediates—including glucose, glucose-6-phosphate (G6P), fructose-6-phosphate (F6P), fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (FBP), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P), dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP), 3-phosphoglycerate (3PG), 2-phosphoglycerate (2PG), and glucose-1-phosphate (G1P)—were significantly elevated (Fig. 2C). Notably, ELA reversed these changes, with glucose, G6P, F6P, FBP, 3PG, and 2PG levels markedly reduced compared to vehicle controls (Fig. 2C). Correspondingly, proteomic analysis showed upregulation of glycolytic enzymes hexokinase 1 (HK1), phosphofructokinase M (PFKM), pyruvate kinase M1 (PKM1), and lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) post-resuscitation, while lactate dehydrogenase B (LDHB) was suppressed (Fig. S3A–E). ELA significantly downregulated HK1 and PFKM but upregulated LDHB and pyruvate dehydrogenase E1 subunit alpha (PDHA1) (Fig. S3F), indicating a shift from lactate production toward utilization.

TCA cycle metabolism exhibited minimal disruption following CA/CPR, with no significant changes in acetyl-CoA or most intermediates (Fig. 2D & S4). Isocitrate was elevated after CA/CPR, while ELA increased fumarate and malate level, with a significantly reduced malate/fumarate ratio in lactate versus vehicle groups. The sole significant proteomic change in the TCA cycle was succinate dehydrogenase complex subunit A (SDHA) upregulation (Fig. S5), which had a dual role in TCA and electron transport chain (ETC). Besides, AAs metabolism displayed compensatory adaptations after resuscitation, maintaining TCA cycle stability, with ELA specifically reducing glutamate and cysteine (Fig. S6). The glutamate decrease may reflect diminished excitotoxicity and reduced flux through glutamate dehydrogenase, whereas cysteine reduction likely indicates modulation of redox balance, potentially limiting oxidative stress during recovery. Post-resuscitation, the PPP was significantly activated, reflecting an adaptive response to restore NADPH for antioxidant defense, whereas this activation was unaffected by ELA (Fig. S7).

Mitochondrial ETC (Complexes I to IV) generates a proton gradient, which is then utilized by ATP synthase (Complex V) to produce ATP (Fig. 3B). CA/CPR-induced dysregulation with upregulation of complexes I (NDUFV1, NDUFA9) and IV (Cox4l1) and downregulation of cytochrome c (CYCS) (Fig. 3C, F, H). ELA partially restored ETC function, elevating SDHA (Complex II) and mitochondrial-encoded cytochrome c oxidase subunit II (mt-co2) while suppressing nuclear-encoded Cox4l1, suggesting the repair of ETC complexes (Fig. 3D, F). More importantly, ELA significantly recovered the ATP/ADP ratio after its post-CA/CPR decline, indicating restoration of cellular energy homeostasis (Fig. 2E).

In summary, CA/CPR caused severe cerebral energy dysregulation, which ELA effectively reprogrammed, restoring energy homeostasis and enhancing neuronal resilience.

ELA improves mitochondrial function and mitigates oxidative stress in OGD/R neurons

Building on the in vivo evidence suggesting ETC recovery, we next investigated whether lactate improves mitochondrial function using an OGD/R model in HT22 neurons. Dose-response screening determined optimal treatment concentrations for subsequent experiments: 4 mM lactate, 4 mM α-CHCA (an inhibitor of MCTs, blocking cellular uptake of lactate), and 2 µM AXKO-0046 (an inhibitor of LDHB, impairing intracellular utilization of lactate) (Fig. S8).

JC-10 staining revealed that OGD/R significantly depolarized mitochondria, indicated by a shift from red (aggregated, high ΔΨm) to green fluorescence (monomeric, low ΔΨm). In contrast, ELA preserved ΔΨm, exhibiting a markedly higher red/green fluorescence ratio (Fig. 4A, B). Flow cytometry analysis showed a dramatic increase in intracellular ROS levels following OGD/R, which was robustly suppressed by ELA to near-baseline levels (Fig. 4C, D). Further real-time metabolic flux analysis demonstrated that OGD/R severely impaired mitochondrial respiration, as evidenced by reduced basal respiration, ATP-linked respiration, maximal respiratory capacity, and spare respiratory capacity. ELA significantly restored these parameters, with partial but significant recovery of respiratory function (Fig. 4E).

Although ELA significantly improved cell viability following OGD/R compared to control cells, co-administration of α-CHCA completely abolished this protective effect, indicating that the neuroprotective action of ELA requires its intracellular entry (Fig. 4F). Furthermore, AXKO-0046-mediated inhibition of LDHB attenuated lactate’s protective effect of, which nonetheless remained markedly greater than that of the OGD/R group (Fig. 4G). This suggests that while intracellular metabolism of lactate substantially contributes to neuroprotection, additional non-metabolic mechanisms may also be involved.

ELA suppresses pathological mitophagy and preserves mitochondrial homeostasis

Prompted by the above finding, to investigate the non-metabolic mechanisms underlying the neuroprotective effects of ELA, we performed RNA-sequencing on HT22 neurons under control, OGD/R, and lactate-treated conditions. PCA analysis demonstrated clear separation among three groups (Fig. S9A). OGD/R induced 1,153 upregulated and 612 downregulated genes versus control, while lactate reversed 541 upregulated and 984 downregulated genes relative to OGD/R (Fig. S9B). A Venn diagram identified 1,277 overlapping differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two comparisons (Fig. S9C). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) demonstrated that OGD/R and ELA had no significant effects on glycolysis or the TCA cycle in HT22 neurons (Fig. S9D, E). In contrast, OGD/R significantly downregulated mitochondrial functional pathways, whereas ELA upregulated these pathways (Fig. S9F, G). Notably, the autophagy pathway was significantly enriched in the OGD/R group compared to control, and this enrichment was reversed by ELA (Fig. S9H).

These transcriptomic findings were corroborated by ultrastructural analysis. TEM of HT22 neurons showed numerous autophagosomes containing mitochondrial remnants in the OGD/R group, while lactate-treated cells exhibited markedly fewer autophagosomes and better-preserved mitochondrial integrity (Fig. 5A, B). Quantitative analysis showed that the number of mitochondria per field of view was significantly higher in the lactate group than in the OGD/R group (Fig. 5C), consistent with the suppression of excessive mitochondrial degradation. In parallel, NeuN/LC3B double IF in rat hippocampal and cortical neurons confirmed these findings in vivo. CA/CPR significantly increased LC3B-positive neurons in both regions versus sham (Fig. 5D, E), while ELA markedly reduced the proportion of LC3B-positive neurons (vs. vehicle), indicating attenuated neuronal autophagy. LC3B signals were prominently localized in both neuronal cell bodies and axons (Fig. 5F), confirming widespread activation of autophagy in the post-resuscitation brain.

Overall, these findings demonstrate that ischemic injury induces pathological mitophagy, leading to mitochondrial depletion, whereas ELA mitigates this process, which constitutes a crucial non-metabolic mechanism contributing to the preservation of mitochondrial homeostasis.

ELA inhibits pathological mitophagy through the FOXO3A/BNIP3 axis

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying ELA-mediated suppression of pathological mitophagy, we performed GSEA, which revealed that the FOXO signaling pathway was significantly enriched in OGD/R-treated neurons and reversed by lactate treatment (Fig. 6A, B). Among downstream targets, Bnip3 mRNA levels were markedly upregulated upon OGD/R and downregulated by lactate (Fig. S9I). Consistent with this, qPCR analysis in HT22 cells showed that Foxo3a and Bnip3 transcripts were elevated after OGD/R, and ELA significantly reduced Bnip3—but not Foxo3a—mRNA levels (Fig. 6C, D). Intriguingly, FOXO3A protein levels were significantly increased following OGD/R and markedly decreased by ELA (Fig. 6E, F), suggesting post-transcriptional regulation.

This reduction in FOXO3A protein was recapitulated in vivo. IF staining of rat hippocampal sections revealed increased FOXO3A signal after CA/CPR, which was attenuated by ELA (Fig. 6G, H). Subcellular localization analysis further demonstrated that, FOXO3A was present in both the nucleus and cytoplasm in the vehicle group (Fig. 6I), whereas ELA led to predominantly cytoplasmic distribution with markedly reduced nuclear signal (Fig. 6J). These findings indicate that lactate modulates both the abundance and subcellular localization of FOXO3A. In line with the inhibition of the FOXO3A-BNIP3 axis, protein levels of BNIP3 and other key mitophagy regulator were significantly reduced in the hippocampus of lactate-treated rats (Fig. 6K and L).

Given the discrepancy between mRNA and protein levels and the altered subcellular localization, we hypothesized that lactate induces post-translational modification of FOXO3A. Subsequently, we performed Co-IP followed immunoblotting with a pan-Kla antibody revealed enhanced FOXO3A lactylation in hippocampal tissues from lactate-treated rats (Fig. 6M, N), suggesting that lactylation may regulate FOXO3A stability and function.

ELA-mediate pathological mitophagy suppression is FOXO3A lactylation-dependent

To determine whether ELA-induced FOXO3A degradation is lactylation-dependent, we generated a stable Crebbp-knockdown HT22 cell line (shCBP) using lentiviral shRNA, as CBP (encoded by Crebbp) is a well-established lactyltranseferase responsible for protein lysine lactylation [12]. Scrambled shRNA served as the control. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed at both mRNA and protein levels by qPCR and Western blotting, respectively (Fig. 7A–C).

Upon subjecting these cells to OGD/R with or without lactate treatment, Co-IP of FOXO3A revealed that lactate-induced FOXO3A lactylation was significantly attenuated in shCBP cells compared to scramble controls (Fig. 7D, E), indicating that CBP is required for FOXO3A lactylation under these conditions.

qPCR analysis confirmed that CBP knockdown does not alter Foxo3a transcription under all conditions (Fig. 7F). At the protein level, the characteristic ELA-induced reduction of FOXO3A abundance following OGD/R was observed in scramble cells but was markedly blunted in shCBP cells (Fig. 7G). This indicates that CBP and, by extension, lactylation are required for lactate-triggered FOXO3A degradation. Consistent with the reliance of BNIP3 expression on FOXO3A, the suppressive effects of lactate on both Bnip3 mRNA and protein levels after OGD/R were abolished in shCBP cells (Fig. 7H, I). Finally, measurement of autophagic flux (via LC3-II/LC3-I ratio) demonstrated that ELA suppressed autophagy in scramble cells, as evidenced by reduced LC3-II. These effects were absent in shCBP cells, where lactate failed to modulate either marker (Fig. 7J).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that ELA suppresses pathological mitophagy through a novel mechanism involving CBP-mediated FOXO3A lactylation, thereby promoting FOXO3A degradation and inactivating the FOXO3A-BNIP3 mitophagy axis.

Discussion

Key findings and novelty

Our study began with the observation that early post-resuscitation administration of exogenous L-lactic acid markedly improved survival and neurological outcomes in rats after CA/CPR (Fig. 1 and S2). To unravel the mechanisms, integrated proteomic and metabolomic profiling of hippocampus showed that ELA induces a profound reprogramming of cerebral energy metabolism by redirecting glycolytic flux toward oxidative phosphorylation and restoring ATP production (Figs. 2 and S3–5).

Due to limitations in directly assessing mitochondrial dynamics in vivo, we employed OGD/R neuronal models to confirm that ELA preserved ΔΨm, reduced ROS, and restored respiratory capacity (Fig. 4A–E). In addition, the ELA’s neuroprotection required cellular uptake, as pharmacological inhibition of MCTs completely abolished its benefits in OGD/R neurons (Fig. 4F). Given that GPR81 mediates extracellular lactate signaling independently of uptake, its contribution to ELA’s neuroprotective effect may be limited. Even a study demonstrated that inhibition of the lactate-activated GPR81 signaling pathway is protective against ischemic brain injury [35]. In contrast, the inhibition of LDHB—the enzyme responsible for lactate utilization—only partially attenuated ELA-mediated protection in OGD/R neurons, suggesting the involvement of non-metabolic mechanisms (Fig. 4G).

This prompted a broader transcriptomic and functional screening (Fig. S9), which led to the discovery that identified BNIP3-mediated pathological mitophagy as a potential non-metabolic mechanism targeted by ELA. We further established that this detrimental process was robustly activated across both in vivo and in vitro models and was effectively suppressed by ELA (Fig. 5). Critically, in vivo experiments demonstrated ELA potently suppresses BNIP3-mediated pathological mitophagy through a novel FOXO3A lactylation-dependent pathway (Fig. 6). In vitro, CBP knockdown in HT22 neurons abolished ELA-induced FOXO3A lactylation, BNIP3 downregulation, and mitophagy suppression, functionally validating the pathway (Fig. 7). Together, these results provide a dual in vitro and in vivo validation of the identified mechanisms, strengthening the translational relevance of our findings.

Collectively, our findings unveil a dual-mechanism paradigm for neuroprotection: ELA acts not only by reprogramming cerebral energy metabolism to restore bioenergetics but also via the epigenetic suppression of destructive mitochondrial turnover. To our knowledge, this work provides the first integrated map of lactate-driven metabolic reprogramming in PCABI and, more significantly, discovers a previously unrecognized non-metabolic pathway where lactate acts as an epigenetic modulator via FOXO3A lactylation to preserve mitochondrial integrity. This dual-mechanism paradigm—synergistically coupling fuel utilization with mitochondrial quality control—represents a paradigm shift in understanding lactate’s therapeutic role in global cerebral ischemia.

Metabolic reprogramming: redirecting glycolytic flux and promoting oxidation

The first pillar of lactate’s mechanism involves rectifying the severe post-ischemic energy deficit. Contrary to the traditional view of lactate as a detrimental byproduct of hypoxia, our multi-omics data reveal that ELA effectively normalizes the pathological hyperglycolysis observed after CA/CPR. The coordinated downregulation of key glycolytic enzymes (HK1, PFKM) and the upregulation of LDHB and PDHA1 indicate a metabolic shift from lactate production to lactate consumption, channeling pyruvate toward oxidative phosphorylation in the mitochondria. This reprogramming is ultimately evidenced by the critical restoration of the hippocampal ATP/ADP ratio, confirming that ELA successfully mitigates the core energy crisis. This supports the emerging concept of lactate as a preferred aerobic energy substrate for the brain during stress, providing direct evidence for its beneficial role in the extreme pathological context of global cerebral ischemia.

It is generally accepted that lactate serves as an oxidative fuel in various cell types, including neurons, where it is oxidized in the TCA cycle to generate ATP through mitochondrial respiration [36, 37]. Notably, emerging evidence indicates that lactate can act as a mitochondrial messenger to directly activate the ETC, independent of its metabolic utilization, thereby shifting ATP production from glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation in certain cancer cells [38]. This observation aligns with our findings and raises the possibility that, under conditions of severe mitochondrial dysfunction, neurons may adopt metabolic features reminiscent of those observed in tumor cells.

Beyond metabolism: pathological mitophagy suppression

Beyond metabolism, our study unveils a second, more innovative mechanism: the suppression of pathological mitophagy via epigenetic regulation. Mitophagy presents a double-edged sword; it is indispensable for mitochondrial quality control and cellular homeostasis, yet its dysregulation can contribute to pathological cascades and cell death [20, 39]. We identified that excessive, aberrant mitophagy contributes to mitochondrial depletion after CA/CPR. Crucially, we discovered that lactate acts as a signaling molecule to inhibit this process. Our data delineates a clear mechanistic pathway: ELA enhances the FOXO3A lactylation in a CBP-dependent manner. This post-translational modification inhibits FOXO3A’s nuclear translocation and proteasomal degradation, preventing the transcription of the key mitophagy receptor BNIP3. BNIP3 inserts into the mitochondrial membrane, causing depolarization and ROS accumulation, and subsequently promotes LC3 binding and mitophagy. The knockdown of Crebbp severs this pathway, unequivocally demonstrating that the anti-mitophagic and neuroprotective effects of lactate are dependent on CBP-mediated lactylation. This represents a significant conceptual advance, linking lactate metabolism to the epigenetic control of mitochondrial quality surveillance.

Synergy between these two mechanisms is fundamental to ELA’s efficacy

Metabolic reprogramming acts as “fueling the engine”—it immediately improves the efficiency of ATP production from available substrates. Concurrently, pathological mitophagy suppression acts as “preventing engine destruction “—it prevents the catastrophic loss of functional mitochondria, the very organelles essential for that production. Thus, ELA simultaneously addresses both the production and preservation of cellular energy, providing a comprehensive solution to the post-ischemic energy crisis. The improved metabolic state likely creates a cellular environment conducive to survival, allowing the long-term benefits of mitochondrial preservation to be realized.

Implications and translational potential

Our findings carry significant translational implications, challenging the conventional clinical interpretation of lactate. In current practice, serum lactate levels and its clearance are employed as well-established prognostic biomarkers indicative of illness severity and poor outcomes in CA patients [23–28, 40]. However, our work demonstrates that lactate itself may offer therapeutic benefit, suggesting a crucial distinction between the prognostic value of endogenous lactate metabolism and the controlled administration of exogenous lactate as a neuroprotective agent. This shifts the paradigm of lactate from a passive marker of metabolic derangement to an active therapeutic candidate. Clinically, ELA represents a simple, cost-effective, and readily implementable intervention that could be integrated into existing post-resuscitation care protocols. Notably, while ELA has demonstrated significant neuroprotective efficacy in patients with TBI, its potential therapeutic value in PCABI remains largely unexplored. In patients with TBI, ELA enhances cerebral energy metabolism by shifting the brain from net lactate release to uptake [41], thereby promoting mitochondrial oxidative metabolism [14] and directly serving as an oxidizable substrate, as confirmed by isotopic tracing [15]; high-dose infusion improves the cerebral energy state and alleviates intracranial pressure through combined metabolic and osmotic mechanisms [42]. However, to date, no clinical studies have evaluated ELA in PCABI. Our findings reveal that in rats with PCABI, lactate’s neuroprotection extends beyond its established role as an energy substrate in TBI, involving a dual mechanism of metabolic support and non-metabolic signaling. Collectively, ELA represents a promising, clinically feasible strategy for PCABI.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, this study exclusively used male rats. While this approach was intended to minimize hormonal interference and ensure a homogenous baseline for metabolic flux, it may limit the generalizability of our findings, as females often exhibit greater tolerance to cerebral ischemia. Future studies are warranted to validate these results in both sexes. Second, although our multi-omics data strongly suggest a shift in lactate metabolism, the use of 13 C-lactate tracing would provide direct and quantitative evidence of lactate utilization and metabolic flux, further validating the proposed mechanism of metabolic reprogramming. Third, the current study focuses primarily on neurons; the contribution of glial cells (e.g., astrocytes, microglia) to lactate metabolism, FOXO3A signaling, and neuroinflammation remains unexplored. Future studies employing single-cell RNA sequencing will help delineate cell-type–specific metabolic and signaling alterations and clarify the role of glial populations in mediating lactate-driven neuroprotection. Fourth, while lactate levels were quantified in serum and hippocampal tissue, cerebrospinal fluid lactate concentrations were not measured, which may limit a comprehensive assessment of global cerebral metabolic clearance. Fifth, the mechanistic insights into FOXO3A lactylation, were primarily derived from in vitro knockdown experiments; future studies using neuron-specific Crebb or Foxo3a knockout animals would provide stronger in vivo validation. Finally, although lactate shows promising neuroprotective effects, its optimal dosing, timing, and safety profile in clinical settings require further investigation before translation into human trials.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that ELA exerts robust neuroprotective effects against PCABI through a dual-mechanism action (Fig. S10). It not only reprograms cerebral energy metabolism to enhance substrate utilization and restore mitochondrial ATP production, but also suppresses pathological mitophagy, thereby preserving functional mitochondrial mass. These two mechanisms act synergistically to resolve the post-CA brain energy crisis by simultaneously improving energy generation efficiency and safeguarding energy production infrastructure. Our findings establish exogenous lactate as both a metabolic substrate and a signaling molecule, which coordinates metabolic and mitochondrial quality-control processes to restore energy homeostasis in PCABI.
