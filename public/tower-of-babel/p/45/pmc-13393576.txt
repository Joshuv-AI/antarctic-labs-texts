Validating the Utility of Super-Resolution for Downstream Tasks in Periapical Films
Authors: Junran Peng, Meiyu Hu, Qianli Zhang, Jiarong Kong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and aimsPeriapical radiography is widely used in dental practice, but its diagnostic yield is often compromised by motion artefacts, sensor limitations, and storage compression. This study evaluates whether a deep-learning-based super-resolution (SR) framework can enhance degraded periapical films to improve automated diagnostic accuracy without additional radiation exposure.MethodsA clinical evaluation pipeline was established using 6283 periapical films. First, 5998 images were used to train an artificial intelligence-driven restoration model. Then, 285 meticulously annotated clinical images validated the SR preprocessing impact. We compared automated diagnostic performance on original low-resolution images vs SR-enhanced reconstructions, focusing on multistructure semantic segmentation and pathology/restoration detection.ResultsSR-enhanced images consistently outperformed baseline inputs. For segmentation, SR increased mean intersection over union (IoU) from 55.17% to 60.24% (P < .0001) and boundary precision (boundary IoU) improved from 23.64% to 48.22% (P < .0001), with significant improvements observed across tooth structure, restorative, and pathological category groups, demonstrating superior margin delineation. In detection tasks, SR increased recall by 4.24% (P = .0121), while mean Average Precision (mAP@50) showed a modest nonsignificant increase from 67.18% to 69.30% (P = .1114), indicating that SR primarily reduces missed detections rather than uniformly elevating detection precision.ConclusionsDeep-learning-driven SR effectively recovers critical diagnostic details from suboptimal periapical films. By significantly enhancing boundary sharpness and reducing missed detections, SR serves as an effective preprocessing step that improves geometric precision and reliability of automated dental analyses.Clinical relevanceThis software-based enhancement standardizes image quality and elevates diagnostic accuracy cost-effectively, particularly in resource-constrained settings. It maximizes the diagnostic utility of routine 2D periapical radiographs, supporting precise clinical decision-making without the radiation burden of 3D imaging.

Introduction

Periapical films are one of the most fundamental imaging modalities in clinical dentistry and serves as an essential diagnostic tool for evaluating dental structures, pulpal pathology, and periapical conditions.1,2 However, despite its widespread use, image quality is frequently compromised by sensor limitations, motion artefacts, and storage compression. These degradations manifest as reduced resolution and increased noise, which obscure critical diagnostic features, particularly for early caries and subtle periapical lesions.3 In recent years, the application of artificial intelligence (AI) in dentistry has advanced automated diagnostic systems, demonstrating satisfactory performance in image analysis tasks such as classification, detection, and segmentation.4,5 These advances have been enabled by transfer learning techniques that adapt large-scale pretrained models to domain-specific tasks.6,7 Nevertheless, the efficacy of these AI diagnostic tools depends on input image quality. Low-resolution (LR) or degraded films impair feature extraction, and blurred boundaries may introduce localization errors in automated segmentation and detection networks, thereby limiting the clinical utility of automated analysis systems.8,9 Therefore, image quality enhancement of periapical films prior to automated analysis is necessary.

Super-resolution (SR) technology reconstructs high-resolution (HR) images from LR counterparts through computational methods, offering a viable solution to address such image degradation. Early SR methods employed conventional interpolation algorithms, such as bicubic and Lanczos interpolation; however, these techniques typically produce excessively smooth results with limited preservation of high-frequency details.10 Deep learning approaches have transformed the SR research paradigm, with convolutional neural networks and generative adversarial networks achieving notable progress in natural image enhancement tasks.11,12 Although these methods improved image quality to some extent, they are constrained by localized receptive fields. Subsequently, vision transformer (ViT)-based SR methods gained attention for their global modelling capabilities, However, standard attention-based architectures scale quadratically with sequence length, which may increase computational burden in ultrahigh-resolution digitized scans, panoramic or full-mouth imaging, and higher-resolution restoration settings.13 Recently, state space models (SSMs), particularly the Mamba architecture, have emerged as an alternative approach.14 They provide linear complexity O(N) and a global receptive field capable of modelling long-range dependencies, which is applicable for tracing elongated structures such as root canals.

In medical imaging, SR has been successfully applied to various modalities, including magnetic resonance imaging, computed tomography (CT), and ultrasound.15, 16, 17 Recent studies demonstrate that SR can effectively enhance chest radiographs, improve lesion detection in low-dose CT scans, and facilitate more accurate segmentation of anatomical structures.18 However, the application of SR in dental imaging remains limited. Moran et al19 conducted an early study using SRCNN to enhance periapical radiographs, reporting improved visual quality but no significant enhancement in periodontal bone loss detection. Subsequently, Mohammad-Rahimi et al investigated multiple SR algorithms for panoramic image enhancement, while Çelik et al compared four SR models for panoramic images, demonstrating peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) improvements of up to 9 dB.20,21 Kong et al22 integrated SR with dental implant classification tasks, showing that SR-enhanced panoramic images improved tooth type classification accuracy. Chen et al23 introduced micro-CT guided SR for dental CBCT, achieving improvements in root canal visualization through edge-aware loss functions. Previous studies have primarily evaluated SR quality using image-level metrics (PSNR, structural similarity index [SSIM]) or subjective visual assessment by clinicians.20,23 However, the clinical value of SR should be assessed through its impact on downstream diagnostic tasks, specifically whether enhanced images enable AI models to achieve better performance in clinical tasks such as anatomical structure segmentation and pathological lesion detection.

The guiding research question was whether deep-learning-based SR can improve downstream AI-assisted segmentation and detection performance in periapical films compared with LR baseline images. To address this question, the present study designed a three-stage experimental workflow. In the first stage, we employed a synthetic degradation simulation strategy to train the MambaIRv2 model on unlabelled periapical films, establishing both training-from-scratch and fine-tuning protocols using pretrained weights.24 In the second stage, we constructed downstream diagnostic tasks using 285 meticulously annotated images, employing Mask2Former (with DINOv2 backbone) for seven-class semantic segmentation and YOLOv11 for multiclass object detection.25, 26, 27 In the third stage, we systematically compared model performance under three image quality conditions: original LR input, SR-enhanced reconstruction, and HR ground truth reference. The experimental results demonstrate that the MambaIRv2-based SR network enhances geometric feature representation in periapical films, thereby improving downstream segmentation and detection performance.

Materials and methods

Dataset

This study utilized two distinct datasets for different stages of the experimental pipeline. For upstream SR training, we collected 6198 unlabelled periapical films. For downstream diagnostic task validation, we utilized a previously established dataset of 285 annotated periapical films.28 All images were acquired during routine clinical care at our institution between 2012 and 2024, with ethical approval obtained from the Institutional Review Board (PKUSSIRB-2025107012). Each film typically contains 3 to 4 teeth, capturing the clinical region of interest for periapical diagnosis. All periapical films were acquired using a standardized imaging protocol with a periapical X-ray unit (Planmeca). Acquisition parameters were maintained consistently: 220 to 240 V, 60 kVp, 2 mA, and 0.05-second scan time.

For the downstream segmentation and detection tasks, we established rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure dataset quality and clinical relevance. This study aimed to evaluate AI-assisted analysis of routine anatomical structures, restorations, and common lesions in periapical radiographs of the permanent dentition. Images were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) poor-quality images or significant artefacts, because blur, abnormal exposure, geometric distortion, truncated apices, or incomplete crown coverage may obscure anatomical and pathological boundaries and reduce annotation reliability; (2) dental implants, because their assessment relies on implant-specific features rather than natural-tooth categories; (3) fixed bridges, because pontics and connectors are incompatible with single-tooth-based tooth/crown annotation rules; (4) supernumerary teeth, because their abnormal position or morphology deviates from normal permanent dentition assumptions; (5) deciduous teeth, because their morphology, root resorption, and clinical management differ from those of permanent teeth; (6) residual roots, because the absence of an anatomical crown makes consistent tooth/crown/pulp annotation unreliable; and (7) teeth with developmental dysplasia, because abnormal enamel, dentin, or pulp morphology alters the radiographic patterns targeted by the model. These criteria were applied to maintain anatomical and clinical homogeneity and to ensure consistent application of the predefined seven-class annotation scheme. The descriptive statistics of the dataset included a mean (SD, min, max) age of 35.6 (15.2, 16, 80) years and a gender ratio of 51% to 49% of males and females, respectively.

Data annotation

The labelled dataset underwent rigorous annotation with tooth structures (tooth, pulp), restorative materials (prosthetic crown, fillings, root canal fillings), and common diseases (caries, periapical lesions), meticulously annotated in pixel-wise fashion. In this seven-class scheme, ‘prosthetic crown’ refers specifically to restorative crowns (eg, metal crowns, porcelain-fused-to-metal crowns, all-ceramic crowns), not the anatomical crown of natural teeth. The hard tooth tissue above and below the cementoenamel junction is classified under the ‘tooth’ category, while the pulp chamber and root canal system are classified under the ‘pulp’ category. Annotations were performed by a dental specialist with over 10 years of clinical experience and verified by another equally experienced professional. The open-source software labelMe was used for annotation. Annotations are stored in JSON format containing polygon coordinates for seven anatomical categories: tooth, pulp, prosthetic crown, fillings, root canal filling, caries, and periapical lesions. To assess annotation consistency, three experienced dentists independently annotated 20 randomly sampled periapical films, yielding a Fleiss’ kappa coefficient of 0.948.

Study design

This study was designed to evaluate the deep learning-based image restoration paradigm and its impact on automated clinical diagnosis. Our experimental framework comprises two sequential stages: upstream SR reconstruction and downstream clinical task assessment. The dataset comprised 6198 unlabelled periapical films for SR training and 285 meticulously annotated images for downstream validation. This hierarchical design enables systematic quantification of how image quality enhancement translates to diagnostic performance improvement in clinically relevant tasks.

Upstream restoration benchmark establishment: The 6198 unlabelled images were partitioned into training (5998 images) and hold-out validation/testing (200 images) sets. The upstream SR stage used the large unlabelled image set to learn the LR-to-HR restoration mapping, while the 200-image hold-out set was used for independent reconstruction benchmarking and model comparison. We compared three SR architectures with distinct design principles: SwinIR (transformer-based), HAT (hybrid attention), and MambaIRv2 (SSM).24,29,30 To investigate the effectiveness of transfer learning in dental imaging, we evaluated two training strategies for each model: (1) Training from scratch – models initialized with random weights and trained exclusively on the dental dataset; and (2) fine-tuning – models initialized with weights pretrained on large-scale natural image datasets and subsequently adapted to dental films. This comparison was designed to determine whether domain-specific training from scratch or cross-domain knowledge transfer yields superior restoration fidelity for periapical films.

Downstream clinical validation: We validated the clinical utility of SR-enhanced images by evaluating their performance on two downstream tasks: semantic segmentation and object detection. The downstream segmentation and detection models were trained using the original HR training images and their corresponding annotations. To evaluate the effect of SR enhancement at inference, each held-out HR test image was converted into three paired input conditions: bicubic LR input, MambaIRv2 SR-enhanced input, and original HR reference input. The same HR-trained downstream model was evaluated on all three input conditions. Thus, the comparison among LR, SR-enhanced, and HR reference inputs reflected the effect of test-time image quality while keeping the downstream model, annotations, train-test split, and evaluation metrics unchanged. For the segmentation task, we employed Mask2Former with a DINOv2 backbone to delineate anatomical structures across seven categories. For the detection task, we utilized YOLOv11 to identify and localize dental targets, including restorative materials and pathological lesions. For clinical interpretation, these categories were grouped into tooth structures, including tooth and pulp; restorative materials, including prosthetic crown, fillings, and root canal fillings; and pathological categories, including caries and periapical lesions. Because one periapical film may contain structures from multiple groups, grouped statistics were calculated at the category level rather than by dividing the 285 images into mutually exclusive image-level groups. For each of the five independent Monte Carlo cross-validation runs, group-level metrics were obtained by averaging the corresponding class-wise metrics within each clinical group. The five-run mean and 95% confidence interval were reported, and SR was compared with LR using paired two-sided t tests, with P < .05 considered statistically significant. The objective was to determine whether SR-generated images improve diagnostic accuracy compared to original low-quality inputs, thereby establishing SR as a clinically meaningful preprocessing step rather than merely a visual enhancement tool.

Clinical rationale: LR periapical films may suffer from loss of high-frequency details, blurred anatomical boundaries, and reduced visibility of subtle findings. SR reconstruction was therefore investigated as a preprocessing step to restore edge and texture information and to bridge degraded image quality with reliable image analysis. The intended clinical benefits included clearer boundaries, restored edge and texture details, and improved diagnostic cues.

SR training strategy: To address the absence of naturally paired LR and HR dental films, we employed a synthetic paired data generation strategy. Original digital periapical films served as HR reference images, while corresponding LR inputs were generated using 4 × bicubic downsampling. This controlled degradation setting was used to isolate the impact of spatial resolution recovery on subsequent analysis. The paired LR-HR data were then used to train the SR model. The SR module was based on MambaIRv2, an attentive state-space image restoration framework. MambaIRv2 combines local image detail reconstruction with efficient long-range dependency modelling, making it suitable for restoring fine anatomical structures in dental radiographs. Its detailed network architecture, including the attentive state-space groups and blocks, is provided in Supplementary Figure S1. The complete super-resolution and downstream validation pipeline is illustrated in Supplementary Figure S5. MambaIRv2 has been proposed as an image restoration framework that improves noncausal image modelling and balances global receptive-field modelling with computational efficiency.

Downstream clinical task validation: To evaluate whether SR preprocessing improved clinically relevant image analysis, we performed downstream validation under matched LR, SR-enhanced, and HR input conditions. For the segmentation task, we employed Mask2Former with a DINOv2 (ViT-s/14) backbone. Based on prior validation showing that this architecture achieved superior segmentation performance on the present dataset, we selected this configuration to ensure reliable downstream evaluation. Mask2Former is designed for universal image segmentation and uses masked attention to focus feature extraction within predicted mask regions. DINOv2 provides robust self-supervised visual representations, which are useful for capturing subtle greyscale and structural differences in dental radiographs. The model generated pixel-level predictions for anatomical and pathological structures. For the detection task, we used YOLOv11 to identify and localize dental targets. YOLOv11 was selected for efficient object detection and multiscale feature extraction, enabling localization of both small pathological findings and larger anatomical structures. Both downstream models were trained to identify seven annotated categories: tooth, pulp, prosthetic crown, fillings, root canal filling, caries, and periapical lesions. During testing, the same trained downstream model was evaluated on three paired input conditions: bicubic LR input, MambaIRv2 SR-enhanced input, and original HR reference input. This matched design kept the downstream model, annotations, train-test split, and evaluation metrics unchanged, thereby isolating the effect of test-time image quality and SR preprocessing.

Clinical utility interpretation: SR-enhanced images were interpreted in relation to four downstream-relevant aspects: boundary delineation, lesion visibility, missed-detection reduction, and reliability of AI-assisted image analysis.

Model training

All experiments were conducted on a unified computational platform comprising Ubuntu 18.04, CUDA 11.7, and PyTorch 2.0.0, with Python serving as the primary programming interface. Training and inference procedures were performed on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU equipped with 24 GB memory. Specific training hyperparameters are provided in the Supplementary Material (Table S5).

Upstream SR training: The 6198 unlabelled images were partitioned into training (5998 images) and validation (200 images) sets. Three advanced architectures – SwinIR, HAT, and MambaIRv2 – were evaluated using 4 × upscaling on 64 × 64 input patches. We compared two distinct training protocols: (1) training from scratch, (2) transfer learning using weights pretrained on the DF2K dataset. This latter approach leveraged high-frequency natural textures to provide a robust foundation for image reconstruction, followed by fine-tuning on periapical films to calibrate the models to the specific greyscale and anatomical characteristics of dental imaging.

Downstream clinical task training: For both segmentation and detection, we configured seven categories (tooth, pulp, prosthetic crown, fillings, root canal fillings, caries, and periapical lesions). To ensure robust performance estimation, a fivefold Monte Carlo cross-validation strategy was employed, with an 8:2 split ratio for training and testing in each fold. (1) Segmentation task: We utilized Mask2Former with a step-wise learning rate decay. To mitigate the inherent class imbalance (eg, caries and periapical lesions), the model was optimized using a composite loss function comprising Cross-Entropy, Binary Cross-Entropy, and Dice Loss. Data augmentation was enhanced via Large Scale Jittering (LSJ) with a random scale range of 0.1 × to 2.0 × to improve multiscale feature learning. (2) Detection task: YOLOv11s was selected to balance diagnostic accuracy and computational efficiency. To refine the localization of minute or irregular pathological features, we employed complete intersection over union (IoU) and distribution focal loss. Furthermore, Mosaic augmentation was disabled during the final training phase to prevent the fragmentation of small features and ensure precise boundary localization.

Model evaluation

We employed domain-specific metrics for quantitative assessment, integrating standard image quality measures with clinically interpretable indicators. For upstream reconstruction, PSNR and SSIM were computed on the luminance (Y) channel. PSNR is defined as PSNR = 10log10(MAX²MSE), quantifying pixel-level fidelity. SSIM evaluates perceptual similarity by modelling luminance (μ), contrast (σ), and structure (σxγ): SSIM=(2μxμγ+c1)(2σx/+c2)(μx2+μγ2+c1)(σx2+σγ2+c2), where c₁ and c₂ are stabilization constants. Beyond these conventional metrics, we introduced clinically guided indicators specifically relevant to dental practice. For downstream segmentation, mean IoU (mIoU) evaluated overall accuracy across N classes: mIoU = 1NΣTP(TP+FP+FN) where TP, FP, and FN represent true positives, false positives, and false negatives, respectively. Boundary IoU additionally assessed precision along anatomical contours within a 2-pixel band surrounding ground truth boundaries, which is clinically critical as precise delineation of lesion margins directly determines the extent of caries removal and surgical incision design. For downstream detection, the mean Average Precision at IoU threshold 0.5 (mAP@50) is calculated as the mean of average precision scores for all classes: mAP@50 = 1N∑i=1NAPi. Additionally, Recall quantified detection performance: Recall = TPTP+FN.

Results

Evaluation of image quality restoration in periapical films

Quantitative analysis demonstrated that all deep-learning-based image enhancement techniques significantly outperformed the traditional Bicubic interpolation baseline (31.28 dB/0.9568) in both PSNR and SSIM metrics (Table 1). Among the evaluated architectures, when trained from scratch, MambaIRv2 attained a PSNR of 38.80 dB, compared to 36.09 dB for SwinIR and 38.75 dB for HAT. Transfer learning further improved reconstruction fidelity across all three architectures, with statistically significant gains in both PSNR and SSIM. The pretrained MambaIRv2 achieved the best overall performance, reaching 40.85 dB in PSNR and 0.9754 in SSIM. Discussion

This study establishes that deep learning-based SR preprocessing improves downstream automated diagnostic performance on periapical radiographs. In semantic segmentation, SR-enhanced images achieved a higher mIoU (60.24% vs 55.17%; P < .0001) and a striking improvement in boundary IoU (48.22% vs 23.64%; P < .0001), indicating that SR reconstruction recovers the high-frequency edge information necessary for precise anatomical delineation. In object detection, overall recall improved significantly (60.28% vs 56.04%; P = .0121). The increase in overall mAP@50 was modest (69.30% vs 67.18%; P = .1114). This suggests that SR primarily reduces missed detections rather than uniformly elevating detection precision. Grouped analyses further revealed significant improvements across all three clinically defined categories: tooth structures (IoU: 68.06%-71.06%; P = .0002), restorative materials (IoU: 68.13%-73.24%; P = .0014), and pathological lesions (IoU: 22.85%-29.90%; P = .0013). Notably, the restorative and pathological groups showed the largest boundary IoU gains, underscoring that low-contrast and fine-structured regions benefit disproportionately from resolution recovery.

The present study extends previous dental SR research by evaluating SR-enhanced periapical radiographs in downstream segmentation and detection tasks, rather than relying solely on image restoration metrics or visual assessment. This task-oriented perspective is consistent with broader discussions in dental AI, where recent comprehensive reviews have charted the expanding role of AI across diagnosis, treatment planning, and patient care, while simultaneously emphasizing risks including data privacy, algorithmic bias, limited generalizability, and the need for rigorous preclinical validation.31,32 Within this context, the clinical relevance of SR should not be inferred solely from visual improvement or reconstruction metrics, but should also be examined through its impact on downstream diagnostic workflows. Previous studies have shown that SR can improve the visual quality and quantitative reconstruction metrics of dental radiographs, particularly in panoramic imaging. For example, Mohammad-Rahimi et al and Çelik et al investigated SR-based enhancement of panoramic radiographs, with evaluations mainly based on reconstruction quality metrics and visual assessment.20,21 More recent work indicates that dental SR is moving towards more task-aware and clinically oriented evaluation. Lee et al33 proposed a confidence-aware transformer-based SR framework for panoramic radiographs, integrating pixel-wise uncertainty estimation to reduce overenhancement in noise-sensitive regions and evaluating performance with both quantitative metrics and mean opinion scores. Similarly, Çelik et al34 directly examined whether SR improves dental radiographic classification accuracy, showing improved performance in several SR-classifier combinations. In parallel, PanoDiff-SR extended dental SR towards generative modelling by combining diffusion-based panoramic radiograph synthesis with transformer-based SR to generate HR synthetic panoramic images.35 Nevertheless, these studies remain focused on panoramic radiographs, tooth-type classification, diagnostic image quality, or synthetic image realism rather than periapical segmentation and lesion detection. This distinction is clinically important because visually sharper images do not necessarily lead to better AI performance. SR algorithms may recover useful anatomical detail, but they may also introduce artificial textures, alter subtle lesion boundaries, or amplify image features that are not diagnostically meaningful. Although Moran et al36 investigated resolution-enhanced periapical radiographs and reported improved visual quality after deep-learning-based enhancement, the improvement did not consistently translate into better periodontal bone-loss classification performance. Therefore, by comparing LR, SR-enhanced, and HR periapical radiographs within matched segmentation and detection workflows, the present study provides a more direct assessment of whether SR contributes to clinically relevant automation rather than merely improving image appearance.

We further analysed the differential benefits of SR across tasks and anatomical categories. Segmentation is a pixel-wise operation that relies heavily on high-frequency boundary information; because bicubic downsampling disproportionately degrades these spectral components, SR recovery yields geometrically precise anatomical maps and explains the doubling of boundary IoU.37 In contrast, object detection networks such as YOLO depend more on global semantic features and contextual cues that remain relatively robust under moderate resolution loss. Consequently, SR sharpening improves recall by recovering obscured fine structures, most evident in pulp (77.34%-82.02%) and root canal filling (71.28%-83.90%), but does not create new semantic information for object recognition, accounting for the modest mAP@50 improvement. Within segmentation, the improvement varied markedly by category. High-contrast structures with density-defined boundaries, such as tooth and prosthetic crown, showed moderate gains because their margins remain partially discernible even in LR images. Conversely, low-contrast interfaces, including pulp, periapical lesions, and root canal fillings, exhibited substantially larger relative improvements, suggesting that SR rescues anatomical details that fall to the noise floor in degraded inputs. The architectural choice of MambaIRv2 aligns with these observations. Its effective global contextual modelling is unconstrained by the windowing limitations of standard Transformers. This facilitates precise restoration of fine, elongated anatomical textures across the full image. This capability is reflected in its superior PSNR (40.85 dB after pretraining) compared with SwinIR and HAT. The consistent benefit of transfer learning across all architectures further indicates that low-level visual features learned from natural images transfer efficiently to radiographic anatomy, accelerating convergence in a domain where large annotated datasets are scarce.38,39

The geometric precision gains carry direct clinical relevance. For a 5-mm periapical lesion, the observed segmentation improvement corresponds to approximately 0.25 mm better edge localization, a margin meaningful for surgical planning and caries extent determination. Moreover, SR offers a software-based means to standardize image quality across heterogeneous clinical environments, potentially allowing resource-limited clinics to approach modern diagnostic standards without immediate hardware upgrades. Nevertheless, ethical and operational risks require attention. As Bommanavar et al40 highlighted, dental AI systems face data privacy vulnerabilities, dataset bias, and validity concerns. We mitigated these through institutional IRB approval (PKUSSIRB-2025107012), comprehensive deidentification, local offline processing, and expert-verified annotations (Fleiss’ kappa = 0.948). Bommanavar and Prabhu41 further underscored black-box behaviour and hallucination risks. Our deterministic image-processing pipeline outputs reconstructed images, masks, and bounding boxes rather than free-text recommendations. This substantially reduces semantic hallucination risk. However, SR-induced artefacts may still distort boundaries and mislead interpretation. We addressed transparency through interpretable metrics, matched LR/SR/HR validation, privacy-preserving local processing, and inference-time reporting (∼0.3 seconds per image). Despite these safeguards, single-centre origin and limited representation of rare pathologies remain residual biases necessitating multicentre validation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the downstream dataset comprised only 285 annotated images with class imbalance (caries ∼6.56%, periapical lesions ∼3.94%), constraining statistical robustness for rare categories despite specialized loss functions. Second, synthetic bicubic downsampling simplifies real-world degradation (motion blur, scatter, uneven exposure); while supplementary JPEG and Gaussian noise analyses demonstrated broader robustness (Supplementary Analysis S1 and S2), complex mixed degradation requires further validation. Quantitative category-level and grouped metrics for these analyses are detailed in Supplementary Tables S1–S4, with representative segmentation visualizations provided in Supplementary Figures S2–S4. Third, the crown was analysed as a cumulative category because two-dimensional projection radiography limits reliable separation of enamel, dentin, and coronal pulp without dedicated fine-grained protocols. Fine-grained crown substructure segmentation may be explored using bitewing radiographs or micro-CT in future work. Fourth, and most importantly, the absence of a clinician-integrated reader study limits direct evidence that SR improves dentist diagnostic accuracy; this work should be interpreted as an algorithm-level proof-of-concept. Fifth, the upstream hold-out set comprised 200 images (3.3% of 5998), a proportion consistent with representation learning paradigms prioritizing maximal training data, though larger independent validation would strengthen generalizability. Additionally, the exclusion of implants, fixed bridges, supernumerary teeth, deciduous teeth, residual roots, and dental dysplasia ensured cohort homogeneity for the current seven-class scheme, but limits generalizability to these clinically encountered structures. Finally, single-centre data limit external validity. Future work should expand pathological diversity, explore blind SR for complex real-world degradation, and develop fine-grained crown annotation protocols. Most critically, prospective multicentre reader studies should compare LR and SR-enhanced films using diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, false-positive rate, and interobserver variability as outcomes. Such studies will help determine whether algorithmic gains translate into reduced interobserver variability and improved real-world diagnostic decisions among diverse practitioners, addressing key challenges in the broader integration of AI into dental medicine.42,43

In conclusion, this study validates SSM-based SR as an effective preprocessing step for periapical radiographs. By recovering high-frequency geometric details, SR significantly improves automated segmentation boundary precision and detection recall, particularly for low-contrast anatomical and pathological structures. These findings position SR enhancement as a practical tool to elevate diagnostic image quality and support reliable automation in dental image analysis workflows.

Conclusion

This study validates the utility of SSM-based SR as a preprocessing paradigm for periapical films. Our findings demonstrate that the proposed framework effectively recovers anatomical details from LR inputs, which translates into observable performance gains for downstream automated tasks. Specifically, the restoration process improved the geometric precision of semantic segmentation, particularly regarding boundary delineation, and enhanced the recall and localization accuracy of object detection for clinically relevant structures. These results confirm that SR can serve as a valuable tool to upgrade legacy imaging data, facilitating more reliable automated analysis in dental diagnostic workflows.
