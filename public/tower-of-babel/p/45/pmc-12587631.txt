Neurovascular coupling abnormalities in first-episode drug-naïve major depressive disorder patients
Authors: Shuyang Cai, Qinger Guo, Xiumei Wu, Yu Fang, Yafei Zhang, Weihua Zhou, Desheng Shang, Tao Chen, Yating Lv, Jinfeng Duan, Hong Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMajor depressive disorder (MDD) is currently recognized as the most common and debilitating psychiatric disorder worldwide, while the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying MDD remain unclear. This study aims to investigate neurovascular coupling (NVC) alteration in MDD patients and its correlation with disease severity and sex.MethodsArterial spin labeling and blood-oxygen-level-dependent data were collected from 51 MDD patients and 51 healthy controls (HCs). Spatial and temporal correlations between the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation (ALFF) and cerebral blood flow (CBF) were analyzed as NVC metrics. Differences between groups and subgroups, as well as correlations between ALFF-CBF coupling and clinical measurements, were explored.ResultsFirst, spatial correlations found that, compared to HCs, MDD patients showed reduced whole-brain ALFF-CBF coupling, with subgroup analysis revealing significant reductions in severe and female MDD patients. Second, temporal correlation analysis showed that, compared to HCs, moderate MDD patients demonstrated increased ALFF-CBF coupling in the left insula, whereas severe MDD patients showed reduced coupling in the left anterior cingulate cortex and increased coupling in the right superior occipital gyrus. In subgroup analysis, male MDD patients presented lower ALFF-CBF coupling in the left superior frontal orbital gyrus. Third, the spatial correlation of ALFF-CBF coupling in female MDD patients was negatively correlated with anxiety symptom scores.ConclusionsNVC decoupling reflects a potential neuropathological mechanism in MDD, with heterogeneous spatial-temporal patterns based on disease severity and sex.Keywords: Major depressive disorder, Neurovascular coupling, Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging, Arterial spin labeling

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is currently recognized as the most common and debilitating psychiatric disorder worldwide, characterized by highly heterogeneous clinical features (persistent mood disturbances, loss of interest, cognitive deficits, as well as somatic symptoms) [1]. MDD has become the second leading cause of years lived with disability worldwide, closely linked to heavy socioeconomic costs [2]. However, the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying MDD remain unclear.

The human brain only weighs 1.5 kg but accepts an estimated one-fifth of the cardiac output [3]. Cerebral blood flow (CBF) delivers oxygen and glucose to activated brain cells and clears metabolic byproducts to maintain the homeostasis of the central nervous system [4]. The close temporal and spatial coordination between CBF response and neuronal activity to ensure appropriate metabolic demand of the human brain is neurovascular coupling (NVC) [3, 5]. NVC is a crucial contributor to brain development and the stabilization of the homeostatic microenvironment [4, 6]. A decreased capacity of CBF to match the energy demands of neurons could result in NVC decoupling. Evidence from mouse models implicated that alterations in the NVC are related to impaired cognitive and sensorimotor functions [7]. Furthermore, NVC alterations are correlated with brain dysfunction in several neuropsychiatric disorders, such as Alzheimer’s disease [4], attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder [8], and mood disorders [9].

Over the past decade, multiple magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques have emerged as noninvasive proxies for assessing intrinsic regional brain activity, including the blood-oxygen-level-dependent functional MRI (BOLD-fMRI) and arterial spin labeling (ASL). Amplitude of low frequency fluctuations (ALFF) provides a task-free index for measuring regional spontaneous neuronal activity in the BOLD signal [10], with the best balance between test–retest reliability and replicability in resting state fMRI (rs-fMRI) metrics [11]. ASL is a neuroimaging technique that reliably measures regional CBF (rCBF) by an endogenous contrast, which might serve as the most comparable method to the positron emission tomography perfusion imaging technique [12].

Studies utilizing BOLD or ASL techniques have identified altered ALFF and/or CBF in MDD patients, particularly within key brain regions such as the default mode network (DMN), limbic-subcortical-cortical circuits, and motor-related brain regions [13]. Notably, despite the close association between BOLD signals and CBF in healthy brains [14], accumulating evidence suggests that this relationship may be altered in MDD patients [9, 13]. A multimodal meta-analysis revealed that in certain brain regions, changes in CBF do not always demonstrate consistent alterations in ALFF [13]. Similarly, using a non-parametric approach to co-analyze the concordance and dissociation between functional connectivity (FC) and CBF, Sheng reported FC-CBF decoupling in regions such as the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and occipital cortex in MDD patients [9]. While several studies have been published based on unimodal MRI techniques, combining BOLD and ASL modalities offers a more comprehensive understanding of NVC abnormalities, which has been illustrated in patients with generalized anxiety disorder for mapping abnormal NVC patterns linked to pathophysiology [15]. Nonetheless, the alterations in NVC and the associated factors in MDD patients remain incompletely understood.

Evidence suggests that NVC might exhibit different patterns in relation to the depression disease severity, which could be reflected by changes in the regional properties of CBF and intrinsic brain activity in MDD patients. For instance, distinct cerebral hemodynamic patterns in MDD patients allowed for the classification of mild or severe depression [16]. Concurrently, the variations in reduced voxel-mirrored homotopic connectivity (VMHC) potentially served as a biomarker for effectively differentiating healthy controls (HCs), mild to moderate, and severe MDD groups [17]. Furthermore, NVC can be influenced by sex, with males and females exhibiting different NVC patterns [6, 18], which might be due to hormonal influence [18]. Specifically, females exhibit higher baseline CBF than males [19], and the susceptibility of females to MDD [2] might be associated with vulnerability to CBF declines [19]. Additionally, female MDD patients also exhibited more widespread differences in ALFF values compared to male MDD patients, with alterations observed in the frontoparietal, auditory, attentional, and cerebellar networks [20]. However, few studies have investigated the effects of severity and sex on NVC in MDD patients. To gain a comprehensive understanding of NVC alterations in MDD, it is essential to explore both the effects of disease severity and sex in subgroups.

Accordingly, the aims of this study are to investigate NVC alterations in drug-naive first-episode MDD patients by analyzing the spatial and temporal correlation between ALFF and CBF maps [21] and to further define NVC changes in disease severity and sex. We hypothesized that, first, MDD patients would show abnormal NVC patterns at both the whole brain and regional levels. Second, distinct neurovascular decoupling patterns will be observed in different stages of depression and sex subgroups.

Methods

Participants

Fifty-one patients with MDD (25.16 ± 0.79 years, 37 females) were recruited from First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, and underwent assessments including the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17) and Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA) [22–24]. Each patient met the following inclusion criteria: (1) diagnosis of MDD by two experienced licensed psychiatrists based on the Diagnostic Statistical Manual of Mental Disorder, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria; (2) HAMD score ≥ 17 points; (3) confirmed to be the first episode of major depression without a history of any treatment. Fifty-one age- and sex- matched HCs (26.61 ± 0.92 years, 31 females) were also included in the current study.

Participants were excluded if they had: (1) left-handed; (2) a history of brain trauma, a diagnosis of neurological disease, mental retardation, or developmental disorder; (3) a history of drug or alcohol abuse; (4) Women during pregnancy and breastfeeding; (5) other MRI contraindications.

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Research Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University, and all the participants signed a written informed consent.

Image acquisition

All images were acquired on a 3.0T GE Discovery MRI scanner with a 32-channel head and neck combined array coil at First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine.

The T1-weighted images were acquired with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 8.208 ms, echo time (TE) = 3.2 ms, field of view (FOV) = 256 × 256 mm2, flip angle = 8°, matrix = 256 × 256, slice thickness/spacing = 1 mm/0 mm, scanning time = 292 s.

The resting-state functional images were acquired with 43 slices, TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, FOV = 220 × 220 mm2, flip angle = 90°, matrix = 64 × 64, slice thickness/spacing = 3.2 mm/0 mm, scanning time = 400 s.

The 3D ASL images were acquired with 40 slices, TR = 4635 ms, TE = 10.5 ms, FOV = 240 × 240 mm2, post-labeling delay = 1525 ms, slice thickness = 4 mm, no gap, number of excitations = 3, scanning time = 267 s.

During the scan, each participant was instructed to keep their eyes closed, stay awake, and hold still.

Data preprocessing

The preprocessing of resting-state functional images was carried out using the RESTplus toolbox (http://restfmri.net/forum/RESTplus) [25] based on the MATLAB platform. The preprocessing steps included: (1) removed the initial 10 time points of the functional images. (2) sliced time correction. (3) realigned spatially for head motion. Participants with maximal displacement > 3 mm, rotation > 3° or mean framewise displacement >0.5 were excluded [26]. (4) normalized spatially to the MNI standard space by deformation fields derived from tissue segmentation of the T1-weighted images. (5) smoothed spatially using a Gaussian kernel of 6 mm full width at half maximum (FWHM). (6) removed of linear trend (7) regressed out 24 head motion parameter, white matter signals, and cerebrospinal fluid signals [27]. The Anatomical Automatic Labeling (AAL) atlas was used with 8 subcortical regions removed to obtain the mask for subsequent analyses.

ALFF calculation

After transforming the time series of each voxel to the frequency domain via Fast Fourier Transformation and obtain the power spectrum, the ALFF map was obtained by computing the average square root of the power within the frequency band of 0.01–0.08 Hz for each voxel. ALFF map was further divided by the mean ALFF (mALFF) of the corresponding individual map for further analyses [10].

Processing of CBF

The CBF images were preprocessed using SPM12 (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm) based on the MATLAB platform. The preprocessing steps included: (1) the CBF images were automatically constructed in the GE workstation. (2) co-registered ASL and CBF images to T1-weighted images. (3) normalized co-registered T1 images into MNI space, applied the transform parameters obtained from the prior step to individual CBF maps, and normalized co-CBF images to MNI space. (4) each CBF map was further divided by the mean CBF (mCBF) of the corresponding individual image for standardization and subsequent analyses.

ALFF-CBF spatial correlation and inter-group differences

Group analyses

To evaluate MDD-related NVC alteration between MDD and HCs, Pearson correlation coefficients between ALFF and CBF maps were spatially correlated at the whole brain level within the AAL mask. A generalized linear model (GLM) was performed to investigate group differences with age, sex, and education as covariates (P < 0.05/3 = 0.017, Bonferroni corrected).

Subgroup analyses

To determine the possible impact of disease severity and sex on NVC, we conducted two sets of subgroup analyses: (1) According to the previous study [24], the severity of MDD symptoms can be reflected by the HAMD-17, and the cut-off score of 24 was usually used to define moderate depression and severe depression. In this study, MDD patients were further subgrouped into groups of severe MDD patients (MDDs, HAMD scores ≥ 24) and moderate MDD patients (MDDm, HAMD scores < 24). Similar to group analyses, a GLM was applied to the comparisons between the MDDs and HCs, as well as the MDDm and HCs, with age, sex, and education years treated as covariates (P < 0.05/3 = 0.017, Bonferroni corrected). (2) Another subgroup analysis was conducted to explore sex differences, separately for male and female subgroups (P < 0.05/3 = 0.017, Bonferroni corrected).

ALFF-CBF temporal correlation and inter-group differences

To investigate MDD-related NVC alteration at the voxel level, we performed Pearson correlations between ALFF and CBF maps across participants for each voxel. Next, we normalized the resultant temporal correlation maps by Fisher’s r-to-z transformation. Then we compared the NVC difference between MDD and HCs. In the same way, subgroup comparisons were conducted between MDDs and HCs, between MDDm and HCs, as well as between different sex subgroups. Age, sex, and education level were used as covariates. The statistical threshold was set at voxel with P < 0.01 and the cluster size > 20.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out with SPSS v25.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, New York). Demographic and clinical characteristics were checked by conducting two-sample student t-tests and Chi-squared tests. P < 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance. Spearman correlation analysis was used to analyze the relationship between ALFF-CBF coupling and clinical scales in MDD patients. To assess sex differences in anxiety scores, GLM was performed between male and female participants on HAMA total scores, with age and education level used as covariates.

ALFF-CBF spatial correlation alterations

The spatial correlation method was used to reflect NVC changes at the whole-brain level. In all subjects, both MDD patients and HCs exhibited significant spatial correlation between ALFF and CBF maps (all P < 0.001).

The MDD group exhibited globally lower ALFF-CBF coupling (rMDD = 0.336 ± 0.011, rHCs = 0.353 ± 0.010, P = 0.005, Bonferroni corrected) compared to HCs (Fig. 1A).

The comparisons of ALFF-CBF spatial correlations between groups and subgroups. A The MDD group exhibited reduced ALFF-CBF coupling compared with HCs. B MDDs exhibited decreased ALFF-CBF coupling compared with HCs. C Male HC showed significantly decreased ALFF-CBF coupling compared with female HC. D Female MDD patients showed reduced ALFF-CBF coupling compared with female HC

In subgroup analysis stratified by disease severity, MDDs exhibited significantly reduced ALFF-CBF coupling (rMDDs = 0.322 ± 0.016, rHCs = 0.353 ± 0.010, P = 0.012, Bonferroni corrected) compared with HCs. There was no difference when comparing MDDm and HCs (P = 0.05, uncorrected) (Fig. 1B). In subgroup analysis stratified by sex, we observed that male HC showed significantly decreased ALFF-CBF coupling (rmaleHC = 0.308 ± 0.016, rfemaleHC = 0.383 ± 0.011, P < 0.001, Bonferroni corrected) relative to female HC (Fig. 1C). Moreover, female MDD patients manifested decreased ALFF-CBF coupling (rfemaleMDD = 0.357 ± 0.012, rfemaleHC = 0.383 ± 0.011, P = 0.009, Bonferroni corrected) compared with female HC, while this difference was not found when comparing male MDD with male HC (Fig. 1D).

ALFF-CBF temporal correlation alterations

The temporal correlation method was used to reflect NVC changes at the regional level. The ALFF-CBF temporal correlation maps of groups and subgroups are shown in Fig. 2. No brain regions exhibiting abnormal ALFF-CBF coupling were identified in MDD patients when compared to HCs. However, in subgroup analysis stratified by disease severity, the MDDm showed increased ALFF-CBF coupling in the left insula (INS.L) (Fig. 3A), whereas the MDDs demonstrated reduced ALFF-CBF coupling in the left anterior cingulate cortex (ACC.L) and increased coupling in the right superior occipital gyrus (SOG.R) (Fig. 3B). In subgroup analysis stratified by sex, the male MDD patients presented lower ALFF-CBF coupling in the left superior frontal orbital gyrus (ORBsup.L) compared to male HC (Fig. 3C), while this difference was not found when comparing female MDD with female HC. Correlation analysis

Female MDD patients exhibited higher HAMA total scores compared to male MDD patients (P = 0.01). Discussion

This study examined the spatial and temporal correlation between the resting-state rCBF and ALFF in first-episode drug-naive MDD groups and subgroups to provide general insights into our understanding of NVC alteration in the disease. Spatial correlation found that MDD patients exhibited lower ALFF-CBF coupling than HCs at the whole brain level, with the decreased ALFF-CBF coupling more pronounced in MDDs and female MDD groups. The primary findings of temporal correlation included: abnormal ALFF-CBF coupling was not identified in comparison between HCs and all MDD patients. But subgroup analysis found that MDDm showed increased ALFF-CBF coupling in the INS.L, whereas MDDs exhibited decreased coupling in the ACC.L and increased coupling in the SOG.R. Moreover, the male MDD group showed lower ALFF-CBF coupling in the ORBsup.L, while the difference wasn’t found in female MDD patients. Correlation analysis revealed that the spatial correlation of ALFF-CBF coupling in female MDD patients was negatively correlated with anxiety symptom scores. Our findings indicated the neurovascular decoupling in the MDD group, which was similar to previous findings [9]. Notably, our study proved the existence of heterogeneity in NVC among both male and female patients and in the progression of MDD.

In the spatial correlation analysis, our result showed a significant close ALFF-CBF coupling in all subjects at the whole brain level, which is consistent with previous findings [8], indicating that neuronal activity highly relies on the regulation of CBF, and NVC plays a crucial role in maintaining the physiological mechanisms of the human brain. Additionally, ALFF-CBF coupling in MDD group was decreased compared to HCs, which is also in line with the finding of earlier research [9], suggesting a widespread neurovascular decoupling phenomenon in MDD pathophysiology. This decoupling reflects an imbalance between neural activity and CBF, which may perturb the brain’s homeostatic microenvironment and contribute to functional impairments [5, 7]. Similarly, the pathophysiology of MDD involves FC-CBF decoupling in key brain regions, including the prefrontal cortex and occipital cortex, suggesting that neurovascular decoupling plays a crucial role in MDD progression [9]. We cautiously speculate that the NVC dysfunction in MDD patients may be closely related to the impairments in the neurovascular unit (NVU), a critical component of NVC. The NVU is composed of neurons, astrocytes, microglia, and vascular cells (endothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle cells, and pericytes) [4], and its integrity and stability are essential for the function of NVC. For instance, astrocytes surround synapses and thus can send signals from neuronal cells to vascular cells [4]. Some researchers have reported reduced synaptic density in MDD patients, especially involving regions related to affective processing [28]. Additionally, a reduced number of astrocytes [29] and impaired vascular cells [30] have been observed in depression. Moreover, as part of the NVU in the brain [31], the blood-brain barrier (BBB) was disrupted in MDD patients, with this disruption being more pronounced in the brain regions involved in emotional regulation [32]. In the subgroup analysis, we found that the reduction in ALFF-CBF coupling is more pronounced in MDDs. We hypothesize that these results may reflect either adaptive compensatory mechanisms or mild brain abnormalities in MDDm. This phenomenon is consistent with Ramasubbu’s findings that less severe forms of MDD may exhibit heterogeneity in fMRI data acquired under both resting-state and task-state conditions [33] and is also similar to a study on the compensatory increase in neuroactive steroid in mild-to-moderate depression subjects [34]. In summary, our results suggest that the changes in NVC may serve as a key mechanism in MDD progression. Further research is needed to clarify the potential physiological mechanisms underlying changes in NVC in MDD.

In the temporal correlation analysis, we did not observe the differences between all MDD patients and HCs. Considering the potential confounding effects of disease severity, we further conducted subgroup analyses. Our findings showed increased ALFF-CBF coupling in the MDDm compared to HCs in the INS.L, suggesting an abnormal pattern of NVC in this region. The INS serves as an interface between sensory, mood control, and cognition functions [35], characterized as a dynamic switch between the DMN and the central executive network [36]. In pathological conditions, the INS responds to emotional stimuli through activation, compensating for the impaired emotional processing ability [37], which might indicate a compensatory response associated with the severity of depression. We postulate that the compensatory mechanism in moderate stage depression maintains the adaptive regulation of information flow between different neural networks, but it should be validated through longitudinal studies.

In this study, MDDs showed decreased ALFF-CBF coupling in the ACC.L. As a core component of the DMN and a critical hub for emotional regulation [38], the ACC plays a crucial part in the pathophysiology of MDD [13]. The observed ALFF-CBF decoupling in the ACC of MDDs is consistent with a study on the biochemical level, which revealed a negative relationship between γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) levels in the ACC and HAMD scores [39]. Specifically, GABA receptors mediated intracellular Ca2+ signals in cerebral microvascular endothelial cells and constituted a critical pathway for neuro-to-vascular signaling communication at the NVU. The features of GABA-mediated reductions in NVC may explain the decreased ALFF-CBF coupling in this region [40]. We also observed increased ALFF-CBF coupling in the SOG.R when comparing MDD s to HCs. The occipital lobe is concerned with receiving and processing visual information [41]. Previous studies have reported higher ALFF values in SOG among patients with depression and non-suicidal self-injury (more than half of those with severe depression) [42], and this hyperactivation appears to be normalized after antidepressant treatment [43]. Another study has demonstrated the positive correlation between increased CBF in this region and the severity of depression [44]. These results may reflect enhanced coupling in the visual network between neural activity and CBF, which helps meet the increased brain energy demand for processing visual information in MDD patients [45].

when analyzing HCs, a notable sex effect was observed with significantly higher baseline ALFF-CBF coupling demonstrated in female HC at the spatial level. This result was consistent with the earlier research [46, 47]. In addition, our result found a significant decrease in ALFF-CBF coupling in female patients compared to female HC, whereas the difference was not statistically significant between male MDD and male HC. One potential reason is the lower statistical power of the male subgroup, as a lower number of male subjects were recruited in this study. However, as is widely known, the incidence of depression is higher in females than in males [2]. The higher baseline level of NVC in female might reflect their greater metabolic needs [48], and could make them more susceptible to NVC disruptions due to their higher baseline energy requirements [19]. Estrogen plays an important role in neurovascular protective effects through preventing inflammation-induced BBB dysfunctions [49]. Additionally, there is also an association between decreased estrogen levels and depression [50]. Moreover, evidence suggests that estrogen deficiency can disrupt NVC by influencing NVU cell types through activation of estrogen receptors or G protein-coupled estrogen receptor 1 [51], as well as disrupt BBB functions [49]. These findings might indicate potential mechanisms underlying the increased susceptibility of females to depression.

At the temporal level, decreased ALFF-CBF coupling was observed in the left superior frontal orbital gyrus in male patients. The superior frontal orbital gyrus is part of the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), which is involved in various functions such as decision-making, processing rewards and punishments, emotional and executive functions [52]. It is noteworthy that androgen receptors are expressed in the OFC [53]. Studies have reported that genetic polymorphisms of androgen receptors in this area are associated with gray matter volume (GMV) [54], and gender-specific reductions in gray matter volume in the OFC have been observed in male patients [55]. These findings suggest that sex hormones might exert effects on the OFC through receptors. Testosterone has been implicated as a critical factor in the physiopathology of depressive disorder [56]. Some researchers have reported that testosterone has major actions in promoting cerebral angiogenesis and vasculature formation, as well as modulating the integrity of the BBB [31, 57]. Therefore, testosterone might influence NVC through mechanisms involving neuroplasticity and vascular. This finding may partly explain the regional NVC changes in the orbitofrontal region of male patients.

In this study, male and female patients differed significantly in HAMA total scores. Anxiety disorders are common comorbidities in MDD [1]. Consistent with our result, women are more likely to experience a higher burden from MDD or anxiety disorders than men [2]. Additionally, the ALFF-CBF coupling values in female MDD patients at the whole brain level showed a significant negative correlation with the scores of HAMA and mental anxiety, suggesting that neurovascular decoupling could be a key pathophysiological basis for anxiety symptoms in female MDD patients. Animal models have confirmed that damage to the NVU can lead to anxiety symptoms [58]. Imaging studies have also demonstrated that patients with generalized anxiety disorder exhibit reduced functional connectivity strength and CBF coupling, further supporting the association between NVC abnormalities and anxiety disorders [15].

Several limitations should be mentioned. First, this study recruited a relatively small number of subjects, which may influence the statistical power. Second, this study was a cross-sectional design. Further longitudinal studies are required to reveal dynamic progressive pathological changes in MDD. Third, the absence of hormone level measurements precludes the ability to verify the correlation between hormones and depression. To further validate our findings, large-scale dynamic longitudinal cohort studies and sex-related investigations are warranted. Additionally, mechanistic studies are needed to elucidate the potential mechanisms of neurovascular uncoupling.

In conclusion, drug-naïve first-episode MDD patients show abnormality in ALFF-CBF coupling, indicating an imbalance in CBF and spontaneous neural activity. This neurovascular decoupling may represent a key pathophysiological mechanism in MDD, exhibiting distinct spatiotemporal patterns that vary with disease severity and sex.
