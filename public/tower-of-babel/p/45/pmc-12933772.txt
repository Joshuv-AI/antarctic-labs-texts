The path forward for T cell engagers in patients with prostate cancer
Authors: Sumit K Subudhi, Bilal A Siddiqui, Kevin K Zarrabi, William K Kelly, Charles G Drake
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractT cell engagers (TCEs) recruit T cells to the tumor microenvironment (TME) to induce antitumor immune responses. TCEs have demonstrated promising clinical responses in patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer and have advanced into phase 3 clinical trials. Here we provide an overview of the mechanisms of action of TCEs, including both CD3-targeted and CD28-targeted agents, and review the clinical development of these agents for prostate cancer. We propose a path forward for TCEs in prostate cancer, in which innovative clinical trials will facilitate a biological understanding of mechanisms of efficacy and toxicity to inform: (1) development of predictive biomarkers for patient selection; (2) rational combination strategies; and (3) targeted treatments for toxicity management, ultimately delivering broad clinical benefit for patients with lethal prostate cancer.Keywords: Bispecific T cell engager - BiTE, T cell

The immunosuppressive prostate tumor microenvironment

Intratumoral effector T cells are required to mount effective antitumor immune responses. Cancers are classified by the degree of intratumoral T cell infiltration within the tumor microenvironment (TME), described as a “tumor immunity continuum” into: (1) inflamed; (2) immune-excluded; and (3) immune-desert.1 “‘Inflamed” cancers, such as melanoma, are characterized by a high degree of effector T cell infiltration, intact antigen presentation, induction of interferon gamma signaling and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, all leading to relatively high rates of response to immune checkpoint therapies (ICTs).1 “Immune-excluded” tumors are those in which effector T cells have accumulated at the invasive margin of the tumor but are unable to migrate into the tumor center, in part due to reactive stroma and high transforming growth factor (TGF)-β signaling, leading to low rates of response to ICTs.1 Finally, “immune desert” tumors are characterized by few overall effector T cells in the tumor center or periphery, with minimal responses to ICTs.1

Prostate cancer generally falls into the “immune desert” category, with few intratumor effector T cells, low tumor mutational burden (TMB) and neoantigen formation, high density of immunosuppressive myeloid cells, suppressive cytokines such as TGF-β, and metabolic pathways such as adenosine signaling.2,4 Accordingly, response rates to ICTs with prostate cancer have been markedly lower than for those of inflamed tumor types, ranging from 3% to 5% with single-agent anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) therapies to up to 25% with combination anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) and anti-PD-(L)1 therapy.5,8

Principles of T cell engagers

T cell engagers (TCEs) aim to overcome these immunosuppressive barriers by directly bringing T cells into the TME. TCEs are designed so that one domain binds to a protein expressed on T cells, whereas the other binds to a protein expressed on tumor cells. To induce an effective antitumor immune response, TCEs must not only induce proximity between T cells and tumor cells but must also facilitate T cell activation. This concept of using bispecific antibodies to engage CD8+ T cells and induce cancer cell lysis was first demonstrated in 1985 by Staerz and colleagues.9

Classical T cell activation requires two fundamental signals (figure 1). Signal 1 is delivered when the T cell receptor (TCR), in complex with CD3, recognizes antigen bound to major histocompatibility complex (MHC) on antigen presenting cells (APCs) (eg, dendritic cells, macrophages, and activated B cells) (figure 1). Signal 1 alone generally leads to T cell anergy. Therefore, a second, costimulatory signal (Signal 2) is required for optimal T cell activation (figure 1). The best-characterized T cell costimulatory receptor, CD28, is constitutively expressed on T cells and binds to the B7 family molecules CD80 (B7-1) and CD86 (B7-2). Together, Signal 1 plus Signal 2 leads to optimal T cell activation and survival (figure 1).

The majority of TCEs under investigation (and all TCEs currently approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA)) target CD3. However, newer classes of TCEs targeting T cell costimulatory receptors such as CD28 and 4-1BB (CD137), as well as agents targeting gamma delta T cells, are also emerging and are under active investigation. Early evidence demonstrated that high-affinity crosslinking of CD3 could induce a non-classical T cell activation independent of TCR specificity, T cell costimulation, or antigen presentation (figure 1). Seminal work by Kufer and colleagues demonstrated that reduction of the antibody structure to their minimal binding domains with a linker sequence, engineering two single-chain antibodies of differing specificity in tandem, could engage CD4+ and CD8+ T cells with high potency, leading to redirected cancer cell lysis at low effector to target ratios.10 Based on these principles, efforts led by Baeuerle and colleagues led to the development of blinatumomab, a CD19xCD3 TCE, initially approved by the FDA for relapsed/refractory B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia.11

Since then, several TCEs have gained FDA and EMA approval, primarily in hematologic malignancies, except for two approvals in solid tumors: (1) tebentafusp, a gp100 TCRxCD3 TCE for uveal melanoma (approved by both FDA and EMA); and (2) tarlatamab, a delta-like ligand 3 (DLL3)xCD3 TCE for small cell lung cancer (currently approved by FDA only).12 13 Studies of tebentafusp have shown a rapid increase in CD8+ T cell infiltration (measured by CD8 immunohistochemistry (IHC) of tumor biopsies obtained 3–17 days after treatment initiation) with transcriptional profiles consistent with T cell activation (including interferon (IFN)-γ signaling), cytotoxicity, and antigen presentation.14 Therefore, CD3-targeted TCEs are capable of both localization and activation of effector T cells within the TME of solid tumors.

Common Toxicities of CD3-Targeted TCEs

As a class, the most common adverse event observed across CD3-targeted TCEs is cytokine release syndrome (CRS). Activation of immune effector cells can lead to release of a wide array of cytokines, including interleukin-6 (IL-6), IFN-γ, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, interleukin-1 (IL-1), and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor, among others.15 16 This cytokine cascade can lead to amplification via myeloid cells (including monocytes and macrophages), ultimately leading to increased vascular permeability with capillary leak syndrome and end organ dysfunction.

Studies in humanized mouse models of leukemia treated with chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells demonstrated that monocyte-derived IL-6 was a key driver of CRS and that the syndrome could be prevented by monocyte depletion or blockade of the IL-6 receptor with tocilizumab.17 Consistent with this putative mechanism, CRS clinically presents most commonly with fever and can range from mild (fever only, managed supportively) to severe, including hypotension requiring vasopressors and/or hypoxia requiring mechanical ventilation, in addition to other constitutional symptoms.18 19 Within the context of the systemic inflammatory response, sequelae from CRS can include disseminated intravascular coagulation and end-organ dysfunction including renal and liver failure. Management of CRS is reviewed extensively elsewhere, but usually includes supportive management with acetaminophen and intravenous fluids for low-grade cases, while more severe CRS may require medical management with corticosteroids, IL-6 pathway blockade (eg, tocilizumab), IL-1 blockade (eg, anakinra), or JAK1/2 inhibition in refractory cases (eg, ruxolitinib).18 19

Several mitigation strategies have been incorporated into clinical trials of TCEs, which are discussed throughout this review, though it should be noted that individual TCEs have unique safety profiles for which label guidance should be followed. These mitigation strategies include (1) step-dosing: low initial dose of TCE, with one or more intermediate doses before achieving target dose; (2) prophylactic medications including acetaminophen, corticosteroids, and anti-IL-6 biologics; (3) clinical monitoring, including hospitalization for initial doses of most TCEs, although certain TCEs are now being administered fully in the outpatient setting; and (4) patient education for hydration and strict precautions to present for emergency care.20 TCEs in development are continuing to be refined to improve the CRS profile.

In addition to CRS, some CD3-targeted TCEs may be associated with the development of immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), whose pathophysiology includes endothelial cell activation and blood-brain barrier disruption.20 There are notable differences in the mechanisms of ICANS versus CRS. Preclinical models have demonstrated that blockade of the IL-6 receptor failed to protect from ICANS, whereas IL-1 pathway blockade abrogated toxicity.17 ICANS can manifest with a wide range of neurologic symptoms, including delirium, handwriting changes, aphasia, encephalopathy, tremor, seizures, and less commonly, cerebral edema and coma.18 Neurologic assessment scores (CARTOX-10 or ICE score) are helpful in the grading and assessment of ICANS.18 Management of ICANS is also reviewed elsewhere but similarly involves medical management with corticosteroids (with tocilizumab typically added only if concurrent CRS is present), with close neurologic consultation and monitoring, including with brain imaging. A systematic review evaluating the prevalence of adverse events with TCEs (n=511 patients across 11 clinical trials) and CAR-T cells (n=55 patients across five clinical trials) identified a lower rate of neurologic adverse events with CD3-directed TCEs (5%) compared with CAR-T cells (39%), which may be due to the prolonged persistence, higher cytokine burden, and potential for greater endothelial and blood-brain barrier disruption with CAR-T cells.21

In rare cases, a positive feedback loop of pro-inflammatory cytokine production can lead to a syndrome resembling familial hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), with clinical features including fever, hepatosplenomegaly, hyperferritinemia, coagulopathy, liver dysfunction, multiorgan failure and hemophagocytosis.22 Treatments here also include high-dose corticosteroids and IL-1 pathway blockade (eg, anakinra), with other therapies under investigation extrapolated from familial HLH, including ruxolitinib (JAK inhibition), etoposide-based chemotherapy, and emapalumab (anti-IFN-γ).22

CD3-targeted TCEs in prostate cancer

Multiple cell surface protein targets are under active clinical investigation for CD3-targeted TCEs in prostate cancer (other targets in preclinical development are beyond the scope of this review and are covered elsewhere) (table 1).23 24 The relative expression of surface targets on tumor cells and normal host cells influences drug-specific toxicity profiles, as described in table 1. The most studied target for CD3 TCEs in prostate cancer has been prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA). The experience with early generations of CD3 TCEs has provided lessons for the advancement of subsequent TCEs into challenges in development, including: (1) anti-drug antibodies (ADAs); (2) high rates of toxicity; and (3) relatively short duration of clinical responses, which are summarized in table 2.

Prostate-specific membrane antigen

PSMA is a widely investigated and credentialed therapeutic target in prostate cancer, as PSMA-targeted radioligand therapy has a demonstrated overall survival benefit in patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC).25 26 PSMA is highly expressed on prostate tumor cells, with increasing expression as the cancer progresses to metastatic and castration-resistant disease states. However, PSMA also has widespread expression in non-neoplastic tissues, including salivary glands, gut, and kidney (table 1).

The first TCE to be evaluated in prostate cancer was pasotuxizumab (AMG 212/BAY 2010112), a PSMAxCD3 TCE evaluated in patients with mCRPC refractory to at least one taxane regimen and androgen receptor pathway inhibitor (ARPI).27 The drug was initially dosed subcutaneously (SC); however, all patients developed neutralizing ADAs, prompting a switch to continuous intravenous dosing.27 Notably, no patients receiving continuous intravenous dosing of pasotuxizumab developed ADAs.27 28 Subsequent analyses identified non-tolerant T cell epitopes within pasotuxizumab.28 Thus, it is likely that the trafficking through secondary lymphoid tissue occurring following SC delivery of a >20 kD protein combined with the presence of potentially immunogenic epitopes contributed to high rates of ADAs with the SC delivery. In total, among all patients treated with pasotuxizumab (SC and intravenous), confirmed 50% declines in serum PSA (PSA50 responses) were observed in 29% (9/31) of patients in the SC cohort and 19% of patients (3/16) in the intravenous cohort.27 Ultimately, the study was discontinued, and a maximum tolerated dose (MTD) for intravenous pasotuxizumab remains undetermined.27

Acapatamab (AMG 160) was the successor PSMAxCD3 TCE to pasotuxizumab.29 30 Acapatamab was designed as a half-life extended molecule to enable less frequent dosing intervals than the continuous intravenous dosing required by the short half-life of pasotuxizumab.30 In a study of 133 patients with refractory mCRPC, CRS was observed in nearly all patients (94.7% and 98.2% in dose exploration and dose expansion, respectively), and approximately 20% of patients experienced grade 3 or higher CRS.29 The objective response rate (ORR) among patients with measurable disease was 7.4%, with PSA50 responses observed in 30.4% of patients in dose expansion.29 Limited clinical durability was observed, with a radiographic progression-free survival (PFS) of 3.7 months.29 Notably, treatment-emergent ADAs were also observed in 55% of patients, leading to decreased serum drug exposure in 36% of patients during dose expansion.30 Based on these results, the development of acapatamab was discontinued.

In an alternative attempt to extend half-life, HPN424 was a “tri-specific” PSMAxCD3 TCE containing an anti-albumin domain.31 32 Modest clinical activity was observed, with a PSA50 response rate of 4.8% (3/63 patients), one radiographic partial response, and all grade CRS occurring in 63% of patients, and development was accordingly halted.31 Consistent findings have been observed with CC-1, a PSMAxCD3 IgG-format bispecific antibody with promising PSA responses but also all-grade CRS in 86% of patient.33 Similarly, voxalatamab (JNJ-63898081) was a PSMAxCD3 TCE evaluated for both intravenous and SC delivery.34 A total of 39 patients were treated, with transient declines in PSA but no radiographic responses observed.34 Finally, REGN4336 is an IgG4 PSMAxCD3 bispecific antibody under active investigation in a phase 1/2 trial; safety and efficacy data have not yet been released (NCT05125016).35

The evidence to date has prompted the question of why clinically meaningful initial responses appear to be followed by early relapses. One leading hypothesis is that excessive T cell activation with CD3 engagement led to activation-induced cell death (AICD) and/or T cell exhaustion. Multiple factors contribute to the degree of T cell activation generally, including CD3 affinity, TCR affinity for MHC molecules, TCR avidity (the accumulated strength of multiple affinities of non-covalent binding interactions), and degree of costimulatory signals. Overly high activation signal strength induces AICD, whereas weak signal strength leads to T cell anergy or absence of response. Moreover, repeated or chronic antigen presentation may lead to overstimulation of CD8+ T cells within the TME, impairing or exhausting the effector functions and proliferative capacity of responding antigen-specific CD8+ T cells.36 Thus, optimal T cell activation is critical for induction of appropriate antitumor T cell responses. The high rates of CRS observed with the first generation of CD3 TCEs linked to short duration of responses supported the hypothesis of T cell overactivation. Consistent with these observations, ex vivo data from patients treated with CD19xCD3 indicated that dose interruptions with treatment-free intervals improved T cell proliferation and cytotoxicity functions.37 Similarly, data with a BCMAxCD3 TCE used for the treatment of multiple myeloma demonstrated that the presence of T cell subsets with an exhausted phenotype was associated with resistance to the TCE (in addition to other previously described mechanisms of resistance including downregulation of surface target and MHC expression).38

To address these problems, the next generation PSMAxCD3 TCE AMG 340/TNB585 was developed.39 The agent was engineered with a low-affinity anti-CD3 arm and demonstrated killing of PSMA+ prostate tumor cells in co-culture with human T cells with minimal cytokine release.39 Efficacy was also demonstrated in patient-derived prostate tumors ex vivo with tumor regression in vivo.39 In the phase 1 trial of AMG 340, 42 patients with mCRPC who had received at least two prior lines of therapy were treated with the TCE monotherapy every 3 weeks.40 A lower rate of CRS (52%) was observed compared with the other PSMAxCD3 agents, and an MTD was not reached, in addition to no clinically significant ADAs being noted.40 However, minimal activity was observed, with no radiographic responses among 27 patients with measurable disease, and PSA50 responses observed in 4/41 patients (9.8%).40 Accordingly, the development of AMG 340 was discontinued.

An alternative approach to optimize efficacy and toxicity of PSMA-directed TCEs has been taken with JANX007, which incorporates a CD3 inhibitory peptide mask fused to the molecule through a protease-cleavable linker.41 The proposed mechanism of action is that tumor-resident proteases cleave the linker and expose the active CD3 only within the TME, thereby reducing systemic toxicity.41 A phase 1 trial (NCT05519449) of JANX007 is currently ongoing, and as of October 15, 2025, data from 109 patients had been reported in press release format, with an ORR of 30% (in 8 of 27 patients with measurable disease), with a median radiographic progression-free survival (rPFS) of 7.9–8.9 months (95% CI not provided).42 43 The specific rates of CRS have not yet been publicly disclosed.43 44 Another agent based on masking technology is VIR-5500, a “dual-masked” PSMAxCD3 TCE under evaluation in a phase 1 trial (NCT05997615). Data from 12 patients reported in a press release reported PSA50 responses in 58% (7/12 patients), with a 28% rate of CRS (grade ≤2).45 Data on durability or radiographic responses similarly have not yet been released.32 Since PSMA-targeted therapy has been shown to improve overall survival, PSMA continues to remain an attractive therapeutic target for TCE development.

Six-transmembrane epithelial antigen of the prostate 1

As target downregulation remains a major mechanism of resistance to CD3-targeted TCEs (observed preclinically in neuroendocrine prostate cancer xenografts with DLL3xCD346 and clinically in other malignancies such as acute lymphoblastic leukemia with CD19xCD3)47 and the number of PSMA-targeted therapies multiplies (including radioligand therapies and antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) in addition to TCEs), there has been strong interest in alternative tumor targets. STEAP1 (six transmembrane epithelial antigen of the prostate 1) is strongly expressed on the surface of prostate cancer cells throughout the treatment continuum in the hormone-sensitive and castrate-resistant settings, including in prostate, lymph node, bone, and liver metastases, providing a potential advantage over PSMA, which is expressed at relatively lower levels in liver metastases (table 1). Xaluritamig is an STEAP1xCD3 TCE designed in a “2+1” format, containing two fragment antigen-binding (Fab) regions for STEAP1 for one single-chain variable fragment targeting CD3, with an engineered effectorless Fc domain to extend serum half-life and abrogate antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity. This 2:1 stoichiometry is intended to improve the selectivity of the TCE by reducing the avidity of T cell binding.48

In the first-in-human trial of xaluritamig, 97 patients with mCRPC (primarily taxane pretreated) were treated, with an initial PSA50 response rate of 49% and ORR of 24% reported at all dose levels.49 An updated report including a target dose level of 1.5 mg every two weeks (Q2W) (n=29 patients) reported a PSA50 response rate of 45% and ORR of 26%.50 The most common toxicity with xaluritamig was CRS, occurring in 78% of patients, of which approximately half were grade 2.49 50

A unique toxicity to xaluritamig is the development of musculoskeletal inflammation, manifesting most commonly as myalgia (61% total, 30%≥grade 3) and arthralgia (22% total, 3%≥grade 3).50 In the initial report, 19% of patients discontinued treatment due to toxicity, with the most common reasons being CRS and musculoskeletal events, classified on study as myalgia and arthralgia.49 These musculoskeletal inflammation events have been characterized by normal levels of serum creatine kinase (arguing against classical myositis) and are typically responsive to management with corticosteroids, allowing treatment continuation in most patients.49 50 The mechanism of this toxicity remains unclear and may be related to localized inflammation secondary to low levels of STEAP1 expression within connective tissue cells (ie, fibroblasts and adipocyte precursors) in the skeletal muscle, consistent with a myofasciitis syndrome (table 1). The high rate of this toxicity lends urgency to understanding its biological mechanisms to identify biomarkers for patient selection and to identify rational immunosuppressive strategies. On-treatment biopsies of toxicity-involved tissues are essential to developing this understanding.

Xaluritamig has now advanced into a phase 3 clinical trial (NCT06691984) in patients with progressive mCRPC following treatment with an ARPI and taxane-based chemotherapy, and studies are ongoing in the localized setting with neoadjuvant xaluritamig in patients with high-risk prostate cancer prior to radical prostatectomy (NCT06613100), as well as in biochemically recurrent prostate cancer (NCT06555796).

Kallikrein-2

Kallikrein-2 (KLK2) is a trypsin-like serine protease secreted by prostate epithelial cells.51 A member of the kallikrein family, KLK2 shares 80% amino acid homology with PSA (also known as KLK3).51 KLK2 is regulated by androgen receptor signaling, and its expression has been shown to be highly prostate-specific, with minimal expression in other normal tissues.51 KLK2 is strongly and homogeneously expressed in localized prostate cancer and metastatic castration-sensitive prostate cancer (mCSPC).51 Heterogeneous expression is observed in mCRPC, with lower KLK2 expression in visceral metastasis than in bone or lymph node.51

Pasritamig (JNJ-78278343) is a KLK2xCD3 TCE that has been investigated in a phase 1 clinical trial (NCT04898634).52 Notably, doses were administered fully outpatient, in contrast to most TCEs that require hospitalization for initial doses, and a recommended phase 2 dosing was less frequent than other TCEs, delivered every 6 weeks following two once-weekly step-up doses.52 A total of 174 patients were treated in the phase 1 clinical trial.47 In the 45 patients treated at the recommended phase 2 dose evaluated for safety, a low rate of CRS was observed (8.9%), with all events grade 1 in severity.52 No ICANS was observed in this dose level.52 Potential hypotheses to explain this low rate of systemic toxicity include (1) selective expression of KLK2; (2) reduced frequency of dosing; (3) specific characteristics of the CD3-binding moiety (eg, binding affinity).

With respect to the efficacy, the PSA50 response rate in the n=33 evaluable patients treated at the recommended phase 2 dose was 42.4%, and an ORR of 0% (although only n=7 patients in this cohort harbored measurable disease).52 The median rPFS was 7.85 months (95% CI 2.89 to not estimable) in a heavily pretreated patient with mCRPC population.52 Based on these results, two randomized phase III clinical trials of pasritamig have now been launched in patients with mCRPC as monotherapy (NCT07164443) and in combination with docetaxel (NCT07225946).

Transmembrane protein with epidermal growth factor-like and 2 follistatin-like domains 2

Transmembrane protein with epidermal growth factor-like and 2 follistatin-like domains 2 (TMEFF2) is a transmembrane cell surface protein with a diverse range of functions, including tumor suppressor function in prostate cancer. TMEFF2 expression is under androgen regulation and is highly expressed in tumors across prostate cancer subtypes, with prominence in high-grade tumors including tumors with features of neuroendocrine histology suggesting non-androgen-dependent expression.53 54 TMEFF2 is detectable in prostate cancer, brain parenchyma and neural development tissue (fetal brain and spinal cord).

JNJ-70218902 (JNJ-902) is a TMEFF2xCD3 TCE that was evaluated in a first-in-human, open-label, multicenter, phase 1 study in 82 patients with mCRPC. The PSA50 response rate was 12.2% across dosing cohorts, with an ORR of 15.2%.55 46 participants (56.1%) experienced a grade ≥3 treatment-emergent adverse event (TEAE), and 4 participants (4.9%) experienced a CRS event.55 Interestingly, 56.1% patients experienced a toxicity related to the nervous system, not all necessarily falling within the constellation of ICANS symptoms.55 The unique toxicity profile may suggest on-target off-tumor (OTOT) interaction of the TCE with endogenously expressed drug antigen expressed within the central nervous system. Drug immunogenicity was low, with 3.8% of patients developing ADAs, and their presence did not appear to impact drug pharmacokinetics.55 Pretreatment assessment of TMEFF2 was performed with IHC on archival tissue, but due to limited sample sizes and dose escalations, no correlation could be assessed between TMEFF2 expression and drug activity.55 JNJ-902 was not advanced into further clinical development.

Delta-like ligand 3

DLL3 is a non-canonical Notch ligand expressed on the surface of high-grade neuroendocrine tumors, including most neuroendocrine prostate cancers (NEPC).56 DLL3 expression in non-neoplastic tissue is restricted to the developing nervous system during embryonic stages, with limited to no expression in adult tissue. Tarlatamab is a DLL3xCD3 TCE that is FDA-approved for the treatment of small cell lung cancer on the basis of the DeLLphi-301 trial.13 Notably, this trial evaluated tarlatamab dosing of 10 mg and 100 mg every 2 weeks, finding comparable clinical activity, with a lower rate of grade 3 CRS in the lower dose.13 Thus, 10 mg every 2 weeks was selected as the dose for the labeled indication.

The DeLLpro-300 trial (NCT04702737) evaluating tarlatamab in patients with de novo or treatment-emergent NEPC preceded the publication of the DELLphi-301 data, and therefore evaluated a dosing schedule of 100 mg every 2 weeks.57 Of note, patients were enrolled on this study on the basis of either histologic diagnosis of NEPC or genomic or immunohistochemical criteria for aggressive variant prostate carcinoma (alterations in at least two of three tumor suppressors (PTEN, TP53, and/or RB1)).58 59 The ORR in all patients was 10.5% with median PFS of 1.9 months; however, among patients with DLL3 tumor positivity by IHC, the ORR was 22.2%, with median PFS of 3.75 months.57 CRS was observed in 65% of patients and was grade 1 or 2 in all cases except for one.57 Tarlatamab is being used off-label for patients with DLL3+ NEPC.

CD28-targeted TCES in prostate cancer

CD28 is a homodimeric, signal-transducing protein that is constitutively expressed on T cells and serves as a costimulatory receptor for the TCR-CD3 complex (figure 1). Seminal work following initial identification and characterization of CD28 established its essential role in T cell activation.60 61 Activation of the TCR-CD3 complex by recognition of antigen presented by MHC through the TCR (Signal 1) in the absence of costimulation leads to a state of T cell anergy. This anergy is a particular challenge in most solid tumors, whose TMEs lack expression of positive costimulatory molecules (eg, CD80 (B7-1) and CD86 (B7-2)).62 Furthermore, CD28 activation alone via costimulatory ligands or agonists (Signal 2) in the absence of TCR-CD3 signaling does not activate T cells. Thus, CD28 agonism may be a targeted approach to enhance existing TCR-CD3 complex signaling. This reliance on endogenous Signal 1 represents a distinct mechanism from CD3-targeted TCEs, which deliver an artificial Signal 1 through CD3 engagement (figure 2).

Nezastomig (REGN5678) is a PSMAxCD28 TCE that was the first CD28-targeted TCE to be evaluated in patients. Preclinical data were consistent with the proposed mechanism of action, demonstrating that PSMAxCD28 monotherapy alone demonstrated minimal antitumor activity in a humanized mouse model (MC38/hPSMA tumor cells implanted subcutaneously hCD3/hCD28/hPSMA), but displayed potent activity when combined with anti-PD-1.63 64 These findings are consistent with PD-1’s function in suppressing T cell activation via dephosphorylation of CD28 via Shp2 phosphatase.65 Indeed, in vitro experiments showed that anti-PD-1 promoted CD28 localization at the immune synapse.63 T cell depletion experiments demonstrated that this activity was dependent on CD8+ T cells.63 64 Furthermore, mice previously treated with nezastomig that cleared tumors and were subsequently rechallenged with implanted tumor were able to successfully reject these tumors, confirming the presence of an immunological memory antitumor response.63

Based on these data, a phase 1/2 trial (NCT03972657) of nezastomig combined with cemiplimab (anti-PD-1) was launched in patients with mCRPC refractory to at least two prior lines of treatment.66,68 Safety and efficacy data have been reported for n=78 patients treated with this combination.68 Consistent with the distinct mechanism of action from CD3-targeted TCEs, a low rate of CRS was observed (13%, with all but one event being grade 1).68 A distinct pattern of toxicities was observed, more consistent with immune-related adverse events associated with anti-PD-1, with grade 3 or higher events occurring in 14% of patients, with the most common being neurologic toxicity in 9% of patients.68 Two fatal events were reported, including a case of HLH and immune-mediated hepatitis similar to an HLH-like syndrome, consistent with systemic inflammation arising from CD28 agonism.68 Notably, severe immune-mediated toxicities all occurred after the addition of anti-PD-1, potentially suggesting enhancement of anti-PD-1-driven immunotoxicities, although the mechanisms of toxicity remain undefined at this time.68

A dose of 30 mg of nezastomig was identified as the minimal clinically active dose in combination with cemiplimab, with a PSA50 response rate of 25%.68 A post hoc overall survival analysis suggested a trend toward improved survival at active dose levels of nezastomig plus cemiplimab (≥30 mg) compared with lower dose levels.68 Most strikingly, a tight association between efficacy and toxicity was observed, with severe immune-mediated toxicities occurring almost exclusively in patients with evidence of antitumor response as measured by PSA decline.68

This study is currently ongoing, with further monotherapy dose exploration underway and evaluation in combination with low-dose anti-PD-1 as well as CD3 TCEs.35 67 Concurrently, a presurgical clinical trial (NCT06085664) is evaluating nezastomig in patients with high-risk localized prostate cancer followed by radical prostatectomy to understand mechanisms of response and resistance within the prostate TME to identify predictive biomarkers for patient selection and potential additional rational combinations to improve efficacy.69 Finally, a single-center clinical trial (NCT06826768) of nezastomig with full-dose cemiplimab every 3 weeks following a weekly lead-in period (in contrast to the multicenter study that is currently evaluating nezastomig monotherapy weekly with the addition of low-dose cemiplimab on disease progression) has been launched incorporating aggressive toxicity monitoring and mitigation strategies with longitudinal blood and tissue sampling from tumors and toxicity-involved sites aiming to optimize the risk and benefit with this therapy.

Other CD28-targeted TCEs are also under study for prostate cancer. JNJ-87189401 is another PSMAxCD28 TCE that is currently being evaluated in combination with the KLK2xCD3 TCE described above (JNJ-78278343) (NCT06095089).70 XmAb808 is a B7-H3xCD28 TCE that showed preclinical activity when combined with anti-PD-1 or with an EpCAMxCD3 TCE (NCT05585034).71 72 Peer-reviewed data from patients with mCRPC have not yet been released; however, it was reported that two dose-limiting toxicities with the combination of XmAb808 plus pembrolizumab were observed (infusion-related reaction and immune-related hepatitis with grade 4 liver enzyme elevation), leading to discontinuation of clinical development of this combination.73

Gamma-Delta T cell Targeted TCEs in Prostate Cancer

TCEs under development have chiefly targeted classical T cells, whose TCRs are comprised of a heterodimer consisting of an alpha chain and a beta chain. Gamma delta (γδ) T cells are a minority subset of T cells that bridge innate and adaptive immunity through (1) recognition of phosphoantigens by the γδ TCR; (2) acquisition of professional APC function with uptake of tumor-associated antigens via pinocytosis, phagocytosis, or trogocytosis; and (3) subsequent antigen cross-presentation to classical CD4+ and CD8+ αβ T cells.74 LAVA-1207 is a PSMAxVδ2 TCE that was evaluated in a phase 1/2 clinical trial (NCT05369000) in patients with mCRPC.75 76 Clinical responses were observed as measured by PSA declines; however, development of LAVA-1207 in mCRPC was discontinued, reportedly due to failure to reach prespecified efficacy benchmarks, though data have not yet been publicly released.

Path forward

TCEs represent a highly promising therapeutic modality for patients with mCRPC, and TCE monotherapies are advancing into phase 3 clinical trials with encouraging activity, including xaluritamig (STEAP1xCD3, NCT06691984) and pasritamig (KLK2xCD3). However, the greatest potential for these agents lies in combination approaches with non-overlapping mechanisms of action, which remain incompletely understood. Critical biological questions remain, including: (1) Does antitumor efficacy depend on the pre-existing effector T cell repertoire within the tumor or in the circulating bloodstream? (2) How do mechanisms of response and resistance vary across distinct organ sites, such as lymph node versus bone (the most common site of prostate cancer metastasis)? (3) Are mechanisms of efficacy and toxicity identical or do distinctions exist that can be exploited to decouple antitumor response from adverse reactions? Answering these questions will be critical to identify the optimal biomarkers for patient selection and define rational combination strategies that can be delivered safely. Doing so will require applying the lessons learnt to date to design clinical trials that efficiently evaluate safety, efficacy, and biological activity (figure 3).

Lessons learnt

Dose and schedule

Phase 1 clinical trials have historically sought to identify the “MTD” and select a recommended phase 2 dose that is one or two dose levels below the MTD. This approach is acceptable for chemotherapy-based strategies, in which a correlation exists between drug dose, host toxicity, and cell killing. By contrast, increasing doses of immunotherapy to maximally tolerated levels do not necessarily lead to concomitant increase in efficacy, likely due to AICD and/or T cell exhaustion, as well as premature therapy discontinuation due to autoimmune host toxicity. As described earlier in this review, clinical administration strategies can address this issue. The experience with tarlatamab (DLL3xCD3) indicated that a 10-fold lower dose (10 mg vs 100 mg) was associated with comparable clinical efficacy but lower rates of severe CRS.13 Similarly, less frequent dosing intervals have been explored with CD3-targeted TCEs and are suggestive of improved efficacy.37

Binding affinity and avidity

The first generation of TCEs aimed to maximize binding strength to CD3, thereby leading to maximal T cell activation. These agents were therefore associated with high rates of CRS and additionally increased the risk of early clinical relapse due to AICD and/or T cell exhaustion. The PSMAxCD3 TCE AMG 340/TNB585 demonstrated that decreasing CD3 affinity could markedly lower rates of CRS without sacrificing antitumor efficacy in preclinical models, although it should be noted that this did not necessarily translate into improved clinical efficacy, suggesting that the optimal CD3 affinity had not been achieved. Alternative approaches include altering the stoichiometry of binding, such as in xaluritamig (AMG509, STEAP1xCD3), whose 2+1 design binds two STEAP1 molecules on tumor cells, for each CD3 molecule on T cells. Masking technology (described above with VIR-5818) is a novel approach in developmental therapeutics, which is now being applied to TCE under investigation. This strategy may prove efficacious at mitigating OTOT and may become a mainstay for future TCE drug development.

Early detection and intervention in toxicities

Dose-limiting toxicities dictate the fate of novel therapeutics in phase 1 clinical trials. Severe toxicities typically have early signs and symptoms that can prompt early intervention prior to the development of grade 3 or higher adverse events that can lead to termination of drug development. This problem is of great urgency in the drugs that are advancing into phase 3 clinical trials and intended to be used in broad community practice beyond academic centers. For example, nearly 20% of patients treated with xaluritamig (STEAP1xCD3) discontinued treatment due to toxicities in the phase 1 trial.49 Incorporating clinical strategies for early recognition and intervention in toxicities is essential, and tools are being developed to facilitate timely detection of toxicities. One example is the ApricityCare service, which provides patients access to digital health tools (SMS, telephone, smartphone application) to report symptoms to a centralized nurse for further triage and management. The service is currently being tested in multiple clinical trials for both standard of care and investigational cancer therapies (NCT06693687; NCT04571398; NCT05812274).77 Wearable devices such as TempTraq for continuous temperature monitoring have demonstrated feasibility for early detection of febrile adverse events, which may further support outpatient dosing of TCEs with elevated risk of CRS.78

There is a further need to elucidate mechanisms of these toxicities through longitudinal evaluation of toxicity-involved sites in the phase 1 trials to thread the needle of efficacy and toxicity. This is the approach being taken with nezastomig (PSMAxCD28) and cemiplimab (anti-PD-1), which demonstrated a close link between efficacy and toxicity (NCT06826768). To date, there is no accepted pre-medication strategy or prophylaxis standard embedded within protocols, with some studies not employing any immunosuppressive therapy to others mandating glucocorticoids and even biologics. Data from a retrospective study with teclistamab (BCMAxCD3) demonstrated no difference in objective response rate or PFS in patients managed with dexamethasone versus tocilizumab for CRS, suggesting that short course corticosteroids did not impair antitumor efficacy and supporting the use of corticosteroid pre-medications.79 However, it is important to note that there have not been direct comparisons of steroid-based versus non-steroid-based approaches in TCEs for prostate cancer, and that repeated high-dose or prolonged corticosteroid exposure may impair antitumor efficacy. Defining the correct balance of immunosuppression with drug dosing to allow for proper pharmacokinetics without blunting the effector T cell function is critical.

Clinical trial design and biomarker development

Given that a subset of patients is expected to respond to therapy, a “one-size-fits-all” approach to therapy will be inadequate for most patients. There is a major unmet need to “de-risk” therapy development along the continuum from first-in-human to registrational studies. This can be achieved in early phase clinical trials through acquisition of tumor biopsies both at the pretreatment time point (to identify prognostic and predictive biomarkers) as well as at early on-treatment time point (to directly evaluate pharmacodynamic activity and assess for candidate mechanisms of response and resistance) (figure 3). To control costs, initial pharmacodynamic assessments should incorporate lower cost, rapid turnaround assays that can be performed in all evaluable patients, reserving more expensive, labor-intensive studies in selected groups of patients.

Single tumor-specific biomarkers are likely to be insufficient to predict response—well-validated biomarkers such as microsatellite instability and high TMB only predict for responses to immune checkpoint therapies in a fraction of patients with mCRPC (among the 2–3% of patients who harbor them to begin with).2 80 81 Furthermore, the degree of tumor target expression required for response to TCEs remains incompletely understood. Quantitative whole body68 Ga-PSMA-11 PET/CT imaging in the phase 1 trial of acapatamab (PSMAxCD3) indicated that higher maximum standardized uptake values were associated with PSA50 response, though responses were observed in patients with relatively low PSMA expression, and multiple patients with high PSMA expression did not respond.29 Indeed, immunohistochemistry studies from pretreatment biopsies in patients treated with xaluritamig (STEAP1xCD3) demonstrated that neither STEAP1 IHC intensity nor the percentage of STEAP1-positive tumor cells correlated with response to xaluritamig, although greater than 95% of biopsies assessed contained at least 1% STEAP1-positive tumor cells, suggesting that a minimum level of expression is required to induce response.82

Finally, tissue-based biomarkers will need to ultimately inform blood-based biomarkers that can be sampled more frequently and can provide a more dynamic assessment of markers of treatment response and resistance than tumor biopsies, which capture a single point in time. A recent analysis of longitudinal peripheral blood samples in patients treated with pasritamig (KLK2xCD3) identified a higher frequency of TCF1+CD8+ T cells (harboring a progenitor, “stem-like” phenotype previously associated with response to anti-PD-1 therapy)83 in patients responding to therapy.84 Ultimately, combinatorial biomarkers incorporating elements of the tumor epithelial, immune (including specific T cell subsets and T cell receptor clonality), and stromal compartments, ideally accessible in blood, may assist in optimally selecting patients for therapy. 80 81 Techniques such as assays of extracellular vesicles—organized packages of nucleic acids, lipids, and proteins—may provide a path forward for longitudinal, non-invasive, combinatorial biomarkers.

Intelligent clinical trial design will incorporate rapid pharmacodynamic and biomarker readouts into dose selection decisions to ensure that the optimal biological dose is selected, not merely the MTD. In one example, SD-101 (a Toll-like receptor 9 [TLR9] agonist) was studied in combination with nivolumab (anti-PD-1) for patients with uveal melanoma.85 Whereas the MTD was identified at 10 mg, the optimal biological dose, measured by favorable changes within the TME, was fivefold lower, at 2 mg, which was chosen as the recommended phase 2 dose (RP2D).85 Novel statistical designs are taking advantage of the ability to rapidly integrate information on safety, efficacy, and biological changes as assays for gene expression profiling and spatial proteomics such as multiplex immunofluorescence are becoming faster and cheaper. These include derivatives of Bayesian designs such as the Bayesian optimal interval design with backfill (BF-BOIN) that enables backfilling patients to doses that are cleared for safety during dose escalation, thereby generating additional information on safety, tolerability, and preliminary activity at doses below the MTD.86

As we develop novel classes of therapies with unique mechanisms of action, standard clinical endpoints may not be sufficient to properly characterize antitumor activity, or in the case of TCEs, immune activation and effector cell activity. For example, a high proportion of patients with metastatic prostate cancer have bone-predominant disease, which is not measurable by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST)—therefore, time-based endpoints such as disease control rate at 6 months are likely to be a more meaningful endpoint than ORR in patients with measurable disease, which biases the population. Furthermore, novel clinical endpoints will likely arise in due time and should be welcomed as an advancement in developmental therapeutics and trial design. These strategies align with the priorities laid out by FDA’s Project Optimus.

Rational combinations

Rational combination studies with TCEs are underway in prostate cancer. A promising approach is via the combination of CD3-targeted and CD28-targeted TCEs, which are currently being pursued in two different clinical trials with PSMAxCD3 (REGN4336) plus PSMAxCD28 (REGN5678/nezastomig)35 (NCT05125016) and with KLK2xCD3 (JNJ-78278343) plus PSMAxCD28 (JNJ-87189401) (NCT06095089).70 The hypothesis behind this approach is that the CD3 TCE will provide Signal 1, which will then be enhanced through the Signal 2 delivered by the costimulatory CD28 TCE. Data from preclinical models demonstrated that this approach enhances the localization of CD28 within the immune synapse and leads to improved antitumor efficacy compared with either TCE alone.64

Dual-targeting of independent tumor targets represents a potential strategy to induce deeper clinical responses.87 As evidence indicates that PSMA and STEAP1 may be independently expressed on certain tumor cells, a clinical trial combining radioligand therapy with 177Lu-PSMA-617 and xaluritamig (STEAP1xCD3) is under development in patients with mCRPC. Patients will be treated with two doses of 177Lu-PSMA-617 as an induction therapy followed by xaluritamig as consolidation in those patients who demonstrate a treatment response to 177Lu-PSMA-617.75 76 It is hypothesized that a short course induction treatment with 177Lu-PSMA-617 will induce pro-inflammatory changes in the immune TME, potentially enhancing the effectiveness of subsequent consolidation immunotherapy with xaluritamig. This hypothesis is supported by recent evidence demonstrating higher 12 month PSA PFS in patients treated with 177Lu-PSMA-617 plus combined ipilimumab and nivolumab compared with 177Lu-PSMA-617 alone.88 Furthermore, recent studies have shown that metastasis-directed radiotherapy can enhance systemic antitumor immunity, with durable clinical responses associated with modulation of the TCR repertoire, upregulation of Th1-polarizing cytokines, and CD8+ T cell proliferation.89 90 A similar mechanism may promote additive efficacy between the radioligand and TCE. As TCEs, CAR-T cells, radioligands, and ADCs continue to advance in therapeutic development, a key outstanding question is how best to select and combine these agents. TCEs offer potent, off-the-shelf immune activity, and with improving toxicity profiles offer improved ease of combinations, although durability has been a challenge, particularly with CD3-targeted TCEs. CAR-T cells, by contrast, can offer potential long-term control, but are expensive and patient-specific and carry higher risk of severe CRS and ICANS, as well as have challenges in trafficking into the solid TME. ADCs offer scalable manufacturing for targeted cytotoxic payload delivery (and may be best suited for highly proliferative tumors), but their activity is constrained by antigen internalization, payload resistance, and off-tumor toxicity and may offer less durable responses than immunotherapy-based approaches. Radioligands offer predictable pharmacokinetics for targeted radiation delivery but depend on adequate antigen density and carry cumulative marrow and organ radiation risks.

Ultimately, defining the optimal approaches for rational combinations will require an understanding of the mechanisms of response and resistance to TCEs within the TME, for which matched tumor biopsies on clinical trials will be essential. Beyond targeting of other T cells, other components of the TME including immune cells (eg, macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells) and non-immune cells (including stromal elements) also offer opportunities for rational combination approaches. Furthermore, given the known differences in the immune microenvironments across organ sites (eg, between primary prostate, bone, and soft tissue sites such as lymph node), representative sampling will be critical.3 6 91

Conclusions

TCEs represent a highly promising therapeutic modality for patients with prostate cancer, and a rich opportunity to build on the lessons learnt from immunotherapy in the past. CD3-targeted TCEs are the most advanced in clinical development, with agents in phase 3 clinical trials, and early phase studies with CD28-targeted TCEs are underway. The next steps for these agents will require clinical trials to facilitate a biological understanding of mechanisms of efficacy and toxicity to inform: (1) development of predictive biomarkers for patient selection; (2) rational combination strategies; and (3) targeted treatments for toxicity management, ultimately delivering broad clinical benefit for patients with lethal prostate cancer.
