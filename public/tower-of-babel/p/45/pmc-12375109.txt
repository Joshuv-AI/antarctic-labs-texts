Knowledge on HIV postexposure prophylaxis and associated factors among healthcare workers in Kigoma region, Tanzania
Authors: Zekky Mussolini Lyatula, Theresia A Ottaru, Hussein H Mwanga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHIV infection remains a major public health challenge globally. Healthcare workers (HCWs) face an increased risk of occupational exposure to HIV through their routine duties. Adequate knowledge on HIV postexposure prophylaxis (PEP) is imperative for preventing HIV infection following exposure to blood and body fluids. However, few studies have examined the level of knowledge on HIV PEP and the associated factors among this at-risk population of HCWs in Tanzania. The aim of this study was to assess the level of knowledge on HIV PEP and associated factors among HCWs in Kigoma Region, Tanzania. A cross-sectional study was conducted among HCWs at Kigoma Regional Hospital, Kibondo District Hospital and Kasulu Town Council Hospital from November to December 2022. Data was collected using interviewer-administered questionnaire. A total of nine questions were used to assess knowledge on HIV PEP. The total score was calculated by summing the correct responses, with a maximum possible score of 17. Knowledge of HIV PEP was classified as inadequate (score < 11, moderate (score 12–14), and adequate (score > 14). Bivariate analysis and multivariable logistic regression models were used to assess the association between the level of knowledge on HIV PEP (inadequate/moderate (score ≤ 14) vs. adequate (score > 14) and the independent variables. Data analysis was performed using Statistical Package for Social Scientists version 23.0 and p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Out of 377 study participants [female (200, 53.1%), mean age of 38.7 ± 9.1 years, nurse 129 (34.2)] the majority 234 (62.1%) had adequate knowledge on HIV PEP. HCWs from Kasulu Town Council Hospital (AOR = 1.4; 95% CI: 1.02–8.73) and those from facilities with HIV PEP services (AOR = 3.9; 95% CI: 2.41–6.29) had significant higher odds of having adequate knowledge on HIV PEP. A sizeable proportion of study participants had adequate knowledge on HIV PEP. Working in Kasulu Town Council Hospital and presence HIV PEP services within the facility were associated with adequate knowledge on HIV PEP. Healthcare authorities in the Kigoma region are advised to strengthen the implementation of formal HIV PEP training, timely access to HIV PEP service, and dissemination of updated information education communication materials alongside protocol guidelines to improve the knowledge of HCWs on HIV PEP in all healthcare facilities.Keywords: Human immuno-deficiency virus, Healthcare workers, Postexposure prophylaxisSubject terms: Occupational health, Health occupations

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) are of public health concern. Globally, 39.9 million [36.1–44.6 million] people were living with HIV at the end of 20231. An estimated 0.6% [0.6–0.7%] of adults aged 15–49 years worldwide are living with HIV1. African Region remains most severely affected, with one in every 30 adults (3.4%) living with HIV and accounting for more than two-thirds of the people living with HIV worldwide1.

Healthcare workers (HCWs) are at higher risk of being infected with HIV as per the nature of their duties2. In developing countries, approximately 1 to 10 HCWs are prone occupational exposure to blood and body fluids in the healthcare industry3. Globally, it is approximated that 4.4% of all HIV infections among HCWs are related to occupational exposure to blood and body fluids4. Research conducted among HCWs at Tumbi and Dodoma hospitals in Tanzania, reported that the proportion of occupational exposure to blood and body fluid among HCWs is 47.9%5.

HIV PEP is a short course of antiretroviral therapy (ART) that is administered as early as possible following exposure to blood and body fluids to minimize the risk of HIV infection. When HIV PEP is utilized properly and ideally initiated within two hours of exposure and no later than 72 h, the risk of acquiring HIV can be reduced by 81%2. A survey conducted in Mbeya region among HCWs on PEP following occupational exposure to HIV found that 62.2% had inadequate knowledge on HIV PEP4. Factors such as lack of formal training, limited access to HIV PEP services and lack protocol guidelines in place are among the leading cause to inadequate knowledge on HIV PEP among HCWs5–7. Insufficient knowledge on HIV PEP may hinder compliance, especially regarding the proper use of ART in HIV PEP management among HCWs.

A lack of regular PEP services, inadequate support for HCWs in reporting incidents of exposure to infectious hazards in the workplace, as well as limited knowledge and poor attitudes toward PEP, increase the risk of infection among HCWs5. These challenges can be addressed through comprehensive PEP training and effective implementation of PEP guidelines in the workplace4,8. A study conducted in Ethiopia revealed delays in PEP initiation, low utilization, and incomplete adherence to prescribed drugs use, despite adequate knowledge and a favourable attitude toward HIV PEP9. Work environment and behavioural characteristics of HCWs did not show a significant relationship with their knowledge of HIV PEP9.

Despite the high prevalence of occupational exposure to blood and body fluids in the healthcare setting, knowledge on HIV PEP remains inadequate globally, including Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA)10. In Tanzania, there is limited data on the level of knowledge on HIV PEP among HCWs in rural areas such as Kigoma Region. Importantly, the factors associated with knowledge of HIV PEP remain poorly understood. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the level of knowledge on HIV PEP and its associated factors among HCWs in Kigoma Region, Tanzania.

Methods

Study design and setting

An analytical cross-sectional study was conducted among HCWs at Kigoma Regional Hospital, Kibondo District Hospital and Kasulu Town Council Hospital. These three hospitals were purposely selected because they are the largest in the region, receiving a high volume of patients and provide specialised services. Additionally, these facilities represent facilities at the regional and district levels. Kigoma Regional Hospital serves all the districts in Kigoma region and beyond catchment areas, handling cases such as road traffic accidents and complicated obstetric emergency cases requiring surgical consultation.

Kibondo District Hospital was selected because it serves as a district referral hospital, offering emergency obstetric and newborn care and other complicated emergency cases. It serves a population of approximately 261,331 but also serves a nearby Nduta refugee camp. Kasulu Town Council Hospital provides emergency obstetric and newborn care and manages referrals from various health centers. It serves a population of approximately 234,452 including refugees in the Nyarugusu refugee camp.

Study population

The study population consisted of 426 HCWs from Kigoma Regional Hospital (200), Kibondo District Hospital (129), and Kasulu Town Council Hospital (97) who were either directly or indirectly in frequent contact with patients on daily duties. The composition of HCWs included medical specialists, medical doctors, pharmacists, assistant medical doctors, clinical officers, laboratory technologists, radiographers, assistant health laboratory technologists, nursing officers, nurses, assistant nursing officers, health laboratory scientists and health attendants.

HCWs in frequent contact with patients upon execution of their duties were included, and HCWs on holiday, study leave and those with long sick leave were excluded.

Sample size

The sample size was determined by using Cochran’s equation, and the anticipated proportion was determined with a previous study proportion of 62.2% of HIV PEP knowledge among HCWs based on a study performed in Mbeya10 with a 1.96 value of 95% confidence interval and 0.05 tolerable error. The sample size was 362 with a 10% nonresponse rate, yielding a total of 398. A 10% nonresponse rate was anticipated from factors such as refusal and unwillingness to participate8.

Sampling strategy

Due to the small size of the study population, all HCWs in the selected hospitals (n = 426) were approached to participate in the study.

Data collection

Data were collected through an interviewer-administered questionnaire from November to December 2022. The questionnaire was based on research objectives, and the respondents were HCWs at Kigoma Regional Hospital, Kibondo District Hospital and Kasulu Town Council Hospital. The English version of the questionnaire was developed based on published studies on factors associated with knowledge on HIV PEP among HCWs in similar settings 5,6,11. The questionnaire was translated to Swahili by a professional translator (linguistic expert) and then back-translated by a different translator. Subsequently, the study team, consisting of native Swahili speakers and subject matter experts, reviewed and refined the translation to ensure validity and reliability. The questionnaire was administered to HCWs in Swahili.

A total of nine questions were used to assess the main outcome variable i.e., knowledge on HIV PEP. Of these, five questions had a maximum score of 1 and a minimum score of 0. For the remaining four questions, one had a maximum score of 2, two had a maximum score of 3, and one had a maximum score of 4. The total score was calculated by summing the correct responses, with a maximum possible score of 17. Knowledge of HIV PEP was classified as inadequate for scores below 11, moderate for scores between 12 and 14, and adequate for scores between 15 and 17. Independent variables included sociodemographic factors such as age, sex, education level, job experience, and job carder category. Work-related factors included availability of HIV PEP service, PEP protocol guidelines, and protocol guidelines for reporting exposure. We also assessed factors related to the primary source of information to HIV PEP, such as training, and presence of focal person for reporting exposure, and information education communication materials12.

Pre-testing of the study questionnaire was carried out with five HCWs which were not part of the study population. This number was deemed sufficient given the smaller questionnaire size and the relatively homogeneous study population. The feedback obtained from pre-testing was used to refine the questionnaire in terms of clarity, comprehension, and the overall structure.

Data analysis

Data cleaning and analysis were performed using Statistical Package for Social Scientists (SPSS) version 23.0. The Chi-square test was used to compare the distribution of independent variables between participants with adequate and inadequate knowledge on HIV PEP. For bivariate and multivariable analyses, the three categories of the level of knowledge on HIV PEP (adequate, moderate and inadequate) were collapsed into two by combining the moderate and inadequate categories into a single category. Multivariable logistic regression analyses adjusted for sex, age, education level and job experience were used to assess the association between level of knowledge on HIV PEP (inadequate/moderate (score ≤ 14) vs. adequate (score > 14) and independent variables. Potential confounders included in the multivariable models were selected a priori based on biological plausibility or identified through bivariate testing if p-value was less than 0.2. The results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of study participants

A total of 377 HCWs based at Kigoma Regional Hospital (175, 46.4%), Kibondo District Hospital (119, 31.6%) and Kasulu Town Council Hospital (83, 22.0%) in the Kigoma region were interviewed (Table 1). The estimated sample size was 398 HCWs; however, only 377 were available at the time of data collection, and all approached HCWs agreed to participate in the study (response rate = 100%). The majority 234 (62.1%) had adequate knowledge on HIV PEP, 95% CI of the proportion (55.89%, 68.31%). The majority were female (200, 53.1%), and aged between 30 and 39 years 163 (43.2%), with a mean age of 38.7 ± 9.1 years that were normally distributed. In terms of education level, 159 (42.1%) were certificate holders, and 124 (32.9%) had diplomas. More than three quarters of the study population were married 300 (79.6%). More than one-third (129, 34.2%) were nurses professionals, followed by 97 medical doctors (25.7%). The majority of those health professionals who were included in the study had 4 to 6 years of experience working in the health facility.

Discussion

In this study on the level of knowledge of HIV PEP and its associated factors among HCW in Kigoma region, we found that the majority of study participants (62%) had adequate knowledge of HIV PEP. However, more than one-third of the participants had inadequate knowledge of HIV PEP. HCWs from Kasulu Town Council Hospital and those from facilities with HIV PEP services had significant higher odds of having adequate knowledge of HIV PEP.

The proportion of HCWs with adequate knowledge of HIV PEP reported in this study is consistent with a study conducted among HCWs at Gondar University Hospital in Ethiopia, where the majority (63.1%) of participants had adequate knowledge of HIV PEP12, while a significant proportion of HCW (36.9%) had inadequate HIV PEP knowledge. In comparison to previous studies conducted in Tanzania, our study found a relatively higher proportion of HCWs with adequate knowledge of HIV PEP. Two of the three hospitals included in this study (Kigoma Regional Hospital and Kasulu Town Council Hospital) are teaching hospitals, and this may have contributed to the higher proportion observed. Teaching hospitals are more likely to be exposed updated information regarding disease prevention and control including PEP information through ward rounds and trainings held by hospital management.

A notable proportion of HCWs had inadequate knowledge (38%) in this study. Interventions needed to improve knowledge on HIV PEP such as regular HIV PEP training sessions, surveys to assess the knowledge gap and strategies to address these gaps are of paramount importance.

In this study, there was an association between facility type in which HCWs worked with the level of knowledge of HIV PEP. This association was not significant in a previous study conducted in Dar Es Salaam, Tanzania11. Factors that were found significant in this previous study including level of education (certificate level of education) were not significant in our study. HCW from Kasulu Town Council Hospital had higher odds of having adequate knowledge on HIV PEP compared to HCW from other facilities. This hospital provides healthcare services to refugee camps and receives support from internation organizations such as WHO and United Nations agencies such as United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) and United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR)13,14. With this support, Kasulu hospital has a relatively more structured management and leadership as well as healthcare quality improvement initiatives, compared to other facilities. This structure ensures availability of essential services including PEP services and may have contributed to the observed findings. Extending similar support to other facilities could help standardize service delivery and improve access to critical interventions such as PEP across the region.

In this study, the availability of PEP services at facilities was associated with adequate knowledge of HIV PEP, in contrast to a study in Nigeria, which showed no association of HIV PEP services with adequate knowledge of HIV PEP15. In Tanzania HIV PEP is one of the services recommended in national guideline for management of HIV and AIDS to be provided at the HIV clinics16. However, not all health facilities in Tanzania have HIV clinics and among those that do not all provide PEP services as recommended particularly regarding raising awareness on PEP use following occupational exposure. Despite all facilities recruited in this study having HIV clinics, and expected to have HIV PEP services, only 39.3% of the participants reported that PEP services are available in their facilities. These findings highlight the need for the national HIV program to strengthen efforts to increase awareness among healthcare workers about the availability and importance of PEP services, ensuring they are both informed and empowered to access and utilize them when needed.

In this study, the availability of HIV PEP guidelines had no association with the level of knowledge among HCWs, similar to a study conducted in Nigeria that showed no association between doctors knowledge of HIV PEP and the existence of PEP guideline policies in hospitals, other studies prevail strong association between guidelines and knowledge on HIV PEP15,17,18. Contrary to a study conducted in Dar Es Salaam, Tanzania, where the majority of respondents acquired knowledge on HIV PEP from HIV treatment guidelines11, The difference in current study on HIV PEP guidelines that did not associate with knowledge could be because of reluctance to emphasize and disseminate updated HIV PEP guidelines for usage compared to previous studies when PEP guidelines were newly implemented as well as a time lag between earlier studies and the current study’s level of influence to adhere with guidelines may differ. Other work-related explored in this study including information education communication materials and available focal persons on reporting exposures were not associated with adequate HIV PEP knowledge. Contrary to other research studies reported on profession and attitude that was associated with adequate knowledge on HIV PEP4,9. Hence, there are limited studies conducted that focused on work-related factors and their association with knowledge on HIV PEP rather than knowledge, attitude, and practice on HIV PEP.

Study strengths and limitations

This study was conducted among two district hospitals and a regional hospital based on fact that these facilities had obvious plenty number of HCWs and high volume of patients.

hospitalized which were assessed quickly on current level of knowledge. The results might not be generalized to all facilities especially health centres and dispensaries as data suggests were at secondary levels where these facilities are regarded as referral hospitals.

Conclusions

The findings of this study revealed that the majority of study participants had adequate knowledge of HIV PEP; while a significant proportion of HCWs still have inadequate knowledge of HIV PEP. Type of the facility and availability of HIV PEP service were associated with adequate knowledge of HIV PEP. Our findings highlight the critical need to strengthen the implementation of formal HIV PEP training, timely access to HIV PEP service, and enhance the dissemination of updated information education communication materials alongside protocol guidelines to improve HCWs overall HIV PEP knowledge all healthcare facilities.
