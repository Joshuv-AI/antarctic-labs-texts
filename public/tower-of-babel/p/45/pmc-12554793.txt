Tibial component baseplate fracture following robot assisted bicompartmental knee arthroplasty: A case report
Authors: B Hawayek, A Kovacs, J Mogerman, M Bayers-Thering, E Dyskin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCaseWe present the case of a 44-year-old man who underwent robot-assisted bicompartmental knee arthroplasty (BiKA). Post-operatively the patient continued to experience knee pain and recurrent effusions. Radiographs and CT scans revealed no obvious signs of hardware failure, and aspiration was negative for all markers of infection. He underwent revision surgery and was found to have a fracture of the tibial baseplate.ConclusionsThis complication should be considered in patients with persistent knee pain following BiKA, especially in those without signs of infection or obvious hardware failure. Radiographic and CT images should be heavily scrutinized to evaluate for this complication.Keywords: Tibial, Baseplate, Fracture, Case, Report

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is the gold standard treatment for patients with severe knee osteoarthritis (OA) as it addresses disease in the knee's medial, lateral, and patellofemoral compartments [1]. For isolated medial or lateral compartment OA, unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) is a viable alternative to TKA [2,3].

Osteoarthritis typically progresses from the medial to the patellofemoral compartments [4,5]. In patients with isolated medial and patellofemoral OA, bicompartmental knee arthroplasty (BiKA) is an alternative treatment to TKA or UKA [6]. BiKA is less invasive than TKA and retains the native lateral compartment of the knee as well as the cruciate ligaments [7]. This allows BiKAs to mimic natural knee mechanics more closely during weightbearing and may also allow for shorter recovery times and decreased post-operatively pain [8]. The use of robotic assistance in performing knee arthroplasty has become more popular and is widely accepted as a beneficial tool while performing surgery. It has been associated with lower revision rates, lower incidences of manipulation under anesthesia, decreased occurrence of systemic complications, and lower opiate consumption for postoperative pain management [9,10].

Tibial baseplate fractures in BiKA are a rare complication, with only 2 previous cases being reported both involving the Deuce Journey implant (Smith and Nephew Inr., Memphis, TN, USA) [11,12]. It is also a rarely reported complication of UKA, with suggested risk factors being higher patient BMI and progressive varus or valgus deformity [13]. We present a case of a 44-year-old man who presented with recurrent pain and effusions after undergoing robot-assisted BiKA using the Stryker Mako and Stryker Medial BiKA components. He was subsequently found to have a fracture of the tibial baseplate. He was revised to TKA with symptomatic improvement. We believe this to be the first reported case of a tibial baseplate fracture in robot-assisted bicompartmental knee arthroplasty.

Informed consent

The patient in this case report was informed that the data concerning their case would be submitted for publication. The patient has agreed to this.

Patient Information

We present a case of a 44-year-old physically active man who is 183.4 cm tall and weighs 106.6 kg with a BMI of 31.7 who initially injured himself when he slipped and fell on a wet floor and sustained a hyperextension injury to his knee. He was treated non-operatively with physical therapy and intraarticular steroid injections and later went on to undergo an arthroscopic partial meniscectomy due to persistent symptoms. Intraoperatively it was noted he had full-thickness cartilage loss in his medial and patellofemoral compartments with an intact ACL and PCL. He continued to have substantial left knee pain following the surgery and a platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injection was given. The patient had minimal improvement in his pain following the injection and was referred to the senior author for discussion of further treatment options.

Given the debilitating nature of the patient's knee pain as well as degenerative changes and chondral damage that was isolated to the medial and patellofemoral compartments the decision was made to proceed with robot-assisted BiKA. The patient had a BMI of 31.7, had never smoked, and had no other pertinent comorbidities. He had no history of prior injuries or surgeries to the knee. His pre-operative radiographs are shown in Fig. 1A. Surgery was performed 3 years after his initial injury was performed through a standard medial para-patellar approach to the knee using the Stryker Mako robot and the following components: Stryker Orthopaedics MAKO Restoris MCK femoral component 4 LM/RL, onlay tibial baseplate 4 LM/RL, patellofemoral component 5 left, patella component 35 mm, X3 Uni onlay tibial insert size 4, thickness 8 mm. Femoral, tibial, and patellar components were fixated with cement. No intra-operative complications were noted. Immediate post-operative radiographs are demonstrated in Figs. 1B and 2.

Clinical findings

Post-operatively the patient continued to report knee pain which was improved from prior to surgery but worsened with activity. At 5 and 6 months post-operatively the pain in his left knee still had not resolved, as well as recurrent effusions which were worse with activity. He remained weightbearing as tolerated and continued to work with physical therapy for range of motion and strengthening exercises. At 10 months postoperatively he had still been unable to return to work full-time and reported pain and swelling that continued to limit his day-to-day activity (Table 1).

Diagnostic assessment

Radiographs taken at 5 and 6 months post op demonstrated an intact prosthesis with no obvious evidence of fracture or hardware failure (Fig. 3). At 10 months, left lower extremity doppler was performed to rule out DVT and was negative. Aspiration was performed and 5 cc of hemorrhagic fluid was obtained. All markers of infection were negative. To differentiate between intra-articular versus extra-articular pathology an injection of 10 cc of 1 % lidocaine was injected with immediate pain relief experienced by the patient. Due to the intraarticular nature of the patient's pain the decision was made to undergo a revision arthroplasty from BiKA to TKA. A CT of the left knee was ordered and was interpreted by a radiologist to not demonstrate any obvious signs of hardware failure (Fig. 5).

Therapeutic intervention

Ten months after the initial surgery the patient underwent revision to TKA using the following components: Stryker triathlon posterior stabilized femoral component, size #4, size left, type PS, triathlon total knee universal tibial baseplate, size #5, triathlon femoral distal fixation peg x2, triathlon X3 tibial bearing insert, size #5, thickness 13 mm, type PS. All components were cemented in place. At the time of surgery, there were no signs of infection, and the trochlear and medial femoral components were found to be well fixed and were removed with osteotomes in standard fashion. When attention was turned to the tibial component it initially appeared well fixed, however, after removal of the polyethylene liner, it was discovered that the tibial baseplate had fractured straight across the mid-portion of the component in the frontal plane (Fig. 4). Following further scrutinization, the area of the baseplate fracture was able to be identified on the pre-operative radiographs and CT scan (Fig. 5). Robotic assisted revision TKA was performed with the use of the Stryker Mako and Stryker Triathlon TKA posterior-stabilized components (Fig. 6).

Follow-up and outcomes

Following revision surgery, the patient's knee pain improved significantly. He was able to return to work full-time and was satisfied with his procedure. At final 1 year follow-up, he had full range of motion of his left knee and radiographic imaging demonstrated a stable prosthesis with no evidence of fracture or loosening (Fig. 6). All patient-reported outcomes improved from prior to revision surgery.

Discussion

Our patient was diagnosed with a fracture of the tibial baseplate component of the robot assisted BiKA at the time of his revision surgery. To our knowledge, this is the first case reported of tibial baseplate fracture using the Stryker Mako and Stryker medial BiKA components. Two other reports of tibial baseplate fracture exist in the literature, both involving the Deuce Journey implant (Smith and Nephew Inr., Memphis, TN, USA). Palumbo et al. investigated the Deuce Journey BiKA in 32 patients, reporting a survival rate of 86 % and one patient who sustained a fracture of the tibial baseplate which was diagnosed at 15 months postoperatively [12]. Stuyts et al. reported on a case of a fractured tibial baseplate in a patient who had undergone BiKA with the Deuce Journey implant [11]. Their case was diagnosed roughly 5 years after the initial procedure. They suggest that the fracture of the tibial baseplate following BiKA was potentially due to the thin design of the modular titanium component with two fixation pegs being unable to withstand daily cyclical loading forces associated with higher impact activities such as running.

In this case, it is difficult to hypothesize the cause of the baseplate fracture. Our patient was not performing any high impact activities such as running and had consistent pain post-operatively which worsened with activity. He did not have any traumatic injury to his knee which may have caused the baseplate fracture. Manzotti et al. reported the incidence of tibial baseplate fracture in UKA to be 1.6 % (2/121). Patients with BMI > 30 and deterioration in limb alignment were found to be at greater risk [13]. Our patient did have a BMI of 31.7, which may have placed him at greater risk for sustaining this complication. Additionally, upon scrutinizing the radiographs presented in Fig. 3 there appears to be a slight lucency surrounding the pegs of the tibial component. Implant loosening may have caused micromotion which in turn may have resulted in implant fatigue and subsequent fracture. Although, the patient did report symptoms shortly post-operatively which may suggest the fracture occurred intra-operatively or shortly post-operatively.

Tibial baseplate fracture following BiKA is rarely reported and can be difficult to diagnose on routine radiographic imaging. We suggest that this complication be considered in patients with persistent knee pain following BiKA, especially in those without signs of infection or obvious hardware failure. We recommend that the radiographic and CT images be heavily scrutinized to evaluate for this in this subset of patients. We hypothesize that a thin tibial component as well as increased patient BMI and implant loosening may be risk factors for tibial baseplate fracture following BiKA and suggest recommending low-impact activities such as walking, swimming, and leisurely cycling in patients who have undergone this procedure.
