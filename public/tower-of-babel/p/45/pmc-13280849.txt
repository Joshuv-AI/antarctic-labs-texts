Machine Learning-Driven Prediction and Mechanistic Insight into CO 2 Adsorption on Biomass-Derived Activated Carbons Using Explainable AI (XAI)
Authors: Dalia A. Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To improve CO2 uptake in Biomass-Derived Activated Carbon (BDAC), this study develops a multiscale hybrid digital twin framework. By integrating microscopic descriptors from Density Functional Theory and Molecular Dynamics (DFT/MD) with experimental data from 63 chemically diverse biomass precursors, a Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) model was developed using the Matérn 5/2 Automatic Relevance Determination (ARD) kernel. The framework achieved high internal training accuracy (R 2 = 0.968) and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE = 0.2552), while providing a realistic generalization baseline across heterogeneous precursors with a 5-fold Cross-Validated (CV) R 2 of 0.1567 and CV RMSE of 0.283. Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) identified a synergistic mechanism for pore filling, revealing the interaction between ln Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) specific surface area and ln total pore volume (ln S BET × ln V total) as the primary mechanical driver (rank 1). Sensitivity analysis identified a maximum thermal window near 400 °C, with 800 °C identified as the critical sintering threshold where structural breakdown begins. Furthermore, the model validates the nonreliance theory of raw material, demonstrating that the geometric surface properties exert a more dominant influence on performance than the biomass origin. The model was effectively stabilized by Bayesian optimization at a minimum internal training loss of 0.065, providing a scalable, materials information-based scheme for using high-resolution virtual screening to accelerate the circular carbon economy.

Introduction

Global Climate Imperatives and the Role of Biomass-Derived Activated Carbons (BDACs)

The growing concentration of CO2 in the atmosphere, which is largely due to human activities, poses a serious threat to global climate stability, and therefore there is considerable interest in reliable Carbon Capture & Sequestration (CCS) technologies. , Adsorption with porous materials is one of the most studied CCS technologies. BDACs has been widely considered as excellent candidates for adsorbents owing to their various merits in the aspects of effective management and utilization of sustainable waste sources, low cost, the tailorability of surface characteristics. , It has come to attention that BDACs are a promising alternative to traditional CO2 capture methodologies, which often suffer from high energy requirements and environmental issues. With the intrinsic difficulty in establishing the structure–property relationship between BDACs and their CO2 adsorption performance, advanced modeling approaches are required to accelerate material design and optimization. The effect of factors such as pore size distribution, surface area, and adsorption condition are often cannot be illustrated effectively through the common prediction models of CO2 adsorption capacity. , Therefore, to achieve accurate modeling and prediction of CO2 adsorption behavior in porous carbonaceous materials, Machine Learning (ML) methods are used specifically, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Gradient Boosting Decision Trees (GBDTs), and Random Forests (RF). , These ML methods have excessive ability in capturing complex, nonlinear relationships between material properties and CO2 capacity. These models are extremely useful for investigating complex material data sets because they can handle smaller data sets, reduce overfitting, and handle noisy or correlated characteristics. Furthermore, improving the transparency and accuracy of predictions requires the integration of XAI methodologies with ML models. XAI techniques efficiently convert black-box models into more comprehensible white-box models by providing insights into the critical elements controlling CO2 adsorption. XAI can direct the synthesis and modification of BDACs to maximize CO2 capture efficiency by determining the most important variables, such as ultramicropore volume and surface area. This approach enables a more rational design process, going beyond experimental trial-and-error methods to a more comprehensive and objective analysis, which can be applied to a wider range of porous carbon materials. According to standard scientific assessments, ML and BDACs are increasingly being used to collect CO2. This work aims to provide a reliable and easy-to-understand ML framework used to predict CO2 uptake on porous carbon composites, leveraging the advantages of XAI and tree-based models. Current Landscape of ML in CO2 Adsorption on BDAC

Overview of Established ML Models and Their Typical Applications

The application of ML to the study of CO2 adsorption, especially with regard to BDAC, is widely documented. , Predictive modeling and optimization problems often involve a range of supervised ML methods. These include tree-based ensemble models as Extra Trees Regressor, XGBoost, Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGBM), Categorical Boosting (CatBoost), Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost), RF, Gradient Boosting Regressor (GBR), Decision Tree (DT), and GBDT. Furthermore, neural networks (Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Radial Basis Function-Neural Networks (RBF-NN)) and Support Vector Machine (SVM) are frequently used. , To interpret these, methodology like SHapley Additive explanations (SHAP) is applied. These models are used mainly to make predictions of CO2 adsorption capacity based on a variety of material characteristics and operating conditions. Furthermore, they support optimization and reduce the need for trial-and-error in the development of novel materials by directing experimental synthesis to achieve desired material characteristics or maximize adsorption performance. The following error functions are used to investigate the accurate performance of these models including; Coefficient of Determination (R 2), RMSE, and Mean Absolute Error (MAE). ,

Analysis of Key Material Properties and Synthesis Parameters as ML Descriptors for CO2 Uptake

Biomass source, pyrolysis/carbonization temperature, V total, S BET, activation type, activation agent and activation temperature factors were included in the data set inputs for accurate prediction of the CO2 adsorption behavior on activated carbon materials.

Textural Features: It has been repeatedly demonstrated that ln V total, and ln S BET particularly the characteristics of micropores and ultramicropores have a significant impact on CO2 adsorption capacity. These fine pores produce strong confining effects and increased van der Waals forces, both of which are necessary for physical adsorption. ,

Synthesis criteria: The type of biomass feedstock, pyrolysis/carbonization temperature, activation temperature, and activation method (chemical or physical), and the activating agent used greatly influence the chemical composition of the activated carbon surface and its final surface properties. For example, carbon is preferred to be chemically activated rather than physically activated. Chemical processes typically produce materials with better surface quality and higher porosity, which explains this preference. In addition, the chemical activation process is more energy efficient, operating at lower temperatures than its physical counterpart. The choice of activating agent (such as potassium hydroxide (KOH), phosphoric acid (H3PO4), zinc chloride (ZnCl2)) affects the development of surface area and pore structure. ,

Fixed Operating Conditions (25 °C and 1 atm): The adsorption of CO2 using BDACs for each sample in the data set has been performed at fixed ambient operating conditions of temperature = 25 °C and pressure of 1 atm. The fixed conditions in this data set enable a deeper, more focused investigation into the intrinsic material properties and synthesis parameters that dictate CO2 uptake at these particular, industrially relevant conditions, whereas many studies vary temperature and pressure as inputs to optimize adsorption conditions. Because of this limitation, research is now more focused on improving material synthesis and design than the actual adsorption process.

Research Gaps

Although current ML applications of CO2 adsorption on BDAC show high predictive accuracy (R 2 > 0.95 in controlled experiments), a significant research gap remains regarding the transparency of the mechanism and generalizability across different feedstocks. , Most existing models remain vague, often overfitting to certain types of biomass or limited laboratory conditions. , Given the specialized nature of these materials, high-fidelity modeling requires more than just statistical correlation. While this study achieves a high training fit (R 2 = 0.968), it moves beyond simple curve-fitting by prioritizing predictive reliability through an integrated multiscale approach. XAI was utilized to decode the black-box nature of tree-based models, identifying the specific pore structures and surface characteristics that drive CO2 uptake. These data-driven findings are then cross-validated through atomistic DFT-MD simulations to provide a robust physical basis for the model’s logic, bridging the gap between laboratory observation and fundamental theory.

Beyond Predictive Accuracy: Toward Mechanistic Understanding and Causal Inference

The Black-Box Challenge and the Need for XAI

The direct use of XAI algorithms on the BDAC data set is an important aspect of this study. SHAP analysis is used to determine the discriminating effect of each input characteristic, from the biomass source to the activation temperature, rather than viewing ML as a black box for prediction. These techniques were applied to the selected data set under constant adsorption conditions (25 °C and 1 atm) to identify and rank the basic physical properties affecting CO2 adsorption. This approach enables us to determine the exact effect of ultrafine pore size and specific surface area on adsorption efficiency. Driven by a shift from feasibility study to applied explanation, mere correlation can now be gone beyond, and an accurate, model-based explanation can be provided for why some synthesis pathways are superior to others.

Integrating ML with Atomistic Simulations (DFT/MD) for Deeper, Fundamental Insights

The insights obtained by macroscopic ML models are frequently restricted to the size of the input features (e.g., S BET, V total), even with XAI. Atomic and molecular insights are essential to comprehending the fundamental mechanics of CO2 adsorption. This basic knowledge of adsorbate–adsorbent interactions, pore-level diffusion, and the precise function of functional groups is frequently lacking in conventional experimental techniques and macroscopic ML models. MD simulation and DFT are powerful computational techniques that allow a very accurate understanding of these processes at the molecular level. , It shows how CO2 molecules interact with specific pore shapes, bind to different surface functional groups, and diffuse throughout the carbon matrix. Due to their high computational cost, these simulations can only be used in large-scale examination of materials or in comprehensive parametric studies. In this work, coupling ML/XAI with DFT-MD simulations bridge the gap between macroscopic descriptors and molecular interactions. Rather than using simulations in isolation, XAI-identified critical features is used to parametrize the atomistic models. This facilitates explanation at the molecular level, why specific activation types or surface functional groups highlighted by the ML model enhance physical or chemical adsorption. The constant temperature and pressure (25 °C, 1 atm) in the data set are ideal in such a comprehensive atomic simulation, because they eliminate the complexity of variable thermodynamic effects on molecular interactions, allowing a focused study of the interactions of matter with CO2. A predictive model is trained using the data set’s experimental data in an integrated approach that combines ML, XAI, and DFT/MD. The XAI is used to identify important macroscopic properties (such as specific V total ranges and activation types) that influence CO2 uptake. Next, the atomic-level reasons for the importance of this property at 25 °C and 1 atm can be explained by simulating DFT/MD enhanced by ML. For example, this may show how specific surface chemical properties (affected by type of activation, and biomass source) or pore geometry (derived from pore size and BET data) can enhance the physical or chemical adsorption of CO2 at ambient conditions. This multiscale method makes a highly innovative contribution by providing fundamental insight beyond empirical correlations.

Addressing Data Heterogeneity and Model Generalizability

Quantifying and Managing Uncertainty (UQ) in ML Predictions

Both experimental data and ML algorithms are inherently uncertain. Natural biomass composition fluctuation, small variations in synthesis conditions, and measurement errors during material characterization are examples of sources of UQ in the context of CO2 adsorption. For example, the biochemical makeup of biomass sources varies greatly, which directly affects the characteristics of the activated carbon that is produced. A high-impact study would quantify the forecast’s reliability by providing a prediction interval or confidence boundaries in addition to a single CO2 uptake number. This entails differentiating between epistemic UQ (UQ resulting from model limitations or inadequate data) and aleatoric UQ (inherent variability in the data itself). For this reason, ensemble methods with UQ estimates (such as bootstrapping in RFs) or Bayesian ML methodologies are appropriate. Less research has been done on the methodical use of UQ quantification to account for biomass variability in activated carbon synthesis for CO2 adsorption. The reliability and usefulness of the ML model for practical application and well-informed decision-making in industrial contexts are greatly increased when quantified UQ is included with forecasts.

Developing Robust ML Models for Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Generalization and Transfer Learning

The capacity of models to generalize well to OOD samples materials or situations that differ significantly from those encountered during the model’s training phase is a major difficulty in applying ML to materials science. An example of an OOD difficulty is biomass source variability, where a model trained on one type of biomass may perform badly on a new, untested biomass source. Broad application requires models that are resilient to these kinds of fluctuations. Research can concentrate on creating ML models with robust OOD generalization capabilities to address this. The flexibility of models can be enhanced using methods such as transfer learning, which applies knowledge from a particular task or field (such as activated carbon from a particular biomass source) to another similar task (such as activated carbon from a different biomass source). Cutting-edge technologies, including competitive learning to generalize goal-oriented design models, may improve the ability of models to adapt to unexpected changes in biomass types or synthesis conditions. , Developing models that work efficiently on unknown biomass sources or under dramatically changing synthesis conditions is one of the most difficult tasks in materials science. Overcoming this barrier is critical to commercial growth and the widespread application of activated carbon produced from biomass in CO2 capture, making it a critical research and innovation topic.

The Present Study: A Hybrid ML/XAI/DFT-MD Framework

This study contributes a three-tiered hybrid framework designed to move beyond black-box predictions:

Level 1 (Macro-Scale): Level 1 (Macro-Scale): GPR, XGBoost, RF, and GBDT are used to predict CO2 uptake across diverse biomass feedstocks.

Level 2 (Interpretability): SHAP analysis is applied to rank descriptor importance, identifying the critical thresholds.

Level 3 (Atomistic-Scale): DFT and MD simulations are performed on the top-performing configurations identified by Level 2 to elucidate the binding energies and MD patterns that drive performance. This integrated methodology ensures that findings of this study are both statistically robust and physically grounded.

Data Diversity and Biomass Precursor Classification

The data set that forms the basis of the forecasting framework is characterized by the diversity of raw materials and their chemical multidimensionality. While the extreme variability of these 63 separate sources naturally limits global R 2 metrics, this classification ensures that the internal logic of the model is based on the basic biochemical building blocks of biomass rather than a single homogeneous waste stream.

Fundamental Adsorption Theory and Pore-Filling Mechanism

Adsorption of CO2 onto BDAC is a multiscale physical phenomenon that is primarily subject to physical adsorption. At ambient conditions (25 °C, 1 atm), the interaction between the quadrupole CO2 molecules and the carbon surface is driven by London scattering forces and electrostatic interactions.

The Role of Microporosity and the Dubinin–Radushkevich Theory

In contrast to high-pressure adsorption that follows the single-layer Langmuir model, the process of ambient CO2 capture on BDACs is best described by pore filling theory. In ultrafine pores, adsorption efforts from opposite pore walls overlap, creating a deep potential well that greatly enhances the attraction of gas molecules. This is mathematically represented by the Dubinin–Radushkevich (DR) (eq ): 3 W=Woe[−(AβEo)2] where the Adsorption Potential (A) is defined by the Gibbs free energy of adsorption. It is calculated from the following (eq ): 4 A=RTln⁡PoP where W (cm3/g): The volume of the adsorption space filled by the condensed CO2 at a given temperature and pressure. W o (cm3/g): The limiting volume of the adsorption space, representing the total micropore capacity. In this ML model, this is the physical equivalent of the feature V total. E o (kJ/mol): The characteristic energy of adsorption, which reflects the structural heterogeneity of the carbon pores. β (Dimensionless): The affinity coefficient, which is a scaling factor for the polarizability of CO2 relative to a reference gas (usually benzene, where β = 1). For CO2, β ≈ 0.35. R (8.314 J/mol K): The universal gas constant. T (K): The temperature in Kelvin.

Bayesian Hyper-Parameter Optimization and Multiscale Training

The Expected Improvement (EI) acquisition function was used in Bayesian optimization to prevent overfitting. In this iterative process, a set of hyper-parameters for optimal kernel metrics and noise bounds were processed. The use of a minimum (σ = 0.0001) significantly improved the training stability. This limitation ensures that the model provides a reliable quantitative measure of UQ. It was detected during a 50-repeat optimization cycle (Figure b). Even when biomass waste streams are projected OOD, the digital twin maintains a high degree of generalization by distinguishing between experimental measurement noise (randomness) and model’s UQ (cognitive). K-type cross-validation (k = 5 for the integrated model) was used to ensure that performance measures, specifically the CV RMSE of 0.283, reflect the model’s ability to predict the properties of materials that have not yet been physically manufactured.

Mathematical Architecture of the XAI Framework

To ensure transparency to the Ensemble Trees model, SHAP was used based on alliance game theory. The goal is to distribute the final prediction of CO2 adsorption among input features such as S BET and V total.

The SHAP Value Equation and Feature Attribution

To quantify the individual contribution of each synthesis and textural parameter to the final CO2 adsorption prediction, SHAP method is used. This method is based on coalitional game theory, where the final prediction is considered a payout and each input feature is a player. The SHAP value (Φ i ) for a specific feature i is defined as represented in (eq ): 5 Φi(f,x)=∑S⊆N\(i)|S|!(n−|S|−1)!n![f(S∪{i})−f(S)] where Φ i : The SHAP value (attribution) for feature i. A positive Φ i indicates the feature increased the CO2 uptake prediction relative to the average, while a negative value indicates a decrease. n: The total number of input features in the model (e.g., temperature, S BET, V total, etc.). S: A subset (coalition) of features that does not include the feature i. |S|: The number of features currently in the subset S. f(S ∪ {i}): The model’s prediction of CO2 uptake when feature i is included in the set of predictors. f(S): The model’s prediction of CO2 uptake based only on the subset S, excluding feature i. [f(S ∪ {i}) – f(S)]: The marginal contribution of feature i. This represents how much the CO2 capacity changes specifically because of that one property. |S|!(n−|S|−1)!n! : The weighting factor.

To ensure the identified feature attributions were not biased by specific biomass outliers in the data set (N = 63), SHAP values were aggregated across the CV iterations. This approach confirms that the global importance of descriptors specifically the synergy between ln S BET and ln V total remains stable regardless of which specific biomass sample is excluded from the training set. This stability validates that the model is capturing universal structural drivers rather than overfitting to individual feedstock characteristics.

Performance Evaluation Metrics

Three main statistical variables were used to measure the accuracy of the resulting models’ predictions and their ability to generalize accurately.

Coefficient of Determination (R 2)

The R 2 represents the ratio of the variation in CO2 adsorption capacity that can be predicted by independent input properties (e.g., S BET and pyrolysis/carbonization temperature). It can be calculated from (eq ): , 6 R2=1−∑i=1n(ypredicted−yactual)2∑i=1n(ypredicted−ymean)2

Root Mean Square Error (RMSE)

RMSE provides a measure of the average magnitude of the error between the predicted values (ŷ i ) and the actual observed values (yi ), expressed in the same units as the CO2 uptake (mmol/g). It can be calculated from (eq ): , 7 RMSE=(1n)∑i=1n(yi−ŷi)2

RMSE is quite sensitive to extreme outliers since it squares errors before averaging. Even when predicting extremely complex and challenging samples like diverse biowaste streams, the model did not encounter large mistakes, as evidenced by its low CV RMSE of 0.283.

Mean Absolute Error (MAE)

MAE calculates the average absolute difference between experimental and predicted values, providing a linear score that represents the typical distance of a prediction from the actual that can be calculated from (eq ): , 8 MAE=(1n)∑i=1n|yi−ŷi|

MAE does not penalize huge errors as severely as RMSE does. A standard diagnosis in scientific articles is to compare CV MAE (0.218) to CV RMSE (0.283). , Because these values are remarkably close, this suggests that the error distribution in this study is small and entirely free of substantial extreme outliers.

Model Validation and Robustness

Given the specialized nature of BDAC data sets (N = 63), a rigorous CV strategy was employed to mitigate the risk of overfitting. Unlike a standard 80/20 split, which can lead to high variance on small data sets, this approach ensures that the reported R 2 and RMSE reflect the model’s ability to generalize to unseen feedstock categories. Furthermore, feature pruning was utilized to maintain a high observations-to-features ratio, ensuring model complexity was commensurate with the data set size. To ensure mechanistic drivers were not artifacts of the small sample size, a feature stability analysis was performed. As shown in Figure , the trained ARD hyper-parameter weights for key descriptors including multiscale features such as ln S BET, ln V total, and atomistic DFT/MD signatures remained consistently stable across 63 random subsampling iterations. This confirms the robustness of the physical signals captured by the model and demonstrates that the integration of atomistic-scale data provides stable, predictive power despite the data set constraints (N = 63).

Global Feature Importance and Parameter Rankings

To address the complexity of the high-performance GPR digital twin, a Global Permutation Feature Importance approach was used to identify the primary drivers of CO2 adsorption. By quantifying the increase in MSE when permuting individual predictors, this methodology provides a rigorous mathematical basis for variable significance, as illustrated in Figure .

Global feature importance metrics demonstrating the relative impact of processing parameters and multiscale descriptors based on permutation-induced increase in MSE.

Analysis of the importance of general properties showed that the reaction coefficient (ln S BET × ln V total) had the greatest influence on the model’s predictions, explaining the highest increase in MSE. This demonstrates a fundamental synergistic relationship: while an increase in ln S BET provides the necessary binding sites, its effects are physically limited unless offset by an increase in ln V total to serve as a storage vessel. This result is fully consistent with the well-known pore filling theory. Furthermore, the high importance of activation type (rank 2) compared to biomass source (rank 5) provides experimental evidence supporting the neutral theory of raw materials. This suggests that the final carbon structure is more influenced by chemical activation mechanisms than by the chemical composition of the raw material. Finally, the pyrolysis temperature is the primary thermodynamic driver, determining the initial structural composition of biochar prior to chemical activation. To accurately evaluate the performance of the GPR on the N = 63 data set, multiscale cross-verification using an improved feature set was used. As shown in Figure , integrated validation achieved CV R 2 value of 0.1567, representing a realistic lower bound for predicting biomass classes outside the distribution range. This model is based on a calculated base value of 1.5282, which corresponds to the average physical CO2 absorption capacity of the data set of 3.7906 mmol/g. To address the potential over conciliation problem and verify these results, a stability analysis was performed (Figure ), which confirmed that the importance of the interaction between ln S BET and ln V total remains statistically stable (p < 0.05) across multiple subsets of random data, even when global predictive variance is high.

The Trade-Off between Temperature and Porosity

The sensitivity of the GPR model to synthesis conditions is demonstrated by the correlation between carbonization/pyrolysis temperature and CO2 adsorption. As shown in the PDP (Figure ), the model defines an ideal thermal range centered around 400 °C within the chemical range of the raw materials under study. Volatile materials remain that clog potential pore networks, and incomplete carbonization occurs at temperatures well below this limit. On the other hand, a steady decrease in the CO2 capacity is observed after exceeding 400 °C. The model concludes that 800 °C is a critical temperature at which sintering effects begin to overcome pore formation. At these high temperatures, carbon sheet fusion can cause very fine pores to collapse into larger, less efficient structures, reducing the effective CO2 adsorption capacity. Although the carbonization temperature ranks ninth in fundamental importance globally (Table ), it serves as the critical thermodynamic guardian of subsequent activation success.

Textural Synergy: The (ln S BET × ln V total) Interaction

The interaction between ln S BET and ln V total is the most important mechanistic factor identified by XAI analysis (Table ). This synergy represents the mathematical embodiment of the pore-filling mechanism, as shown in the 3D response surface (Figure ). While ln V total ensures the structural capacity and accessibility of CO2 molecules, the density of ln S BET provides the necessary number of adsorption sites. As shown in Figure , CO2 absorption is not linearly proportional to either property individually. Rather, the GPR model shows a nonlinear optimal region (represented by the yellow peak and star) where the ratio of ln S BET (≈7.5–8) to ln V total (≈0.5–1) is balanced. If ln S BET increases without a parallel increase in ln V total, which often results in thinning of the pore wall, the expected capacity decreases, as shown in the cool blue areas of the graph. This confirms that the designed structure, rather than the specific biomass source, is the key determining factor for high-performance sorbents.

Chemical Modification and Multi-Scale Descriptor Bridging

The basic chemistry of chemical activation pathways must be put into context to bridge the gap between atomic electronic scales and total histological indicators (ln S BET, ln V total) (Table ). High surface oxygen contents are produced when polar oxygen-containing functional groups (hydroxyl and carboxyl groups) are introduced while carbon structures are actively broken down by alkaline activating agents (such as KOH). By changing the local charge density of the carbon matrix, these polar groups create strong electrostatic interactions with the quadrupole moment of the CO2 molecules. This local polarization of the structure is directly mechanically related to the higher binding energies calculated using functional density theory simulations. On the other hand, pyrrole and pyridine compounds that act as primary Lewis sites are produced by activation conditions or nitrogen-introducing feedstocks, such as chitosan. The electron-poor carbon core of the CO2 molecule receives a more targeted electron donation thanks to this basicity. Mechanistically, the higher binding energies calculated using DFT simulations are directly correlated with this localized framework polarization. On the other hand, activation environments or precursors that include nitrogen, like chitosan, produce pyrrolic and pyridinic structures that serve as fundamental Lewis sites. Targeted electronic donation to the CO2 gas molecule’s electron-deficient carbon core is improved by this basicity. This chemical-to-electronic bridge is well captured by the GPR-ARD model: instead of depending only on nonspecific physical pore-filling mechanics, the internal network shifts its sensitivity baseline toward explicit electronic density features when these heteroatom signatures change local framework basicity.

The model reveals that final performance is determined by geometric porosity rather than biomass origin, if the material can be converted into a stable structure, as shown in the SHAP analysis (Figure ).

Thermal Windowing: The 400 °C Optimum and the 800 °C Sintering Threshold

Based on the PDP analysis (Figure ), the GPR model identifies an optimal carbonization/pyrolysis temperature near 400 °C for maximizing initial pore development and CO2 capture capacity. While traditional synthesis often targets higher temperatures, the model designates the 800 °C mark as a sintering threshold (saddle point). Beyond this temperature, any incremental benefit from volatile removal is physically outweighed by the structural collapse of ultramicropores and the integration of carbon sheets. Therefore, synthesis strategies should prioritize the 400–500 °C range for skeletal formation before entering high-temperature activation phases to avoid irreversible porosity loss.

Strategic Outlook and Future Directions

Beyond the Current ML/XAI/DFT-MD Framework

While this study successfully establishes a multiscale framework for predicting and understanding CO2 adsorption, several emerging computational frontiers remain for future exploration. The hybrid ML/XAI/DFT-MD approach used here provides the mechanistic foundation necessary to eventually support more autonomous discovery methods.

Integration of Generative AI and Reinforcement Learning

Future iterations of this study may use generative AI models to model BDAC reversibly, such as Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) or Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs). These models can suggest new synthesis paths for better porous architectures by learning the latent distribution of the high-performance materials found in this work. Furthermore, real-time optimization of composition parameters to achieve adsorption targets is made possible by the dynamic management of carbonization and activation processes that reinforcement learning offers.

Causal Inference and Scalability Assessment

Moving from the importance of features provided by XAI techniques to causal inference will enable a deeper understanding of the “cause and effect” relationships between biomass feedstocks and final tissue properties. Furthermore, to direct these materials toward commercialization, future research must integrate technical and Economic Analysis (TEA) and Life Cycle Assessment (LCA). Integrating predictive ML models with TEA and LCA frameworks will be key to ensuring that materials with the highest CO2 absorption are economically viable and environmentally sustainable on an industrial scale.

Addressing Data Heterogeneity: UQ and OOD Generalization

The GPR’s capacity to measure predictive variability guarantees that the digital twin stays grounded in statistical reality, even though the R 2 indicates the significant chemical variability of the 63 raw materials. Reliable quantitative prediction must replace point prediction due to the chemical variety present in organic waste flows. The CV parity plot (Figure ) illustrates how the GPR framework successfully distinguishes between stochastic UQ (indicated by a minimized training loss of 0.065) and cognitive UQ by providing predictive variance for each estimate. For highly variable waste flows, the model avoids overconfident predictions by establishing an increased noise threshold (σ = 0.0001). Without requiring thousands of new experimental data points, this probabilistic technique guarantees that the digital twin stays grounded in statistical reality and offers a scalable roadmap for predicting performance across different carbon matrices. By converging with the Matérn 5/2 ARD kernel throughout the Bayesian optimization phase, the model has developed into a numerical simulation of physics. The framework offers a more physically consistent foundation for investigating OOD because it learns fundamental gas–solid interactions, such as first-order rank (ln S BET × ln V total). The model can further close the gap between laboratory accuracy and real-world biomass fluctuations by using atomic markers, such as binding energies obtained from DFT.
