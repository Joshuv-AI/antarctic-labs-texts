Development of a predictive marker panel for the individualised bDMARD therapy of psoriatic arthritis: protocol for a prospective observational study (Integrate-PsA)
Authors: Prem Kant Shekhar, Anna Mai, Dimitra Melides, Christian Schütz, Stefan Schreiber, Ingo Schmitz, Nikolaos Stavropoulos, Falk G Bechara, Eggert Stockfleth, Nina Timmesfeld, Xenofon Baraliakos, Philipp Sewerin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPsoriatic arthritis is a chronic inflammatory disease that can affect the axial skeleton as well as the joints and enthesis. Following the failure of conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), numerous biological DMARDs (b-DMARDs) or targeted synthetic DMARDs are now available, although current guidelines do not recommend a specific mode of action in the absence of comorbidities or contraindications. The aim of the study is to identify markers associated with the achievement of Minimal Disease Activity (MDA) at week 16 in patients treated with tumour necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFi), interleukin-17 inhibitors (IL-17i) or interleukin-23 inhibitors (IL-23i).Methods and analysisA total of 100 patients with active psoriatic arthritis (PsA) with a clinical indication for treatment escalation of methotrexate (MTX) (ie, non-response or intolerance to MTX) to a b-DMARD are to be included in the prospective observational cohort study. Patients are escalated according to physician’s choice to one of the following modes of action: TNFi, IL-23i or IL-17i. Participant recruitment is currently ongoing and is expected to be completed in July 2026. This protocol was finalised prior to database lock and before any outcome analyses were conducted. The primary endpoint is achieving MDA at 16 weeks. Secondary endpoints include changes in clinical scores: Psoriasis Area and Severity Index 75% improvement, Disease Activity in Psoriatic Arthritis, Nail Psoriasis Severity Index and biomarker evaluation from blood and skin biopsy samples. Machine learning techniques will be applied to identify marker patterns associated with treatment response, while treatment-specific marker interactions will be explored on a hypothesis-generating basis.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval was obtained from the Ruhr University Bochum Ethics Committee (no. 23-7918) on 1 December 2024 for study protocol number 2.0 (30 October 2024). Study results will be published in an international journal, regardless of whether the results are positive or negative. Introduction

Background and rationale

Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory skin disease characterised by vascular and epidermal abnormalities.1 Epidemiological studies show that the prevalence of psoriasis in Germany ranges from 2.00% to 2.53% based on population-level data.2 Among individuals with psoriasis, the prevalence of psoriatic arthritis (PsA) varies widely—from 5% to 40%, with a cumulative prevalence of 20.5% after 30 years of psoriasis.3 In the general adult population in Germany, PsA affects approximately 0.2% of individuals.4 Globally, population-based studies report PsA prevalence estimates ranging from 0.001% to 0.42%.5 Overall, around 15% of patients with psoriasis who are cared for by dermatologists have undiagnosed PsA,6 while the annual incidence of PsA in patients with psoriasis is 2%–3% and the incidence of PsA in the general population is unclear.4 The mortality rate in patients with PsA is increased, particularly due to the systemic inflammation and the increased rate of disease-related comorbidities (metabolic syndrome, hypertension, depression, etc),7 which are more severe in untreated patients.

The individual burden of disease in PsA is related to the symptoms caused by inflammation and structural damage. The resulting psychological and functional burden is considerable and is similar in extent to other chronic inflammatory diseases of the musculoskeletal system, such as axial spondyloarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis.8 In addition, PsA is significantly associated with high levels of absenteeism and productivity loss, with up to 54% of patients reporting absenteeism from work due to PsA,9 all of which correlate with the presence of disease activity and physical impairment.10 The disease burden of PsA patients is, of course, also strongly determined or even dominated by skin changes and additional comorbidities. Very important subjective symptoms are itching, skin pain and severe impairment of quality of life due to skin changes. All of these aspects of the disease improve considerably with effective systemic treatment.11

The current treatment of PsA is based on the recommendations of the Group for Research and Assessment of Psoriasis and PsA (GRAPPA12) and the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR13), whereby the Treat-To-Target (T2T) strategy is a well-established and widely accepted approach for the disease-specific goals of this treatment.14 The main goal of T2T is to achieve remission (measured by the Minimal Disease Activity (MDA) score15). If the MDA status is not achieved during treatment, an alternative should be considered after 3 (up to a maximum of 6) months. Although the treatment spectrum for PsA has expanded considerably over the last two decades16 with the introduction of biologic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (bDMARDs), around 35%–50% of patients still fail to achieve clinical remission or even low disease activity. Up to 54% of patients17 have to change their index bDMARD, sometimes several times, within 4 years of starting treatment. The general opinion to date has been that tumour necrosis factor-alpha inhibitors (TNFi) are the current bDMARD standard due to the overall longer experience since their approval. In recent years, however, there has been increasing evidence that the interleukin (IL) 23/17 axis18 plays a dominant role in the development of inflammation of both the skin and joints in PsA, making agents that target these cytokines a more specific treatment option. There is currently no general recommendation as to which of these bDMARDs should be favoured for which PsA patients or in which order they should be administered most effectively. However, there is good data showing that switching from one mode of action to the other makes sense in order to guide patients to the desired goal of a good response using the T2T strategy. Overall, however, more frequent switching between different agents and modes of action is associated with lower response rates and a higher risk of further treatment discontinuations.17

The main reason for these effects is the lack of knowledge about how to predict response to treatment—as is increasingly established in oncology, for example. Two recent network meta-analyses have at least pointed to relevant differences between the mechanisms of action19 20; although no comparisons with Interleukin 23 inhibitor (IL23i) therapy have yet been made. Against this background, the EULAR and GRAPPA have proposed a research agenda as part of their recommendations for the development of PsA therapy. Among other things, the focus was on determining biomarkers in connection with the prognosis and response to treatment. The aim is to identify the right bDMARD for the right patient phenotype—an area that has not yet been consistently investigated. In a recently published systematic review, the evidence regarding the known predictive biomarkers in psoriasis and PsA was summarised.21 Most of the studies listed there looked at individual inflammatory markers or genetic markers. In many cases, the markers were also only analysed in patient collectives with uniform therapy, meaning that the predictive properties of a marker could not be validly investigated, as this always requires several therapy options in order to consider the interaction between therapy and marker. In summary, there were only a few correlations between the analysed markers and a response to therapy, mainly for known markers of disease severity, such as C reactive protein and genetic markers.

There is convincing evidence that the IL-17/IL-23 signalling pathway plays a central role in the pathogenesis of PsA.22 It has also been shown that synovial fluid from PsA patients contains an increased proportion of Th17 cells and IL-17 CD8+T cells (Tc17), both compared with peripheral blood and compared with synovial fluid from patients with other rheumatic diseases of the peripheral joints such as rheumatoid arthritis, in which Th17 but not Tc17 cells are increased. TNF-alpha gene polymorphisms combined with the neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio in the blood appear to predict a response to TNFi in patients with PsA.23 A number of free serum markers of bone and cartilage turnover in PsA and psoriatic spondyloarthritis have been identified as potential disease biomarkers. These include matrix metalloproteinase 3 (MMP-3), Dickkopf 1 (DKK-1), macrophage colony stimulating factor (M-CSF) and osteoprotegerin (OPG). A recent prospective cross-sectional comparative study has confirmed that these four biomarkers are associated with the presence of PsA. MMP-3 and M-CSF proved to be prognostic biomarkers for the presence of psoriasis in psoriatic diseases; they could be useful for screening for PsA in psoriasis patients.24

Clinical markers that have proven to be prognostically relevant, for example, nail dystrophy, scalp lesions and intertriginous psoriasis, can also be taken into account with a view to predicting treatment response.25 However, it is also possible that the duration of disease could be used as a predictor of response to IL23i, as was observed in psoriasis in the GUIDE study.26 27

Ultimately, however, and despite all this preliminary work, longitudinal studies to validate the biomarkers listed for the differential response to different bDMARDs are not yet available.

The long-term goal of research in this field is to support more individualised biologic treatment selection in PsA based on patient-specific marker profiles. In this context, the aim of this study is to contribute to the development of predictive marker strategies for individualised bDMARD therapy in PsA by identifying clinical markers, serological markers through blood tests, and histological/immunohistochemical markers assessed through skin biopsies associated with treatment response in patients initiating TNFi, IL-17i or IL-23i therapy. Exploratory analyses will assess marker–treatment interactions as potential indicators of differential response across biologic classes. Given the observational design, sample size and number of candidate markers, all analyses are considered hypothesis-generating and will require confirmation in independent cohorts or interventional studies.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This is a prospective observational study with three parallel intervention groups (treatment with a bDMARD of the TNFi, IL-23i or Interleukin 17 inhibitor (IL17i) class according to the clinician’s discretion). Participant recruitment is currently ongoing and is expected to be completed in July 2026. This protocol was finalised prior to database lock and before any outcome analyses were conducted.

Study setting

The study is being conducted in two departments of the university hospital of the Ruhr University Bochum, the Rheumatology Center Ruhrgebiet and the Clinic for Dermatology in North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany.

Interventions

Rationale for the choice of comparators

All current guidelines today recommend escalation to a bDMARD after failure of conventional therapy, whereby all available therapy principles are recommended equally, as no good and predictive biomarkers for one or other bDMARD therapy principle are available to date. The decision in favour of one of the three preparations is made according to clinical practice based on the assessment of the treating physicians.

Intervention description

Patients are escalated to a bDMARD due to clinical reasons according to physician’s choice to one of the following modes of action: TNFi, IL-23i or IL17i.

The mode and dose of application will be according to the summary of product characteristics, European Medicines Agency approval and national guidelines.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions

In case of worsening disease, the patient may consult the treating rheumatologist at any time before the final study visit at week 16. Modifying the chosen interventions is defined as drug dose change of the prescribed drug. The switch to another bDMARD class is a discontinuation of the chosen baseline intervention. All changes to the drug prescription at baseline will be documented.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions

Patients are encouraged to take their medication conscientiously. Compliance is assessed and documented during routine visits.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial

Since this is an observational study, all other medications that can be used to treat PsA such as MTX are permitted during the study and are documented in detail at each visit. Other therapeutic measures are also permitted as part of the study and are documented in clinical practice.

Provisions for post-trial care

Patients receive the best possible individualised care in accordance with current guidelines and the best possible clinical assessment by the attending physician. According to clinical routine, the suitability of prescribed medication is checked at each visit and adapted, if necessary, even after the observation period has ended. Provisions for post-trial care and for potential compensation are not applicable.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

The treatment response is measured as a binary outcome (responder/non-responder) based on whether patients achieve clinical remission 16 weeks after starting treatment with a bDMARD. Week 16 was selected as the primary time point because it reflects early treatment response and aligns with clinical decision-making in routine practice. For patients with PsA, the MDA has been an established tool for assessing clinical remission status since the TIght COntrol of Psoriatic Arthritis study. In addition, MDA is considered one of the two preferred treatment goals in the T2T recommendations for PsA and is associated with significantly more favourable radiographic outcomes than in patients who do not achieve this goal.11 Another tool for measuring treatment outcomes in PsA is the Disease Activity in Psoriatic Arthritis (DAPSA).28 We chose the MDA as the primary endpoint in this study because the MDA covers the full spectrum of PsA, including enthesitis, function and skin involvement.

Secondary outcomes

Secondarily, the change in established clinical scores is assessed: the dactylitic finger/toe count,29 the PASI 75%,30 the DAPSA score, the Nail Psoriasis Severity Index (NAPSI).31 Continuous measures of disease activity, such as DAPSA, will be analysed as secondary outcomes to enhance sensitivity for marker discovery.

Changes in PsA medication will be analysed in detail regarding drug, dosage, route of administration and interval. The physician’s global assessment of disease activity (Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) 0–10) is assessed in addition to several patient-reported outcomes: patient global assessment of disease activity and pain (NRS 0–10), the Patient Acceptable Symptom State,32 the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index33 and the HAQ.34

Predictive markers of treatment response

Sociodemographic factors, clinical characteristics, blood/serum biomarkers, gene expression and parameters from skin biopsies are evaluated as potential predictive markers in this study. The compilation of these disease-relevant ‘readouts’ allows the creation of a predictive marker pattern, which makes a therapy success prediction more likely through the combination of peripheral and local markers.

As the field of biomarkers in PsA is currently being heavily researched, a comprehensive scoping review is carried out before the measurements of the biomarkers begin. This allows the proposed biomarker panel to be updated based on the latest knowledge. Subject to this update, clinical markers such as skin involvement will be assessed using various scores such as PASI, BSA, NAPSI and psoriasis-related itching (recorded using the VAS). In addition to the neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio, new and more complex systemic immune inflammation markers such as the Systemic Immune Inflammation Index and the pan-immune inflammation value are also determined from the complete blood count. PsA-relevant serum levels of cytokines (eg, IL-1, IL-2, IL-8, IL-12, IFNg, transforming growth factor beta (TGF-ß)) and biomarkers (eg, sRANKL, human Dickkopf-1 (DKK-1), OPG, sclerostin (SOST)) are collected at baseline and only in selected cases, also analysed at week 16. In addition, previously described predictive markers for therapy success with TNFi are determined (eg, IL-6, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), MMP3, C3) in order to check their significance for the other therapies as well. TNF, IL17 and IL23 are determined both at week 0 and week 16 to monitor the response to therapy. With regard to the molecular skin biomarkers, samples from the lesional skin biopsy will be collected for the study at baseline. Moreover, transcriptome analyses will be performed at week 0 and 16 to determine biomarkers on a genome-wide level and their potential predictive role in this study. Sample size

The aim of the study is to select possible predictive markers in the sense of a marker panel. The markers found are to be validated in a further project following this study. The data are therefore analysed in an exploratory manner. For this reason, an exact estimate of the sample size is not possible. It is planned to include 100 patients in the study.35

Recruitment

The Rheumatology Centre Ruhr is affiliated with all neighbouring rheumatology practices in the Ruhr region in the Rheumatism Research Group Ruhr, which has already successfully conducted investigator-initiated studies (CORRA, StaerkeR) in the past and can therefore make a significant contribution to such a study. In addition, recruitment may additionally take place via dermatological cooperation partners. The target number of 100 patients from two clinics is realistic in view of the recruitment period of 1 year.

Recruitment should fit as well as possible into the respective clinic’s daily routine to ensure feasibility and success. During regular follow-up visits, a study physician screens the eligibility of PsA patients. All necessary data for screening is obtained during the routine visit. If a patient seems eligible, after verbal and written information, written consent for study participation is obtained at screening or at least before the baseline assessments.

Data collection will be the responsibility of the clinical trial staff at the Rheumatology Center Ruhr Area and the dermatological clinics under the supervision of the respective site investigators. Plans for collection, laboratory evaluation and storage of biological specimens for genetic or molecular analysis in this trial/future use

Besides the general medical blood count, additional blood and serum samples are obtained during routine blood drawing using a Lithium/Heparin-coated or uncoated S-Monovette, respectively. Both samples are processed and stored on the day of extraction. Serum samples are centrifuged, aliquoted and immediately stored at −80°C until use. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) are isolated by density gradient separation, aliquoted into freezing media (50% media, 40% fetal calf serum and 10% dimethyl sulfoxide), frozen at −80°C using a Mr. Frosty Freezing Container and transferred to liquid nitrogen on the following day. An aliquot of PBMCs is stored at −80°C as a dry pellet. The processing time for all samples is recorded. For biomarker analysis, state-of-the-art immunological techniques are used such as ELISA-technology, flow cytometry, micro-well-based cell capturing, multiplexing, single-cell and bulk-RNA sequencing, TCR-sequencing and Immunohistochemistry (IHC). Skin biopsies were taken according to clinical routine using a 6 mm punch biopsy tool. All biopsies were fixed in formalin and embedded in paraffin. Tissue sections from these paraffin blocks were stained with H&E to confirm diagnosis of psoriasis. In addition, immunohistochemical analyses will be performed using antibodies against psoriasis-associated cytokines and antimicrobial peptides including IL12, IL17, IL22, IL23, psoriasin and LL37, as well as other potentially suitable biomarkers that may result from the transcriptome analyses of the T helper cell subtypes. Stained IHC sections will be digitalised using the NanoZoomer slide scanner (Hamamatsu) for documentation and standardised evaluation by medical staff.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes

The aim of the study is to develop a predictive biomarker panel for treatment response under the different bDMARDs. The planned analyses of a marker panel for the defined outcomes of treatment response are complex. To address confounding by indication arising from non-random treatment allocation, analyses will adjust for baseline clinical variables that are likely to influence both treatment choice and treatment response, such as disease activity measures, extent of skin involvement, prior biologic exposure and relevant comorbidities. As this is an observational study and randomisation is not feasible, the selected bDMARD class may reflect clinical judgement and patient-specific factors. Therefore, where available, the clinician’s reason for choosing TNFi, IL-17i or IL-23i therapy will be recorded at baseline, including clinical disease pattern, previous treatment history, comorbidities, contraindications and patient preference. In the analysis, we will consider both main effects of clinical and biomarker variables and treatment–marker interactions. This will help us separate general predictors of response, which also adjust for imbalance, from markers that may suggest a differential response to a specific bDMARD class. Given the observational design and planned sample size, these analyses will be interpreted as exploratory, and residual confounding will be acknowledged. The analysis is carried out using machine learning methods, in particular supervised learning methods, such as random forest with subsequent variable selection (feature selection)38 or regression methods with penalisation like Lasso.26 39 40 Unequal group sizes are anticipated, with TNFi expected to represent the largest treatment group, which may limit statistical power for treatment-specific interaction analyses. To reduce the risk of overfitting, model development will consider the available sample size and the expected number of treatment responders. Candidate predictors will be prioritised based on clinical relevance, previous evidence and biological plausibility, rather than relying only on automated data-driven selection. Model complexity will be kept appropriate for the dataset, and internal validation will be performed using resampling methods such as cross-validation or bootstrapping, where suitable. For machine-learning models, tuning and performance assessment will be carried out within the resampling process to reduce overly optimistic estimates of model performance. Given the observational design and available sample size, analyses of treatment–marker interactions aimed at identifying potential predictive signals will be considered exploratory and interpreted with appropriate caution. The analytical framework described above is intended to improve transparency and reduce analytical flexibility; further technical details will be finalised prior to database lock.

To enhance the interpretability of the machine learning models and to clarify the contribution of individual markers and their interactions with treatment,41 42 explainable artificial intelligence techniques, including SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), will be applied. Prior research in PsA has demonstrated that SHAP can be effectively integrated with ensemble learning approaches such as random forests and XGBoost to estimate treatment outcomes, including MDA43 and bDMARD ineffectiveness,44 underscoring its relevance for clinical interpretation. In this study, SHAP will be used to quantify the influence of each clinical, serological and histological biomarker, and to examine how these markers interact with treatment in shaping model predictions. This analysis will support marker selection and contribute to evaluating the biological plausibility of the model outputs. Additionally, the H-statistic will be employed to assess non-additive effects in two-dimensional partial dependence plots, providing an independent means of validating marker-by-treatment interactions identified through SHAP.45

Demographic and baseline variables will be summarised in a table, stratified by intervention groups TNFi, IL-23i and IL-17i. Continuous variables will be summarised using the arithmetic mean and SD; in case of skewed distribution, median and IQR will be used. Categorical variables will be summarised using total counts and percentages. No inferential statistics will be used to compare baseline characteristics.

Discussion

A predictive marker panel for the individualised bDMARD therapy of PsA will be developed in this prospective observational study. Although the study is designed to explore predictive marker signals, the observational design and sample size limit definitive causal inference, and any treatment-specific associations will require validation in independent cohorts. This serves as a prerequisite for a randomised controlled trial to test the prediction of the best therapy after failure of conventional synthetic DMARDs. Personalised therapy strategies could be established; higher remission rates could be achieved by choosing the best therapy option and costs could be reduced.
