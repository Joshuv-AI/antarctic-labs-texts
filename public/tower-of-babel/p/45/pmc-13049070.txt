Machine learning algorithm reveals neurodevelopmental signatures of combined family income and neighborhood disadvantage in adolescents
Authors: Kavari Hercules
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Socioeconomic status (SES) has been linked to brain-based markers, but most studies rely on conventional statistical methods that overlook the complexity and inherent multicollinearity of the brain. We trained elastic net models to predict SES from multimodal neuroimaging data: diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), structural MRI (sMRI), and resting-state functional connectivity (RSFC) data. Neural features independently predicted SES, and demographic information only minimally enhanced performance. The income and multimodal models performed best; accordingly, the best-performing primary model predicted income using multimodal data, achieving AUCs of 0.75 (test) and 0.811 (train) without demographic information and 0.779 (test) and 0.836 (train) with demographic information. The performance of the secondary multimodal models for predicting income had a positive relationship with income disparity; expectedly, the best performing model distinguished between children from the top and bottom ~ 10–20% of income brackets, reaching AUCs of 0.81 (test) and 0.969 (train) without demographic information and 0.863 (test) and 0.986 (train) with demographic information. Among the modalities, DTI was the most discriminative, followed by sMRI. Globally distributed along with executive functioning (EF) and language features were the most discriminative. Multimodal neuroimaging can predict SES, especially income, even without demographic data, and the most discriminative features tended to be measurements of white matter integrity and organization; more globally distributed than isolated to specific regions; and linked to cognitive control and language.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42346-w.

Introduction

The significant and long-lasting impact of socioeconomic status (SES) on one’s health is well documented, as socioeconomic status is associated with a lower life expectancy and a greater incidence of negative health outcomes1,2. Additionally, individuals from lower SES backgrounds have been shown to have an increased risk for the onset of mental health conditions3. Specifically, several studies have shown a well-documented SES achievement gap and difference in neurocognitive profiles between individuals with varying SES levels4–7. When paired with the findings of other studies reporting that students themselves are unlikely to close the gap with persistence and increased learning time as the achievement gap widens, understanding what underlies these disparities becomes even more crucial7,8. Beyond its direct relevance to life outcomes more broadly, a deeper understanding of SES in developmental neuroscience and psychology can enhance the validity, generalizability, and replicability of research findings—bringing us closer to uncovering the subtleties of the mechanisms of brain development9. However, until recently, studies investigating socioeconomic status and, more broadly, social conditions have become increasingly popular in developmental cognitive neuroscience research4.

Despite only recently developing into a branch of developmental cognitive neuroscience research, much has been amassed in terms of knowledge and insight surrounding socioeconomic status and potential brain correlates. For example, one study showed that higher income was associated with increased cortical thickness34. Another study revealed differences in cortical brain volume and surface area, both globally and in specific brain regions, as well as reduced connectivity in certain canonical brain networks in some groups11–13. Similarly, Vargas and colleagues reported that higher levels of neighborhood deprivation were associated with decreased cortical surface area, especially within the prefrontal cortex14. These differences have also been linked to behavioral and functional correlates; for example, researchers have shown associations between family income, prefrontal cortical thickness, and executive function, and others have suggested that some of the most profound effects of SES extend to the entire frontoparietal regions of the developing brain and partially mediate differences in language, spatial, and executive functioning abilities in addition to externalizing and internalizing behavioral problems12,15. In addition, there has also been a substantial amount of research dedicated to the effects SES has on the circuitry implicated in learning and memory, such as the hippocampus; however, overall, there seems to be substantial evidence that disruptions in neural circuits implicated in cognitive control (frontoparietal and frontolimbic networks), language (temporal-parietal networks), and social functioning (salience and limbic networks) may be implicated through factors linked to SES level4,16. Some of these potential SES-linked factors, such as prenatal stress and environmental exposures such as lead poisoning, have also been identified17,18.

Despite the richness of this rapidly growing body of research, much of the research suffers from two major weaknesses: a small sample size and reliance on conventional statistical methods in which neural features are analyzed in a univariate fashion with a given neural feature as the outcome and SES variables along with other demographic variables as the predictors12,14,16,19. While this approach can provide valuable insights, it has key limitations. First, examining features in isolation rather than as part of an intricate, interconnected system often oversimplifies the complexity of the brain20. Moreover, growing evidence shows that social, psychological, and clinical characteristics likely do not map onto isolated regions or connections but instead likely reflect widespread differences in features and connections throughout the entire brain21. Despite these limitations, few studies have explored multiple imaging modalities simultaneously, and to the best of our knowledge, no study has implemented a combination of features from functional and structural MRI modalities in a single analytic step while investigating SES. Conventional statistical methods are not suited for analyzing many highly correlated features in a single analysis because they are prone to loss of statistical power or to overfitting, limiting replicability and generalizability20,22. This impedes researchers from being able to measure the strength and durability of associations while accounting for the influence of other highly correlated features.

In contrast, machine learning methods, particularly multivariate pattern recognition techniques such as elastic net, offer a more comprehensive alternative by simultaneously analyzing highly correlated neural features, and when accompanied by standard machine learning practices such as cross validation and external testing, such methods offer unparalleled generalizability22,23. Using elastic net and similar algorithms, one can easily identify the most predictive brain-wide features of SES, offering more detailed insight into which features are both strongly and reliably associated with SES using a single model22,23. Recently, several researchers have begun to use machine learning techniques such as linear support vector machines and partial least squares regression in the context of SES and neuroscience, but none have used penalized regression techniques such as ridge or elastic net24,25. Nonetheless, regularization techniques such as elastic net are particularly well suited for handling multicollinearity in high-dimensional data, allowing for a more nuanced understanding of the relationship between brain structure and socioeconomic status (SES)21,26,27. Elastic net al.so has the added advantage of being able to shrink the coefficients of nonrelevant features to zero; thus, the final model accounts for the effects of brain features that bear at least some relevance to the outcome variable, providing a final model that is less influenced by noise and closer to reflecting the true nature of relationships of interest22,26,27.

The current project attempts to uncover the neural signatures of social circumstances in the developing brain by using logistic elastic net regression to predict social circumstances or SES from multimodal neural features. We leveraged the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study dataset, the largest long-term study of brain development and child mental health in the United States, which included more than 11,800 participants. Our primary objective was to measure how well brain structure‒function can predict SES at the household level and neighborhood level by predicting combined family income and neighborhood disadvantage based on two neighborhood disadvantage indices, the Child Opportunity Index and the Area Deprivation Index.

Given the associations demonstrated between various neural features and social conditions in prior research, our first and primary aim is to examine the extent to which the brain can predict various measures of social circumstance both with and without demographic information. Our primary hypothesis is that all models, regardless of the SES metric, will at the very least have discriminative ability. In addition, we had specific hypotheses related to performance; in particular, we hypothesized that models predicting income along with multimodal models would perform better than their neighborhood disadvantage and unimodal counterparts because neighborhood disadvantage indices are subjectively designed and weighted for purposes beyond the scope of this study; thus, some information used to formulate the scores may not have traceable neurodevelopmental footprints, and additional neural information is provided by multiple modalities4,28. Finally, we hypothesize that model performance will be consistent across the sexes.

Our second aim is to examine which neural features are leveraged the most by models to make accurate predictions. We hypothesized that structural neural features, particularly cortical surface area, would be the most discriminative based on the vast representation of structural findings in the literature surrounding SES, and this would be reflected in both feature importance across all models and unimodal model performance12,16. Conversely, we believe that the most predictive regions/networks are likely involved in cognitive control and language, namely, the temporoparietal/temporoparietal-occipital junction/area and frontal lobe circuitry, specifically the frontoparietal, frontolimbic, and frontostriatal circuitry.

Finally, our post hoc analysis based on our results explored whether there were differences in neurocognitive and behavioral metrics between the groups with differing social circumstances, as used in the models. We hypothesized that there would be statistically significant differences between the strata used within the machine learning models for various neurocognitive and behavioral metrics related to language and cognitive control.

First, as hypothesized, all our models had discriminative ability; when performed above chance, the area under the curve (AUC) was > 0.5, and the models predicting combined family income were generally better than those predicting the neighborhood disadvantage index despite their similar performance during training (Table 1; Fig. 2). Accordingly, the single best-performing set of primary models tested on our external test set included multimodal models predicting combined family income (brain features only: AUC test = 0.752, AUC train = 0.779; brain features with demographics: AUC test = 0.811, AUC train = 0.836) (Table 1). Furthermore, there was a general pattern with respect to modality; multimodal models regularly performed best, followed by DTI and sMRI, with similar performances and, in the case of the most salient differences, sMRI lagged DTI; and ultimately, RSFC was regularly the worst performer. This was true for models predicting income, but when predicting the COI and ADI with both brain features and demographic information, there were some idiosyncrasies during external testing. This is likely due to the uneven distribution of some demographic parameters within the test set, as the general pattern of multimodal > sMRI and DTI > RSFC described above was observed during the training of these models and during both the training and the external testing of their corresponding brain-only configurations (Table 1). Finally, demographic inclusion enhanced performance but only by approximately 2–4% for each model, with RSFC models being the only exception where external performance improved by nearly 10% each time, which can likely be attributed to RSFC features being less discriminative overall and the corresponding demographic model prioritizing demographic covariates in the predictions to maximize performance, as evidenced by the relatively low unimodal performance and lack of heavily weighted features that were consistent across configurations (Figs. 2 and 3). Finally, all the observed performance patterns were largely maintained when primary models were tested on males and females from the test set individually; though there was slightly greater performance for boys and lower performance for girls than was the case in the present study for the entire test set (Table S1).

Global features

Several of the most heavily weighted features across SES variables—especially for neighborhood disadvantage metrics—were global in nature, reflecting hemispheric or whole-brain patterns rather than specific regions or tracts. These global features were particularly prominent in models for neighborhood deprivation, which relied less on localized structure and more on broad measures such as whole-hemisphere diffusivity, FA, or tract volume. Notably, the cortical thickness of the right hemisphere was one of the most heavily weighted sMRI features for all SES metrics.

Temporoparietal/temporo-parieto-occipital region/junction (TPJ/TPO)

A consistent theme across all SES metrics was the importance of neural features located in the temporoparietal/temporo-parieto-occipital junction. A distinct portion of the area was identified for each SES metric; for income, features of the lateral occipital gyrus were identified; for ADI, features of the inferior parietal gyrus; and for COI, features of the superior temporal gyrus. Similarly, the fiber tract volume of the superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF) was weighted heavily for combined family income models.

Frontoparietal, frontolimbic, and frontostriatal circuitry

Fronto-parietal features were distinctively important in differentiating income groups. The cortical surface area of the medial orbitofrontal cortex and gray matter volume of the frontal pole were among the most heavily weighted features. Additionally, features of the superior cortico-striate fibers, specifically projections into the frontal and parietal lobes, were specifically heavily weighted by the models. Within and between the brain’s cognitive control networks, the cingulo-opercular, fronto-parietal, and salience networks, were the most heavily weighted RSFC features, especially the cingulo-opercular network.

Secondary.

Model Performance.

Income was further explored in a more granular fashion, with multimodal models trained to distinguish between those at the bottom of the sample’s income distribution (levels 1–5; < $5,000 to $34,999) and those at all other levels individually due to the superior performance of the multimodal income models. The models tasked with distinguishing between the bottom 10–20% of the distribution and the most contrasted in terms of income level (levels 9 and 10) outperformed the primary models, which were split by median (Table 1; Fig. 6). However, those closer to the bottom 10–20% (levels 6–8) underperformed the primary models (Table 2; Fig. 6). There was a nearly linear decline in performance as the groups became more similar in terms of income (Fig. 6). Accordingly, the best performing of these models were the two models for classifying children from the top 10–20% from the bottom 10–20% on the income distribution (brain features only: AUC test = 0.81, AUC train = 0.969; brain features with demographics: AUC test = 0.863, AUC train = 0.986; Table 2). However, the AUC was greater than 0.70 when classifying all groups at or above the median, but the AUC decreased by more than 10% when distinguishing the bottom 10–20% from the most similar group and when directly below the median (levels 6–7; $35,000 to $74,999) during external testing both with (AUC = 0.611) and without demographics (AUC = 0.643) compared to the AUC from distinguishing between the preceding group and those at the median income level (level 8; $75,000 to $99,000; Table 2).

Global Features

Even in our secondary models, the top DTI features continued to outweigh the top features of the other modalities. Interestingly, as income level decreased, the features became increasingly more global (Fig. 7). There were no global features present in the top 5 features for any modality in the Top ~ 10–20% model, as they were all region/tract specific (Fig. 7A). However, for all the other groups, global features started dominating the top sMRI and DTI features.

Temporoparietal/temporo-parieto-occipital region/junction (TPJ/TPO)

The TPJ/TPO area had very little importance across income levels, appearing only in the models for Levels 9 and 10; rather, it was very sparse (lateral occipital, default mode network, and temporal SLF). Importantly, the within-network connections of the default mode network were especially predictive in the Level 10 models and were the only RSFC feature that reached the level of importance of the sMRI and DTI features. These connections were also the most important RSFC feature in Level 9 models but were not as important as any sMRI or DTI features.

Frontoparietal, frontolimbic, and frontostriatal circuitry

The frontoparietal and frontostriatal circuitry had more varied importance across income levels. The features of both were highly discriminative in the Level 9 and 10 models, but as the income level decreased, they became increasingly less dominant in the top discriminative features (Fig. 7). For example, features of the pars orbitalis, lateral orbitofrontal cortex, forceps minor, striatal inferior frontal fibers, right putamen, superior frontal gyrus, and cingulo-opercular network were all present in these models. In the Level 6–7 and 8 models, they were somewhat present, but nearly all these features were either hemispheric or sensorimotor processing networks and regions (precentral gyrus, cuneus, precuneus, auditory, visual, and sensorimotor hand/mouth networks) (Fig. 7C-D). However, in these models, features of the pars opercularis, medial orbitofrontal, frontoparietal network, and superior corticostriatal fibers were among the top features.

Behavioral and neurocognitive differences

Across nearly all neurocognitive and behavioral metrics selected based on the features that models found to be the most important for differentiating between levels of a given SES variable, there were statistically significant differences in the performances of the strata for each SES variable used within the machine learning models, with members of the lower strata scoring lower on neurocognitive metrics but higher on metrics measuring behavioral problems (Table 3). Notably, the only metric in which there was no significant difference between participants in different strata was CBCL Internalizing Problems, and this was only true when looking at the COI strata. The greatest differences were seen when comparing the Picture Vocabulary and Oral Reading Test scores between the strata of each metric. Particularly, when comparing the scores of those from the top/bottom ~ 10–20% of the income distribution; notably, an entire standard deviation difference in scores was observed. These findings demonstrate that the neural differences detected by the models not only occur in the neuroimaging data but also in the functional data, which are believed to underlie the corresponding brain features.

Discussion

This study investigated the extent to which neural features can predict social circumstances in young people. We found that models were able to accurately distinguish between different categories of combined family income and neighborhood disadvantage indices using neural features exclusively over two-thirds of the time. A set of expected patterns of performance emerged, such as multimodal models outperforming unimodal models and income models outperforming neighborhood disadvantage models. Along with some unexpected patterns, such as models being able to predict SES metrics for males slightly better than for females, performance has a monotonic relationship with the similarity/contrast of income groups in our secondary models, and unimodal DTI models occasionally outperform other unimodal models and even multimodal models. We found that the feature importance of our models mirrored the modality-based performance patterns of the models and that there is some accuracy in the longstanding belief that the neurodevelopmental effects of SES are global, but some areas may be affected more than others4,5,15. Locally, consistently discriminative neural features were those associated with the frontal lobe, temporoparietal/temporo-parieto-occipital junction area, superior longitudinal fasciculus, superior corticostriatal white matter and other tracts and regions involved in not only cognitive control and language processing, as initially hypothesized but also much more. Our post hoc analysis based on the most important features confirmed that there were neurocognitive and behavioral differences between the strata that were used within the models. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to use a logistic elastic net regression model to predict social markers from neural features and one of the first studies to use machine learning more generally to do so.

First, the neural features were able to predict social circumstances consistently, and our best-performing primary model, multimodal income, had a test AUC of 0.75; on average, across our primary models, the test AUC was approximately 0.70. These numbers were on par with similar studies using other machine learning methods25. There were several expected performance patterns that emerged, such as combined family income models outperforming their neighborhood disadvantage counterparts; this outcome was expected because of the subjective nature of the design, weighting, and selection in the development of these indices, which potentially leads to a number of problems ranging from the misclassification of disadvantaged groups in areas with high home values to simply the choice to include specific variables that may not be as representative of SES as developers may believe which ultimately can cause these indices to not always map as well onto the brain28,29. This finding suggests that income maps onto the brain in a more generalizable way than neighborhood disadvantage indices despite common criticism of income representing only one dimension of SES in neuroscience research; moreover, while this may be true, income is more easily and objectively measured than other dimensions, such as neighborhood disadvantage4. However, there is clear value in studying both as unique neural correlates were identified for both income and neighborhood disadvantage, suggesting distinct neural pathways for different dimensions of SES.

In addition, models including demographic information outperformed those with only neural features, which was to be expected given the well-known racial disparities present in America30. However, the addition of demographic information only marginally improved model performance, often only by a relatively marginal increase, underscoring the predictive power of the brain alone in predicting social circumstances and, in turn, likely the influence of social circumstances on the developing brain or, at the very least, the strength of the relationship between the two. The channels through which socioeconomic status could impact neurodevelopment are many and variable, as the literature has pointed to increased levels of prenatal stress, allostatic load, instances of adverse childhood experiences, and experiences with structural bias that may lead to nutritional and educational disparities that contribute more generally to differences in brain development and health outcomes along SES lines18,31–34.

The first surprising pattern was that the models were better at predicting male SES metrics than female SES metrics. Moreover, models for predicting SES metric levels in males regularly performed slightly better than did those for predicting SES in females, regardless of the inclusion of demographic variables or SES metric, suggesting that the neurodevelopmental impact of SES may be more pronounced in males than in females. The next surprising finding was that performance had a somewhat linear relationship with the similarity/contrast of the groups when predicting income levels in our secondary models. The best performing secondary model without demographic data compared the most disparate groups in terms of income, with a test AUC of 0.81 and a training AUC of 0.969. When paired with performance decreasing, as in the same lowest income group, was compared to more similar income groups, this demonstrated a clear relationship between combined family income and the brain. This finding aligns with prior studies that have shown linear relationships between income and individual neural features4,12,14. Moreover, the substantial (more than 10%) decrease in AUC when distinguishing between those at income levels 6 to 7 and those in the bottom 10–20% compared to differentiating between the former and those at the median income level (Level 8) was also illuminating because it suggests that there is a rather stark difference between the brains of those at or above the median income level both within the sample and in America at large and those below it because the models were not able to identify as many differences to rely on to distinguish between those within the two lowest income groups35.

The final rather astonishing pattern was the superior discriminative power of DTI features compared to features from other modalities throughout the analysis. First, unimodal DTI models outperformed other unimodal models, even though the sMRI models were quite close and even multimodal models were occasionally used. The performance pattern of DTI > sMRI > RSFC was then reflected in the feature weights of the top features across models for each modality. Based on the volume of prior work dedicated to sMRI features, we initially expected these features to contribute the most to the models4,12,13,15,25. However, the dominance of DTI features throughout the analysis suggested that SES may especially impact or be associated with the integrity of white matter microstructure, as the cumulative weight of diffusivity and fractional anisotropy measures were the highest of any measure type, both of which measure the integrity and organization of the white matter microstructure, revealing information about the degree of myelination, cell density, and the orientation of fibers. However, there are a few studies investigating the relationship between DTI features and SES, and there are not as many RSFC features and especially sMRI features4,36. The lack of discriminative power of RSFC in comparison to that of DTI likely reflects the fact that the networks used to measure RSFC are not personalized and rather based on canonical atlases derived by researchers; a recent study explored this further and demonstrated the individual variation in personalized functional network topography with respect to SES24. Collectively, these findings highlight both significant methodological considerations and a potential direction for future studies in the field of SES neuroscience. Moreover, future research should focus more on modalities that were shown to be more predictive as they likely better capture the relationship between SES and the brain, but we should not completely disregard functional measures as further investigation into whether personalized functional can better capture the relationship is warranted.

Although global brain features (whole-brain and hemispheric) dominated more generally, as hypothesized, the most heavily weighted region/tract-specific features were those associated mainly with the temporoparietal/temporo-parieto-occipital junction area along the frontostriatal and frontoparietal circuitry along with their associated canonical networks. While these areas are associated with executive function/cognitive control and language, the most discriminative features tell a more comprehensive story about the salient difference between neurodevelopment and varying SES in multiple ways. First, these areas are not only associated with executive functioning/cognitive control and language but also with a host of other higher-order cognitive processes (e.g., attention, memory, social cognition, decision-making, and emotional processing and regulation)36–39. Additionally, a map of SES footprints was essentially uncovered through the results that started with and/or often led back to the frontal lobe, as frontal regions are connected to parietal, occipital, and temporal regions, including the TPJ/TPO regions, by way of the SLF; connected to the striatum through superior corticostriate fibers; and connected to each other through forceps minor fibers. Considering the connectivity between networks as measured by RSFC and the importance of cingulo-opercular, fronto-parietal, and default mode networks, the path from frontal regions to limbic structures becomes apparent, and the connections to temporal, parietal, and occipital regions, especially the TPJ/TPO area, are further corroborated. Nonetheless, the frontal regions themselves and their connections seem to vary the most with SES along with the TPJ/TPO. However, based on our supplemental analyses, these differences in areas often linked to higher-order cognitive processes may exist mostly between those from the highest and lowest income brackets because, in our secondary models, for the income groups that were more similar in terms of income to the bottom 10–20% of the income distribution networks, regions and white matter associated with higher-order processing started to have a progressively less apparent presence in the most heavily weighted features from each modality and models instead of relying on hemispheric features and features associated with lower-order processing, such as the visual, auditory, and sensorimotor networks42. This was mostly the case only for the models distinguishing between Levels 1–5 and Levels 6–7, but features associated with lower-order processing became increasingly important as the income level we were attempting to distinguish from the very bottom of the income distribution decreased. Nevertheless, the patterns of feature importance aggregated together show that SES is associated not only with differences at the global level (brain-wide/hemispheric) but also with more local factors, particularly in areas associated with higher-order cognitive functions.

Finally, with this diverse set of features identified to be discriminative and the diverse set of functions with which they are associated, our post hoc analysis of neurocognitive and behavioral differences had to mirror this variety. For example, the SLF and TPJ/TPO areas, specifically the inferior parietal gyrus, are both associated with language processing; therefore, we tested for differences in performance between the strata used in the models for the Picture Vocabulary and Oral Reading Tests, and significant differences were observed36,43]. Similarly, the features of brain structures associated with cognitive control, such as the superior frontal gyrus, frontal pole, superior corticostriatal white matter, and cingulo-opercular connections, are regularly highly discriminative across models, and there are significant differences in performance between strata for metrics that measure cognitive control/executive function and attention, such as the Flanker Inhibitory Control and Attention and Dimensional Change Card Sort Tests, along with CBCL behavioral metrics measuring attention and externalizing problems42–44. Some of the other identified features are commonly associated with cognitive functions such as visuospatial processing and episodic and working memory; therefore, we tested for differences in performance on other metrics, such as the Little Man and Matrix Reasoning Tasks, along with the Picture Memory Sequence and List Sort Working Memory Tests, and the same pattern emerged, and the results remained statistically significant47. Additionally, unsurprisingly, the differences in performance and behavior were greater when comparing those from the highest income bracket to those from the lowest income bracket than when just comparing differences between those above and below the median. These findings help reveal some of the neural differences that the models relied on to distinguish between groups in context, verifying what would be expected functionally given the location of the neural differences.

Limitations

Despite the thoroughness of the current work, there are several notable limitations, such as the exclusion of participants due to artifacts and motion. However, head motion in developmental populations is common and a challenge in the field of developmental cognitive neuroscience48. Additionally, this project was cross-sectional, and future longitudinal studies will be necessary to determine causal links with SES in addition to studies aimed at studying neural differences between more granular levels of SES. However, SES is inherently difficult to measure, so all metrics have flaws in that they capture only one dimension of SES, such as combined family income; moreover, they are multidimensional, such as neighborhood disadvantage indices. The selection and weighting of variables are subjective and may not capture important nuances present in diverse populations; moreover, all of these metrics have varying levels of utility in various contexts, but they may not always map to the brain and its development4,28.

Conclusion

Overall, socioeconomic status (SES) leaves a strong and early imprint on the developing brain. Using neuroimaging alone, machine learning models predicted adolescent SES in the ABCD study with ~ 75% accuracy, indicating widespread neural representation of SES. These effects were evident globally, particularly in brain structure and white matter microstructure/connectivity, and locally in regions supporting executive function and language (e.g., frontal cortex and TPO/TPJ). Regional differences were most predictive when distinguishing the lowest versus highest income groups, while more global features became informative as income differences narrowed. Importantly, SES-related neural variation was associated with differences in behavioral problems and neurocognitive performance. Together, these findings underscore the need to consider preventative and ameliorative interventions, with prior work implicating pathways such as nutrition, educational opportunity, prenatal stress, and caregiving quality in shaping SES–brain associations18,31,34.. While others have shown interventions such as poverty reduction cash transfers to low-income families, family-based training programs emphasizing specific cognitive skills and/or parenting techniques, and high-quality early care and education programs were shown to improve brain development and functioning along with behavior in children47–51. Taken together, unfortunately, the avenues through which low SES may negatively impact the brain are plentiful, but this also means there is substantial opportunity for the development of effective intervention.

Combined family income

The ABCD Study asks the parents of participants to report their combined household income by selecting from one of 10 predefined income categories. These categories range from less than $5,000 to $200,000 or more, providing a comprehensive representation of household income levels.

In the present study, participants were further aggregated into a “Low” income group, which consisted of those below the sample median income level (Level 8), and a “High” income group, which included those at or above the sample median income level (Level 8). Notably, the U.S. median household income at the end of 2023, approximately $80,610, falls within the median household income level (level 8: $75,000 to $99,999) within the ABCD dataset, as reported by the U.S. Census Bureau, adding an additional layer of generalizability to any findings35. Models were constructed to differentiate between the “low” and “high” income groups.

To assess the sensitivity of the best performing model, new multimodal combined family income models were trained in which those in the bottom ~ 10–20% of the income distribution within the ABCD dataset were aggregated into one class, and models were trained to distinguish between that aggregated class and classes representing more granular upper income brackets (Top 10–20%: Level 10, Above Median: Level 9, Median: Level 8, Below Median: Levels 6–7) separately to further explore the nature of income’s impact on the developing brain from a more nuanced perspective. The bottom ~ 10–20% of the ABCD income distribution (Level 1: Less than 5,000 to Level 5: $25,000 to $34,999) was also aligned with the bottom 10–20% of the U.S. household income distribution ($18,980 to $33,000)35.

Neighborhood disadvantage

Area deprivation index (ADI)

The area deprivation index (ADI), a validated, multidimensional measure of neighborhood-level socioeconomic disadvantage, was used to quantify neighborhood disadvantage in an effort to provide a more complete picture of SES beyond household income52. The ADI has also been used in prior developmental neuroscience studies as a proxy for SES and neighborhood disadvantage24,53. The ADI ranks neighborhoods on a scale from 1 to 100, with higher values indicating greater socioeconomic deprivation. Rankings are determined based on census tract-level data, incorporating factors such as income, educational attainment, employment status, housing quality, and median home value56. Like in combination with family income, in combination with an ADI, individuals were divided into 2 groups: those with an ADI below the median within the sample and those with an ADI above the median for model training and testing.

Child opportunity index (COI)

The Child Opportunity Index (COI) is another neighborhood-level measure of socioeconomic status that is not as widely used and validated as the ADI but is specifically designed to evaluate factors relevant to healthy child development across three domains: educational opportunity, health and environmental opportunity, and social and economic opportunity28. The educational domain focuses on access to quality early childhood education, proficient elementary schools, and advanced educational programs57. The health and environmental domain assesses neighborhood conditions such as access to healthy food, green spaces, and exposure to environmental pollutants, which significantly impact physical well-being57. The social and economic domain considers factors such as neighborhood poverty rates, employment levels, and housing stability, which influence the overall social environment and resources available to children57. The COI’s comprehensive assessment of neighborhood assets and challenges that affect children’s health and development is particularly suitable for studies focusing on pediatric populations, in contrast to the area deprivation index (ADI), which primarily measures socioeconomic disadvantage without specific emphasis on child-centric factors28. Like income and the ADI, the COI was divided into 2 groups, dichotomized according to the median COI, for model training and testing.

Neuroimaging

We focused on a comprehensive set of functional MRI (resting-state connectivity) and structural MRI (sMRI) (neuroanatomy, diffusion tensor imaging) features available in the ABCD Study. Structural brain features were derived from FreeSurfer parcellations and segmentations and included gray matter volume, surface area, cortical thickness, and cortical folding defined by the Desikan atlas for 34 bilateral regions, along with global measurements totaling 283 features. To account for differences in brain volume, estimated intracranial volume was also extracted from FreeSurfer parcellations and included in predictive models. Similarly, for diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), we used a set of metrics related to white matter microstructure and integrity, also defined by the Desikan atlas for consistency with FreeSurfer structural features, including the following: fractional anisotropy, mean diffusivity, mean diffusivity from parcellations of cortical gray matter, and fiber tract volume for 34 bilateral regions, along with global measurements totaling 328 features. Finally, for functional MRI (fMRI), we used resting-state functional connectivity (RSFC) strength derived from the average correlation defined by the Gordan parcellation for 13 networks with 169 features. A list of all neural features used in the analysis is provided on GitHub. Participants were excluded if they did not have complete imaging data or if the FreeSurfer quality control assessments performed by the ABCD Data Analysis, Informatics & Resource Center (DAIRC) failed. In addition, ABCD conducts a series of imaging preprocessing steps. MR images are corrected for distortions and cross-modality registrations are performed. The cortical surface is reconstructed, and subcortical and white matter regions are segmented using the T1-Weighted sMRI scans. Following this, modality-specific analyses and extracting imaging-derived measures using a variety of regions using a variety of regions of interest (ROI). Lastly, ROI analysis results are compiled across participants and tabulated.

Statistical analyses

Model Selection The “caret”, short for Classification And REgression Training, package in R was used to train Logistic Elastic Net Regression models to predict the levels of each SES metric (income, ADI, and COI). The use of a penalized regression technique, in this instance, elastic net, was selected given its ability to prevent overfitting due to the multicollinearity likely present in large neuroimaging datasets22. While other algorithms can correct for multicollinearity, the elastic net al.gorithm possesses the unique advantage of hybrid use of the L1 and L2 regularization methods, as observed in LASSO and ridge regression, respectively. The flexibility of the algorithm allows for the feature selection seen in LASSO while preserving the stability of ridge regression in the presence of highly correlated features. Furthermore, elastic net has been shown to be effective using neuroimaging data27.

Model Training and Parameter Tuning Data were split into training (80%) and test (20%) sets by familial relationships to ensure that each family was represented in either the test or training set but not both sets, maintaining independence of the test set. Using the training data, each model was trained using 10-fold cross-validation, in which folds were also created based on familial relationships. Although family leakage or the representation of one family in both the training and test sets is often not considered a major concern relative to other forms of data leakage, it is the most relevant to the current analysis and accounts for family structure within one’s cross-validation scheme; moreover, data splitting is generally the best practice when handling large datasets in a machine learning context and has been adopted in other studies58. Nested within the main loop of cross-validation, the hyperparameters (alpha and lambda) for elastic net regression were tuned across several values using a tuning length of 5, resulting in alpha, the hyperparameter that dictates the mix of L1 and L2 regularization penalties being tuned across 5 values ranging from 0.1 to 1, respectively, while lambda, the shrinkage hyperparameter that determines the strength of regularization, was tuned across a grid of 100 values logarithmically spaced for each alpha setting using the R package Caret; see documentation.

Model Configurations We used two model configurations: one model with brain features exclusively as predictors and another model with brain features alongside demographics as predictors. This was done because sociodemographic covariates have the potential to mask the effects of brain correlates, and modeling under both conditions would allow us to observe brain effects that persist in the presence of sociodemographic covariates, underscoring the durability of their association with the given target variable; similar approaches have been used in other research using elastic net59.

Model Evaluation Final model performance was assessed primarily using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) based on the model’s predictions for ~ 20% of the participants assigned to the test set before the models were trained. The training performance, calculated as the average performance across folds accompanied by p values reflecting whether the difference between the observed performance and chance (AUC = 0.5) was statistically significant, was also calculated. All primary models and the best performing secondary model were tested on the full test set and then tested on males and females within the test set individually to assess performance across sex lines.

Feature Engineering The effects of motion were regressed out of all fMRI and dMRI features separately for the training and test sets using mean framewise displacement. Additionally, all brain features were centered and scaled within features prior to model fitting/training and evaluation to ensure that all variables were assessed without scale bias.

Feature Importance Assessment We assessed feature importance by extracting the assigned coefficient values or feature weights assigned by each model for each feature and summing them together. We reported the top 5 features from each modality based on the cumulative weight across all our models.

Unimodal feature weights + multimodal feature weights = cumulative feature weights.

Post hoc neurocognitive and behavioral analysis.

Post-hoc ancillary analyses were conducted on several neurocognitive and behavioral domains that are associated with some of the important neural features identified by the models using metrics from the National Institute of Health (NIH) Toolbox and Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) Problem Behavior Scale. T-tests were conducted on these metrics to test for differences between the groupings that the models were trained to classify: Combined Family Income - Low (Levels 1–7) | High Income (Levels 8–10), Bottom 10–20% (Income Levels 1–5) | Top 10–20% (Income Level 10), Area Deprivation Index (Low/High), and Child Opportunity Index, COI - (Low/High COI).
