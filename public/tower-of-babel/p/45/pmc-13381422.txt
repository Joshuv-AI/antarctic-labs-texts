Exploring the immunomodulatory mechanisms of Astragalus membranaceus in esophageal cancer treatment through network pharmacology, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulation
Authors: Yonglang Zheng, Jianghua Chen, Chen Chen, Kun Chen, Meitong Pan, Lihua Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Esophageal cancer (EC) is one of the most aggressive malignancies with limited therapeutic options and poor prognosis. Astragalus membranaceus (AM), a traditional Chinese herbal medicine with established immunomodulatory properties, has demonstrated anti-tumor potential, but its systematic immunomodulatory mechanisms in EC remain unclear. Here, we employed an integrative strategy combining network pharmacology, survival analysis, and molecular dynamics simulation to elucidate AM's immunotherapeutic mechanisms in EC. We identified 17 active compounds from AM and 445 target genes associated with them, Among these, 113 were classified as immune target genes (ITGs) with potential relevance to EC. Functional enrichment analysis revealed significant involvement of ITGs in TNF signaling, PI3K-AKT signaling, and PD-L1/PD-1 checkpoint pathways.The prognostic model constructed by five ITGs (AHR, AKT1, GPER1, IL4, and MAPK1) can be used as a reliable prognostic feature of EC and was validated in an independent cohort. Protein–protein interaction analysis identified eight core ITGs (TNF, RELA, IL6, NFKB1, JUN, AKT1, TP53, and IL1β) significantly associated with immune cell infiltration, immune checkpoint expression, and immunotherapy response. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation confirmed stable binding of isorhamnetin to AKT1, revealing key interaction residues. These findings suggested the multi-compound, multi-target, and multi-pathway immunomodulatory mechanism of AM in EC, providing a prognostic tool for patient stratification and identifying the isorhamnetin-AKT1 axis as a potential therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05275-8.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer (EC) remains one of the most lethal cancers worldwide. It is estimated that there were approximately 511,000 new cases and 445,000 deaths globally in 2022 [1, 2]. Conventional treatments primarily rely on surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy. Although early-stage patients may be cured through surgical intervention, most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, and the efficacy of conventional therapies is limited, with a 5-year survival rate below 20% [1]. Additionally, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy combined with surgical treatment can induce significant complications, which may lead to markedly reduced quality of life for patients [3]. While targeted molecular therapy has achieved some progress in EC treatment, its efficacy in advanced EC has plateaued, with the objective response rate remaining below 30% [4]. The emergence of immune checkpoint inhibitors, monoclonal antibodies, and adoptive cell immunotherapy has opened new avenues to address this challenge, establishing novel therapeutic strategies [5]. Despite the remarkable potential of immunotherapy, issues such as significant variability in treatment response among individual patients, immune-related adverse events, and toxicities associated with combination therapies remain [6]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore innovative immunotherapeutic approaches for EC.

In recent years, the potential of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) in the treatment of EC has attracted considerable/growing attention [7, 8]. Astragalus membranaceus (AM) is a clinically used herb with over 2000 years of medicinal history in China, known for its effects in replenishing Qi and promoting blood circulation [9]. AM also contains a series of bioactive compounds that exhibit anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties [10, 11], and it has also demonstrated remarkable anti-tumor potential. Mechanistically, it modulates the tumor microenvironment either by inhibiting PD-L1 expression via the TLR4-MyD88 pathway or by preserving the structure of the thymus and spleen to restore immune function [12, 13]. Furthermore, AM extract exerts direct anti-tumor effects by upregulating Bax/caspase-3 and downregulating Bcl-2, thereby inducing apoptosis [14]. Astragaloside IV has been shown to inhibit breast cancer cell invasion by suppressing Vav3-mediated Rac1/MAPK signaling [15]. Previous studies have confirmed the significant role of AM in various tumor types. Astragalus polysaccharides induce apoptosis in non-small cell lung carcinoma cells by inhibiting the Notch1/3 signaling pathway [16]. Yu et al. isolated a novel cold-water-soluble polysaccharide from AM and found that this polysaccharide induces apoptosis in gastric cancer MGC-803 cells via the mitochondrial pathway [17]. Thus, AM serves as a multi-target and multi-pathway anti-tumor herb, and its unique combination of immunomodulatory properties and direct anti-tumor effects endows it with significant value in cancer therapy. Despite these promising findings, EC-specific studies on AM remain limited, and its immunomodulatory mechanisms in EC have not been characterized. This research gap is particularly critical, given the central role of immune dysregulation in EC pathogenesis and the urgent need for effective immunotherapeutic adjuncts.

This study aims to integrate multi-database network pharmacology, survival analysis and molecular dynamics simulation to decode the immunomodulatory mechanism of AM in EC. Our objectives were to: (i) identify immune target genes (ITGs) and active compounds of AM in EC; (ii) construct and validate a prognostic model based on ITGs for patient stratification; and (iii) elucidate the molecular interactions between key compounds and core targets using computational approaches. These findings will provide a theoretical basis for the clinical application of AM in EC immunotherapy.

Materials and methods

Public dataset acquisition

The mRNA expression data and corresponding clinical information for EC were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, project ID: TCGA-ESCA) and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, project ID: GSE19417) databases. Detailed information on these cohorts was provided in Supplementary Table 1. After excluding samples with incomplete survival and clinicopathological data, 231 EC patients were included (TCGA: n = 161; GSE19417: n = 70). Additionally, immune-related genes (IRGs) were retrieved from the ImmPort database (https://immport.niaid.nih.gov), yielding in a final set of 3,123 IRGs (Supplementary Material 1).

Screening of AM active components and targets

The active compounds of AM and their corresponding target genes were identified by screening of the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology database (TCMSP, https://www.tcmsp-e.com/) [18] and the Encyclopedia of Traditional Chinese Medicine (ETCM, http://www.tcmip.cn/ETCM/) [19]. Screening was performed using the criteria of oral bioavailability (OB) ≥ 30% and drug-likeness (DL) ≥ 0.18. The retrieved target genes, defined as proteins, were standardized using the UniProt knowledgebase database (UniProt KB, http://www.uniprot.org/help/uniprotkb). The union of genes obtained from TCMSP and ETCM screenings wasdefined as the target gene set for AM.

Collection of EC-related target genes

EC-related target genes were retrieved from the following databases: DrugBank (https://www.drugbank.ca/), GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM, https://omim.org/), and the Therapeutic Target Database (TTD, http://db.idrblab.net/ttd/). For GeneCards, a relevance score threshold of > 10 was used as the inclusion criterion. Finally, the union of the genes identified from these four databases was established as the EC therapeutic target gene set.

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis

ITGs were identified by intersecting AM targets, EC-related genes, and IRGs using Venn diagram analysis. Subsequently, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed on these ITGs using the DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/). The enrichment results were visualized via a bioinformatics online analysis platform (http://www.bioinformatics. com.cn/).

Establishment and validation of a prognostic model based on ITGs

Based on the identified ITGs, univariate Cox regression analysis was performed in the TCGA cohort to screen for ITGs significantly associated with overall survival (OS) in EC. ITGs with p-value < 0.05 were subsequently entered to multivariate Cox regression analysis to construct a prognostic model. The risk score for each patient was calculated using the following formula: risk score = ( − 1.192)*AHRexpression + ( − 2.479)*AKT1expression + ( − 0.688)*GPER1expression + (3.540)*IL4expression + ( − 2.173)*MAPK1expression. Patients in the TCGA cohort were stratified by the median risk score into low-risk (n = 81) and high-risk (n = 80) groups. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, performed with the "Survival" R package, was used to assess the correlation between risk score and OS. The prognostic model was further validated in the GSE19417 cohort. Additionally, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated using the "survivalROC" R package to evaluate the prognostic performance of the model and compare it with clinical characteristics such as age, gender, grade, and stage.

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network and traditional chinese medicine regulatory network

A PPI network of the potential ITGs was constructed using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/), with a confidence score threshold ≥ 0.9, and visualized in Cytoscape (v3.9.1). Topological analysis of the PPI network was performed using the CytoNCA plugin in Cytoscape to identify core ITGs. As reported previously, the screening method used the medians of six topological parameters as cutoff values [20]. Finally, a TCM regulatory network was constructed and visualized in Cytoscape based on the active components of AM from TCMSP and their targeting relationships with ITGs.

Correlation analysis between core ITGs and EC immune features

The "CIBERSORT" R package was used to estimate the relative infiltration proportions of 22 immune cell types [21]. Samples with p < 0.05 were retained for subsequent analyses. The transcriptional profiles of 15 immune checkpoint genes were extracted from the TCGA cohort as previously described [22]. Tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) scores, which predict immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) response based on T-cell dysfunction and exclusion signatures, were calculated using the TIDE online platform (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu) [23]. Finally, Spearman’s correlation analysis was performed to evaluate associations between core ITGs, immune cells infiltration, immune checkpoints, and immunotherapy responses.

Molecular docking

The 3D structures of the receptor proteins corresponding to the core ITGs were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB, https://www.rcsb.org/) and saved in PDB format. Their PDB IDs were as follows: 8ZUI (TNF), 7LEU (RELA), 8QY5 (IL6), 7LFC (NFKB1), 5T01 (JUN), 7NH4 (AKT1), 8E7B (TP53), and 8C3U (IL1β). Chemical structures of active compounds from AM were obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) in SDF format. Molecular docking (blind docking) between the active compounds and the 3D structures of the core ITG receptor proteins was performed using the DockThor online platform (https://dockthor.lncc.br/v2/), followed by visualization of low-binding-energy conformations.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

MD simulation were conducted using GROMACS 2022 with AMBER14SB/GAFF force fields and the TIP3P water model. Systems underwent energy minimization, NVT/NPT equilibration (100 ps each), and 100-ns production runs (triplicates) at 298 K/1 bar. Trajectories were analyzed using VMD/PyMOL for RMSD/Rg/hydrogen bonds, and g_mmpbsa for MM-PBSA binding free energy calculations.

Results

From TCMSP and ETCM databases, 17 active compounds and 445 target genes were identified for AM. Intersection with 2744 EC-related genes (from GeneCards, OMIM, TTD, DrugBank) and 3123 IRGs (from ImmPort) yielded 113 ITGs (Fig. 1A–C, Supplementary Tables 2–3, Supplementary Material 1). Next, pathway enrichment analysis of ITGs was performed to explore the biological pathways of AM in the treatment of EC. GO analysis revealed significant enrichment in response to lipopolysaccharide, membrane raft, and cytokine receptor binding (FDR < 0.05, Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table 4). The ITGs serve as a reliable prognostic signature for EC

We explored the prognostic value of ITGs in the TCGA cohort. Univariate Cox regression analysis identified five ITGs significantly associated with EC prognosis (p < 0.05, Fig. 3A). IL4 was associated with high-risk prognosis (HR > 1, p < 0.05), while AHR, AKT1, GPER1, and MAPK1 were associated with a low-risk prognosis (HR < 1, p < 0.05). In these ITGs, final prognostic risk models were constructed by multivariate Cox regression analysis. Risk scores were calculated using regression coefficients from multivariate Cox analysis (Supplementary Table 6). The ITG prognostic model demonstrated superior performance over traditional clinical factors in predicting overall survival (AUC = 0.750, Fig. 3B). Based on the median risk score, patients were stratified into low-risk (n = 81) and high-risk (n = 80) groups. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed a significant difference in OS between the two groups (Fig. 3C, p < 0.001), with the high-risk group exhibiting worse OS. This finding was independently validated in the GSE19417 cohort (Fig. 3D). Meanwhile, ‌to further demonstrate the overall prognostic potential of ITGs‌, we also performed Kaplan–Meier survival analysis for another set of five ITGs (‌CDK6, FOS, GPER1, JUN, and NFKB1‌) that were also significantly associated with OS (p < 0.05) in univariate Cox analysis. The results confirmed their significant correlation with patient prognosis (Fig. 4A–E). Association of core ITGs with immune characteristics

To explore the role of core ITGs in EC, we investigated their associations with immune characteristics, including immune cell infiltration, immune checkpoints, and responses to ICI therapy. Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed significant associations between core ITGs and 22 immune cell types (Fig. 6A). IL1β positively correlated with Neutrophils and Mast cells activated, and negatively with Mast cells resting. B cells memory positively correlated with TNF, RELA, and NFKB1. Notably, core ITGs TNF, IL6, and NFKB1 exhibited significant positive correlations with all immune checkpoint genes (Fig. 6B). Elevated TNF/IL6/RELA/NFKB1 expression correlated with higher TIDE scores (indicating poor ICI response) and dysfunction scores (reflecting T cell exhaustion), suggesting these ITGs may mediate immune evasion in EC (Fig. 6C). Taken together, these results indicated that core ITGs were key mediators within the tumor immune microenvironment and were closely associated with immunotherapy responses. Molecular dynamics simulation validation

Binding free energy quantifies the stability of receptor-ligand interactions; lower values indicate stronger binding. Given isorhamnetin’s lowest binding energy with AKT1, we selected the isorhamnetin-AKT1 complex for MD simulation. Root mean square deviation (RMSD) analysis indicated initial fluctuations (0-20 ns) that stabilized over time, demonstrating the complex’s structural convergence and stability during simulation (Fig. 8A). The radius of gyration (Rg), a measure of overall compactness, remained stable (2.19–2.22 nm) throughout the simulation, confirming the complex’s structural integrity (Fig. 8B). Superimposed simulation snapshots (Fig. 8C) showed good overlap, with the small molecule consistently binding to the protein. Buried solvent-accessible surface area (Buried SASA) further confirmed stable protein–ligand interactions (Fig. 8D). Analysis of binding forces revealed stable hydrophobic interactions (VDW) and electrostatic interactions (ELE) (Fig. 8E), with hydrogen bonds fluctuating between 0 and 4 during simulation (Fig. 8F). The isorhamnetin-AKT1 complex structure (Fig. 8G) showed key interactions: hydrogen bonds with TYR-272; and Pi-Cation, Pi-Pi Stacked, Pi-Pi T-shaped, Pi-Alkyl hydrophobic interactions with TRP-80, TYR-272, LEU-264, LEU-210, and LYS-268. Discussion‌

AM, recorded in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia as a superior Qi-replenishing herb, has a clinical history spanning over two thousand years [24]. In recent years, AM has been reported to play significant roles in various types of tumors, including breast cancer, gastric cancer, and colorectal cancer [15, 17, 25]. This study integrated multiple database analysis, network pharmacology, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulation methods, systematically elucidating the immune regulatory mechanism of AM on EC. Our study identified 17 active AM compounds and 113 ITGs of AM in the treatment of EC, revealing that AM exerts anti-tumor effects primarily through tumor immune-related pathways. Subsequently, from these 113 ITGs, we screened out five genes significantly associated with the prognosis of EC and constructed a prognostic model based on them. This model demonstrated robust performance in an independent validation cohort, further confirming the important role of ITGs in evaluating EC prognosis. Finally, molecular docking and MD simulation further validated the stable binding of key AM compounds to core ITGs, providing molecular-level evidence for their interaction. The research results indicated that AM exerts its anti-tumor effects through a multi-compound, multi-target, and multi-pathway network, offering a novel immunotherapeutic strategy for EC.

In this study, the immune-related target genes of AM were enriched in multiple tumor immune-related pathways, including the PD-L1 expression and PD-1 checkpoint pathway in cancer. The interaction between PD-1 and its ligand PD-L1 is recognized as key immune checkpoint molecules [26]. The interaction between PD-1 and PD-L1 negatively regulates adaptive immune responses primarily by inhibiting the activity of effector T cells and enhancing the function of immunosuppressive regulatory T cells (Tregs), thereby facilitating tumor immune escape [27]. Notably, we found a significant positive correlation between core ITGs and immune checkpoint genes. Additionally, restoring T-cell activity by blocking PD-1/PD-L1 to enhance anti-tumor immunity has achieved remarkable success [28, 29]. Multiple studies report that AM or its extracts can directly or indirectly modulate anti-PD-1 activity and block the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway, exerting anti-tumor immune effects [30–32]. Similarly, the NF-κB signaling pathway plays an important role in the tumor microenvironment and cancer progression [33]. In vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that astragaloside IV can block NF-κB degradation, prevent nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65, and reduce the secretion of IL-6 and TNF-α, thereby alleviating the immunosuppressive microenvironment [34, 35]. Furthermore, positive correlations were observed among RELA, IL6, TNF, TIDE, and dysfunction scores, suggesting that AM-mediated inhibition of the NF-κB/IL-6 axis may reverse immune escape in EC. Therefore, AM may enhance anti-tumor effects by regulating core ITGs and participating in tumor immune-related pathways.

This study employed network pharmacology methods to screen and predict the active ingredients of AM, and analyzed the potential immune mechanisms of AM in treating EC. A total of 17 active compounds and 445 target genes were identified for AM, including 113 ITGs related to EC. The component-target network revealed that even a single active component can act on multiple targets, indicating that AM exerts anti-EC effects through multiple pathways and targets. We also screened out 4 key compounds (quercetin, kaempferol, isorhamnetin, formononetin) with degree values ≥ 10. Quercetin, a known dietary antioxidant, has been shown to inhibit EC cell invasion and angiogenesis by downregulating VEGFA, MMP2, and MMP9 [36]. Kaempferol, isorhamnetin, and formononetin have also been reported to exhibit effects in EC [37–39]. The PPI network analysis identified 8 core ITGs, among which AKT1 exhibited high affinity for all 4 key compounds. Prior research indicates that AKT1 activation is associated with poor prognosis in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [40]. Notably, the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway was significantly enriched. The PI3K-AKT pathway is crucial for growth, cell proliferation, and metabolism [41], and its dysregulation is implicated in EC proliferation and invasion [42, 43]. Yang et al. demonstrated that luteolin inhibits the PI3K-AKT pathway, enhancing the chemosensitivity of taxol-resistant esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [44]. Molecular docking results showed that the 4 key AM compounds exhibited binding affinity with these core ITGs, with the lowest binding energy observed for isorhamnetin with AKT1. MD simulation further confirmed the stable binding of isorhamnetin to AKT1, with the complex maintaining acceptable molecular conformation and binding stability during the simulation. These findings suggest that isorhamnetin and AKT1 may be the primary active component and target of AM in treating EC.

While the MD simulations in this study focused on the high-affinity interaction between isorhamnetin and AKT1, this does not negate the hypothesis of multi-component synergy. On the contrary, our ‌component-target network analysis reveals a more complex, four-component synergistic flavonoid network‌. Firstly, the four key compounds may achieve functional superposition or amplification by targeting ‌different nodes within the same signaling pathway‌. Taking the PI3K-AKT pathway as an example, AKT1, as a core hub, may be targeted by multiple compounds simultaneously. Quercetin exerts therapeutic effects in endometrial cancer by downregulating AKT1 expression [45]. Similarly, isorhamnetin inhibits the proliferation and metastasis of CCA cells by regulating AKT1 [46]. Secondly, ‌different compounds may exhibit complementary effects by preferentially modulating distinct pathways‌. Quercetin may more potently regulate inflammation and apoptosis-related pathways, kaempferol may primarily affect the cell cycle, while isorhamnetin and formononetin may exert stronger effects on metabolic regulation [47–50]. Additionally, future studies should evaluate whether these compounds exhibit pharmacokinetic interactions, such as mutual promotion, or competitive antagonism for the same target protein pocket in combination administration models. In particular, ‌it will be critical to investigate whether quercetin and isorhamnetin bind to the same region of AKT1, and whether their structural differences induce distinct conformational changes in AKT1, leading to differential downstream signaling outputs‌. This will provide a theoretical basis for the development of standardized, fixed-ratio innovative Chinese medicine compounds based on AM extracts.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, while network pharmacology predicted the pharmacological effects of the TCM, the identified active compounds often suffer from low oral bioavailability, first-pass effects, and short plasma half-lives, presenting significant challenges for clinical translation [51]. Secondly, the findings of this study are mainly derived from computer simulation analysis and network pharmacology predictions. While the integration of multi-source databases and molecular dynamics simulation provides robust theoretical evidence and mechanistic hypotheses, the interactions between AM active compounds and core ITGs, as well as the associated immunomodulatory pathways, await further experimental validation. Critically, computer simulation analysis does not elucidate whether binding leads to activation or inhibition of the biological function of the target protein.‌ The precise pharmacological direction of the identified compounds requires subsequent cellular and animal studies. Nevertheless, the findings of this study also provide the molecular basis for the immunotherapy of AM against EC. Finally, the ITGs prognostic model, while robust in the TCGA and GEO cohorts, requires validation in larger, multicenter, prospective studies.

Conclusions

In summary, this study ‌suggests‌ a potential multi-compound, multi-target immunomodulatory mechanism of AM in EC ‌through integrated bioinformatics and computational simulation, highlighting the isorhamnetin-AKT1 interaction as a ‌promising axis for future investigation. AM may act on core immune genes through quercetin/kaempferol/isorhamnetin/formononetin, and play an anti-tumor effect through the TNF/NF-κB/PI3K-AKT pathway. The construction of an ITGs prognostic model further demonstrated its significant role in EC prognosis. These findings suggested AM as a complementary agent to enhance ICI efficacy and provided a basis for personalized EC immunotherapy. Future studies should validate the isorhamnetin-AKT1 interaction in EC organoid models and assess the prognostic model in prospective ICI-treated cohorts.
