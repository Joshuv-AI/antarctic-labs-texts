Trajectories of Serum Total Carbon Dioxide and the Risk of Cardiorenal Outcomes in Chronic Kidney Disease: Results From the KNOW-CKD Study
Authors: Semin Cho, Wookyung Chung, Young Youl Hyun, Jong Cheol Jeong, Seung Hyeok Han, Sue Kyung Park, Kook-Hwan Oh, Ji Yong Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rationale & ObjectiveSerum bicarbonate concentrations vary over time in chronic kidney disease (CKD), but most studies have focused on baseline values rather than longitudinal trajectories. We aimed to determine the association between long-term patterns of serum total carbon dioxide (tCO2) and adverse renal and cardiovascular outcomes.Study DesignLongitudinal cohort study.Setting & ParticipantsPatients with CKD enrolled in a prospective cohort with repeated tCO2 measurements.ExposuretCO2 trajectories identified using group-based trajectory modeling: Low, Intermediate, High, and Improving.OutcomeRenal composite outcome and major cardiovascular events.Analytical ApproachCox proportional hazards models adjusted for demographic, clinical, and laboratory covariates.ResultsCompared with the Intermediate group, the Low trajectory group had higher risk of renal events (HR, 2.08; 95% CI, 1.25-3.46). The High trajectory group showed a trend toward lower renal risk (HR, 0.57; 95% CI, 0.31-1.03), with time-dependent protective effects during prolonged follow-up. The Improving group showed no significant differences in renal or cardiovascular risk but demonstrated a numerically lower incidence of the renal endpoint. Cardiovascular outcomes did not differ across trajectories.LimitationsObservational design and potential residual confounding.ConclusionsPersistent low tCO2 was associated with increased renal risk, whereas high levels conferred delayed protection. The Improving group showed intermediate risk. Longitudinal monitoring of tCO2 may help guide individualized bicarbonate therapy in CKD.

Renal function, typically assessed by estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), declines over time in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), although the rate and pattern of decline vary widely among individuals.1 Metabolic acidosis is a common biochemical abnormality in CKD and has been linked to adverse renal and systemic outcomes, including accelerated kidney function loss, bone disease, muscle wasting, and increased morbidity.2,3 However, the clinical implications of low serum bicarbonate concentrations, particularly in earlier stages of CKD, remain incompletely understood.

Over the past two decades, randomized controlled trials evaluating alkali therapy in CKD have produced heterogeneous and sometimes conflicting results.4 Although early single-center studies suggested that bicarbonate supplementation could slow eGFR decline and delay kidney failure,5 more recent multicenter or pragmatic trials have reported neutral or modest benefits, especially in patients with preserved or mildly reduced kidney function.6 Differences in study populations, baseline eGFR, severity of acidosis, bicarbonate targets, intervention duration, and background CKD care likely contribute to these discrepancies. Recent reviews have emphasized the heterogeneity of metabolic acidosis in CKD and variable treatment responses across patient subgroups.7 These insights have informed the 2024 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) CKD guideline, which now recommends a more individualized approach rather than routine correction of mildly reduced bicarbonate concentrations.8

In this context, reliance on a single bicarbonate measurement may inadequately capture the dynamic nature of acid–base status in CKD. Serum bicarbonate concentrations fluctuate over time in response to kidney function, dietary acid load, nutritional status, comorbid conditions, and clinical management, and such longitudinal patterns may provide prognostic information beyond cross-sectional values.7,9 Nevertheless, most observational studies have focused on baseline or time-averaged bicarbonate concentrations, limiting insight into temporal changes and their clinical relevance.

Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) offers a data-driven approach to identify heterogeneous longitudinal patterns by clustering individuals with similar biomarker trajectories.10 Applying this method to repeated serum total carbon dioxide (tCO2) measurements may better characterize the dynamics of metabolic acidosis and their prognostic significance in CKD while also helping to bridge inconsistencies between interventional trials and increasingly individualized guideline recommendations.

Accordingly, we sought to identify distinct longitudinal trajectories of serum tCO2 in a large prospective CKD cohort and to examine their associations with renal and cardiovascular outcomes, with a focus on patients with predominantly preserved kidney function.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This study utilized data from the KoreaN Cohort Study for Outcome in Patients With Chronic Kidney Disease (KNOW-CKD), a prospective, multicenter cohort study enrolling predialysis patients with CKD stages 1-5 from 9 major university-affiliated hospitals in Korea. The detailed cohort design has been published previously.11 From the initial 2,238 participants recruited between 2011 and 2016, we included 1,233 patients with ≥3 eGFR measurements and repeated serum tCO2 measurements during follow-up. Patients with baseline eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2 or observation period <1 year were excluded to minimize confounding from advanced CKD and ensure sufficient data for trajectory modeling.

The study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards of all participating centers, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki principles.

Clinical and Laboratory Measurements

Baseline demographic and clinical data, including age, sex, body mass index, smoking status, cause of CKD, and comorbid conditions, such as diabetes mellitus and hypertension, were collected using standardized case report forms. Diabetes mellitus was defined by fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dL, history of diabetes diagnosis, or use of antidiabetic medications. Hypertension was defined by systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg, diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg, or use of antihypertensive medications.

Blood samples were obtained after an overnight fast (≥8 hours) at baseline and follow-up visits. All laboratory measurements, including serum tCO2, were obtained from venous blood samples collected during routine outpatient assessments. tCO2 (used as a surrogate marker for serum bicarbonate), along with hemoglobin, albumin, uric acid, calcium, phosphorus, intact parathyroid hormone, C-reactive protein, and total cholesterol were measured at certified laboratories of each center using standard automated assays. Serum creatinine was measured using isotope dilution mass spectrometry-traceable methods, and eGFR was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) creatinine-based equation.12

Urine samples (second voided or random) were collected for measurement of protein and creatinine excretion, expressed as urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio (in grams per grams creatinine).

Study Outcomes

The primary outcome was renal events, defined as a composite of end-stage kidney disease (initiation of dialysis or kidney transplantation) or sustained 50% decline in eGFR from baseline. Secondary outcomes included major adverse cardiovascular events, such as myocardial infarction, stroke, hospitalization for heart failure, and cardiovascular death, as well as all-cause mortality.

Group-Based Trajectory Modeling

To evaluate longitudinal patterns of serum tCO2 concentrations during follow-up, we performed GBTM using the gbmt package in R. GBTM is a specialized statistical method that identifies subpopulations within a cohort that follow similar developmental trajectories over time. This approach enables characterization of heterogeneous response patterns that may not be captured by conventional regression models using baseline or average values.13,14

tCO2 measurements were treated as continuous variables and modeled over time (in years) since baseline. We tested models with 2-7 trajectory groups, allowing for various polynomial shapes (linear, quadratic, and cubic) for each group (Fig 1). Model selection was based on multiple information criteria, including Akaike information criterion, Bayesian information criterion, corrected Akaike information criterion, Hannan–Quinn information criterion, and sample-size adjusted Bayesian information criterion. Models with lower values for these criteria were considered better fitting. In addition to statistical goodness-of-fit, we considered model parsimony, adequate group size (>5% of total cohort), and interpretability of trajectory patterns in the clinical context. The final model was selected based on the overall balance between statistical fit and clinical interpretability.Figure 1Statistical metrics for model selection. GBTM classifies individuals according to shared longitudinal patterns, incorporating not only average tCO2 concentrations but also temporal directionality, stability, and within-person variability. Accordingly, overlap in absolute tCO2 values across trajectory groups is expected. Participants classified in the Low trajectory group may transiently exhibit tCO2 values within the high-normal range, yet remain characterized by persistently lower or unstable patterns over time. Similarly, the High trajectory group includes individuals with consistently elevated or upward-shifted trajectories, even when individual measurements exceed conventional thresholds. All trajectory analyses were restricted to participants with ≥3 repeated tCO2 measurements during the follow-up period to ensure robust estimation of individual-level patterns.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) depending on distribution, assessed by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance or Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables and χ2 or Fisher exact tests for categorical variables.

Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between tCO2 trajectory groups and clinical outcomes. Multivariable models were adjusted for age, sex, smoking, body mass index, diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, baseline eGFR, medications (renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system blockers, calcium channel blockers, β-blockers, statins, and diuretics), hemoglobin, albumin, cholesterol, and proteinuria. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals and was met. Two complementary Cox modeling strategies were applied. First, conventional Cox proportional hazards models compared each trajectory group with the Intermediate group as the reference category. Second, models allowing for group-specific baseline hazards were fitted, in which each trajectory group was permitted its own baseline hazard function. In these models, HRs reflect relative risk compared with the overall baseline hazard rather than a single reference group.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted stratified by baseline eGFR levels and observation duration to evaluate the consistency of the associations.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Subcomponents of Renal Outcomes

The trajectory-related risks were consistent in analyses of renal outcome subcomponents. For the outcome of serum creatinine doubling or eGFR halving, the Low group had higher risks compared with the Intermediate group, whereas the other groups did not exhibit statistically significant differences (Table S1). Similarly, for initiation of renal replacement therapy, the Low group had the highest incidence and risk, whereas the Improving group showed no excess risk (Table S3). Group-specific hazard comparisons based on individual baseline hazards produced results consistent with those of the composite renal outcome (Tables S2 and S4).

Cardiovascular Outcomes

For cardiovascular events, no statistically significant associations were found between tCO2 trajectory groups and outcomes after full adjustment (model 4; Table 4). In group-specific comparisons (Table 5), the High group showed a nonsignificant trend toward reduced risk (HR, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.38-1.75; P = 0.60), and the Low group’s risk was not elevated (HR, 1.04; 95% CI, 0.52-2.12; P = 0.91). Similarly, the Improving group did not differ significantly from the Intermediate group (HR, 0.64; 95% CI, 0.21-1.92; P = 0.42). Sensitivity Analyses

To assess the robustness of the observed associations, sensitivity analyses were performed in several subgroups. Among patients with baseline eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2, overall trends were preserved, with the Low trajectory group continuing to exhibit a higher risk of renal outcomes (HR, 6.22; 95% CI, 2.04-18.95; P < 0.001; Table S5), although statistical significance was attenuated due to the limited number of events. In the subgroup with baseline eGFR ≥30 mL/min/1.73 m2 and an observation period ≥2 years, the High group demonstrated a significant renal benefit (HR, 0.55; 95% CI, 0.30-1.00; P = 0.05; Table S7), supporting the long-term association between favorable tCO2 trajectory and renal preservation. Similar findings were observed in patients followed for ≥3 years (HR, 0.54; 95% CI, 0.30-0.98; P = 0.04; Table S9) and ≥5 years (HR, 0.51; 95% CI, 0.28-0.94; P = 0.03; Table S11), with progressively stronger associations over time. Conversely, the Low trajectory group consistently showed elevated risks across all subgroups (Tables S5-S12). Group-specific hazard comparisons based on individual baseline hazards yielded results consistent with trajectory group comparisons (Tables S5-S12). These results reinforce the role of sustained metabolic stability, as reflected by longitudinal tCO2 patterns, in mitigating renal deterioration in patients with CKD.

Discussion

In this large, longitudinal CKD cohort, we identified 4 distinct serum tCO2 trajectories—Low, Intermediate, High, and Improving—using GBTM. These trajectories were independently associated with long-term renal outcomes. Patients in the Low tCO2 group had the highest risk of adverse renal events, whereas those in the High group demonstrated protective associations. Notably, patients in the Improving group, characterized by rising tCO2 concentrations over time, exhibited intermediate outcomes, suggesting that partial correction of metabolic acidosis may be clinically meaningful. These findings were robust across sensitivity analyses and model specifications, including models allowing for group-specific baseline hazards.

Randomized controlled trials of alkali therapy over the past 2 decades have reported heterogeneous results, ranging from early studies suggesting renoprotective effects to more recent trials showing neutral or modest benefits.7,15 Differences in baseline kidney function, bicarbonate targets, intervention intensity, and overall study design likely explain these discrepancies and have informed the more individualized recommendations of the 2024 KDIGO CKD guideline. In this context, our findings provide complementary observational evidence by demonstrating that longitudinal patterns of acid–base status, rather than single measurements, are associated with renal prognosis in routine clinical practice.

A substantial body of observational literature has consistently linked low serum bicarbonate concentrations to adverse renal outcomes, including accelerated eGFR decline, progression to kidney failure, and mortality.4,5,16 In the Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort (CRIC) cohort, patients with serum bicarbonate concentrations <22 mmol/L had significantly higher risks of kidney failure and death compared with those with higher concentrations.4 However, most prior studies relied on baseline or sparsely repeated measurements, potentially underestimating the dynamic nature of acid–base balance. By contrast, our trajectory-based approach captures the longitudinal evolution of tCO2, allowing discrimination between persistently low levels, stable metabolic homeostasis, and improvement over time, each associated with distinct renal risk profiles.

Randomized trials targeting metabolic acidosis have yielded mixed renal outcomes. In the phase 3 VALOR-CKD trial, treatment with the hydrochloric acid–binding agent veverimer in 1,480 patients with CKD and metabolic acidosis (eGFR 20-40 mL/min/1.73 m2) did not reduce the risk of a composite renal endpoint, including kidney failure requiring kidney replacement therapy, sustained ≥40% eGFR decline, or kidney failure-related death, compared with placebo.17 This null result has been attributed, at least in part, to inadequate separation of serum bicarbonate concentrations between treatment groups.7 These findings suggest that renal risk may be more closely related to the chronicity and trajectory of metabolic acidosis than to modest short-term changes in bicarbonate achieved in interventional trials.

Our results are consistent with prior studies applying trajectory-based methods in nephrology. Coresh et al18 demonstrated that distinct eGFR decline trajectories were differentially associated with adverse outcomes, whereas Hsu et al19 reported that blood pressure trajectory groups predicted CKD progression. Together, these studies support the concept that trajectory-based classification provides prognostic information beyond cross-sectional assessments.

The persistently elevated risk observed in the Low tCO2 group is of particular concern. These patients not only started with low bicarbonate levels but remained acidotic throughout follow-up, suggesting cumulative deleterious effects of sustained metabolic acidosis. Proposed mechanisms include tubulointerstitial injury, increased endothelin production, and complement activation.20, 21, 22 In contrast, patients in the High group maintained stable and higher tCO2 concentrations and experienced the most favorable renal outcomes, supporting the renoprotective role of preserved acid–base homeostasis.

Several biological pathways may underlie the adverse outcomes associated with persistently low tCO2. Chronic metabolic acidosis promotes muscle protein catabolism and sarcopenia, potentially worsening metabolic reserve and accelerating CKD progression.23 Acid retention has also been linked to bone demineralization, insulin resistance, and systemic inflammation, further exacerbating kidney dysfunction and cardiovascular risk.24,25 Experimental studies have shown that correction of acidosis with oral bicarbonate can attenuate renal fibrosis and slow CKD progression.26

The Improving group represents a clinically important intermediate phenotype. Despite low baseline tCO2 concentrations similar to those of the Low group, these patients demonstrated sustained increases over time and experienced significantly lower risks of adverse renal outcomes. This finding suggests that even partial or delayed improvement in metabolic acidosis may confer renal benefit and highlights a potentially modifiable window during which timely improvement of acid–base status may attenuate CKD progression.

In contrast to renal outcomes, the association between bicarbonate levels and cardiovascular outcomes appears more heterogeneous. Prior studies have reported conflicting associations, with increased cardiovascular risk observed at both low and high bicarbonate levels.4,27 In a kidney transplant cohort, metabolic acidosis 1 year after transplantation was independently associated with higher risks of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality.28 Conversely, analyses from the CRIC study reported increased incident heart failure among participants with higher serum bicarbonate concentrations (>26 mmol/L).27 In the present study, however, longitudinal tCO2 trajectories were not significantly associated with overall cardiovascular events, and no excess cardiovascular risk was observed in the High tCO2 group. These discrepancies may reflect differences in populations, outcome definitions, and analytic approaches, suggesting that longitudinal metabolic stability may be more informative than isolated bicarbonate values for cardiovascular risk assessment in CKD.

The robustness of our findings is supported by multiple sensitivity analyses. Trajectory groups remained predictive of renal outcomes across strata defined by baseline kidney function, including among patients with preserved eGFR, although statistical significance was attenuated due to fewer events. Longer follow-up durations further strengthened the protective association of stable high tCO2 concentrations, suggesting cumulative benefits of sustained metabolic stability.

From a clinical perspective, trajectory-based monitoring of tCO2 may aid CKD risk stratification. Although current guidelines recommend alkali therapy primarily when bicarbonate concentrations decrease to <18 mmol/L,8 monitoring longitudinal trends—particularly identifying transitions from low to improving trajectories—may support more individualized management strategies.

This study leveraged a large, well-characterized national CKD cohort with repeated tCO2 measurements and applied flexible survival models allowing for group-specific baseline hazards. Nevertheless, several limitations warrant consideration. Serum tCO2 was used as a surrogate for bicarbonate and may be influenced by respiratory status or laboratory variability, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. In addition, detailed data on alkali therapy were not uniformly available, limiting causal inference. Accordingly, findings should be interpreted as prognostic rather than causal.

In conclusion, 4 distinct tCO2 trajectories in CKD were associated with renal outcomes. Persistent metabolic acidosis conferred increased risk, whereas stable high tCO2 concentrations were protective, and improvement over time was linked to more favorable outcomes. These findings suggest that trajectory-based assessment of acid–base status may enhance risk stratification and inform future interventional studies.
