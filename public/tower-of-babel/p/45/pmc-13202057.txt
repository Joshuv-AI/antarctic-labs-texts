Sticky black stain that steals everything from you: the lived experiences of women living with human papillomavirus in Tehran, Iran
Authors: Azin Mohammadi, Farzaneh Mokhtary, Akram Sanagoo, AbouAli Vedadhir, Leila Jouybari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aims to explore lived experiences of women with human papillomavirus (HPV) in the Iranian socio-cultural context, focusing on their understanding of the disease, associated challenges and the impact of these challenges on health-seeking behaviours.DesignThis qualitative study was conducted using Smith’s Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) method, employing in-depth semi-structured interviews.SettingCommunity-based recruitment in Tehran, Iran, with data collection conducted in 2025.Participants10 women aged 24–44 years, diagnosed with HPV, were recruited in Tehran through purposive and convenience sampling. Purposive sampling ensured rich experiential data relevant to the research question, while convenience sampling facilitated access to participants willing to discuss a sensitive topic.InterventionsNone. This was an exploratory qualitative study.ResultsFour main themes emerged from this IPA study: (1) the heavy burden of social stigma (unequal moral landscapes, forced loneliness, body shame and loss of femininity); (2) a challenging therapeutic journey (unknown territory, verbal violence and a ray of hope); (3) persistent worries (living with uncertain tomorrow, fear of cancer and broken trust); and (4) from wound to wisdom (becoming an agent again and not being alone in the experience).ConclusionThis qualitative exploration indicates that for women in the Iranian sociocultural context, the impact of HPV may extend into psychosocial domains, involving gendered stigma and identity concerns that can influence health-seeking behaviours. These experiences appear to be shaped by local perceptions of femininity and social norms. While many participants faced significant difficulties, some also reported instances of personal growth and strengthened social support. These insights point to the potential benefit of integrating psychological support into HPV care and enhancing healthcare provider training to address the psychosocial needs of patients.Trial registration numberNot applicable (qualitative study).Keywords: QUALITATIVE RESEARCH, Human Papillomavirus Viruses, Anxiety disorders

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a family of approximately 200 viruses and is among the most common sexually transmitted infections affecting all sexually active individuals.1 2 The highest prevalence of HPV infection occurs in early adulthood, between the ages of 18 and 28, and certain types can progress to specific cancers, including cervical, vaginal, penile, anal and oropharyngeal cancers in both men and women.3 HPV types 16 and 18 are associated with more than 90% of cervical cancers. Despite the significant harm caused by the infection, no known cure currently exists.1 2 4

85% of cervical cancer cases occur in low- and middle-income countries. Despite the global importance of national vaccination programmes, such initiatives are not common in the Middle East, except in the United Arab Emirates. Only a few countries in the region have plans to implement them in the future.3 5 Given that 85% of cervical cancer cases occur in low- and middle-income countries, in 2021, the WHO recommended HPV screening in these regions to enable substantial reductions in cervical cancer incidence through the implementation of national vaccination programmes.3 5 In Iran, routine HPV screening is not widely implemented in the national health system, and cervical cancer screening primarily relies on Pap smear testing rather than organised HPV-based programmes. HPV vaccination is not included in the national immunisation schedule and remains largely unavailable or self-funded, resulting in very low vaccination coverage among women.3 Consequently, many HPV diagnoses occur incidentally during gynaecological visits for other reasons or when symptoms emerge, often leading to unexpected and distressing experiences for women.

In addition, this infection is highly prevalent among Iranian women, with a comprehensive meta-analysis estimating an overall pooled rate of 23% across various populations and provinces, highlighting significant regional variations.6 Recent trends indicate an increasing positivity rate over time, reaching approximately 40.4% in female patients (mean age 32 years) between 2016 and 2022, with notable rises in genotypes such as HPV 6, 11, 16, 31, 53 and 54.7 In clinic-based or sexually transmitted infection cohorts, prevalence is higher, with 52.2% low-risk and 47.8% high-risk HPV reported in northern Iran, where HPV 6 dominates low-risk and HPV 16 high-risk categories.8 Urban areas like Tehran and Sanandaj consistently show elevated rates, with high-risk genotypes such as HPV 16 (17%) and HPV 52 (13%) prevailing in western regions during 2022–2023.9 These patterns, combined with forecasts suggesting ongoing shifts in genotype distribution, underscore the urgent need for reevaluated prevention strategies, including targeted awareness, screening and vaccination efforts to mitigate cervical cancer risks.6 8 9

Women living with HPV experience multiple interpersonal and intrapersonal challenges that influence their health-seeking behaviours.10 These challenges include feelings of shame, worry, social judgement and anxiety about the impact of the disease on interpersonal relationships.4 For example, stigma, defined as labelling, stereotyping, social separation and judgement, affects the health-seeking behaviours of women with HPV, leading to delays in seeking care and avoidance of treatment.4 10 Communication barriers and inconsistent information of healthcare providers also intensify women’s worries, representing significant intrapersonal and interpersonal challenges.11

Furthermore, due to the virus’ impact on female reproductive function abnormalities and cancer risk, women experience considerable concern following diagnosis.12 13 Women who receive a positive HPV result during cervical cancer screening often report high levels of worry and anxiety, particularly at the early stages of diagnosis, which tend to decrease over time.13 14

Women’s perceptions of the disease, shaped mainly by collective consciousness and interactions with healthcare providers, are another consequence of living with HPV. Women who hold more negative attitudes toward the disease tend to experience greater anxiety after receiving a positive diagnosis.13 Worry, chronic stress and cortisol secretion patterns play a role in the persistence of HPV infections and are associated with the progression of HPV-related cervical cancer.15

Given the prevalence of this infection in Iran, the absence of a national vaccination programme and the lack of formal public education, gaining a deeper understanding of the lived experiences of women with HPV can help identify the challenges posed by the disease and provide a foundation for designing educational, preventive and supportive programmes, both for women and for healthcare advocates. In this study, an interpretative phenomenological approach was employed to explore women’s lived experiences, aiming to understand their perspectives on the disease, the challenges they face and the impact of these challenges on their health-seeking behaviours.

Methods

Sampling and data collection

This qualitative study employed an interpretative phenomenological approach to explore women’s lived experiences of HPV infection. Eligibility for participation required a medically confirmed diagnosis of HPV infection. The inclusion criteria were women aged 18 years or older, a confirmed diagnosis based on laboratory tests (HPV DNA test, abnormal Pap smear with follow-up confirmation or colposcopy), ability to speak and understand Persian, residence in Iran and provision of verbal informed consent. Exclusion criteria included medical or psychological conditions hindering informed consent or meaningful participation, such as severe depression or advanced comorbidities (eg, terminal cancers unrelated to HPV) that could overshadow the individual’s lived experience of HPV.

During initial screening (via telephone or secure messaging), potential participants self-reported their diagnosis, including the type of test performed and approximate date. In the subsequent interview, participants provided further details about the diagnostic process and the healthcare provider involved (gynaecologist or oncologist). All participants described a positive laboratory result and a formal diagnosis by a specialist. No participant was included based solely on symptoms without laboratory confirmation. To protect privacy and minimise potential distress in this highly stigmatised condition, copies of medical records were neither requested nor retained.

Participants were recruited using a combination of purposive and convenience sampling. Purposive sampling was primarily employed to ensure that participants had direct lived experience of HPV and could provide rich, relevant data aligned with the phenomenological research question.16 This approach enabled the selection of women varying in age, time since diagnosis and perceived impact of the condition, thereby enhancing experiential diversity, a key requirement of Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA).17 Participants were not financially compensated; their participation was entirely voluntary and based on informed consent.

However, given the highly sensitive and stigmatised nature of HPV within the Iranian sociocultural context, accessing potential participants was challenging, with many women reluctant to disclose their diagnosis due to fears of judgement or privacy breach. Consequently, convenience sampling was used alongside purposive sampling to facilitate recruitment of willing participants.18 This combined strategy is commonly recommended in qualitative research on hard to reach or hidden populations, balancing the need for information-rich cases with practical access constraints.18 19

The recruitment process continued until data saturation was achieved, determined through ongoing analysis when no substantially new themes emerged.16 The final sample consisted of 10 women, all residing in the capital city of Tehran.

Data were collected through a phenomenological in-depth, semi-structured interview conducted by the first author, a PhD candidate in clinical health psychology with prior experience in qualitative research. The interviews lasted approximately 30–90 min (mean duration around 45–60 min), were audio-recorded with participants’ consent and were conducted in a private setting chosen for participant comfort and privacy. To facilitate access, reduce time and location barriers, and preserve anonymity in this sensitive topic, interviews were primarily conducted via telephone, with some face-to-face options available.

A semi-structured interview guide was developed based on the research objectives and existing literature on the lived experiences of individuals with similar health-related concerns.5 12 The guide consisted of open-ended core questions asked in all interviews, such as: ‘When I say HPV, what comes to your mind?’ ‘What has it been like for you to live with HPV?’ ‘How has your life changed before and after being diagnosed with this virus?’ and ‘What have your experiences and feelings been from the time of diagnosis until now?’. During the interviews, follow-up questions and probing prompts (‘How?’, ‘Tell me more’, ‘Can you give me an example?’) were used flexibly to explore emerging topics in greater depth, in line with the principles of IPA (online supplemental file).16

The interview guide was pilot-tested before the main study with two women who had similar experiences but were not included in the final sample. This process aimed to assess clarity, cultural sensitivity and flow, with minor revisions made based on feedback. All interviews were transcribed verbatim and prepared for analysis.

All interviews were conducted in Persian, the native language of both the participants and the researcher. This ensured that participants were able to freely and comfortably express their experiences using their everyday language, including culturally specific expressions and terms related to stigma, femininity and health. The first author, whose native language is Persian and who is well acquainted with the sociocultural characteristics of the Iranian context, conducted all interviews and the initial data analysis. Interview transcripts were retained in Persian throughout the analytical process to preserve their linguistic and cultural meanings. For the English translation of selected quotations, careful attention was paid to maintaining participants’ original intent and emotional tone, and back-translation was used to verify accuracy.

Data analysis

Data were managed and analysed using the IPA approach described by Smith et al16 following these steps: repeated reading of interview transcripts, initial coding, identification and clustering of concepts, extraction of main and subthemes, and finally, developing the overall interpretation. Each participant’s experiences were first analysed individually, and then, using a comparative approach, common themes across different participants’ experiences were identified.16

Reflexivity and competence of researchers

The first author is an Iranian woman with an academic background in social sciences and psychology and a strong interest in psychosocial issues related to women. This shared gendered position with the participants facilitated the development of trust and a safe space for sharing sensitive and personal experiences, and helped participants feel more comfortable discussing stigmatised topics such as sexual relationships.

At the same time, the researcher was aware that her professional background in health and social sciences might both reinforce a medically oriented perspective and place greater emphasis on women’s concerns. To manage this potential influence, reflexive note-taking was used throughout the research process. After each interview, the researcher documented her emotions, assumptions and personal reactions, and these reflexive notes were reviewed during discussions with colleagues to reduce bias.

In addition, during the analytical phase, interpretations were repeatedly checked against the raw data and participants’ quotations were retained as the primary foundation for theme development to ensure that their voices took precedence over the researcher’s perspectives. This reflexive approach contributed to maintaining the credibility and authenticity of the findings.

Rigour of this study

In order to confirm the rigour of the study, the criteria proposed by Morse20 were applied. Credibility was enhanced through pilot-testing of the interview guide with two women who had similar experiences to assess clarity and cultural sensitivity, and by employing member checking: summaries of the themes derived from each interview were shared with several participants, and their feedback was used to refine and validate the interpretations. Additionally, the results were returned to two participants, who confirmed the authenticity of the findings throughout the research process. The interviews were conducted by the first researcher, a woman trained in qualitative research methods.

Transferability was supported by providing rich descriptions of the study context (educated women living in Tehran, aged 24–44 years), the sampling process and participants’ experiences, enabling readers to judge the extent to which the findings may apply to similar contexts.

Dependability and confirmability were ensured through maintaining a detailed audit trail, including raw interview transcripts, analytical memos and step-by-step documentation of theme development from initial coding to final themes. The analytical process was reviewed by an independent qualitative researcher to reduce researcher bias, and all stages were transparently described in accordance with the methodological guidelines outlined by Smith et al.16 Measures were also taken to prevent prolonged interview duration and to provide a comprehensive description of all research steps, including data collection, analysis and theme development, thereby enabling auditability of the study by readers.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

In this study, 10 Iranian women diagnosed with HPV participated, with ages ranging from 24 to 44 years (mean age: 32 years). Of these, seven were single and three were married. Regarding educational level, half of the participants (n=5) held a master’s degree, four held a bachelor’s degree and one was a PhD student.

Among the patients grappling with the disease, some are recently diagnosed and others have had it for years. None of the participants reported having received the HPV vaccine before their diagnosis, except for one woman who had been vaccinated but was still infected. This pattern is consistent with the limited availability and low coverage of the HPV vaccine in Iran. In addition, because there is no organised national screening programme for this condition in Iran, diagnoses were most often made during routine annual gynaecological examinations or following an individual decision to undergo HPV testing.

Based on the IPA, 4 main themes and 11 subthemes were identified: The heavy burden of social stigma (unequal moral landscapes, forced loneliness, body shame, loss of femininity), a challenging therapeutic journey (unknown territory, verbal violence, a ray of hope), persistent worries (living with uncertain tomorrow, fear of cancer, broken trust) and from wound to wisdom (becoming an agent again, not being alone in the experience)(table 1).

The heavy burden of social stigma

Analysis of the interviews revealed that the heavy burden of social stigma was a superordinate theme, profoundly shaped by the sociocultural context of Iran. This theme encompassed four interrelated subthemes: unequal moral landscapes, forced loneliness, body shame and loss of femininity.

Unequal moral landscapes

Participants highlighted societal hypocrisy in judging sexual behaviour, where men are often excused or normalised, while women face severe condemnation for the same actions. This double standard creates a landscape where women feel disproportionately targeted. One participant stated:

For boys, if they contract this disease, it’s somehow cool, but for girls, it feels dirty (Participant one).

Body shame

In the Iranian cultural context, premarital or extramarital sexual activity remains a strong taboo for women, leading to intense pressure from family, community and society to conceal any related health issues. This pressure manifests as a deep sense of shame regarding their physical and social presence. Participants described an overwhelming fear of judgement, resulting in deliberate silence and secrecy even with those closest to them. For example, Participant nine explained:

I couldn’t even tell my husband. The same goes for my family. I knew I wouldn’t be able to live under their gaze.

Forced loneliness

The combined effects of unequal moral landscapes and body shame contributed to the enforced isolation of women living with HPV. Participants reported reluctance to disclose their diagnosis due to fears of betrayal or gossip, which left them navigating the emotional burden alone. One woman shared:

Honestly, I didn’t tell anyone for a while because I didn’t see anyone as safe. I was afraid that if I told, they might use it against me later. Then, when I finally told one of my friends, I really regretted it; I kept thinking, ‘What if she tells someone and it spreads?’ (Participant six).

Another described the emotional toll of this isolation:

The first thing that comes to mind about this virus is loneliness; that you can no longer be with anyone (Participant two).

Loss of femininity

One of the most profound impacts was the loss of femininity, driven by internalised cultural ideals of purity and womanhood. Many participants reported feeling devalued after diagnosis, perceiving themselves as ‘stained’ (P1, P9), ‘impure’ (P9, P6) or ‘unworthy of healthy relationships’ (P9, P1, P6). One woman reflected:

When you say HPV, I think of a virus that clings to you and not only affects your health but also takes away your morality and chastity, in a way, disgracing you (Participant one).

Another noted the lasting effects on self-esteem and her sense of self as a woman:

This whole situation really hurt my self-esteem. I was afraid of physical intimacy. I can say that for a year I really withdrew into myself (Participant two).

These feelings stem from associating HPV with promiscuity, leading women to feel they no longer belong to the category of ‘good and pure girls’.

Overall, this theme illustrates how HPV diagnosis in Iran is not merely a medical event but a deeply stigmatising experience that reinforces gendered social norms and threatens women’s sense of self and belonging.

A challenging therapeutic journey

Some participants became aware of their HPV infection through the appearance of physical symptoms such as genital warts, while others were diagnosed during routine check-ups without any symptoms. After receiving this diagnosis, one of the most important aspects of women’s lived experiences was their initial encounter with the healthcare system and medical staff. Although the diagnosis alone caused confusion and fear, it was the journey through the medical landscape that defined their coping process. This theme includes three subthemes: unknown territory, verbal violence and a ray of hope.

Unknown territory

A prominent aspect of the challenging therapeutic journey was the pervasive lack of clear and reliable information, which participants experienced from two main sources. Many participants reported receiving inadequate, inconsistent or confusing information directly from physicians and clinicians. Treatment recommendations varied widely between providers, and explanations about the nature of HPV, its prognosis and transmission were often minimal or contradictory. For instance, Participant four described a wide variety of treatments involving ointment, laser treatment and ultimately traditional remedies like fig sap to remove warts.

Participant three shared:

There was so much incorrect information surrounding this disease that I thought it was the end. I told myself, ‘It’s over; I’ll get cancer and die.

Participants also emphasised a broader societal silence around HPV, noting that they had little to no prior knowledge of the virus before diagnosis. This general lack of public education through schools, media or health campaigns left them unprepared and vulnerable to misinformation. Several women turned to foreign websites for reliable information, as local resources were scarce. Participant three described:

At the same time, I searched a lot and read on foreign websites that by changing lifestyle, quitting smoking, and such, those cellular changes can return to their original state.

This inconsistent information, both clinical and societal, amplified participants’ confusion and fear, leading them to search for answers in ‘unknown territory’, often relying on unverified sources or foreign websites to find clarity.

Verbal violence

Some participants reported experiences of indifference or minimisation of the disease by physicians and apathy, all of which are considered forms of medical violence. Participants described encounters with healthcare providers that they perceived as forms of medical violence and discrimination, encompassing indifference, lack of empathy, stigmatising attitudes and breaches of confidentiality. Although no physical violence was reported, women felt emotionally harmed by providers who minimised their concerns, focused solely on physical treatment without addressing psychosocial impacts or violated their privacy. They believed that some doctors, without considering the patient’s emotional state or expressing empathy, merely provided treatment solutions such as prescribing topical medications or freezing warts. In some cases, initial encounters with the healthcare system even led to violations of patient privacy. For example, Participant two, who learnt about her infection through a laboratory test, reported that the attending physician shared her test result on social media without her permission:

The attending physician shared my test result on social media without my permission. This not only violated my privacy but also caused severe anxiety due to the disclosure of my condition.

These experiences reflected broader discriminatory treatment within healthcare settings, where HPV-related stigma appeared to influence provider behaviour, leading women to feel judged or devalued. Such encounters intensified feelings of isolation and mistrust toward the medical care system.

A ray of hope

Throughout this challenging process, some participants found strategies to manage the disease. A ray of hope emerged through supportive healthcare providers and information found through proactive searching. For some, learning about the possibility of viral clearance through lifestyle changes provided a sense of agency. Participant one reflected:

“I read an article that said, for example, if I don’t smoke or exercise, the virus might be cleared from my body after two years. I don’t smoke at all, and reading this story made me feel more hopeful.

Persistent worries

Worry about the future and possible infidelity in the past were among the most significant aspects of the experience of women living with HPV, to the extent that individuals can be haunted by their concerns. These disturbing thoughts and feelings are multilayered, affecting both physical health and interpersonal relationships. This theme consists of three subthemes: living with uncertain tomorrow, fear of cancer and broken trust.

Living with uncertain tomorrow

One dimension of these worries relates to their future marital life. Women were concerned that their partners might learn about the infection in the future, despite treatment or that the virus could affect their ability to bear children. The future appeared uncertain and foggy to them. Participant five stated,

I feel a sense of hopelessness. Hopelessness about not being able to become a mother, that maybe one day this virus will return, that if I ever enter a relationship, I might be heavily blamed and humiliated for having it.

For many participants, contracting this virus signified a change in identity and a loss of hope for a stable and fulfilling marital life. This represented a deep and long-lasting challenge.

Fear of cancer

Another source of concern for these women was the ever-present shadow of the virus looming over their future and the fear of developing cancer as a potential consequence of HPV infection. Participant six shared,

Now that I’m infected, I constantly fear that I might develop cellular changes. I’m stuck in a cycle where I have to keep checking and thinking about what I should do if I get cellular changes.

Broken trust

Doubt about the honesty of sexual partners and broken trust were other common experiences among participants, particularly among married women. Participant four stated,

The first thing that came to mind was that I doubted my husband; I was full of distrust and suspicion.

Even after her husband tested negative, she continued to experience mistrust and noted that this affected their relationship.

Single women also reported anger toward the irresponsibility of their sexual partners and the denial of virus transmission. Participant six said, “After breaking up, I realized he was seeing someone else at the same time. I was filled with anger, and in the end, he even gave me the disease!”

From wound to wisdom

The theme ‘From wound to wisdom’ illustrates that, for many women, the experience of living with HPV represents a transformative journey from suffering to personal growth.

Becoming an agent again

One stage of this journey is becoming an agent again, which involves the redefinition of an individual’s identity and values. Participant eight reflected on her commitment to a healthy lifestyle:

Before, I was somewhat more careless; after, I started thinking more about taking care of myself.

Participant two described changes in her relational standards:

This virus raised my standards; if a partner wants to judge me, they are simply not my type.

Similarly, participants rebuilt their self-esteem by letting go of self-blame and accepting themselves.

Not being alone in the experience

Another stage in participants’ transformative journey involved the formation and strengthening of human connections, highlighting social support from family, friends and empathetic healthcare providers as a bridge through the crisis.

One participant described the empathy of her partner:

My partner knew and supported me throughout the treatment. He was patient and constantly encouraged me.

The evidence of family support has been clearly stated below:

My father was by my side throughout the illness. He noticed when I was feeling unwell and took care of me with every small gesture. It felt excellent. (Participant one).

Women also encountered knowledgeable and empathetic doctors during their treatment, which provided them with a sense of security and helped them navigate the care process more effectively. Participant five said,

My doctor treated me very well and told me not to worry at all, to strengthen my immune system, and to get vaccinated.

Discussion

The findings of this phenomenological study reveal the profound psychosocial dimensions of living with HPV among Iranian women, shaped by the cultural and social context of Iran. The heavy burden of social stigma included the subthemes of unequal moral landscapes, body shame and loss of femininity. Participants felt that society judged women with HPV due to the association of the disease with promiscuity, while men were largely exempt from this stigma. The study showed that social stigma related to HPV is often women-centred, with men being less blamed. This finding is supported by other studies as well.21 22

Experiences of enforced isolation and reduced self-esteem among women with HPV have also been reported. Galeshi et al, in their exploration of Iranian women’s experiences with HPV, highlighted multiple challenges, including feelings of helplessness, confusion, interpersonal relationship difficulties, social stigma, decreased self-confidence, negative impacts on social life and the tendency of women to conceal their disease status.23 Similarly, Ginjupalli identified manifestations of stigma across three interrelated dimensions: discriminatory attitudes (licentiousness, infidelity, stereotypes, assumptions of HIV infection), anticipations (discrimination, rumours, mistreatment by service providers) and internalised stigma (shame/self-blame, feeling dirty/impure, fear of death, embarrassment, hopelessness).4 Collectively, these findings indicate that HPV is not merely a physical illness for women but represents a profound challenge affecting individual identity and social relationships.

Women’s feelings of anxiety and uncertainty following interactions with some physicians, contradictory information from healthcare providers, judgmental attitudes and lack of empathy resulted in a heightened experience of negative emotions among women living with HPV. In response, the affected women sought solutions to improve their situation. These findings are consistent with the study by Ghaderi et al, which explored the needs and experiences of women with HPV regarding patient–provider communication. Their qualitative research indicated that therapeutic interactions for women with HPV are associated with three key dimensions: quality of communication and counselling, adherence to professional ethics and level of provider expertise and knowledge. Participants emphasised the need for clear explanations about the disease, psychological support, professional guidance and attentive care during examinations. They requested respectful, empathetic and non-judgemental behaviour from physicians.24 Similarly, Galeshi et al showed that clear communication and appropriate counselling strategies help women better understand and manage their health status.23 Since women experience multiple concerns following a positive diagnosis, creating a safe and informative patient–provider relationship can reduce their anxiety.11 In Iran, clinicians and patients are advised to consult academic research centres and specialised gynaecology clinics within universities of medical sciences across the country. These centres provide the most reliable evidence-based guidance and professional counselling for managing HPV-related concerns.

Morse et al and Petagna et al demonstrated that the behaviour of the healthcare system and the stigma transmitted to women delay their screening and vaccination.10 25 Conversely, when women feel safe within the healthcare system, feelings of shame and stigma decrease and health-seeking behaviours increase. However, increasing vaccination coverage requires more than just education. In Iran, structural factors such as the high cost of the vaccine and its current absence from the national routine immunisation programme are significant barriers. Therefore, policy-level interventions, including subsidies and integrating the HPV vaccine into national health priorities, are recommended alongside public awareness campaigns to improve accessibility and uptake.

Persistent worries, including living with an uncertain tomorrow, fear of cancer and broken trust. Other studies have similarly reported that women with HPV experience concerns regarding symptom control, risk of cervical cancer, fear of transmitting the infection to a partner or fetus, recurrence, mortality and incurability of the disease.26,29 Women in this study expressed deep worries about the impact of the disease on their future lives, including marriage and childbearing. Ghaderi’s study indicated that one of the main concerns among participants was the potential effects of HPV on fertility. These concerns encompassed reduced fertility in both sexes, possible complications for fetal health during pregnancy, pregnancy-related risks including preterm birth or miscarriage and the potential for virus transmission to the newborn through breastfeeding.12 Participants sought more information about different HPV types, modes of transmission, consequences for sexual partners, prevalence, latency and regression of HPV, management options and the implications of infection for cancer risk and fertility.11 At an emotional level, a positive diagnosis affected women’s relationships with their partners, whether legally married or not. Some women reported reduced sexual desire and reluctance to enter new romantic relationships following a positive diagnosis, consistent with Alay et al (2019).30 Some participants also experienced distrust and suspicion toward their partners. In line with this, Galeshi et al found that women with HPV often experienced a sense of insecurity and mistrust toward their partners after diagnosis.11 Nick et al identified the primary concern of women with HPV as ‘life stress’.22

The theme ‘From wound to wisdom’ included becoming an agent again and not being alone in the experience. Some women in this study, after overcoming initial challenges, achieved personal growth and redefined their identity. This finding is consistent with Nick et al’s study, in which participants employed coping strategies, including adopting a spiritual lifestyle, consulting psychologists and using tranquillisers.22 The results of this study indicated that women living with HPV benefited from support and companionship from family, friends and healthcare providers throughout the course of their illness. Galeshi et al also emphasised that healthcare providers not only play a role in managing the physical symptoms of HPV but can also significantly help reduce anxiety and psychological distress resulting from awareness of the infection.23

These findings have important implications for clinical practice, public health policy and stigma-reduction efforts in Iran. The pervasive social and gendered stigma surrounding HPV indicates that the infection is not merely a biomedical issue but a significant psychosocial challenge requiring multifaceted interventions.

Based on the results of this study, several future actions are proposed to improve the experiences of women with HPV:

First, in a context like Iran, where national HPV vaccination programmes are not implemented, healthcare providers and public health practitioners could use non-official or self-directed educational resources to improve their understanding of how to interact with women living with HPV. Such resources can support culturally sensitive, non-judgemental and patient-centred care, helping to reduce stigma and improve women’s lived experiences even within existing legal and social constraints.31

Second, considering Iran’s policies, there is an urgent need for more education to inform the public about the HPV vaccine and cervical cancer. Because, as the findings of this study revealed, the maximum number of women were vaccinated after being infected due to a lack of awareness, which could have consequences for them.32

Third, as for HPV being taboo, psychological support services within gynaecology and oncology clinics would help Iranian women to manage anxiety, shame and interpersonal connections for improving the quality of life and treatment adherence.33 Considering these measures, women’s experiences can transfer from isolation and fear to empowerment and resilience.

This study has several methodological limitations. First, the findings are based on a small, purposive sample of 10 educated women from Tehran, which limits the geographical, socio-economic and cultural diversity of the perspectives captured. Consequently, the transferability of results to broader populations, such as women from rural areas, with lower education or from different ethnic backgrounds, is constrained.

Second, the sensitive nature of the topic may have introduced self-selection bias, as women experiencing higher levels of shame or distress might have declined to participate, potentially silencing the most marginalised voices. The use of convenience sampling alongside purposive sampling, while pragmatic, may have further shaped the range of experiences represented.

Third, the study did not systematically record the precise time elapsed since each participant’s HPV diagnosis (eg, in months or years). This limits the ability to analyse how experiences and coping mechanisms might vary across different phases of living with the condition. Finally, as the primary researcher conducted all interviews and led the analysis, her position as a woman within the same cultural context, while facilitative of rapport may have influenced data collection and interpretation.

Despite these limitations, the in-depth, idiographic approach of IPA has yielded rich insights into an under-researched area in Iran, providing a valuable foundation for future research and intervention development.

Building on the findings of this study, several avenues for future research are recommended. First, longitudinal studies could track how the psychosocial experiences of women with HPV evolve over time, from diagnosis through treatment and beyond. Second, to enhance generalisability, future studies should include more diverse samples, such as women from rural areas, those with lower educational attainment or from different socioeconomic backgrounds. Finally, interventional research is needed to develop and test the efficacy of culturally adapted support programmes (eg, peer-support groups, digital health interventions) aimed at reducing stigma and improving mental health outcomes for this population.

Conclusion

The findings of this study illustrated that for the participating women, living with HPV was associated with multidimensional challenges, including experiences of severe social stigma, an unequal moral landscape, enforced isolation and concerns about loss of femininity. Furthermore, their narratives described encounters with a healthcare system perceived as challenging, characterised by a lack of clear information and non-empathetic behaviours from some providers, which appeared to exacerbate their psychological distress.

Conversely, the analysis also indicated that through the adaptation process, many women described a journey toward empowerment, forging stronger human connections and navigating a path of personal growth. These insights, drawn from the participants’ lived experiences, highlight the potential value of multilevel interventions. Specifically, for this population, training healthcare providers to improve communication, gender sensitivity and information provision could play a vital role in enhancing care experiences. Additionally, interventions such as public awareness programmes, peer-support networks and access to specialised counselling may help mitigate the psychological impact of the diagnosis, potentially improve treatment adherence and support better quality of life.
