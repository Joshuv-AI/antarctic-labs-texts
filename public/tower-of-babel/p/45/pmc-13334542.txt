Neurotropic potential and docking investigations of newly synthesized triazolopyridine and triazolothienopyridine derivatives
Authors: Ervand G. Paronikyan, Shushanik Sh. Dashyan, Suren S. Mamyan, Lernik S. Hunanyan, Tigranuhi A. Nikoghosyan, Lilit S. Mirzoyan, Ruzanna G. Paronikyan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A series of novel tricyclic and tetracyclic S-alkyl derivatives based on fused triazolopyridine and thienotriazolopyridine scaffolds were synthesized and evaluated for potential neuroactive properties. The compounds were obtained through optimized alkylation and intramolecular cyclization procedures, yielding the target heterocycles in high purity. Their anticonvulsant, anxiolytic and antidepressant activities were assessed using pentylenetetrazole (PTZ) induced seizure, tonic seizure threshold, open-field, elevated plus maze (EPM) and forced swim (FS) models. Ten derivatives demonstrated marked anticonvulsant activity in the PTZ model, with ED50 values lower than that of ethosuximide under the present experimental conditions. Within the tested series, compound 2g showed the highest activity without inducing muscle relaxation. Compound 3f demonstrated notable anxiolytic effects. Compounds 3e and 3f significantly reduced immobility time in the FS test, indicating antidepressant-like activity. Molecular docking studies suggested that triazolopyridine derivatives (2a, 2b, 2g, 2h, 2i) may interact with GABAA, SERT and 5-HT1A receptors, whereas thienotriazolopyridine analogues appear to preferentially target serotonergic systems, supporting a possible multitarget mechanism of action. Compound 2g showed the strongest GABAA binding affinity (ΔG = −8.42 kcal mol−1), and 3f exhibited the highest affinity for SERT (ΔG = −10.45 kcal mol−1). These findings highlight the potential of fused triazolopyridine-based heterocycles as promising lead structures for the development of novel neuroactive agents with combined anticonvulsant and psychotropic properties.

Introduction

Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by recurrent seizures resulting from abnormal neuronal excitability. It remains among the most common neurological diseases worldwide and continues to represent a significant public health concern.1 Despite the availability of numerous antiepileptic drugs (AEDs), a considerable proportion of patients still experience inadequate seizure control or develop adverse effects during long-term therapy.2,3 Therefore, the identification of structurally novel compounds with improved efficacy and safety profiles continues to be an important goal in medicinal chemistry and neuropharmacology.4,5

In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward multifunctional neuroactive agents capable of simultaneously exhibiting anticonvulsant and psychotropic effects.6,7 These multifunctional agents may provide therapeutic benefit in epilepsy accompanied by comorbid psychiatric and behavioral symptoms, including anxiety and depressive disorders.

Heterocyclic scaffolds represent a major class of structures in medicinal chemistry due to their structural versatility and tunable pharmacological properties.8–11 The broad synthetic accessibility of heterocyclic systems has contributed to their widespread application in the design of biologically active molecules with diverse therapeutic properties. Among these scaffolds, thiophene-, triazole-, and pyridine-containing derivatives are frequently encountered in pharmaceutical chemistry and drug development.12,13 Compounds incorporating thiophene fragments have been associated with antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and anticonvulsant effects, whereas triazole derivatives are known for their antifungal and neurotropic activities.14,15 Pyridine-containing systems are also widely utilized in the development of CNS-active agents because of their ability to interact effectively with neuronal receptor systems.16–18 Integration of multiple heterocyclic fragments within one molecular framework can significantly affect receptor affinity, selectivity, and overall pharmacological behavior. In many cases, fusion of heterocyclic rings generates conformationally restricted systems with altered electronic properties that can enhance receptor binding and biological response. Condensed thienopyridine derivatives continue to attract attention because of their diverse pharmacological properties and clinical significance. Several clinically important drugs, including ticlopidine, clopidogrel, prasugrel, and vicagrel, contain the thienopyridine scaffold as a key structural component (Fig. 1).19–22

Thienopyridine and triazolopyridine-containing medications structures.

Trazodone (Fig. 1), a clinically used triazolopyridine derivative, exerts antidepressant and sedative effects through modulation of serotonergic neurotransmission, including interaction with 5-HT2 receptors and inhibition of serotonin reuptake.23

Previous studies reported triazoleisoquinoline derivatives displaying anticonvulsant activity in maximal electroshock (MES) and PTZ experimental models.24 Pharmacological evaluation demonstrated that out of all the synthesized compounds, only 9-(heptyloxy)-5,6-dihydro[1,2,4]triazolo[3,4-a]isoquinoline (I) (Fig. 2) exhibited excellent anti-epileptic activity with an ED50 value of 48.19 mg kg−1. This compound was found to be more effective than valproate but less active than carbamazepine. Incorporation of thiophene, triazole, and pyridine moieties into fused systems has also produced compounds with notable anticonvulsant activity.

Triazolopyridine and triazolothienopyridine-containing compounds structures with antiepileptic activity.

The design strategy involving fusion of thiophene, triazole, and pyridine pharmacophoric units has also resulted in compounds with pronounced anticonvulsant properties. In particular, 5-(4-chlorophenyl)-4,5-dihydrothieno[2,3-e][1,2,4]triazolo[4,3-a]pyridine (II) (Fig. 2) demonstrated high activity together with favorable protective index (PI) values relative to reference anticonvulsant agents.25

In our earlier investigations, condensed triazolopyridine derivatives bearing S-substituted fragments were synthesized, in which substitution occurred directly at the triazole ring, and examined for neurotropic activity.26 Among the compounds tested for anticonvulsant activity using PTZ and MES models, six out of eleven were found to be active. Behavioral studies revealed anxiolytic effects for several representatives of the synthesized series. The most pronounced effect was observed for the compound 2-[(5-anilino-6-cyano-8,8-dimethyl-7,10-dihydro-8H-pyrano[3,4-c][1,2,4]triazolo[4,3-a]pyridine-3-yl)thio]-N,N-diphenylacetamide (III) (Fig. 2). In other study,27 thieno[3,2-e][1,2,4]triazolo[4,3-a]pyridines were obtained via a one pot synthetic approach without isolation of the corresponding S-alkyl intermediates, where substitution occurred directly at the pyridine ring. Pharmacological screening additionally revealed behavioral effects indicative of psychotropic activity. The structure–activity relationships (SAR) of these compounds are summarized in Fig. 3.

Summarization of SAR analysis for the thieno[3,2-e][1,2,4]triazolo[4,3-a]pyridines.26

Overall, published data together with our earlier findings support the potential of fused triazolopyridine derivatives as valuable scaffolds for the development of novel CNS-active compounds. Unlike previously described one pot synthetic procedures,27 the present methodology enabled the isolation and characterization of intermediate S-alkyl derivatives prior to cyclization. This approach allowed a more detailed evaluation of SAR between intermediate and fused tetracyclic systems. Additionally, replacement of CONHR fragments by COR groups was performed to assess the influence of electronic and structural factors on anticonvulsant and psychotropic properties.

Results and discussion

Chemistry

The target compounds 2a–i were synthesized according to the route outlined in Scheme 1. For the synthesis of the target compounds, tricyclic fused pyrano[3,4-c][1,2,4]triazolo[4,3-a]pyridine (1a)28 and [1,2,4]triazolo[3,4-a]isoquinolines (1b and c)29 have been used. A key advancement in this work is the deliberate isolation and study of S-alkyl intermediates 2a–i, which enables a direct structural and biological comparison with the corresponding cyclized derivatives. Reactions of thione derivatives 1a–c with alkyl halides predominantly afforded the corresponding S-alkylated products 2a–i (Scheme 1). The S-alkylation step proceeds in good to high yields with high chemoselectivity favoring sulfur over N-alkylation. Preferential sulfur alkylation is likely associated with the higher nucleophilicity and polarizability of sulfur atoms in the thione–thiol system. In the thione–thiol tautomeric equilibrium, the sulfur atom exhibits greater nucleophilicity and polarizability than the nitrogen centers, making it more reactive toward electrophilic alkyl halides. Consequently, sulfur alkylation predominates under the employed reaction conditions. Additionally, the reactions are carried out under relatively mild conditions using readily available reagents and simple bases (NaOAc in EtOH), avoiding the need for expensive catalysts or harsh conditions, which represents a practical advantage of the method.

Preparation of the target compounds 2a–i.

The obtained intermediates 2a–i were subsequently employed for preparation of tetracyclic derivatives – pyrano[3,4-c]thieno[3,2-e][1,2,4]triazolo[4,3-a]pyridine and thieno[2,3-c][1,2,4]triazolo[3,4-a]isoquinoline (3a–i) (Scheme 2). Compounds 2a–i were subjected to intramolecular cyclization using sodium ethoxide, resulting in the formation of compounds 3a–i (method A). Compounds 3a–k were obtained in a one pot reaction from compounds 1a–c and alkyl halides, using potassium carbonate as the base (Scheme 2). In a one pot reaction, alkylation is carried out initially, followed by the subsequent closure of the thiophene ring (method B). Notably, the one pot protocol (method B) reduces the number of synthetic steps, minimizes solvent use, and simplifies purification, thereby improving operational efficiency and partially aligning with green chemistry principles.

Synthesis of aimed compounds 3a–k.

Structural confirmation of compounds 3a–k was obtained using IR and NMR spectroscopy. In the IR spectra, absorption bands corresponding to NH2 groups were observed in the 3236–3512 cm−1 region, whereas signals characteristic of nitrile functionality were absent. In the 1H NMR spectra, resonances assigned to amino protons appeared within the 5.34–8.19 ppm range, supporting formation of the proposed tetracyclic structures.

Biological evaluation

Evaluation of anticonvulsant activity

Pharmacological screening showed that all synthesized compounds displayed different levels of protection in the PTZ-induced seizure model. The percentage of PTZ antagonism ranged from 20% to 80% depending on compound structure. Among the studied compounds, the most active ones—2a, 2b, 2g, 2h, 2i, 3a, 3d, 3e, 3f, and 3k—were selected for further evaluation using the thiosemicarbazide (TSC) seizure model, which affects GABA exchange. The anticonvulsant activity data for the ten most active compounds, compared with the reference drugs ethosuximide and diazepam, in mice are shown in Table 1. The evaluation revealed that the studied compounds demonstrated pronounced anticonvulsant activity in the PTZ model, with ED50 values approximately fourfold lower than those of ethosuximide under the present experimental conditions, while diazepam exhibited higher potency.

According to the MES test, the studied compounds, as well as the reference drugs, did not show anticonvulsant effects and were ineffective against tonic and tonic–clonic seizures. The absence of MES protection indicates limited influence on seizure spread mechanisms associated with voltage-gated sodium channel modulation. Importantly, this finding does not contradict a possible GABAergic mechanism, as the MES model is relatively insensitive to agents that enhance inhibitory neurotransmission and is primarily responsive to sodium channel blockers.

The SAR evaluation revealed that both substituent pattern and scaffold rigidity contributed substantially to anticonvulsant potency. Compounds 2a, 2b, 2g, 2h, 2i, 3a, 3d, 3e, 3f, and 3k showed significant activity in the PTZ model, with ED50 values ranging from 34 to 52 mg kg−1, demonstrating clear structure-dependent potency. The presence of electron-withdrawing substituents, particularly nitro and carbonyl-containing benzoyl-type fragments (COC6H5), was generally associated with higher anticonvulsant activity (e.g., compounds 2b, 2g and 3f), indicating that electron-withdrawing fragments may enhance interaction with molecular systems associated with anticonvulsant activity.

Evaluation of psychotropic effects

The ten most effective compounds—2a, 2b, 2g, 2h, 2i, 3a, 3d, 3e, 3f and 3k—were assessed for their psychotropic properties at a dose of 50 mg kg−1 in the “open field”, “EPM”, “FS” and “learning and memory”(electroshock retrograde amnesia model) tests. This dose was selected because the ED50 of these compounds is within the 50 mg kg−1 range according to the confidence intervals.

In the “open field” behavioral model, control group rats showed an average of 25.12 horizontal movements, 5.81 vertical movements, and examined 0.54 cells (see Table 3). Administration of the tested compounds produced measurable alterations in behavioral parameters relative to control animals. Compounds 2a, 2g, 2h, 2i, 3a, 3e, 3f and 3k caused a statistically significant increase in horizontal movements of the animals, while the other compounds did not affect horizontal movement behavior. For vertical movements, compounds 2a, 2g, 2i and 3k significantly increased these movements, indicating an activation of behavior. However, all the selected compounds significantly increased the number of cell examinations compared to the control, which may reflect anxiolytic-like behavioral properties (see Table 3).

This effect was particularly pronounced with compounds 2a, 2g, 2h, 2i, 3a, 3e, 3f and 3k. Ethosuximide, at an effective dose of 200 mg kg−1, had no impact on any of the research activity indicators. In contrast, diazepam (2 mg kg−1) significantly increased the number of cells examined compared to the control group, demonstrating a pronounced anti-anxiety effect. Similar to the studied compounds 2a, 2g, 2h, 2i, 3a, 3e, 3f and 3k, diazepam also increases horizontal movements, indicating mild behavioral stimulation.

Behavioral effects appeared to depend on both electronic properties of substituents and scaffold organization. In particular, incorporation of a condensed dimethylpyrano-triazolo[4,3-a]pyridine scaffold enhances CNS activity, suggesting that increased molecular rigidity and planarity may improve target interaction affinity. The presence of a 4-nitrobenzyl substituent on the pyridine ring (compound 2g) is associated with higher locomotor and exploratory activity, indicating a possible electronic contribution to receptor binding efficiency. Similarly, alkyl/aromatic substitution on the heterocyclic framework, such as the phenyl group on the thiophene ring (compound 3a), correlates with increased behavioral activation, likely due to improved hydrophobic interactions. Overall, the observed activity trend suggests that electron-withdrawing substituents (nitro), hydrophobic/aromatic groups, and fused-ring collectively contribute to enhanced diazepam-like CNS activation.

Following administration of these compounds and diazepam, the experimental animals spent between 22.87 seconds (with compound 3e) and 99.04 seconds (with compound 3f) in the open arms, in contrast to the control animals and those receiving ethosuximide at a dose of 200 mg kg−1 (see Table 4). These behavioral changes are consistent with anxiolytic-like activity for these compounds. Among them, compound 3f was the most active, followed by compounds 3k, 2b, 2i and 2a.

Analysis of the obtained data suggests that incorporation of the fused methylcyclohexane-triazolopyridine system contributes positively to anxiolytic-like effects. Compounds bearing a 4-nitrophenyl-substituted thiophene moiety (3f and 3k) show a marked increase in activity, with approximately a twofold higher anxiolytic effect compared with diazepam, indicating a quantitative contribution of the electron-withdrawing nitro group to potency.

In the “FS” model, some of the selected compounds (2a, 2b, 2g, 2h, 2i, 3a, 3d, 3e, 3f and 3k) significantly increased the latency to the first instance of immobilization (see Table 5). In control mice, the first instance of immobilization occurs after 52.01 seconds. These findings of studied compounds may indicate antidepressant-like behavioral activity at the tested dose. In contrast, ethosuximide at a dose of 200 mg kg−1 shows comparable data to the control group. The reference drug diazepam, at a dose of 2 mg kg−1, similarly increases the latency to the first instance of immobilization, as observed with the noted compounds. However, diazepam significantly reduces the total immobilization time, to contrast to all compounds. Compounds 2a, 2b, 2g, 2h, 2i, 3a, 3d, 3e, 3f, 3k and ethosuximide decrease the total immobilization time (see Table 4). Thus, in this model, the most active compounds are 2b, 2g, 2i, 3d, 3e, 3f, which significantly increase the duration of active swimming.

The SAR study, indicates that antidepressant-like activity is influenced by both scaffold features and substituent effects. Compounds containing a fused alicyclic-triazolo[4,3-a]pyridine system show improved activity, suggesting that increased conformational constraint and hydrophobic character enhance CNS activity. A pronounced effect is observed for derivatives bearing a 4-nitrophenyl group on the thiophene ring (3e and 3f), which demonstrate the strongest increase in active swimming time and reduced immobility, indicating a positive contribution of the electron-withdrawing nitro group to potency. Overall, activity is enhanced by the combined presence of fused alicyclic architecture, nitro substitution, and hydrophobic interactions.

Importantly, in contrast to our previously reported compounds,26 which predominantly exhibited sedative-like profiles associated with fully cyclized tetracyclic systems, the present series demonstrates a shift toward behavioral activation with retained anticonvulsant efficacy.

In the learning and memory test—specifically, the conditioned response of passive avoidance (CRPA)—control rats spent almost the entire 6 minute period in the light compartment on the 1st and 2nd days of the experiment, with an average time of 290.0 seconds. In contrast, under conditions of retrograde amnesia, administration of the compounds at a dose of 50 mg kg−1 every other day, following electric shock, did not increase the time spent reproducing the reflex. These results were statistically similar to the control values and indicate an amnestic effect of the compounds. Diazepam exhibited a similar effect.

Limitations of the psychotropic models: while the EPM, FS test, and open field tests are standard for evaluating anxiolytic- and antidepressant-like activity, they have inherent constraints. The EPM primarily reflects exploratory behavior under novel conditions, the FS test measures behavioral despair, which can be influenced by general locomotor activity or stress sensitivity, and the open field test mainly assesses locomotor activity and exploration, which may not directly reflect anxiety or mood-related effects. Therefore, although our compounds increased open-arm time, active swimming, and exploratory behavior, these results suggest potential psychotropic effects rather than definitive anxiolytic or antidepressant efficacy.

Thus, the pharmacological profile of the investigated compounds indicates selective anticonvulsant activity predominantly in chemically induced seizure models. All active compounds demonstrated pronounced efficacy in the PTZ model, which is commonly used to identify agents effective against absence and myoclonic seizures and is closely associated with dysfunction of GABAergic neurotransmission. Activity observed in the TSC model additionally supports possible involvement of inhibitory neurotransmitter pathways. In contrast, the absence of activity in the MES test suggests that these compounds do not significantly affect seizure propagation mediated by voltage-gated sodium channels, which are typically involved in tonic–clonic seizures. Importantly, the lack of MES efficacy does not contradict a GABAergic mechanism, since many GABAergic agents are inactive in this model but effective in PTZ-like paradigms. Therefore, the observed activity profile indicates that the studied compounds are more relevant to absence-type seizures rather than tonic–clonic seizures. In comparison with the reference drug ethosuximide, the investigated compounds demonstrated higher anticonvulsant potency in the PTZ model and exhibited significantly greater therapeutic indices, while similarly lacking myorelaxant effects at the studied doses. In addition, unlike ethosuximide, the synthesized compounds showed a broader spectrum of central nervous system activity, including anxiolytic- and antidepressant-like effects in behavioral models. Compared with diazepam, the studied compounds were less potent in anticonvulsant activity; however, they did not produce observable myorelaxant effects within the tested dose range and demonstrated a more favorable safety profile. Furthermore, several compounds exhibited behavioral effects partially comparable to diazepam, particularly in anxiolytic models, which may suggest involvement of related neurochemical pathways, including modulation of GABAergic neurotransmission. Collectively, the obtained results support further investigation of these compounds as multifunctional CNS-active agents.

Docking analysis

Based on our observed data from in vivo investigations, the tested compounds exhibited anxiolytic and anticonvulsant effects. The observed anticonvulsant and anxiolytic effects may be associated with modulation of GABAergic and serotonergic systems. To explore possible molecular targets, in silico docking studies were performed toward GABAA, SERT, and 5-HT1A receptors. Moreover, molecular docking was performed to clarify whether the observed difference between S-alkyl intermediates and cyclized derivatives could be associated with differential engagement of GABAergic and monoaminergic targets. Importantly, this docking study is intended as a preliminary mechanistic interpretation rather than a dynamic or fully validated binding model.

Interaction of the studied compounds with the GABAA receptor

Docking analysis reveals that compounds 3a, 3d, 3e and 3f do not interact with the GABAA receptor. The remaining compounds interact with different regions of the protein, exhibiting various types of interactions (see Fig. 4).

Thus, complexation of compounds 2h and 2i occurs at subsite 2 (ketamine site)30 of the ECD domain in chains B and C. The interaction of compound 2i involves amino acid residues Ala45, Ser46, Asp101, Ala135, Met137, and Glu182, demonstrating a mixed type of interaction. Most compounds demonstrated physicochemical characteristics consistent with drug-like molecular frameworks. Materials and methods

Chemistry

General information: all chemicals and solvents were of commercially high purity grade purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Saint Louis, MO, USA). Melting points (m.p.) were determined on a Boetius micro table. They are expressed in degrees centigrade (°C). 1H NMR and 13C NMR spectra were recorded in DMSO-d6, DMSO-d6/CCl4, 1/3, v/v solutions (300 MHz for 1H and 75.462 MHz for 13C) on a Varian mercury spectrometer (Varian Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA). Chemical shifts are reported as δ (parts per million) relative to TMS (tetramethylsilane) as the internal standard. Coupling constants (J) were estimated in Hertz (Hz), and splitting patterns were denoted as s (singlet), br s (broad singlet), d (doublet), t (triplet) and m (multiplet).IR spectra were recorded on Nicolet Avatar 330-FTIR spectrophotometer (Thermo Nicolet, Foster, CA, USA) and the reported wave numbers are given in cm−1. Mass spectra were recorded on XEVO G3 QTof spectrometers (Waters Corporation Company, Milford, Massachusetts). Elemental analyses were performed on a Euro EA 3000 Elemental Analyzer (EuroVector, Pavia, Italy).

General procedure for the synthesis of compounds 2a–2i

In the mixture of sodium acetate (10 mmol) and ethanol (40 mL), the corresponding compounds 1a–c (10 mmol) was added. After complete dissolution, the appropriate alkyl halide (10 mmol) was added, and the reaction mixture was stirred for 2 h at room temperature. The obtained crystals were filtered off, washed with water, dried, and recrystallized from ethanol.

General procedure for the synthesis of compounds 3a–k

Method A – to a solution of sodium ethoxide prepared from sodium (2 mmol) and ethanol (20 mL), the corresponding compounds 2a–i (2.0 mmol) was added. The mixture was maintained at 60 °C for 0.5 h. Method B – in the mixture of potassium carbonate (10 mmol) and ethanol (20 mL), the corresponding compounds 1a–c (5.0 mmol) and the appropriate alkylating agent (10 mmol) was added. The mixture was refluxed for 3 h. After cooling, obtained crystals were filtered off, washed with water, dried, and recrystallized from ethanol/dioxane mixture (1 : 1). Biological evaluation

Compounds were studied for their potential neurotropic activities (anticonvulsant, sedative, and anti-anxiety effects) as well as side effects in 450 white mice of both sexes (weighing 18–24 g) and 50 male Wistar rats (weighing 120–140 g). Mice of both sexes were used for anticonvulsant tests (PTZ, MES) and most psychotropic assessments (EPM, FS) due to their well-characterized responses in these models. Rats of both sexes were used for open field and learning/memory tests because their larger size allows more accurate tracking of locomotor activity and conditioned responses. This species selection ensures reliable and reproducible behavioral measurements across all models. Each dose in mouse-based tests was tested in 6 animals per group, and in rat-based tests in 8 animals per group. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) to reflect variability and reproducibility. For most behavioral tests, a single-dose evaluation of 50 mg kg−1 was selected based on preliminary studies, which identified it as an effective dose for observing consistent pharmacological effects. All groups of animals were maintained at 25 ± 2 °C in the same room and fed a common diet. As reference compounds, the known antiepileptic drug ethosuximide and the tranquilizer diazepam were used.42,43 To determine the ED50, neurotoxic TD50, and LD50, the statistical method of Litchfield and Wilcoxon was used.44 In Tables 1 and 2 in brackets are confidence intervals of the mean activity and toxicity values calculated using the Litchfield and Wilcoxon method. Acute toxicity (LD50) was assessed by calculating the number of fatalities after 24 hours of exposure to doses ranging from 100 to 1020 mg kg−1. All biological experiments were conducted in full compliance with the European Convention for the Protection of Vertebrate Animals. All animal procedures were performed in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (ETS NO. 123, Strasbourg, 03/18/1986).

Methodology for assessing anticonvulsant potency in synthesized compounds

The anticonvulsant effect of the new synthesized compounds was investigated using the following tests: PTZ induced convulsions, TSC-induced convulsions, and MES.50–54 Outbred mice weighing 18–22 g were used for the study (6 mice per dose group).

PTZ-induced convulsions

PTZ was injected subcutaneously at a dose of 90 mg kg−1, inducing convulsions in 95% of the animals (CD95%). Test substances were administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) at doses ranging from 10 to 200 mg kg−1 in a suspension of carboxymethylcellulose with Tween-80, 45 minutes before PTZ injection. Control animals received an emulsifier. Each dose of the test compounds was tested in six mice. Each animal was placed in an individual plastic cage for 1 hour of observation. Seizures and clonic convulsions were recorded. The evaluation criterion was the prevention of PTZ clonic convulsions.

Thiosemicarbazide-induced convulsions

Thiosemicarbazide, an antimetabolite that inhibits GABA synthesis by targeting glutamic acid decarboxylase in the brain, was administered subcutaneously to mice at a dose of 18 mg kg−1 as a 0.5% solution, inducing clonic convulsions. Test substances were administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 100 mg kg−1 in a suspension of carboxymethylcellulose with Tween-80, 45 minutes before thiosemicarbazide administration. The comparative drugs were administered as follows: ethosuximide at a dose of 200 mg kg−1 and diazepam at a dose of 2 mg kg−1. Each group included 6 mice. The anticonvulsant activity of the test compounds was evaluated based on the latency time to the onset of seizures.

MES test

The MES test, used as an animal model for generalized tonic seizures in epilepsy, was conducted with the following parameters: 50 mA, duration of 0.2 s, and an oscillation frequency of 50 impulses per second. The anticonvulsant properties of the tested compounds were assessed by their ability to prevent the tonic-extensor phase of convulsions. Each group included 6 mice.

Examination of psychotropic effects of synthesized compounds

Psychotropic properties of the selected compounds were evaluated using the following tests: the Open Field test51,52 the EPM53,54 the FS test55,56 and the Learning and Memory test.57–59

Open field test

Research on motor behavior in rats was conducted using a modified Open Field model. The setup featured an instrument with a floor divided into squares, each with perforated cells. Experiments were carried out during the daytime under natural light conditions. Each test group included 8 rats per compound, as well as control and reference groups. The compounds were administered intraperitoneally at an effective dose of 50 mg kg−1 in a suspension of carboxymethylcellulose and Tween-80. Within 5 minutes of administration, indicators of both sedative and activating behavior were recorded, including the number of horizontal movements, instances of standing on the hind legs (vertical movements), and sniffing of the cells. The criterion of the Open Field model was the observation of behavior changes in animals (including changes in horizontal and vertical movements, and an increase in sniffing of the cells).

EPM test

The EPM test was used to evaluate the anti-anxiety and sedative effects of the tested compounds in mice. The maze consists of a cruciform apparatus elevated above the floor, with a pair of open arms and a pair of closed arms positioned opposite each other. The test compounds and the reference drug were administered intraperitoneally at doses of 50 mg kg−1 and 2 mg kg−1, respectively, before the experiments. Control animals were given an emulsifier. Each group included 6 mice. The anxiolytic effect of the compounds was assessed by measuring the increase in the number of entries into the open (light) arms and the time spent in them, while ensuring that total motor activity did not increase. The sedative effect was evaluated based on the time spent in the closed (dark) arms and the number of attempts to enter the center of the maze.

FS test

The FS test was used to evaluate the ‘despair’ and ‘depression’ effects of the tested compounds. The test compounds and the reference drug were administered intraperitoneally at doses of 50 mg kg−1 and 2 mg kg−1, respectively, prior to the experiments. Following injection, the experimental animals were placed in a glass container (22 cm in height and 14 cm in diameter) filled to a depth of one-third with water. The latency to immobility, total duration of active swimming, and time spent immobile were recorded during the 6 minute period that the animals were in the container. The experiments were conducted under natural light. Each group included 6 mice.

Learning and memory test

The antiamnesic properties of the compounds were evaluated using a modified electroshock amnesia model. In this model, rats were trained to exhibit a CRPA in a dark–light setting, with the training duration recorded. The animals' time spent in the light and dark compartments was measured over 5 minutes. Following this, a single electrical shock (0.4 mA) was administered through the floor electrodes in the dark compartment (training phase). To induce amnesia, a maximum electroconvulsive seizure was performed using an electric current of 50 Hz for 0.2 seconds through corneal electrodes. This electric shock, administered immediately after training, disrupts the memory trace. The test for memory recall was conducted 24 hours post-training. During this test, control animals typically forget the training and prefer the dark compartment. An increase in the time spent by experimental rats (8 rats per group) injected with the new synthesized compounds at a dose of 50 mg kg−1 in the light compartment, compared to controls on the second day indicates the antiamnestic properties of the compounds.

Evaluation of coordination of movement (muscle relaxant)

To evaluate the coordination of movement (muscle relaxant) in experimental animals, the Rotating Rod Test45,60 in mice was used. To this end, mice were placed on a metal rod with a corrugated rubber coating, which rotated at 5 rpm. The number of experimental mice (administered with the new synthesized compounds at a dose of 50 mg kg−1 to 500 mg kg−1) that were unable to stay on the rod for 2 minutes was recorded. The number of experimental mice 6 mice per dose group. The test determines the neurotoxic effects of compounds.46–49

Docking studies

Molecular models of the studied compounds were generated using the ChemOffice software package.61 Energy minimization of the 3D models was performed using the MM2 force field, which is specifically designed for optimizing small molecule models.62 Standardization of the molecular models was achieved by obtaining Canonical SMILES representations and using *.mol2 and *.pdb file formats. Molecular models of the studied targets were retrieved from the RCSB database63 using the following identification numbers: SERT Transporter (PDB ID: 5I6X), GABAA Receptor (PDB ID: 4COF), and 5-HT1A Receptor (PDB ID: 3NYA). For molecular docking, the software packages AutoDock Vina and AutoDockTools were used.64 The reliability of the docking results was ensured by performing 5-fold repeats with 20 initial conformations, using a virtual box volume not exceeding 27.000 Å3, and conducting 200 iterations for each calculation. The selection of the best conformers was carried out on the basis of the root mean square deviation (RMSD) values during complex formation, with RMSD ≤ 2 Å.

Conclusions

Novel S-alkyl derivatives of tricyclic fused pyrano[3,4-c][1,2,4]triazolo[4,3-a]pyridine and [1,2,4]triazolo[3,4-a]isoquinolines were synthesized with higher yields by developed method. New tetracyclic derivatives of pyrano[3,4-c]thieno[3,2-e][1,2,4]triazolo[4,3-a]pyridine and thieno[2,3-c][1,2,4]triazolo[3,4-a]isoquinoline were obtained through two approaches: intramolecular cyclization of the corresponding S-alkyl derivatives and a one pot reaction based on tricyclic fused triazolo[4,3-a]pyridine and [1,2,4]triazolo[3,4-a]isoquinolines. The anticonvulsant activity data for the ten most active compounds revealed that they exhibit significant activity in the PTZ model, with ED50 values lower than those of ethosuximide under the tested conditions. While diazepam showed higher potency, direct comparisons remain qualitative due to differences in dosing. However, unlike diazepam, these compounds do not induce muscle relaxation at the studied doses. The order of activity of tested compounds toward the PTZ test can be presented as follows: 2g ≥ 2b > 2i = 3f ≥ 3a > 3k > 2a ≥ 3d > 3e > 2h. Moreover, these compounds increased the latency of TSC seizures compared to the control. In the investigation of psychotropic activity, it was found that the selected compounds exhibit activating behavior and anxiolytic effects in both the “open field” and “EPM” models. Compounds 2a, 2g, 2i and 3k significantly increased vertical movement, indicating an activation of behavior. However, all the selected compounds significantly increased the number of cell examinations compared to the control, which may indicate anxiolytic effects. The order of anxiolytic activity of tested compounds can be presented as follows: 2g ≥ 3a > 3e > 3f ≥ 3k ≥ 2i ≥ 2h > 2a > 3d > 2b. In the EPM model, the data obtained indicate anxiolytic activity, as evidenced by a statistically significant increase in the time spent in the open arms, for all selected compounds, with particularly pronounced effects observed in compound 3f. According to the “FS” model, studied compounds decrease the total immobilization time and increase the duration of active swimming exhibit antidepressant-like effects, which can be presented as follows: 3f = 3e > 2i > 3d > 2g ≥ 2b > 2h > 3a > 3k > 2a. Docking analysis reveals that all selected compounds interact with the GABAA receptor except of compounds 3a, 3d, 3e and 3f. The calculated binding constant values indicate that the interaction between the compounds and GABAA can be ranked in the following decreasing order: 2g > 2b > 2h > 2i > 2a. Compound 2g exhibits the strongest interaction (ΔG = 8.42 ± 0.42 kcal mol−1). Moreover, all the studied compounds, except 3e, interact with the Sert receptor. The obtained spatial parameters of the studied complexes indicate that the interaction is primarily carried out through amino acid residues that form the allosteric binding site. Based on the calculated binding constants, compound 3f exhibits the highest affinity (ΔG = −10.45 ± 0.52). Molecular docking investigation results indicate that all studied compounds interact with 5HT1A.Compounds 2a, 2b, 2g, 2h, 2i, 3a, and 3f bind to the intracellular loop (ICL) and the highest affinity was shown by compound 3f, with a value of 4.2 × 106. Docking analysis suggests that compounds 2a, 2b, 2g, 2h and 2i may have the potential to interact with GABAA, SERT and 5HT1A receptors, whereas compounds 3a, 3d, and 3f bind to both SERT and 5HT1A and only 3e exhibits specific binding to 5HT1A. Thus, our study highlights that the newly synthesized condensed triazolopyridine and thienotriazolopyridine derivatives exhibit neurotropic activity, with the former interacting with GABAA, SERT, and 5HT1A receptors, and the latter primarily targeting SERT and 5HT1A. The synthesized compounds show promise as new drug candidates with anticonvulsant and psychotropic properties, making them suitable for future preclinical research. These findings suggest that these scaffolds could serve as promising leads for the development of novel, effective pharmaceutical.
