High lipoprotein(a) is associated with elevated arachidonic acid levels
Authors: Waqas A. Malick, Phyu Mar, Chen Gurevitz, Jay Krishna Katragadda, Honey Zaw, Sascha N. Goonewardena, Robert S. Rosenson
Published: 2025

Introduction

High levels of lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] and Lp(a)-bound oxidized phospholipids (OxPL) are associated with an increased risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) [1]. Lp(a) is the primary carrier of OxPL on both its apolipoprotein(a) and apolipoprotein B (apoB) components [2]. Lp(a), when combined with elevated OxPL carried on apolipoprotein B particles (OxPL-apoB), is associated with significantly increased risk of ASCVD [3] and OxPL-apoB enhances transmigratory monocyte-endothelial transmigration, and serves as a trigger for systemic inflammatory responses [4]. Lp(a) promotes thrombosis by increasing platelet aggregation [5], particularly in patients carrying the rs3798220 variant in the LPA gene [6,7]. This process is accentuated in patients with high arachidonic acid (AA) levels [5].

Although Lp(a) increases platelet aggregation, the mechanisms by which this occurs remain undetermined [8]. AA is a pro-inflammatory omega-6 fatty acid that serves as a precursor to the potent platelet aggregant thromboxane A2. Conversely, eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) has anti-inflammatory effects and EPA supplementation has resulted in benefits with respect to cardiovascular outcomes [9]. The interaction between Lp(a) and eicosanoids is unknown, and a greater understanding of these interactions may guide future studies and treatment for high Lp(a) levels. In this study, we sought to explore the associations between Lp(a), OxPL-apoB, AA, and EPA in a cross-sectional analysis.

Methods

Study population

364 outpatients with known elevated Lp(a) levels (≥ 75 nmol/L) referred to the Metabolism and Lipids Program at Mount Sinai Fuster Heart Hospital between April 2021 and September 2024 underwent repeat testing for Lp(a) and measurement of OxPL-apoB, EPA and AA concentrations. This retrospective analysis was approved by the Mount Sinai Institutional Review Board (IRB 21–01,821, date of approval 07/07/2022). We excluded 52 patients with missing laboratory data.

Measurement of laboratory values

Subjects included in the study had laboratory measurements collected after an overnight minimum 12 h fast. Lp(a) (nmol/L) was measured using an immunoturbidemetric assay comprising polyclonal antibodies against apolipoprotein(a) (Diazyme, Poway, CA). All blood samples were shipped to Boston Heart Diagnostics (Framingham, MA) to measure OxPL-apoB, eicosanoids and fatty acids. Saturated, trans, and unsaturated fatty acid indices, and EPA, docosahexanoic acid, alpha-linoleic acid, and omega-6 fatty acids including AA were measured using gas liquid column chromatography/mass spectrometry after lipid extraction [10]. A lipid panel, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein and comprehensive chemistry panel were measured routinely as part of the outpatient visit (LabCorp, Inc. Burlington, NC).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages. Lp(a), OxPL-apoB, and AA: EPA were reported as medians with interquartile range. Multivariable linear regression was conducted to evaluate associations. To meet multivariable regression model assumption, log transformations of Lp(a), OxPL-apoB, AA, EPA, and AA: EPA were performed. The multivariable regression models were adjusted for age, gender (male vs female), race (White vs non-White), total cholesterol, HDL-C, statin use, aspirin use, and PCSK9 inhibitor use. Statistical significances were conducted at a two-sided alpha level of 0.05. Data cleaning and statistical analyses were performed using SAS Enterprise Guideline 9.4 (SAS Institute) and RStudio version 4.3.1 (Rstudio, PBC).

Discussion

This cross-sectional analysis of patients with high Lp(a) showed significant associations between high Lp(a), OxPL-apoB, and AA. We observed the following: (1) Lp(a) and OxPL-apoB are significantly associated with AA concentrations; (2) Neither Lp(a) nor OxPL-apoB are associated with EPA; (3) Aspirin use may attenuate the association between Lp(a) and OxPL-apoB and AA levels (Central Illustration).

Our analyses provide insights into an association between Lp(a) and AA, a mediator involved in platelet aggregation and tissue inflammation. AA is a polyunsaturated omega-6 fatty acid present in phospholipids of cell membranes. AA is pro-aggregatory and leads to the production of thromboxane A2, prostaglandin E2, and leukotriene B4, all of which are involved in promoting platelet aggregation. AA also serves as a key intermediary in inflammatory pathways. The significant association seen in our study between Lp(a) and AA suggests a potential mechanism whereby Lp(a) increases platelet aggregation and inflammation [5]. Conversely, it is also possible that the AA mediated an acute phase response resulting in increased Lp(a) and OxPL-apoB levels.

Interestingly, we observed no significant associations between Lp(a), OxPL-apoB, and EPA. In this cohort, the correlation coefficient between Lp(a) and OxPL-apoB was lower than previously reported [1]. The specimens were analyzed by Boston Heart Labs by a modified method that requires analysis on unfrozen samples versus prior studies that used frozen specimens (Diazyme, Poway, CA). Given the potent anti-inflammatory effects of EPA, we expected an inverse relationship between Lp(a), OxPL-apoB, and EPA levels. A secondary analysis of REDUCE-IT (Reduction of Cardiovascular Events with Icosapent Ethyl-Intervention Trial) showed that EPA supplementation lowered the risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes across strata of Lp(a) [11]. In our study, Lp(a) was associated with the AA:EPA ratio. AA:EPA ratio may be clinically relevant as it indicates the balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory lipid mediators. In patients with reduced levels of EPA and high AA:EPA ratio, EPA supplementation may reduce cardiovascular risk [12,13]. However, the impact of EPA supplementation on platelet reactivity in patients with high Lp(a) requires further study.

Our study showed that aspirin may attenuate the association of Lp(a) and OxPL-apoB with AA levels. These results must be interpreted cautiously due to low power, particularly as the beta-estimate for the associations were similar between aspirin users and non-aspirin users. Post hoc analyses of large prospective studies report that the benefits of aspirin on cardiovascular events is limited to patients who carry the LPA rs3798220-c genetic variant [14,15]. A recent mechanistic study conducted in 60 patients with high (≥125 nmol/L) versus low Lp(a) levels (<75 nmol/L) and platelet function studies showed no difference in the response to AA-mediated platelet aggregation in patients with and without the rs3798220-c genetic variant [5].

Limitations of our study include its small sample size conducted at a single center. However, despite the small sample size from a single healthcare center, we demonstrated significant associations between Lp(a), OxPL-apoB, AA levels, and AA:EPA ratio. Another limitation is the cross-sectional nature of our study; these findings are hypothesis generating and should not lead to any practice changes. Patients with high Lp(a), OxPL-apoB, and AA levels may benefit from early aspirin initiation, though additional studies are required to validate this link. Measurement of eicosanoid levels further risk stratify patients with high Lp(a). Last, our study population was exclusively patients with high Lp(a) as we did not measure AA levels in patients with low Lp(a) levels (<75 nmol/L). This limits our ability to quantify the difference in AA levels in patients with high Lp(a) vs low Lp(a). Further study is required to assess the link between all levels of Lp(a), OxPL-apoB, and AA.

Overall, in this single-center retrospective cross-sectional analysis of patients with high Lp(a), Lp(a) and OxPL-apoB were significantly associated with increased AA levels and AA:EPA ratio, but not EPA levels.
