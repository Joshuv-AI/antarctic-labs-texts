The nationwide prevalence of anaemia and the associated factors in the general adult population in Malaysia
Authors: Kim Sui Wan, Nor’ain Ab Wahab, Yee Mang Chan, Wan Fei Khaw, Nur Hamizah Nasaruddin, Siti Hafizah Zulkiply, Kah Kian Chong, Ping Foo Wong, Lee-Ling Lim, Noor Ani Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnaemia is a common blood disorder of global public health importance. However, the up-to-date prevalence of anaemia in Malaysia remains unknown. Therefore, this study determined the prevalence of anaemia and the factors associated with the condition in the country’s general adult population.MethodsA secondary analysis of a nationally representative dataset from a 2023 community-based prevalence study was conducted. Complete blood counts from venous blood samples were also collected. Anaemia was defined as low haemoglobin below 120 g/L in non-pregnant women and 130 g/L in men. Complex sample analyses were carried out.ResultsThere were 1,030 eligible participants. The overall prevalence of anaemia was 15.0% (95% CI: 12.1–18.4), affecting an estimated 3.6 million people in Malaysia. The prevalence of mild, moderate, and severe anaemia was 9.9%, 4.5%, and 0.6%, respectively, whereas 8.1%, 6.6%, and 0.3% had microcytic, normocytic, and macrocytic anaemia. Women (aOR: 5.83; 95% CI: 3.21–10.57) and people with diabetes (aOR: 2.15; 95% CI: 1.31–3.55) were more likely to have anaemia. In contrast, the top 20% household income category (aOR: 0.28; 95% CI: 0.11–0.67) was less likely to have the condition compared to the bottom 40% household income category.ConclusionAnaemia is a mild public health problem in Malaysia according to the WHO classification. Public health policymakers, technical programme managers, and physicians can target higher-risk populations to improve the implementation of measures to manage the anaemia epidemic in Malaysia.Keywords: Anaemia, Epidemiology, Malaysia, Public health

Background

Anaemia is a common blood disorder that happens when blood haemoglobin concentrations fall below specific predefined threshold levels [1]. The disease causes a significant global health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, affecting approximately 1.92 billion people with a prevalence rate of 24.3% [2]. It ranks as the third-leading cause of morbidity, contributing to 52 million years lived with disability, primarily due to iron deficiency [2]. In addition to its health impact, anaemia imposes substantial economic burdens by reducing productivity and increasing healthcare costs [3].

Malaysia is a Southeast Asian country with a multiethnic population. According to the Malaysian National Health and Morbidity Survey in 2015 and 2019, the overall prevalence of anaemia among the general population has decreased from 24.6% to 21.3% [4, 5]. Despite the improvement, the prevalence was still above 20% and was considered a moderate public health problem in terms of significance [6]. Various efforts and initiatives have been proposed and implemented, including the inclusion in the national action plan, to manage the issue in Malaysia [4, 7, 8]. These national programmes include the anaemia prevention programme for pregnant and postnatal mothers, nutrition education and counselling, and a supplementary feeding programme [8].

The last nationwide survey on anaemia among the general population was conducted in 2019, and the current prevalence remains unknown. Knowing the updated prevalence is essential for monitoring the magnitude of the issue in Malaysia and for post-implementation review of various public health efforts. Hence, this study aims to determine the nationwide prevalence of anaemia and the factors associated with the condition in the country’s general adult population. Identifying risk factors can help formulate targeted interventions later.

Methodology

Data source and sample size estimation

This was an analysis of a nationally representative secondary dataset on the prevalence of metabolic syndrome in 2023 [9]. The study was a cross-sectional, community-based design using stratified random sampling. Participants were enrolled from all states and federal territories in Malaysia, with the number of enumeration blocks and living quarters from each state and federal territory proportionate to its population size. Adults aged 18 years and above residing in non-institutional living quarters were included. In accordance with the original study protocol, pregnant women and women within the 12-week postpartum period were excluded from the study sample to avoid potential bias in metabolic assessments [10]. Therefore, all haemoglobin cut-off values applied in this analysis for women corresponded to non-pregnant women. Data collection included interviews, physical assessments, and venous blood sampling, including for complete blood count analysis. More details of the methodology can be found in the published study protocol [10].

The sample size was estimated using the single-proportion formula for prevalence [11]. The sample size was 637, calculated based on a national anaemia prevalence of 21.3% in 2019. We used a confidence interval of 95%, an error margin of 5%, a design effect of 2.0, with a finite population correction [4, 12]. The number was 765 after being increased by 20% to account for potential missing data. The dataset used in this study comprised 1,035 participants. After removing 5 with missing haemoglobin values, the final sample was 1,030, exceeding the required sample size.

Definition of variables

Anaemia was defined as a haemoglobin concentration below 120 g/L in non-pregnant women and 130 g/L in men [13]. Mild, moderate, and severe anaemia were defined as having haemoglobin between 110 and 119 g/L, 80–109 g/L, and below 80 g/L, respectively, in non-pregnant women. The corresponding threshold levels were 110–129 g/L, 80–109 g/L, and below 80 g/L for men [13]. Anaemia was also classified morphologically based on the mean corpuscular volume (MCV) into microcytic (MCV < 82 femtolitres), normocytic (MCV 82–98 femtolitres), and macrocytic (MCV > 98 femtolitres) anaemia, according to the laboratory reference ranges. This classification was used because it is the most clinically useful in delineating the underlying causes of anaemia [14, 15].

The sociodemographic variables included gender, ethnicity, age, education level, and household income categories. The main ethnic groups in Malaysia are Malays, Chinese, Indians, and the Bumiputera groups. Household income was categorised into the Top 20% (T20), Middle 40% (M40), and Bottom 40% (B40) groups in accordance with the Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM) classification [16]. Inadequate sleep among adults aged ≥ 18 years was defined as an average sleep duration below seven hours [17]. Body mass index (BMI) was categorised according to Malaysian Clinical Practice Guidelines: <18.5 kg/m2 for underweight, 18.5 to 22.9 kg/m2 for normal weight, 23.0 to 27.4 kg/m2 for overweight, and ≥ 27.5 kg/m2 for obesity [18]. Physical activity level was categorised into physically active or inactive based on the WHO recommendations [19]. Diabetes, hypertension, and hypercholesterolemia were self-reported by participants based on previous diagnoses by medical personnel, following the World Health Organisation questionnaire [20].

Statistical analysis

We conducted complex sample analyses with weightage factors applied. The weights were derived from the inverse probability of choosing a living quarter, non-response rates, and post-stratification factors by gender, age, and ethnic group [21]. We reported anaemia prevalence alongside projected populations. We compared the weighted proportions of people with and without anaemia using complex-samples tests of independence of rows and columns, and reported the P values. Variables with P values < 0.25 and those considered clinically important were entered into a complex-samples multiple logistic regression analysis [22]. The classification table, coefficient of determination (R2), and area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) curve were reported. Interaction and multicollinearity were checked. IBM SPSS Statistics 27 was used for all analyses.

Discussion

Taken together with the previous national surveys, the prevalence of anaemia in Malaysia has consistently reduced from 24.6% in 2015 to 21.3% in 2019 to the current 15.0% [4]. Similarly, the prevalence of mild, moderate, and severe anaemia has also decreased across all severity categories [4]. Nevertheless, the overall burden of anaemia is still huge, as it affects an estimated 3.6 million people and remains a public health problem in Malaysia [6]. The observed reduction in anaemia prevalence may partly reflect the cumulative impact of national nutrition and public health initiatives [8, 23]. However, given methodological differences across surveys and the exclusion of pregnant women from the present study, the improvement in the temporal trend should be cautiously interpreted. The study population in those two national surveys was individuals aged 15 years and above, instead of adults aged 18 years and above in this current study [4, 5]. Moreover, previous surveys assessed capillary haemoglobin levels using point-of-care testing (POCT) systems instead of laboratory-based venous haemoglobin levels used in the current study [4, 5]. Capillary samples measured using POCT devices could yield higher haemoglobin levels than venous samples measured using automated analysers, causing some false negative results [24]. A systematic review reported that most studies found a higher level of haemoglobin in capillary than venous blood [25]. Hence, the anaemia prevalence in the current study, using laboratory-based venous haemoglobin levels, should be higher than in previous studies based on higher haemoglobin levels from capillary samples using POCT devices.

A direct comparison of anaemia prevalence with the 24.3% global average and 43.0% in South Asia cannot be made, as the latest Global Burden of Disease Study reported prevalence across all ages, whereas our current study focused only on adults aged 18 years and above [2]. If anaemia prevalence is measured across all ages, we expect our result to be higher because children, especially those 6–59 months, tend to have a higher prevalence [1]. Indeed, the previous national survey reported that 46.5% of children aged 6–59 months in Malaysia had anaemia [26]. According to the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), the global and Southeast Asian prevalence of anaemia among the population aged 20 years and above was 22.3% and 23.0%, respectively, both higher than our current study estimate [27]. Our anaemia prevalence fell between those of our neighbouring countries: 8.6% in Singapore, 20.7% in Thailand, and 23.9% in Indonesia [27].

The proportions of microcytic, normocytic, and macrocytic anaemias in this study are comparable to those from the Malaysian Cohort (TMC) study [28]. It is not surprising that microcytic anaemia is the most prevalent because iron deficiency is the most common cause of anaemia worldwide [2]. The common differential diagnosis for microcytic anaemia includes iron deficiency anaemia and thalassemia [14]. In the Malaysian Cohort (TMC) study, iron deficiency was postulated as the primary cause of anaemia in the country [28]. The Malaysian Adult Nutrition Survey further reported that the overall population consumed only 78.4% of the recommended iron intake, and adult women achieved only 52.0% of the recommended level [29]. Nevertheless, thalassaemia is not uncommon in Malaysia [30]. The disease is the most common hereditary blood disorder in Malaysia, and about 4.5% of Malaysians are estimated to be thalassaemia carriers [31]. As iron biomarkers and haemoglobin electrophoresis were not performed in the present study, the underlying aetiology of microcytic anaemia cannot be determined. Given the relatively high prevalence of thalassaemia carriers in Malaysia, morphological classification alone is insufficient to attribute microcytic anaemia solely to iron deficiency, and further aetiological investigation is warranted.

Normocytic anaemia is expected to be fairly common as it is associated with the early phase of iron deficiency anaemia and anaemia of chronic disease; the latter is the second most common anaemia globally [14]. Noncommunicable diseases such as diabetes and chronic kidney disease are common public health issues in Malaysia [21, 32, 33], and these diseases are associated with anaemia [14, 34, 35]. Meanwhile, macrocytic anaemia is expected to be less common, as the condition is associated with relatively rare differential diagnoses such as primary bone marrow failure and megaloblastic anaemia [14].

Women are more likely to develop anaemia than men due to a combination of factors, including inadequate intake of essential nutrients, menstruation, and underlying health conditions [1, 28]. A study in Pakistan reported that women who mainly consumed vegetables had the highest prevalence of anaemia [36]. Additionally, a local study reported that over a quarter of vegetarian women were anaemic, further emphasising the link between dietary preferences and anaemia risk [37]. Besides that, anaemia is frequently caused by heavy menstruation and bleeding during and after childbirth, especially if iron stores are not adequately replenished [1].

Our findings that individuals in the top income group are less likely to experience anaemia align with existing literature, highlighting the protective role of higher socioeconomic status. The Global Burden of Disease Study 2019 reported that regions with higher socio-economic development experienced a lower burden of anaemia [38]. Individuals with higher incomes typically have better access to healthcare services [39], enabling early diagnosis and effective management of conditions that can contribute to anaemia. In addition, higher income is often linked to the ability to afford and access a more diverse and balanced diet, which reduces the risk of nutritional deficiencies, particularly iron, vitamin B12, and folate, which are the common nutritional contributors to anaemia [40].

An important insight in this study is that over a quarter of our population with diabetes had concurrent anaemia, similar to a meta-analysis with a pooled anaemia prevalence of 27% among type 2 diabetes patients [35]. Despite anaemia being common in type 2 diabetes patients, the coexistence of anaemia and diabetes seemed to be inadequately recognised and insufficiently mentioned throughout the clinical guidelines [34, 35]. It was found that anaemia is an independent risk factor for mortality in patients with diabetes [34]. Thus, anaemia screening should be included in the routine assessment of diabetes complications to enable effective treatment [34, 35, 41].

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is the use of automated haematology analysers in a single accredited laboratory to assess haemoglobin levels using venous samples. Venous blood assessment by automated analyser, rather than capillary POCT, is the gold standard for diagnosing anaemia in the clinical setting [25]. Using a single centralised and accredited laboratory service ensures quality assurance of analysers to prevent measurement bias [10]. Thus, our study provides an accurate, up-to-date, nationally representative estimate of anaemia prevalence for policymakers and programme managers to gauge the disease burden and strategise accordingly. While some local studies have reported anaemia prevalence by morphological categories, the study populations were targeted to specific populations [28, 42, 43]. To our best knowledge, this is among the first studies to report anaemia prevalence by morphological features based on a nationally representative dataset in Malaysia.

We acknowledge several limitations in this study. First, due to the cross-sectional study design, the temporal relationships between the independent and dependent variables are unclear, and causality cannot be established. Second, the apparent temporal decline in anaemia prevalence in Malaysia should be interpreted with caution, given differences in study design, haemoglobin measurement methods, and the exclusion of pregnant women in the current analysis. Third, this analysis was based on a secondary dataset originally designed to investigate metabolic syndrome. As such, variable selection was constrained by data availability rather than an anaemia-specific conceptual framework. Important biological determinants of anaemia, such as iron status indicators (e.g., ferritin), renal function parameters, inflammatory markers, menstrual history, and dietary intake, were not available. Therefore, causal pathways could not be examined, the explanatory depth of the regression model was limited, and residual confounding might remain [1]. Fourth, diabetes, hypertension, and hypercholesterolaemia were self-reported and may introduce misclassification and recall bias, particularly among undiagnosed individuals. Finally, the underlying causes of anaemia were unknown because further tests, such as serum iron, ferritin, and haemoglobin electrophoresis, were not done.

This study shows that anaemia remains a public health problem in Malaysia [6]. It is essential to raise awareness and knowledge of anaemia among the general population in the country. We recommend that future preventive and interventional programmes target higher-risk populations to improve the implementation of measures to manage the anaemia epidemic in Malaysia. Given the high co-occurrence of anaemia and diabetes, clinical practice guidelines on diabetes management may consider incorporating annual anaemia screening to facilitate early detection and treatment [44]. Besides that, future research can be conducted to determine the prevalence of specific causes of anaemia.

Conclusion

Anaemia is a mild public health problem in Malaysia according to the WHO classification, affecting approximately 3.6 million adults [6]. Women and people with diabetes are more likely to have anaemia. In contrast, the highest household income category is less likely to have the condition. Public health policymakers, technical programme managers, and physicians can target especially the higher-risk populations to manage the anaemia epidemic in Malaysia.
