Plasma exosome lncRNA panel as a non-invasive biomarker for molecular diagnosis of systemic lupus erythematosus
Authors: Zhi Li, Shujun Wan, Xueqin Li, Hui Yang, Kun Lv, Xiaolong Zhu, Mengying Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Exosome-derived long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have emerged as promising diagnostic biomarkers in various diseases. However, their diagnostic potential in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) remains largely unexplored. This study aimed to characterize plasma exosomal lncRNA profiles and identify candidate lncRNAs with diagnostic and disease-monitoring potential in SLE. Plasma exosomes were isolated from SLE patients and healthy controls (HCs), followed by high-throughput sequencing to characterize exosomal lncRNA expression profiles. Differentially expressed lncRNAs were identified and subsequently validated using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). Correlations between the validated exosomal lncRNAs and clinical indicators of SLE were assessed. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the identified lncRNAs. Three plasma-derived exosomal lncRNAs—ENSG00000229882, MIR4713HG, and LINC00620—were significantly upregulated in SLE patients compared with HCs. Logistic regression analysis identified MIR4713HG and LINC00620 as predictors of SLE. A combined diagnostic model incorporating these two lncRNAs demonstrated diagnostic capacity for distinguishing SLE patients from HCs, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.759. Notably, exosomal LINC00620 expression was significantly upregulated in patients with active SLE compared with those with inactive disease and showed a positive correlation with SLE Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2 K scores). Plasma exosomal ENSG00000229882, MIR4713HG, and LINC00620 were significantly upregulated in SLE, indicating their potential as non-invasive diagnostic biomarkers. Particularly, LINC00620 showed a positive correlation with disease activity, suggesting its clinical utility for both SLE diagnosis and disease monitoring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10238-026-02180-w.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic, systemic autoimmune disorder that involves multiple organ systems and is associated with significant morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. SLE predominantly affects women of reproductive age, with an estimated female-to-male ratio of 9:1 [3]. The pathogenesis of SLE is multifactorial, arising from the complex interplay of genetic, immunological, endocrine, and environmental factors. These interactions disrupt immune tolerance to self-antigens, leading to the formation of pathogenic autoantibodies that mediate tissue damage through multiple immune mechanisms [4]. Accurate assessment of SLE disease activity is essential for timely intervention and effective disease management. Conventional clinical and laboratory parameters—such as the SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI), complement component 3 (C3), complement component 4 (C4), anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) antibodies, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and C-reactive protein (CRP)—are widely employed to evaluate disease activity and organ involvement [5, 6]. However, these parameters often lack sensitivity and specificity, reflecting only certain aspects of the disease process. Consequently, the identification of novel, reliable, and non-invasive biomarkers is crucial to enhance diagnostic accuracy, disease monitoring, and the development of individualized therapeutic strategies in SLE management.

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), a class of non-protein-coding RNA transcripts exceeding 200 nucleotides in length, have emerged as key regulators of gene expression at epigenetic, transcriptional, and post-transcriptional levels and have been implicated in SLE pathogenesis [7]. Several long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) such as GAS5, MALAT1 and NEAT1 are dysregulated in SLE patients. The dysregulation of lncRNAs in the peripheral blood (including peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), serum, plasma, and exosomes) of SLE patients has potentiated them as biomarkers for diagnosis, disease activity and therapeutic response [8]. For instance, PBMC-derived lncRNAs such as NEAT1, LINC0949, and LINC0597 have shown promise as diagnostic biomarkers for SLE, while taurine upregulated gene 1 (TUG1) has been associated with both SLE diagnosis and disease activity [9]. Similarly, a plasma lncRNA panel comprising LINC0597, LINC0640, LINC5150, GAS5, and LINC7074 demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy for distinguishing SLE from other autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [10]. Additionally, RP11-273G15.2 expression in B cells has been identified as a potential biomarker for both SLE diagnosis and disease activity, underscoring the clinical value of lncRNA-based molecular profiling in the management of this condition [11].

Exosomes are small endocytic membrane-derived vesicles ranging from 30 to 120 nm in diameter that mediate intercellular communication by transferring proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids to recipient cells [12]. Emerging evidence suggests that exosomal-derived non-coding RNAs, such as lncRNAs, microRNAs (miRNAs), and transfer RNA-derived small RNAs (tsRNAs), play critical roles in the pathogenesis of various autoimmune diseases, such as SLE and RA [13, 14]. In SLE, exosomal miRNAs were shown to be differentially expressed in plasma [15], with plasma-derived exosomal miR-122-5p identified as a potential therapeutic target [16]. Serum exosomal miR-21 and miR-155 expression have been identified as promising diagnostic biomarkers for both SLE and lupus nephritis (LN) [17]. Furthermore, renal tissue-derived exosomal let-7f-5p has been identified as a potential biomarker for SLE with anti-inflammatory properties [18], while urinary exosomal tsRNAs have emerged as a novel diagnostic and predictive marker for lupus nephritis [19]. Despite growing evidence implicating exosomal non-coding RNAs in autoimmune pathophysiology, the diagnostic potential and mechanistic roles of exosomal lncRNA in SLE remain underexplored and warrant further investigations.

This study comprehensively characterized plasma exosomal lncRNA expression profiles in SLE patients (including both active and inactive disease states) and in health controls (HCs) to identify specific lncRNAs with diagnostic potential for SLE. By identifying plasma-derived exosomal lncRNA signatures associated with SLE, this study aimed to develop a highly sensitive and specific non-invasive biomarker panel for early diagnosis and disease monitoring, potentially advancing personalized clinical management of SLE.

Materials and methods

Study approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Yijishan Hospital, Wannan Medical College (Approval No.: 20240113) and strictly adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment in the study.

Study population and sample collection

Peripheral blood samples were obtained from 102 patients with SLE and 102 HCs at Yijishan Hospital of Wannan Medical College. SLE diagnosis was established based on the 2019 European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR)/American College of Rheumatology (ACR) Classification Criteria for SLE. Patients with concurrent autoimmune disorders, active infections, chronic systemic diseases, or pregnancy were excluded to minimize confounding factors. General demographic characteristics and relevant clinical laboratory data were systematically collected for all participants. Disease activity was quantitatively evaluated using the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2 K). The study included consecutive SLE patients diagnosed between October 1, 2023, and February 28, 2025. Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics of participants are summarized in Table 1.

Exosome isolation and identification

Plasma exosomes were isolated from peripheral blood samples using a differential ultracentrifugation protocol. Briefly, frozen plasma samples were rapidly thawed at 37 °C and transferred to sterile centrifuge tubes. Samples were centrifuged at 2000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 45 min at 4 °C to eliminate larger extracellular vesicles and apoptotic bodies. The supernatant was subsequently filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane to remove residual particles. The filtrate was subjected to ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 70 min at 4 °C using an ultracentrifuge equipped with an ultraspeed rotor (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). The resulting pellet was resuspended in 10 mL of pre-chilled 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and subjected to a second ultracentrifugation under the same conditions to further purify the exosomes. The final exosomal pellet was resuspended in 300 µL of ice-cold 1× PBS for downstream analyses. For morphological examination, 5 µL of the exosomal suspension was placed on a carbon-coated copper grid, stained with 3% uranyl acetate dihydrate, and visualized under a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi) at 100 kV. Another 5 µL aliquot was analyzed using a Flow NanoAnalyzer (NanoFCM) to determine particle size distribution and concentration. To confirm exosomal identity, 70 µL of the suspension was subjected to western blot analysis for canonical exosomal surface markers (Alix, CD9, and Flotillin-1). The remaining exosomal suspension was used for subsequent RNA extraction and molecular analysis.

RNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from purified plasma exosomes using the miRNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen, cat. no. 217004, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop™ OneC Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and RNA integrity was evaluated using the LabChip GX Touch Nucleic Acid Analyzer (PerkinElmer, USA). Only RNA samples meeting the quality thresholds were quantified using the Qubit3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to ensure accurate RNA input for library construction. High-quality RNA samples were used for lncRNA library preparation using the KCTM Digital Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Kit (Seqhealth Tech. Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were size-selected to obtain fragments between 200 and 500 base pairs, enriched by PCR amplification, and re-quantified using the Qubit3.0 Fluorometer. Final libraries were subjected to paired-end sequencing (PE150 mode) on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (MGI), generating comprehensive profiles of exosomal lncRNA expression.

RNA sequencing data processing and analysis

Raw sequencing reads were first filtered using Trimmomatic (version 0.36) to remove low-quality bases and adapter sequences. Clean reads were further processed with custom scripts to eliminate duplication biases introduced during library preparation and sequencing. Reads sharing identical unique molecular identifiers (UMI) were clustered together, and within each cluster, sequences with > 95% similarity were grouped into sub-clusters. A single consensus sequence was generated for each sub-cluster through multiple sequence alignment, thereby removing sequencing errors and PCR-derived duplicates. This process yielded a high-confidence set of deduplicated reads for downstream analysis. Deduplicated reads were aligned to the Homo sapiens reference genome using STRA software (version 2.5.3a) with default settings. Read counts corresponding to exon regions of each gene were quantified using featureCounts (Subread version 1.5.1; Bioconductor). Gene expression levels were normalized across all samples as reads per kilobase per million mapped reads (RPKM). Differentially expressed genes between experimental groups were identified using the edgeR package (version 3.12.1). Statistical significance was defined as a p < 0.05 and fold-change (FC) > 2.

Discovery phase

Six plasma exosome samples (three from SLE patients and three from HCs) were analyzed by high-throughput lncRNA sequencing using the Seqhealth platform (Wuhan, China). The three SLE cases exhibited typical clinical features, covered moderate to high disease activity. Differentially expressed lncRNAs between SLE and HC groups were identified using the Mann–Whitney U test. Candidate lncRNAs with p < 0.05 and log2FC > 5 were selected for further validation by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR).

Training phase

Candidate lncRNAs identified in the discovery phase were validated in a training cohort consisting of 72 participants (36 SLE patients and 36 HCs) using qRT-PCR. We used stepwise logistic regression for variable selection, with an entry criterion of P < 0.05 and a removal criterion of P > 0.1. Overfitting was controlled through variable screening, resampling, cross-validation, and ridge regression. The expression levels of the validated lncRNAs were used to construct a diagnostic lncRNA panel through a logistic regression model to distinguish SLE patients from HCs.

Validation phase

The diagnostic performance of the lncRNA panel was assessed in an independent validation cohort comprising 126 samples (63 SLEs and 63 HCs). Model parameters derived from the training phase were applied to the validation dataset, and diagnostic accuracy was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from plasma-derived exosomes using the HiPure Exosome RNA Midi Kit (Guangzhou Magen Biotechnology, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The purified RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific). qRT-PCR was subsequently performed using the QuantiNova™ SYBR® Green PCR Kit (Qiagen) on a Bio-Rad CXF96 PCR system (Hercules, CA, USA). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the internal control for normalization of lncRNA expression levels. Relative expression levels of target lncRNAs were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. All qRT-PCR primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), and the corresponding primer sequences are provided in Table S1.

Statistical analyses

Baseline demographic and clinical data were summarized using appropriate descriptive statistics, with continuous variables expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences in lncRNA expression levels between the SLE and HC groups were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test. For qRT-PCR validation, intergroup comparisons were performed using either the unpaired t test for normally distributed variables or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for non-normally distributed variables. A stepwise logistic regression analysis was employed to identify diagnostic lncRNA biomarkers from the training dataset. The predicted probability of an SLE diagnosis generated from the logistic regression model was used as the continuous surrogate variable to construct ROC curves, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate diagnostic accuracy. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 18.0 and GraphPad Prism 10.0 software. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

A total of 204 participants were included in this study, comprising 102 patients with SLE and 102 age- and sex-matched HCs. Key demographic and clinical characteristics of SLE patients in both the training and independent validation cohorts are summarized in Table 1.

Isolation and characterization of plasma-derived exosomes

Plasma-derived exosomes were successfully isolated from both SLE patients and HCs and characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), NanoFCM, and western blotting. TEM examination revealed that the isolated vesicles predominantly exhibited the characteristic round or cup-shaped morphology with well-defined membrane structures, and diameters ranging from 50 to 150 nm (Fig. 1A and B). Quantitative analysis using NanoFCM showed a reduced concentration of exosomal particles in plasma samples from SLE patients compared with HCs. Western blot analysis further confirmed the expression of established exosomal protein markers, including Alix, CD9, and Flotillin-1, thereby validating the successful isolation (Fig. 1C).

Plasma-derived exosomal lncRNA screening

To identify plasma exosomal lncRNAs associated with SLE, high-throughput sequencing was performed on plasma-derived exosomal RNA isolated from SLE patients and HCs (n = 3 per group). The sequencing analysis identified a total of 2,529 differentially expressed lncRNAs between the SLE and HC groups (threshold: FC > 2 and p < 0.05). Among the differentially expressed lncRNAs, 2,269 lncRNAs were upregulated, while 260 were downregulated in SLE (Fig. 2A). To ensure analytical robustness and biological relevance, 21 lncRNAs exhibiting strong differential expression (log2FC > 5) and relatively high expression levels (RPKM > 10 in both groups) were prioritized for further evaluation. Hierarchical clustering analysis of these 21 candidate lncRNAs demonstrated distinct expression patterns that effectively differentiated SLE patients from HCs (Fig. 2B), as visualized by heatmap analysis, suggesting their potential diagnostic utility.

qRT-PCR validation of exosomal lncRNA expression

Eight lncRNAs from the 21 prioritized candidates were selected based on qRT-PCR detectability and expression stability for subsequent validation. The expression levels of these eight lncRNAs were first analyzed by qRT-PCR in an independent validation cohort comprising 36 SLE patients and 36 HCs. Of these eight candidates, three lncRNAs—ENSG00000229882, MIR4713HG, and LINC00620—showed significantly elevated expression levels in plasma-derived exosomes from SLE patients compared with HCs (p < 0.05; Fig. 3). To confirm these findings, qRT-PCR validation was further conducted in a larger independent cohort comprising 63 SLE patients and 63 HCs, which consistently demonstrated upregulation of these three exosomal lncRNAs (Fig. 4).

Correlation between exosomal lncRNAs and SLE clinical parameters

To elucidate the clinical relevance of the identified plasma-derived exosomal lncRNAs, correlation analyses were performed between their expression levels and key clinical indicators of SLE disease activity. Among the three upregulated lncRNAs, LINC00620 expression exhibited a significant positive correlation with the SLEDAI score (r = 0.261, p < 0.05). LINC00620 expression also correlated with C3 and C4, but not with anti-dsDNA antibody levels. In contrast, ENSG00000229882 and MIR4713HG showed no significant correlation with SLEDAI scores (r = 0.018, p = 0.890; r = 0.098, p = 0.446, respectively), C3, or C4 levels. However, both were significantly associated with anti-dsDNA antibody levels (Table 2). All three lncRNAs exhibited a consistent negative correlation with platelet count (PLT) in SLE patients (r = − 0.263, p = 0.037 for LINC00620; r = − 0.337, p = 0.007 for ENSG00000229882; r = − 0.284, p = 0.024 for MIR4713HG). Additionally, MIR4713HG expression correlated positively with activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) values (r = 0.381, p = 0.002).

Correlation between relative expressions of plasma exosomal lncRNAs and clinical parameters in SLE patients

To further explore their clinical significance, the 63 SLE patients in the validation cohort were stratified into active (n = 35) and inactive (n = 28) SLE subgroups based on SLEDAI scores. LINC00620 expression was significantly higher in the active SLE subgroup than in the inactive subgroup (p < 0.05; Table S2 and Fig. 5), supporting its role as a potential biomarker of disease activity. Stepwise logistic regression applied to the training dataset identified LINC00620 as an independent risk factor for active SLE (OR = 2.566 [95% CI: 1.188–5.543], p < 0.05; Table S3). Collectively, these findings suggest that LINC00620 reflects SLE disease activity, while ENSG00000229882 and MIR4713HG may contribute to autoantibody-associated immune dysregulation.

Establishment of the predictive exosomal lncRNA panel

The diagnostic performance of the identified exosomal lncRNAs was evaluated using ROC curve analysis. All three exosomal lncRNAs demonstrated comparable diagnostic accuracy in distinguishing SLE patients from HCs, with AUC values of 0.662 (95% CI: 0.566–0.758, p < 0.01) for ENSG00000229882, 0.702 (95% CI: 0.610–0.794, p < 0.0001) for MIR4713HG, and 0.718 (95% CI: 0.629–0.808, p < 0.0001) for LINC00620 (Fig. 6A). Stepwise logistic regression applied to the training dataset identified MIR4713HG and LINC00620 as predictors of SLE diagnosis (Table S4). A combined diagnostic model integrating MIR4713HG and LINC00620 expression was subsequently constructed, and the predicted probability derived from this model was used for the ROC curve analysis. The result revealed that the combined lncRNA panel exhibited enhanced diagnostic performance compared with individual lncRNAs, achieving an AUC of 0.759 (95% CI: 0.675–0.843; Fig. 6B; Table S5). We used the Hosmer‑Lemeshow test to assess the calibration of the model (training cohort: χ² = 7.34, p = 0.55. validation cohort: χ² = 9.37, p = 0.32), indicating that the predicted probabilities of the model were consistent with the observed probabilities. Collectively, these findings indicate that the combined exosomal lncRNA panel (MIR4713HG and LINC00620) provides superior diagnostic accuracy over single biomarkers in distinguishing SLE patients from healthy individuals, indicating its potential clinical applicability as a non-invasive diagnostic tool for SLE.

Discussion

Previous studies have shown that the concentration of EVs is a key determinant of their biological and therapeutic activity across various diseases. The abundance of exosomes influences their regulatory effects on target cells and modulates downstream cellular responses. For instance, exosomes derived from odontoblasts have been shown to suppress the differentiation of dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) in a concentration-dependent manner [20]. Furthermore, exosome concentrations are influenced by cell type. A previous study reported significantly elevated exosome concentrations in human keratoconus fibroblast cells (HKC) compared with healthy corneal fibroblasts (HCF) [21]. Similarly, six of eight studies evaluating exosome concentrations in patients undergoing remote ischemic conditioning (RIC) reported significant increases in circulating exosome levels, suggesting a systemic adaptation to ischemic stress [22]. In oncology, elevated exosome concentrations have also been observed in the blood of cancer patients, where they transport tumor-specific proteins and nucleic acids that promote tumor proliferation, angiogenesis, and metastasis [23]. Previous study has reported increased exosome levels in SLE [24]. In contrast to these findings, the present study demonstrated a significant reduction in plasma exosome concentration in SLE patients compared with HCs. The inconsistent results regarding plasma exosome levels in SLE patients compared with previous reports may be due to differences in sample size. In our study, we only detected plasma exosome concentrations in 3 healthy controls and 3 SLE patients separately. In previous studies, dozens of samples were used to analyze the differences in plasma exosome concentrations between healthy controls and SLE patients. Additionally, such alterations may reflect impaired vesicle secretion by immune cells or enhanced clearance of exosomes by phagocytic mechanisms during systemic inflammation.

Early and accurate diagnosis is essential for improving disease management and prognosis in SLE. In recent years, non-invasive diagnostic strategies, particularly plasma exosomal lncRNAs profiling, have gained increasing attention due to their potential for high specificity and sensitivity in early disease detection [25]. Transcriptomic studies have linked specific lncRNAs, including LINC01015, LINC01986, AC087257.1, and AC022596.1, to key biological processes, such as inflammation, fibrosis, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and actin cytoskeleton regulation. Additionally, several of these lncRNAs have been implicated as promising therapeutic targets in LN [26]. Notably, exosomal lncRNAs such as LINC00667 and DANCR were found to be significantly upregulated in SLE, with DANCR expression positively correlating with SLE disease activity, further supporting the diagnostic and prognostic potential of exosome-associated lncRNAs [27]. In our study, 21 lncRNAs were identified in the discovery phase. However, lncRNAs previously reported in similar analyses of SLE do not appear to be included. Inconsistencies between our results and previously reported SLE-associated lncRNAs may be attributed to multiple factors, including variations in patient cohorts, disease activity, sequencing platforms, and bioinformatic filtering criteria. Such discrepancies do not invalidate the value of exosome-derived lncRNAs. Instead, they show their unique biological source, stability, and disease-specific accumulation in exosome. The low overlap with previously reported lncRNAs also suggests that exosomal lncRNAs can serve as specific and sensitive biomarkers. They reflect local immune disorders and cell-to-cell communication in SLE, rather than general systemic gene expression changes.

Consistent with these findings, the present study identified three plasma-derived exosomal lncRNAs—ENSG00000229882, MIR4713HG, and LINC00620—that were significantly upregulated in SLE patients compared with HCs. Subsequent stepwise logistic regression analysis identified MIR4713HG and LINC00620 as predictors of SLE. The final diagnostic model integrating these lncRNAs exhibited discriminative ability in differentiating SLE from HCs, achieving an AUC of 0.759. These findings indicate the potential of these lncRNA signatures as non-invasive biomarkers for SLE diagnosis. It is necessary to conduct comprehensive biomarker analysis on patients and HCs in the future to verify whether the panel provides additional diagnostic value beyond traditional indicators. Multiple studies have shown that MIR4713HG is associated with tumors and immunity. MIR4713HG may become an important therapeutic target for oral tongue squamous cell carcinoma (OTSCC) and colorectal cancer (CRC). MIR4713HG has been identified as an immune-related lncRNA and has been found to have prognostic value in tongue squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) and bladder cancer (BLCA) [28–31]. Furthermore, several researches have confirmed that let-7c-5p, a ceRNA of MIR4713HG, can not only serve as a potential predictor of autoimmune diseases, but also act as a key post transcriptional regulator [28, 32, 33]. In contrast, LINC00620 is a newly identified lncRNA whose biological function remains largely unexplored.

A key challenge in SLE management lies in accurately assessing and monitoring disease activity. In this study, SLE activity was evaluated using the SLEDAI-2 K, a well-established clinical metric for quantifying disease severity [34]. The results revealed that plasma exosomal LINC00620 was significantly upregulated in SLE patients compared with HCs. Its expression levels also significantly differed between active and inactive SLE subgroups. Furthermore, LINC00620 expression was positively correlated with SLEDAI-2 K scores, suggesting that it may reflect the systemic inflammatory burden and overall disease activity. These findings contrast with studies on cell-associated lncRNA. For example, ENST00000597482 expression in PBMCs was downregulated in SLE patients compared with HCs and negatively correlated with both SLEDAI-2 K scores and autoantibody titers [35]. Similarly, lnc-FOSB-1:1 expression in neutrophils was significantly reduced in SLE patients compared with healthy individuals and negatively correlated with SLEDAI-2 K scores [36]. Such disparities emphasize that exosomal lncRNAs, which are encapsulated and transported through stable extracellular vesicles, may convey distinct biological information compared with intracellular lncRNAs, reflecting intercellular communication processes rather than intrinsic cellular expression changes. The level of anti-dsDNA is an important serological parameter for the diagnosis and monitoring of SLE [37]. Therefore, there is a positive or negative relationship between the expression level of SLE associated lncRNAs and the level of anti-dsDNA. For instance, elevated FAS-AS1 expression levels have been significantly associated with positive anti-dsDNA titers [38], whereas reduced lnc7514 expression levels have been observed in anti-dsDNA(+) patients compared with anti-dsDNA(–) patients [39]. In contrast, the present study found no significant correlation between LINC00620 expression and circulating anti-dsDNA antibody levels. This observation suggests that LINC00620 may be more suitable for studying the pathogenesis of SLE rather than as a qualified potential biomarker.

The high heterogeneity in the course of SLE hinders the comprehensive application of SLE biomarkers, and a single or few lncRNA biomarkers are difficult to cover all patient subgroups. The recent observation of lncRNA dysregulation in SLE patients provides an opportunity to elucidate the pathological biology of this disease. LncRNA has been shown to affect several key pathways in the pathogenesis of SLE, and these studies enable the identification of therapeutic targets for SLE [8]. Exosomes are nanosized extracellular vesicles (EVs) that serve as essential mediators of intercellular communication between cells. By transferring bioactive molecules—nucleic acids (lncRNA), lipids, proteins, exosomes have emerged as attractive candidates for targeted drug delivery and therapeutic applications in precision therapy [40]. In SLE, circulating exosomes have been shown to trigger proinflammatory immune responses, contributing directly to disease pathogenesis through the transfer of immunostimulatory molecules such as RNA, DNA, and autoantigens [41]. This pathogenic involvement highlights the therapeutic potential of exosomes in SLE and related autoimmune conditions. For example, mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosome-educated macrophages alleviate systemic lupus erythematosus by delivery of miR-16 and miR-21 [42]. Future research should focus more on improve exosome isolation, heterogeneity, cargo loading, and targeting precision to ensure cell and SLE targeting specificity.

Conclusions

This study comprehensively assessed plasma-derived exosomal lncRNA expression profiles in SLE patients using high-throughput sequencing followed by qRT-PCR validation. The identified lncRNA panel, particularly MIR4713HG and LINC00620, demonstrated discriminative capacity in distinguishing SLE patients from HCs. Notably, LINC00620 emerged as a novel and predictor of active SLE, showing a significant positive correlation with SLEDAI-2 K scores. Collectively, these findings indicate the diagnostic potential of circulating exosomal lncRNA as non-invasive biomarkers for SLE diagnosis and disease monitoring. However, large-scale, multicenter prospective studies, inclusion of other connective tissue diseases, are warranted to validate these findings and elucidate the precise molecular mechanism through which exosomal lncRNAs contribute to SLE pathogenesis.
