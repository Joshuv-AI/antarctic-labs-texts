Red Blood Cell Parameters as Cost-effective Biomarkers in Normal and Altered Glycemic States: A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: Priyadarsini Dhavakumar, Latha Ramalingam, Vanathy Karunamoorthy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Hemoglobin A1C levels, along with fasting and postprandial blood glucose levels, are the tests routinely used in the diagnosis and management of diabetes. Complete blood count (CBC) is a simple blood test commonly done for various conditions, from which various red blood cell (RBC) parameters can be assessed. The aim of this study is to assess the relationship between blood glucose levels and RBC parameters, which can be assessed from routine blood investigations done during follow-up.Materials and Methods:The subjects were categorized into three groups based on their hemoglobin A1C (HbA1C) levels: normal, prediabetic, and diabetic groups. The first normal group (HbA1C <5.7%), the prediabetic group (HbA1C 5.7%–6.4%), and the diabetic group (HbA1C >6.5%). CBC was done, and total RBC count, hematocrit, hemoglobin, mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), MCH concentration (MCHC), and RBC distribution width (RDW) were determined. The data from the three groups were analyzed and compared for statistical significance.Results:Chi-square test and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used for analysis of data in testing for statistical significance between the groups. Significant statistical difference was observed between the groups in MCV, MCHC, and RDW. The diabetic group observed the highest MCV (93.84 ± 8.98), highest RDW (44.59 ± 1.67), and least MCHC (33.09 ± 2.01) when compared with the prediabetic and normal groups.Conclusion:This study concludes that RBC parameters, which are often overlooked need to be considered as important indicators of glucose regulation and inflammatory status in addition to routine HbA1C and blood glucose assessment for comprehensive management of patients.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a growing major public health concern globally, which continues to intensify at a rapid rate. The international diabetes federation has estimated that by the year 2035, there will be more than 500 million people living with diabetes worldwide.[1] There has been a dramatic increase in the number of cases in recent years, where in diabetes mellitus not only involves the elderly population but also a significant portion of the middle-aged community, most of whom remain undiagnosed.[2] The sudden and rapid rise could be attributed to urbanization as well as a sedentary lifestyle, which predisposes the individual to various issues related to metabolic disorder with both microvascular and macrovascular complications. The chronic nature of the disease requires regular follow-up once diagnosed, which is a hassle for both the patient and the clinician. Most importantly, the cost incurred in monitoring the blood sugar levels is a constant burden on the patient, and the socioeconomic status plays a huge role in his or her well-being.[3]

Prediabetes is a condition where the blood glucose levels are higher than normal but not high enough to be diagnosed as diabetes. It is an intermediary state of altered glycemic status. The criteria for diagnosing prediabetes; an fasting blood sugar 100–125 mg/dL, hemoglobin A1C (HbA1c) 5.7–6.4, or oral glucose tolerance 140–199 mg/dL.[45] The reason why this condition is studied extensively is that prediabetes by itself has been identified as a cardiometabolic risk factor associated with poor outcomes.[6] International Diabetes Federation defines prediabetes as a state of impaired glucose tolerance or impaired fasting glucose; it is a state which predisposes the individual to the development of frank diabetes mellitus and its subsequent complications.[7] As reported by the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), in the year 2021, there were 541 million people diagnosed to be prediabetics around the world.[8] Left unaddressed, prediabetics too followed in the same trend as diabetics, to as far as even developing coronary artery disease and heart failure in the future.[9]

Estimation of HbA1c is a reliable method of assessing the glycemic status which functions as a well-recognized marker of glycemic marker in diabetes.[10] Taking into account the lifespan of red blood cells (RBCs) of around 120 days, the HbA1c level is a good measure of the blood glucose levels in the past 2–3 months. Hence, HbA1c is an established assay used in the diagnosis of diabetes.[11] The complete blood count (CBC) is one of the most common blood tests used in the clinical scenario, which has become a rapid and easy process owing to the presence of automated analyzers. The CBC is most frequently used to examine the total and differential blood counts. The RBC distribution width (RDW), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC) are other parameters which are also obtained but often overlooked. The RDW is a value produced from the variability in size of the different RBCs. If the RBCs vary widely in their size overall, the RDW is high and vice versa.[12] Studies have shown that the RDW is an inflammatory marker in diabetics and is also associated with a higher risk for cardiovascular diseases.[1314]

Different levels of blood glucose have shown to have the ability to influence any of the RBCs indices that are both observed and calculated, such as RBC count, the packed cell volume, MCV, mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), MCHC as well as the RDW.[1516] These variations have not only been seen in diabetics but has also been observed in the prediabetic population.[17]

A constant hyperglycemic status has a detrimental effect on the RBCs causing structural and functional changes owing to the osmotic disturbance. The changes in blood cell counts in different levels of glycemic status are yet to be seen. In this study, we try to determine any association between glycated hemoglobin level and the various hematological parameters in normal, prediabetic, and diabetic states.

Materials and Methods

This is a cross-sectional study through consecutive sampling. The primary objective of the study was to look for any association between RBCs parameters, such as total RBC count, hematocrit, hemoglobin, MCV, MCH, MCHC, RDW, and HbA1c level in normal, prediabetic, and diabetic individuals. The sample size was calculated using the study conducted by Kumari et al. where the Pearson correlation between RDW parameter and HBA1C level was found to be statistically significant.[18] The minimum sample size of 27 in each group was accepted for the study with the assumption of 80% power and 5% level of significance. Formula used was: N = ([Zα + Zβ]/C) 2 + 3, where C = 0.5 × ln ([1 + r]/[1 − r]) and α is the probability of type I error (significance level), β is the probability of type II error (1 – power of the test), and 0 is the correlation coefficient.

Adult men and women who were newly diagnosed diabetics, i.e., known duration of diabetes <1 year, were included in the study. This was done to avoid the effect of associated co-morbidities and drug intake in long-term diabetics, which may have a bias on the blood counts. Pregnant and lactating women, subjects with a history of any hematological disorders (including subjects with anemia, folic acid, and B12 deficiency), acute or chronic infections, malignancy, known cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, hepatic, renal, or thyroid disorders were excluded from the study. After obtaining ethical approval from the institution, the study was initiated, and data collection was done between July 2022 and July 2023. Informed consent was obtained from the study subjects.

The participants underwent a complete routine physical examination, and a blood sample was withdrawn for determination of HbA1c levels, CBCs, and derived parameters. Blood samples were taken from the brachial vein (Finecare™ HbA1c Rapid Quantitative Test). Subjects were categorized according to their HbA1c levels into three groups: the first category with HbA1c level <5.7%, i.e., the normal group, the prediabetic group with HbA1c level of 5.7%–6.4%, and the diabetic group with HbA1c level of >6.5%. The CBC was also done, and total RBC count, hematocrit, hemoglobin, MCV, MCH, MCHC, and RDW were determined and calculated (Sysmex Xp-300 Automated Hematology Analyzer).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the software IBM Corp. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows Version 26.0. Armonk, NY, USA: IBM Corp.; 2019. Continuous variables were represented as mean and standard deviation, whereas categorical variables were represented as frequency percentage. Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact tests were used to assess differences in categorical data. One-way ANOVA was used for differences in means of more than two independent data. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 87 subjects fulfilling the criteria were included for analysis, 45 male subjects and 42 female subjects. According to the HbA1c levels, the subjects were divided into 3 groups: normal, prediabetic, and diabetic groups. The HbA1c level in diabetics was 8.07% (±1.03), prediabetics was 5.81% (±0.63), and normal was 4.83% (±0.33). The average age of the subjects was 39.42 ± 8.07 years, whereas age in normal, prediabetic, and diabetic groups was 38.72 ± 9.54 years, 35.90 ± 7.68 years, and 43.66 ± 4.27 years, respectively. The differences in mean age were statistically significant, with older individuals being more prevalent in the diabetic group compared to the other groups.

Similarly, the packed cell volume was highest in the diabetic group (44.14%) compared to the normal group (40.67%) and the prediabetic group (42.02%). The differences in mean PCV levels between the groups were found to be statistically significant (P < 0.05). The MCV was higher in the diabetic group compared to the other groups, and the difference was statistically significant (P < 0.05). However, there were no significant differences in hemoglobin, total RBC counts, and MCH values between the three groups [Table 1].

Discussion

Our study is the first study from the regional part of the country to examine the relationship between the glycemic levels and various RBC indices between three groups. In this study, there was a strong statistical difference in various RBC parameters between the normal, prediabetic, and diabetic groups.

In our study, the MCV had a direct correlation with the blood glucose level and was highest in the diabetic group, with a statistically significant difference when compared between the three groups. This is similar to the study conducted by Kumar et al., where the MCV as well as MCH values were significantly higher in the diabetic group[18] and also to the study conducted by Alamri et al., which showed that the state of hyperglycemia led to an increase in the volume of the RBC, which reflects in the MCV.[19] The hyperglycemic state could result in the influx of glucose into the erythrocytes through the glucose transporters on the RBC membrane. The increased level of glucose inside the RBCs could have a hyperosmolar effect, leading to the infusion of water molecules across the RBC membrane and causing an increase in the volume of the cell.[20] Another theory to explain the finding could be due to the ionic changes occurring in the altered glycemic states. Displacement of sodium and chloride ions due to nonenzymatic glycosylation of cell membrane proteins could lead to changes in the cell volume as well.[21] However, other studies have reported a decrease in the MCV in diabetic patients compared to a control group, contrary to our findings.[2223]

Our study also showed that the MCHC was lowest in the diabetic group, and the difference between the groups was statistically significant. The findings are similar to the findings of the study conducted by NeamŢu et al. between diabetic and nondiabetic groups, where in the MCHC was significantly lower in the diabetic group. Erythrocytes undergo structural as well as functional changes when exposed to altered glycemic states for prolonged periods of time, leading to anisocytosis, poikilocytosis, and hypochromia.[24] The finding of hypochromia is commonly seen in inflammatory states such as diabetes, which shows up as a decrease in MCHC in findings.[25] The finding could be explained by the fact that diabetes is a long-standing state of stress. The chronic inflammatory state in diabetes mellitus has been associated with an increase in inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin 1 and interleukin 6. The increased levels of these cytokines cause a hypochromic state due to their action on the reticuloendothelial cells, causing retention of iron and leading to a state of hypochromia.[26] This is contrary to the findings of other studies, where a hyperglycemic state was associated with an increase in the MCHC values.[272829]

The RDW, perhaps showed the strongest correlation with the HbA1c level. RDW is a routinely assessed parameter which reflects the variability in the size of the erythrocytes. A high RDW is seen in anemic conditions due to Vitamin B12 or folic acid deficiency, iron deficiency, or in conditions where there is impaired RBC production, such as in various types of hemolytic anemia. RDW has consistently shown to be positively correlated in conditions associated with acute myocardial infarction, heart failure, and coronary disease.[3031] RDW was highest in the diabetic group, and the difference in this value across the groups was strongly significant. An increase in the RDW occurs whenever there is an increased RBC destruction or ineffective cell turnover. As already seen, an increase in RDW can occur in various conditions, such as nutritional deficiencies and bone marrow depression.[32] However, a hyperglycemic state can also cause anisocytosis, leading to an increase in RDW, and this is seen in diabetes due to the long-standing inflammatory state. Reduced cellular deformability, alteration in the cell membrane causing altered mechanical properties, especially in adhesion and osmotic fragility, combined with oxidative stress, may all lead to an increase in RDW.[3334] RDW was higher in the prediabetic group and highest in the diabetic group; hence, it may be a direct marker of the inflammation associated with hyperglycemic states.

Conclusion

Our study has shown that RBC parameters are correlated with the HbA1c levels. MCV and RDW were higher, whereas the MCHC was lower with an increase in the blood glucose levels. Of particular interest is the RDW, which has repeatedly shown to be associated with HbA1c and can also be considered as a novel biomarker for glycemic control in patients with diabetes. RBC parameters, which are often overlooked can be an important tool in patient care when compared to routine methods such as determination of HbA1c. They show a simple and effective way of monitoring the progression of the disease; being an inexpensive tool, it can also promote patient compliance and cooperation.

The current study also shows that RBC indices are greatly impacted by persistent hyperglycemia in diabetes mellitus, demonstrating both changes in erythrocyte structure and function. Reduced RBC count, hemoglobin, and hematocrit, as well as higher MCV, MCH, and RDW, were observed in patients with poor glycemic control. These findings are consistent with documented hematologic disorders linked to oxidative stress and decreased erythropoiesis. RDW may be a sensitive and affordable diagnostic for identifying patients at risk of poor metabolic control, according to the substantial positive association between RDW and glycemic indicators, especially HbA1c.

Altogether, routine RBC parameters, which are readily available through conventional hematological testing, may offer useful further information for assessing glycemic state and tracking the course of the disease in diabetic patients. Regular clinical evaluation using these indices may help identify problems earlier and enhance long-term treatment results.

Limitations of the study

One of the drawbacks of this study is that a cause-and-effect relationship cannot be established due to its cross-sectional methodology. In addition, the physical assessment of the study participants and other traits that are closely linked to RBC parameters, including vitamin levels, were not ascertained. Our study is a single-center study with a minimal sample size; multicentric program with a larger set of population may provide more insights into the present investigations.
