A synopsis of competence-regulated virulence in Streptococcus pneumoniae
Authors: Sook Yin Chong, Shi Qian Lew, Gee W Lau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStreptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococcus) remains a significant cause of pneumonia, bacteremia, sepsis, meningitis, and otitis media. The ability of pneumococcus to enter a state of genetic competence is central to pneumococcal adaptability and virulence. Activation of the competence state enables pneumococcus to uptake exogenous DNA for genetic transformation, acquire genome plasticity and antimicrobial resistance, while also regulating stress tolerance, biofilm formation, and coordinated expression of virulence factors. This review summarizes current evidence on competence-regulated virulence across diverse infection models, including nasopharyngeal colonization, otitis media, meningitis, bacteremia, and pneumonia, highlighting how the competence regulon impacts pneumococcal host colonization, invasion, and disease progression. Comparative insights into pneumococcal competence in vitro versus in vivo are provided to highlight context-dependent regulation of disease progression. Finally, this review examines emerging competence regulon-targeted therapeutic strategies, including dominant-negative competence-stimulating peptides and inhibitors of competence induction, which have been shown to suppress transformation and attenuate pneumococcal virulence.KEYWORDS: Streptococcus pneumoniae, competence regulon, virulence, animal models

Introduction

Streptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococcus) is a significant bacterial pathogen responsible for various human infectious diseases associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. These diseases include community-acquired pneumonia [1], otitis media [2], as well as more severe invasive conditions such as pneumonia-derived sepsis and associated multi-organ dysfunction, and meningitis [3]. Pneumococcal diseases represent a significant global public health burden, with young children [4] and the elderly [5] being the most vulnerable groups. Young children under five years of age are particularly susceptible due to their immature immune systems, while elderly adults face an increased risk as a result of age-related immunological decline [6]. Recent studies have shown that close contact with children facilitates transmission to older populations [7]. Eradication of pneumococcal diseases poses a significant challenge, owing to the alarming rise in resistance to commonly used β-lactam antibiotics, such as penicillin, amoxicillin, and cephalosporins, with rare reports of increased resistance to vancomycin [8,9].

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), global vaccination initiatives have successfully reduced pneumococcal-associated deaths from one million young lives to approximately 300,000 in developing nations annually [10]. Although vaccination remains the most effective prevention strategy, currently approved vaccines cover only a fraction of the more than 100 known serotypes. Among the most recent pneumococcal conjugate vaccines, Prevnar 20 (PCV20) protects against 20 serotypes and is approved for both adults and children, whereas Capvaxive (PCV21) targets 21 serotypes but is currently indicated only for adults. A detailed overview of presently licensed and next-generation pneumococcal vaccines was recently reviewed by Ganaie et al [11]. However, the highly recombinogenic nature of pneumococcus has led to the emergence of vaccine escape strains through “capsule switching” [12], enabling pneumococci to replace their capsular polysaccharide (CPS) with non-vaccine serotypes [13], and thereby, evade vaccine-induced immunity [14]. CPS is a major virulence factor that contributes to colonization efficiency and is essential for the development of invasive disease [15] by hindering phagocytosis, reducing complement deposition (C3b opsonization), interfering with complement activation, and masking pneumococcal antigens from immune recognition [16,17], allowing pneumococci to persist within the host [18]. Colonization typically occurs in the nasopharynx, which serves as the primary reservoir for pneumococcus and is often asymptomatic [19]. This initial colonization is a prerequisite for bacterial transmission and for progression to other diseases such as pneumonia, bacteremia, sepsis, and meningitis [20]. In addition to capsule-mediated immune evasion, pneumococcus also employs genetic and regulatory strategies for host adaptation, most notably through its capacity for natural competence [21].

Pneumococcus exhibits natural competence for genetic transformation, enabling uptake of exogenous DNA from the environment and its integration into the genome through homologous recombination [21]. Competence develops in response to the accumulation of competence-stimulating peptide (CSP), encoded by the comC gene [22]. The 24-amino acid pre-CSP, with a double-glycine leader sequence, is cleaved into a mature 17-amino acid form and exported by the ComAB ABC transporter, which possesses an N-terminal protease activity [23]. Once the extracellular CSP concentration reaches a critical threshold, it binds and activates the histidine kinase ComD, activating the ComDE two-component regulatory system. Phosphorylated ComE directly or indirectly triggers the transcription of three distinct phases of “early, late, and delayed” competence (com) genes [24]. There are approximately 24 early com genes, including comAB, comCDE, and comX1 and comX2 [25]. The identical comX genes encode the alternative sigma factor σX (ComX) [26], which drives the expression of a subsequent wave of ~ 81 late com genes [25]. Among these, ~16 late com genes encode proteins that are directly involved in DNA uptake and recombination, including ComG (Cgl) proteins that assemble a type IV pilus-like DNA uptake apparatus [27], the single-stranded DNA-binding protein SsbB [28], the recombinase RecA [29], and the DNA-processing protein DprA [30]. Additionally, 14 late com genes are important for virulence and fitness in mice [31]. Among these are the competent state-specific virulence genes that encode fratricide factors [31], including cbpD [32], cibAB [33], and lytA [34]. These allolytic factors along with the cell wall hydrolase LytC [35], promote the lysis of non-competent sibling cells, releasing DNA that can be taken up by nearby competent bacteria [32], as well as the intracellular virulence factors, such as pneumolysin [33]. Competent cells, in contrast, produce the immunity protein ComM, which protects them from CbpD-mediated lysis and transiently delays cell division [36] (Figure 1).

Pre-CSP is processed, cleaved into a mature peptide, and exported via the ComAB transporter. Once extracellular CSP reaches a critical concentration, it binds to the histidine kinase ComD, triggering autophosphorylation. Activated ComD subsequently phosphorylates the response regulator, ComE. Phosphorylated ComE then activates the transcription of early competence (com) genes, including comAB, comCDE, comX1comX2, and comW. The alternative sigma factor ComX, stabilized by ComW, directs RNA polymerase (RNAP) to activate late com genes such as dprA, ssbB, and recA, which are essential for DNA uptake and recombination. Simultaneously, ComX positively increases the expression of late com genes, including lytA, cibAB, and cbpD, which encode the allolytic factors. It has been hypothesized that the pneumococcal competence system is a stress-response system that temporarily activates specific genes [37], not only to promote transformation but also to enhance tolerance to antibiotics and genotoxic stresses [38–41]. During competence, the homologous recombinase RecA [29] and the helicase RadA are highly expressed, contributing to DNA repair and survival under damaging conditions. However, radA is not required for optimal genetic transformation [42]. In addition, DprA regulates the exit of S. pneumoniae from the competent state. Deletion of dprA leads to the overexpression of ComM, delays cell separation after division, and increases cellular energy consumption [43].

Beyond horizontal gene transfer, competence also facilitates the acquisition of advantageous traits such as virulence and antibiotic resistance, metabolic versatility, and enhanced survival under host-induced stress [38]. However, because the competent state is energetically costly and potentially detrimental if uncontrolled [43], its duration is tightly restricted to approximately 30–40 minutes under in vitro conditions [25,44]. Termination of competence involves negative feedback mediated by DprA, which binds phosphorylated ComE to prevent further activation of early com genes [45,46], as well as degradation of ComX by the ClpEP protease [47]. These precise temporal regulations ensure that the competent state is transient, allowing pneumococcus to balance genetic plasticity with cellular fitness.

Apart from its role in horizontal gene transfer, competence also plays a direct role in pneumococcal pathogenesis [48] and host interaction [49]. Fratricide-mediated by competence-activated allolytic factors, not only releases DNA but also potent virulence factors such as pneumolysin [50] and proinflammatory cell wall components such as peptidoglycan and teichoic acids [49]. Pneumolysin exacerbates host tissue damage by forming pores in cell membranes [51], induces the release of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species that promote inflammation [52,53], and facilitates bacterial dissemination [54,55]. Competence is further supported by the signal recognition particle (SRP) pathway, as the deletion of the small cytoplasmic RNA (ScRNA) or its protein partners (Ffh and FtsY) markedly impairs competence induction, transformation efficiency, and attenuates virulence in mouse models of acute pneumonia and bacteremia [56]. The loss of competence in scRNA mutants disrupts type IV pilus assembly and DNA uptake [56]. Moreover, competence activation remodels the pneumococcal cell wall to mediate host adherence by increasing the exposure of key cell surface choline-binding proteins (CBPs), including PspA and PspC, in a CbpD-dependent manner [49], thereby enhancing colonization and persistence [49,57]. Together, these findings suggest that competence coordinates pneumolysin release through allolysis [33], cell wall remodeling and adhesin expression [49], as well as SRP-dependent secretory pathways to promote colonization, invasion, and progression to severe disease [56].

Additional recent studies have suggested that the pneumococcal competence functions as a population-level stress response governed by the ComABCDE quorum-sensing system through a biphasic Self-Induction and Propagation (SI&P) mechanism. DNA-damaging agents and replication inhibitors, including mitomycin C, fluoroquinolones, and HPUra, activate competence by inducing replication stress [39]. Under antibiotic-induced replication stress, a small subset of self-induced cells activates competence, which then propagates throughout the population once a critical threshold is reached [39,40,58]. This process is finely tuned by the ComE/ComE~P ratio, ensuring that competence is only triggered under actual stressful conditions, preventing unnecessary energy expenditure. The SI&P mechanism establishes competence as a population “health sensor” that promotes phenotypic diversification and stress tolerance [37].

Aminoglycoside antibiotics such as kanamycin and streptomycin cause translational errors [38,59], leading to the accumulation of misfolded proteins [60]. These misfolded proteins sequester the HtrA protease, which would otherwise degrade CSP and repress competence. When HtrA becomes preoccupied with managing protein stress, its ability to suppress competence is reduced, leading to derepression and activation of the competence system [61]. HtrA contributes to survival under oxidative stress, growth at elevated temperatures, and the ability to undergo genetic transformation [62]. In vitro, the htrA mutant exhibits reduced transformation efficiency [63]. Although CSP may accumulate in an htrA mutant due to the loss of CSP degradation, the marked reduction in transformation efficiency indicates that HtrA has additional roles beyond CSP turnover. Ibrahim et al. proposed that HtrA facilitates the proper folding or maturation of one or more protein components required for competence development, which could explain why competence is impaired despite elevated CSP levels [63]. Thus, HtrA serves as a pivotal link between stress-induced activation of competence and pathogenesis. Collectively, this bet-hedging strategy enhances pneumococcal adaptability and survival under hostile host microenvironments or antibiotic selection pressures.

Pneumococcal competence has been extensively characterized under in vitro conditions [22,25,64,65]. The ability of pneumococci to uptake DNA from their environment for genetic transformation has served as a valuable research tool for over 90 years [66]. This review will emphasize how competent state intersects and contributes to pneumococcal pathogenesis, focusing on the in vivo competence development in various disease models.

Strain-dependent variation in competence induction and pathogenesis

Although more than 100 serotypes of pneumococci have been identified to date, the efficiency of competence induction is not uniform across the species, and is highly dependent on the genetic background of the specific lineage. Thus far, six distinct CSP pherotypes (CSP-1 to CSP-6) and their corresponding comC alleles have been identified [67] and this polymorphism may contribute to varying levels of genetic transformation efficiency across different lineages. Despite this diversity, most isolates express pherotypes CSP-1 or CSP-2 [68]. For instance, two of the most widely used pathogenic strains, the serotype 2 D39, and the serotype 4 TIGR4, express CSP-1 and CSP-2, respectively [68]. Both genomes have been fully sequenced, providing a genetic foundation for comparing lineage-specific variations in competence and virulence. In vitro, both D39 [69] and TIGR4 [25] exhibit comparable transcriptional profiles for early and late competence genes, with the overall state of competence persisting for approximately 30–40 minutes. Interestingly, the recombination frequencies within nasopharyngeal pneumococcal biofilm consortia between the D39 and TIGR4 strains are independent of CSP cross-talk and occur in a unidirectional manner [70]. Lin et al. demonstrated that the D39 and TIGR4 strains develop natural competence that persists for a prolonged duration within a murine model of pneumonia-derived sepsis [71].

Beyond D39 and TIGR4, investigations into contemporary clinical isolates reveal the broader impact of lineage-specific variation. For example, the serotype 14 clinical isolate CGSP14, originally recovered from a case of necrotizing pneumonia and hemolytic uremic syndrome in Taiwan, represents one of the most prevalent lineages associated with invasive pneumococcal disease [72]. This strain exhibits high competence and genomic plasticity, having acquired at least eight exogenous genetic elements from other organisms via horizontal gene transfer [72]. This extensive genomic mosaicism includes multidrug resistance and enhanced pathogenicity, which contribute to the significant therapeutic challenges associated with treating this lineage. Similarly, the Taiwan-19F (PMEN clone 14) lineage, originally isolated from a cerebrospinal fluid specimen, is a multidrug-resistant strain highly resistant to penicillin [73]. This lineage is characterized by high competence [74] noted for its high capacity for capsular switching (e.g. from serotype 19F to 19A), thereby promoting enhanced colonization in the mouse nasopharynx and subsequent vaccine escape [75,76]. This underscores the clinical danger posed by hyper-competent pneumococcal lineages.

Comparative insights into pneumococcal competence: in vitro and in vivo perspectives

The molecular mechanisms underlying pneumococcal competence development have been intensively studied for decades, primarily using capsule-deficient strains derived from the serotype 2 strain D39, such as CP1200, CP1250, and R6, along with hypercompetent lineages including R800 and Rx [61]. These studies are conducted under defined laboratory conditions, most commonly in the competence-permissive C+Y medium, where pH control is critical. For instance, C+Y medium at a pH of 6.8–7 inhibits competence, whereas a pH range of 7.8–8 allows spontaneous competence development [77]. There are several iterations of C+Y medium [41,61], and their formulations markedly influence competence development. C+YA [41,78] is the richest formulation, containing casein hydrolyzate, multiple free amino acids, glucose, and a broad spectrum of trace metals, including Cu2+, Fe2+, Mn2+, and Zn2+, in addition to Ca2+ and Mg2+. C+YCAA [61] is moderately rich and relies on casamino acids supplemented with a limited set of free amino acids; similar to C+YA, it contains glucose and trace metals, although at lower concentrations. In contrast, C+YYB [61] is the most defined and nutrient-limited formulation, providing individual amino acids rather than peptide hydrolyzates and using maltose instead of glucose as the primary carbon source. Notably, C+YYB lacks Cu2+, Fe2+, and Zn2+, which may constrain competence induction due to metal-dependent regulation of competence development, as described in Oh et al [79]. All three formulations contain Ca2+, Mg2+, Mn2+, vitamins, choline, acetate, and pyruvate, but differ substantially in amino acid complexity, trace metal availability, and carbon sources, factors likely to underlie the variability in competence phenotypes reported across studies. In contrast to the aforementioned hypercompetent derivatives, the parental strain D39 can only attain the competent state when cultured in Todd Hewitt supplemented with yeast extract (THY) broth or in C+Y medium with the exogenously provided CSP [49,69]. Other derivatives of the Rx strain, such as CP1200, CP1415, and CP1500, are occasionally grown in casein tryptone medium (CAT) for transformation and competence assays [21,80]. CAT is a relatively simple medium composed of tryptone, casein hydrolyzate, yeast extract, NaCl, glucose, and K2HPO4 [21,81,82]. Collectively, these optimized systems provide more precise parameters for competence induction and synchronization, leading to many elegant molecular dissections of the underlying regulatory pathways [83–85].

Although the aforementioned well-controlled laboratory models have contributed to our understanding of mechanisms of competence developmental, they do not fully recapitulate the complex environmental milieus encountered by pneumococcus inside a host. Competence is known to be induced naturally in vivo during infection [86], particularly in environments where host-derived nutrients (e.g. amino acids, sugars [87], transition metals [79], and other metabolites [88,89]) and various stress conditions (e.g. oxidative stress [90], nutrient limitation [44], and antibiotic exposure [38,39]) act as triggering signals. Animal models of pneumococcal infection, including in rat (Rattus) [91], murine (Mus musculus) [44,71,79,92,93], chinchilla (Chinchilla lanigera) [94–98], and zebrafish (Danio rerio) [99–103] infection models have demonstrated genetic transformation and competence gene expression during bacteremia, pneumonia, otitis media, and meningitis, providing valuable insights into how pneumococci integrate host-derived signals to initiate competence within distinct tissue environments. These in vivo systems reveal that competence development is spatially and temporally heterogeneous, in contrast to the synchronous activation observed in vitro [104], underscoring the influence of host microenvironment on pneumococcal adaptive behavior.

Polymicrobial landscape: interspecies modulation of pneumococcal competence

Pneumococcal competence and colonization are significantly modulated by the polymicrobial landscape within the nasopharynx, particularly Haemophilus influenzae, Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus mitis, and Streptococcus oralis. These microbes co-habitat within the nasopharyngeal niche, forming persistent and multi-species biofilms [105,106]. However, the introduction of the PCV vaccines has shifted the ecological equilibrium and intensified the competition between S. pneumoniae and S. aureus, often favoring the emergence of the latter [106,107]. To assert dominance, pneumococcus produces hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) to eliminate competing bacteria and monopolize limited nutrients, thereby aiding colonization [108]. By killing neighboring species, pneumococcus acquires critical fitness traits, including antibiotic resistance, through competence-dependent interspecies gene exchange [109]. In vivo murine co-colonization models demonstrate that S. pneumoniae effectively inhibits the dispersal of S. aureus and its subsequent transition to disease, a phenomenon that is particularly pronounced during influenza A virus coinfection [106]. This competitive dominance is further supported by in vitro evidence showing that H2O2 produced by pneumococcus is lethal to S. aureus, in a unidirectional manner [110]. Interestingly, these antagonistic effects may be niche or host specific. For instance, in a neonatal rat model, H2O2 release from S. pneumoniae failed to eliminate S. aureus, likely due to the lower levels of H2O2 produced within the neonatal nasal passages [111]. Furthermore, this predatory behavior appears to be a core trait of the species rather than a strain-specific one, as the killing of S. aureus does not depend on specific pneumococcal pherotypes [112].

Beyond physical competition, the virulence profile of S. pneumoniae is dynamically regulated in response to co-colonizing species. In co-culture with H. influenzae, the pneumococcus undergoes a distinct transcriptomic shift, where the expression of the pneumolysin gene (ply) is downregulated, while the H2O2-producing pyruvate oxidase gene (spxB) is significantly upregulated [113]. This increase in SpxB activity leads to higher production of H2O2, which serves a dual purpose. In addition to its role in interspecies antagonism, H2O2 serves as a metabolic regulator of the competence regulon and influences the expression com genes such as comC and recA, thereby modulating competence-associated DNA release and horizontal gene transfer [114]. Furthermore, co-incubation of S. pneumoniae and H. influenzae promotes robust pneumococcal biofilm formation in vitro and enhances pneumococcal persistence on the mucosal surfaces of the middle ear [115]. In addition, S. pneumoniae, S. oralis, and S. mitis are closely related commensals that engage in frequent horizontal gene transfer [116]. In particular, S. oralis, and S. mitis serve as significant genetic reservoirs for capsular polysaccharide diversity [117] and antibiotic resistance genes such as penicillin [118,119]. This genetic exchange is often bidirectional, for instance, certain S. mitis lineages have been found to harbor key virulence factors such as pneumolysin and autolysin originated from S. pneumoniae [120]. Such intra- and inter-species recombination events drive the continuous evolution and diversification of pneumococcal lineages [121]. Beyond genetic sharing, these species also compete within the same ecological niche. Borralho et al. recently reported that seven strains (one S. oralis and six S. mitis) contain an extensive array of bacteriocin-related genes with broad inhibitory activity against multiple serotypes and genotypes of pneumococcus. These bacteriocins can prevent and disrupt pneumococcal biofilms as they compete for the same resources, representing potential biotherapeutics against pneumococcal colonization [122].

Genome-wide analysis of the association between competence development and virulence

The first evidence of genetic transformation in vivo was reported by Frederick Griffith, who observed that a capsule-deficient avirulent S. pneumoniae strain attained virulent after acquiring the capsule gene during co-infection with a heat-killed capsule-expressing pneumococcal strain in mice [86]. Although the concept of “competence” was not yet understood at the time, this laid the foundation for the discovery of competence development in pneumococcus and its association with virulence. Since then, significant efforts have been made to understand the virulence of pneumococci. Polissi et al. employed signature-tagged mutagenesis (STM) to discover novel pneumococcal virulence factors and identified competence-regulated genes such as SPN-1264 (lytA), SPN-1589 (pstB), SPN-1055, (clpC), SPN-948 (blp locus/comAB locus), SPN-1634 (mutX), and SPN-636 (radA) which play role in a mouse bacteremia model [123]. A subsequent mouse bacteremia STM screen by Lau et al., identified comB, comD, aliB, and nox genes attenuated virulence [124]. Building on the known involvement of aliB [125,126] and nox [127] in pneumococcal adhesion and competence, they further demonstrated that insertion mutagenesis of these genes attenuate virulence in both mouse models of pneumonia and/or bacteremia infection [124]. With the same approach, Hava et al. identified competence-regulated genes, such as comD, cbpD, coiA, cglB, cinA, dinF, and htrA, as important for lung infection [128]. Opijnen et al. utilized the transposon sequencing (Tn-seq) technique and identified the interactions between regR and competence genes, comFA (SP_2208) and comFC (SP_2207) [129]. A regR mutation in the serotype 6 (23477) strain results in attenuated virulence in a murine intraperitoneal sepsis model, and the same mutation confers reduced competence in strain Cp1015 [130]. ComFA is a DNA-dependent ATPase that binds to single-stranded DNA and forms a complex with ComFC. Together, ComFA-ComFC interact with DprA for genetic transformation [131]. In a later study applying the same approach, Opijnen et al. identified over 1,800 genotype-phenotype interactions and uncovered new gene functions related to competence-mediated genetic transformation and stress responses. In addition to the known competence regulators such as comDE and ciaRH, they identified 113 additional genes involved in this complex process. The overlap of 49 transformation-associated genes with those responsive to DNA damage, hydrogen peroxide, and pH stress further highlights the interconnected nature of these cellular processes [89]. More recently, Liu et al. developed an IPTG-inducible CRISPR interference system (CRISPRi-seq) to identify essential pneumococcus genes [132] and those critical in a murine pneumonia model [133]. These authors provided a more detailed role of the Clp proteolytic system in competence regulation, where ClpX functions as the ATPase subunit and works together with ClpP protease to repress competence development [132]. To further explore how the competence regulon influences virulence, we next discuss findings across various animal infection models.

Nasopharyngeal colonization model

The nasopharynx is the primary site of pneumococcal colonization [134], which often occurs asymptomatically [135] and act as a reservoir for person-to-person transmission [136]. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the asymptomatic carriage rate of pneumococcus ranges from 20% to 60% in school-aged children and 5% to 10% in adults. Colonization is a prerequisite for disease development, providing an opportunity for the bacteria to acquire new genetic material through competence [137], which contributes to antibiotic resistance, altered virulence, and immune evasion. During episodes of immune suppression, the bacteria can disseminate to the distal portion of the respiratory tract to cause pneumonia as well as invade sterile sites, such as the blood and the central nervous system. In an infant rat model, Sebert et al. demonstrated that deletion of the CiaRH two-component system, known to negatively regulate competence development [138,139], markedly impaired nasopharyngeal colonization, an effect attributed to reduced htrA expression [139]. Loss of CiaRH also derepresses the competence regulon, resulting in increased expression of early competence genes such as comCDE [140]. Interestingly, Kowalko et al. demonstrated that ComE may function as a regulatory switch where nasopharyngeal colonization is enhanced when its expression is reduced and vice versa when its expression is elevated [137]. However, Zheng et al. reported that comE deletion in the D39 strain reduces nasopharyngeal colonization in a mouse model due to enhanced production of a thicker capsule, which limits the ability of pneumococcus to adhere to respiratory epithelial cells [141]. Together, these studies highlight that the impact of ComE on nasopharyngeal colonization may vary depending on the host model and immune milieu.

In the nasopharyngeal niche, pneumococci frequently form biofilms, which protect them from antibiotics and host defenses, thereby supporting persistent colonization [48,142]. Marks et al. reported that pneumococci isolated from the nasopharyngeal tissues of infected mice exhibited higher transformation efficiency, primarily driven by nasopharynx-specific conditions such as lower temperatures, nutrient limitations, and epithelial cell interactions. These host conditions, when replicated in vitro, promote biofilm formation and enhance the expression of comA, comE, and comX [48,143], and genetic transformation [144]. However, as discussed above, competence activation imposes a fitness cost, as suggested by increased ability of ΔcomE mutant to colonize the nasopharynx. Therefore, pneumococcus needs to finely tune competence activation to optimize host colonization [137]. Aggarwal et al. reported that the competence-induced gene briC serves as a molecular bridge linking competence development to biofilm formation [145]. The expression of briC is regulated by ComE and relies on the ComAB transporter, contributing to biofilm maturation and enhanced colonization of the nasopharynx. Consistent with this role, deletion of briC resulted in reduced nasopharyngeal colonization. Overall, these studies suggest that competence development and biofilm formation and are intertwined through the ComE-BriC signaling pathway, which reinforces biofilm development and adaptation in pneumococcus to the host microenvironment [145].

Before establishing colonization in the nasopharynx, S. pneumoniae must first traverse the nasal cavity. In this initial step, the negatively charged pneumococcal CPS prevents entrapment in mucus and promotes access to epithelial surfaces [146]. Also, CPS functions as a protective barrier against host immunity [17,20,147]. During early colonization, transparent variants with thinner capsules adhere more efficiently to host cells through cell surface adhesins, such as phosphorylcholine (ChoP) and CbpA, which bind to host receptors, including the platelet-activating factor receptor (PAFR) and the polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (plgR) [148,149]. Additional adhesins, such as PavA and enolase, promote binding to host extracellular matrix components, including fibronectin and plasminogen, thereby aiding tissue colonization and spread [150,151]. As the infection progresses, opaque variants with thicker capsules are selected, enhancing immune evasion and promoting invasion into distal lung tissues and the bloodstream [152]. Although non-encapsulated S. pneumoniae (NESp) strains can efficiently colonize the nasopharynx, they are unable to cause invasive disease [153]. Sakai et al. demonstrated that comD, lytA, and lytC are among the most highly expressed competence genes during asymptomatic nasopharyngeal carriage in healthy children [154].

S. pneumoniae senses the universal quorum-sensing signal autoinducer-2 (AI-2), produced by S-ribosylhomocysteine lyase (LuxS), through the fructose-specific phosphoenolpyruvate phosphotransferase system component FruA. AI-2 signaling promotes the transition from asymptomatic colonization to invasive disease by enabling efficient galactose utilization in the upper respiratory tract [155]. The luxS gene is ubiquitously present across all S. pneumoniae isolates, regardless of their origin from healthy children or patients with invasive diseases [156]. LuxS plays a dual role in controlling competence and LytA-dependent autolysis [157]. Specifically, Trappetti et al. demonstrated that deletion of luxS impairs competence and fratricide because of the downregulation of the competence genes such as comD, comX, comW, cglA, dltA, cbpD [158]. Furthermore, LuxS modulates iron uptake and promotes biofilm formation [159] by facilitating the release of extracellular DNA (eDNA), a critical structural component of the biofilm matrix [158]. In a murine intranasal infection model, administration of synthetic AI-2 increases virulence in both the D39 strain and its ΔluxS derivative, primarily through enhanced capsule production [155]. The luxS mutant failed to translocate from the nasopharynx to the lung or blood [160], and had a significantly lower bacterial burden when compared to the parental wild-type strain D39 [155]. Additionally, the ΔluxS strain exhibits aberrant pneumolysin expression, resulting in a persistence defect in a murine model of nasopharyngeal carriage [156].

Otitis media model

Otitis media often originates in the nasopharynx, where alterations in the local environment disrupt colonization dynamics and facilitate the spread of S. pneumoniae to the adjacent middle ear [135]. A primary respiratory viral infection followed by a secondary pneumococcal infection is a well-recognized sequence in disease development [161,162]. Viral infections, induce host signals, including fever-range temperatures, norepinephrine, extracellular ATP, and increased nutrient availability [163], triggering the release of pneumococci from biofilms. These dispersed pneumococci exhibit altered virulence gene expression and an enhanced ability to disseminate [164,165]. Additionally, viral infection increases pneumococcal adherence to respiratory epithelial cells, thereby further enhancing colonization and disease progression [166,167]. Using a chinchilla model of experimental otitis media, Sabharwal et al. demonstrated that capsular switching enhances pneumococcal virulence [94]. Additionally, competence-regulated biofilms are commonly observed pathophysiology of otitis media [98,168–171], and potentially serve as a reservoir for competent bacteria, enabling horizontal gene transfer and capsular switching [70]. The late competence gene lytA, which encodes a muramidase and serves as the major pneumococcal autolysin, along with lytB, lytC, cbpA, pcpA, and pspA, is required for biofilm formation in vitro, as its deletion significantly reduces biofilm development. In contrast, deletion of cbpD has no detectable effect on biofilm formation [172].

Interestingly, LuxS, previously discussed in nasopharyngeal colonization model, also plays a role in otitis media, but seems to be strain-specific. In the S. pneumoniae middle ear isolate 947, which highly expresses luxS and fruA, deletion of luxS actually enhanced transmission to the ear and brain in the mouse model [173]. This phenotype stands in contrast to the D39 strain, originally a blood isolate, where luxS deficiency impairs translocation and reduces bacterial burden [160]. These findings suggest that while LuxS promotes an invasive phenotype in some genetic backgrounds that favor invasive pneumococcal diseases, in otitis media isolates, it may instead facilitate an adherent, localized lifestyle that prevents transit to other host niches [173].

Meningitis model

For meningitis to develop, S. pneumoniae must first establish bacteremia and then cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) to access and colonize the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), where it triggers inflammation of the meningeal membranes [174]. However, Ginkel et al. and Audshasai et al. reported evidence that pneumococci may reach the brain directly from the nasal cavity without detectable bacteremia [175,176]. Pneumolysin, a key virulence factor, which is released through competence-regulated allolytic factors, plays a critical role in compromising the endothelial tight junctions of BBB [177]. Also, pneumolysin can disrupt cerebral endothelial cells, while interaction with the PAFR further facilitates pneumococcal invasion [178–180]. PAFR activation leads to upregulation of platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule (PECAM-1) and polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (PIGR), which serve as binding sites for pneumococcal adhesins such as pneumococcal surface protein (PspC) and pneumococcal pilus-1 (RrgA), thereby facilitating bacterial adherence and invasion into the brain [181]. In addition, the surface-anchored sialidase (NanA) has been shown to promote pneumococcal interaction with the BBB and airway epithelial cells [182], with both its enzymatic sialidase activity and N-terminal lectin-like domain of NanA contributing to adherence and invasion in vitro and in vivo [183,184]. The late com gene, candidate combox site 4 (ccs4), acts as a virulence factor that facilitates pneumococcal adherence to and invasion of mouse brain tissue, and a Δccs4 mutant is markedly attenuated in meningitis infection. The authors further suggested that Ccs4 may function in competence-dependent allolysis [92], and the subsequent release of pneumolysin that disrupts the integrity of the BBB, thereby promoting meningitis.

Rats have been more widely used as an experimental model to study pneumococcal-mediated meningitis due to their larger brain size when compared to mice. The phosphorylcholine moieties on the pneumococcal surface serve as binding sites for choline-binding protein (CBP) adhesins which mediate attachment to host cells [185]. In a rat meningitis model, Gehre et al. demonstrated that choline-containing pneumococcal strains establish persistent infection, replicate more efficiently, and cause severe brain damage compared to the choline-deficient licA mutant [91]. Choline-expressing pneumococcal strains elevate levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and CINC-1 (CXCL1) in the rat brain [91,186]. The secretion of these pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines is driven by pneumolysin released by the competence-regulated allolytic factors. Besides its pore-forming capabilities, Hupp et al. reported that pneumolysin acts as a pro-endocytotic factor. In a murine meningitis model, pneumolysin triggers rapid and dynamin-dependent endocytosis in microglial membranes, forcing host cells to internalize S. pneumoniae lysates. This accelerated uptake activates the intracellular innate receptor Nod1, thereby exacerbating neuroinflammation and alters the membrane dynamics permanently [187]. In addition, pneumolysin activates TLR4 and signals through MyD88 to induce the release of TNF-α and IL-6 [188]. These cytokines disrupt the integrity and increase the permeability of the BBB by altering tight junction proteins (occludin, tricellulin, ZO-1, claudin-3, claudin-5) and adherens junction proteins (such as vascular endothelial cadherin) [189,190]. This disruption facilitates the translocation of pneumococci from the nasopharynx to the bloodstream and ultimately to the brain.

The zebrafish (Danio rerio) has emerged as a valuable tool for studying pneumococcal pathogenesis due to its evolutionarily conserved innate immune system. Due to their optical transparency and genetic tractability [191], zebrafish uniquely allows for real-time visualization and quantitative analysis of pneumococcal infection, providing mechanistic insights that inform human diseases and potential therapeutic targets [100], including systemic pneumococcal infection [101] and meningitis [99,103]. These valuable features enable genome-wide screens and CRISPR-based mutagenesis to identify key host factors, such as C-reactive protein isoforms essential for complement activation and pneumococcal clearance [102]. Transcriptomic profiling in zebrafish has also revealed that pneumolysin-dependent activation of bacterial competence plays a crucial role in driving meningitis, cell death, and inflammatory responses [99]. Notably, Jim et al. demonstrated the development of pneumococcal competence in vivo at the zebrafish single-cell level and revealed that competence was comCDE-dependent and induced heterogeneously in spatially clustered bacterial foci during infection [99]. However, the study did not clarify whether the competence observed in the zebrafish model is persistent for a prolonged period of time as recently demonstrated in murine model of pneumonia-derived sepsis [71]. Schmidt et al. performed a proteomic analysis of the CSF in a murine meningitis model and revealed the importance of the oligopeptide-binding lipoprotein AliB, and the ComDE two component regulatory system. Deletion of aliB and comDE genes lowers proinflammatory white blood cell counts and CXCL2 level during pneumococcal infection of the CSF [192], likely due to the fact that AliB is required for induction of competence [126,193], and that the ComDE acts as the central regulator of the competence regulon that drive the release of the proinflammatory pneumolysin and cell wall components [24].

Together, studies in rats, mice, and zebrafish demonstrate that during meningitis, pneumococcal competence is tightly regulated by host-specific cues, including immune mediators and microenvironmental stresses. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 [92], along with complement activation, may create inflammatory and oxidative stress conditions that can stimulate competence development during infection [90]. In parallel, microenvironmental conditions characteristic of specific tissues, such as lower temperature of zebrafish culture [99], nutrient limitation [44], and epithelial or endothelial interactions [194,195], may further modulate competence activation. These complementary systems highlight that pneumococcal adaptation to the host environment is essential for the development and maintenance of naturally induced competence in vivo [44,71,99], unlike in vitro conditions, where competent state can be synchronized simultaneously [36].

Bacteremia and bacteremia-derived sepsis model

According to the CDC, bacteremia has an overall case-fatality rate of approximately 20%, rising to as high as 60% in older adults, and represents one of the most severe manifestations of pneumococcal disease [196]. While nasopharyngeal carriage is necessary, it is not sufficient for bacteremia, which likely depends on serotype-specific virulence factors and the occasional disruption of nasal mucosal or alveolar-capillary (air-blood) barrier integrity [197]. Similar to the original study by Griffith [86], Bradshaw et al. demonstrated the genetic transformation of the non-encapsulated NESp strains MNZ41 and MNZ67 during bacteremia co-infection with heat-killed encapsulated strain WU2 [198]. As mentioned earlier, Lau et al. reported that a plasmid-insertion comD mutant was attenuated in both bacteremia and pneumonia infection models. In contrast, a comB mutant showed attenuation only in the bacteremia model [124]. In addition, the deletion of the delayed phase com gene htrA in the D39 and TIGR4 strains severely attenuated virulence in the bacteremia [63]. Following intranasal infection, both htrA mutants were rapidly cleared from the lungs and were unable to invade the bloodstream. Similarly, the intravenously injected htrA mutant was rapidly cleared from the blood [63]. In addition, the deletion of comE in D39 was reported to enhance virulence in mouse models of bacteremia and pneumonia using Balb/c mice, likely due to increased cps transcription, thicker capsule production, and increased resistance to opsonophagocytosis [141].

Lin et al. reported that an ΔscRNA mutant was outcompeted by the wild-type D39 strain in a mouse model bacteremia infection. Furthermore, the ΔscRNA mutant has impaired competence for genetic transformation due to disrupted type IV pilus formation, rendering the mutant incapable of DNA uptake. These findings highlight the essential role of scRNA in both virulence and genetic transformation [56]. In addition, Zhu et al. reported that ComX-regulated late com genes, including stress response genes (radA, recA, rmuC, prsW, radC), allolytic factors (lytA, cbpD, cibAB), and competence shutoff/DNA processing protein (dprA), contribute to bacterial survival and fitness during bacteremia. The reduced virulence observed upon deletion of these allolytic factor genes could be partially attributed to decreased pneumolysin release in vivo, especially LytA and CbpD. Although deletion of dprA results in attenuation in a murine bacteremia model, but not in acute pneumonia, the mutant shows increased pneumolysin release, consistent with an inability to exit the competent state, ultimately leading to other defects and bacterial death [31].

Oggioni et al. compared pneumococcal gene expression during bacteremia versus more “compartmentalized” tissue infection in brain and lung [48]. In the bloodstream, expression of ply and pspA was elevated, whereas in brain and lung tissues, the neuraminidases nanA and nanB, the zinc metalloproteinases zmpB and zmpC, the oxidative stress-associated enzyme sodA and nox, and the competence genes comA, comE, and comX were upregulated. By comparing gene expression profiles of planktonic versus biofilm pneumococcal bacteria in vitro, the authors proposed that pneumococci in the bloodstream grow in a planktonic state in contrast to the static biofilm-like state observed during tissue infection [48]. The same group of authors also reported that competence gene expression is reduced during bacteremic sepsis in CD-1 mice intravenously infected with TIGR4. Additionally, the deletion of comD further suppresses competence and exacerbates disease severity. Conversely, additional CSP2 administration to mice challenged with TIGR4 wild-type strain exhibited antibacterial activity and mitigated disease progression [8]. However, these findings contrast with most published studies, which show that comD mutants are attenuated in murine bacteremia infections by D39 and 0100993 (serotype 3) strains [124,199]. The discrepancy may arise from differences in the infection route (intravenous versus intranasal/intraperitoneal) and the pneumococcal strains used.

Pneumonia and pneumonia-derived sepsis model

Acute pneumonia, and pneumonia-derived bacteremia and sepsis represent the most common forms of pneumococcal invasive disease. Hematogenous spread of pneumococcus during bacteremia can progress to sepsis, a life-threatening clinical syndrome associated with high morbidity and mortality if not treated. The disruption of the air-blood barrier during pneumococcal pneumonia is driven by both the pore-forming pneumolysin [200] and proinflammatory cell wall components, which are released by the competence-regulated autolytic enzymes such as LytA, CbpD, and CibAB [33]. A compromised air-blood barrier enables pneumococci to access the bloodstream and initiate bacteremia and sepsis [201]. Consistent with this, pneumolysin-deficient mutants inoculated intranasally [202] or intratracheally [55] show a markedly reduced ability to penetrate from the alveoli into the lung interstitium and invade the bloodstream, underscoring the central role of pneumolysin in lung injury and pneumonia-derived sepsis (pneumonic sepsis) [203]. Zhu et al. further demonstrated that competence induction in pneumococcus increases the release of pneumolysin during acute pneumonia, and that ΔcomX1ΔcomX2 double mutant, ComX-regulated ΔlytA, ΔcbpD, and ΔcibAB, are all attenuated in pneumonia and bacteremia infection, in a competence-state-specific manner [31]. These findings indicate that ComX-dependent competence activation enhances pneumococcal lung injury and promotes invasion into the bloodstream. This conclusion is further supported by the observed upregulation of both comX and comA expression in lung washes from intranasally infected mice [48], and by our RNA-Seq analysis [79]. Interestingly, the serine/threonine kinase StkP has been identified as an upstream regulator of pneumococcal competence development by promoting the expression of comCDE and competence-dependent lung infection and blood invasion while repressing the CiaRH-mediated competence shutoff [204].

Upon dissemination to the lung or bloodstream, pneumococcal proteases can degrade host structural and immune proteins, promoting tissue invasion and immune subversion [182]. Genome-wide analyses have identified over 30 proteases in S. pneumoniae, underscoring the diversity of its proteolytic machinery, as detailed in a 2021 review by Marquart [182]. The ATP-dependent protease ClpP acts as a negative regulator, preventing comCDE expression under conditions unfavorable for competence development [205]. Deletion of clpP in intraperitoneal and lung infection models also leads to a pronounced reduction in pneumococcal virulence [206,207], suggesting the impairment of additional pneumococcal virulence components. Chastanet et al. reported that ClpC and ClpE do not participate in competence regulation but are required for thermotolerance [205]. In contrast, other studies reported that a clpC mutant in the R6 strain exhibited reduced genetic transformation efficiency, loss of pneumolysin production, and impaired adherence to human type II alveolar cells due to the functional impairment of the adhesin CbpA and mistranslated forms of the autolysin LytA [208,209]. These findings suggest a role for ClpC in competence development [209]. The interplay between protease activity, competence signaling, and environmental sensing supports pneumococcal survival across various niches, from asymptomatic carriage to invasive disease [182].

In recent pneumonic sepsis studies, we have shown that S. pneumoniae exhibits a prolonged and persistent competent state that contrasts sharply with the transient induction observed under in vitro conditions. Competence activation was visualized spatiotemporally using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS) in CD-1 mice intranasally infected with the ssbB-luc reporter strains of S. pneumoniae D39, TIGR4, and the serotype 3 strain 0100993. All strains naturally developed competence that persisted for an extended period of time [71]. Using the pneumonic sepsis model, our recent RNA-Seq analyses showed that, compared to the baseline (0-hour post-infection, hpi), 20%, 44%, and 26% of the top 50 most upregulated pneumococcal genes in infected mouse lungs at 12, 24, and >40-hpi were com genes (Figure 2). These data highlight the critical role of the competence regulon in early adaptation to the lung environment (0–12 hpi), progression of lung infection and breaching of the alveolar-capillary barrier (12–24 hpi), and maintenance of the septicemic stage (24–40 hpi) [79]. In contrast to the transient in vitro competent state that lasts 30–40 min, IVIS imaging indicates that the in vivo competent state typically emerges between 20 and 24-hpi, coinciding with the transition from localized pneumonia to systemic bacteremia and sepsis, ultimately resulting in host mortality [44,71,79]. However, our RNA-Seq results revealed that both early and late com genes were upregulated as early as 12-hpi. This discrepancy likely arises because IVIS detects bioluminescence from reporter proteins, whereas RNA-Seq directly measures mRNA levels with greater sensitivity [44].

Interestingly, competence development occurs naturally during pneumonic sepsis without requiring exogenously provided CSP [44,71]. Intranasal administration of CSP1 at the early stages of acute pneumonia infection transiently induced competence but failed to sustain it, and the competent state only consistently develops around 20–24 hpi [71]. A possible explanation is that the pneumococcal HtrA protease, which constitutes the primary extracytoplasmic CSP-degrading activity in S. pneumoniae, cleaves CSP produced endogenously or administered exogenously, thereby highlighting the role of HtrA in modulating the timing and sustainability of the naturally developed competent state [210]. In addition to CSP degradation, HtrA also supports competence by ensuring the proper folding of proteins within the competence machinery [63]. The htrA mutant is attenuated in a murine lung infection, characterized by reduced inflammation, lower levels of IL-6 and TNF-α, and fewer inflammatory lesions at the pleural interface [63].

Another likely explanation for the delayed onset of competence during pneumococcal sepsis is the need for pneumococcus to adapt to the host environment before achieving a sustained, naturally developed competent state [71]. We have reported that the phtABDE genes encoding pneumococcal histidine triad proteins, which contribute to metal ion homeostasis through chelation of divalent cations [211], and pneumococcal adherence to the respiratory epithelium [212], were upregulated at 12-hpi, but downregulated to near basal and below basal levels at 24 and 40-hpi, suggesting that Pht proteins participate in the early adaptation to the lung environment [79]. In the murine pneumonia model, mutants lacking phtA, phtB, phtD, and phtE exhibit significantly reduced virulence [213], and delayed entry into the competent state compared to the wild-type strain [79]. Interestingly, while in vitro studies indicate that divalent cations, particularly zinc and manganese, can modulate transformation [214], Oh et al. found that ΔphtABDE mutants exhibited an elevated competent state in the divalent cation-rich C+YA medium, but not the divalent cation-insufficient C+YB medium [79], most likely due to compensatory effects by AdcA and AdcAII zinc transporters. These results highlight the importance of divalent cations and suggest that their leakage into the lungs, due to a compromised air-blood barrier, may help overcome host-imposed nutritional immunity and trigger competence induction, exacerbating inflammation and facilitating bacterial dissemination [201]. Abundant nutrients in the blood further amplifies the competent state, as reflected by elevated bacterial burdens in distal organs, such as the spleen during the late stages of infection [71].

S. pneumoniae encodes two cyclic-di-AMP phosphodiesterases, Pde1 and Pde2 [215]. Deletion of either or both pde genes impairs pneumococcal growth and attenuates virulence in a mouse pneumonia [215]. Interestingly, transcriptomic analysis revealed that pde1 and pde2 mutant strains exhibit elevated expression of 20 com genes, relative to the wild-type, suggesting that cyclic-di-AMP may negatively regulate pneumococcal competence. However, despite the derepression of the com genes, the pde1 and pde2 mutants display reduced transformation efficiency, likely due to impaired responsiveness to CSP caused by elevated levels of cyclic-di-AMP [216]. The growth defects in the pde mutants may have contributed to both attenuated virulence and transformation efficiency as well. In addition, cyclic di-AMP has been reported to mediate biofilm formation in Streptococcus mutans [217]. Although its role in biofilm formation has not yet been investigated in S. pneumoniae, biofilm development during pneumonia in vivo is known to enhance virulence, partly through activation of the competence system [48,218].

Non-human primate model

In 1952, Austrian reported the transformation of pneumococcal genes encoding capsular polysaccharide and type-specific somatic M protein in several mammalian species, including mouse, rat, cat, guinea pig, rabbit, and monkey. He further discussed the potential relevance of these findings to the natural occurrence of pneumococcal transformation [219]. To date, this remains the only documented evidence of pneumococcal genetic transformation in a non-human primate model, marking an early milestone in understanding the induction of competence. Although Reyes and colleagues established a non-human primate model of pneumococcal pneumonia using adult baboons (Papio cynocephalus) that closely mimics human disease to study the lung infection and demonstrate the crucial virulence role of pneumolysin [220], competence induction was not investigated.

Pneumococcal competence development: ex vivo models

Recent advances in ex vivo systems have provided a more simplified framework to dissect the environmental and host-derived cues that regulate bacterial competence. Among these, the ex vivo perfused human lung model provides a clinically relevant platform for investigating lung physiology and host-pathogen interactions in isolation, without confounding effects from other organs, thereby bridging the gap between traditional in vitro studies and complex in vivo infection models [221]. Although the mechanisms underlying natural competence induction in vivo remain unclear, studies suggest that adaptation to the lung microenvironment is a key trigger [79]. In ex vivo human lung models, CSP is rapidly degraded by host-derived trypsin-like serine proteases abundant in the airways, leading to reduced transformation efficiency, an effect similarly observed in mouse lung homogenates [222]. This may also explain the delayed competence induction observed during pneumonia-derived sepsis, where activation is not detected until approximately 20-hpi by IVIS imaging and around 12-hpi based on RNA-Seq analysis of competence gene expression [44,71,79]. Moreover, high-dose intravascular pneumococcal infection in ex vivo lungs was rapidly cleared without epithelial injury, indicating that the lung environment imposes substantial barriers to bacterial persistence in bacteremia. This clearance was partly mediated by neutrophils in the perfusate and alveolar macrophages in the airspaces [223].

Competence regulon as therapeutic targets

Antibiotic use and pneumococcal conjugate vaccines have contributed to the emergence of antibiotic-resistant and vaccine-escape pneumococcal strains [12,224]. As a result, there is growing interest in targeting the competence regulon in S. pneumoniae as an alternative therapeutic strategy [225–228], and a detailed review can be found in Lella et al [229]. One such approach involves dominant-negative analogs of CSP (dnCSP), including CSP1-E1A (substituting the first glutamate residue with alanine) [93], CSP2-E1Ad10 (with an additional modifiable hydrophilic face) [225], a cyclic form of dnCSP, CSP1-E1A-cyc (Dap6E10) [228], and urea-bridged cyclic dnCSP (Dab6Dab10) [226]. These peptides block the activation of ComD1 and ComD2 without affecting their binding affinity. Consequently, they inhibit pneumococcal competence development, reduce pneumolysin release, and attenuate disease severity in mouse models of acute pneumonia and bacteremia caused by both CSP1-expressing D39 and CSP2-expressing TIGR4 S. pneumoniae strains [93,225,228]. Domenech et al. conducted high-throughput screening and identified several small-molecule inhibitors of pneumococcal competence (COM blockers) that interfere with the export of CSP by disrupting the proton motive force (PMF), thereby impairing ComD activation. These compounds, including a biocide (triclosan, TCL), an antimalarial drug (proguanil, PROG), and an antipsychotic (pimozide, PIM), inhibit competence and effectively block DNA uptake without significantly affecting bacterial growth. Mechanistically, impaired ComD activation prevents the phosphorylation of ComE, resulting in reduced transcription from the PcomAB and PcomCDE promoters. Notably, PROG reduced horizontal gene transfer efficiency in a mouse intraperitoneal infection model [230].

In addition to targeting ComD, Ishii et al. employed a high-throughput screen in S. mutans to identify small-molecule inhibitors, including quinuclidine derivatives, that bind the peptidase domain of ComA. These compounds inhibit biofilm formation and reduce transformation efficiency without impairing bacterial growth [231]. Although this work was conducted in S. mutans, the approach is likely applicable to S. pneumoniae, given their close evolutionary relationship and homologous quorum-sensing pathways [232]. The DNA adenine methyltransferase inhibitor pyrimidinedione was shown to inhibit biofilm formation and suppress the expression of virulence and competence genes, such as comC, comX, ciaR, ciaH, and dnaK [233]. Sinefungin, a nucleoside analog of S-adenosylmethionine and a methyltransferase inhibitor, has been shown to inhibit pneumococcal colonization in a rat otitis media model, as well as suppress pneumococcal biofilm formation in vitro [234]. Beyond direct competence inhibition, certain natural compounds demonstrate multifaceted anti-virulence properties. For example, alnustone, a non-phenolic diarylheptanoid derived from the alder plant Alnus pendula, has been shown to neutralize key pneumococcal virulence factors. Specifically, it inhibits the oligomerization of pneumolysin and suppresses the peptidase activity of sortase A (SrtA) [235]. In Staphylococcus aureus, the Agr quorum-sensing system regulates virulence genes through the effector RNAIII, which stabilizes the mRNA of the global regulator MgrA [236]. This stabilization subsequently upregulates sortase expression, facilitating the surface display of proteins required for tissue colonization [237] and the progression of severe complications such as septic arthritis and sepsis [238]. Consequently, mgrA and srtA mutants demonstrated a significantly impaired ability to colonize and persist in renal tissues (kidneys), resulting in markedly lower bacterial titers compared to the wild-type strain [237,238]. Although alnustone treatment significantly attenuates virulence in murine models of pneumococcal infection [235], its impact on the pneumococcal competence regulon remain unexplored. Nevertheless, SrtA inhibitors are increasingly recognized as promising anti-infective candidates because they target bacterial pathogenesis and host-tissue colonization rather than essential growth pathways, thereby exerting less selective pressure for the development of resistance [239]. By concurrently repressing the competence regulon in vivo, the aforementioned dominant-negative analogs of CSP (dnCSP), ComDE pathway inhibitors, and anti-virulence agents not only attenuate virulence but also limit horizontal gene transfer of antibiotic resistance and virulence genes.

Conclusion

Pneumococcal competence is a central adaptive mechanism that allows S. pneumoniae to respond to environmental stresses, acquire exogenous DNA, and modulate virulence. While in vitro studies have provided detailed mechanistic insights into the molecular regulation of competence, they often fail to recapitulate the spatial and temporal heterogeneity observed in vivo. In natural infections, competence is influenced by host-derived signals, tissue-specific microenvironments, and nutrient availability, resulting in a persistent and prolonged competent state during pneumonia-derived sepsis, in contrast to the transient induction observed under laboratory conditions. Animal models, ranging from nasopharyngeal colonization to otitis media, meningitis, bacteremia, and pneumonia and pneumonic sepsis, have revealed that the competence regulon contributes to both adaptation and virulence in a context-dependent manner (Table 1 and Figure 3). Key competence regulators such as ComE and ComX, as well as competence-regulated factors including pneumolysin and autolysins, orchestrate processes from genetic transformation, biofilm formation, to tissue invasion and immune evasion. The pivotal role of the competence regulon in pneumococcal pathogenesis presents promising opportunities for therapeutic intervention to reduce horizontal gene transfer and the emergence of antibiotic-resistant and vaccine-escape strains, as well as virulence. Future studies integrating multi-omic analyses, advanced imaging, and translational models will be crucial for understanding how competence contributes to disease progression in natural host settings and for developing competence-targeted strategies to prevent pneumococcal infections and their complications.
