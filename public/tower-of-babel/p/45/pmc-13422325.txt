Post-traumatic growth following the COVID-19 pandemic: a qualitative phenomenological study of Iranian ICU nurses
Authors: Abdoljavad Khajavi, Ali Mohammadpour, Moosa Sajjadi, Bahareh Payvar, Haydeh Hashemizadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDuring the COVID-19 pandemic, intensive care unit (ICU) nurses faced severe psychological, ethical, and physical challenges. Although many studies have focused on the negative consequences of this crisis, there is scant evidence regarding the positive dimensions of nurses’ experience and Post-Traumatic Growth (PTG), especially in spirituality-centered and collectivist cultural contexts like Iran. This study aimed to explore the experiences of PTG among Iranian ICU nurses in the final stages of the pandemic.MethodsThis qualitative study employed a descriptive phenomenological approach and Colaizzi’s 7-step method. Ten ICU nurses from two hospitals in Northeast Iran were purposefully selected. Data were collected through in-depth, semi-structured, face-to-face interviews between September 2022 and March 2023. Upon reaching saturation, the data were manually analyzed based on the Colaizzi steps.ResultsData analysis led to the extraction of five main themes and sixteen sub-themes. The main themes included: (1) Spirituality, (2) Resilience, (3) Cognitive Reconstruction, (4) Transformation of Social Role, and (5) Sustainable Healthy Lifestyle. These themes illustrate the experience of PTG across multiple dimensions, including spiritual, individual, professional, and social transformations among nurses following exposure to the crisis.ConclusionsNurses were able to transform the suffering and pressures of the COVID-19 period into opportunities for strengthening spirituality, personal growth, and professional development. Culture-centric factors such as spirituality, collective solidarity, and ethical commitment played a significant role in shaping PTG. These findings emphasize the necessity of reinforcing spiritual care, psycho-social support, and transformative learning-based education in nursing programs to enhance preparedness for future crises.Clinical trial numberNot applicableSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04772-z.Keywords: Post-traumatic growth, COVID-19, Intensive care nurses, Qualitative study, Phenomenological study, Nursing, Iran

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic was one of the greatest crises in the health system in the current century. Iran, as a country facing severe waves of infection and high mortality, experienced considerable pressure on its health and medical infrastructure [1]. Amidst this, Intensive Care Unit (ICU) nurses, who were on the front lines of critical patient care, encountered conditions that went beyond the usual standards of service delivery. These included shortages of personal protective equipment.

) PPE), lack of personnel, increased patient mortality, exhausting workload, and anxiety stemming from the possibility of self-infection or transmitting the disease to family members [2]. These conditions led to numerous psychological and physical consequences, including anxiety, depression, moral distress, occupational fatigue, and high levels of burnout [1, 3–8].

However, evidence suggests that when facing life-threatening events, humans do not exclusively suffer from post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [9], but may also experience positive changes referred to as Post-Traumatic Growth (PTG). PTG is a key concept in positive psychology, referring to cognitive, spiritual, personal, and interpersonal transformation after overcoming severely challenging events. The classic model by Tedeschi and Calhoun (2004) defines this phenomenon across five dimensions: Relating to Others, Appreciation of Life, Personal Strength, New Possibilities, and Spiritual Change [10]. This process also aligns with Mezirow’s Transformative Learning Theory (2018), which posits that critical experiences can activate a process of critical reflection, changes in cognitive frameworks, and a redefinition of individual and professional identity [11].

Research indicates that factors such as social support, self-compassion, resilience, and wisdom play a role in the emergence of PTG [12]. Scholarly interest in nurses’ experience with PTG has grown notably in recent years. For example, a 2024 scoping review revealed that nurses’ journey through the COVID-19 period transitioned from initial emotional turmoil and compassion fatigue to the culmination of professional role expansion and personal growth [13]. Furthermore, a qualitative study by Peng in China (2022) showed that many nurses experienced positive changes after the COVID-19 pandemic, such as sublimation of personal faith and strength, changes in understanding of the meaning of life, and new possibilities [14]. Also, despite intense work pressures during the COVID-19 pandemic, some nurses reported experiences of finding meaning, increased empathy, personal maturity, and strengthening of their professional identity [15]. PTG helps nurses re-reflect on their experiences, and this reflection, in addition to promoting their professional trajectory, also leads to increased overall life satisfaction [16].

The vast majority of existing knowledge primarily focuses on the negative outcomes nurses faced during the pandemic, such as stress, anxiety, and burnout. Consequently, the positive dimensions of their lived experiences remain significantly under-explored. Furthermore, few studies have specifically addressed PTG in ICU nurses, and most of the available research originates from Western cultural contexts. This is a critical limitation, as the trajectory of PTG in cultures like Iran—where values such as family-centricity, spirituality, and social solidarity are prominent—may differ significantly from Western models that typically emphasize individual autonomy. Therefore, a deep understanding of the lived experiences of Iranian nurses can fill the existing knowledge gap and lead to the design of culturally appropriate supportive interventions. Clarifying the mechanisms and cultural moderators of PTG is particularly important for nursing, given its direct impact on nurse retention, quality of patient care, and long-term psychological well-being [9]. Recent studies have shown that nurses who experience PTG report higher job satisfaction, stronger self-compassion, and greater resilience in crises [17]. Insights into how spirituality, collective solidarity, and ethical commitment shape PTG in collectivist contexts can inform culturally tailored interventions that sustain the nursing workforce during future health emergencies. Therefore, this study was designed and implemented with the aim of investigating the lived experiences of ICU nurses regarding PTG following the COVID-19 pandemic, using a phenomenological approach.

Methodology

Design

This qualitative study was conducted using Colaizzi’s descriptive phenomenological approach. Phenomenology is generally employed to deeply understand complex human phenomena, including individuals’ feelings, perceptions, and lived experiences [18]. This approach offers a systematic tool for uncovering the meaning and essence in people’s experiences to facilitate understanding [19]. In the present research, Colaizzi’s descriptive phenomenological approach was utilized to gain a deeper understanding of the PTG experiences among ICU nurses.

Setting and participants

Participants were recruited using purposeful sampling with maximum variation from two hospitals affiliated with Mashhad University of Medical Sciences in Khorasan Razavi province, located in Northeast Iran. The participants consisted of 10 nurses (4 men and 6 women). Inclusion criteria required participants to be actively working as ICU nurses, have a minimum of one year of work experience, and crucially, possess at least three months of documented experience providing direct care to COVID-19 patients in the ICU setting. Furthermore, participants needed the verbal capacity to articulate their emotional experiences. The sole exclusion criterion was a participant’s decision to withdraw. Table 1 provides a summary of the nurses’ demographic characteristics. Prior to commencement of the interviews, all nurses provided their written informed consent to participate in the research. The time and place of the interviews were arranged according to the participants’ preferences, and the interviews were held in a quiet, suitable room within the hospital. The interviews were conducted by the corresponding author, who had significant experience in interviewing and a deep understanding of qualitative research methods, and who had no prior relationship with the participants. This study is reported in compliance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist to ensure methodological transparency and rigor.

Data collection

In-depth, semi-structured interviews (10 main interviews along with follow-up interviews) were conducted face-to-face from September 2022 to March 2023. The interviews were scheduled at a time and location convenient for participants to ensure privacy and comfort, typically taking place in a quiet room at the hospital. The semi-structured interview guide was developed based on a comprehensive literature review [14, 16]. The interviews began with broad open-ended questions, such as: “Please describe your experience working in the Intensive Care Unit during the COVID-19 pandemic and its impact on your personal and professional life.“; “Despite the difficulties, did this experience bring any positive outcomes for you? If yes, please elaborate on the positive impacts or experiences that led to satisfaction, growth, or change in you.“; and “Did caring for COVID-19 patients change your values, priorities, or outlook on life? If yes, how?” (see Supplementary File 1). Subsequently, probing questions were used to explore more deeply the meaning and underlying factors of feelings of growth or satisfaction, including changes in human relationships, understanding of life, and its spiritual dimensions. To ensure the clarity of the questions and resolve any potential ambiguities, two ICU nurses participated in a pre-interview. These pre-interviews were purely for pilot purposes and were not included in the final data analysis. The data collection process continued until data saturation was achieved, defined as the point at which no new phrases or meaning units emerged (specifically, no new themes appeared after the eighth interview). Although saturation was initially reached at the eighth interview, two additional interviews (9th and 10th) were conducted to confirm the stability of the themes and ensure maximum variation in demographic characteristics (such as gender and work experience). This approach aligns with phenomenological standards that prioritize the depth of lived experience over sample size, ensuring robustness without compromising the richness of data. All interviews were digitally recorded using a high-quality audio recorder and varied in length from 35 to 90 minutes, with an average duration of approximately 60 minutes. Furthermore, field notes were taken during and after interviews to capture non-verbal cues and contextual observations.

Data analysis

Immediately following the interviews, the audio recordings and field notes were transcribed verbatim and prepared by the corresponding author. To ensure the accuracy and fidelity of the data, each transcript was reviewed against the audio files within 24 h of the interview. Once all interviews were completed, the data underwent immediate and independent manual analysis by two researchers who possessed substantial experience in qualitative methodologies. The analysis was systematically guided by Colaizzi’s seven-step descriptive phenomenological framework [20], and all seven steps were strictly adhered to without modification or omission. This process began with the repeated reading of the texts for familiarization with the data. This was followed by the extraction of significant statements and the subsequent formulation of meanings. These formulated meanings were then used for organizing the themes and grouping similar concepts together. Next, the process moved to providing a comprehensive description of the phenomenon, leading finally to the identification of the fundamental structure of the phenomenon. The final step involved returning to the participants for review and confirmation of the results. This process involved careful and repetitive examination of the collected data to achieve a comprehensive understanding of the participants’ information. Key sentences were isolated and recorded, recurring concepts were coded, and summaries of similar meanings were prepared to form initial themes. Subjective judgment was avoided throughout all stages. Every identified theme was meticulously described and documented with direct quotes from the participants. Subsequently, the resulting themes and descriptions were repeatedly compared and reviewed to extract common perspectives, formulate the final overarching themes, and ensure the accuracy of the results. Two researchers independently coded the data, and any discrepancies identified during comparative analyses were resolved through discussion. If consensus was not immediately reached, a third senior researcher was consulted to ensure neutrality and accuracy. Disagreements were resolved through discussion among the research team members until final consensus was reached. The final version of the transcribed data, along with the extracted themes and sub-themes, was sent to the participants. Their feedback and confirmation indicated that the research findings accurately reflected their actual viewpoints and experiences, and that researcher bias had not influenced the results (Member Checking).

Trustworthiness

To ensure the trustworthiness and rigor of the data in this study, Lincoln and Guba’s evaluation criteria were employed: Credibility, Dependability, Confirmability, and Transferability [21]. To enhance credibility, several measures were taken. Member checking was performed by sending the interview transcripts and extracted codes to the participants for verification of accuracy and perspective. Researchers focused on prolonged engagement, continuous observation, and dedicating sufficient time for data collection. Also, any ambiguities that arose during the analysis process were clarified through telephone interviews. To strengthen the credibility of the research and aid in revealing diverse interpretations of the data, a qualitative methodologist was engaged for active participation in the analysis and review process (Peer Debriefing).

Furthermore, to enhance credibility and confirmability, the researchers reflected on their positionality and maintained reflexivity throughout the study. The research team comprised experts in nursing and community medicine. Except for AK (a community medicine physician), all team members possessed extensive backgrounds in nursing, ensuring diverse yet clinically grounded perspectives during analysis. The corresponding author (HH), who conducted the interviews, is an experienced nursing researcher with a strong background in qualitative methodologies. She had no prior professional or personal relationship with the participants, minimizing potential power dynamics. Throughout the study, the team engaged in reflexivity practices, including regular debriefing sessions to discuss preconceptions and bracket personal biases regarding ICU nursing challenges. These practices ensured that the participants’ voices remained central to the findings rather than the researchers’ expectations.

To guarantee dependability, all methodological steps, including sampling and data analysis methods, were described in full detail to allow for an audit trail. Researchers also utilized guidance and supervision from experts to ensure the systematic nature of the processes. To ensure confirmability, researchers strived to avoid confirmation bias: they did not solely seek to validate their own hypotheses when interpreting the data, and no findings that contradicted their views were excluded. Finally, for the transferability of the findings, the method of maximum variation sampling was used, ensuring that participants exhibited diversity in terms of age, gender (both sexes), and marital/parental status. Furthermore, a rich and dense description of the sample characteristics and the research context was provided to allow other researchers to assess the generalizability of the results to similar settings.

Ethical considerations

Ethical considerations were rigorously implemented, beginning with securing written informed consent from all participants who were fully apprised of all research objectives, and explicit permission was obtained for recording the interviews. Confidentiality was strictly maintained throughout all interviews, and anonymity was guaranteed by utilizing assigned codes or numbers instead of names. The right to withdraw from the study at any time was assured. Furthermore, the accuracy and fidelity of the interview transcripts, along with proper citation of sources, were confirmed. All data were stored on a password-protected, encrypted device with access limited to the research team. Identifiable information was removed during transcription, and participants were assigned code identifiers to ensure anonymity; the code key was stored separately. After study completion, participants were offered a summary of the findings. The entire research process adhered completely to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Final ethical approval (Ref ID: IR.GMU. REC.1401.045) was obtained from the Research Ethics Review Committee of Gonabad University of Medical Sciences before study commencement.

Spirituality

Spirituality emerged as a key supportive resource for all participants facing the crisis. It was characterized by a sense of connection to a higher power, which frequently resulted in transformative outcomes, specifically: discovery of meaning in suffering and a deep engagement in prayer and spiritual practices.

Discovery of meaning in suffering

Amidst the heavy stress and profound suffering encountered in the COVID units, many nurses engaged in a search for a deeper, transcendent meaning in their professional service. For a subset of these nurses, this challenging experience provided a critical opportunity to strengthen their faith and enhance their closeness to God. As Participant 7 articulated:

In those busy days in the COVID unit, my faith in God grew much stronger. Every patient whose condition improved felt like a sign of God’s grace to me. I felt God was beside me, and that helped me persevere, and my work took on a different meaning. (Participant 7)

Deep engagement in prayer and spiritual practices

The experience of caring for patients during the critical COVID-19 conditions transformed into an opportunity for many nurses to deepen their spiritual connection and redefine their practice of prayer. The restrictions of the work environment and the difficulty of performing ritualistic worship under protective conditions caused prayer to transcend a daily ritualistic act, evolving instead into an intimate, soothing, internal dialogue with God. Participant 6 expressed:

In those days, prayer was no longer just reciting a few sentences. I was always talking to God, silently, in the middle of all that stress. I felt like I was confiding in Him; I felt calm. It was as if prayer had found a new meaning for me. (Participant 6)

Resilience

By drawing on internal and external resources, nurses were able to resist, adapt, and even grow despite intense pressures. Based on the narratives of the participants, this theme was the common denominator for five sub-themes: Social Support, Attachment to Care, Self-Efficacy, Growth of Informal Leaders, and Sense of Humor.

Social support

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the psychological and physical pressures of working in critical conditions unexpectedly led to the strengthening of nurses’ social support networks. Families provided effective support by increasing empathy, emotional contact, and taking on household responsibilities, which reinforced the nurses’ sense of security and appreciation. Participant 10 stated:

Relatives would call frequently, checking on us… When I was off duty and at home, my family managed all the responsibilities and tasks that I used to do myself, and they wouldn’t let me get more exhausted than I already was… (Participant 10)

Concurrently, within the workplace, the spirit of solidarity and collaboration increased among colleagues, fostering more intimate and meaningful professional relationships.

Attachment to care

The sustainability of care for COVID-19 patients fostered a profound emotional and professional attachment among nurses toward their roles and the individuals under their charge. This protracted engagement caused their responsibilities to transcend mere job duty, becoming fundamentally integrated into their professional identity and a source of deep personal meaning. The depth of this commitment was evident in the resistance some nurses displayed against external pressures, such as family concerns, to leave their unit. They perceived separation from their patients not merely as resignation, but as an abandonment of their core moral commitment and internal responsibility. This experience unequivocally demonstrates the capacity of crisis care to forge a resilient professional identity and cement a profound bond between the nurse and the humanitarian vocation of their profession. Participant 1 admitted:

If I leave the COVID unit, I will be sad… My mind and focus will still be on the COVID patients. I will ask myself: What are the COVID nurses doing now? What is the condition of the patients? (Participant 1)

Self-efficacy

The COVID-19 crisis served as a transformative learning environment, leading to significant professional growth among the nurses. The continuous confrontation with critically ill patients and resource limitations reinforced their clinical skills, independent decision-making, and sense of self-efficacy. Participant 1 said:

I personally treated my mother, who had COVID with an oxygen saturation of 69%, at home… Now, physicians pay attention to my opinions. (Participant 1)

Nurses considered this period a turning point in their professional journey. Participant 2 stated:

COVID turned a nurse with one year of experience into a seasoned nurse with ten years of experience, or maybe more. (Participant 2)

The limitation of equipment also fostered clinical creativity, prompting nurses to innovate and design effective alternative methods and tools. Participant 10 mentioned:

Because we didn’t have a spirometer, we used a glove and the bottom of a 50 cc syringe to create a simple respiratory spirometer for patients, and we performed the respiratory physiotherapy ourselves. (Participant 10)

Growth of informal leaders

The experience of the COVID-19 pandemic provided an opportunity for some nurses to redefine their roles. Relying on their clinical expertise, psychological resilience, and professional patience, they stood out from their colleagues and took on a supportive and motivational role. Participant 1 expressed:

Because of the experiences I gained during COVID, I am now a good role model for other nurses. (Participant 1)

This finding indicates that the COVID-19 crisis was not only a source of pressure but also a platform for the emergence of informal organizational leaders and professional growth among nurses.

Sense of humor

Sense of humor served as an effective coping strategy, playing a crucial role in the psychological resilience and emotional dynamism of nurses during the pandemic. In the absence of systematic support and under high workload pressure, nurses used humor and laughter as a tool to reduce stress, maintain team morale, and strengthen solidarity among colleagues. Participant 8 stated:

The pressure was huge, and bad news was coming in nonstop. Plus, we were totally on our own without system support. We took it upon ourselves to lighten things up with humor. A simple joke sometimes made the whole team laugh, and we’d forget the fatigue for a moment. Honestly, when I look back at those times, tough as they were, I still smile.(Participant 8)

Cognitive reconstruction

The COVID-19 crisis compelled nurses to re-examine their lives, careers, and values. This overarching theme points to the sub-themes of revisiting values and deeper appreciation, professional evolution from duty-orientation to empathy, self-rediscovery, and forming a positive and constructive outlook. This process of cognitive reconstruction signifies a deep change in the professional and existential perspectives and beliefs of nurses following the crisis experience.

Revisiting values and deeper appreciation

The continuous experience of confronting death and patients’ suffering during COVID-19 led nurses to revisit their values and gain a deeper appreciation for the simple, human aspects of their existence. Participant 6 stated:

After COVID, small things make me very happy… like a calm day or a simple conversation with my family. I feel I appreciate life much more than before. (Participant 6)

Participant 8 also said:

During that time, I was surrounded by fatalities… Now, when I see my mom and dad are still around, I truly feel grateful.

Professional evolution from duty-orientation to empathy

The experience of caring for COVID-19 patients caused nurses to move beyond mere execution of duty toward a more conscious, humanistic, and empathy-based form of care. As Participant 1 put it:

Now, serving the patient has a different meaning for me… I feel like I am working from the bottom of my heart. Before Corona, it was just my duty, but now I truly enjoy giving care. (Participant 1)

Participant 7 said:

If I am honest, before COVID, I saw the patient as just a commodity… Now, I am much more compassionate toward the patient than before Corona. (Participant 7)

These narratives show that the COVID-19 crisis not only presented professional challenges for nurses but also fostered a kind of ethical and emotional transformation in the nature of nursing care.

Self-rediscovery

Overcoming the crisis provided an opportunity to redefine professional and existential identity. Many nurses considered this experience a turning point for inner growth and personal flourishing. Participant 3 stated:

In my opinion, COVID was like a sharp knife; it cut short many lives, and it gave many others a new life. I reached flourishing and growth… I rediscovered myself… I feel I have grown stronger. (Participant 3)

Forming a positive and constructive outlook

By comparing the COVID-19 crisis with past challenges, nurses were able to develop a positive and hopeful outlook toward future threats. This perspective helped them manage anxiety and gain confidence in their collective abilities. Participant 1 stated:

I had worked with Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever before… I told myself, well, Corona is not worse than that. We got through SARS too, so we will get through this. (Participant 1)

Transformation of social role

The experience of serving on the front lines of the COVID-19 crisis not only reinforced nurses’ professional responsibility but also transformed their social identity and role within society. This transformation points to the sub-themes of: Elevation of the Nursing Status, Empathy Beyond Gender, and Interest in Knowledge Dissemination.

Elevation of the nursing status

During the COVID-19 pandemic, nurses were recognized as frontline heroes, and society and other members of the treatment team gained a greater appreciation for the importance of their role in preserving health and saving patients’ lives. This recognition led to the elevation of the nursing profession’s status within the healthcare system. Participant 7 stated:

I feel that society’s view of nursing has improved significantly, and that is truly satisfying. Now, physicians show more respect for the nursing role in care; it wasn’t like this before COVID… Physicians didn’t count us at all… but now they understand our work can genuinely impact treatment. (Participant 7)

Empathy beyond gender

During the COVID-19 pandemic, male and female nurses, clad in full coverage and PPE, adopted a uniform appearance, and gender differences in the workplace significantly diminished. This situation, driven by the shared goal of patient care, led professional relationships to be built on humanity, cooperation, and shared responsibility, rather than on gender or physical appearance. The experience showed that even in a society where outward appearance and gender differences are highly emphasized, a crisis can foster a sense of equality and empathy and blur gender stereotypes. Participant 7 stated:

With full equipment and protective clothing, there was no difference between men and women anymore; we all worked side-by-side, and our only goal was to help the patients. Even our appearance became the same… This was very interesting to me…. No one had a gendered view at all… This was a beautiful occurrence. (Participant 7)

Interest in knowledge dissemination

The confrontation with complex and unknown conditions spurred nurses to continuously update their knowledge and apply experiential learnings. This process strengthened the nurses’ sense of educational responsibility and their desire to share knowledge with colleagues and students, such that many voluntarily took on mentoring and guiding roles. Participant 3 stated:

COVID made me interested in knowledge dissemination. During Corona, I spontaneously wanted to teach others so they could provide better care for patients. (Participant 3)

Sustainable healthy lifestyle

Based on the narratives of the participants, the COVID-19 pandemic experience led to the formation of a sustainable healthy lifestyle in their daily lives. This theme includes the sub-themes of Personal Hygiene and Frequent Hand Disinfection and Adherence to Nutritional Hygiene, both of which evolved beyond temporary behavior into stable, long-term habits at both the individual and family levels.

Personal hygiene and frequent hand disinfection

The need to prevent virus transmission made personal hygiene and continuous hand disinfection an inseparable part of nurses’ daily routines, a habit that even spread to their family members. Participant 5 stated:

Even now when I go out, I have alcohol sanitizer in my bag. My children also got used to it and constantly remind me, ‘Mom, use the alcohol.’ Or my daughter, who goes to kindergarten, says, ‘Mom, aren’t you going to put alcohol in my bag?’ These things have become habits for us now. (Participant 5)

Adherence to nutritional hygiene

During the pandemic, adherence to hygiene practices in the purchase and consumption of food gained significant importance. This focus on hygiene also influenced family interactions, leading to changes in older behavioral patterns. Participant 9 said:

I always had a challenge with my father over this, and I used to tell him: ‘Dad, when you get bread from the bakery, at least carry a bag so the bread doesn’t get contaminated.’ Well, adherence to these hygiene issues truly materialized during Corona. (Participant 9)

Discussion

This study demonstrated that following the COVID-19 pandemic Iranian ICU nurses experienced PTG across five dimensions: Spirituality, Resilience, Cognitive Restructuring, Transformation of Social Role, and Sustainable Healthy Lifestyle. The findings suggest that nurses were able to transform intense professional and psychological pressures into an opportunity for personal, professional, and moral growth, a theme consistent with classic PTG models and transformative learning theory.

In the heart of the crisis, the nurses found inner peace and hope by relying on spiritual beliefs. As one participant articulated, ‘In those busy days in the COVID unit, my faith in God grew much stronger. I felt God was beside me.’ This finding aligns with the results of Babaheidari et al. [22]. Furthermore, studies in Shanghai and Iran have shown that the experience of crisis care led to spiritual growth, strengthened faith, and the redefinition of professional identity in nurses [23, 24]. In the Iranian cultural context, where spirituality is deeply woven into both daily life and professional practice [24], it functions not merely as a contributing element but as a central force driving post‑traumatic growth. This contrasts markedly with common Western theoretical models, where spirituality is usually treated as one of several dimensions of PTG [25]. This observed cultural divergence strongly emphasizes the need for localizing and adapting theoretical frameworks of PTG.

The prominence of spirituality in our findings can be understood within the contours of Iranian cultural context. First, core Shi’a concepts—such as sabr (active perseverance), ebtelā (divine testing), and Shafa’at (intercession)—offered participants culturally resonant frameworks for meaning-making. Second, collectivist social structures, characterized by the cohesion of extended-family networks, fostered a relational mode of spiritual growth distinct from more individualistic Western models. Third, institutional features of Iran’s healthcare system, including the availability of integrated spiritual care, further supported this process. It is important, however, to recognize cultural heterogeneity; our findings reflect tendencies specific to the study’s context rather than universal or essentialist claims. This underscores the value of future comparative research aimed at exploring diverse trajectories of posttraumatic growth across different cultural ecosystems.

The theme of Resilience in this study also demonstrated that nurses coped with intense work pressures by utilizing family and colleague support and internal resources such as self-efficacy and humor. This finding is consistent with the study by Jiang et al. (2023) in China, where peer support not only reduced stress but also led to the psychological reconstruction of nurses [16]. It is also aligned with Foli’s middle-range theory of nurses’ psychological trauma (2022), which emphasizes the role of social support in reducing nurses’ vulnerability [6]. However, in the collectivist context of Iran, resilience goes beyond simple psychological coping and becomes a platform for professional and spiritual growth, a phenomenon less reported in individualistic Western societies. For instance, a study in Sweden showed that many nurses tended towards leaving the profession instead of growth [26]. Reflecting this transformation, a nurse noted, ‘COVID turned a nurse with one year of experience into a seasoned nurse with ten years of experience,’ a form of accelerated professional growth that appears less frequently emphasized in more individualistic contexts. Commitment to care was a distinct dimension that elevated care from a job duty to an existential and moral obligation. Therefore, although resilience is not a direct dimension of PTG, it acts as a facilitating mechanism that provides the grounds for inner and spiritual growth in nurses through cognitive restructuring and social support [27].

The findings regarding cognitive restructuring showed that, following the crisis, the nurses revised their attitudes toward life, values, and the nursing profession, attaining a deeper understanding of their humanistic and ethical role. This process aligns with Mezirow’s transformative learning theory (2018), which emphasizes the role of crises in prompting reflection and changing mental frameworks [11]. During this process, nurses experienced a deeper appreciation for life, a shift toward ethics-centered care, self-rediscovery as a resilient individual, and the formation of a positive attitude. Illustrating this shift, one nurse shared, ‘After COVID, small things make me very happy. like a calm day or a simple conversation with my family. I feel I appreciate life much more than before.’ These findings are consistent with the results of Jiang et al.‘s 2024 study [23], which noted a transformation in the meaning of life and the redefinition of nurses’ professional identity following the pandemic. However, in the Iranian cultural context, this transformation was not purely individual; rather, it was rooted in human relationships, ethical commitment, and collective spirituality. This highlights the need for localizing the transformative learning theory within non-Western societies.

Transformation of social role was another significant finding. Nurses were recognized as frontline heroes during the crisis, and the community’s perspective on the nursing profession was noticeably elevated. As one participant expressed, ‘I feel that society’s view of nursing has improved significantly, and that is truly satisfying.’ Furthermore, the reduction of gender stereotypes and the nurses’ inclination towards education and knowledge transfer demonstrated the internalization of an educational and scientific role within their professional identity. These findings are consistent with international research. A study in Poland, for example, indicated that the increased visibility of nurses during the pandemic improved the profession’s social image [28]. Similarly, research in Turkey reported that the public’s attitude toward nurses became significantly more positive during COVID-19 [29]. These changes align with one of the five dimensions of PTG, namely improved social relationships.

In addition to the elevation of social status, two sub-themes emerged: Empathy beyond Gender and Inclination for Knowledge Transfer. The reduction of gender stereotypes in crisis environments—captured by one participant’s statement, “We all became one”—indicated a human-centered and collaborative experience. The nurses’ willingness to educate and share knowledge, extending beyond job duties, signified the internalization of a scientific role in their professional identity. This was also reported in Jiang et al.‘s study as a feeling of educational responsibility following the pandemic [23]. In summary, the transformation of the nurses’ social role in this study was not limited to an improved public image but was a reflection of a redefined professional identity based on ethical commitment, knowledge orientation, and shared crisis experience.

Finally, the theme of a Sustainable healthy lifestyle revealed that behavioral changes adopted by nurses during the pandemic—such as frequent handwashing and healthy eating—persisted as lasting habits in their personal and family lives after the crisis. This finding is consistent with Bagheri Sheykhangafshe (2022), which showed that the COVID-19 pandemic led to a sustainable transformation in health-promoting behaviors across society [30]. In the present study, these behavioral changes did not merely appear as a temporary coping strategy but evolved into stable patterns in individual and family life, to the extent that the participants’ children also mimicked these behaviors. This was highlighted by a nurse who said, “Even now when I go out, I keep an alcohol sanitizer in my bag. My children also have got used to it and constantly remind me, ‘Mom, use alcohol.’ These practices have become habits for us now.” This process is consistent with Mezirow’s transformative learning theory (2018), which views crises as “disorienting dilemmas” that prompt individuals to review beliefs and internalize new values in daily behaviors [11].While Western post-crisis growth frameworks primarily focus on internal transformations, the findings of this study suggest that in collectivist cultural contexts like Iran, growth can also manifest as objective, social, and sustainable changes in lifestyle. This observation necessitates a theoretical re-evaluation of existing frameworks.

Overall, this research demonstrated that PTG among Iranian nurses is a multidimensional, culture-sensitive, and ethics-centered phenomenon. In contrast to Western models of PTG, which emphasize autonomy and individual cognitive restructuring, growth here was shaped from within spirituality, collective solidarity, and an ethical commitment to care. This finding highlights the necessity of considering cultural contexts when designing psychological empowerment and educational programs for nurses. In contrast, studies conducted in Italy [31] and Sweden [26] primarily focused on nurses’ feelings of helplessness and burnout, with less attention paid to positive transformations—a difference that likely reflects the role of the cultural and spiritual context in facilitating growth and meaning-making among Iranian nurses.

Theoretical integration

Our findings indicate a strong alignment with Tedeschi and Calhoun’s five-domain model of PTG, while also offering cultural extensions. The theme of Spirituality directly corresponds to the Spiritual Change domain of PTG, although in the context of the present study, this factor acted as a foundational driver rather than merely a separate outcome. The theme of Resilience, which includes self-efficacy and social support, maps onto the domains of Personal Strength and Relating to Others. The theme of Cognitive Reconstruction, characterized by revisiting values and a deeper appreciation of life, aligns with the domains of Appreciation of Life and New Possibilities [10]. However, the theme of Sustainable Healthy Lifestyle challenges the traditional boundaries of PTG, suggesting that in collectivist cultures, growth manifests not only cognitively but also through tangible behavioral adaptations in daily living. These findings are also consistent with Mezirow’s Transformative Learning Theory [11]. The pandemic acted as a disorienting dilemma, activating the process of critical reflection, which is evident in the theme of Cognitive Reconstruction. The subsequent perspective transformation is also visible in the themes of Social Role Transformation and Self-Rediscovery, where nurses redefined their professional identities. This theoretical integration suggests that while Western models provide a structural foundation, the Iranian cultural context shapes the trajectory of growth, emphasizing collective solidarity and spiritual meaning-making over individual autonomy.

Implications for nursing practice and policy

The results of this study emphasize the need to cultivate supportive ICU environments that foster critical reflection, professional dialogue, and strong peer support. Specifically, concrete interventions could include structured peer debriefing sessions post-crisis and mentorship programs pairing experienced nurses with novices to foster informal leadership. For spiritual care, ICU settings could integrate dedicated quiet spaces for prayer or reflection, accessible religious counselors, and flexibility in scheduling to accommodate religious practices, ensuring alignment with local cultural values. As far as nursing education is concerned, incorporating the transformative learning theory framework into curricula can help foster critical thinking, collective resilience, and ethical development among students and practicing nurses. Utilizing methods such as experiential learning, narrative writing, and mentorship can facilitate this process. At the health policy level, nurses’ mental health programs must be designed with a culture-sensitive and growth-oriented approach—programs that emphasize not only stress reduction but also empowerment, meaning-making, and collective resilience, ultimately leading to the improvement of nurses’ mental health and the quality of patient care.

From a theoretical perspective, these findings extend the traditional models of PTG by highlighting the indispensable role of cultural and spiritual coping mechanisms in non-Western contexts. Practically, healthcare administrators and nursing managers should move beyond basic psychological first aid. The findings suggest a critical need to proactively design targeted organizational interventions, such as structured peer-support networks, spiritual care integration, and continuous resilience-building modules. By fostering such supportive environments, hospital leadership can facilitate the transition from occupational trauma to post-traumatic growth among ICU nurses facing chronic professional stressors.

Strengths

The study was conducted in the late stages of the COVID-19 pandemic (18–24 months after the peak), a time when the perceived positive effects of the crisis had become apparent, contributing to the richness of the findings. All interviews were conducted face-to-face and were long-duration, which allowed for deeper interaction and a more precise understanding of the nurses’ psychological experiences. Unlike previous studies that mainly focused on negative experiences, this research investigated the positive outcomes and multidimensional growth of nurses.

Limitations

Due to its qualitative nature, the results of the study are not generalizable to other health systems or races/ethnicities. The translation of perceptions from Farsi to English may have introduced limitations, although this was mitigated with the help of a native English editor. The time interval of 18–24 months after the peak of the pandemic may have created a “positive memory bias”. Specifically, the temporal distance likely influenced the narratives through retrospective reconstruction. Participants may have engaged in meaning-making processes over time, potentially softening acute negative emotions to align with a coherent story of growth. Of course, this does not invalidate the growth experienced but suggests that it may be interpreted differently than during the acute phase. Future studies conducted closer to the crisis event could compare these temporal effects.

Conclusions

This study underscores the multidimensional nature of PTG among ICU nurses, emphasizing that growth is not merely an individual cognitive process but is deeply embedded in cultural, spiritual, and social contexts. Theoretically, these findings necessitate the localization of Western PTG frameworks to account for collectivist values and spirituality as central drivers rather than peripheral dimensions. Practically, nursing policies must integrate spiritual care resources, structured peer support systems, and transformative learning modules into education curricula to foster resilience proactively. Future research should employ longitudinal designs to track PTG trajectories over time and conduct comparative cross-cultural studies to refine indigenous theories of crisis-related growth. Ultimately, leveraging these insights can transform crisis management from damage control to opportunities for sustainable professional development.
