Case Summary

A 28-year-old woman presented for routine medical follow-up. Medical history was notable for a Fontan procedure, with the last operation performed 12 years earlier and followed by an unspecified percutaneous cardiac intervention. Electrocardiography demonstrated a frontal QRS axis between −90° and ±180°, with right ventricular (RV) pressure overload (Figure 1). Physical examination revealed a pansystolic murmur audible over the tricuspid and mitral areas, digital clubbing, and an oxygen saturation of 85%. Laboratory evaluation showed a hemoglobin level of 19 g/dL and a hematocrit level of 62%.

Discussion

The “northwest QRS axis,” a manifestation of extreme right-axis deviation, is an infrequent electrocardiographic pattern characterized by a QRS axis ranging from −90° to ±180°. The aVR and lead I findings represent mirror-image phenomena, reflecting their opposing orientations relative to ventricular depolarization. Under these conditions, the frontal-plane depolarization vector travels from left to right and from inferior to superior, directed toward aVR and away from lead I, leading to a dominant R-wave in aVR and a markedly deep, wide S-wave in lead I.1

Extreme right-axis deviation on electrocardiography may reflect technical errors such as incorrect lead placement or underlying conditions including abnormal cardiac position, ventricular rhythms, severe hyperkalemia, toxicity from sodium channel–blocking agents, pulmonary emphysema with RV hypertrophy, and conduction abnormalities such as right superior fascicular block.1,2 Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (Figure 2, Video 1, Video 2) demonstrated an atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD) and Fontan circulation, with occlusion of the pulmonary trunk, a total cavopulmonary connection via an extracardiac conduit between the inferior vena cava and pulmonary artery, a bidirectional Glenn shunt, and a patent stent at the atrial fenestration.

AVSD accounts for 4% to 5% of congenital heart defects (CHDs).3 The condition involves septal defect in the crux of the heart, extending into both the interatrial and interventricular septum.4 In affected patients, posterior displacement of the atrioventricular node and inferior positioning of the His bundle near the ventricular septal defect result in left-axis deviation on electrocardiography.3, 4, 5

The Fontan procedure separates the systemic and pulmonary venous circulations in the absence of a subpulmonary ventricle, restoring a series circulation.3,4 Contemporary Fontan anatomy consists of a total cavopulmonary connection using an intracardiac or extracardiac conduit between the inferior vena cava and the pulmonary artery, combined with a superior vena cava to pulmonary artery anastomosis (bidirectional Glenn shunt).4 This circulation directs systemic venous return to the pulmonary arteries without ventricular pumping and is used to palliate multiple forms of single-ventricle CHD. The Fontan circulation is inherently characterized by chronic systemic venous hypertension and reduced cardiac output, and long-term exposure may lead to a progressive multisystem disorder with diverse phenotypes of circulatory failure.3

A QRS axis in the northwest quadrant suggests a marked disturbance of RV outflow tract conduction.1 Such electrocardiographic findings have been reported in patients with tetralogy of Fallot and may be comparable in individuals with AVSD or Fontan circulation, in whom a northwest QRS axis associated with RV pressure overload reflects altered RV activation and remodeling, often persisting from childhood into adulthood and related to RV outflow tract obstruction.1,2

Options A and B are incorrect because they would cause an artifactual axis shift without explaining the associated signs of RV pressure overload, whereas options C and D produce distinct electrical patterns unrelated to extreme axis deviation in the context of the patient's medical history. This case illustrates an uncommon electrocardiographic pattern that should be recognized in patients with complex CHD and Fontan circulation, reflecting longstanding alterations in ventricular activation and remodeling.
