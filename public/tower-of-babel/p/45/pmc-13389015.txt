Constrained speed reducer design optimization using a novel Scuba Diver Optimization Algorithm
Authors: Saman M Almufti, Amira Bibo Sallow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe speed reducer design problem is a classical benchmark in constrained mechanical optimization, characterized by a highly nonlinear objective function, mixed discrete–continuous design variables, and a narrow feasible region governed by multiple stress, deflection, and geometric constraints. Although numerous evolutionary and swarm-based metaheuristic algorithms have been applied to this problem, many existing methods exhibit premature convergence, instability in feasibility maintenance, and strong sensitivity to control parameters, particularly when operating near constraint-active regions. These limitations indicate a persistent need for more robust and adaptive optimization strategies. This paper proposes a novel application of the Scuba Diver Optimization Algorithm (SDOA) for minimizing the total weight of a speed reducer subject to eleven nonlinear inequality constraints. From an optimization perspective, SDOA is formulated as a population-based metaheuristic that employs an adaptive control variable to regulate search depth, exploration–exploitation balance, and operator selection throughout the optimization process. The algorithm integrates staged global exploration, controlled local refinement, and diversity restoration mechanisms, enabling effective navigation of highly constrained design spaces. A penalty-augmented constraint-handling scheme is incorporated to ensure reliable discrimination between feasible and infeasible solutions. Extensive numerical experiments were conducted under 30 parameter scenarios spanning three population sizes (50, 200, and 500 divers), multiple iteration budgets, and different oxygen-decay and elite-preservation settings. Each scenario was evaluated over 30 independent runs, yielding a total of 900 optimization runs. The best overall configuration attained a minimum reducer weight of 2992.859644, while the complete campaign achieved an overall feasibility rate of 899/900 runs (99.89%). Best, mean, worst, and standard deviation statistics consistently indicate stable convergence, narrow best–mean gaps, and strong repeatability across the tested settings. The results demonstrate that SDOA provides an effective and competitive optimization framework for constrained mechanical design problems. Introduction

Engineering design optimization problems frequently involve nonlinear objective functions, tightly coupled constraints, and mixed discrete–continuous decision variables. Among the most extensively studied benchmarks in this domain is the speed reducer design (SRD) problem, which seeks to minimize the total weight of a gear transmission system subject to mechanical strength, deflection, and geometric constraints. Owing to its narrow feasible region and strong interdependence among variables, the SRD problem has become a standard testbed for assessing the effectiveness, robustness, and constraint-handling capability of optimization algorithms. Recent work in constrained global optimization has also emphasized surrogate assistance, adaptive repair, and diversity-aware search as important ingredients for improving feasibility reliability and convergence robustness in complex design problems1–4.

Over the past decades, a wide range of optimization techniques have been applied to the SRD problem, including gradient-based methods, evolutionary algorithms, and swarm intelligence approaches such as particle swarm optimization (PSO), differential evolution (DE), and artificial bee colony (ABC) algorithms. While these methods have demonstrated reasonable performance, their effectiveness is often limited by premature convergence, loss of population diversity, and difficulty in maintaining feasibility throughout the search process. These shortcomings become particularly pronounced when the optimal solution lies close to multiple active constraints, as is typically the case in minimum-weight mechanical design problems.

Despite numerous improvements and hybrid variants, most PSO-, DE-, and ABC-type methods rely on fixed or weakly adaptive control parameters. Their exploration–exploitation balance is usually governed by externally tuned coefficients rather than by an internal, dynamically evolving control mechanism. As a result, these algorithms often lack the ability to autonomously adjust search behavior in response to stagnation, constraint violations, or diminishing diversity. In the context of the SRD problem, this limitation frequently leads to oscillations between feasible and infeasible regions, unstable convergence near constraint boundaries, or excessive dependence on penalty parameter tuning.

Constraint feasibility remains a central challenge in SRD optimization. The feasible region occupies only a small fraction of the search space and is defined by multiple nonlinear and tightly coupled constraints. Many existing methods either approach constraint boundaries too aggressively—resulting in frequent violations—or adopt overly conservative strategies that compromise solution quality. Consequently, achieving both high-quality objective values and consistent feasibility across independent runs remains an unresolved issue in the literature. This gap motivates the development of optimization frameworks that integrate adaptive search control and constraint awareness in a unified and principled manner.

In this study, the Scuba Diver Optimization Algorithm (SDOA) is investigated as an alternative metaheuristic framework for addressing these challenges. Unlike conventional swarm-based algorithms, SDOA employs an internal adaptive control variable that regulates search intensity, operator selection, and diversification over the course of the optimization process. This mechanism enables a structured transition from global exploration to local exploitation while explicitly supporting feasibility recovery and diversity preservation. Such characteristics are particularly well suited for constrained engineering design problems with narrow feasible regions, such as the SRD benchmark.

From a methodological standpoint, the primary distinction of SDOA from many adaptive swarm and evolutionary algorithms lies in the use of a single internal state variable, oxygen level, to regulate search depth, operator selection, and diversification within a unified control framework. In contrast, related methods typically rely on multiple externally tuned coefficients, separate repair procedures, or loosely coupled restart heuristics. The proposed formulation does not claim formal optimality guarantees; rather, its theoretical rationale is functional and algorithmic: the monotonic decay of oxygen imposes an ordered transition from wide-range exploration to increasingly localized refinement, while the reset stage prevents irreversible loss of diversity when the search approaches infeasible or weakly improving regions. This interpretation provides a principled explanation for the role of the oxygen mechanism beyond metaphorical inspiration.

Literature review

The speed reducer design (SRD) problem remains a standard benchmark in constrained mechanical optimization because it combines a nonlinear objective function, mixed discrete–continuous decision variables, and multiple tightly coupled inequality constraints. Its feasible region is narrow, irregular, and highly sensitive to perturbations in the design variables, so high-quality solutions are typically located close to several active constraint boundaries. These characteristics make SRD an informative test case for assessing not only objective minimization, but also feasibility preservation, convergence stability, and robustness across repeated runs. Deterministic formulations have addressed SRD through mathematical reformulation and global optimization strategies; for example, Lin et al. transformed the problem into a tractable form to obtain an approximate global solution, thereby establishing a useful baseline for later metaheuristic studies5.

Because deterministic approaches often require substantial problem-specific reformulation, a large body of subsequent work has focused on evolutionary and swarm-based methods. These include particle swarm optimization, differential evolution, artificial bee colony algorithms, crow-search variants, grey wolf optimizers, and numerous hybrids tailored to constrained engineering design. Their appeal lies in their derivative-free nature and their ability to search nonconvex and discontinuous spaces. However, the SRD literature also shows that good best-case results alone are insufficient to demonstrate algorithmic strength. In constrained engineering optimization, the central challenge is to achieve low objective values while maintaining feasibility consistently, especially when the optimum lies near multiple active constraints. Recent SRD-oriented studies continue to illustrate this tension between aggressive search and feasibility reliability. For instance, Cheng et al. proposed an improved crow-search framework for engineering design optimization, while Sahu et al. introduced a hybrid social-group optimization and differential-evolution scheme to strengthen the balance between global exploration and local exploitation on the SRD benchmark. Likewise, Hijjawi et al. reported competitive engineering-design results using an arithmetic optimization algorithm enhanced by cuckoo search, and Ma presented an enhanced grey wolf optimizer for the same class of problems. These studies confirm the continued relevance of hybridization and adaptive search scheduling, yet they also suggest that constraint-handling reliability remains a major determinant of practical performance6–9.

A closer reading of the literature reveals three persistent methodological issues. First, many algorithms remain vulnerable to premature convergence, especially when the population contracts too quickly around an infeasible or weakly feasible region. Second, many methods still rely on fixed or weakly adaptive control parameters, which limits their ability to adjust search pressure as the population approaches constraint-active regions. Third, constraint handling is frequently treated as an auxiliary mechanism, often through static penalty terms or loosely coupled repair rules, rather than as an integral part of the search dynamics. This is a serious limitation for SRD because feasibility violations can arise from very small changes in the decision variables. A recent state-of-the-art review by Lagaros et al. emphasizes that constraint-handling remains one of the most critical issues in metaheuristic optimization and that performance depends strongly on how feasibility information is integrated into the search process10.

Recent developments in constrained optimization have increasingly moved beyond conventional penalty-based formulations toward more adaptive and structurally informed strategies. Ji et al. proposed an ε-constrained multiobjective differential-evolution framework with an adaptive gradient-based repair mechanism for real-world constrained optimization problems. Their results demonstrate that integrating objective information into repair operations can improve both feasibility attainment and solution quality, particularly when feasible regions are limited and difficult to enter. In a related direction, Ji et al. later introduced a surrogate-assisted evolutionary algorithm for expensive optimization with equality and inequality constraints, highlighting the growing importance of efficiency-aware constrained search when exact evaluations are costly. These studies indicate a broader trend in the field: effective constrained optimization increasingly depends on the coordinated management of objective improvement, constraint satisfaction, and computational efficiency, rather than on a single update rule or penalty formulation1,2.

These developments also sharpen the point of departure of the present work. Whereas recent constrained optimizers often improve performance through surrogate modelling, adaptive repair, or explicit multiobjective diversity management, SDOA instead emphasizes an internally regulated transition mechanism that couples exploration pressure, feasibility recovery, and population renewal through oxygen-driven search stages. This distinction is important because it frames SDOA not as another metaphor-only variant, but as a unified adaptive-control strategy for narrow feasible-region search1,2.

Another important trend in recent literature concerns diversity preservation. In constrained and multimodal optimization, diversity is not merely beneficial for exploration; it is often essential for preventing the population from collapsing around infeasible attractors or suboptimal local basins. This issue has been emphasized in recent multiobjective and application-driven studies. For example, Ji et al. addressed a heat pipe-constrained IoT device layout problem using multiobjective differential evolution, demonstrating how constraint management, gradient-based repair, and diversity-aware population search can be combined in a challenging engineering context. Similarly, Zhang et al. proposed a region division and merging strategy for multimodal multiobjective optimization, explicitly targeting the preservation and organization of solution diversity across multiple promising regions. Although these studies are not SRD-specific, they are highly relevant because they show that modern constrained optimization increasingly treats diversity control as a central design principle rather than a secondary by-product of stochastic search. From this perspective, the diversity-restoration and reset mechanisms of SDOA are consistent with the broader methodological direction of maintaining population heterogeneity throughout the search process, although a more explicit diversity-control analysis remains a valuable direction for future work3,4.

Taken together, the literature suggests that robust constrained optimization depends on the interaction of three core factors: constraint-handling reliability, adaptive search control, and diversity preservation. Yet many existing swarm and evolutionary approaches still treat these factors through separate heuristics, such as externally tuned coefficients, standalone repair procedures, or occasional restart mechanisms. This leaves a methodological gap: relatively few studies provide a unified internal control mechanism that regulates search intensity, feasibility recovery, and diversification within a single adaptive framework. The present study positions the Scuba Diver Optimization Algorithm (SDOA) in that gap. Its oxygen-regulated internal control variable is intended to coordinate exploration, exploitation, and diversity restoration in an integrated manner, rather than through loosely connected parameter schedules. From the standpoint of constrained engineering design, this is particularly relevant for SRD, where the feasible region is narrow and the optimum lies near several active constraints. In this sense, the motivation for SDOA arises directly from persistent limitations in the existing literature rather than from metaphor alone.

Broader methodological context is also provided by adjacent optimisation literature. Recent studies have examined multi-objective metaheuristic resource-allocation and scheduling models11, metaheuristic optimisation in manufacturing processes12, and swarm-intelligence strategies for difficult combinatorial search problems13. Related algorithmic background also includes the social group optimization framework14, the arithmetic optimization algorithm15, the Serval Optimization Algorithm16, and improved wild horse optimization17. Comprehensive surveys of metaheuristics for mechanical design and new-generation search methods further frame the field18,19, along with seminal foundations such as particle swarm optimization20, differential evolution21, grey wolf optimization22, and artificial bee colony search23. More recent comparative and algorithmic developments include broad empirical comparisons for engineering design24, adaptive FOX-based search25, and improved honey badger optimisation26.

Overall, the existing body of work shows that SRD remains a demanding benchmark not because near-optimal values are unattainable, but because reproducible feasibility, low variance, and stable convergence near active boundaries are difficult to achieve simultaneously. The present study contributes to this line of research by investigating whether an oxygen-regulated adaptive mechanism can improve feasibility reliability and convergence robustness while retaining competitive solution quality on the SRD benchmark. At the same time, recent constrained-optimization literature also makes clear that broader validation on additional test suites will be necessary before any strong claim of generality can be established1–4,10.

Problem formulation

Despite its widespread use as a benchmark problem, the speed reducer design problem remains nontrivial due to the strong coupling among design variables and the simultaneous activation of multiple nonlinear constraints at the optimum. The feasible region occupies a small and irregular portion of the search space, and minor perturbations in design variables can easily lead to constraint violations. These characteristics significantly complicate the optimization process and demand robust feasibility-preserving search strategies9,14.

Methodology

Overview of the Scuba Diver Optimization Algorithm

The Scuba Diver Optimization Algorithm (SDOA) is a population-based metaheuristic inspired by the staged behavior of scuba divers operating under limited oxygen supply. Each search agent, referred to as a diver, represents a candidate solution in the design space. The algorithm introduces an explicit physiological control variable—oxygen level—which governs the diver’s allowable depth and search behavior throughout the optimization process.

Unlike conventional swarm algorithms that rely primarily on global-best attraction, SDOA regulates the balance between exploration and exploitation through oxygen-driven depth stages shown in Fig. 2. This mechanism enables structured transitions from aggressive global exploration to cautious local refinement, while preserving diversity through controlled reset operations.

SDOA stages.

The oxygen mechanism can therefore be interpreted as a monotone scheduling variable that controls search resolution over time. At high oxygen levels, the expected search radius is intentionally large and supports long-range transitions; at intermediate oxygen levels, the algorithm places greater emphasis on bounded mixing and coordinate-wise refinement; and at very low oxygen levels, it favours small perturbations together with targeted reset for non-improving or infeasible agents. Although this interpretation remains heuristic rather than formally derived from convergence theory, it provides a coherent explanatory basis for why the algorithm can balance exploration, exploitation, and feasibility recovery in a single adaptive framework.

Reset stage

The reset stage is not invoked as a global restart. Instead, it is activated when a diver reaches the lowest oxygen stage, indicating that the current search trajectory has exhausted its allocated search depth. At that point, reinitialization is applied primarily to divers that remain infeasible or have failed to produce a meaningful improvement in penalized fitness, so that diversity can be restored without discarding productive feasible individuals. In this sense, reset acts as a stage-dependent diversification operator linked to persistent infeasibility or non-improving stagnation rather than as an unconditional random restart.

Under this scheme, the coefficient mainly affects the scaling of penalized fitness among infeasible solutions during the search, rather than the final preference for feasible designs. In the present implementation, the coefficient was chosen to be sufficiently large to prevent objective-function improvements from dominating substantial constraint violations. Although this design reduces practical sensitivity relative to purely static penalty methods, a dedicated coefficient-sensitivity study was not conducted in the current work and should be considered in future research.

Acceptance rule and elite preservation

A newly generated solution is accepted if it improves the penalized fitness or achieves feasibility when the previous solution was infeasible. Elite preservation is enforced by retaining the best-performing solutions at each iteration and reinserting them after population updates, ensuring monotonic improvement of the global best.

Algorithm workflow

The overall workflow of the proposed Scuba Diver Optimization Algorithm (SDOA) is structured to ensure a systematic balance between exploration, exploitation, and constraint satisfaction throughout the optimization process. As shown in Fig. 3, the algorithm begins with the initialization of a population of divers, where each diver represents a candidate solution in the design space. The initial positions are generated randomly within the prescribed lower and upper bounds of the decision variables, and an initial oxygen level is assigned to each diver to regulate its search behavior.

SDOA flowchart.

Following initialization, the objective function and constraint violations are evaluated for all divers. A penalty-based constraint-handling mechanism is employed to distinguish feasible and infeasible solutions, ensuring that feasible designs are always preferred during the selection process. The best feasible solution is identified and stored as the global elite.

The optimization process then proceeds iteratively through oxygen-regulated depth stages. At high oxygen levels, divers operate in deep exploration mode, performing large stochastic movements to explore the global search space. As oxygen gradually depletes, the algorithm transitions into moderate exploration and local improvement stages, where movement amplitudes are reduced and attraction towards elite solutions is introduced. At low oxygen levels, a fine-tuning stage is activated, employing non-uniform mutation to refine promising solutions near constraint boundaries and local optima.

Throughout the iterations, oxygen levels are updated according to a predefined decay model, and divers that experience stagnation or excessive constraint violation are reinitialized to restore population diversity. Elite preservation is enforced to prevent loss of the best feasible solutions. This iterative process continues until the maximum number of iterations is reached or a convergence criterion is satisfied.

Finally, the algorithm outputs the best feasible design variables along with the corresponding objective function value and constraint verification results. The structured flow of SDOA enables effective navigation of the highly constrained design space while maintaining robustness, feasibility reliability, and convergence stability.

Computational complexity

The computational cost of SDOA can be analysed in terms of population size N, problem dimension D, and maximum iteration count T. In the deep-exploration, moderate-exploration, fine-tuning, and reset stages, each diver update involves vector operations, boundary control, and one penalized-fitness evaluation, which together require O(D) arithmetic per individual apart from the objective and constraint calculations. The local-improvement stage is more demanding because it performs coordinate-wise neighbourhood refinement; its per-diver cost is O(D) trial moves, each followed by fitness and constraint evaluation. Accordingly, the worst-case per-iteration complexity of SDOA is O(ND) for the global-update stages and O(ND^2) when the full local coordinate search is activated for the entire population.

Over T iterations, the resulting worst-case time complexity is O(TND^2), while the practical cost is often closer to O(TND) because the more expensive local-improvement operator is only activated during intermediate search stages rather than at every update for every diver. The space complexity is O(ND), since the algorithm stores the population, elite solutions, and a small set of auxiliary vectors. From this perspective, SDOA remains in the same general complexity class as many population-based metaheuristics such as PSO and DE with additional local-search or repair components: its dominant cost is still driven by repeated objective-function evaluations across the population, while the adaptive oxygen-regulated mechanism mainly adds lightweight control logic rather than asymptotically dominant overhead.

Results and discussion

Experimental setup

The proposed SDOA was implemented in MATLAB and applied to the speed reducer design problem using multiple parameter configurations scenarios and independent runs. Each run was executed with a population size of 500 divers. The stopping criterion was defined by a maximum number of iterations, which varied across scenarios to evaluate convergence behavior and robustness. Comparative context for recent 2025 metaheuristic developments is available in9,27–29.

All simulations were performed on a computer equipped with an Intel(R) Core(TM) i7-9750 H CPU running at 2.60 GHz, 32 GB of RAM, and MATLAB R2025b. These hardware and software details are reported to provide context for the execution times presented in the “Results and discussion” section.

For each scenario, 30 independent runs were performed, resulting in a total of 900 optimization runs. The performance indicators considered include the best, mean, and worst feasible objective values, standard deviation, execution time, and number of feasible runs. This evaluation protocol provides a substantially more reliable basis for assessing stochastic performance than single-run or best-only reporting.

Beyond reporting raw objective values, the revised experimental campaign explicitly quantifies feasibility reliability. Across all 30 scenarios, SDOA produced 899 feasible solutions out of 900 runs, corresponding to an overall feasibility rate of 99.89%. Twenty-nine scenarios achieved 30/30 feasible runs, while one scenario achieved 29/30 feasible runs. The standard deviations and narrow best–mean gaps observed in the higher-performing settings indicate controlled convergence rather than dependence on rare favorable runs.

Formal statistical analysis of population-size effects

To complement the descriptive statistics, formal nonparametric significance tests were performed on the repeated 30-scenario design using the scenario-level summaries extracted from the final results workbook. Because the same ten parameter configurations were repeated across three population sizes (50, 200, and 500 divers), the resulting groups form a matched blocked design. A Friedman test applied to the scenario-level mean objective values showed a statistically significant difference among the three population-size groups (chi-square = 20.000, p = 4.54 × 10^-5). Post-hoc Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction indicated that the 200-diver setting significantly outperformed the 50-diver setting, and the 500-diver setting significantly outperformed both the 200-diver and 50-diver settings in terms of mean objective value (adjusted p = 0.0059 for all three pairwise comparisons).

A parallel Friedman analysis of execution time also revealed a statistically significant dependence on population size (chi-square = 20.000, p = 4.54 × 10^-5), confirming the expected computational trade-off: larger populations provide better objective-value concentration and lower median scenario means, but they do so at increased runtime. These results statistically support the qualitative interpretation of the convergence tables. Since matched run-level distributions for competing literature algorithms are generally unavailable, the present inferential analysis is restricted to the internal SDOA configuration groups rather than cross-study comparisons with external methods (Table 4).

Feasibility reliability

In constrained engineering optimization, solution quality must be assessed not only in terms of the attained objective value, but also in terms of the consistency with which the algorithm preserves feasibility. This aspect is especially important for the speed reducer design problem, whose feasible region is narrow and bounded by multiple nonlinear mechanical, geometric, and design constraints. In the expanded experimental campaign, the proposed SDOA produced 899 feasible solutions out of 900 runs, corresponding to an overall feasibility rate of 99.89%. This result indicates that feasibility preservation is not limited to isolated successful trials, but is instead a stable and repeatable property of the algorithm across a wide range of parameter settings and independent runs.

Engineering interpretation and implications

The optimal designs obtained using SDOA consistently lie near the boundaries of several critical constraints, particularly those associated with gear tooth stress and shaft stress. This behavior is characteristic of minimum-weight mechanical design problems, where optimal solutions typically exploit allowable stress limits while maintaining structural integrity. The presence of small but strictly negative constraint margins confirms that the proposed algorithm is capable of navigating the narrow feasible region without violating safety requirements.

From an engineering perspective, the resulting design variables indicate a preference for minimal module size and tooth count, coupled with shaft diameters that balance bending stiffness and stress resistance. The face width converges toward its upper bound, reflecting its role in reducing contact stress without significantly increasing weight. These trends are consistent with established mechanical design principles, further validating the physical credibility of the obtained solutions.

The staged oxygen-guided search mechanism plays a crucial role in achieving this outcome. Early deep exploration enables the algorithm to sample widely across the design space, while the gradual transition to exploitation ensures that feasibility is not sacrificed in pursuit of lower objective values. The reset mechanism proves particularly effective in preventing stagnation when divers become trapped in infeasible or weakly feasible regions, a common failure mode of many swarm-based optimizers.

Robustness and convergence characteristics

The statistical results demonstrate low variance across independent runs, particularly in the medium- and large-population settings. This robustness indicates that SDOA is not excessively sensitive to moderate parameter variation, although the expanded campaign also shows that performance predictably improves when additional population diversity and iteration budget are available.

Convergence histories show smooth and largely monotonic improvement in the objective function, with feasibility typically achieved early in the successful runs. Once feasibility is established, subsequent iterations focus primarily on refinement rather than repeated feasibility recovery. This behavior is consistent with the intended role of the oxygen-regulated transition from exploration to exploitation.

The distribution of divers across depth stages over time confirms that the oxygen-decay mechanism successfully enforces a natural progression from exploration to exploitation. Importantly, reset-stage occupancy appears intermittently throughout the search rather than continuously, indicating that reinitialization is used as a targeted recovery mechanism when certain divers become trapped in non-improving or weakly feasible regions. Because the current implementation tracks stage occupancy rather than a separate per-run reset counter, the present study characterizes reset activity qualitatively rather than reporting an exact activation frequency; a dedicated event-level analysis will be a useful extension in future work.

Algorithmic comparison

To evaluate the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed Scuba Diver Optimization Algorithm (SDOA), a comparative study was conducted against a wide range of well-established metaheuristic and hybrid optimization algorithms reported in the literature. These include differential evolution–based methods, swarm-intelligence approaches, evolutionary algorithms, and recent nature-inspired optimizers. The comparison is performed on the standard speed reducer design benchmark, ensuring consistency in decision variables, constraint definitions, and objective formulation across methods; however, the manuscript also acknowledges that cross-benchmark validation remains necessary for a broader generalization claim.

It should nevertheless be noted that these literature-based comparisons are cross-study in nature and therefore do not provide the same level of inferential strength as matched experimental campaigns with identical stopping criteria, hardware environments, and run-level statistical outputs. The comparative table is thus intended to establish broad competitiveness on the SRD benchmark rather than to substitute for controlled head-to-head testing with the newest peer methods.

In contrast, algorithms such as APSO and SBOA exhibit significantly higher objective values and larger deviations from the best-known solution. This behavior suggests weaker constraint-handling capability, premature convergence, or limited adaptability when operating near constraint-active optima. Such deficiencies are particularly critical in the SRD problem, where the feasible region is narrow and optimal solutions lie close to multiple active constraints.

From a statistical perspective, the strength of SDOA is not limited to a single best-case outcome. The expanded 30 × 30 experimental campaign shows near-perfect feasibility, low variance in the best-performing configurations, and narrow best–mean gaps. These observations indicate stable convergence behavior and reduce the likelihood that the reported best result is merely an artifact of an exceptional single run.

Moreover, the 899/900 feasible-run outcome demonstrates a high level of feasibility reliability across the tested scenarios. This result suggests that feasibility preservation in SDOA is strongly supported by the adaptive search mechanism and is not solely a consequence of a single tuned parameter setting, although further evaluation on additional constrained benchmarks is still needed.

Overall, the ranking, objective deviation analysis, and statistical consistency collectively confirm the superior accuracy, robustness, and feasibility reliability of the proposed SDOA for the speed reducer design problem.

At the same time, three limitations should be kept explicit. First, the present manuscript does not yet include a component-wise ablation study isolating the individual contribution of Lévy exploration, crossover, local refinement, fine-tuning mutation, and reset. Second, the current validation remains benchmark-specific and is confined to the canonical speed reducer problem. Third, although formal nonparametric significance tests have now been added for the repeated internal SDOA configuration groups, matched run-level distributions are not available for many literature baselines; therefore, inferential significance testing against peer algorithms remains an outstanding issue rather than a fully resolved one.

Conclusion

In computational terms, SDOA retains the polynomial-time behaviour typical of population-based metaheuristics, with the main overhead arising from the coordinate-wise local-improvement stage rather than from the oxygen-regulated control mechanism itself.

This study presented the Scuba Diver Optimization Algorithm (SDOA) as an effective and robust solution approach for the speed reducer design (SRD) problem, a widely used benchmark in constrained mechanical optimization. The SRD problem is characterized by nonlinear objective and constraint functions, mixed discrete–continuous variables, and a narrow feasible region, making it particularly challenging for many optimization methods. The proposed algorithm was formulated to address these challenges through an adaptive control mechanism that balances global exploration, local exploitation, and feasibility preservation throughout the search process.

Extensive numerical experiments demonstrated that SDOA consistently achieves high-quality feasible solutions with low variance across repeated runs. In the expanded campaign of 30 scenarios and 30 runs per scenario, the algorithm achieved an overall feasibility rate of 99.89% and attained its best reported objective value of 2992.859644 in Scenario 30. Comparative analysis against a broad range of established metaheuristic and hybrid algorithms indicates that SDOA remains highly competitive on the SRD benchmark and achieves the strongest reported solution within the comparison set included here.

Beyond solution quality, the statistical behavior of the algorithm provides important insight into its reliability. The narrow best–mean gaps and modest standard deviations observed in the strongest configurations indicate stable convergence dynamics and a low likelihood of premature stagnation. These characteristics suggest that feasibility preservation and robustness are intrinsic properties of the proposed method within the tested SRD setting.

Future research will focus on extending SDOA to more complex engineering design scenarios. In particular, the algorithm will be adapted to multi-objective gearbox and transmission design problems involving conflicting objectives such as weight minimization, stiffness maximization, and vibration reduction. In addition, coupling SDOA with surrogate-based finite element models will be investigated to enable efficient optimization of computationally expensive designs. Further studies will also explore hybridization with local gradient-based refinement techniques and the application of SDOA to other constrained mechanical and structural optimization problems. Future work will also examine more explicit diversity-maintenance strategies and diversity metrics to further clarify their role in the robustness of the proposed algorithm. Future work should also include a systematic sensitivity analysis of the penalty coefficient to quantify its influence on feasibility attainment and convergence behaviour.

In particular, future validation should include three complementary directions: additional constrained benchmark suites to test generality, formal ablation analysis to quantify the contribution of each search component, and inferential statistical tests under matched experimental settings to support stronger claims of comparative significance.

Overall, the results of this study indicate that SDOA constitutes a promising optimization framework for constrained engineering design. The manuscript nevertheless recognizes several boundaries of the present evidence: the absence of multi-benchmark validation, the lack of a dedicated ablation study, and the fact that formal significance testing could be carried out only for the repeated internal SDOA configuration groups rather than for matched peer-algorithm distributions. These limitations do not negate the strong SRD results reported here, but they do delimit the scope of the current conclusions and define the most important priorities for subsequent work.
