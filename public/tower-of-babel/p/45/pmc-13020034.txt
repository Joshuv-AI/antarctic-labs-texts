Amphiregulin attenuates sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction by maintaining the localization of connexin43
Authors: Man Chen, Shinnosuke Takamori, Koichi Isayama, Yuqing Wang, Shuntaro Matsunaga, Sayoko Narahara, Masanori Honsho, Masaharu Murata, Shintaro Kinugawa, Kohtaro Abe, Tomohiko Akahoshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction (SIMD) is a prevalent complication among septic patients, significantly worsening patient prognosis and elevating the mortality rate. Connexin 43 (Cx43), a pivotal cardiac gap junction protein, maintains cardiac function, and its disarrangement is closely linked to cardiac diseases. However, the role of Cx43 localization changes in SIMD remains unclear. Amphiregulin (AREG) was recently reported to promote the recovery of Cx43 disarrangements. This research aimed to explore the role of Cx43 in SIMD and the preventive potential of AREG.MethodsA mouse model of SIMD was induced using lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and treated with AREG. Cardiac function and electrical conduction were assessed using echocardiography and an electrocardiogram. Inflammatory responses, Cx43 regulation, and related signaling pathways were further investigated in serum and cardiac tissues. Relevant signal pathway analysis was investigated in cultured cardiomyocytes.ResultsLPS administration significantly reduced cardiac ejection fraction and left ventricular fractional shortening, which were accompanied by disorganization, fragmentation, and lateralization of Cx43 at 6 h. These pathological alterations were associated with increased phosphorylation of pS368-Cx43, mediated by p38 activation. AREG pretreatment improved cardiac function and QRS interval, and preserved Cx43 localization at intercellular discs, along with pS368-Cx43 phosphorylation with reduction of p38 inhibition. Myocardial cell studies confirmed that AREG inhibited p38 phosphorylation, independent of AKT, in LPS-induced cardiac dysfunction.ConclusionThis study highlights the role of Cx43 phosphorylation in SIMD and demonstrates the preventive potential of AREG in SIMD, which is associated with a reduction in p38 activation and a decrease in the phosphorylation level of pS368-Cx43. Introduction

Sepsis, a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in intensive care units [1]. Sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction (SIMD), a common complication, is associated with increased mortality [2] and is characterized by intrinsic systolic and diastolic dysfunctions of both the left and right ventricles [3]. However, its underlying mechanisms are not fully understood, and available treatment options remain limited.

Connexin 43 (Cx43), the primary gap junction protein in ventricular myocardium, is pivotal in maintaining normal cardiac function. In healthy myocardium, Cx43 hemichannels are typically closed; however, under myocardial ischemic or inflammatory conditions, these channels open, contributing to increased vascular permeability [4]. Additionally, myocarditis has been shown to increase Cx43 phosphorylation at serine-368 (S368) [5]. Alterations in Cx43 expression, localization, or phosphorylation are closely associated with various heart diseases [6]. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated Cx43 lateralization in the cardiac sections from autopsied patients with sepsis [7].

Amphiregulin (AREG), a recently identified cardioprotective mediator secreted by cardiac-resident Ly6Clo macrophages, has been shown to promote electrical conduction in the heart by regulating Cx43 [8]. A recent study demonstrated that AREG plays a critical role in promoting survival during cardiac stress by preserving cardiac electrical conduction and regulating cardiac gap junction proteins, including Cx43 [9]. However, the therapeutic effect of AREG in SIMD remains unclear.

This study aimed to investigate the role of Cx43 in SIMD and evaluate whether AREG could improve cardiac function in a murine model of SIMD. These findings may provide preclinical insights for potential clinical application.

Materials and methods

Animals and treatment

Male C57BL/6N mice aged 8–12 weeks and weighing 20–28 g were obtained from Charles River Laboratories Japan, Inc. (Yokohama, Japan). The mice were housed in chambers under controlled conditions (22 ± 2 °C and 12-h light/dark cycle) with unrestricted access to food and water. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines approved by the Ethics Committee of Kyushu University (Project No. A23-493-0) and adhered to institutional protocols. Sample size was determined to meet minimum requirements for statistical significance, following the 3R principle.

Sepsis-induced cardiac dysfunction model

The sepsis-induced cardiac dysfunction model was established by intraperitoneal injection of lipopolysaccharide (LPS; L2880, Sigma) at a dose of 6 mg/kg. Given the high mortality associated with high-dose LPS, this study focused on inducing cardiac failure using a low dose of LPS administered over a short duration [10]. The control group received an equivalent volume of saline intraperitoneally. Initially, we divided the mice into three groups: the control group (6 h), the LPS group (6 h), and the LPS group (24 h).

To specifically target Cx43, 5 μg per mouse of AREG (#989-AR, R&D Systems) was injected intraperitoneally 30 min before LPS administration, dissolved in 100 μL of phosphate-buffered saline. The AREG dosage was determined based on prior research [9]. In further experiments, the mice were divided into three groups: control, LPS, and AREG + LPS.

After LPS injection, cardiac function and electrical conduction were evaluated using echocardiography and electrocardiogram. Mice were euthanized at 6 h and 24 h after LPS administration for the collection of heart tissue for histological analysis, western blotting, and immunostaining. Heart tissue and serum samples were collected and stored at −80 °C for further biochemical analysis.

Echocardiographic measurements

Cardiac function was assessed 6 h and 24 h after LPS injection. Mice were lightly anesthetized with 1%–2% isoflurane, maintaining heart rates between 400 and 450 beats per minute. Transthoracic echocardiography was performed using the Vevo 2100 ultrasonography system (Visual Sonics, Toronto, Canada). To determine the left ventricular (LV) volume, the maximum short-axis dimensions of the LV at end-diastole (LVDd) and end-systole (LVDs) were measured in the parasternal long-axis view using M-mode echocardiography, assuming the LV to be a spheroid. The left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was calculated using the following formula: LVEF = [(LVEDV − LVESV)/LVEDV] × 100%, where LVEDV and LVESV represent the end-diastolic and end-systolic LV volumes, respectively. LV volume was typically calculated using the Teichholz formula: V = [7.0/(2.4 + LV diameter)] × LV diameter3. The left ventricular systolic fraction (LVFS) was expressed as a percentage and calculated using the following formula: LVFS% = [(LVDd − LVDs)/LVDd] × 100%.

Electrocardiogram

Mice were lightly anesthetized with 1%–2% isoflurane. Electrocardiogram monitoring was performed concurrently with cardiac echocardiography using the Vevo 2100 ultrasonography system (Visual Sonics, Toronto, Canada). Three 4-s ECG video clips were recorded for each mouse. Five cardiac cycles were obtained from each screenshot to record QRS interval variations. ImageJ software version 1.53k (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used to measure QRS interval lengths, followed by statistical analysis.

Biochemical and serum analysis

Blood samples were collected from the inferior vena cava, and serum was separated by centrifugation for subsequent analysis. Serum creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels were measured using an automatic biochemical analyzer (FujiFilm Dri-Chem NX500V system, FujiFilm, Tokyo, Japan). Serum levels of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) were measured using mouse enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Histological analysis

The LV was chosen due to its susceptibility to early cardiac dysfunction, as it is the first site affected. Heart tissues were fixed in 10% formalin, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 6 µm. The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to assess LPS-induced cardiac injury and AREG-mediated cardiac repair. Stained sections were visualized using an Axio Scan Z1 (Carl Zeiss AG, Ltd., Thornwood, NY, USA) and Zen (Carl Zeiss AG, Ltd.).

Immunohistochemical studies

Based on the distribution of Cx43 in the heart [11], immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of the left ventricle was performed to assess cardiomyocyte damage and the phosphorylation of Cx43 (1:100; ab230537, Abcam). A minimum of five to eight randomly selected fields were examined at 50× to 60× magnification, with the analysis conducted in a blinded manner [7]. Cx43 quantification at gap junctions was performed by measuring the area of Cx43 at gap junctions and total Cx43 using ImageJ 1.53a software (NIH, USA).

Cell culture

H9c2(2-1) (ATCC, CRL-1446) cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), antibiotic-antimycotic (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. The medium was changed every 2–3 days, and the cells were passaged regularly.

To assess the relationship between p38 and the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, H9C2 was divided into three groups: control, LPS, and AREG + LPS. To determine the AREG concentration, H9C2 was treated with AREG (0.1 ng/ml, 10 ng/ml) for 2 h separately. H9C2 cells were treated with AREG (10 ng/ml) for 2 h, followed by LPS (1 μg/ml). Since AREG takes effect within 30 min, we experimented to verify the inhibitory effect of AREG on p38 by pretreating with AREG (10, 100 ng/ml) for 30 min, followed by LPS (1 μg/ml) for 1 h.

Western blot analysis

Heart tissue and the cultured H9C2 cells were homogenized in ice-cold Pierce RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (complete ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid-free; Sigma-Aldrich), NuPAGE™ sample reducing agent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and PhosSTOP easy pack (Roche). Protein concentration was determined using a protein quantification kit (Dojindo Laboratories). Twenty-eight micrograms of protein were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Wako) and transferred to a membrane using the Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% EveryBlot Blocking Buffer (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) and incubated at 4 °C overnight, immunoblotted with primary antibodies that target the following proteins: Cx43 (1:8000; ab230537, Abcam), p-Cx43 (Ser368) (1:1000; #3511), α-tubulin (1:2000; #2144), p-p38 (1:1000; #4511), p38 (1:1000; #8690S), p-AKT (Ser473) (1:1000; #4060), and Akt (1:1000; #4691) obtained from Cell Signaling Technology. Signal detection was performed using horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG, rabbit anti-mouse (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA), and rabbit anti-goat (Abcam). Chemiluminescence signals were captured using Clarity Western enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Bio-Rad) and digitized using ImageJ software version 1.53k (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test and two-sample Student’s t-test, using GraphPad Prism 10.2.3 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

LPS stimulation for 6 h induces septic cardiac dysfunction in mice

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a significant reduction in both LVEF and LVFS in the LPS group, by 36.4% and 17%, respectively (Fig. 1A). After 24 h, cardiac function in the mice showed signs of recovery, with LVEF and LVFS increasing to 60.6% and 32.2%, respectively. Histological analysis of heart sections using H&E staining demonstrated abnormal myocardial tissue structure and inflammatory cell infiltration in the SIMD group, in contrast to the control group. However, the 24-h group showed a gentle recovery (Fig. 1B). Moreover, the serum CK-MB and LDH levels were elevated in mice with SIMD at 6 h after LPS injection (Fig. 1C, D). LPS-induced Cx43 disarrangement and phosphorylation in SIMD mice at 6 h via the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinases signaling pathway

Immunohistochemical studies analysis of LV heart sections showed that 6 h of LPS stimulation led to a disordered arrangement of Cx43, with fragmentation and lateralization observed. However, after 24 h, Cx43 gradually returned to the myocardial intercalated disc. As shown in the figure, the proportion of Cx43 in myocardial intercalated discs was 86.1% in the control group, dropped to 7.1% at the peak of cardiac injury, and recovered to 27.2% after 24 h (Fig. 2A). Initially, we assessed the protein expression levels of Cx43 over 6 h (Fig. 2B). Subsequently, an increase in phosphorylated pS368-Cx43 was observed following LPS stimulation (Fig. 2B). Even though the expression of Cx43 did not change within 6 h, it decreased after 24 h (S3A). Moreover, previous studies have shown that the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK) pathway plays a role in the regulation of pS368-Cx43 [5]. Effect of AREG treatment on cardiac function following SIMD

To evaluate the effectiveness of AREG, the mice were administered AREG (5 μg/mouse) 30 min before LPS injection. Compared with the LPS group, SIMD mice treated with AREG showed significant improvements in LVEF and LVFS, by 58.4% and 30%, respectively (Fig. 3A). Additionally, the QRS interval was prolonged in the LPS group compared to the control group, while AREG treatment led to a return of the QRS interval (Fig. 3B). In addition, H&E staining of heart tissues showed inflammatory changes in the LPS group, whereas the AREG + LPS group exhibited a tissue structure similar to that of the control group, with a denser structure (Fig. 3C). Following 6-h LPS stimulation, serum levels of inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 increased in mice. AREG increased AKT phosphorylation and decreased p38 MAPK phosphorylation in the hearts of SIMD mice

AREG has been shown to activate extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) signaling and to recover Cx43 gap junction dislocation [9]. Therefore, we examined ERK1/2 phosphorylation. As shown in Fig. 5, ERK1/2 phosphorylation increased in the heart tissue in SIMD mice at 6 h after LPS compared with control mice, and an additive increase of ERK1/2 phosphorylation was not recognized. It suggests that ERK1/2 phosphorylation is not involved in maintaining Cx43 gap junctions. Since the AKT pathway has also been reported to contribute to the recovery of the injured heart [12], we examined AKT phosphorylation as an alternative pathway relating to Cx43 phosphorylation. Results showed that AKT phosphorylation levels decreased in the LPS group at 6 h compared to the control group, but the AREG + LPS group exhibited upregulation of AKT phosphorylation. AREG inhibited p38 phosphorylation without mediating AKT signaling pathways

To investigate the relationship between AKT and the p38 signaling pathway, we conducted a series of cellular experiments utilizing H9C2 cells (S1). Following previous research [13], our findings indicate that a concentration of 1 µg/ml LPS effectively promotes the phosphorylation of p38 and is correlated with the phosphorylation of pS368-Cx43 (Fig. 6A). Hence, given the existing limited availability of articles that substantiate the concentration and duration of AKT activation in H9C2 cells induced by AREG, we conducted a stimulation of H9C2 for 2 h utilizing concentrations of 0.1 and 10 ng/ml (S2). Given that both were identified as capable of phosphorylating AKT, we chose to use 10 ng/ml AREG. In the case where only LPS was administered, there was a notable activation of p38 and inhibition of AKT. Conversely, AREG promoted the activation of AKT while concurrently inhibiting p38 (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, it was reported that the peak effectiveness of AREG was observed at 30 min and maintained its efficacy for 24 h [9]. We examined the impact of AREG stimulation at 30 min on the phosphorylation of p38. Discussion

According to previous studies, sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction is typically reversible within 7–10 days in clinical settings [14]. In various mouse models, the severity of cardiac injury depends on the concentration of LPS. In a non-lethal endotoxemia mouse model, cardiac dysfunction was primarily observed 6 h after LPS stimulation and gradually improved over the subsequent 24 h [15, 16]. Actually, the 6–12-h timeframe represents the period of most severe myocardial injury. In line with previous studies, this study revealed that mice subjected to LPS exhibited a reduction in cardiac function at 6 h after LPS and recovered after 24 h. However, the serum CK-MB and LDH levels indicated that the damage to the myocardial tissue and throughout the body seemed to recover more slowly after 24 h. Although the expression of Cx43 protein by western blot did not change at 6 h, it seems to be reduced after 24 h. Immunohistochemical staining revealed both lateralization and fragmentation of Cx43 in the heart of the SIMD mouse at 6 h, but progressively re-established at the myocardial intercalated disc after 24 h of LPS treatment, accompanied by improved cardiac function. This phenomenon corresponds to the restoration of cardiac function observed in patients with SIMD. Previous studies have shown that the lateralization and fragmentation of Cx43 are related to abnormal cardiac conduction [9]. Thus, Cx43 translocation is thought to be at least partly responsible for SIMD. These findings of Cx43 have not been reported in SIMD mice. As consistent with our results, lateralization of Cx43 to the cardiomyocyte membrane has been reported in autopsied cases with sepsis [7]. In contrast to previous studies [17, 18], our research found that the protein expression of Cx43 was not significantly reduced in response to LPS. The reason for the difference is due to the amount of LPS. The dosage of LPS used in our study was 6 mg/kg. That is less than previous studies examining Cx43 protein expression. In contrast, phosphorylation at serine-368 (S368) was increased in the SIMD mouse. Phosphorylation at serine-368 (S368) is known to alter gap junction conductance in SIMD mice [6]. Therefore, we speculated that the phosphorylation of Cx43 is a key factor in inducing SIMD. Some studies have shown that Cx43 phosphorylated at S368 leads to gap junction disarrangement, as evidenced by the expression of Cx43 protein in ischemia/hypoxia [19, 20]. The phosphorylation of Cx43 at S368 is frequently associated with inflammation and cardiac dysfunction in ischemic heart disease and myocarditis [6, 19, 21, 22]. However, the association between the translocation of gap junction protein Cx43 and its phosphorylation has not been reported. Therefore, this result is thought to provide a new therapeutic target in SIMD.

As for treatment, recent research has also highlighted the role of AREG, a macrophage-derived factor, in positively influencing cardiac electrical conduction mediated by Cx43 [9]. In our study, AREG maintained Cx43 localization in the intercalated disc of the myocardium alongside significantly improved LVEF and LVFS in SIMD mice. Meanwhile, consistent with previous studies [23], LPS stimulation caused a prolongation of the QRS interval in mice, while AREG effectively improved this condition. At 6 h, AREG demonstrated better therapeutic effects than automatic recovery after 24 h. We also aim to investigate whether AREG influences the phosphorylation of Cx43 in SIMD mice. Previous studies have indicated that AREG modulates Cx43 activity by triggering the ERK1/2 signaling pathway in an arrhythmia mouse model [9]. However, in the present study, the levels of ERK1/2 phosphorylation were similar between the LPS group and the AREG + LPS group. Because additive induction of ERK1/2 was not observed, it seems that there is no relationship between the ERK1/2 signal pathway and phosphorylation of pS368-Cx43. Since the phosphorylation of pS368-Cx43 was reported to be induced by the IL-1β/p38 MAPK signaling pathway in myocarditis [5], when treated with AREG, serum IL-1β levels decreased in mice. In contrast, serum TNF-α and IL-6 levels remained unchanged. We considered that AREG inhibits the phosphorylation of pS368-Cx43 and blocks the conversion of pro-IL-1β in heart tissue to serum IL-1β levels, while maintaining Cx43 at the intercalated disc of the myocardium and preserving cardiac function and electrical conduction. Several studies have shown that inhibition of the p38 signaling pathway and activation of the Akt signaling pathway are involved in the recovery process of SIMD [12, 24, 25]. Moreover, one report indicates that AREG improved cardiac dysfunction by activating the Akt signaling pathway in a myocardial infarction mouse model [26]. An inverse expression between p-AKT and p-p38 was found in SIMD mice. The relationship between them was validated in H9C2 cardiomyocytes. To explore the relationship between p-AKT and p-p38, we pretreated the cells with AREG and subsequently administered LPS. Since AREG demonstrated peak efficacy at the 30-min mark and maintained its effects for 24 h [9], we performed 30-min and 2-h AREG stimulation to determine whether activation of the AKT signaling pathway was related to the p38 MAPK signaling pathway. In contrast to the results of 2-h AREG stimulation, 30-min AREG pretreatment did not activate the AKT signaling pathway; however, the p38 signaling pathway remained inhibited, which occurred earlier than AKT activation. The results of this study suggest that AREG exerts a protective effect by inhibiting the p38 signaling pathway, which is independent of the AKT signaling pathway. This observation may indicate an essential mechanism in the recovery of SIMD.

We considered AREG as a preventive agent for LPS-induced SIMD, associated with reduced p38 activation, decreased pS368-Cx43 phosphorylation, and improved lateralization of Cx43.

This study still has several limitations. Firstly, all experiments were performed in a murine model and thus may not fully recapitulate the human pathophysiology of SIMD. Secondly, we primarily focused on the preventive administration of AREG; future studies are warranted to evaluate its preventive potential after disease onset. Thirdly, the effects of longer-term AREG stimulation on Cx43 and cardiac function, as well as on SIMD, should be investigated in future studies. Lastly, while our findings are consistent with the involvement of the pS368-Cx43/p38 axis, they are largely correlative; additional mechanistic studies would be valuable to further clarify causality.

Conclusion

Cx43 is disorganized in LPS-induced SIMD mice. Furthermore, AREG has preventive potential for SIMD by restoring the proper localization of the gap junction protein Cx43, which is associated with reduced p38 activation and decreased pS368-Cx43 phosphorylation.
