Perineural fresh frozen plasma with local anaesthetics in coagulopathic patients undergoing peripheral nerve blocks: A clinical case series
Authors: Sandeep Diwan, Archana Areti, Nitin Gawai, Parag Sancheti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Peripheral nerve blocks (PNBs) in coagulopathic patients are typically avoided due to the risks of bleeding and potential nerve compression. This case series explores the use of perineural fresh frozen plasma (FFP) to enhance local haemostasis in such settings. Five patients with an international normalised ratio of > 1.8, requiring urgent surgery, received PNBs with perineural FFP after haematology consultation. Seven ultrasound-guided blocks were performed, with FFP injected perineurally and along the needle tract, followed by systemic FFP 20 minutes later. All procedures were completed under PNB without conversion or need for additional analgesia. One patient developed a localised haematoma post-supraclavicular block, resolving after perineural FFP. No neurological deficits, major bleeding, or infections were observed. These preliminary results suggest that perineural FFP may reduce bleeding complications in high-risk patients, though further research is needed. Until validated, its use should remain investigational and be undertaken with multidisciplinary oversight and strict adherence to aseptic technique and ultrasound guidance.

INTRODUCTION

Urgent life-or limb-saving surgeries in patients with significant comorbidities are often complicated by coagulopathy, posing a major anaesthetic challenge. While peripheral nerve blocks (PNBs) are generally safer than neuraxial techniques or general anaesthesia, they are often avoided in coagulopathic patients due to the risk of haematoma and nerve compression, especially with deeper blocks. Ultrasound guidance has improved PNB safety by enhancing needle accuracy and vascular avoidance. However, challenging anatomy or deep blocks such as the lumbar plexus may still require multiple passes, increasing the risk of bleeding in coagulopathic patients, even from minor vascular trauma.[1]

To address this, we explored the use of perineural fresh frozen plasma (FFP) infiltration, inspired by its haemostatic use in interventional radiology and animal models. We hypothesise that perineural FFP promotes local clot formation and tamponade, while systemic FFP supports overall coagulation. This strategy may offer a dual benefit, systemic correction of coagulopathy via intravenous FFP, and localised haemostatic support via perineural FFP, to reduce the bleeding risk during deep nerve block procedures. This case series presents our initial experience with this technique in high-risk coagulopathic patients undergoing urgent surgery with ultrasound-guided PNB, aiming to enhance safety and avoid general anaesthesia.

CASE SERIES

Over 1 year, five American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status III–IV patients with an international normalised ratio of >1.8 requiring urgent limb- or life-saving surgery (e.g., septic limb debridement or open fracture fixation) were managed with PNBs as the primary anaesthetic technique. All patients underwent routine laboratory tests, electrocardiograms, echocardiography, and specialist consultations, as appropriate. Coagulation profiles were reviewed in detail and discussed with a haematologist; overt disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) was excluded. All patients received ultrasound-guided PNBs in the operating room under aseptic conditions. The thawed FFP was drawn into a 10-mL sterile syringe and set aside. The operating room temperature ranged from 18°C to 20°C. A 13–5-MHz linear array transducer was used with the Sonosite M-Turbo® ultrasound system (Fujifilm Sonosite Inc., Bothell, Washington, USA). A linear probe was used for superficial blocks, and a 2–4-MHz curvilinear probe for deeper targets. Blocks were performed using 50 mm or 100 mm, 20-G insulated needles (Pajunk GmbH Medizintechnologie, Geisingen, Baden-Württemberg, Germany). After local anaesthetic injection, FFP was administered under ultrasound guidance via the same needle—first at the neural target and then along the needle track during withdrawal. Post-block, all injection sites were scanned with ultrasound to rule out haematoma. Major vascular structures (e.g., inferior gluteal, lateral circumflex femoral, transverse cervical, and axillary branches) were identified and avoided. Perineural FFP appeared as hyperechoic dispersion in connective tissue. After 20 minutes, the remaining FFP was infused over 15 minutes. Block success was confirmed by the absence of pain, loss of pinprick sensation, and motor weakness in the target area. Surgery proceeded after achieving complete sensory and motor blockade. Any need for analgesic supplementation or conversion to general anaesthesia was noted. Patients were monitored for bleeding, infection, or neurological deficits until discharge. At 1-month follow-up, they were evaluated for paraesthesia, weakness, neuropathic pain, and local site complications such as redness, swelling, or tenderness.

RESULTS

The outcomes of FFP injection with PNB used for various surgeries in five patients, highlighting their comorbidities and procedural specifics, are summarised in Table 1. Only one patient who received a supraclavicular block developed neck swelling post-block. USG revealed a haematoma that started at a size of 3 cm × 3 cm, progressing to roughly 5 cm × 5 cm in 5 minutes. All surgeries were completed without the need for additional analgesia and sedation. Postoperatively, none of the ultrasound scans revealed hypoechoic shadows suggestive of haematoma. No infections related to the PNBs were observed during the study period. None of the patients exhibited neurological or infectious adverse events at discharge or during their first outpatient visit at 1 month.

DISCUSSION

Guidelines recommend deferring elective surgery in coagulopathic patients; however, urgent procedures may proceed with haematology guidance and risk-benefit assessment.[2] Few studies have shown a benefit from needle track FFP injections, with volumes adjusted to needle depth and path—administering 10–20 mL when the path is greater than 10 cm and 5 mL for paths less than 5 cm.[345] In our series, we used a total of 5–9 mL (5–7 mL for deep blocks and 2–3 mL for superficial blocks), based on needle depth and block location, which we determined as the minimum amount to mitigate complications without compromising block quality. Despite a few reports that bleeding and neurological complications after PNBs may be delayed, especially in coagulopathic patients, no such events were observed in our cases.[678]

The dynamic nature of coagulopathy means that systemically administered FFP would have transient and reduced efficacy, may cause volume overload, and may not reach the intended site to mitigate bleeding complications, which is where local infiltration may prove beneficial over systemic administration. We hypothesise that the liquid FFP seeps through fascial planes, filling spaces and providing haemostasis, with seroma formation potentially sealing bleeding points, thereby mitigating bleeding complications. In our series, only one case developed a haematoma during a supraclavicular block, which regressed after perineural FFP injection, pressure application, and concomitant systemic platelet and FFP transfusion.

Perineural injection of FFP is not standard of care or evidence-based; however, it has been proposed here in specific high-risk situations, such as coagulopathic patients receiving deep nerve blocks, as a potential means to mitigate bleeding complications and enable life-saving surgery. The potential mechanisms by which perineural FFP may confer a haemostatic advantage are multifactorial. Firstly, FFP contains all major coagulation factors, including fibrinogen, prothrombin, and factors V, VII, VIII, IX, X, XI, and XIII, which, when injected near nerves, may facilitate local thrombin generation and fibrin clot formation at the site of needle-induced vascular injury, thereby limiting bleeding in a manner analogous to surgically applied fibrin sealants.[4] Secondly, the injected volume of FFP may exert a tamponade effect, mechanically compressing damaged vessels and limiting hematoma propagation, while concurrent clot formation may seal the needle tract.[9] Thirdly, in patients with underlying coagulopathies such as liver dysfunction or DIC, or in those who cannot tolerate systemic plasma administration, perineural FFP may offer targeted correction of coagulation abnormalities without the systemic risks of volume overload or transfusion reactions. Fourthly, FFP contains endothelial-stabilising proteins such as antithrombin and protein C, which may enhance vascular integrity and exert anti-inflammatory effects, thereby reducing the propensity for microvascular bleeding.[10] Lastly, local anaesthetics such as bupivacaine possess vasodilatory properties that may exacerbate bleeding at the site of injection; FFP may mitigate this by promoting local haemostasis and countering vasodilatory-induced vascular leak.

To reduce costs and avoid wastage, 10 mL of FFP was aseptically aspirated from the same unit intended for IV infusion and reserved for perineural injection. Based on prior studies and our experience, the use of local FFP in PNBs appears justified.[4] Block quality remained unaffected, but the interaction between local anaesthetics and FFP is unclear. No patients reported local or radiating pain, although it is premature to confirm safety. Notably, similar blood products, such as platelet-rich plasma, have been used for diabetic neuropathic pain.[11]

Limitations include the small sample size and heterogeneous patient profiles, which limit comparability. While our findings suggest that perineural FFP may reduce bleeding and improve outcomes, randomised trials are needed to confirm its safety and efficacy, as well as to compare local versus systemic administration.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this case series suggests that perineural fresh frozen plasma injection, in addition to systemic administration, may be a feasible approach to reduce bleeding complications in coagulopathic patients undergoing urgent surgeries with peripheral nerve blocks. However, further prospective, controlled studies are needed to establish the efficacy and safety of this technique.
