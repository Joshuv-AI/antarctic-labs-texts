Enhanced antibiofilm activity of ciprofloxacin-loaded quatsomal gel against Multidrug-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA)
Authors: Jianguo Shi, Zanib Farid, Sanaa Anjum, Syed Tatheer Alam Naqvi, Muhammad Arfat Yameen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMRSA biofilms remain a major challenge in clinical settings because they reduce antibiotics susceptibility and contribute to persistent infections. This current study was designed to evaluate quatsomal gel as a proof-of-concept topical ciprofloxacin delivery platform aimed at enhancing localized antimicrobial and antibiofilm activity against MRSA. The ciprofloxacin loaded quatsomes were prepared using a probe-sonication method with CTAB and cholesterol and were then incorporated in a hydroxypropyl methylcellulose-based gel.ResultsThe resulting formulation was characterized by particle size, zeta potential, TEM, entrapment efficiency, and FTIR spectroscopy. A HPMC-based gel was developed for topical delivery and its antimicrobial and antibiofilm activities were assessed. An in vivo wound excision model in mice was used to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of this formulation. The loaded formulation exhibited a mean particle size of 153 ± 1.3 nm, a zeta potential of + 34.6 ± 1.4 mV, and 79 ± 0.4% ciprofloxacin entrapment efficiency. FTIR confirmed drug incorporation, while the pH, spreadability, and viscosity were remained within appropriate ranges for skin application. The optimized formulation significantly reduced MRSA growth, with a minimum inhibitory concentration of 64 µg/mL and 91.32 ± 0.14% biofilm inhibition. In vivo studies showed that the ciprofloxacin-loaded quatsomes treated group had 98% wound contraction and ~ -4 log10 CFU reduction, compared with 82% wound contraction and ~ -1.8 log10 CFU reduction in ciprofloxacin treated group.ConclusionCiprofloxacin-loaded quatsomes showed significant antimicrobial and antibiofilm activity against MRSA in both in vitro and in vivo analysis, representing a promising proof-of-concept approach for localized ciprofloxacin delivery in biofilm-associated skin infections. Further studies should focus on pharmacokinetic evaluation, safety profiling and clinical evaluation to facilitate translation into an effective nanomedicine.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05326-8.Keywords: MRSA, Antibiofilm Activity, Multidrug Resistant Bacteria, Ciprofloxacin, Drug-Loaded Quatsomes

Background

Staphylococcus aureus is a significant human pathogen and can cause persistent infections such as deep-seated abscesses, osteomyelitis, and endocarditis because of its resistance to antibiotics and its ability to form biofilms on living tissues and medical devices [1]. Among antibiotic-resistant S. aureus strains, methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) is one of the most clinically important. MRSA can cause moderate to severe infections and can also asymptomatically colonize host tissues. Previous studies on biofilm formation have reported that a high proportion of MRSA isolates are multidrug resistant, partly because of their ability to form biofilms. Recently, MRSA has become one of the leading causes of nosocomial infections and a major contributor to morbidity and mortality in hospitalized patients. Because of antibiotic resistance and its clinical impact, MRSA is recognized as a high-priority drug-resistant pathogen. Formation of biofilms on biotic and abiotic surfaces is the key factor contributing to reduce antibiotic susceptibility in MRSA infections [2].

Biofilms are structured microbial communities attached to various surfaces. They consist of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) containing proteins, polysaccharides, and extracellular DNA. The EPS matrix provides structural stability to biofilms and protects bacterial cells from antimicrobial agents and host immune responses [3]. Biofilms contribute to persistent infections and are associated with high hospitalization rates, increased health care expenses, and increased morbidity and mortality. Respiratory tract infections, endocarditis, chronic otitis media, chronic wounds, diabetic foot ulcers, urinary tract infections, and periodontitis are all associated with biofilm-related diseases. Quorum sensing is the process that regulates biofilm formation and the expression of other virulence factors [4].

Biofilm formation is a major contributor to severe and chronic infections and hospital outbreaks. It is a widespread phenomenon among microorganisms and an important virulence factor responsible for infections involving indwelling devices and host tissues. Treatment failure in biofilm associated infections is largely due to reduced susceptibility to antimicrobial agents and resistance to the host immune response [5]. Antibiotic resistance in biofilms arises for several reasons, including reduced or incomplete penetration of antibiotics into the biofilm matrix, an altered microenvironment in the matrix, and the presence of persistent cells in the biofilm [6].

Ciprofloxacin (CIP), a fluoroquinolones antibiotic, has been widely used for bacterial infections. It is a broad-spectrum antimicrobial agent that acts by inhibiting bacterial DNA replication, thereby compromising bacterial growth. However, MRSA strains have developed resistance to CIP [7]. In the present study, CIP was selected as a model antimicrobial agent to investigate the potential of quatsomal nano vesicular systems for enhanced localized drug delivery and antibiofilm activity against multidrug-resistant bacterial biofilms. Due to the widespread emergence of fluoroquinolone resistance, well-established physicochemical characteristics of ciprofloxacin, its documented limitations in biofilm penetration, and its extensively characterized antibacterial profile make it a suitable candidate for proof-of-concept evaluation of advanced nanocarrier-based delivery approaches. QASs are cationic vesicles composed of phospholipids or non-ionic surfactants, and their positively charged surfaces may facilitate penetration into the negatively charged biofilm matrix [8], thereby potentially improving localized drug retention and antibacterial efficacy within biofilm-associated infections.

Additionally, QASs can protect drugs from early degradation, promote intracellular penetration, and provide a potential platform for targeted drug delivery [9, 10]. Their inherent antimicrobial properties further support their use as carriers for antibiotic delivery. The antimicrobial activity of the quaternary ammonium compounds is attributed to their positive surface charge, making QASs a promising alternative to traditional liposomes [11]. A previous study also demonstrated that CIP-loaded cationic liposomes reduced the expression of NorB and MepA efflux pump proteins in MRSA and showed low association with these efflux proteins, thereby enhancing intracellular drug accumulation [12].

The aim of the present study was to develop a CIP-loaded QASs gel and evaluate the feasibility of quatsomal gel as a targeted CIP delivery platform intended to improve the localized delivery performance of encapsulated CIP against resistant bacterial biofilms. The formulation was also assessed for its antimicrobial effect in a mice wound excision model of MRSA infection.

Materials and methods

Materials

Analytical-grade CIP (MP Biomedicals, USA) was used in the study as the test antibiotic. Pharmaceutical-grade HPMC E15 (Merck, Germany) was used to prepare the gel. CTAB and cholesterol (Sigma-Aldrich, UK) were used to prepare the QASs. Milli-Q water (Merck, Germany) was used as the solvent during formulation preparation. Mueller Hinton Agar and broth (Oxoid, UK) were the growth media used in antimicrobial studies.

Preparation of blank and CIP-loaded QASs dispersion

With slight modifications, QASs were prepared by the probe sonication method. Probe sonication was performed using a QSonica probe sonicator (Q700, QSonica LLC, Newton, CT, USA) equipped with a 6 mm diameter titanium microtip, operating at a frequency of 20 kHz and 40% amplitude under a 30/10 s on/off pulse cycle. QASs were fabricated using different ratios of CTAB and Chol in glass vials. Then 20 mL of Milli-Q water was added, and the mixture was sonicated for 01 min in an ice bath. After that, the dispersion was agitated overnight at a speed of 400 rpm on a magnetic stirring plate at room temperature [13]. The final quatsomal formulation was stored in the refrigerator at 4 °C until characterization.

QASs formulations were optimized by varying the concentration of CTAB and Chol. Same procedure was used to prepare CIP-loaded QASs dispersion, with different quantities of CIP added to the optimized blank formulation.

Preparation of CIP-loaded QASs gel

The gelling agent HPMC E15 was used to prepare blank QASs gel and CIP-loaded QASs gel. To formulate the 3% gel, HPMC was added to half of the total water required. The remaining amount of water was then added and mixed after complete swelling. To remove air bubbles, the gel was kept at room temperature overnight. Triethanolamine was used to lower the pH of the formulation to 5.0 ± 0.2. Using a magnetic stirrer, the purified blank QASs and CIP-loaded QASs pellets were continuously mixed with the gel bases [13].

Particle Size (PS) and Zeta Potential (ZP)

The QASs dispersion was diluted in water 1:10 (v/v) with Milli-Q water before measurement of PS and ZP using a DLS Zetasizer (Malvern Instruments, UK) under standard conditions [13].

Surface morphology evaluation by Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

TEM was used to determine particle morphology and size. The size and surface morphology of blank QASs were evaluated using a TEM (Philips CM-10, Eindhoven, The Netherlands) with a magnification range of 4900–46,000 × and an accelerating voltage of 80 kV. A single drop of the sample prepared in distilled water was placed on a copper grid and dried at room temperature before analysis [14].

Chemical interaction assessment by fourier Transforms Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR was performed to determine possible chemical interactions among CIP, CTAB, and Chol. KBr pellets were prepared and analysis by FTIR. The IR spectra of all samples were recorded over the range of 500 to 4000 cm− 1. The appearance of characteristic peaks was observed, and the characteristic peaks of CTAB, Chol, blank QASs, and CIP-loaded QASs were compared to confirm drug loading into QASs with an FTIR (Thermo Scientific, USA).

Optimization of QASs formulations

QASs formulations were optimized by varying the concentration of CTAB: Chol. The formulation with the smallest particle size and the highest %EE was considered the optimized formulation.

Homogeneity was evaluated by applying a small amount of gel to the dorsal side of the hand. Stability study

The stability study of QASs was conducted under controlled conditions according to a previously defined method with slight modifications to evaluate their physicochemical integrity over time. Both blank and CIP-loaded QASs were stored under different environmental conditions [15]. Three temperature settings were selected: 4 °C, 25 °C, and 40 °C. Samples were withdrawn at intervals of 0, 1, 3, and 6 months and assessed for PS, ZP, %EE, and visual appearance, including clarity, precipitation, and color change.

Antimicrobial assay

Mueller Hinton agar plates were used to refresh the ATCC strain of MRSA (ATCC 43300). Bacterial colonies were picked and transferred to sterile glass tubes containing 0.9% saline and adjusted to the 0.5 McFarland standard. For biofilm production, Mueller Hinton broth (MHB) was used to dilute the suspension, which was then used to inoculate wells of 96-well microplates [16].

Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) with blank and CIP-loaded QASs dispersion

MIC was determined using the standard broth microdilution technique in a sterile 96-well microplate. Briefly, 100 µL of sterile MHB was dispensed into each well. An equal volume (100 µL) of the test sample, prepared at an initial concentration of 1024 µg/mL, was added in triplicate to designated columns. CIP was used as the control at a concentration of 256 µg/mL and was included in duplicate, as appropriate. Serial two-fold dilutions were performed by transferring 100 µL from the first row to subsequent rows, followed by thorough mixing, and discarding 100 µL from the final row to maintain a uniform volume across wells.

A standardized microbial inoculum was prepared by suspending the MRSA in normal saline and adjusting the turbidity to match the 0.5 McFarland standard. Subsequently, 100 µL of this suspension was added to 10 mL of sterile MHB to obtain the working inoculum. Thereafter, 100 µL of the inoculated broth was dispensed into each well, except for the last column and the final four wells, which served as sterility and growth controls. The microplate was incubated at 37 °C for 18–20 h under appropriate conditions.

Following incubation, 40 µL of INT solution (1 mg/5 mL) was added to the first two columns of each sample to facilitate visualization of bacterial growth. Plates were incubated with dye at room temperature for 30 min and the results were recorded [17].

Antibiofilm assay

MRSA biofilm was grown in 96-well microplates at 37 °C for 48 h by using a gyratory shaker at 70 rpm. Before treatments, the developed biofilms were washed twice with sterile water to remove any planktonic bacteria [16].

The established biofilm was heat-fixed for 01 h at 60 °C and then its biomass was measured by staining for 15 min at room temperature with a 0.1% crystal violet solution. Sterile water was used to rinse and remove excess dye, and the microplates were allowed to dry. To dissolve the dye, the crystal violet stain was incubated for 15 min at room temperature in 200 µL of 30% acetic acid. Thereafter, the contents of each well were transferred into another microplate, and absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, UK). Using the untreated-control absorbance and treatment absorbance values, the reduction in biofilm mass (% BMR) was computed [16, 18].

In vivo study on murine wound excision model

The in vivo study was performed according to a previous protocol with slight modifications [19]. Healthy male BALB/c albino mice weighing 25–30 g ± 5 g, obtained from the National Institute of Health, Islamabad, Pakistan, were used. All the experiments conducted on these animals were in accordance with internationally accepted guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and complied with the ARRIVE guidelines. The in vivo protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, Continuum Research Center (Pvt.) Ltd., Wah Cantt, Pakistan (Reference No. CRC/RD/IAC/EAL/002/2025, dated 06-01-2025). All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering and reduce the number of animals used. All mice were housed under standard laboratory conditions with free access to food and water, and humane endpoints were strictly followed throughout the study.

Animals were allocated into experimental groups without formal randomization. Outcome assessment was performed by an investigator blinded to group allocation to minimize measurement bias. Treatment frequency was selected based on previously published studies and standard experimental practice [20, 21]. Sample size was determined in accordance with established wound-healing models of comparable design and scale. Overall protocol was in compliance with institutional ethical committee guidelines, ensuring minimal animal use while maintaining adequate experimental validity. Each activity was performed in single independent experiment with three replicates to ensure reproducibility. All measurements were carried out using standardized procedures to reduce observational bias and inter-operator variability [20, 22].

Animals were divided into four groups: Group I (MRSA wound infection and no treatment), Group II (MRSA wound infection treated with CIP), Group III (MRSA wound infection treated with blank QASs gel), and Group IV (MRSA wound infection treated with CIP-loaded QASs gel). Each group consisted of 5 animals, a sample size determined based on previously published wound-healing studies employing comparable experimental designs. Intraperitoneal injection of xylazine and ketamine at doses of 12.5 and 80 mg/kg body weight, respectively, was used to anesthetize the animals. Hair from the targeted area was removed with a razor, and once anesthesia was achieved, a 1 × 1 cm² wound was made on the dorsal side. Cyclophosphamide was administered intravenously via the tail vein at a dose of 100 mg/kg from day 0 to day 3 to induce transient immunosuppression, thereby facilitating consistent establishment of MRSA infection and accelerating infection development in the excision wound model. On day 4, a bacterial suspension in normal saline (1107 CFU/mL) was prepared and applied to the wound with a sterile cotton swab to induce infection. Treatment started on day 4 and continued up to day 14 by applying the formulations to the target area once daily with a sterile applicator. Outcomes were recorded as reduction for microbiological burden by using Lavine swab method as a log10 CFU reduction and percent wound contraction (%W.C) as a wound-closure marker [15, 23]. The %W.C was measured on days 8, 10, 12 and 14, by capturing images and analyzing them through ImageJ software, along with graphical measurement through a transparent sheet with markers. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed only for the in vivo wound healing study using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Prior to parametric analysis, data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, while homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. Differences in percentage wound contraction (%WC) among time points within each treatment groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When significant differences were detected, Duncan’s multiple range test was employed for post hoc pairwise comparisons because of its sensitivity in detecting differences among treatment means in biological studies. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. In vitro physicochemical characterization studies were conducted in triplicate and reported descriptively as mean ± SD without inferential statistical analysis.

Results

Preparation of blank and CIP-loaded QASs dispersion and gel

Blank and CIP-loaded QASs dispersions were successfully prepared by probe sonication using three CTAB: Chol concentration ratios. The formulations were lyophilized and stored under standard conditions until further characterized. The optimized dispersion was incorporated into an HMPC-based gel for topical evaluation.

PS and ZP

PS and ZP were used to assess the colloidal properties of the QASs dispersion. Smaller vesicles generally formed more uniform suspension than larger particles. When the CTAB: Chol ratio exceeded 1:1, the PS increased, possibly because of aggregation or less uniform vesicle formation. Blank QASs showed a PS of 115.1 ± 0.5 nm and a ZP of + 31.2 ± 2.1 mV. CIP-loaded QASs showed a PS of 153 ± 1.3 nm and a ZP of + 34.6 ± 1.4 mV (Fig. 1).

Panels (c) and (d) shows the corresponding PS and ZP distribution of CIP-loaded QASs. The plots summarize the nanoscale size range and positive surface charge of the vesicular dispersions used for subsequent gel preparation and biological evaluation.

Surface morphology evaluation by TEM

TEM imaging of CIP-loaded QASs showed predominantly spherical vesicles distributed across the field (Fig. 2). The particles appeared as well-defined, electron-dense spheres, with most vesicles measuring approximately 110–160 nm in diameter. These observations are consistent with nanosized vesicle formation, although further imaging-based studies would be required to confirm vesicle distribution across larger sample field.

TEM image of CIP-loaded QASs captured at 4900× magnification. The image shows predominantly spherical nanosized vesicles with visible electron-dense boundaries. The scale bar represents 500 nm. The morphology supports formation of vesicular structures after CIP loading, while quantitative size interpretation should be considered together with DLS measurements

Chemical interaction assessment by FTIR

FTIR spectra provided evidence supporting QASs formation and CIP entrapment. Chol and CTAB showed characteristic peaks around 3400 cm⁻¹ (O–H stretching), 2950–2850 cm⁻¹ (C–H stretching), and 960–720 cm⁻¹ (C–N stretching), corresponding to their functional groups (Fig. 3).

FTIR spectra of Chol, CTAB, blank and CIP-loaded QASs recorded across 500–4000 cm⁻¹. The spectra compare characteristic functional group bands of the individual components with those observed after vesicle formation and CIP loading. Peak shifts, broadening, and the appearance of CIP-associated bands support drug incorporation and possible intermolecular interactions within the QASs system

In blank QASs, reduced intensity and slight peak shifts suggested possible hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions between CTAB and Chol during vesicle formation. After CIP loading, the spectrum showed broadening and shifting of the O–H/N–H stretching region around 3400 cm⁻¹ and additional bands near 1720 cm⁻¹ (C = O stretching), ~ 1610 cm⁻¹ (aromatic C = C and N–H bending), and ~ 1240 cm⁻¹ (C–F stretching). These changes are consistent with CIP incorporation into the QASs matrix and possible intermolecular interactions between CIP and QASs components.

Estimation of %EE of CIP and optimization of QASs formulations

The %EE of CIP in the optimized QASs formulation was 79 ± 0.4%, indicating incorporation of CIP in QASs vesicular system. Among the tested formulations, F1 exhibited the smallest PS (153 ± 1.3 nm) with a high %EE (79 ± 0.4). Although F2 showed a higher %EE, its larger PS suggested lower colloidal suitability. Therefore, F1 was selected as the optimized formulation based on its balance between nanoscale size and CIP entrapment (Table 1).

MIC with blank and CIP-loaded QASs dispersion

Compared with the CIP dispersion, both blank and CIP-loaded QASs showed lower MIC values against MRSA (Fig. 4). This activity may be attributed to the nanosized vesicles and the cationic surface of QASs which may increase contact with the bacterial envelop. The MIC results showed that blank and CIP-loaded QASs inhibited MRSA at 128 µg/mL and 64 µg/mL respectively, whereas free CIP showed an MIC of > 128 µg/mL.

MIC of Blank, CIP-loaded QASs and free CIP against MRSA determined by the broth microdilution method. The figure compares bacterial growth inhibition across formulation and indicates lower MIC values for QASs-based treatment than for free CIP under the tested conditions

Antibiofilm assay

CIP-loaded QASs produced 91.32 ± 0.14% inhibition of MRSA biofilm mass, whereas blank QASs and free CIP produced 45.25 ± 0.75% and 60.85 ± 0.66% inhibition, respectively (Fig. 5). These results indicate enhanced antibiofilm activity following incorporation of ciprofloxacin into QASs compared with both free drug and blank formulation. However, it should be noted that the blank QAS formulation contains CTAB, which has intrinsic antimicrobial and antibiofilm activity; therefore, part of the observed inhibition in the blank group may be attributed to CTAB rather than the vesicular system alone. A CTAB-free vehicle control was not included in the present study, which represents a limitation in fully delineating the contribution of the carrier system.

Antibiofilm activity of blank, CIP-loaded QASs and free CIP against MRSA biofilm. Biofilm mass reduction was quantified using the crystal violet assay and expressed as percent inhibition relative to untreated control biofilm

The enhanced antibiofilm activity of CIP-loaded QASs may be attributed to improved local contact between the cationic vesicles and the negatively charged biofilm matrix, facilitating greater drug–biofilm interaction. However, the present study did not investigate specific mechanisms such as biofilm penetration, disruption of the extracellular polymeric matrix, or intracellular bacterial killing.

In vivo study on murine wound excision model

The in vivo wound excision model showed greater bacterial biomass reduction in CIP-loaded QASs gel group than in the comparator groups. The untreated group showed severe infection. The blank QASs gel group showed moderate improvement, whereas the CIP-loaded QASs gel group showed ~ -4 Log10 CFU reduction compared with ~ -1.8 Log10 CFU for the CIP-treated group (Fig. 6). Visual examination also showed less redness and ulceration in the CIP-loaded QASs gel group. These results suggest improved antibacterial activity relative to free CIP under the tested conditions.

Reduction in MRSA wound bioburden expressed as Log10 CFU among the in vivo treatment groups. The figure compares untreated infected wounds, free CIP-treated wounds, blank QASs gel-treated wounds and CIP-loaded QASs gel-treated wounds

The %W.C was assessed on 8, 10, 12 and 14 days after treatment initiation. (Fig. 7a). By day 14, CIP-loaded QASs gel group showed 98% contraction in wound, compared with 82% in the CIP-treated group, 50% in the blank QASs gel group, and 24% in the untreated group (Fig. 7b). Visual observations were consistent with faster wound closure in the CIP-loaded QASs gel group (Fig. 7a).

In vivo wound-healing outcomes in the murine MRSA wound excision model. Panel (a) shows representative wound images from the treatment groups over the observation period, illustrating visual changes in the wound closure. Panel (b) presents %W.C across treatment groups on day 8, 10,12, and 14

One-way ANOVA revealed statistically significant differences in %WC across different time points for all treatment groups. Highly significant variation was observed in the positive control group (F(3, 8) = 463.688, p < 0.001, η² = 0.994), blank formulation group (F(3, 8) = 112.750, p < 0.001, η² = 0.977), and CIP-loaded QASs gel group (F(3, 8) = 525.500, p < 0.001, η² = 0.995), whereas the negative control group also showed significant but comparatively lower variation (F(3, 8) = 10.692, p = 0.004, η² = 0.800).

Post hoc analysis using Duncan’s Multiple Range Test revealed significant differences among all groups at every time point (p < 0.05; Supplementary Table S1). Negative Control consistently exhibited the limited %W.C (17–24%, group a), whereas blank QASs (group ab–b) and Positive Control (group b–c) demonstrated intermediate activity throughout the study period. The CIP loaded-QASs progressively outperformed all groups, advancing from 32 ± 2.65% at Day 8 (group c) to 98 ± 2.65% at Day 14 (group d), significantly surpassing all other formulations at the study endpoint. Overall, the CIP-loaded QASs gel exhibited the greatest improvement in %W.C throughout the study period, indicating enhanced wound-healing efficacy compared with the blank formulation and negative control groups and superior healing performance relative to the positive control at later time points.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that CIP-loaded QASs gel showed greater antibiofilm and antimicrobial activity against MRSA than free CIP and blank QASs under the tested conditions. The optimized CIP-loaded QASs had a nanoscale PS (153 ± 1.3 nm) and a positive ZP (+ 34.6 ± 1.4 mV), both of which are favorable for colloidal stability, dermal retention and may support local interaction with negatively charged bacterial or biofilm-associated surfaces. Similar observations on the importance of PS and surface charge in the nanocarrier-based drug delivery have been reported previously [9].

The %EE of 79 ± 0.4% supports incorporation of CIP into the QASs vesicular system. The FTIR shifts and newly observed bands further suggest integration of CIP within the vesicular system, rather than simple physical mixing. These findings align with previous reports describing drug entrapment in quatsomal systems containing quaternary ammonium surfactants and Chol [11, 13].

The lower MIC of CIP-loaded QASs (64 µg/mL) relative to free CIP (> 128 µg/mL) suggested increased antibacterial activity against MRSA. Likewise, the higher antibiofilm inhibition (91.32 ± 0.14%) compared with blank QASs and free CIP supports the potential value of vesicular delivery for biofilm-associated infection model. However, direct biofilm penetration, release kinetics, efflux modulation, and biofilm-disruption mechanisms were not experimentally evaluated; therefore, mechanistic interpretation should remain cautious. These findings are consistent with previous literature indicating that nanocarrier-based systems may help address bacterial resistance mechanisms and support antibiotic activity in biofilm-associated infections [3, 7, 16].

The topical gel formulation also showed acceptable spreadability, viscosity, pH and homogeneity, making it suitable for application on infected skin wounds. Previous formulations using quatsomal gels have reported similar physicochemical suitability for topical use [13].

In the in vivo studies, the formulation produced greater wound contraction and bacterial reduction compared with both free CIP and blank QASs gel, suggesting that incorporation into QASs may improve both the antibacterial and wound healing performance of CIP in this model. These results are in agreement with earlier reports on nanoparticle-based antimicrobial formulations [19, 23, 25].

The present research work was designed as a preliminary formulation-development and proof-of-concept study; therefore, several limitations should be acknowledged. Only one MRSA ATCC strain was evaluated, which may not fully reflect the diversity of clinical isolates with different resistance profiles and biofilm-forming capacities. In addition, no release kinetics study was performed, limiting interpretation of drug-release behavior from the gel matrix. Cytotoxicity, skin irritation and broader dermal biocompatibility studies were also not conducted. The formulation was not compared with a standard marketed topical anti-MRSA therapy and the blank formulation, the mechanisms underlying the observed antibacterial and antibiofilm effects were not directly investigated. Addressing these limitations in future studies will be essential for mechanistic clarification, therapeutic validation and clinical translation.

Conclusion

CIP-loaded QASs represent a promising proof-of-concept delivery platform for addressing important limitations of conventional antibiotic therapy against multidrug-resistant pathogens, particularly MRSA. Incorporation of CIP into quatsomal systems improved physicochemical stability, increased antibacterial activity, and markedly increased antibiofilm activity compared to free CIP under the tested conditions. The nanoscale size and positive surface charge of QASs may contribute to improve local interaction with bacterial and biofilm-associated surfaces; however, direct mechanistic studies are required to confirm penetration, release, safety, and intracellular-delivery behavior.

Overall, CIP-loaded QASs gel may offer a useful strategy for improving localized antibiotic delivery and wound healing outcomes in MRSA-infected wounds. Nevertheless, additional studies on pharmacokinetics, ex vivo skin permeation, in vitro release, safety, and comparative efficacy against established topical therapies are required before clinical translation can be considered.
