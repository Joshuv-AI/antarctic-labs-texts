Spike destabilization attenuates Mink Cluster 5 SARS-CoV-2
Authors: Julia N. Faraone, Pei Li, Junping Hong, Jinkai Zang, Yajie Liu, Yan Xu, Panke Qu, John P. Evans, Jie Chen, Yi-Min Zheng, Phylip Chen, Mark E. Peeples, Kai Xu, Shan-Lu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceSpillover and subsequent evolution of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) in nonhuman hosts is not well understood. The largest cases of this have occurred in farmed mink, which have resulted in variants of SARS-CoV-2 including the Mink Cluster 5 variant investigated in this study. This work reveals a critical role for an S2 mutation in destabilizing the spike and attenuated phenotypes of this variant in humans. Overall, this broadens our understanding of animal SARS-CoV-2 transmission and the role of S2 in dictating spike conformation and stability.

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) continues to evolve, acquiring mutations that enhance transmissibility, alter tissue tropism and pathogenicity, and promote immune evasion (1–3). Although the precise origin of SARS-CoV-2 remains unknown, it is well established that most emerging human viruses originate from animals, particularly bats, which represents persistent public health threats (4). Importantly, reverse zoonosis, in which a human virus is transmitted to a nonhuman host, can drive rapid viral adaptation and generate constellations of mutations with unpredictable consequences, including the possibility of spillover back to humans (5–8). Indeed, SARS-CoV-2 has repeatedly spilled over into multiple species during the pandemic, including dogs, cats, hamsters, mink, white-tailed deer, lions, tigers, leopards, and raccoon dogs (9–16). These documented cases largely reflect chance detection through limited surveillance, suggesting that many additional spillover events have likely occurred undetected.

Most cases of spillover of SARS-CoV-2 have been dead ends for the virus, resulting in little to no disease manifestation and no detectable transmission in the new host. A notable exception to this is the transmission of SARS-CoV-2 into farmed mink. In April of 2020, an outbreak occurred on a mink farm in the Netherlands, leading to the subsequent infection of several handlers that worked at the farm and their household contacts (6, 17). Since then, many independent outbreaks have taken place on mink farms around the world, including in the Netherlands, Denmark, Poland, Italy, Spain, Greece, France, Canada, and the United States (6, 18–26). Mink farms present an ideal site of propagation for the virus, with many susceptible hosts housed closely together and experiencing consistent contact with humans (27). This had led to a rapid evolution of the virus in the mink (7, 8, 18, 28), yielding mutations such as Y453F, F486L, and N501T in the receptor binding domain (RBD) of the spike protein (S), which are present in some major lineage of human SARS-CoV-2 variants, including Omicron and have been shown to affect interactions with human ACE2 (29–35). Of particular note was the emergence of the variant known as mink cluster 5 (MC5V) from these outbreaks, characterized by S mutations del69-70, Y453F, I692V, and M1229I (Fig. 1A). Concern around the variant was quickly curbed by the fact that it appears to only have infected 12 people total (36, 37). However, the emergence of MC5V has demonstrated how quickly SARS-CoV-2 spike can adapt upon transmission into a new host, resulting in altered properties and unpredictable outcomes.

In this study, we investigated the properties of the MC5V variant and the mechanisms underlying its attenuation, with a focus on the spike protein. We identified a single mutation, I692V, located in the S2 subunit rather than the RBD of S, as the principal determinant impairing viral entry into host cells. I692V reduces MC5V infectivity and fusogenicity by disrupting spike processing and stability, as evidenced by enhanced S1 shedding and decreased virion incorporation. Moreover, I692V, together with the RBD-localized Y453F mutation, diminishes binding of neutralizing antibodies present in convalescent sera from early in the pandemic. These findings provide insights into the structural and functional interplay between the S1 and S2 subunits of spike, highlighting their critical role in shaping viral biology, fitness, and zoonotic transmission.

Results

MC5V Exhibits Attenuated Infectivity in Human Cells Attributable to Spike Mutations, Especially I692V in S2.

To examine the infectivity of MC5V and key determinants, we generated two different panels of lentiviral pseudotypes bearing SARS-CoV-2 spikes: reversion and forward mutants. Reversion mutants refer to pseudoviruses carrying MC5V S proteins with a reversion of one of the MC5V mutations to ancestral D614G, i.e., MC5V with Ins69-70, F453Y, V692I, or I1229M. Forward mutants are single MC5V mutations introduced into D614G S, i.e., D614G with del69-70, Y453F, I692V, or M1229I (Fig. 1A). We produced pseudotyped lentiviral vectors expressing a secreted Gaussia luciferase reporter and bearing the individual SARS-CoV-2 S proteins, and examined their infectivity in two target cell lines, i.e., HEK293T cells overexpressing human ACE2 (293T-hACE2) (Fig. 1 B and C) and human lung epithelial cell line CaLu-3 (Fig. 1 D and E).

We observed that pseudovirions bearing MC5V S had markedly lower infectivity relative to D614G in both cell lines, with an 18.9-fold decrease in 293T-hACE2 (P < 0.0001) and a 5.5-fold reduction in CaLu-3 (Fig. 1 B–E). Remarkably, reversion of the mink I692V mutation to V692I led to a dramatic recovery in infectivity, with an 11.6-fold increase relative to MC5V (P < 0.0001), bringing it to levels almost comparable with D614G in 293T-ACE2 cells (Fig. 1B). Conversely, introduction of mink I692V into the D614G backbone led to a significantly decreased infectivity, with an 11.4-fold drop relative to D614G (P < 0.01), making it almost comparable to MC5V in 293T-hACE2 (Fig. 1C). We found that reversion mutants F453Y and I1229M consistently, but insignificantly, recovered the infectivity relative to MC5V (P > 0.05), while both forward mutants Y453F and M1229I remained a comparable infectivity to D614G alone (P > 0.05) in 293T-hACE2 (Fig. 1 B and C). Interestingly, reversion mutant Ins69-70 had an even worse effect on infectivity, causing a 1.7-fold drop relative to MC5V (P > 0.05), whereas forward mutant Del69-70 exhibited a 1.8-fold increase relative to D614G (P < 0.05) in 293T-hACE2 (Fig. 1 B and C). Similar trends were seen in CaLu-3 for each mutation (Fig. 1 D and E), though not as dramatic as shown in 293T-hACE2 cells, suggesting that the infectivity measured here is independent of viral entry pathways.

We then used the virus-like particles (VLP) system of SARS-CoV-2 to validate the impact of individual mutations on viral infectivity. We produced VLPs by transfecting HEK293T cells with plasmids encoding the nucleocapsid (N), membrane (M), envelope (E), and S proteins, and infected 293T-hACE2-Lgbit cells (38). Consistent with the results obtained using lentiviral pseudovirions, infectivity of MC5V VLPs was lower than that of D614G (~2.9-fold, P < 0.01) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B), albeit not as dramatic as shown in the lentiviral pseudotypes (Fig. 1 B and C). Importantly, reversion of the MC5V I692V mutation to V692I increased infectivity by ~2.2-fold (P < 0.01), and introduction of I692V to the D614G background markedly reduced infectivity by ~35.8-fold (P < 0.0001) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B). Surprisingly, we found that reversion mutation I1229M substantially decreased the infectivity of MC5V by ~13.6-fold (P = 0.078), while the corresponding forward mutation (M1229I) had no detectable effect on the D614G infectivity (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B), suggesting that M1229I may be critical for maintaining the infectivity of MC5V but not essential for SARS-CoV-2 VLPs. Together, these results corroborated the attenuated infectivity phenotype of MC5V and support the conclusion that the I692V mutation is a major contributor of this phenotype.

We next assessed the replication of live MC5V and SARS-CoV-2 viruses in VeroE6-TMPRSS2 and primary human bronchial epithelial cells (HBEs). VeroE6-TMPRSS2 cells were infected with infectious MC5V and D614G, with viruses passaged twice in VeroE6-TMPRSS2 cells. Cytopathic effect (CPE) was tracked starting 3 d postinfection to calculate a tissue culture infectious dose at 50% (TCID50). MC5V showed markedly lower and delayed CPE relative to D614G and a dramatically lower growth rate overall (Fig. 1F). On day 4 postinfection (96 h), the TCID50 of MC5V was over 3,000-fold lower than D614G. On day 6 postinfection, the last day of data collection, this difference had lessened to about 100-fold and cells infected with MC5V were still largely intact (Fig. 1F). Overall, MC5V was 1,350-fold less infectious in VeroE6-TMPRSS2 cells relative to D614G (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1G). In primary HBEs, replication of MC5V was also less efficient than that of D614G, as evidenced by ~3-fold lower nucleocapsid levels in released virions measured by ELISA (P < 0.05) and even 26~56-fold decreased infectivity (P < 0.0001) in both donors (Fig. 1 H and I). Because primary mink airway epithelial cells were not available, we were unable to directly assess MC5V infectivity in this physiologically relevant system (Discussion).

I692V Mutation Impairs MC5V Entry into Mink ACE2 Expressing Cells.

The next question became whether MC5V would exhibit different phenotypes in cells expressing mink ACE2 as it displayed in 293T cells expressing human ACE2. To investigate this, we transduced human ACE2 knockout (KO) 293T cells (293T-ACE2-KO) with retroviral vectors expressing either mink or human ACE2, followed by infection of D614G or MC5V (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). We found that MC5V had a reduced infectivity in mink ACE2-expressing cells, with a drop of 4.5-fold relative to D614G (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2 A, Left panel); this reduction was smaller than the 6.2-fold drop observed in 293T-ACE2-KO cells expressing human ACE2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B) and the 18.9-fold drop seen in 293T-hACE2 cells (Fig. 1B), suggesting that MC5V may be relatively better adapted to mink ACE2. In addition, the pseudoviral infectivity of D614G was reduced in 293T-ACE2-KO cells expressing mink ACE2 compared to those expressing human ACE2, showing a 3.3-fold decrease (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2 A, Left panel), despite comparable ACE2 expression levels (Fig. 2 B and C). Together, these findings indicate that mink ACE2 mediates SARS-CoV-2 entry less efficiently than human ACE2, and that infectivity of MC5V is lower than that of D614G, regardless of target cells expressing mink or human ACE2.

Next, the impact of MC5V mutations in mink ACE2-expressing cells was determined. Consistent with the effects of MC5V mutants in 293T-ACE2-KO cells expressing human ACE2 SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B) and 293T-hACE2 cells (Fig. 1B), the reversion mutant V692I in 293T-ACE2-KO cells expressing mink ACE2 showed an approximately 9.6-fold increase in infectivity relative to parental MC5V (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2 A, Middle panel). Conversely, the forward mutant I692V reduced infectivity by 16.0-fold relative to D614G (P < 0.0001), resulting in levels that were even 3.5-fold lower than MC5V (Fig. 2 A, Right panel). Notably, the reversion mutants Ins69-70 and F453Y exhibited 0.6 to 1.7-fold reduced infectivity (P > 0.05) whereas I1229M showed no change (P > 0.05), compared with parental MC5V in mink-ACE2 cells (Fig. 2 A, Middle panel). In contrast, the forward mutations Del69-70 and Y453F resulted in 1.9-fold increased (P < 0.0001) and 1.7-fold decreased (P < 0.01) infectivity in mink-ACE2 cells, while M1229I did not significantly affect infectivity in mink-ACE2 cells (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2 A, Right panel). Overall, the findings in mink ACE2-expressing cells mirror those in human ACE2 cells, indicating that I692V is the primary determinant of the reduced infectivity of MC5V, regardless of human or mink ACE2, with additional mutations contributing to this phenotype.

Key Role of MC5V Y453F in Enhancing Mink-ACE2 Binding.

Human and mink ACE2 are highly conserved overall, including many contact residues that engage the SARS-CoV-2 S (e.g., K31, Y41, and K353). However, several substitutions at the spike-binding interface (such as positions 24, 30, 34, 38, and 354) subtly alter local interactions, creating a receptor environment in mink in which RBD mutations can enhance binding and species-specific adaptation. To determine the effect of individual mutations on S binding to human or mink ACE2, we performed Bio-layer Interferometry (BLI) to measure the affinity for MC5V mutations Del69-70, Y453F, and I692V in comparison with the ancestral D614G (Fig. 2 D–F). M1229I was not included in this panel due to the deletion of the transmembrane domain to generate soluble S proteins (Fig. 1A). Binding was carried out by using a panel of increasing concentrations of soluble spike and affinity was calculated by Gator’s proprietary data analysis software (Fig. 2 D and E). We observed that, similar to D614G, all these three S mutants bound to human ACE2 with nanomolar affinity, i.e., 1.22 to 8.96 nM in equilibrium disassociation constant (KD) (Fig. 2F). Interestingly, the only S mutant to efficiently bind mink ACE2 was the Y453F mutant, with a KD of 33.9 nM (Fig. 2 D–F). All other spikes, including D614G, exhibited 31 to 186-fold decreased binding of mink ACE2 compared to human ACE2 (Fig. 2 E and F). Together, these findings help explain the generally low infectivity of MC5V in mink-ACE2 cells (Fig. 2A), but they do not account for the markedly decreased infectivity of the forward I692V mutant in human-ACE2 cells (Fig. 1) (see below).

MC5V Spike Exhibits Decreased Fusogenicity and Processing, Mainly Due to I692V.

Another critical aspect of viral entry is the S protein’s ability to trigger fusion of the viral and host cell membranes. To investigate this, we coexpressed the S protein of interest described above and GFP in 293T-hACE2 cells for 24 h, imaged and quantified syncytia formation (Fig. 3 A–C). MC5V S demonstrated markedly reduced fusion compared to D614G, with a 2.4-fold reduction in total area of fused cells (P < 0.0001). Reversion of each of the individual mutations present in MC5V, except for Ins69-70, enhanced fusion, with 1.5-fold (P > 0.05), 2.0-fold (P < 0.01), and 1.8-fold (P < 0.01) increases for F453Y, V692I, and I1229M relative to MC5V, respectively (Fig. 3 A and B). In contrast, introduction of individual MC5V mutations Y453F, I692V, and M1229I in D614G all reduced fusion, with 1.6-fold (P < 0.0001), 1.8-fold (P < 0.0001), and 1.4-fold (P < 0.01) decreases relative to D614G, respectively (Fig. 3 A and C). Flow cytometric analysis of 293T cells transfected with the spike plasmids of interest showed largely comparable levels of surface expression, with the exceptions of the forward I692V mutation and the reverse V692I/F453Y mutation, which displayed modest decreases and increases, respectively, relative to D614G and MC5V (likely reflecting differences in S1 shedding; see below) (Fig. 3 D and E). These results indicate that the observed differences in syncytia formation were largely not attributable to variations in spike expression.

We next assessed the processing of MC5V S and its defining mutations by analyzing lysates from 293T cells producing pseudotyped lentiviral vectors and probing the S1 and S2 subunits via western blotting (Fig. 3F). MC5V S showed markedly reduced cleavage efficiency, with an S1/S ratio of 0.57(±0.12) compared to 1.00 for D614G (P < 0.01) (Fig. 3G). Notably, introducing the I692V mutation into the D614G backbone drastically impaired processing, yielding an S1/S ratio of 0.33(±0.04) (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3 F and G), whereas reverting this mutation in MC5V S to D614G significantly enhanced processing, with an S1/S ratio of 1.43(±0.17) (~2.5-fold, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3 F and G). Interestingly, we observed that the forward Del69-70 mutation in the D614G background increased S processing, with S1/S ratio of 1.86(±0.11) compared to 1.00 for D614G (P < 0.0001), whereas reversion of Del69-70 to Ins69-70 in MC5V modestly decreased it [S1/S ratio of 0.43(±0.05)] (P > 0.05) (Fig. 3 F and G). Similar trends were observed in the S2/S ratios of these mutants (Fig. 3 F and H), collectively indicating that MC5V S processing is substantially impaired, primarily due to the I692V mutation in S2, with additional contributions from Del69-70 and Y453F.

I692V Destabilizes MC5V Spike, Resulting Increased S1 Shedding.

The pronounced attenuation of infectivity, processing, and fusion observed for MC5V S may stem from altered conformational stability. To test this, we performed a spontaneous S1 shedding assay by transfecting 293T cells with the spike plasmids of interest and collecting their corresponding culture supernatants for FLAG pulldown, targeting the N-terminal domain (NTD) of S1 (Fig. 4A). An increased S1 detected in the media would be interpreted as elevated spontaneous shedding, thus indicative of reduced spike stability (39, 40). We found that, compared to D614G, the S1 shedding band for MC5V was ~5.28(±0.82)-fold more intense (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4 B and C). Strikingly, among all the forward mutations tested, I692V presented the strongest S1 shedding band that was over 9.22 (±2.27)-fold more intense than D614G (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4 B and C). In contrast, among the reverse mutations only V692I showed a 5.80 (±2.10)-fold reduced S1 band (P < 0.01), nearly comparable to D614G. The other reversion mutants Ins69-70, F453Y, and I1229M—all displayed strong S1 bands similar to MC5V (Fig. 4 B and C). The expression pattern of full-length S, as well as their processing to S1 and S2, in S-transfected cells are shown in Fig. 4 B, Bottom panel consistent with results from the virus-producing cells as shown in Fig. 3F. Because S1 shedding is tightly linked to S1/S2 processing, we normalized shedding across mutants using S2/S ratios, as shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S3A. Notably, since the I692V mutation also impairs S processing in MC5V relative to D614G, the normalized S1-shedding differences became even more pronounced.

To confirm the observations from S-transfected cell media, we assayed S1 shedding from purified pseudovirions for D614G, D614G_I692V, MC5V, and MC5V_V692I. Viral particles bearing the SARS-CoV-2 S variants of interest were incubated with 0, 3, or 10 µg/mL human soluble ACE2 (sACE2) at 37 °C for 1 h. Virions were then purified and subjected to pulldown with an anti-FLAG antibody (41), and the amount of S1 shed and remaining associated with the particles was quantified by western blotting. In the presence of sACE2, MC5V showed ~1.65(±0.06)-fold greater S1 shedding than D614G (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4 D, Top panel and E). Critically, the forward mutant D614G_I692V increased S1 shedding relative to D614G by ~1.94 (±0.23)-fold (P < 0.001), whereas the reverse mutant MC5V_V692I exhibited ~0.54(±0.14)-fold reduced S1 shedding (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4 D, Top panel and Fig. 4E). Furthermore, increasing concentrations of sACE2 enhanced S1 release for all spike variants, but the relative order of shedding remained unchanged: MC5V>D614G_I692V>D614G>MC5V_V692I (Fig. 4 D and E). western blotting analysis of the pulldown products confirmed the presence of ACE2 (middle panels), the mouse antibody light chain (middle panels), and HIV-1 p24 (bottom panel) (Fig. 4D). Analysis of S expression and incorporation in producer cell lysates and purified pseudovirions revealed large reductions in the S1/S and S2/S ratios for MC5V (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 B and C), consistent with intrinsically impaired spike processing that is largely attributable to the I692V substitution. Consistent with the findings from culture supernatants (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A), normalization of S1 shedding in pseudovirions using S2/S ratios also revealed more pronounced differences (SI Appendix, Fig. S3D).

Weakened Neutralization of MC5V By Early COVID-19 Sera and Monoclonal Antibodies.

Using a cohort of sera from early COVID-19 patients (n = 15) who were hospitalized in April 2020 when MC5V spillover from human to mink likely had occurred (42), we performed a pseudotyped virus neutralization assay to determine if MC5V would evade the neutralizing antibody (nAb) response. These patients tested positive for COVID-19 between late March and early April 2020. The cohort consisted of 5 females and 10 males, aged 39 to 81 years (median 55). Patients were selected based on prior results showing an NT50 > 1,000 against D614G, an ELISA value >1.0, and availability of complete demographic data. We observed that MC5V exhibited reduced neutralization titer compared to D614G, with a 3.1-fold decrease in NT50 (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5A). Reversion of each of the four MC5V mutations to the D614G sequence increased NT50 values by 1.3-, 1.8-, 2.1-, and 2.3-fold for Ins69-70, F453Y, V692I (P < 0.05), and I1229M (P < 0.05) relative to MC5V, respectively (Fig. 5A). Among forward mutations introduced into the D614G backbone, only Y453F caused a statistically significant reduction, with a 1.6-fold drop in NT50 relative to D614G (P < 0.05), consistent with a previous report (43); whereas the others produced only modest decreases. Taken together, these results suggest that all four mutations contribute to the modest escape of MC5V from neutralization by early COVID-19 sera, with Y453F, I692V, and M1229I exerting the greatest effects. Antigenic cartography analyses revealed that MC5V is antigenically distinct from D614G, showing a separation of antigenic units (AUs) and with an AU of 2.4 (Fig. 5 B and C). In contrast, reversion mutations, particularly F453Y, V692I, and I1229M, displayed shortened antigenic distances from D614G, with AUs of 1.3, 0.7, and 0.6, respectively (Fig. 5 B and C). Consistent with the neutralization titers, all forward mutations clustered closely together, with Y453F and I692V positioned slightly farther from D614G, at 0.7 and 1.3 AUs, respectively (Fig. 5 B and C).

We next evaluated neutralization by two key monoclonal antibodies: the class I antibody 2B04 and class III antibody S309 (Fig. 5 D–F). S309 neutralized MC5V less efficiently, showing a 4.2-fold increase in IC50 relative to D614G (Fig. 5 D and E). Single mutations Y453F and I692V each contributed to this reduction, with 1.75- and 2.69-fold increases in IC50 compared to D614G, respectively. Conversely, reversion of these mutations enhanced S309 neutralization, as reflected by 2.20- and 4.66-fold decreases in IC50 for F453Y and V692I relative to MC5V (Fig. 5 D and E). In contrast, neutralization by 2B04, a class I antibody that targets the receptor-binding domain, remained unaffected, with potent inhibition observed across MC5V, D614G, and all mutants tested (Fig. 5 D and F). However, it is important to note that the Y453F mutation has previously been shown to reduce neutralization by another class I antibody, REGN10933 (43). Collectively, these findings indicate that Y453F and I692V compromise the efficacy of S309.

Structural Analyses of Mutations in MC5V.

To assess how mutations in MC5V S influence its structure and stability, we performed structural modeling using the SWISS-MODEL server. In ancestral SARS-CoV-2 S, residue Y453 in the RBD forms a stabilizing hydrogen bond with H34 of human ACE2, thereby promoting efficient receptor engagement (Fig. 6A). The Y453F substitution in MC5V removes the hydroxyl group required for this hydrogen bond, slightly weakening spike–ACE2 binding. In contrast, modeling with mink ACE2 revealed that Y453 in the ancestral S sterically clashes with Y34 of mink ACE2 due to their bulky side chains, impairing binding (Fig. 6B). Substitution with phenylalanine alleviates this clash, enabling favorable hydrophobic interactions between F453 and Y34 of mink ACE2, thus enhancing binding affinity.

Y453 forms a hydrogen bond with H34 in human ACE2 (A), whereas F453 forms hydrophobic interactions with Y34 in mink ACE2. All contact residues are shown as sticks, and the protein backbones are depicted as ribbons. (C and D) Location of residue 692 within the spike protein. The position of I692 is highlighted in the context of the spike trimer (C). Beyond receptor binding, the conformational stability of S is also influenced by mutations outside the RBD. In ancestral S, residue I692 contributes to trimer integrity through hydrophobic interactions with neighboring residues, especially P600, A609, and A653 (Fig. 6C). Replacement with valine shortens the side chain and disrupts these hydrophobic contacts, thus weakening the trimer stability (Fig. 6D). This destabilization likely promotes spontaneous S1 shedding, as experimentally demonstrated in Fig. 4, contributing to the impaired infectivity and fusogenicity of MC5V (Figs. 1–3). Together, these structural insights illustrate how specific mutations optimize host adaptation, improving engagement with mink ACE2, while simultaneously compromising spike stability and viral fitness.

Discussion

Despite the extensive evolution viruses can undergo in nonhuman hosts, reverse zoonosis of SARS-CoV-2 remains incompletely characterized (7, 8, 18, 28). Here we analyzed MC5V, the mink-associated “cluster 5” constellation that spilled back from humans into farmed mink and showed that MC5V exhibits markedly attenuated infectivity, impaired S processing, reduced membrane fusion, and altered antigenicity (Figs. 1–5). Unexpectedly, these defects are driven primarily by a single, conservative substitution in the S2 subunit, I692V, a region conventionally considered structurally conserved and functionally stable. Our results therefore highlight a substantive, underappreciated role for S2 in governing spike conformational stability, processing, and epitope presentation.

Homology modeling indicates that SARS-CoV-2 I692 participates in intraprotomer packing near the S1/S2 interface, notably contacting P600; substitution of isoleucine (I) by valine (V) weakens this contact and reduces trimer stability (Fig. 6). Functionally, I692V markedly increases both spontaneous and ACE2-induced S1 shedding from S-expressing cells and purified pseudovirions, consistent with premature S1 dissociation and a destabilized prefusion spike (Fig. 4). I692V also diminishes furin-dependent processing, cell–cell fusion and more importantly pseudovirus and live virus infectivity (Fig. 1–3).

In addition to altering S biology, we find that I692V reduces S309 binding and modestly affects convalescent serum neutralization, suggesting that S2 instability can indirectly remodel S1 epitopes, a mechanism that may extend to other emerging variants. Noticeably, we have previously reported that mutations within the C-terminal region of S1, such as residues H655 and T547, can modulate the S stability, proteolytic processing, membrane fusion, and nAb sensitivity (39). Collectively, these phenotypes are consistent with a model in which S2 perturbation loosens the trimer (44), thereby modulating fusogenicity and antigenic exposure, a mechanism that echoes observations for some Omicron lineages (41).

Population data support a strong constraint at the SARS-CoV-2 S I692 site: among >15 million human-derived genomes in GISAID, only ~1,600 carry any change at I692, and I692V to date is essentially restricted to MC5V among 1,431 mink-derived sequences, consistent with a substantial fitness cost for human transmission (45). Related substitutions have appeared only sporadically in wildlife (e.g., I692F in an EG.5.1 isolate from a white-tailed deer, January 2024; I692L in two highly divergent bat viruses in 2021), underscoring the rarity of variation at this locus. In contrast, the Y453F substitution, which is located in the RBD of S1 and previously identified in multiple mink outbreaks, represents a clear adaptive event that facilitates viral replication in mink (29, 46). Structural and binding analyses showed that Y453F removes a steric clash with residue Y34 in mink ACE2 (H34 in human ACE2), thereby increasing receptor affinity and promoting efficient infection in mink (Figs. 2 and 6) (31–35, 44). Notably, other animals such as dogs and cats have had farmed mink-derived variants transmitted to them (10, 47–49). Mutations such as Y453F may thus increase the chances of such transmission events happening, given that dogs also have a Y34 residue in ACE2 (50). This mutation also partially interferes with binding of certain monoclonal and convalescent antibodies, including S309, consistent with its location in the receptor-binding motif (Fig. 5). Collectively, these data indicate that Y453F is critical for mink adaptation (29, 31, 34, 35), but cannot offset the destabilizing effects of I692V, which dominates the attenuated phenotype of MC5V.

Given the attenuated properties associated with I692V in our cell-based systems, it may seem paradoxical that an I692V-containing MC5V lineage nevertheless emerged and spread in mink during mid-2020. Several non-mutually exclusive explanations likely contribute. First, MC5V may retain efficient replication in primary mink airway cells and tissues despite its reduced entry in mink-ACE2–expressing 293T cells and the relatively lower binding affinity observed for I692V spike to soluble mink ACE2 (Fig. 2). Host-specific factors in mink, such as protease usage, cellular tropism, and innate immune pressure, could modulate spike performance in vivo in ways not captured by heterologous cell lines. Second, MC5V may exploit alternative modes of dissemination in mink, including cell-to-cell spread, which can occur with reduced dependence on ACE2 and demonstrate partial resistance to antibody neutralization (51). Unfortunately, we have been unable to directly evaluate MC5V replication in primary mink epithelial cells, or to isolate the specific contribution of I692V in that setting, due to lack of access to such materials.

We speculate that the emergence of I692V in MC5V most likely reflects host-specific selection operating within repeated epidemiologic bottlenecks, rather than a mutation that broadly enhances viral fitness. Mink farms create dense, highly permissive transmission chains in which SARS-CoV-2 can diversify following reverse zoonosis, allowing mutations that would be strongly counter-selected in humans to transiently persist or become fixed. Although I692V destabilizes spike in our cell culture assays, it may have been tolerated, or may even have provided a limited advantage only in mink, because receptor adaptation through Y453F lowers the threshold for productive infection and may subtly remodel antigenic exposure in vivo.

Importantly, the phenotypic consequences of substitutions at residue 692 are expected to be context dependent. This residue lies at the S1/S2 interface, where it contributes to packing interactions that maintain the prefusion trimer. Substitutions at this position therefore act epistatically with other mutations that modulate cleavage efficiency, S1–S2 interactions, or the conformational equilibrium of spike. Variants carrying alternative substitutions (e.g., I692F or I692L) introduce bulkier, more hydrophobic side chains that could partially restore or even enhance local packing relative to I692V, potentially mitigating destabilization while preserving other selective advantages. Nonetheless, the rarity of substitutions at position 692 outside MC5V suggests that I692V most likely arose stochastically and became fixed through a combination of local selection and transmission bottlenecks, illustrating how adaptation in animal reservoirs can produce changes that are tolerated in one host yet detrimental for broader human transmission. Given that ferret ACE2 also contains residue Y34 found in mink ACE2, comparative studies of MC5V with and without I692V in ferrets may help disentangle these host-specific effects in vivo.

From a public health and surveillance standpoint, MC5V demonstrates two broader lessons. First, high-density animal reservoirs, such as mink farms, provide fertile grounds for rapid viral diversification, including the emergence of mutation combinations with unpredictable phenotypes, from receptor optimization to profound attenuation. Second, risk assessment strategies that focus solely on RBD substitutions may overlook destabilizing or stabilizing S2 mutations that critically influence viral transmission and immune escape. Incorporating functional assays that evaluate spike stability, including S1 shedding and fusogenicity, is critical for distinguishing potentially high-risk variants from those constrained by structural instability. Findings from this study not only deepen our understanding of coronavirus host adaptation but also emphasize the need for continued genomic and functional surveillance of animal-derived isolates to anticipate future zoonotic and reverse-zoonotic threats.

Materials and Methods

Viral infectivity was assessed in human cells, including HBEs. Spike binding affinity to ACE2 was determined by BLI. S fusogenicity was measured using a syncytium formation assay. S1 shedding was examined in transfected cells and in purified pseudovirions. Escape from neutralizing antibodies was evaluated by using deidentified human serum samples collected under approved IRB protocols (2020H0228 and 2020H0527) in conjunction with a pseudotyped lentiviral neutralization assay. Detailed experimental procedures, as well as quantification and statistical analyses, are described in the SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.
