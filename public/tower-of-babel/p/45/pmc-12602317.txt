Emerging roles of RNA-binding protein hnRNPM in alternative splicing regulation and UTMD mediated sh-hnRNPM/CMBs suppress the proliferation of colorectal cancer
Authors: Yizhao Hu, Weizhen Liu, Dongkun Qiang, Kaili Huang, Qinli Ruan, Linlin Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein M (hnRNPM) is an RNA-binding protein that is aberrantly expressed in tumorigenesis. However, the alternative splicing regulation by hnRNPM in human colorectal cancer (CRC) remain unclear. Herein, we observed that hnRNPM was highly expressed in CRC tissues. The knockdown of hnRNPM inhibited the proliferation of colon cancer cells both in vivo and in vitro. Using RNA-seq, we screened and identified several alternative splicing events regulated by hnRNPM. Knockdown of PLEKHB2-S splice isoform could reduce the growth of colon cancer cells in vitro and in vivo, predicting its role in malignant proliferation of colon cancer cells. Mechanically, the minigene reporter assay indicated the predominant regulatory roles of hnRNPM in PLEKHB2 splicing. The in vivo crosslinking immunoprecipitation (CLIP) assay demonstrated the direct binding of the RNA recognition motif RRM2 of hnRNPM protein to exon 9 of PLEKHB2 pre-mRNA. HnRNPM facilitated the skipping of alternative exon 8 in PLEKHB2 by binding to the constitutive exon 9. Furthermore, we developed cationic microbubbles shRNA/CMBs and transferred to colon cancer cells via ultrasound-targeted microbubble disruption (UTMD). The size and zeta potentials of CMBs and shRNA-CMBs were measured and the optimal concentration range of shRNA/CMBs were screened with low cytotoxicity. The introduction of sh-hnRNPM/CMBs or sh-PLEKHB2-S/CMBs suppressed the proliferation of colon cancer cells in vitro and in vivo. Collectively, our findings identify that hnRNPM dysregulates colorectal carcinoma proliferation at the molecular level of splicing regulation and predicate sh-hnRNPM/CMBs and its splicing target sh-PLEKHB2-S/CMBs as promising therapeutic drugs and innovative strategies for treating colorectal cancer. Introduction

Alternative splicing (AS) can generate multiple mRNA splice isoforms regulated by splicing regulators, which could enrich the variety of the transcriptome and proteome [1, 2]. Dysregulation of AS has been proved to play critical roles in multiple types of tumors, which is triggered by abnormal expression or mutations of splicing factors [1, 3, 4]. Multiple splice isoforms are generated by the regulation of splicing factors, however, aberrant expression or mutations of splicing factors fail to recognize the splice sites and will lead to exclusion or inclusion of the alternative exons [5, 6].

Heterogeneous ribonucleoproteins (hnRNPs) are one kind of well-studied splicing factors, and they are reported to play important roles in pre-mRNA splicing [1, 7, 8]. As one kind of splicing factors, hnRNPM (heterogeneous ribonucleoprotein M) consists of three RNA recognition motifs (RRM), which is predominantly located in the nucleus [9]. HnRNPM is reported to be involved in RNA splicing, RNA transport, stability and translation [10]. Importantly, recent studies have demonstrated that hnRNPM plays crucial roles in a variety of tumors, and many tumor-related splice variants are dysregulated by the changes in hnRNPM expression [7, 11–14].

PLEKHB2 (pleckstrin homology domain containing B2), also known as Evt2, is a vesicular protein that carries a pleckstrin homology domain (PH domain) as the primary functional domain and a C-terminal hydrophobic region (CT motif) [15]. The PH domain of PLEKHB2 was reported to recognize and bind to phosphoserine and target to cyclic endosomes (REs), while the CT motif play essential roles in retrograde membrane transport pathways [16]. Studies have shown that PLEKHB2 could regulate processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation and migration [15–17]. PLEKHB2 promoted the proliferation of breast cancer cells through inhibiting YAP phosphorylation and increased downstream CTGF mRNA expression in breast cancer cells [16]. Moreover, PLEKHB2 has been reported to involve in cancer progression and neurodegenerative diseases, it is expected to carry out in-depth research in exploring the pathogenesis of the diseases and developing novel therapeutic targets and diagnostic markers [18, 19].

Lentivirus-mediated gene silencing is widely used in RNAi, but lentiviral transfection suffers from the drawbacks of viral leakage, safety risks of insertional mutagenesis and interference with the reaction, long experimental cycles and high costs [20]. In recent years, liposomes have abundant applications in nucleic acid drug delivery and vaccine formulation, with broad application prospects in diseases [21]. The positively charged surface of cationic microbubbles (CMBs) were constructed using the cationic liposome Dlin-MC3-DMA and phospholipid [22]. It is reported that the CMBs can effectively encapsulate anti-tumor drugs and can be delivered into tumor cells. The CMBs with positive charges increase the loading of nucleic acid on the surface through electrostatic interactions with negatively charged cell membranes or nucleic acids [23]. The CMBs are reported to have the advantages of low cytotoxicity, high transfection efficiency, wide applicability, easy operation and good reproducibility, efficiency, wide range of application, easy operation, good reproducibility, which have a broad application prospect [24]. The ultrasound-targeted microbubble disruption (UTMD) is a novel therapeutic and drug-delivery technology that uses ultrasound as a guiding tool for microbubbles encapsulating drugs to the target location, and then ultrasound destroys the microbubbles and releases the drugs inside, so as to deliver drugs precisely [25]. UTMD can promote the permeability of biomembrane and facilitate specific delivery through its cavitation and pore effects [26–29]. Currently, UTMD has made breakthroughs in oncology therapy, drug penetration through the blood-brain barrier, and targeted anti-inflammatory treatments, and has also been used to improve the efficiency of gene therapy [30, 31].

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent tumors with high morbidity and mortality in the world. According to statistics, it is the third most common cancer in the world [32, 33]. However, the alternative splicing profile regulated by hnRNPM remains obscure in CRC.

Here we profiled the alternative splicing events regulated by hnRNPM in CRC and further revealed the functions of PLEKHB2-L and PLEKHB2-S splicing variants and their regulatory factor hnRNPM in colon cancer cell proliferation. Cancer-related splice variant PLEKHB2-S was validated by regulation of hnRNPM at the molecular level. We also developed cationic microbubbles sh-hnRNPM/CMBs and transferred to colon cancer cells via UTMD. The introduction of sh-hnRNPM/CMBs inhibited the proliferation of colon cancer cells. Our data predicted that sh-hnRNPM/CMBs serve as a promising therapeutic drug and hnRNPM-regulated splice variants as candidate prognostic factors in CRC patients.

Materials and Methods

Human CRC samples

The 22 clinical colorectal cancer (CRC) samples and the tumor-adjacent tissues were obtained from Nanjing Hospital of Chinese Medicine Affiliated to Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine (Nanjing, China) and approved by the Ethics Committee (KY2021079). The collected samples were stored in RNAlaterTM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at -80°C refrigerator for the detection of RNA expression and validation of splicing events.

In silico analysis of hnRNPM expression, mutation, and survival in human CRC samples

The hnRNPM mRNA level was analyzed using data of human CRC samples in the TCGA database (https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga) and the GTEx V8 version database (https://www.genome.gov/Funded-Programs-Projects/Genotype-Tissue-Expression-Project). The mutation datasets in CRC samples were obtained from the TCGA database and analyzed using the “maftools” package (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/maftools) and R software v4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), following previous studies [34, 35]. The relationship between hnRNPM expression and survival in patients with colorectal tumor was analyzed from the datasets (214918_at) in Kaplan-Meier plotter (http://kmplot.com/analysis/).

Cell culture

The human CRC cell lines (RKO, HT29) and human embryonic kidney 293 T cells were cultured following our previous study [36]. All these cell lines were cultured in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. All these cell lines were recently authenticated by STR profiling. STR profiling and mycoplasma contamination were performed regularly to maintain the authenticity of the cell lines.

Cell growth, proliferation, and viability assays

Colony formation assays for RKO and HT29 cells were performed to determine cell viability. Low (3000 cells) and high (5000 cells) density of colon cancer cells were seeded in 6-well plates and incubated 8 to 10 days. Crystal violet (Beyotime Biotechnology) was used to stain the cell colonies after fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature. Four random areas for each group were counted to determine the colony numbers. Cell growth curves of each group were plotted by counting the cells after staining with 0.4% Trypan Blue Solution (YEASEN, 40207ES20). EdU staining (5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was used to measure the proliferation of colon cancer cells.

Stable knockdown colon cancer sub-cell lines

The stable colon cancer sub-cell lines were constructed by infecting the knockdown lentivirus expressing pLKO.1-puro in RKO and HT29 cells individually, which were selected with puromycin.

Xenograft tumor formation assay

The in vivo xenograft tumor formation assay was performed following our previous study [36]. 10 mice were used in Fig. 2, all mice were randomly allocated to 2 groups (sh-Luci, sh-hnRNPM) and each group contained 5 mice. 30 mice were used in Fig. 4, all mice were randomly allocated to 6 groups (sh-Luci, sh-hnRNPM, sh-PLEKHB2-S, sh-PLEKHB2-L, sh-hnRNPM+sh-PLEKHB2-S, and sh-hnRNPM+sh-PLEKHB2-L), and each group contained 5 mice. We were not blinded to the group allocation. 3 × 106 RKO cells expressing the indicated shRNA were subcutaneously injected at each dorsal flank into five-weeks old male BALB/c nude mice (CAVENS Laboratory Animal Company). All nude mice were maintained in specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions and all animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine (No. 202112A076).

RNA-seq analysis and GO analysis in CRC cells

Total RNA samples extracted from WT (si-NC) and KD (si-hnRNPM) of RKO cells were subjected to RNA sequencing using HiSeq X (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Data analysis for differentially spliced exons or introns was described previously [36]. For the analysis of differentially spliced genes, the alternative splicing (AS) quantification tool CASH was employed to characterize the AS landscape in hnRNPM-knockdown cells [37]. The raw data have been submitted to Gene Expression Omnibus with accession number GSE300639. Gene ontology (GO) analysis of hnRNPM-affected splicing events were analyzed using Database for Annotation Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID, https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) [38].

shRNAs, siRNAs, and plasmids

The shRNAs specifically targeting hnRNPM (sh-hnRNPM), PLEKHB2-Long isoform (sh- PLEKHB2-L), PLEKHB2-Short isoform (sh-PLEKHB2-S), and negative control (sh-Luci) were independently expressed in the knockdown lentiviruses vector PLKO.1-puro. The designed shRNAs were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China). The shRNA sequences were listed in Table S2. The siRNA oligos targeting SRSF1, SRSF2, SRSF3, SRSF5, SRSF6, SRSF7, SRSF9, SRSF10, hnRNPH1, hnRNPM, hnRNPK, and the negative control si-NC were designed following our previous study [36]. The sequences were listed in Table S4. The siRNAs were transfected into indicated cells using X-treme GENE siRNA Transfection Reagent (ROCHE). The hnRNPM plasmid and hnRNPM domain deletion mutants (hnRNPM-∆RRM1/∆RRM2/∆RRM3) were constructed into pCDNA3.0 vector containing HA tag.

Minigene construction

The PLEKHB2 minigene reporter was constructed into pCDNA3.1 vector using the genomic sequences spanning exons 7 to 9 and introns 7 to 8 of PLEKHB2 gene. The minigene was synthesized and amplified by Sangon Biotech (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China). Cells were transfected with the indicated siRNA oligos or overexpression plasmids and PLEKHB2 minigene, respectively.

Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

293 T cells were transfected with pCDNA.3.0-HA, hnRNPM-HA, and SRSF1-Flag plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher). Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay was performed using Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) Kit (MCE), Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (MCE) and Anti-HA tag antibody (Abcam, ab9110). Samples were analyzed by Western blot using Anti-HA tag antibody (Abcam, ab18181) and ASF/SF2 Monoclonal antibody (Proteintech, 66671-1-Ig).

Reverse transcriptase PCR (RT-PCR), quantitative reverse transcriptase PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNAs of cells were extracted using Trizol Reagent (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and quantified using NanoDrop2000. Reverse transcription from total RNAs was performed using M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI) and oligo-dT primers (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). The qRT-PCR was carried out using SYBR® Green Master Mix (Yeason, Shanghai, China). Primers used for RT-PCR and qRT-PCR analysis were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China) and listed in Table S6.

Western blot and in vivo crosslinking immunoprecipitation (CLIP)

Western blot analysis was performed as previously described [39]. The antibodies used in this study are listed in Table S3. In vivo crosslinking immunoprecipitation (CLIP) of PLEKHB2 pre-mRNA bound to hnRNPM proteins were carried out as described previously [2]. Briefly, hemagglutinin (HA)-tagged hnRNPM, domain deletion mutants of hnRNPM, and empty vector control were transfected into 293 T cells transiently, followed by the manufacturers’ instructions of Magna RIP kit (Millipore).

Motif analysis of hnRNPM-regulated alternative exons

Motif analysis for hnRNPM was performed as previously described [40]. Multiple Em for motif elicitation (MEME) was used to predict and characterize the preferential RNA binding motif(s) of hnRNPM [41]. (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme).

MaxEntScan score analyses

The MaxEntScan (MES) algorithm was applied to score the 9 bp sequence at the 5’ss and the 23 bp sequence at the 3’ss of introns flanking the alternative exons. The higher the MES score assigned to a given sequence, the stronger that sequence will be considered, as it will be more closely related to a real annotated splice site [42]. (http://hollywood.mit.edu/burgelab/maxent/Xmaxentscan_scoreseq.html).

Construction of cationic microbubbles (CMBs) and shRNA/CMBs

Cationic microbubbles (CMBs) were synthesized following the thin-film hydration method. DPPG (Dipalmitoyl phosphatidyl glycerol, MCE, China), DSPE-PEG2000 (1, 2-Distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-polyethylene glycol 2000, AVT, Shanghai, China), and Dlin-MC3-DMA (dilinoleylmethyl 4-(dimethylamino) butanoate, AVT, Shanghai, China) were dissolved in chloroform and mixed in a molar ratio of 7.5:0.5:2 following the previous study [43]. The mixture was dried using the rotary evaporator, and resuspended in 0.1 M Tris buffered saline (containing 10% glycerol and 10% propylene glycol) to a final concentration of 3 mg/mL. After being dispersed using an ultrasonic crusher at the lowest frequency, the solution was placed in light-safe glass vials and filled with SF6 gas in the top space. CMBs were obtained after blending using a vortex. The shRNA/CMBs were constructed using 60 μg shRNA and 120 μL CMBs, with 500 μL PBS solution. After incubation for 20 min at room temperature, the shRNA/CMBs were formed by charge-charge interactions. The prepared CMBs are stored at 4 °C and used in one week to ensure the stability of the liposome-nucleic acid complexes [27, 28]. The nanoparticle analyzer (Malvern Panalytical, England) was used to conduct multiple particle size analyses and potential measurements of CMBs and CMBs/shRNA samples.

Transduction by ultrasonic microbubble disruption

The shRNAs/CMBs (sh-Luci/CMBs, sh-hnRNPM/CMBs) at the concentration of 16 nM, 32 nM, 64 nM, and 80 nM were ultrasonically transduced into RKO cells respectively using the ultrasound diagnostic instrument. Ultrasound was performed 3 times at 1 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 1.5 W/cm2, each lasting 10 seconds. The interval between two adjacent ultrasounds is 30 seconds [27, 44].

Fluorescent staining and fluorescent photography of CMBs and shRNA/CMBs

We added 10 ul prepared CMBs or shRNA/CMBs to 1 ml Dil working solution (MCE, China) to the final concentration of 5 nM respectively and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. The Gel Green nucleic acid dye (Beyotime) was then added to the above mixtures and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. After the incubation, the mixtures were washed three times for further observation [27].

Intratumoral injections of shRNA/CMBs into tumors in xenograft mice

The in vivo xenograft tumor formation assay was performed following Xenograft tumor formation assay. 20 mice were randomly allocated to 4 groups and each group contained 5 mice. We were not blinded to the group allocation. 3 × 106 RKO cells were subcutaneously injected at each dorsal flank into five-week-old male BALB/c nude mice (CAVENS Laboratory Animal Company). When tumor volumes reached 125–150 mm3 (approximately 2 weeks after injection), the mice received intratumoral two-point injections of sh-Luci/CMBs (control), sh-hnRNPM/CMBs, sh-PLEKHB2-S/CMBs, or sh-PLEKHB2-L/CMBs (40 μL per tumor). Treatments of shRNA/CMBs were administered four times, followed immediately by ultrasound irradiation (1 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 1.5 W/cm2, 5 min) to trigger shRNA release from CMBs as described before [28, 45–49]. All nude mice were maintained in specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions and all animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine (No. 202112A076).

Quantification and statistical analysis of data

The quantification of Western blot and RT-PCR bands was performed using Image J software. Statistical analysis was performed using Student’s t-test.

Results

RNA-binding protein hnRNPM is upregulated in CRC patients

To profile the expression of RNA-binding protein hnRNPM in colorectal cancer tissues, we first detected the expression of hnRNPM in colorectal cancer tissue samples and the paired normal colorectal tissues from 22 clinical CRC patients (Table S1) using quantitative reverse transcriptase PCR (qRT-PCR). We found that hnRNPM mRNA expression was upregulated (>1.5-fold) in 11 (50.0%) colorectal cancer tissues compared with normal colorectal tissues, and hnRNPM mRNA expression was down-regulated (>1.5-fold) in only 3 (13.6%) colorectal cancer tissue samples compared to normal colorectal samples (Fig. 1A). Analysis of datasets from TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas, https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga) and GTEx V8 version (https://www.genome.gov/Funded-Programs-Projects/Genotype-Tissue-Expression-Project) including 789 normal colorectal tissues (normal) and 620 colorectal tumor tissues (tumor) showed that the expression of hnRNPM mRNA was significantly increased in CRC tissues compared to normal colorectal tissues (***p < 0.001) (Fig. 1B). In addition, in silico analysis of the datasets (214918_at) from the Kaplan-Meier plotter (http://kmplot.com/analysis/) revealed longer disease-free survival in CRC patients with low mRNA levels of hnRNPM than in patients with high hnRNPM mRNA levels (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1C).

To investigate whether hnRNPM mRNA was mutated in clinical samples, we mined somatic mutations in CRC samples from the TCGA database. The top 20 genes, including APC and TP53, were highly correlated with colorectal cancer, and were highly mutated in 556 human colorectal cancer samples. However, only 2% somatic mutation rate of hnRNPM was found in 556 CRC samples (Fig. S1). The lollipop plot illustrated the distribution of hnRNPM mutations (Fig. S2).

Together, these results strongly indicate that hnRNPM is increased in human CRC samples and predicted as a potential biomarker for colorectal cancer. Only 2% somatic mutation rate of hnRNPM happened in human CRC samples from the TCGA database.

Silencing of hnRNPM suppresses proliferation of colon cancer cells in vitro and in vivo

To investigate whether hnRNPM played vital roles in human colon cancer cells, three independent shRNAs including sh-Luci, sh-hnRNPM#1 and sh-hnRNPM#2 were synthesized and stably introduced into RKO or HT29 colon cancer cells individually. The efficacy of hnRNPM knockdown was confirmed through Western blot analysis (Fig. 2A). The silencing of hnRNPM significantly inhibited the colony formation in both RKO and HT29 colon cancer cells in vitro (Fig. 2B–D). Strikingly, hnRNPM knockdown reduced the growth of RKO and HT29 cells compared with the cells treated with control shRNA (Fig. 2E, Fig. S3A). Furthermore, the cell proliferating marker 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) was reduced significantly in RKO and HT29 cells knockdown of hnRNPM in comparison with the control cells (Fig. 2F, G, Fig. S3B, C).

To further validate the oncogenic potential of hnRNPM, the in vivo xenograft mice model was constructed. The RKO cells knockdown of hnRNPM and the control RKO cells were injected into nude mice individually. Compared with the control group, a significant decrease was observed in both tumor growth rate and tumor weight in hnRNPM knockdown groups, while there was no significant difference in body weights between the two groups of mice (Fig. 2H-K). Taken together, these in vitro and in vivo data confirmed that the reduction of hnRNPM suppressed the proliferation of colon cancer cells.

Landscape and validation of hnRNPM-affected alternative splicing events in colon cancer cells

As one of the splicing factors, hnRNPM plays vital roles in splicing regulation [7]. To profile the global alternative splicing (AS) events that might be regulated by hnRNPM, we performed RNA high-throughout sequencing (RNA-seq) on wild-type (WT) and hnRNPM knockdown (KD) RKO cells. The cDNA library was constructed after total RNA isolation and fragmentation from RKO cells subjected to si-NC and si-hnRNPM transfection. The alternative splicing events were screened and detected by sequencing using HiSeq X (Illumina) on a 150 bp double-end run (Fig. S4A). The knockdown efficiency of hnRNPM was confirmed by qRT-PCR (Fig. S4B), suggesting a relatively high knockdown efficiency. After appropriate optimization, a total of 55,002,320 (WT of 96. 4%) and 35,865,828 (hnRNPM-KD, 96.5%) mapped reads were generated. The rates of the unique mapped reads aligned to the reference genome were 88.8% for WT and 88.5% for hnRNPM-KD RKO cells, respectively (Table 1). Among the 115 significant AS events affected by hnRNPM knockdown (FDR < 0.05), there were 62 cassette events (skip exon), 6 Cassette_multi events (skip exon), 3 MXE events (mutually exclusive exons), 1 alternative 3’ A3SS event (alternative 3’ splice sites), 4 alternative 5’ A5SS events (alternative 3’ splice sites), 25 AltEnd events (alternative end exon), 13 AltStart events (selective start exon), and 1 IR event (retained introns) (Fig. 3A and Table S5). Overall, hnRNPM was involved in all common AS patterns in colon cancer cells, and the most affected events were cassette exons. In addition, the GO analysis of hnRNPM-regulated AS events (FDR < 0.05) using the DAVID website (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) demonstrated that the splicing targets affected by hnRNPM were associated with tumor-related functions, such as cell proliferation, cell migration, and CENP-A (centromere protein A) containing nucleosome assembly (Fig. 3B).

Knockdown of PLEKHB2-S splice variant suppresses proliferation of colon cancer cells in vitro and in vivo

PLEKHB2 (Pleckstrin homology domain containing B2), also known as evectin-2, is a vesicle protein containing PH domain (Pleckstrin homology domain) [17]. Evectin-2 knockdown was reported to reduce the proliferation of breast cancer cell [15]. However, studies on the involvement of PLEKHB2 in alternative splicing in CRC have not yet been clarified. PLEKHB2 consists of two splice isoforms, named PLEKHB2-L (Long, L) and PLEKHB2-S (Short, S), where PLEKHB2-L isoform includes exon 8 and the PLEKHB2-S isoform excludes exon 8 (Fig. 4A). The transcript NM_001267062.2 was selected as a reference for PLEKHB2-L which contains exon 8 (RefSeq ID was referred from NCBI). The expression patterns of PLEKHB2 were examined by RT-PCR analysis in 10 paired CRC samples with high hnRNPM mRNA expression. Strikingly, decreased inclusion of exon 8 in PLEKHB2 was observed in 8 of 10 CRC samples compared with normal tissues, and the representative RT-PCR images and the PSI values were shown (Fig. 4B). The changes in expression of alternative exon 8 in PLEKHB2 predicted as a potential target for CRC patients.

To identify whether PLEKHB2 splice variants are involved in the proliferation of colon cancer cells, we first designed the specific shRNAs targeting PLEKHB2-L/S splice isoforms respectively. The efficacy of PLEKHB2-L/S knockdown in RKO and HT29 cells were confirmed through RT-PCR analysis (Fig. 4C). The silencing of PLEKHB2-S splice variant strikingly inhibited the colony formation in both RKO and HT29 cells, while knockdown of PLEKHB2-L splice variant had almost no effects on the cell proliferation (Fig. 4D, E). Meanwhile, EdU marker was observed to be reduced significantly in RKO and HT29 cells after knockdown of PLEKHB2-S splice isoform (Fig. S5A–E). In summary, the expression of alternative exon 8 reduced in CRC samples and the knockdown of PLEKHB2-S splice variant suppressed the proliferation of colon cancer cells in vitro.

To further validate the oncogenic potential of PLEKHB2 in vivo, the xenograft mice model was constructed. The RKO cells stably knockdown of hnRNPM, PLEKHB2-S, PLEKHB2-L and the control RKO cells were injected into nude mice individually. Compared with the control group, a significant decrease was observed in both tumor size and tumor weight in hnRNPM or PLEKHB2-S knockdown groups, while there was neglectable difference in body weights between the two groups of mice (Fig. 4F–H). The knockdown of PLEKHB2-L also had a slight reduction effect in tumor size and tumor weight, but its oncogenic potential seemed significantly weaker than that of hnRNPM or PLEKHB2-S splice isoform. Moreover, the double knockdown of hnRNPM and PLEKHB2-S, hnRNPM and PLEKHB2-L in vivo were performed respectively and showed significant reduction in tumor size and tumor weight compared with control group. However, we observed no significant differences between the hnRNPM/PLEKHB2 double knockdown group (KD-hnRNPM + KD-PLEKHB2-S/L) and the hnRNPM single knockdown group (KD-hnRNPM), indicating that, in addition to PLEKHB2, hnRNPM also regulates other targets to modulate tumor initiation and development. Taken together, these in vitro and in vivo data confirmed that the knockdown of hnRNPM, PLEKHB2-S or double knockdown of hnRNPM and PLEKHB2 suppressed the proliferation of colon cancer cells.

Previous studies had shown that PLEKHB2 knockdown inhibited the activation of YAP (Yes-associated protein) by circulating endosomes (REs) and then restrained cancer cell proliferation through phosphorylation degradation of YAP [15]. To further explore the roles of PLEKHB2 and hnRNPM in biological pathways within colon cancer cells, we searched for the targets of YAP in the database of human transcription factor targets (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/hTFtarget/#!/) and predicted several tumor-related genes that might be regulated by YAP. The qRT-PCR results showed that the knockdown of hnRNPM or PLEKHB2-S inhibited the mRNA expression of CCNT2 (cell cycle-related protein T2), ETS1 (ETS proto-oncogene 1) and VEZF1 (Vascular Endothelial Zinc Finger 1) significantly (Fig. S6A–C), while the knockdown of PLEKHB2-L had little impact on these targets. These results indicated potential roles of CCNT2, ETS1, and VEZF1 in colon cancer cells and predicted the possible pathway that hnRNPM and PLEKHB2-S splice isoform play in colon cancer cells.

Insights into splicing mechanism of PLEKHB2 by hnRNPM regulation

To further identify the splicing regulators involved in PLEKHB2 splicing, the depletions of some SR and hnRNP proteins were performed in RKO cells using specific siRNAs. The specific primers of PLEKHB2 were designed to detect alternative exon 8 by RT-PCR, and the PSI (percent of splicing in) of each RNA product was quantified using Image J software.

The percent of exon 8 inclusion in PLEKHB2 was about 33% (Lane 1 in Fig. 5A, B) in untreated RKO cells (si-NC). Depletion of SRSF1 or hnRNPM significantly increased exon 8 inclusion in PLEKHB2 (Fig. 5A, compare lane 2 or lane 11 with lane 1, Fig. 5B), while depletion of the other splicing regulators, including SRSF2, SRSF3, SRSF5, SRSF6, SRSF7, SRSF9, SRSF10, hnRNPH1 and hnRNPK had almost no impact on exon 8 inclusion in PLEKHB2 (Fig. 5A, B). Furthermore, we found that the knockdown of SRSF1 could increase exon 8 inclusion in PLEKHB2 to 45% (Fig. 5A, compare lane 2 with lane 1, Fig. 5B), and the knockdown of hnRNPM improved the inclusion of exon 8 in PLEKHB2 to 57% (Fig. 5A, compare lane 11 with lane 1, Fig. 5B). In total, PLEKHB2 exon 8 is subject to the regulation of splicing factors SRSF1 and hnRNPM, and both SRSF1 and hnRNPM could suppress exon 8 inclusion in PLEKHB2.

Moreover, qRT-PCR analysis was performed to examine the mRNA expression levels of total PLEKHB2 mRNA, PLEKHB2-L, and PLEKHB2-S in PLEKHB2-S, PLEKHB2-L, or hnRNPM knockdown cells. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 7, we observed that knockdown of hnRNPM, PLEKHB2-S, or PLEKHB2-L did not significantly alter total PLEKHB2 mRNA expression (Fig. S7A). However, knockdown of hnRNPM or PLEKHB2-S markedly suppressed PLEKHB2-S mRNA expression, whereas knockdown of PLEKHB2-L triggered a significant compensatory increase in PLEKHB2-S mRNA levels (Fig. S7B). Notably, knockdown of hnRNPM or PLEKHB2-S significantly enhanced PLEKHB2-L mRNA expression, while knockdown of PLEKHB2-L strongly reduced its own mRNA levels (Fig. S7C). Collectively, the knockdown of hnRNPM, PLEKHB2-L, or PLEKHB2-S did not alter total PLEKHB2 mRNA levels. Based on these findings, we conclude that the alternative exon 8 in PLEKHB2 plays a critical role in colorectal cancer cells.

To characterize the functional interplay between SRSF1 and hnRNPM in regulating PLEKHB2 splicing, we performed co-transfection of si-SRSF1 and si-hnRNPM in RKO cells and analyzed exon inclusion via RT-PCR (Fig. S8A). The results demonstrated that exon 8 inclusion was significantly higher in co-transfected cells compared to those with single knockdown of either hnRNPM or SRSF1, suggesting a potential synergistic interaction between the two factors in promoting exon retention. However, the Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay revealed no detectable protein-protein interaction between SRSF1 and hnRNPM, ruling out a direct physical association as the basis for their synergism (Fig. S8B). Collectively, these data establish hnRNPM as the primary splicing regulator of PLEKHB2, with its effect on exon 8 inclusion being markedly more pronounced than that of SRSF1. Importantly, the lack of a direct interaction implies that their synergistic regulation of PLEKHB2 splicing may rely on a more complex, indirect mechanism.

To investigate whether SRSF1 and hnRNPM regulate the splicing of PLEKHB2 in vitro, we constructed a minigene reporter containing exons 7-9 with intervening introns. Co-transfection of siRNAs or overexpression plasmids with PLEKHB2 minigene in 293 T cells showed that hnRNPM knockdown enhanced inclusion of alternative exon 8, while the overexpression of hnRNPM induced exon 8 skipping. Similarly, SRSF1 knockdown enhanced exon 8 inclusion, and overexpression of SRSF1 increased exon 8 skipping, although its effect was weaker than hnRNPM’s (Fig. 5C). These results confirm the predominant regulatory roles of hnRNPM in PLEKHB2 splicing.

To characterize hnRNPM-regulated exons in CRC, we analyzed their length, GC content, and splice acceptor/donor site strength. The length of exons regulated by hnRNPM in CRC range from 42 to 135 bp, with GC content ranging from 42.96% to 62.50%. The downstream introns vary in length from 559 to 2187 bp, with GC content ranging from 38.32% to 56.58% (Table S7). The strength of donor and acceptor splice sites was evaluated using MaxEntScan [42] (Table S8). Statistical analysis of the donor and acceptor splice sites around the alternative exons reveals that, although no statistically significant differences were observed, the mean scores of the donor or acceptor sites flanking hnRNPM-regulated exons are lower than those of adjacent donor or acceptor sites (Fig. S9A). This suggests that these exons are more likely to undergo alternative splicing, potentially leading to exon skipping. The MEME analysis tool (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme) was used to investigate the distribution of hnRNPM-binding motifs in 7 validated targets. As shown in the Fig. S9B, the binding motif of hnRNPM to the targets is CUU(C)U(G)UC(G)AC(U) in the downstream constitutive exon following the alternative exon, where the proportion of U is ≥ 50%, consistent with hnRNPM’s reported preference for G/U-enriched regions [50–52]. This analysis indicates that hnRNPM promotes exclusion of alternative exons by binding to this motif in downstream constitutive exons, thereby enabling construction of an RNA map for predicting hnRNPM splicing regulatory targets.

As hnRNPM has the typical structure of hnRNP family, with three RNA recognition domains including RRM1 domain, RRM2 domain, and RRM3 domain from N-terminal to C-terminal in hnRNPM protein [9, 53]. The HA-tagged hnRNPM (hnRNPM-HA) plasmid was constructed in pCDNA3.0 plasmid. Based on the hnRNPM-HA plasmid, the RRM1 deletion (hnRNPM-ΔRRM1-HA), RRM2 deletion (hnRNPM-ΔRRM2-HA), and RRM3 deletion (hnRNPM-ΔRRM3-HA) mutants were constructed respectively (Fig. 5D). To confirm whether hnRNPM protein bind to PLEKHB2 pre-mRNA, we transiently overexpressed hnRNPM-HA, the RRM1, RRM2 or RRM3 deletion mutants in 293 T cells. The vector-HA plasmid was also transfected into 293 T cells as control to rule out the experimental artifact caused by HA antigen. Western blot analysis was performed to confirm the overexpression efficacy of the indicated plasmids (Fig. 5E).

The CLIP assay (in vivo crosslinking followed by immunoprecipitation) and RT-PCR, qRT-PCR analysis were carried out with specific primers for the indicated exons of PLEKHB2. Both RT-PCR gel results and qRT-PCR analysis showed that hnRNPM-HA and the RRM1/RRM3 deletion mutants (hnRNPM-ΔRRM1-HA, hnRNPM-ΔRRM3-HA) were observed to bind with exon 9 in PLEKHB2 pre-mRNA with significantly high affinity. In contrast, the RRM2 deletion mutant (hnRNPM-ΔRRM2-HA) exhibited weaker binding to the constitutive exon 9. Moreover, hnRNPM-HA and all deletion mutants (hnRNPM-ΔRRM1-HA, hnRNPM-ΔRRM2-HA, hnRNPM-ΔRRM3-HA) showed weak or negligible binding affinity for exons 7 and 8 (Fig. 5F–H). These results indicated that hnRNPM primarily has high affinity for downstream constitutive exons. Collectively, the data suggest that splicing regulator hnRNPM predominantly binds to exon 9 of PLEKHB2 pre-mRNA via the RRM2 domain.

Validation of the characteristic and binding ratio of shRNA/CMBs

In the above study, shRNAs were used for gene silencing for the advantages such as high specificity, high persistence, and easy modification [54, 55]. However, lentiviral transfection suffers potential safety risks of virus leakage, insertion mutagenesis, long experiment cycle and in vivo immune response [56–59]. In recent years, the positively charged cationic microbubbles (CMBs) have been developed to deliver RNA by electrostatic interaction with their loaded nucleic acids on the surface [21, 59–62]. Ultrasound-targeted microbubble disruption (UTMD) could effectively transfer CMBs loaded with nucleic acid in vivo and in vitro with the advantages of low cytotoxicity, high transfection efficiency, easy operation and good reproducibility [27, 30, 31].

Previous studies [53] and our own data collectively establish hnRNPM as a potential therapeutic target in colorectal cancer. In order to develop liposomes targeted hnRNPM with low toxicity and high transfection efficiency, CMBs were synthesized using DPPG (dipalmitoyl phosphatidylglycerol), DSPE-PEG2000 (1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-polyethylene glycol 2000), and ionizable lipids Dlin-MC3-DMA (dilinoleylmethyl-4-(dimethylamino)butanoate) by thin-film hydration method. The shRNA/CMBs were formed using the prepared CMBs incubated with the indicated shRNAs (sh-Luci or sh-hnRNPM). Subsequently, we transferred shRNA/CMBs into RKO cells by UTMD (Fig. 6A). The appearance of the prepared CMBs and shRNA/CMBs were shown in Fig. 6B. To verify whether the construction of CMBs was successful, we observed the morphology of CMBs using an optical microscope. The results showed that the size of the CMBs is about 1 μm (Fig. 6C). Since Dil (dieldrin) is a marker dye for liposomes, the CMBs were confirmed to be stained red using fluorescence microscopy, indicating the successful construction of CMBs (Fig. 6D). These results indicated that the CMBs were successfully constructed and the size of the CMBs were about 1 μm.

The anti-tumor efficacy of shRNA/CMBs in vitro and in vivo

To investigate whether sh-hnRNPM/CMBs, sh-PLEKHB2-S/CMBs and sh-PLEKHB2-L/CMBs play an important role in colon cancer cells, RKO cells were treated with sh-hnRNPM/CMBs, sh-PLEKHB2-S/CMBs, sh-PLEKHB2-L/CMBs or sh-Luci/CMBs at concentrations of 16 nM, 32 nM, 64 nM, and 80 nM. We found that 32 nM, 64 nM, and 80 nM sh-hnRNPM/CMBs significantly reduced hnRNPM mRNA expression by more than 2 times (Fig. S10A). Similarly, 64 nM sh-PLEKHB2-S/CMBs or sh-PLEKHB2-L/CMBs significantly reduced PLEKHB2-S or PLEKHB2-L mRNA expression by more than 2 times (Fig. S10B, C). The shRNA/CMBs at 80 nM resulted in greater cytotoxicity of RKO with visible cell death. Compared with sh-Luci/CMBs, sh-hnRNPM/CMBs and sh-PLEKHB2-S/CMBs at 64 nM significantly reduced the colony formation of RKO cells, while sh-PLEKHB2-L/CMBs also exhibited slight suppression in the proliferation of RKO cells (Fig. 7A−D). To evaluate the anti-tumor efficacy of shRNA/CMBs in vivo, we performed intratumoral injections and UTMD treatment in tumor-bearing nude mice (Fig. 7E, F). Compared with the sh-Luci/CMBs group, significant reductions in tumor size and tumor weight were observed in the sh-hnRNPM/CMBs and sh-PLEKHB2-S/CMBs treatment groups, while sh-PLEKHB2-S/CMBs also exhibited slight suppression in tumor growth (Fig. 7G, H). Moreover, there was no significant difference in the body weights of the nude mice among these mice (Fig. 7I). These data predicted that sh-hnRNPM/CMBs and the splicing target sh-PLEKHB2-S/CMBs as potential drugs for the treatment of colorectal cancer. Based on the above results, we successfully introduced sh-RNA/CMBs into colon cancer cells in vitro and in vivo, transferred by UTMD. We screened the optimal concentration range, and predicted that sh-hnRNPM/CMBs and the splicing target sh-PLEKHB2-S/CMBs as potential drugs for the treatment of colorectal cancer.

Discussion

Our work presented a comprehensive study of hnRNPM and profiled its downstream alternative splicing events in CRC. We first revealed that hnRNPM was significantly upregulated in clinical CRC samples. Multiple splicing events regulated by hnRNPM knockdown were identified in colon cancer cells through transcriptome analysis. Different PLEKHB2 splice variants with distinct functions regulated by hnRNPM were generated with distinct functions in cancer. The knockdown of PLEKHB2-S splice isoform or its splicing regulator hnRNPM inhibited the proliferation of colon cancer cells. Moreover, a more hypotoxic sh-hnRNPM/CMBs were constructed and transferred into colon cancer cells by UTMD method. Our data indicated the optimal concentration range of shRNA/CMBs and proved the effectiveness of sh-hnRNPM/CMBs and sh-PLEKHB2-S/CMBs in CRC. These findings predicted sh-hnRNPM/CMBs and its downstream PLEKHB2-S splice isoform as promising therapeutic targets in colorectal cancer development.

As a tumor-related splicing factor, hnRNPM was demonstrated to play crucial roles in a variety of tumors and regulate many splice variants [7, 11–14]. To profile the correlation between hnRNPM mRNA expression and prognosis in CRC patients, in silico analysis of the datasets (using 214918_at probe) from the Kaplan-Meier plotter was performed and revealed longer disease-free survival in CRC patients with lower hnRNPM expression. Actually, there are three specific probes in the database from Kaplan-Meier plotter. To explain the differences among the specific probes for hnRNPM mRNA from kmplotter.com, we delved into the regions of hnRNPM which are targeted by these probes. The sequence of probe 214918_at targets the junction region between eoxn 7 and intron 7 of hnRNPM pre-mRNA, while the sequence of probe 200072_s_at targets exons 14, 15, and 16 of hnRNPM mRNA. As for the probe 1555844_s_at, unfortunately, we couldn’t find any sequence information from the GEO database or the official website of the CHIP manufacturer. Indeed, the survival analysis using 200072_s_at and 1555844_s_at probes showed that the high expression of hnRNPM correlates with better OS and RFS in CRC patients. In addition, we analyzed the GEPIA database, which is based on the RNA-seq results from TCGA and GTEx. We analyzed the correlation between hnRNPM mRNA expression and the prognosis of colorectal cancer patients in the GEPIA database. It was found that there was no significant difference in the OS (overall survival) of CRC patients between high and the low hnRNPM expression groups (data not shown). When analyzing the RFS of CRC patients in high and low hnRNPM expression groups, it was found that the survival rate of CRC patients with high hnRNPM expression group decreased significantly after 80 months (data not shown). Different databases have great differences in the correlation analysis between hnRNPM mRNA expression and the prognosis of CRC patients. In our manuscript, only 22 pairs of CRC samples were analyzed in the manuscript and all 22 samples were obtained in recent years, and the status of patients used in this manuscript was alive now, so now we couldn’t conclude that hnRNPM predicts prognosis. We believe that additional clinical samples are required to confirm the association between hnRNPM expression and colorectal cancer (CRC) prognosis, whether via existing database analyses or our experimental validations.

AS a well-studied splicing factor, hnRNPM regulated AS events in a position-dependent manner and played essential roles in multiple tumors development [50–52, 63]. Our transcriptome sequencing and RT-PCR validation revealed that hnRNPM mainly promote exon skipping events in colon cancer cells. Supportively, previous studies showed that exon skipping is the most common AS event regulated by hnRNPM in multiple tumors [7, 13, 64]. Previous studies have shown that hnRNPM protein binds to G/U rich consensus motif in downstream pre-mRNAs [50–52]. In our study, motif enrichment analysis on seven validated splicing targets revealed a U-rich binding motif (CUU(C)U(G)UC(G)AC(U)) in exon 9, with U content ≥50%, which is consistent with the reported G/U-rich preferences for hnRNPM. Similar to previous study [63], our CLIP assay confirmed that the RRM2 domain in hnRNPM protein binds to the constitutive exon 9 of PLEKHB2 pre-mRNA directly and activates the alternative exon 8 exclusion. These findings confirmed the direct regulation by hnRNPM in PLEKHB2 alternative splicing.

Furthermore, we observed that SRSF1 also affected the AS of PLEKHB2 significantly, predicting the possible combinative regulation of SRSF1 and hnRNPM in the AS of PLEKHB2. Supportively, hnRNPM was reported to interact with other splicing factors such as RBFOX2 and FMRP and lead to the activation of exon inclusion or exclusion [64–66]. These findings reflected the cooperative or competitive link between SR and hnRNP proteins [10, 67]. Our results demonstrated that exon 8 inclusion in PLEKHB2 was significantly higher in si-SRSF1 and si-hnRNPM co-transfected cells compared to those with single knockdown of either hnRNPM or SRSF1, suggesting a potential synergistic interaction between the two splicing factors in promoting exon retention. However, Co-IP experiments revealed no detectable protein-protein interaction between SRSF1 and hnRNPM, ruling out a direct physical association as the basis for their synergism. Importantly, the lack of a direct interaction implies that their synergistic regulation of PLEKHB2 splicing may rely on a more complex, indirect mechanism (e.g., cooperative binding to RNA, crosstalk with other splicing factors, or modulation of transcriptional output). Moreover, previous pan-cancer analysis revealed a high mutation rate up to 14% of hnRNPM in endometrial cancer (UCEC) [68]. However, only 2% somatic mutation rate of hnRNPM were found in CRC samples. This strongly indicated that aberrant high expression of hnRNPM but not the mutations in hnRNPM was the major factor in CRC development.

Our data identified that hnRNPM was highly expressed in CRC tumors and promoted the proliferation of colon cancer cells in vitro and in vivo. Supportively, previous studies showed the crucial roles of hnRNPM in cancer cell growth and survival [69]. More importantly, we noted that PLEKHB2-S splice variant shared similar proliferation-promoting functions in colon cancer cells. Supportively, studies showed that PLEKHB2 promoted the proliferation of breast cancer cells [15, 17] through inhibiting YAP phosphorylation and increased downstream CTGF mRNA expression in breast cancer cells. PLEKHB2, also known as Evt2, is a vesicular protein that carries a pleckstrin homology domain (PH domain) as the primary functional domain and a hydrophobic region (CT motif) [17]. The PH domain of Evt2 recognizes and binds to phosphoserine and is involved in Evt2’s localization of REs. The CT motif is an essential part in membrane recognition and plays key roles in retrograde membrane transport pathways from the plasma membrane to the Golgi in endosomal membrane [15]. We checked the length of Evt2 in NCBI and found that the cDNA used in previous study [17] was 666 bp (NM_017958.3) which is lack of exon 8. In our study, we proved that the alternative exon 8 in PLEKHB2 play key roles in proliferation of colon cancer cells. The sequence of exon 8 was checked and verified to locate between PH domain and CT motif which is in the adjacent region of CT motif. These observations suggest complicated mechanism and function of PLEKHB2. Our findings confirmed the vital roles of exon 8 in PLEKHB2 in colon cancer progression. Furthermore, we examined the dramatic changes in expression of ETS1, CCNT2, and VEZF1 after hnRNPM or PLEKHB2-S knockdown. These downstream targets regulated by YAP were screened in the website (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/hTFtarget/#!/). Our observations suggest the potential roles of brand-new hnRNPM-PLEKHB2-YAP-ETS1/CCNT2/VEZF1 alternative splicing axis in colorectal cancer.

Moreover, to avoid the shortcomings of lentivirus transfection such as high cytotoxicity, strong cellular immune response, cationic liposome Dlin-MC3-DMA and phospholipid were used to construct cationic microbubbles to coat shRNA [27, 28]. Microbubbles are the latest non-viral vectors capable of carrying nucleic acids and then successfully delivering genes into tumors, myocardium, and bone or other organs by ultrasound-targeted microbubbles destruction (UTMD) [30, 70–72]. UTMD has been maturely applied in treating myocardial infarction and alleviating myocardial inflammation. Sufficient ultrasonic energy increases the permeability of cell membrane and improves the efficiency of gene transfection through the cavitation and pore effects of ultrasound [28, 30, 31]. In our study, we observed the average particle size of CMBs and shRNA/CMBs were 949.7 ± 34.27 nm and 1676.75 ± 126.05 nm, respectively. The average Zeta potentials of CMBs and shRNA/CMBs respectively were 4.85 ± 2.01 mV and -22.89 ± 1.16 mV. Supportively, previous studies found that the particle size and Zeta potential of CMBs were generally between 400 nm ~ 1000 nm and +4 mv ~ +60 mv, but different CMBs have distinct particle size and Zeta potential, which are related to specific experimental conditions and lipid composition [27, 70, 72]. UTMD was reported to break down barrier structures, promote drug delivery, gene transfection, and sensitise cancer vaccines [30]. Microbubbles and UTMD-mediated delivery of siRNA are more effective to reduce the target gene expression in tumor [25]. Similar to previous study, our data revealed that shRNA/CMBs significantly inhibited the proliferation of RKO cells and reduced the expression of hnRNPM, which further confirmed the effectiveness of UTMD in gene transfection promotion. Furthermore, our data indicated that the knockdown of shRNA/CMBs at a concentration ≤ 64 nM showed a dose-dependent tendency to inhibit hnRNPM expression. Related study showed that PLGA-PEG (si-circROBO1) NPs could inhibit the expression of circROBO1 at the dose of 36 nM to 60 nM [44]. These findings predicted possible suitable concentration of CMBs in cells. Moreover, we observed a large number of cell deaths at concentrations ≥ 100 nM of shRNA/CMBs, while there were no obvious cell deaths at concentrations ≤ 80 nM. Supportively, in vivo studies proved that PEG, as one constituent of CMBs, might increase the risk of thrombosis [73]. Our observations indicated that high concentration of CMBs may affect the biocompatibility and stability in vitro and in vivo. In summary, our preliminary data validate the potential application of sh-hnRNPM/CMBs and sh-PLEKHB2-S/CMBs in colon cancer cells, while the drug loading ratio and long-term biosafety still need to be optimized in colorectal cancer research.

Conclusion

Our findings were summarized in Graphical Abstract. We observed high expression of hnRNPM in CRC samples. The RNA-seq and alternative splicing analysis revealed that hnRNPM mainly activated the exon exclusion events. PLEKHB2-S splice isoform was proved to be activated by hnRNPM. Our data provided evidence that the hnRNPM protein binded with the constitutive exon 9 of PLEKHB2 via its RRM2 domain. The knockdown of hnRNPM or its cancer-associated splicing target PLEKHB2-S isoform led to a reduction in the malignant proliferation of colon cancer cells in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, we developed cationic microbubbles sh-RNA/CMBs and transferred them to colon cancer cells via ultrasound-targeted microbubble disruption. Our findings identified the size and zeta potentials of CMBs and shRNA-CMBs. We screened the optimal concentration range of shRNA/CMBs with low cytotoxicity and found that the introduction of sh-hnRNPM/CMBs or sh-PLEKHB2-S inhibited the proliferation of colon cancer cells in vitro and in vivo. Our findings predicted that sh-hnRNPM/CMBs and its splicing target sh-PLEKHB2-S/CMBs serve as promising therapeutic drugs and innovative strategies for treating colorectal cancer.
