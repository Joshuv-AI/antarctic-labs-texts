Habitat-driven variation in gut microbiome composition and function of the pygmy grasshopper (Tetrix japonica) across diverse ecosystems in China
Authors: Yuemei Li, Le Liu, Min Long, Delong Guan, Weian Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe gut microbiome plays an important role in insect adaptation, yet how habitat variation shapes microbial communities in pygmy grasshoppers remains unclear. We investigated this question using Tetrix japonica, which inhabits diverse ecosystems across China and provides an ideal model to study microbiome-environment interactions. Shotgun metagenomic sequencing was performed on gut samples from six populations representing coniferous forest, broadleaf forests in Shandong and Shaanxi, grassland, shrubland, and laboratory-reared populations.ResultsMicrobial diversity and community composition varied significantly among habitats, with field populations exhibiting higher diversity than laboratory-reared ones. Despite environmental differences, a core microbiome comprising 1,162 shared species was consistently detected, suggesting stable symbiotic relationships. Habitat-specific microbial signatures were most evident between forest and grassland populations, with lignocellulose-degrading taxa enriched in forest habitats. Moreover, geographic separation between Shandong and Shaanxi broadleaf forests led to distinct microbial profiles despite similar vegetation. Functional analysis revealed differential enrichment of genes related to plant polymer degradation, nitrogen cycling, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis across habitats.ConclusionThese findings demonstrate that both habitat conditions and geography influence gut microbiome assembly in T. japonica, with microbiome plasticity facilitating adaptation. The reduced diversity observed in laboratory populations highlights the importance of natural habitats for maintaining functional microbiome integrity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12725-8.Keywords: Gut microbiome, Tetrix japonica, Metagenomics, Habitat variation, Insect-microbe interactions, Environmental adaptation

Introduction

The gut microbiome plays fundamental roles in insect physiology, ecology, and evolution, contributing to host nutrition, development, immunity, and environmental adaptation [1–3]. Understanding how environmental factors shape insect-associated microbial communities has become increasingly important for elucidating the mechanisms underlying insect adaptation to diverse ecological niches [4, 5]. Environmentally derived microbes have been proposed to contribute to host adaptive potential, emphasizing the role of habitat-associated microbiota in shaping ecological differentiation [6]. This knowledge is particularly relevant in the context of global environmental changes, where insects must rapidly adjust to shifting habitats and resource availability [7].

Pygmy grasshoppers (Orthoptera: Tetrigidae) represent ecologically important insects that inhabit diverse terrestrial ecosystems worldwide [8]. Unlike many orthopterans that are predominantly herbivorous, pygmy grasshoppers display a more omnivorous feeding strategy, with diets primarily consisting of mosses, algae, lichens, and plant detritus [9–11]. This dietary distinctiveness is expected to shape their gut microbiota in unique ways, as microbial symbionts may play crucial roles in breaking down recalcitrant plant polymers, processing detrital organic matter, detoxifying secondary metabolites, and synthesizing essential nutrients [12–14]. Previous studies have revealed that grasshopper gut microbial communities can vary significantly across species, populations, and environmental conditions [12, 13, 15]. However, most research has focused on pest species with strict herbivorous diets, leaving the microbiome ecology of non-pest and omnivorous pygmy grasshoppers largely unexplored.

Environmental heterogeneity is recognized as a major driver of microbial community assembly in host-associated systems [16, 17]. For herbivorous insects, factors such as host plant diversity, vegetation structure, climate conditions, and geographic barriers can all influence the composition and function of gut microbiota [18, 19]. The relative importance of these factors and their interactive effects on shaping insect gut microbiomes remain poorly understood, particularly across natural habitat gradients.

Tetrix japonica Bolivar, 1887 is a representative species of pygmy grasshoppers, which provides an excellent model system for investigating microbiome-environment interactions. In contrast to many pest grasshoppers that primarily consume grasses, T. japonica exhibits a specialized omnivorous diet dominated by detrital organic matter and mosses, reflecting its remarkable ecological plasticity [10]. Such a feeding strategy may require distinct microbial functions to degrade non-vascular plant tissues and decomposed organic substrates, making its gut microbiome particularly intriguing. Despite its wide distribution and ecological importance, the gut microbiome of T. japonica has never been systematically characterized.

Chinese diverse topography and climate create distinct ecological zones that support T. japonica populations under varying environmental conditions. From the subtropical forests of Guangxi to the temperate ecosystems of Shandong and the transitional zones of Shaanxi, these regions encompass gradients in temperature, precipitation, vegetation composition, and elevation [20]. Such environmental heterogeneity provides a natural laboratory for examining how habitat variation influences gut microbiome assembly and function in wild insect populations.

Laboratory-reared insects often experience significant shifts in their gut microbiota compared to wild populations, potentially affecting their physiology and behavior [9, 21, 22]. Understanding these differences is crucial for interpreting laboratory-based studies and assessing the ecological relevance of captive-rearing practices. Moreover, comparing wild and laboratory populations can help identify environmentally acquired versus vertically transmitted microbial symbionts.

In this study, we employed shotgun metagenomic sequencing to characterize the gut microbiome of T. japonica across six distinct habitats in China, including coniferous forest, broadleaf forests, grassland, shrubland, and laboratory-reared populations. Our objectives were to describe the taxonomic and functional diversity of the T. japonica gut microbiome for the first time. We will assess how different habitat types influence microbial community composition and metabolic potential. We aim to identify core microbial taxa that persist across diverse environments and compare the gut microbiomes of wild and laboratory-reared populations to understand the impact of captivity on microbial diversity. We hypothesized that habitat-specific environmental factors would significantly shape gut microbial communities, with forest habitats supporting more diverse microbiomes due to greater plant diversity and structural complexity.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and preparation

Adult specimens of the T. japonica were collected from six distinct habitats across China between June and September of 2023 and 2024. The sampling sites included: coniferous forest (CF), broadleaf forest in Shandong Province (BF_SD), broadleaf forest in Shanxi Province (BF_SX), grassland (GL), shrubland (SL), and a laboratory-reared population (LAB) serving as a control. The field collections spanned three provinces (Guangxi, Shaanxi, and Shandong) across an altitudinal gradient from 155 to 1,638 m.

For each field habitat, pygmy grasshoppers were collected using sweep nets. A pooled sample, consisting of 30 adult females and 30 adult males, was considered one biological replicate, with three replicates collected per habitat. The laboratory population (LAB) was maintained under controlled conditions (25 ± 2 °C, 60 ± 10% RH, 14:10 h L: D photoperiod) for three generations. To minimize dietary effects, individuals were fed fresh mosses, algae, lichens, and plant detritus directly collected from their native wild habitat, ensuring that the diet composition was highly comparable to that of wild populations. For the lab-reared group, three replicate pools were prepared, each consisting of 25 females and 25 males. This feeding regimen was specifically designed to minimize dietary differences between laboratory and wild populations, ensuring that any observed microbiome differences could be more confidently attributed to captivity-related factors rather than dietary simplification.

All live specimens collected from the field were immediately transported to the laboratory. Within 48 h, the entire gut was aseptically dissected from each individual under sterile conditions, using sterile fine-tipped forceps. The external surface of each dissected gut was briefly rinsed with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove any adhering hemolymph before pooling. Guts from individuals within the same replicate pool were combined, and the pooled gut contents were stored at -80 °C until DNA extraction.

DNA extraction, library construction, and sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from the pooled gut samples using the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration and purity (A260/A280 ratio) were determined using a NanoDrop2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The integrity of the extracted DNA was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis.

High-quality DNA was sheared to an average fragment size of approximately 300 bp using a Covaris M220 focused-ultrasonicator. Paired-end (PE) metagenomic libraries were prepared following the Illumina standard protocol. This involved: (1) end-repair and A-tailing of DNA fragments; (2) ligation of Y-shaped sequencing adaptors; (3) removal of adaptor-dimers using magnetic beads; and (4) PCR amplification to enrich the library templates. The enriched libraries were subjected to bridge PCR on an Illumina flow cell to generate DNA clusters. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform with a PE150 (2 × 150 bp) strategy.

Metagenome data processing and quality control

Raw sequencing reads in FASTQ format were processed for quality control using fastp software [23]. The following steps were applied: (1) low-quality bases were trimmed using a 50 bp sliding window, with reads being truncated at the 5’ end of the window if the average quality score dropped below 20; (2) reads shorter than 50 bp after trimming were discarded; and (3) reads containing ambiguous nucleotides (‘N’) were removed. To remove host contamination, the high-quality reads were aligned to a relevant grasshopper reference genome using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) [24]. Only the unmapped paired-end reads were retained for downstream microbial analysis.

De novo assembly and non-redundant gene catalog construction

For each sample, the high-quality, non-host reads were independently assembled into contigs using MEGAHIT [25]. Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted from all contigs longer than 300 bp using Prodigal [26]. All predicted protein sequences from all samples were pooled and clustered using MMseqs2 [27] at a 95% sequence identity and 90% coverage threshold to construct a non-redundant gene catalog.

Taxonomic and functional annotation and abundance profiling

The abundance of each gene in the catalog was calculated by mapping the high-quality reads from each sample back to the catalog using Bowtie 2 [28] and quantifying the mapped reads. For taxonomic and functional annotation, the protein sequences of the non-redundant gene catalog were aligned against the NCBI Non-Redundant (NR) protein database, the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and the eggNOG database using DIAMOND [29].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and data visualizations were conducted in R (v4.2.1). Alpha diversity metrics, including the Shannon, Simpson, and Chao1 indices, were used to evaluate species richness and evenness. Beta diversity was assessed based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrices and visualized using Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA). Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was performed using the adonis function in the vegan package (v2.6-4) to test for significant differences in community structure among habitats. Differentially abundant taxa and functions were identified using the Kruskal-Wallis test, with post-hoc analysis where appropriate. P-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The R packages phyloseq (v1.38.0), tidyverse (v1.3.2), and ggpubr (v0.5.0) were used for data handling and visualization(https://github.com/kassambara/ggpubr). Data visualization employed online Personalbio (https://www.genescloud.cn/home) and OmicStudio [30] platforms.

Results

Taxonomic composition of the gut microbiome varies across habitats

Following quality filtering, approximately 114 Gb of high-quality, non-host data from the 18 samples was retained for de novo assembly (Table S1). Valid reads per sample ranged from 1.29 to 56.59 million. Despite variation in sequencing depth, overall coverage was sufficient for comparative diversity and functional analyses. The quantity and diversity of the assembled microbial data varied significantly across habitats (Table S2). Notably, samples from broadleaf forests (BF_SD and BF_SX) yielded substantially larger and more contiguous assemblies, corresponding with the highest taxonomic richness. For instance, BF_SD exhibited 59 phyla and 9,736 species. In contrast, GL produced one of the smallest assemblies and displayed the lowest diversity, with only 39 phyla and 2,135 species. Despite these stark differences in richness, a stable core microbiome was identified across all habitats, consisting of 29 shared phyla and 1,162 shared species. These findings indicate that while a consistent core community exists, habitat type is a primary determinant of the overall gut microbial diversity in T. japonica.

To define the taxonomic structure of the T. japonica gut microbiome, we analyzed the relative abundance of microbial taxa at the phylum and species levels across the six habitat groups. At the phylum level, the microbial communities were dominated by Pseudomonadota, Bacillota, and Mycoplasmatota, but their proportions varied dramatically among habitats (Fig. 1A, Table S3). Pseudomonadota was the most prevalent phylum in LAB (80.1%), CF (83.7%), and SL (86.1%) samples. In contrast, the broadleaf forest habitats showed a high abundance of Bacillota, which was the most dominant phylum in BF_SX (46.5%) and co-dominant in BF_SD (44.3%). Most strikingly, GL was overwhelmingly dominated by Mycoplasmatota, which constituted 67.5% of the community, a pattern unique to this group.

This habitat-specific structuring was also evident at the species level, where the dominance of certain phyla was often driven by one or a few species (Fig. 1B, Table S4). For instance, the high prevalence of Pseudomonadota in CF was largely attributable to Escherichia coli (66.5%). The high proportion of Bacillota in BF_SD and BF_SX corresponded to a high abundance of several Lactococcus species, including L. garvieae and L. lactis. The unique profile of the GL samples was driven by a remarkable abundance of multiple Spiroplasma species (phylum Mycoplasmatota), such as S. poulsonii (23.7%), S. endosymbiont of Megaselia nigra (13.3%), and S. phoeniceum (11.2%). These findings demonstrate that distinct microbial signatures, driven by different dominant taxa, characterize the gut communities of T. japonica from different habitats.

We next evaluated the alpha diversity of the gut microbial communities to understand how species richness and evenness varied across the six habitats (Fig. 2, Table S5). Overall, significant differences among the habitat groups were observed for all calculated diversity metrics. Species richness, estimated by the number of observed species (p = 0.022) and the Chao1 index (p = 0.023), was highest in BF_SD and BF_SX. In contrast, indices that account for community evenness, such as Shannon (p = 0.02), Simpson (p = 0.048), and Pielou’s evenness (p = 0.014), revealed a different pattern. The SL, GL, and LAB displayed the highest values for these indices, indicating a more evenly distributed community structure compared to the forest habitats. This suggests that while the broadleaf forest microbiomes were the most species-rich, they were numerically dominated by a smaller number of taxa. Notably, the sequencing coverage was significantly lower in LAB than in all wild populations (p = 0.043), which all approached saturation.

To assess how the overall microbial community structure varied among habitats, we analyzed the beta diversity using Bray-Curtis dissimilarity (Table S6). Both PCoA and Non-metric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS) ordination plots revealed a strong and distinct clustering of samples based on their habitat of origin (Fig. 3). This observation was statistically supported by a PERMANOVA, which showed that habitat explained a significant majority of the microbial community variance (R² = 0.689, p = 0.001). The ordination plots clearly separated LAB and GL microbiomes from all other groups, indicating unique community compositions in these environments. Notably, BF_SD and BF_SX clustered in proximity to one another but formed distinct subgroups, suggesting the presence of both a general “broadleaf forest” microbial signature and fine-scale regional differentiation. Similarly, CF and SL samples were clearly separated from other groups but showed some relative proximity on the ordination plots. The low stress value of the NMDS analysis (Stress = 0.067) confirms that the two-dimensional visualization is a reliable representation of the community dissimilarities. These results indicated that habitat appears to be a major factor shaping the gut microbial community structure in T. japonica.

Biomarker discovery through random forest and support vector machine analyses

To identify the key microbial species that serve as robust biomarkers for distinguishing among the six habitat groups, we employed a Random Forest supervised learning model. The model was trained on the species-level relative abundance data to classify samples into their habitat of origin. The analysis successfully identified a set of 20 species that were most influential in predicting the habitat source of a sample, as ranked by their Mean Decrease in Accuracy (Fig. 4, Table S7). The top predictors included Rahnella aquatilis (Mean Decrease Accuracy = 1.70), Pseudotevenvirus lee (1.70), and Lactococcus cremoris (1.64). The abundance patterns of these biomarkers varied distinctly across the habitats. For instance, several Lactococcus species, including L. cremoris and L. garvieae, were highly abundant in BF_SD and BF_SX, consistent with the overall phylum-level composition. In contrast, species such as R. aquatilis and Cedecea davisae were characteristic of LAB and CF, respectively.

Right panel: A bar chart illustrating the importance score (Mean Decrease Accuracy) for each species

To further validate and explore potential biomarkers from a different analytical perspective, we constructed a Support Vector Machine (SVM) model. The features (species) that lie closest to this hyperplane and are most influential in defining it are considered key biomarkers. We identified the top 20 species that were most critical for the SVM model’s classification performance. These species were subsequently confirmed to be highly significant in distinguishing between habitats via a Kruskal-Wallis test, all showing a False Discovery Rate (FDR) < 1e-9 (Fig. 5, Table S8).

This SVM-based approach revealed a set of biomarkers with a striking pattern of habitat specificity, particularly highlighting the unique microbial profile of BF_SX. A large cluster of co-occurring species, primarily from the Lactobacillales order (e.g., Weissella ceti, Weissella minor, Fructilactobacillus florum, Leuconostoc falkenbergense), were identified as powerful predictors. Their abundance was exceptionally high in BF_SX, but negligible in all other habitats, including the geographically distinct BF_SD. This indicates a strong, localized enrichment of lactic acid bacteria in the BF_SX. Another prominent biomarker, Spiromicrovirus SpV4, showed a markedly different profile, being almost exclusively abundant in SL. Similarly, Kalamiella piersonii was significantly enriched in GL and, to a lesser extent, BF_SX.

In addition, we performed a differential abundance analysis using DESeq2 (Fig. 6, Table S9). Each wild-caught population (CF, BF_SD, GL, SL, BF_SX) was compared against the laboratory-reared control group (LAB) to identify species that were significantly enriched or depleted. This approach allows for the direct assessment of environmental influence by using the homogenous lab population as a baseline.

A striking and consistent pattern emerged across all comparisons: the number of microbial species that were significantly reduced in abundance in the wild populations far exceeded the number of species that increased (Fig. 6A). The most dramatic shifts were observed in the two broadleaf forest populations; compared to the lab-reared group, BF_SD population had 258 upregulated and 278 downregulated species, while BF_SX population had 164 upregulated and 278 downregulated species. GL exhibited the least perturbation, yet still showed more downregulated (74) than upregulated (57) species.

Examining the specific taxa driving these changes revealed consistent patterns of depletion and habitat-specific enrichment (Fig. 6B). Notably, several species belonging to the microsporidian genus Encephalitozoon (e.g., E. cuniculi, E. intestinalis, E. hellem) were among the most significantly downregulated taxa across multiple wild habitats, particularly in CF and BF_SD forests. This suggests that these particular microbes may thrive in the controlled laboratory conditions but are selected against in complex natural environments.

Conversely, the identity of the top upregulated species was highly distinct for each habitat, indicating colonization by local environmental microbes. For example, Spiroplasma species were highly enriched in CF and GL populations. BF_SD saw a significant increase in lactic acid bacteria such as L. lactis and L. cremoris. In contrast, BF_SX was characterized by a surge in Hafnia alvei and other unassigned Hafniaceae members. These results underscore that while the transition to a wild environment induces a general loss of certain core or lab-associated taxa, it also facilitates the acquisition of a unique, habitat-specific microbial signature.

Subsequently, we conducted a Kruskal-Wallis test to identify species with statistically significant differences in abundance across all six habitat groups, and the results were highly consistent with the DESeq2 pairwise comparisons (Fig. 7, Table S10). The most pronounced finding was the extreme enrichment of Enterobacter huaxiensis and Escherichia coli in CF, where they constituted a mean proportion of over 60% and 15%, respectively, while being rare in other habitats. This analysis also reinforced the distinct signatures of the broadleaf forest environments; several species of Lactococcus (L. lactis, L. garvieae, and L. petauri) were significantly more abundant in BF_SD and, to a lesser extent, BF_SX. Furthermore, GL was confirmed as a hotspot for Spiroplasma, with species such as S. poulsonii, S. phoeniceum, and S. endosymbiont of Phyllotreta cruciferae showing their highest proportions in this environment. The congruence between these findings and the DESeq2 results provides robust evidence that the distinct ecological niches drive predictable and significant shifts in the gut microbial community of T. japonica.

Functional profiling of the gut metagenome reveals habitat-specific enrichments

Having established that the taxonomic composition of the gut microbiota is strongly structured by habitat, we next sought to understand the functional consequences of these community shifts. To achieve this, we performed a Gene Ontology (GO) annotation of the assembled metagenomes from each habitat group to compare their functional potential. The analysis revealed distinct functional profiles that were largely congruent with the observed taxonomic clustering. Hierarchical clustering of the top 20 most abundant GO terms demonstrated a clear grouping of samples by habitat types (Fig. 8, Table S11). Notably, the two broadleaf forest populations, BF_SD and BF_SX, clustered tightly together, indicating a high degree of functional similarity despite their geographical separation. These two groups exhibited significant enrichment across a wide array of fundamental biological functions. Core processes such as metabolic process, catalytic activity, binding, and cellular process were all highly represented, suggesting that the microbial communities in these habitats possess a diverse and highly active metabolic toolkit.

Red indicates higher relative abundance, while blue signifies lower relative abundance. Both the GO terms (rows) and the habitat groups (columns) are organized by hierarchical clustering based on their profile similarities

In stark contrast, GL and LAB populations clustered together and showed a comparative depletion in most of these functional categories. This suggests their gut metagenomes may be less functionally versatile, possibly reflecting a more simplified or controlled dietary intake. CF displayed a unique functional signature; while showing intermediate levels for many metabolic functions, it was exceptionally enriched in functions related to response to stimulus. This specific enrichment may reflect an adaptation of the gut microbiota to the unique stressors of the coniferous forest environment, such as the secondary metabolites produced by pines.

To resolve the specific metabolic capabilities underlying the broad functional categories observed in the GO analysis, we mapped the metagenomic data to the KEGG database (Fig. 9, Table S12). The hierarchical clustering of the top 20 most abundant KEGG pathways unequivocally separated the habitats into three distinct functional groups. The two broadleaf forest populations (BF_SD and BF_SX) formed a tight cluster characterized by a profound enrichment across nearly all major metabolic categories. This included core functions like “Carbon metabolism,” “Biosynthesis of amino acids,” and “Ribosome” biogenesis, as well as more complex pathways such as “Biosynthesis of secondary metabolites,” “ABC transporters,” and “Two-component system”. The exceptionally high abundance of these pathways, particularly in BF_SD, points to a gut microbial community with a remarkably diverse and active metabolic repertoire, likely necessary for processing a complex diet and adapting to a competitive environment. Meanwhile, LAB and GL populations clustered together, exhibiting a clear and significant depletion in these same pathways. This functional austerity suggests a less versatile microbial community, consistent with the controlled, potentially associated with the controlled laboratory conditions and differences in environmental complexity in the grassland habitat. CF and SL samples formed a third, intermediate group, indicating unique functional specializations that differ from both the broadleaf forests and the lab/grassland environments.

To elucidate the potential functional contributions of specific taxa within the microbial community, we conducted a correlation analysis to link the relative abundances of the top 20 species with the top 20 most abundant functional categories from both KEGG and GO databases (Fig. 10). This analysis aimed to identify the key species driving the core functional characteristics of the microbiome.

Our analysis revealed that a few specific taxa, primarily belonging to the family Enterobacteriaceae, were strongly associated with the majority of the top functions. All statistically significant correlations observed were positive (P < 0.05), indicating that these species are likely the primary contributors to these functional pathways.

The KEGG pathway correlation analysis showed that Klebsiella michiganensis had significant positive correlations with all 20 of the most abundant pathways, including “Metabolic pathways”, “Biosynthesis of amino acids”, “Carbon metabolism”, and “ABC transporters” (r > 0.38, P < 0.05) (Fig. 10A, Table S13). Likewise, Enterobacter hormaechei was significantly positively correlated with 12 key pathways, demonstrating particularly strong associations with “Starch and sucrose metabolism” (r = 0.60, P < 0.05), “Phosphotransferase system (PTS)” (r = 0.58, P < 0.05), and “ABC transporters” (r = 0.57, P < 0.05). Furthermore, Cedecea lapagei and Unassigned Enterobacteriaceae were also identified as significant functional contributors, showing positive correlations with 11 and 4 pathways, respectively (P < 0.05).

A similar trend was observed in the GO term correlation analysis (Fig. 10B, Table S14). Klebsiella michiganensis was again broadly influential, showing significant positive associations with 13 of the top 20 GO terms, including “metabolic process”, “cellular process”, “catalytic activity”, and “binding” (P < 0.05). Enterobacter hormaechei, Cedecea lapagei, and Unassigned Enterobacteriaceae were also strongly correlated with a suite of core functions. For instance, Enterobacter hormaechei was significantly associated with “transporter activity” and “structural molecule activity” (r > 0.43, P < 0.05). Notably, Kluyvera intermedia also emerged as a key species, showing significant correlations with “homeostatic process”, “molecular function regulator activity”, “structural molecule activity”, and “transporter activity” (P < 0.05). Collectively, these results suggest that a core group of Enterobacteriaceae species are the primary drivers of the fundamental metabolic and cellular activities within this microbial ecosystem.

Canonical correspondence analysis (CCA) was also performed using annual mean temperature (T) and annual mean precipitation (P) as constraining variables (Fig. 11, Table S15). The first two CCA axes explained 48.63% of the total constrained variance in gut microbial community composition (CCA1: 31.7%; CCA2: 16.93%), and the overall model was statistically significant based on a permutation test (P = 0.001), indicating that the measured environmental variables significantly contributed to the observed variation in gut microbiota among habitats.

Red arrows indicate the top dominant bacterial genera, with arrow length and direction reflecting their relative contribution to the ordination axes. Blue arrows represent the environmental variables: T (annual mean temperature) and P (annual mean precipitation)

The CCA biplot revealed clear separation of gut microbial communities across different habitat types. GL samples were positioned in the upper-right quadrant of the ordination space, strongly associated with the genus Spiroplasma, which was the dominant taxon in this habitat. In contrast, CF samples clustered in the left portion of the plot and were closely associated with several Enterobacteriaceae genera, including Escherichia, Tyzzerella, Cedecea, Citrobacter, Kluyvera, and Raoultella. SL samples were positioned near CF but showed a slight vertical displacement, indicating partially overlapping yet distinct microbial profiles. BF_SD samples were distributed in the lower-central region of the plot, associated with Lelliottia and Lactococcus, whereas BF_SX samples were positioned in the lower-right area, strongly linked with Hafnia.

The environmental vectors indicated that both T and P pointed toward the upper-left quadrant. Precipitation was more closely aligned with the CF and SL habitats, suggesting that higher moisture conditions may favor the enrichment of Enterobacteriaceae-related taxa. Temperature showed a similar directional trend but with a stronger upward component along CCA2. The GL samples were positioned in the direction orthogonal to both environmental vectors, suggesting that additional unmeasured environmental factors (e.g., vegetation type, soil properties, altitude) may play important roles in shaping the distinct Spiroplasma-dominated microbiome observed in grassland populations.

Discussion

The intricate relationship between an insect host and its gut microbiome is fundamental to its adaptation and survival in diverse ecological landscapes [1]. In this study, we conducted the first shotgun metagenomic analysis of the representative species of pygmy grasshoppers, T. japonica, revealing how its gut microbial community composition and functional potential are profoundly shaped by various habitats. Our findings provide robust evidence that both local environmental factors and geographic separation drive microbial community assembly [19], and that a core group of bacteria may be responsible for maintaining essential metabolic functions across these distinct ecosystems [31].

Our most prominent finding is the dramatic variation in microbial taxonomic structure across the six habitats, which aligns with our primary hypothesis. Beta diversity analysis demonstrated clear habitat-associated clustering of microbial communities (Fig. 3). Consistent with previous findings [32], recent insect-focused studies have also shown that environmental factors play a dominant role over host genetics in shaping the gut microbiome, as demonstrated in the hindgut microbiota of coconut rhinoceros beetles (Oryctes rhinoceros) across different geographical populations [33]. The distinct microbial signatures observed—the dominance of Pseudomonadota in CF and SL, Bacillota in BF_SD and BF_SX, and a highprevalence of Mycoplasmatota (Spiroplasma) in GL—suggest habitat-associated differences in microbial community composition under varying environmental conditions [6]. The enrichment of Lactococcus in broadleaf forests, for example, is consistent with their known role in fermenting complex plant carbohydrates [34], which are likely more abundant in these diverse floral environments. The extreme dominance of Spiroplasma in the grassland habitat is particularly noteworthy. While Spiroplasma are commonly recognized as heritable endosymbionts in arthropods, with roles ranging from mutualistic to pathogenic [35, 36]. They have also been reported from digestive tissues in certain insects [37], suggesting that their ecological distribution may extend beyond strictly systemic localization and involve more complex host–microbe interactions. In this context, their near-exclusive prevalence in grassland-dwelling T. japonica suggests a strong, environmentally mediated association that warrants further investigation into its functional significance.

A key strength of our study design is the inclusion of two geographically separated broadleaf forest populations. While both BF_SD and BF_SX microbiomes were enriched in Bacillota, they formed distinct clusters in the ordination plots and were characterized by different biomarker species identified by the SVM model. BF_SX population, for instance, harbored a unique consortium of lactic acid bacteria (e.g., Weissella, Fructilactobacillus) that were absent in BF_SD. This suggests that while vegetation type provides a general filter for microbial colonization, local biogeography and fine-scale environmental differences contribute to significant regional variation in the microbiome [19]. This dual influence of habitat and geography has been observed in other insects and highlights the complex interplay of factors shaping wild microbial communities.

The functional profiling of the metagenomes provided a clear link between the observed taxonomic shifts and the metabolic capabilities of the gut microbiome. The high taxonomic richness in the broadleaf forests corresponded directly with a significant enrichment in a wide array of metabolic pathways, including those for carbon metabolism, amino acid biosynthesis, and the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites. This enhanced functional repertoire likely provides the host with the versatility needed to break down diverse and complex plant polymers and detoxify a wider range of plant defensive compounds. Conversely, the functional austerity observed in theLAB and GL populations reflects adaptation to a more simplified and consistent diet [38]. Because the laboratory population was fed fresh materials collected directly from the native wild habitat, major dietary differences were minimized. Therefore, the observed microbial differences between laboratory and wild populations are more likely attributable to captivity-related factors [17], such as environmental stability, reduced exposure to environmental microbes, and altered ecological interactions, rather than substantial differences in diet composition. The unique enrichment of pathways related to “response to stimulus” in the coniferous forest metagenome may represent a microbial adaptation to the specific chemical stressors of that environment, such as the abundant terpenes and phenolic compounds produced by coniferous trees [39]. Recent evidence further suggests that gut microbiota can mediate the ability of insects to cope with environmental stressors [40]. Therefore, this functional enrichment may reflect a potential mechanism by which the microbiome assists the host in adapting to habitat-specific pressures.

Despite the vast differences in species composition across habitats, our correlation analysis suggests that a functionally crucial role might be fulfilled by a relatively small group of core taxa. Members of the family Enterobacteriaceae, particularly Klebsiella michiganensis and Enterobacter hormaechei, were positively correlated with a large number of fundamental KEGG pathways and GO terms across all samples. This indicates that these species may represent important functional contributors, providing essential metabolic services such as central carbon metabolism and nutrient biosynthesis that are indispensable for the host, regardless of the specific environment [31, 41]. This functional contribution by a core group, while the peripheral community members vary, is a common theme in host-microbe systems and suggests a degree of functional redundancy within the community.

Finally, the comparison with the laboratory-reared population provided critical insights into the impact of captivity on the insect microbiome. The stark difference between wild and lab-reared populations highlights the significant role of the natural environment in seeding and maintaining microbial diversity [42, 43]. The observation that many taxa, such as the microsporidian parasite Encephalitozoon, were abundant in the lab but significantly depleted in wild populations suggests that the competitive and complex natural environment actively selects against certain microbes that might thrive under controlled, low-stress conditions. The general pattern of wild populations having more downregulated than upregulated species compared to the lab baseline suggests that the laboratory environment may foster a unique “lab-adapted” microbiome, and that reintroduction to a natural setting involves both the acquisition of new, environmentally-sourced microbes and the loss of these lab-associated taxa. This has important implications for the interpretation of laboratory-based studies and for conservation efforts involving captive breeding, as the loss of a functionally competent microbiome could compromise host fitness in the wild [44, 45].

Several methodological limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The number of biological replicates per habitat was limited to three, constrained by logistical challenges associated with field sampling across geographically distant sites spanning three provinces in China. Although each replicate consisted of pooled gut contents from 60 individuals (30 females and 30 males), which helped reduce individual-level stochastic variation, this sample size may not fully capture within-habitat biological variability. Nevertheless, the consistent and statistically significant separation among habitats observed in PERMANOVA, PCoA, and canonical correspondence analysis supports the robustness of the detected patterns. Although canonical correspondence analysis revealed significant associations between climatic variables (temperature and precipitation) and gut microbiome composition, our study did not incorporate fine-scale environmental measurements such as soil physicochemical properties, vegetation indices, or local microclimate data. Therefore, conclusions regarding habitat–environment–microbiome relationships remain correlative. Future investigations integrating comprehensive environmental monitoring with expanded sampling designs will be essential to disentangle the specific abiotic and biotic drivers of gut microbiome assembly in pygmy grasshoppers.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the gut microbiome of T. japonica is a dynamic and plastic entity, strongly molded by the host’s habitat and geographic location. The habitat-specific taxonomic and functional signatures observed likely facilitate the pygmy grasshopper’s adaptation to a wide range of dietary resources and environmental conditions across China. The identification of key functional contributors and the pronounced differences between wild and captive populations deepen our understanding of insect-microbe symbiosis in natural ecosystems. These findings lay the groundwork for future research aimed at isolating functionally important microbial strains and elucidating the precise mechanisms by which the gut microbiome enhances the ecological resilience of its host.
