Blended learning in laboratory science education: comparison of flipped classroom and traditional lectures in immunohematology training
Authors: Nadia Saniee, Masoumeh Seidi, Fatemeh Amiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUsing blended learning such as combining flipped classroom and demonstration methods not only addresses learners’ needs but also enhances interaction and feedback. This, in turn, leads to greater learner satisfaction and improved effectiveness compared to traditional teaching methods. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the educational outcomes of blended learning versus traditional lectures in the practical immunohematology course for laboratory sciences students.MethodsThis study was conducted over five consecutive fall semesters (2020–2024). The study employed a parallel-group, randomized controlled design. A total of 196 eligible students were randomly allocated, using computer-generated sequencing with concealed allocation, to either a control group (n = 100) that received traditional lectures or an intervention group (n = 96) that underwent a blended learning approach (virtual flipped classroom combined with demonstrations). Outcome assessors and data analysts were blinded to group assignment. Data was collected via structured questionnaires and objective structured practical examination (OSPE) and analyzed using SPSS software (version 26) with descriptive and inferential statistics.ResultsThe intervention group achieved significantly higher mean scores than the control group in knowledge (5.100 vs. 7.656, p < 0.001). OSPE laboratory assessment (18.19 vs. 16.47, p < 0.001), OSPE hospital assessment (6.304 vs. 5.319, p < 0.001), self-perceived laboratory competence (39.82 vs. 31.13, p < 0.001), and student satisfaction (81.73 vs. 42.78, p < 0.001). These findings indicate that blended learning markedly improves practical performance, knowledge acquisition, and satisfaction compared to traditional lecture-based methods. The effect size analysis represented small, medium, and large effects, respectively.ConclusionThese findings affirm the effectiveness of the blended learning model in health professions education, demonstrating its capacity to successfully integrate theory with practice in laboratory settings. The study highlights the transferability of this approach to other lab-based medical and biomedical science courses, suggesting a scalable strategy for enhancing practical competency. Future research should examine the long-term retention of skills, direct impact on clinical laboratory proficiency, and cost-effectiveness to facilitate wider implementation across medical curricula.

Introduction

Blended learning is defined as the thoughtful integration of e-learning and face-to-face instruction [1]. Wang and Bhagat examined key theoretical frameworks underpinning blended learning, such as the cognitive theory of multimedia learning, connectivism, systems theory, the theory of affordances, and the ARCS model of motivation. These frameworks offer a strong theoretical basis, positioning blended learning as an approach grounded in pedagogical principles rather than a mere technological or logistical shift, one informed by a nuanced comprehension of learning processes in diverse and networked settings [2]. Blended learning shows promise in healthcare education. A comprehensive meta-analysis found that blended learning significantly improved knowledge acquisition compared to traditional methods [3]. Blended learning enables active, collaborative learning through various technologies including virtual classrooms, 3D environments, and discussion forums [4]. In surgical training, an online case-based program significantly improved script concordance test scores and trainee confidence in managing surgical emergencies. These approaches offer flexibility for healthcare professionals managing varied clinical schedules while maintaining educational effectiveness [5]. Studies highlight the positive impact of blended learning on education, emphasizing its role in enhancing students’ practical skills, self-directed learning, understanding, and problem-solving abilities [6, 7].

One of the teaching methods that is gaining popularity among educators is the flipped classroom, also referred to in various sources as the inverted classroom, flipped instruction, reverse instruction, or flipped learning [8]. The flipped classroom is a thoughtfully supported instructional process in which learning takes place outside the university setting through individually self-directed engagement with designed electronic content. The acceleration, deepening, and reinforcement of learning are then completed in the university environment through interactive, group-based activities aligned with the content, under the supervision and guidance of the instructor [9]. In other words, the flipped classroom is an active learning approach that shifts from traditional teacher-centered lectures to a student-centered model [10]. It consists of two stages: pre-class learning and in-class activities [11]. This method is also applicable to teaching courses in laboratory-related fields [12].

On the other hand, the demonstration method is an effective teaching strategy for enhancing practical skills, particularly in laboratory, clinical, and nursing education. One study has shown its superiority in improving students’ psychomotor skills and learning outcomes [13]. Using this method, a skill can be taught to a large number of learners within a short period of time. The demonstration method is particularly effective for courses with practical and laboratory components. The implementation stages of this method include preparation, explanation, demonstration, practice, and evaluation. The most significant advantage of this method is the use of real and tangible objects in the teaching process. However, it is rarely used in isolation; the instructor often needs to combine it with other methods such as lectures or the flipped classroom approach [14].

With the shift to remote education in recent years, certain areas of instruction, particularly practical courses, have faced greater challenges in implementation. Additionally, achieving higher levels of learning has been a major concern for instructors of these courses. To address this issue and enhance the depth of learners’ understanding, the use of blended learning methods appears to be a suitable solution. The flipped classroom model can be implemented virtually and covers both lower levels of Bloom’s cognitive domain (knowledge and comprehension) and higher levels (application, analysis, synthesis, and evaluation). Moreover, through group activities, it fosters a problem-based learning environment, ultimately improving learning outcomes and enhancing the quality of education [9].

Recent studies highlight the effectiveness of integrating modern technologies with traditional teaching methods in laboratory science education. Video-based pre-lab instruction has been shown to improve laboratory efficiency, safety, and technical skills [15]. The “Flipped Lab” approach, adapting inverted learning principles to scientific laboratories, shifts focus to the pre-lab phase, reducing cognitive load during lab time and increasing student satisfaction [16]. These virtual labs often use 3D desktop technology, with immersive virtual reality trending recently. Even simple online interventions, such as pre-lab quizzes, can significantly increase student engagement and satisfaction with practical classes [17]. A review of existing evidence indicates that using blended approaches such as combining flipped classroom and demonstration methods not only addresses learners’ needs but also enhances interaction and feedback. This, in turn, leads to greater learner satisfaction and improved effectiveness compared to traditional teaching methods [3, 18–20].

Given the above, the necessity of delivering practical courses for laboratory science students through student-centered approaches with an emphasis on problem-based learning skill is increasingly evident. The combination of the flipped classroom and demonstration-based teaching in laboratory sciences is supported by a strong theoretical foundation. Based on theories such as constructivism and cognitive load theory, the flipped classroom provides an appropriate cognitive foundation by transferring theoretical content outside of class [21], while demonstration-based teaching reduces cognitive load during the acquisition of complex skills by presenting a practical model [22]. The suggested blended learning model is theorized to operate through a sequential two-stage mechanism. First, the flipped classroom section frontloads declarative knowledge (theoretical principles) outside of class. According to cognitive load theory, this approach reduces intrinsic cognitive load during in-class sessions, thereby freeing working memory resources for skill-focused activities. Second, in the classroom, demonstration-based teaching provides a structured, observable model of psychomotor skills (e.g., blood typing, antibody identification). From a constructivist perspective, learners actively construct procedural knowledge by imitating and refining the demonstrated technique, while cognitive load remains practicable because the demonstration reduces the need for trial-and-error search.

This integrated approach, particularly in practical fields like immunohematology, which require the deep integration of conceptual knowledge and psychomotor skills, leads to the optimization of instructional time, increased practical accuracy, and enhanced student self-efficacy. Therefore, this study was conducted with the aim of comparing the learning outcomes of students taught using a blended learning method (virtual flipped classroom combined with demonstration) with those taught using the traditional lecture-based method in the practical course of immunohematology in the laboratory sciences program at Hamadan University of Medical Sciences.

Methods

Study design

This pooled multi-cohort study was conducted over five consecutive fall semesters (2020–2024), aligned with the official offering of the immunohematology laboratory course. The study employed a parallel-group, randomized controlled design.

Participants

The target population was seventh-semester undergraduate students in laboratory sciences at Hamadan University of Medical Sciences, who were enrolled in the mandatory practical Immunohematology course.

A total of 196 eligible students were randomly allocated to either a control group (n = 100) that received traditional lectures or an intervention group (n = 96) that underwent a blended learning approach (virtual flipped classroom combined with demonstrations). Allocation was concealed using computer-generated codes stored in a secure, password-protected database. The allocation sequence was only revealed to the participants upon completion of baseline data collection, ensuring that neither the participants nor the researchers assigning groups could predict the assignment. The same instructor taught both groups to minimize instructor-specific effects. To minimize contamination, the two groups were scheduled in separate time slots/rooms, and there was no shared platform for study materials between the groups during the intervention period. Participants were also instructed not to share specific intervention materials with the other group. Outcome assessors and data analysts were blinded to group assignment.

Out-of-Class Activities (Flipped Component): Student information was collected, virtual groups were formed, and the course plan was shared with all participants, including information on classroom policies, scheduling, and the location of classes and laboratory sessions. The session then moved into educational content, beginning with a brief 10-minute review and summary of the material by the instructor (in lecture format). Throughout the session, continuous monitoring of student activities, feedback provision, and instructional guidance were provided. The instructor maintained an active, facilitative presence and provided ongoing feedback and evaluation during the sessions. Phase 3: student evaluation

In total, evaluations were conducted across multiple levels including knowledge, reaction, and self-perceived laboratory competence using designated questionnaires as well as skill evaluation using objective structured practical examination (OSPE). Formative evaluation (during the semester) was performed by using quizzes, assignments with feedback, lab reports, and classroom participation online and/ or in class methods. Student progress on assignments and classroom engagement (e.g., group discussions, presentations) were also evaluated formatively. Summative evaluation (final assessment) was conducted via exams, including a knowledge-based pre-laboratory content exam and an OSPE in the laboratory. Another OSPE exam was also designed and conducted in workplace/ hospital as final exam of internship career to assess learning durability in the next spring semester.

Learning (Knowledge) evaluation: Knowledge acquisition was measured using pre- and post-tests developed in accordance with the course syllabus for alignment with learning objectives. Students’ knowledge was evaluated through a researcher-developed multiple-choice questionnaire comprising 10 items (options A, B, C, and D) based on the immunohematology course content [9]. The structure of the instrument was examined by two medical education specialists using the Millman checklist, and reviewed by the director of the laboratory sciences and/ or faculty members of the department of laboratory sciences. Each correct response was awarded one point, while incorrect responses received zero points. The minimum score was 0, and the maximum score was 10. For designing the multiple-choice questionnaire instructor was prepared blue print and consider the number of sessions, educational contents, impact and frequency of the session title, lesson plan, and, etc. The pre-test was administered prior to the first instructional session at the onset of the course, and the post-test was conducted after the final session upon completion of the learning process. Reaction evaluation: A psychometrically validated questionnaire with strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.89) was designed to evaluate participants’ satisfaction with the immunohematology laboratory course [9]. The instrument consisted of 18 items across five domains: course content (2 items), assigned tasks (2 items), assignments and in-class activities (5 items), instructional skills (7 items), and the overall course unit (2 items). Satisfaction levels were assessed using a six-point Likert scale ranging from “completely satisfied” to “completely dissatisfied,” with additional options for “neutral” and “no opinion.” (Scored 0 to 5). The anonymous questionnaire was administered on the day of the final examination at the end of each semester and collected upon completion. Self-perceived laboratory competence evaluation: Students’ self-perceived laboratory competence were assessed using a researcher-developed 11-item checklist (e.g., “perform venipuncture,” “prepare cell suspensions at different concentrations,” “identify and confirm the blood group of an unknown sample” [9]. Each item was rated on a 4-point scale, yielding total scores ranging from 11 to 44. The face and content validity of the instrument were confirmed by expert colleagues, and its internal consistency was established with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.75. Response options included: “I have now completely learned how to perform it,” “I have partially learned how to perform it,” “I have not learned how to perform it,” and “I already knew how to perform it beforehand,” emphasizing self-perceived essential laboratory competencies. Two months after course completion, the checklist was administered to evaluate participants’ self-perceived laboratory competence. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the dataset. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test.

The primary outcomes of this study were student satisfaction, knowledge, and self-perceived laboratory competence. The main inferential analysis focused on comparing the intervention and control groups, as well as evaluating changes over time (before and after) where applicable. For outcomes involving both group and time, before and after, (knowledge), the primary analysis was conducted using an Aligned Rank Transform (ART) ANOVA to examine the main effects of group and time, as well as their interaction (Group × Time). This approach was selected to provide a robust, non-parametric analysis. To support and interpret these findings, within-group pre–post comparisons were performed using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Between-group comparisons (at pre-intervention and post-intervention phase) were conducted using independent samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate. For other primary outcomes measured at a single time point (student satisfaction and self-perceived laboratory competence), comparisons between the intervention and control groups were performed using independent samples t-tests for normally distributed data, or Mann–Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed data. The secondary outcome (student skill) was analyzed similarly using independent samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate.

Effect sizes were calculated for all key comparisons for interpretation. Cohen’s d was reported for parametric analyses, and effect size r for non-parametric analyses, along with their 95% confidence intervals. Effect sizes were interpreted using Cohen’s guidelines: d = 0.2 (small), 0.5 (medium), ≥ 0.8 (large); and r = 0.1 (small), 0.3 (medium), ≥ 0.5 (large) [23–25]. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26, R version 4.5.1, and GraphPad Prism version 8. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participations

Of the 196 students included in the study, 100 (51.0%) were assigned to the control group and 96 (49.0%) to the intervention group. As presented in Fig. 1, 51 (51.0%) and 59 (61.5%) of the students in the control and intervention groups were female, respectively. No statistically significant association was observed between gender and study group (P-value = 0.140). The mean age (± standard deviation) of students was 22.34 ± 0.945 years in the control group and 22.26 ± 0.874 years in the intervention group (Fig. 2).

Based on the results presented in Table 1, the mean (± SD) laboratory OSPE score was 16.470 ± 1.113 in the control group and 18.192 ± 1.247 in the intervention group. A statistically significant difference was observed in the mean laboratory OSPE scores between the two groups (P-value < 0.001). The mean ± SD hospital OSPE score was 5.319 ± 0.818 in the control group and 6.304 ± 0.749 in the intervention group. This difference was also statistically significant (P-value < 0.001). Regarding satisfaction, the mean (± SD) scores were 42.780 ± 5.004 and 81.729 ± 5.873 for the control and intervention groups, respectively. The mean satisfaction score was significantly higher in the intervention group (P-value < 0.001). For self-perceived laboratory competence, the mean ± SD scores were 31.130 ± 1.894 in the control group and 39.823 ± 1.522 in the intervention group. This difference was statistically significant, with the intervention group demonstrating a higher mean self-perceived laboratory competence score (P-value < 0.001). The intervention showed a large effect on OSPE lab (effect size = 1.46) and moderate to large effects on OSPE hospital (0.55), satisfaction (0.85), and self-perceived laboratory competence scores (0.87). Overall, these effect sizes indicate a substantial practical impact of the intervention compared with the control group.

Discussion

The present study examined the effectiveness of a blended teaching method, integrating a virtual flipped classroom with demonstration-based instruction, compared to the traditional lecture-based approach for delivering the practical immunohematology course to laboratory sciences students. The findings show that the blended learning approach significantly enhanced students’ knowledge, self-perceived laboratory competence, OSPE performance, and satisfaction relative to the conventional method. Baseline knowledge did not differ significantly between the control and intervention groups, indicating comparable starting levels. However, post-intervention, the blended learning group demonstrated a significant improvement in knowledge scores, whereas the control group showed only modest gains. These results are consistent with previous research on the effectiveness of blended learning in medical education, particularly for laboratory sciences [9, 12]. These studies similar to the present study showed that virtual flipped classrooms, when combined with demonstration-based instruction, could produce substantial improvements in students’ knowledge, practical competencies, and overall satisfaction compared with traditional lecture-based methods. Another study documented that blended learning of dental students (n = 253) scored 7.25 points higher overall than traditional cohorts (n = 364) [26]. R. Donkin et al. also found statistically better practical examination scores in medical laboratory science students using blended video-based learning (n = 14 each group) [27]. These findings consistently support blended learning’s superiority across knowledge, practical performance, and satisfaction metrics.

Qutob et al. indicated that students exposed to flipped classroom approaches demonstrated superior performance in knowledge and cognitive domains, achieving higher average grades and a lower incidence of low grades compared with cohorts taught using traditional methods [12]. The studies showed that the majority of students reported that the flipped classroom approach enhanced their understanding of the subject matter and positively impacted their academic performance [12, 28]. H. Qutob et al. found 83% of students believed the flipped classroom provided better understanding of subject matter [12]. Although one study did not observe a statistically significant improvement in academic performance, it reported higher levels of student satisfaction with the blended learning approach [28]. Overall, these findings indicate that blended learning and flipped classroom approaches have the potential to enhance student engagement, foster critical thinking, and improve the application of knowledge in laboratory education.

Moreover, the blended learning group achieved significantly higher scores in both OSPE laboratory and hospital assessments, as well as in self-perceived laboratory competencies. These findings support the view that incorporating demonstration methods into practical courses can effectively bridge the gap between theory and practice, particularly in laboratory-based disciplines. By combining pre-class preparation with hands-on demonstrations and guided practice, students are better able to internalize procedures and develop the psychomotor skills essential for professional competence. Student satisfaction was also substantially higher in the intervention group, consistent with previous research indicating that blended learning enhances learner engagement, autonomy, and the perceived relevance of course content. The interactive, student-centered structure of the flipped classroom, reinforced by the tangible and visual elements of demonstrations, likely contributed to these positive outcomes. On the other hand, reduced cognitive load during skill performance and optimized in-class time for feedback-rich practice in the flipped classroom approach might explain the difference in satisfaction scores between the study groups. In the control group, students faced unfamiliar theory and psychomotor execution at the same time, resulting in a high cognitive load. In the intervention group, the flipped classroom delivered theoretical content upfront, allowing in-class sessions to focus entirely on skill execution. This directly increased satisfaction and reduced performance anxiety through pre-task observation. Control group students performed skills without a prior demonstration, a known source of anxiety and dissatisfaction. In the intervention group, demonstration-based teaching provided a correct, step-by-step model before students performed the skill, reducing mistakes and fear of errors. In the control group, considerable class time was devoted to theoretical explanations, leaving little time for supervised practice. In the intervention group, nearly all in-class time was dedicated to hands-on practice.

Fisher et al. and Chandler et al. have reported similar benefits, showing that blended learning approaches integrating online and face-to-face instruction can significantly improve psychomotor skills and laboratory competencies. Other studies likewise confirm that blended learning can lead to better student performance, greater engagement, and higher satisfaction compared to traditional methods [29, 30]. Incorporating online resources and pre-laboratory preparation activities improves students’ understanding of procedures and theoretical concepts, leading to enhanced experimental learning outcomes [31]. In physical therapy education, implementing a modified nine-step framework for teaching psychomotor skills in a blended format produced promising initial results [32]. The effectiveness of blended learning is especially evident in laboratory-based disciplines, where online components seamlessly complement hands-on activities [30]. Overall, integrating flipped and blended learning pedagogies has a positive impact on student engagement, performance, and satisfaction, supporting their broader adoption in higher education [29].

The flipped classroom approach has demonstrated substantial benefits in health professions education, including nursing and dentistry. Numerous studies have reported gains in academic achievement, practical performance, and higher-order thinking skills compared to traditional teaching method [33–35] that were align with results of the current study. A meta-analysis found a significant overall effect favoring flipped classrooms, with enhanced effectiveness when instructors used quizzes at the start of in-class sessions [35]. The approach promotes knowledge retention, improves examination performance, and enhances student satisfaction. These benefits are largely attributed to increased opportunities for critical thinking and complex reasoning during class, as well as personalized online support. Although some students initially perceive the method as stressful, offering clear information and explaining its rationale can help alleviate their concerns [36]. The flipped classroom model is particularly effective when integrated with simulation-based education or video-driven discussions [33, 34]. Our findings likewise highlight the value of preparing students in advance of hands-on activities, as this approach reduces cognitive overload and allows for more focused, effective in-class practice.

From a pedagogical perspective, integrating flipped classroom and demonstration-based methods is particularly well-suited to laboratory sciences, where both conceptual understanding and technical proficiency are critical. Providing preparatory materials via the LMS, along with structured communication channels, fosters self-directed learning and ensures that in-person sessions focus on active skill development. Furthermore, the structured demonstration phases help standardize procedural instruction, potentially minimizing variability in skill acquisition among students. The flipped classroom model is particularly beneficial for laboratory students because it enhances preparation, maximizes hands-on practice time, and promotes active learning. By shifting theoretical content to the pre-class phase, students can study foundational concepts at their own pace and come to the laboratory better prepared. This allows in-class time to focus on practical skill development, problem-solving, and immediate instructor feedback, which are essential for mastering technical competencies. Additionally, the model fosters deeper understanding, critical thinking, collaboration, and greater confidence, making it especially well-suited for skill-based and practice-oriented disciplines. In this regard, B. Loveys et al. found that 79% of students agreed pre-laboratory activities enhanced engagement, with significantly improved learning outcomes despite a 26.8% enrollment increase [37]. Seidi and Amiri reported comparable findings as well [9]. Accordingly, educators in various scientific fields are encouraged to consider integrating this innovative method to enhance academic performance. Nevertheless, additional studies with larger sample sizes are needed to generate more robust evidence and strengthen the methodological quality of future research.

Data collection over a five-year period from laboratory science students, compared to previous studies, was considered a strength. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the research was carried out at a single institution and focused on one specific laboratory science course, which may restrict the external validity and limit the applicability of the findings to other universities, disciplines, or educational settings with different curricula, resources, or student characteristics. Second, although students were randomly allocated each term, full blinding of participants and instructors was not possible due to the nature of the educational intervention. This lack of blinding may have introduced performance and expectation biases, as students and faculty were aware of the teaching method being implemented, potentially influencing engagement levels and self-reported outcomes. Third, the study relied partly on self-reported measures such as satisfaction and perceived skill improvement, which may be subject to social desirability or response bias. Additionally, acknowledging that while the study spanned five years, the analysis focused on the aggregated effect of the intervention under stable conditions. Future studies should include multi-center designs with larger and more diverse samples to enhance generalizability. They should also assess long-term outcomes, including professional competency, clinical reasoning, and decision-making performance in authentic settings. Designing longitudinal study to evaluate temporal trends was also suggested. Furthermore, incorporating objective performance-based assessments and conducting cost-effectiveness analyses would provide stronger evidence to guide large-scale implementation of blended and flipped learning approaches.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that combining a virtual flipped classroom with demonstration-based instruction significantly improves learning outcomes in a practical immunohematology course. By moving theoretical content to the pre-class phase, students better prepared for hands-on laboratory sessions, allowing in-class time to focus on skill practice, guided demonstrations, and interactive learning. This approach resulted in notable improvements in overall student satisfaction, knowledge acquisition, self-perceived laboratory competence, and performance on OSPE assessments compared to traditional lecture-based methods. These findings support the effectiveness of blended learning in laboratory-based disciplines where bridging theory and practice is essential. Use of multiple assessment tools strengthen the validity of the results, suggesting that integrating pre-class preparation with hands-on demonstrations can enhance both cognitive and practical competencies. Therefore, incorporating such innovative teaching strategies may improve learning efficiency, engagement, and educational quality in immunohematology and similar laboratory-oriented courses.
