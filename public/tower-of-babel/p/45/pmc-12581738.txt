Analysis of the incidence and risk factors associated with retinopathy of prematurity in patients complicated by bronchopulmonary dysplasia
Authors: Caiyu Zhang, Lu He, Muchun Yu, Zhihong Sun, Huiqing Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) and retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) are common disorders in preterm infants. This study aimed to analyze the difference in the incidence of type 1 or type 2 ROP between the BPD and non-BPD groups in preterm infants of different gestational ages and birth weights, and to explore the risk factors for the combination of type 1 or type 2 ROP in infants with BPD.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on the clinical data of preterm infants hospitalized from January 2019 to December 2023. All preterm infants were divided into BPD and non-BPD groups based on the 2018 BPD diagnostic criteria; ROP was classified according to the Early Treatment for Retinopathy of Prematurity (ETROP).ResultsThe incidence of infant type 1 and type 2 ROP was significantly higher in the BPD group than in the non-BPD group (all p < 0.001). BPD infants with gestational age < 28 weeks (OR12.25) and birth weight < 1000g(OR9.67) have the highest risk of type 1 or type 2 ROP. Logistic regression analysis showed that lower gestational age and birth weight, longer mechanical ventilation, and more blood transfusions within the first 28 postnatal days increased the risk of combined type 1 or type 2 ROP in infants with BPD.ConclusionBPD has a significant impact on the occurrence of ROP. Low gestational age, low birth weight, the duration of mechanical ventilation and the number of blood transfusions within the first 28 postnatal days are all risk factors for BPD infants with combined type 1 or type 2 ROP.Keywords: Bronchopulmonary dysplasia, retinopathy of prematurity, gestational age, birth weight, mechanical ventilation, blood transfusion

Introduction

Due to the continuous development and progress in the medical field in recent years, the survival rate of extremely premature infants has increased compared to before, and the incidence of bronchopulmonary dysplasia and retinopathy of prematurity has also risen accordingly. Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), a frequent complication in very preterm infants, is characterized by abnormal development of alveolar structure and pulmonary vasculature [1]. BPD is based on the immaturity of the lungs in preterm infants, and is caused by prenatal (chorioamnionitis, infections, genetic susceptibility, etc.) and postnatal (respiratory support, sepsis, etc.) factors that cause injury and inflammation of the lungs, which in turn leads to alveolar hypoplasia and vascular dysfunction [2]. BPD can lead to adverse outcomes such as pulmonary arterial hypertension, cardiopulmonary failure and intellectual decline, and its impact on children may last a lifetime [3]. ROP, also known as retrolental fibroplasia (RLF), was first proposed by Terry in 1942. Later, it was valued by scholars at home and abroad due to its high risk of causing blindness [4]. ROP is a retinal vascular proliferative disease that occurs in preterm or low birth weight infants, and its occurrence is fundamentally due to the immaturity of retinal development in preterm infants, and it is the result of abnormal vascular proliferation after retinal vasoconstriction induced by the hyperoxic environment [5]. According to the World Health Organization, it accounts for 6% to 18% of all cases of childhood blindness [6]. The occurrence of both severely affects the prognosis and quality of life of preterm infants.

In recent years, the correlation between BPD and ROP has gradually attracted the attention of researchers. More and more studies have found that the occurrence of BPD and ROP is closely related to factors such as oxygen exposure and abnormal vascular development. Therefore, they share commonalities in pathogenesis and often occur simultaneously. Many studies based on inflammatory mediators (such as interleukin-6, monocyte chemoattractant protein 1) [7,8], oxidative stress [9] etc. also suggest that there may be a common pathological pathway for the occurrence of BPD and ROP. On this basis, according to the relevant literature, 60%–71% of preterm infants with BPD have combined ROP [10], suggesting that there may be a potential link between the two occurrences. The study by Jasleen [11] et al. also confirmed that BPD was significantly associated with the occurrence of ROP. Preterm infants are born with a high dependence on assisted ventilation due to immature lung development and respiratory system functional defects. Hyperoxia and oxygen fluctuations trigger abnormal retinal vascular proliferation in preterm infants [12]. Thus, many therapeutic measures for BPD, such as oxygenation and mechanical ventilation, have been associated with the development of ROP [11]. Type 1 ROP is the most severe type of ROP, progressing rapidly with a very high risk of blindness and requiring treatment as soon as possible. type 2 ROP is in a stage that requires close follow-up, with treatment being considered as necessary depending on the progression of the lesion. In this study, the effect of BPD on ROP was studied in depth, and the difference in the incidence of combined type 1 or type 2 ROP between infants in the BPD group and non-BPD group was compared in groups of preterm infants of different gestational ages and birth weights. We also explored the risk factors for infants with BPD with combined type 1 or type 2 ROP, with the aim of early detection of high-risk infants and timely clinical guidance and intervention.

Methods

Subjects

This was a retrospective cohort study, and the clinical data were obtained from preterm infants hospitalized in the neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) of the Affiliated Children’s Hospital of Zhengzhou University, China, between January 2019 and December 2023.Inclusion criteria for the study: birth gestational age of 24–32 weeks, all infants survived at 45 weeks of postmenstrual age (PMA). Exclusion criteria for the study: those with severe life-threatening congenital heart disease, inborn genetic and metabolic disorders; Abandoning treatment or dying during hospitalization; incomplete data; and mothers of the infants had a history of alcohol abuse, smoking, and drug abuse (Figure 1). The study was reviewed by the Ethics Committee of Henan Children’s Hospital (approval number:2024-109-001) and followed the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was waived by the ethics committee due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Flowchart of participant screening for this study. GA: Gestational Age.

ROP screening and typing

The first ROP screening was performed at 4 weeks postnatally, and each infants was examined with a Retcam III Wide-field Digital Retinal Imaging System (Massie, USA). Half an hour before the examination, the infants were dilated with compound tropicamide eye drops (Mydrin-P®, containing 5 mg/mL tropicamide and 5 mg/mL phenylephrine hydrochloride), and during the examination, surface anesthesia was applied with proparacaine hydrochloride eye drops (Alcaine®, 0.5% equivalent to 5 mg/mL; common packaging: 15 mL:75 mg), and the eyelids were opened with the eyelid opener for preterm infants. The 130° lens of the Wide-field Digital Retinal Imaging System was used to examine the fundus retina of the infants’ eyes, with the right eye examined first and the left eye examined later, and one image was taken of each of the optic papillae and the macula as the center, and then the posterior pole, the superior retina, the nasal side, the inferior side, the temporal side of the fundus, and the vascular condition were examined in the order. The entire examination should be conducted by qualified ophthalmologists and nursing staff, with the final report issued by a certified ophthalmologist. If no ROP lesions are detected at the first screening at 4 weeks postnatal/32 weeks PMA in preterm infants, the screening should be repeated every 1–2 weeks until peripheral retinal vascularisation is complete or until 45 weeks PMA without active lesions; if stage 1 or 2 lesions are detected, in principle, weekly reviews are indicated to monitor progression closely; However, in the case of Zone II stage 2 combined Plus, intervention was required. For other stage 1 and 2 lesions, if the lesions are stable or regressing, the follow-up interval should be extended to once every two weeks until the above conditions for stopping follow-up are met; and in the case of Zone I stage 3(with or without Plus disease), or Zone II stage 3 with Plus disease, an ophthalmologist should promptly initiate interventions such as peripheral retinal laser photocoagulation or intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)injection. The traditional CRYO-ROP term ‘threshold period ROP’ refers to a 3-phase ROP, located in Zone 1 or 2, with Plus, with a range of occupancy of ≥5 consecutive clock hours or ≥8 cumulative clock hours, and usually in time for intervention; Stage 3 of II without Plus is not an indication for treatment and should be closely followed up; for advanced lesions (stage 4 or 5) with detached retina, surgical intervention should be performed promptly (Figure 2). The frequency of follow-up and the treatment plan are individualised by the ophthalmologist based on the infant’s examination results and general condition, and screening for ROP is terminated before 45 weeks PMA.

Flowchart of ROP Screening and Follow-Up in Preterm Infants. ROP: Retinopathy of Prematurity; PMA: Postmenstrual Age; VEGF: Vascular endothelial growth factor; +: with Plus disease; –: without Plus disease.

Zoning (Zone I, II, III), staging (Stage 1–5), and special lesions (Plus Disease) of ROP were evaluated according to the International Classification of Retinopathy of Prematurity (ICROP) [13,14]. The classification of ROP is based on the research criteria of Early Treatment for Retinopathy of Prematurity (ETROP). Lesions that are high-risk and require treatment within 48–72 h are defined as type 1 ROP (zone I, any stage ROP with Plus disease; zone I, stage 3 ROP without Plus disease; zone II, stage 2 or 3 ROP with Plus disease). Lesions at low risk requiring close follow-up observation are defined as type 2 ROP (zone I, stage 1 or 2 ROP without Plus disease or zone II, stage 3 ROP without Plus disease) [15].

Clinical data collection

According to the 2018 revised diagnostic criteria for BPD by The National Institute of ChildHealth and Human Development (NICHD) [16], preterm infants born at a gestational age of <32 weeks, who at 36 weeks PMA were still dependent on varying degrees of oxygen therapy or respiratory support for ≥3 days and persistent parenchymal lung disease confirmed by imaging [16]. Accordingly, all enrolled children were divided into BPD and non-BPD groups. The general information, perinatal conditions, and adverse outcomes during hospitalization of the two groups of extremely premature infants were compared. General information included sex, gestational age, birth weight, surfactant application, the duration of mechanical ventilation and number of blood transfusions within the first 28 postnatal days; perinatal conditions included maternal age, gestational hypertension, premature rupture of membranes, multiple births (twins/more than twins), births by cesarean section, and clinical chorioamnionitis; and adverse outcomes during the hospitalization, in addition to ROP and BPD, included late-onset sepsis(LOS), grade 3–4 intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), necrotizing enterocolitis of stage II and above, and grade II-IV periventricular leukomalacia (PVL). Among these, LOS [17] is defined as a positive blood culture with antibiotic treatment administered for ≥5 days. The IVH was graded according to Papile et al.’s classification system [18]; the NEC was staged according to Bell et al.’s criteria [19]; the PVL was classified based on de Vries et al.’s grading system [20].

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (version 21.0, IBM Crop, New York, USA). Continuous variables conforming to normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and t-test for two independent samples was used for comparison between groups; categorical variables were expressed as cases (%), and chi-square test or Fisher exact test was used for comparison between two groups. To investigate whether the difference in the incidence of combined type 1 or type 2 ROP in BPD and non-BPD infants differed by gestational age and birth weight, we performed an interaction analysis between subgroups using the Mantel-Haenszel test; Univariate logistic regression analysis was used to screen out the factors related to the occurrence of type 1 or type 2 ROP in infants with BPD. These factors were taken as independent variables, and whether type 1 or type 2 ROP occurred was taken as the dependent variable. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to determine the risk factors for type 1 or type 2 ROP in infants with BPD. Two-sided test, p < 0.05 was considered a statistically significant difference.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 750 cases of very preterm babies were included in the study. Out of 750 VLBWI 127 (16.93%) were in the BPD group and 623 (83.07%) were in the non-BPD group. There were no significant differences in gender, maternal age, and other aspects between infants in the BPD group and the non-BPD group(p > 0.05). When comparing the application of surfactant, the duration of mechanical ventilation, and the number of blood transfusions within the first 28 postnatal days, the differences were statistically significant (p < 0.05) (Table 1).

Incidence of ROP and other complications in infants of both groups

There was a statistically significant difference between the BPD group and the control group in the overall incidence of type 1 or type 2 ROP (31.5% vs. 8.2%; p < 0.001). The BPD group had a significantly higher incidence of infants with type 1 (16.5% vs. 4.0%,p < 0.001) and type 2 ROP (15.0% vs. 4.2%, p ≤ 0.001) than the control group. The BPD group had a significantly higher incidence of IVH, LOS, and PVL than the control group (p = 0.008, p < 0.001, p = 0.032). The incidence of NEC was similar in both groups (p = 0.704) (Table 2).

Subgroup analyses

A subgroup analysis of 750 preterm infants according to gestational age (<28 weeks, 28–296/7 weeks, and 30–32 weeks), birth weight (<1000 g, 1000–1499 g, and ≥1500 g), and sex showed that both gestational age and birth weight significantly affected the incidence of type 1 ROP, type 2 ROP, and type 1 or type 2 ROP in infants with BPD (p < 0.01). For Type 1 ROP, the BPD group demonstrated a significantly greater incidence than the control group in both boys (11.5% vs. 4.4%; p = 0.010) and girls (27.5% vs. 3.4%; p < 0.001). The incidence of type 1 ROP in infants in the BPD group was differently higher than in the control group in all three different gestational age subgroups. The incidence of type 1 ROP was significantly higher in infants with BPD at gestational age <28 weeks (43.8% vs. 10.3%; p = 0.009), 28–296/7 (15.8% vs. 2.9%; p < 0.001) weeks than in the control group. Similarly, the incidence of type 1 ROP was significantly higher in infants in the BPD group with birth weights of <1000 g (55.6% vs. 11.4%; p = 0.001), 1000–1499 g (10.8% vs. 3.7%; p = 0.009) than in the control group. The results of the interaction analysis showed that the effect of BPD on type 1 ROP was independent of gestational age stratification (p = 0.344) and birth weight stratification (p = 0.346) (Table 3).

For type 2 ROP, the incidence of type 2 ROP was significantly higher in the case group than in the control group, both in boys (11.5% vs. 4.1%; p = 0.009) and girls (22.5% vs. 4,2%; p < 0.001). Gestational age (OR = 0.599; 95%CI 0.379–0.946) and birth weight (OR = 0.996; 95%CI 0.993–0.998) is a protective factor for type 1 or type 2 ROP in infants with BPD (p < 0.05) (Table 4).

Discussion

BPD and ROP are common diseases that frequently occur in premature infants. The occurrence of both is the result of the combined effect of multiple factors [21,22]. The analysis of this study demonstrates that infants in the BPD group exhibited a significantly higher incidence of ROP compared to the control group, independent of gender or ROP classification (including both Type 1 and Type 2). BPD is common in very preterm infants (gestational age ≤32 weeks) with an underdeveloped respiratory center and lung, resulting in this preterm population’s dependence on longer periods of oxygen therapy or mechanical ventilation to maintain normal oxygen saturation, and also making them at prolonged risk for periods of exposure to hyperoxia and oxygen fluctuations. Preterm infants are extremely sensitive to oxygen, and prolonged ventilation not only directly damages alveolar epithelial cells and capillary endothelial cells [23] but also releases a large number of inflammatory mediators, exacerbating lung tissue damage. After the retina is exposed to hyperoxia, retinal vasoconstriction is first induced, causing retinal hypoxia, prompting the production of VEGF and insulin-like growth factor 1(IGF-1) and triggering abnormal retinal vascular proliferation [24]. A prospective cohort study of infants with BPD by Li W [25]et al. also showed a higher incidence of ROP in the BPD group compared to the non-BPD group, which they also explained as a result of the greater susceptibility to hyperoxia exposure in infants with BPD; The study by Wu H et al. [26] reported congruent findings, demonstrating a significantly higher proportion of infants developing ROP in the BPD group compared to the non-BPD group. The persistent pulmonary inflammation, BPD-associated growth restriction(characterized by reduced serum IGF-1 levels) [27,28], and hemodynamic instability (e.g. pulmonary hypertension) [29] constitute key predisposing factors for ROP development in infants with BPD. All the above findings suggest that the occurrence of BPD and ROP is not independent and often occurs simultaneously. The interaction between the two, inflammatory responses and oxidative stress, etc., can all lead to the occurrence of both diseases.

Subgroup analyses in this study found that the risk of developing combined type 1 or type 2 ROP was significantly higher in infants in the BPD group with decreasing gestational age stratification and birth weight stratification. Multifactorial logistic regression also showed that low gestational age and low birth weight were risk factors for the development of type 1 or type 2 ROP in infants with BPD. This increased risk may be attributed not only to the greater likelihood of prolonged respiratory support in BPD infants with lower gestational age and birth weight (as previously mentioned), but also to greater retinal immaturity and more severe BPD in this subpopulation. The retina is avascular in early fetal life, and its vascularization begins at about 16 weeks, with blood vessels growing from the optic papillae toward the peripheral portion of the retina, developing to the nasolateral serrated edge at about 8 months of age and reaching the temporal edge at 9 months of age [30]. Lung development in preterm infants may be abruptly interrupted at the tubular or saccular stage, resulting in fundamental changes in lung morphology [31]. Therefore, preterm birth aborts the critical process of fetal lung tissue and retina development in the mother’s uterus.

Lower gestational age and birth weight correlate with less mature organ development, increasing vulnerability to oxidative stress, inflammation, and other adverse factors that predispose to BPD and ROP. A study of the incidence and risk factors for ROP found that infants with ROP had significantly lower gestational age and birth weight than those without ROP. Adjusted multivariate analysis accounting for confounding variables found that low gestational age and low birth weight were significant risk factors for ROP [32]. In addition, Xu [33] et al. found that among 168 preterm infants with gestational age <32 weeks, the gestational age and birth weight of infants with moderately severe (grade 2/3) BPD were significantly lower than that of infants with no or mild (grade 0/1) BPD, suggesting that infants with low gestational age and birth weight are more likely to develop severe BPD. These emphasizes the importance of standardized ROP screening for preterm infants of low gestational age and low birth weight, especially in combination with BPD, when preterm birth is unavoidable.

The results of this study also show that the duration of mechanical ventilation and the number of blood transfusions within the first 28 postnatal days are risk factors for type 1 or type 2 ROP in infants with BPD. This is similar to the results of the currently known risk factors for ROP. Abdel-Aziz [34] et al. found that BPD, low gestational age, low birth weight, blood transfusion, respiratory support treatment, and duration are risk factors for the occurrence of ROP. Prolonged mechanical ventilation serves as a clinical indicator of pulmonary immaturity and heightened disease severity in preterm infants. A large retrospective study [12] found a significantly higher incidence of ROP in infants requiring invasive mechanical ventilation compared with those on nasal cannula oxygen and noninvasive positive pressure ventilation, with noninvasive positive pressure ventilation not being independently associated with ROP, suggesting that the effect of mechanical ventilation on ROP is due more to hyperoxia exposure and oxygen fluctuations.BPD is a chronic oxygen-dependent disease of preterm infants, which not only leads to elevated oxygen concentration in preterm infants, but also prolongs the duration of oxygen use. Prolonged mechanical ventilation causes the retina of premature infants to be exposed to excessive oxygen and oxygen fluctuations, thereby triggering oxidative stress and inflammation and interfering with normal retinal angiogenesis [11,35]. Infants with BPD are predominantly very preterm. They are prone to anemia due to poor intrauterine development, inadequate levels of erythropoietin and iron in the blood, and medical blood loss. A cohort by Jun Duan [36] found that the mean hematocrit of infants with BPD was lower than those in infants without BPD at 1, 7, 14, and 21 days postnatal, and the incidence of anemia was significantly higher in the BPD group than in the non-BPD group, which also confirms the predisposition of children with BPD to anemia. In general, blood transfusion in preterm infants can improve oxygen supply, alleviate symptoms of hypoxia, promote growth and development, and increase survival rates [37]. However, a large body of evidence suggests that red blood cell transfusion is associated with the occurrence of ROP [37]. A Meta-analysis [38]including more than 15,000 preterm infants showed that red blood cell transfusion was significantly associated with the development of ROP (pooled OR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.27–1.76), and the effect size magnified inversely with gestational age. A multicentre cohort study by Wang X et al. [39] also showed that the number of blood transfusions within the first 28 postnatal days was associated with an increased risk of ROP in preterm infants. The author, Cibulskis CC et al. [38], concluded that, unlike fetal hemoglobin (HbF), adult hemoglobin (HbA) has a lower affinity for oxygen. Blood transfusion can cause the oxygen dissociation curve to shift to the right and enhance oxygen extraction efficacy in the developing retina, thereby increasing the risk of ROP. Consequently, infants with BPD are more likely to have comorbid ROP.

BPD and ROP occur largely as a result of the application of oxygen therapy to improve the survival of preterm infants. However, prolonged oxygen therapy inevitably increases the risk of hyperoxia exposure and oxygen saturation fluctuations. As pointed out by M Elizabeth and Robert H in their review [40], oxygen saturation fluctuation has been recognized as a risk factor for ROP. Numerous studies have shown that high oxygen saturation (>95%) is associated with the development of BPD and ROP [41], whereas low oxygen saturation (<90%) can lead to preterm infant mortality and increased incidence of NEC [42]. Therefore, maintaining oxygen saturation in the range of 90%–95% is mostly recommended at present. Secondly, be aware of anemia in preterm infants. Delayed cord clamping, use of microblood tests whenever possible, prophylactic use of pro-hematopoietic drugs (e.g. erythropoietin injections), and reduction in the number of unnecessary blood transfusions. Infections and poor weight gain during hospitalisation in preterm infants also play an important role in the development of ROP. A growing number of studies have found that infection and inflammatory load are associated with the development of ROP, especially in very preterm infants. Studies suggest that neonatal sepsis, particularly late-onset sepsis (LOS), may not only be a risk factor for ROP, but also a relatively independent pathogenic factor that is not related to oxygen concentration [43]. Poor weight gain is often seen as an indirect indication of serum IGF-1 and systemic nutritional status.IGF-1 is a key regulator of physiological retinal vasculogenesis, and poor postnatal weight gain in preterm infants results in persistently low levels of serum IGF-1, which are unable to effectively mediate VEGF signalling, leading to impaired retinal vascular development [6]. The Chinese population-based WINROP study [44] included 590 preterm infants with gestational age <32 weeks and found that infants with severe ROP had slower weight gain during the first 9–10 weeks after birth compared to those without severe ROP. And postnatal weight gain may serve as a valuable indicator for predicting the risk of ROP in low-birth-weight infants. The study also found that BPD infants of low gestational age and birth weight are more likely to have complications of type 1 or 2 ROP, so the first priority in preventing BPD and ROP is to avoid preterm birth. If preterm birth is unavoidable, regular funduscopic examination of preterm and low birth weight infants and early intervention for infants at risk of ROP are needed to reduce the incidence and severity of BPD and ROP.

This study has several limitations. First, only preterm infants hospitalized in the NICUs of the Affiliated Children’s Hospital of Zhengzhou University were included as study subjects in this study, so the sample size is still small. Multicenter and large-sample clinical studies are warranted in the future; Second, while this study analyzed the incidence of type 1 and 2 ROP in BPD infants, it did not explore BPD severity stratification. This omission is worth discussing, particularly as existing evidence indicates that infants with moderate to severe BPD are more prone to the occurrence of ROP. Future studies should incorporate the clinical severity grades of BPD and analyze the differences in the incidence of type 1 and 2 ROP in infants with different grades of BPD to more accurately assess the relationship between BPD and ROP; Third, although this study explored the risk factors for combined ROP in children with BPD through multivariate analysis, it has not yet integrated factors such as ventilator parameters, other ventilation modalities (e.g. nasal cannula oxygen, noninvasive positive pressure ventilation), and other factors, which have been found by many studies to play an important role in the development of BPD and ROP; Finally, the study did not analyze and discuss angiogenic mediators, inflammatory markers, etc., as they also affect the occurrence and development of BPD and ROP. Therefore, in the future, more comprehensive clinical data should be collected for integrated analysis.

Conclusion

We found that BPD had a significant effect on the occurrence of ROP. The incidence of type 1 or type 2 ROP in infants in the BPD group was significantly higher than that in the control group. The lower the gestational age and birth weight of BPD infants, the higher the risk of type 1 or type 2 ROP. In addition to gestational age and birth weight, the duration of mechanical ventilation and the number of blood transfusions within the first 28 postnatal days are both risk factors for BPD infants with type 1 or type 2 ROP. The study emphasizes the importance of ROP monitoring in a population of preterm BPD infants of low gestational age and birth weight. For high-risk infants, an individualized oxygen therapy strategy should be adopted, aiming to minimize the duration of mechanical ventilation and reduce unnecessary blood transfusions. Due to the relatively low overall incidence of ROP, larger clinical studies are needed in the future to investigate the effect of BPD on ROP.
