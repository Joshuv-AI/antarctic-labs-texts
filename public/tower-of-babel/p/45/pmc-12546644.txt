Follicular carcinoma of the thyroid with a single metastatic lesion in the skull: A case report
Authors: Amir Hassan Matini, Sam Mirfendereski, Akram Tajoddini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC) represents a significant subset of thyroid malignancies following papillary thyroid cancer (PTC), constituting approximately 10% of all thyroid cancers. Surgical excision of metastatic lesions remains the cornerstone of treatment due to its potential for cure, symptom relief, and favorable response to radioactive iodine therapy. We present an exceedingly rare case involving a 79-year-old woman with a history of thyroidectomy 18 years prior, who presented to our hospital, Kashan Shahid Beheshti Hospital, with metastatic follicular thyroid carcinoma to the skull. FTC is characterized by early hematogenous spread, with the bone representing a typical site of metastasis. In patients diagnosed with FTC, large tumor size and presence of bone metastases are indicative of poor prognostic factors. However, the presence of a solitary bone metastasis should not preclude aggressive therapeutic interventions. Appropriate management strategies can lead to improved outcomes and enhanced quality of life for affected individuals.

Introduction

FTC ranks as the second most prevalent type of thyroid cancer following papillary carcinoma, constituting 10%-20% of all thyroid malignancies [1]. Demographically, FTC typically manifests in individuals aged between 40 and 60 years, with a higher incidence observed in females [2]. This malignancy is characterized by hematogenous spread, with metastases occurring most frequently in the lungs (49%) and bones (7-28%). Among bone metastases, the spine emerges as the most common site (33.9%), followed by the pelvis (30.5%), skull (27.1%), long bones (16.9%), and sternum (11.9%) [1]. Distant metastases develop during follow-up in 2.2% to 23% of patients [3]. Prognosis tends to deteriorate for individuals with metastatic disease, as evidenced by a ten-year survival rate ranging from 3% to 21% in the presence of bone metastasis [1]. Nonetheless, certain additional treatments may alleviate tumor burden for select patients, offering either survival or palliative benefits. Management options for patients with metastatic thyroid cancer include surgical excision, external-beam radiation, radioactive iodine therapy with I-131, or participation in clinical trials. Complete surgical resection of metastases is advocated by the majority of experts, as large tumors and bone metastases are associated with poor response to radioactive iodine therapy [1]. However, instances of well-differentiated thyroid cancer with direct invasion or bone metastases to the skull are infrequent and have seldom been reported. Herein, we present a case of follicular thyroid cancer with metastasis to the skull.

Case presentation

A 79-year-old woman with a history of thyroidectomy 18 years prior presented to our hospital, Kashan Shahid Beheshti Hospital, with a 2-year history of a large, painless swelling on her skull. Physical examination revealed a mass measuring 75 × 50 × 50 mm located in the frontal bone. The patient did not have nausea and vomiting or symptoms of decreased level of consciousness. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) depicted a sizable, heterogeneous mass involving the scalp, accompanied by destruction of the right frontal bone and the tumor touching brain, and first diagnosis is meningioma (Fig. 1). Discussion

In recent years, the incidence of thyroid cancer has steadily increased, making it 1 of the most prevalent malignant tumors. FTC accounts for approximately 10% of thyroid cancer cases and is characterized by hematologic metastasis, with a propensity for distant spread. Its invasiveness, metastatic rate, and mortality surpass those of PTC [4], and it predominantly affects women over 50 years of age [4]. FTC often presents with no overt clinical symptoms and may go undetected until patients undergo ultrasound examinations or develop symptoms related to distant metastasis. Common sites of metastasis include the bone, lungs, and brain [4]. Studies indicate that 10%-15% of FTC cases exhibit distant metastasis, with the lungs and bones being the most prevalent sites, followed by the brain, liver, and skin [4].

While cranial involvement in FTC is rare, it has been documented in the literature. The largest cohorts, based on Korean and Chinese studies, reported frequencies of 0.32% and 0.19%, respectively [5]. Prognostic factors for thyroid cancer metastasis development primarily include female gender, age over 45 years, degree of differentiation, and follicular histological type [5]. These metastases typically manifest long after the initial diagnosis of thyroid cancer [5]. Most cranial lesions described in the literature are located on the vault, with the occipital bone being the most common site, followed by the parietal and frontal bones in similar proportions [5]. Other affected areas include the sphenoidal and ethmoidal sinuses, pituitary gland, pontocerebellar angle, and parapharyngeal region [5]. Inflammatory mass such as chronic inflammatory mass, acute suppurative and granulomatous inflammatory mass, Fibrofatty mass, Lipogranulomatous inflammatory mass, cyst, granuloma, abscess. trauma related hematoma, skin tumor, meningioma, lymphoma, osteoma are the most important differential diagnosis [6].

Our patient's case represents an advanced, aggressive, and life-threatening manifestation due to significant delays in diagnosis. These lesions were initially misinterpreted as sarcomas due to their hypervascularized osteolytic tumor process with necrotic zones. Current therapeutic approaches for cranial metastases include surgical excision of thyroid and cranial metastases whenever feasible, radioactive iodine therapy, external radiotherapy, and targeted therapy as warranted [5]. The prognosis of these patients may be further compromised by the appearance of extracranial metastases or local and regional disease progression leading to mortality [5]. Survival rates appear to improve when only 1 or 2 cranial sites are involved without concurrent intracranial lesions [5]. This case underscores the importance of early intervention in patients to enhance their survival prognosis.

Conclusion

This case, alongside related studies, highlights the challenging yet crucial nature of diagnosing FTC. In cases of FTC with distant metastasis, it is imperative to enhance presurgical examinations, comprehensively evaluate the patient's general condition, and engage multidisciplinary experts to devise individualized treatment plans tailored to the patient's specific circumstances. Such approaches can mitigate postoperative complications, enhance quality of life, and extend survival periods for affected individuals.
