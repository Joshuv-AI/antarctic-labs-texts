Which trajectory should we choose? — Defining safe entry–target pathways and underlying femoral morphology for bone marrow injection in osteonecrosis of the femoral head
Authors: Ken Tashiro, Yasuhiro Homma, Yuto Kawamura, Ryuji Okuno, Eiji Iwasaki, Fumihiro Mukasa, Koju Hayashi, Taiji Watari, Tomonori Baba, Muneaki Ishijima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionsBone marrow aspiration concentrate (BMAC) injection is a promising joint-preserving treatment for osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH). However, the procedure remains unstandardized, and iatrogenic complications, including subtrochanteric fractures, have been reported. Despite its growing clinical adoption, no studies have modeled this procedure in a representative cohort to evaluate its technical safety. This study aimed to establish a morphological basis for safer and more standardized needle placement by identifying entry–target combinations that provide the safest trajectories, and determining the proximal femoral parameters influencing the permissible insertion angle.MethodsForty hips without collapse or deformity from 40 patients with ONFH (20 males and 20 females) were retrospectively analyzed using computed tomography-based three-dimensional reconstructions. Four entry levels (A–D) and four femoral head target points (center–center, center–lateral, anterior–center, anterior–lateral) were defined. Needle trajectories were reconstructed, and the permissible insertion angle was defined as the range within which cortical perforation did not occur. Linear regression analyses were conducted to identify morphological factors associated with the permissible insertion angle.ResultsSix entry–target combinations achieved a 100% needle passage rate, representing the safest configurations for BMAC injection. Among these, the anterior–center target was the most accessible, allowing safe insertion at three different entry levels. The widest permissible insertion angle was observed when targeting the center–center region from entry level D (18.1° ± 3.7°). Morphological analysis revealed that a smaller minimum femoral neck diameter and greater femoral anteversion were independently associated with a narrower permissible insertion angle. Female patients demonstrated significantly narrower safe angular ranges than male patients across all entry–target combinations.ConclusionThis study quantitatively identified six safe entry–target combinations and key morphological determinants influencing the permissible insertion angle. These findings provide practical anatomical reference data to support safer, morphology-based planning for ONFH treatment.Keywords: Osteonecrosis of the femoral head, Bone marrow aspirate concentrate, 3D simulation study, Surgical safety, Needle insertion angle

Introduction

Osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) is an avascular necrosis that predominantly affects younger adults and often results in femoral head collapse and secondary osteoarthritis. The etiology of ONFH is multifactorial, involving corticosteroid use, excessive alcohol consumption, and autoimmune disorders; however, its precise pathogenesis remains incompletely understood. The natural course of ONFH frequently culminates in total hip arthroplasty (THA), particularly in advanced stages, posing long-term challenges regarding implant survival and revision burden; therefore, joint-preserving strategies have gained increasing attention, particularly in the early stages of the disease [1,2].

Among joint-preserving treatments, core decompression (CD) remains one of the most commonly performed procedures, aiming to reduce intraosseous pressure and promote revascularization of the femoral head [[3], [4], [5]]. In recent years, combining CD with regenerative cell therapy, particularly bone marrow aspirate concentrate (BMAC) injection, has shown promise in enhancing osteogenic repair and delaying or preventing structural collapse [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Hernigou et al. reported favorable long-term outcomes, demonstrating in a 30-year prospective randomized study that patients treated with BMAC injection were approximately three times less likely to require THA compared to those treated with CD alone [9]. Several clinical studies have also reported favorable outcomes in patients with pre-collapse ONFH treated with CD with BMAC injections [8,10,11]. The therapeutic potential of regenerative approaches such as BMAC injections depends not only on biological efficacy but also on accurate and safe delivery of cells to the intended target region.

However, despite the growing adoption of CD with BMAC injection, procedural safety, particularly with respect to safe needle placement, has not been sufficiently investigated [[12], [13], [14]]. Because this procedure requires drilling into the proximal femur, iatrogenic complications such as subtrochanteric or femoral neck fractures, cortical perforation, and misdirected needle placement have been reported [12]. Notably, a recent meta-analysis of core decompression procedures indicated that fractures accounted for 18.8% of all reported complications, representing the third most frequent adverse event [4]. In clinical practice, the cortical entry point and femoral head target vary according to surgeon preference, imaging guidance, and intended lesion coverage, resulting in substantial variability in needle trajectory and insertion angle. In the absence of morphology-based quantitative guidelines, these technical decisions continue to rely largely on individual experience and institutional practice. Therefore, the primary endpoint of this study was to identify the entry–target combinations that provide the safest needle placement for CD with BMAC injection. The secondary endpoints were to determine which femoral morphological parameters influenced the permissible insertion angle and to examine potential sex differences, thereby providing a morphological basis for safer and more standardized surgical planning.

Methods

Participants (Fig. 1)

Flow diagram of patient selection.

THA: Total hip arthroplasty, ONFH: Osteonecrosis of the femoral head, JIC: Japanese Investigation Committee.

This retrospective study was approved by the institutional review board. We reviewed the medical records of consecutive patients who underwent primary THA for ONFH at our institution between June 2022 and July 2023. During this period, 56 patients (26 males and 30 females) underwent primary THA for ONFH. Of these, four patients were excluded because preoperative whole-femur CT images were not available. The remaining 52 patients (104 hips, 26 males and 26 females) were assessed for eligibility. Subsequently, hips were excluded if there was a history of femoral or acetabular surgery or if femoral head collapse was present, corresponding to Japanese Investigation Committee (JIC) stage 3 or 4 ONFH. After applying these exclusion criteria, a total of 40 hips from 40 patients (20 males and 20 females) were included in the final analysis.

Radiographic protocol

CT images, rather than plain radiographs, were used to assess femoral morphology to ensure a precise and reproducible evaluation unaffected by magnification or rotational alignment errors. All image reconstruction, measurements, and analyses were performed using the SYNAPSE VINCENT® medical imaging system (Fujifilm Medical, Tokyo, Japan). Scans were acquired with a slice thickness of 1.0 mm.

All measurements in this study were performed using this system unless otherwise specified.

Definition of needle entry level (Fig. 3a)

Definition of entry levels and target points

(3a): The center of the femoral head (O), the inferior edge of the lateral prominence of the greater trochanter (A), and the inferior edge of the lesser trochanter (D) were identified. The angles between these landmarks were trisected, and the intersections with the lateral cortex of the femur were defined as points B and C.

(3b): A sphere (S) with a radius 5 mm smaller than the femoral head radius was created and centered at the femoral head center (O). On the coronal plane passing through point O, the superior-most point of S was defined as point A, the lateral-most point as point B, and their midpoint as point C. On the axial plane passing through point C, the anterior-most point of S was defined as point D, and the midpoint between points C and D as point E.

(3c): The 3D positions of the target points are also shown. Each target point was renamed according to its positional relationship on the sagittal–coronal coordinate system; for example, point E, located anterior to the femoral head center on the sagittal plane and lateral to it on the coronal plane, was renamed as the anteroposterior anterior–lateral target point.

Four needle entry levels were defined along the lateral aspect of the femur. The most proximal entry, designated as level A, was defined as the inferior edge of the lateral prominence of the greater trochanter. The most distal entry level, designated as level D, was defined at the inferior edge of the lesser trochanter, as a more distal entry would require removal of a greater volume of cortical bone, potentially compromising femoral stability and increasing the risk of subtrochanteric fractures [12,21]. Two additional entry levels, designated as levels B and C, were determined by trisecting the angle DOA (formed by entry levels A, O, and D) and identifying the intersection points of each dividing line with the lateral cortical surface of the femur, arranged in a proximal-to-distal order.

Definition of target point (Fig. 3b and c)

In ONFH, the extent and distribution of necrotic lesions vary among patients; however, several studies have reported that necrosis is frequently localized in the anterior region of the femoral head in the sagittal plane [22]. Moreover, necrotic lesions extending anteriorly and laterally toward the weight-bearing surface have been associated with an increased risk of femoral head collapse [[22], [23], [24]]. Accordingly, target points in this study were selected within the anterior and lateral regions of the femoral head adjacent to the weight-bearing surface, where necrosis repair is considered most effective for preventing collapse. The target points were determined as follows. First, the center of the femoral head (point O) and its radius (R) were measured. A virtual sphere (S) with a radius of (R – 5 mm), centered at O, was constructed to represent the subchondral zone located 5 mm beneath the articular surface [9]. In the coronal plane passing through the center of the femoral head, the superiormost and lateralmost points on sphere S were defined as points A and B, respectively. The midpoint between points A and B was defined as point C. Next, in the axial plane passing through point C, the most anterior point on sphere S was defined as point D. The midpoint between points D and C was defined as point E. Four points (A, C, D, and E) were selected as the needle insertion targets (Fig. 3b). For clarity, each target point was renamed based on its anatomical location relative to the center of the femoral head, described first in the sagittal orientation (anterior or center) and then in the coronal orientation (lateral or center): point A as center–center, point C as center–lateral, point D as anterior–center, and point E as anterior–lateral (Fig. 3c).

Needle placement and measurement of insertion angles (Fig. 4)

Needle placement simulation and measurement of permissible insertion angles

An example of a needle trajectory simulation from entry level C to the center-to-center target point is shown. A 6.2 mm cylinder was used to simulate the needle trajectory. An axial oblique image was reconstructed along the needle insertion plane from the target point (center–center) to the entry level (C). The anterior and posterior limits of safe needle placement were also illustrated. α: ngle limit (anterior), β:Angle limit (posterior), γ:Permissible insertion range.

After defining the entry level and target points, planes passing through each combination of the target point and entry level were reconstructed. To simulate the injection pathway, a virtual cylinder with a diameter of 6.2 mm (corresponding to the maximum diameter of the injection needle in the PerFuse system®; Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA, which is widely utilized in clinical practice and has demonstrated clinical efficacy in previous studies [14,25,26]) was created to simulate the trajectory of the injection needle. The permissible insertion range was defined as the angle at which the needle could reach the target without scraping the cortical bone of the femoral neck, ensuring an appropriate entry point. The anterior limit of the entry point was set to prevent scraping of the anterior cortical bone at the entry level, while the posterior limit was defined as the gluteal tuberosity. Both the anterior (α) and posterior declination angles (β) from the retrocondylar plane were measured, and the angle range between them (γ) was calculated to represent the permissible insertion angle for safe needle placement. Fig. 4 illustrates an example using entry level C and target point center-center, showing the simulated needle paths.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were assessed using Pearson's χ2 test. For continuous variables, either Student's t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test was used, depending on the data distribution. Multiple linear regression analyses were performed to investigate which proximal femoral morphological factors were significantly associated with the permissible insertion angle. Femoral morphological parameters and insertion-related parameters (α, β, and γ angles) were independently measured by two board-certified orthopedic surgeons (KT and RO), and the mean of the two measurements was used for the analysis. Interobserver reliability was confirmed with intraclass correlation coefficients exceeding 0.91. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro 18 (JMP Statistical Software, Cary, NC, USA), and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

This study aimed to establish a morphological basis for safe and reproducible needle insertion trajectories to optimize the delivery of BMAC during core decompression. Three-dimensional reconstructions and virtual trajectory simulations were used to evaluate which entry–target combinations provide safe access and to assess how proximal femoral morphology influences the feasibility and safety of needle placement. In the context of this study, “safety” refers specifically to the avoidance of trajectory-related structural complications around the proximal femur. Because the simulated trajectories were designed to remain intraosseous, the primary concern is cortical violation rather than extraosseous injury, such as damage to surrounding vessels or nerves. Unintended cortical violation can compromise structural integrity, create stress concentration points, and increase the risk of subtrochanteric or femoral neck fractures, which account for 18.8% of reported complications following core decompression [4]. The permissible insertion angle quantified in this study represents the angular corridor within which the needle can reach the intended target without violating the cortex, thereby minimizing these structural risks. Our findings identified key anatomical parameters to consider during procedural planning and underscored the importance of accounting for individual anatomical variability—particularly regarding insertion angle and entry level—to enhance technical accuracy and reduce complications. In addition, these findings also have implications for determining which types of ONFH may be most suitable for joint-preserving procedures such as CD with or without BMAC, as lesion location and extent, especially anterior and lateral involvement, are known to influence the risk of femoral head collapse and thus warrant careful patient selection before structural failure occurs.

The key finding of this study was that targeting the lateral region of the femoral head for CD with BMAC injection was technically more challenging, whereas aiming slightly anteriorly provided a safer needle trajectory. This finding is supported by the results in Table 2, which show that no entry level allowed for completely safe insertion when targeting the center-lateral region, whereas the anterior-center target demonstrated an exceptionally high needle passage rate across multiple entry levels. Although selecting an effective target point is clinically important to maximize therapeutic efficacy, procedural safety must first be ensured. Previous studies have suggested that lateral extension of necrosis contributes to femoral head collapse, as reflected in the JIC and Association Research Circulation Osseous (ARCO) classifications [27,28] and confirmed by the biomechanical findings of Karasuyama et al. [29]. Nonetheless, our results indicated that a more central and slightly anterior target represented a practical compromise between safety and efficacy. These findings do not suggest that drilling should be categorically avoided in patients with center–lateral necrotic lesions; they indicate that such trajectories carry higher technical risk. When lateral targeting is clinically necessary, the use of a smaller-diameter drill, together with careful preoperative planning, may help mitigate procedural risks.

With respect to entry level, our data showed that more distal entries were associated with higher needle passage rates, suggesting that the procedural safety zone may extend distally. However, this finding should not be interpreted as a blanket recommendation to favor distal entry, because our results did not show a progressive widening of the permissible insertion range with more distal levels, and distal entry itself has been associated with an increased risk of fracture. A meta-analysis reported a 0.9% incidence of subtrochanteric fractures after core decompression, accounting for 18.8% of all complications [4]. These concerns are supported by finite element analyses showing that distal cortical entry removes a greater volume of cortical bone, resulting in increased mechanical stress and cortical thinning, thereby elevating the fracture risk [30]. Moreover, even without a complete cortical cut-out, repositioning the entry point or creating multiple insertion tracts further compromises structural integrity [13] and such fractures may occur postoperatively without intraoperative signs [12]. Although our study did not identify a universally safe entry level, our findings emphasize the importance of patient-specific surgical planning and performing the procedure with a single, well-aligned trajectory to minimize the risk of iatrogenic fractures.

The multivariate analysis revealed that a smaller minimum femoral neck diameter and greater femoral anteversion were independently associated with a reduced permissible angle range. While it is intuitive that a narrower femoral neck limits the angular corridor for safe needle placement, the finding that increased anteversion also restricts this range is particularly noteworthy. Femoral anteversion showed greater interindividual variability than the minimum neck diameter (18.2 ± 10.0° vs. 24.1 ± 2.4 mm), suggesting that underestimation of anteversion during preoperative planning could markedly affect the permissible range. Moreover, in patients with increased femoral anteversion, the entry point must be positioned more posteriorly and the needle trajectory must be angled more anteriorly to reach the target, which increases the risk of violating the anterior cortical wall of the femoral neck. Thus, increased anteversion not only narrows the permissible range but also increases the technical difficulty and structural risk of the procedure. Moreover, because anteversion cannot be reliably quantified using standard intraoperative fluoroscopic views, special caution is required during surgical planning. In the subgroup analysis based on sex, females demonstrated a significantly smaller permissible angle range. This finding is likely the combined result of a smaller minimum femoral neck diameter and a tendency toward greater femoral anteversion, although the latter did not reach statistical significance. These results underscore the importance of detailed preoperative morphological assessment, particularly in female patients, to ensure safe and accurate needle placement during CD with BMAC injection.

The CT-based findings of this study can be translated into clinical practice through a two-step approach: preoperative CT-based planning to determine patient-specific safe trajectories, followed by fluoroscopy-guided needle insertion. Although CD and BMAC injection is typically performed under fluoroscopic guidance, the permissible insertion corridor is inherently three-dimensional and cannot be fully characterized on two-dimensional radiographs; thus, CT evaluation remains essential for individualized surgical planning. Key morphological parameters, including minimum femoral neck diameter and femoral anteversion, cannot be accurately measured using standard fluoroscopic views. Given the substantial interindividual variation in femoral anteversion, reliance on fluoroscopy alone may increase procedural risk. Importantly, the retrocondylar coordinate system used in this study was selected for its compatibility with intraoperative fluoroscopy, and its anatomical landmarks can be reliably identified on standard fluoroscopic views. Therefore, the entry levels and target points defined in our three-dimensional analysis can be reproducibly implemented during routine fluoroscopy-guided surgery.

This study has several limitations. First, this study was based on a simulation using CT-derived 3D reconstructions and virtual needle trajectories rather than intraoperative or cadaveric validation. Although the models were designed to reflect the clinical anatomy accurately, they do not account for real-time biomechanical responses such as microfractures, thermal necrosis, or deformation during drilling. Second, there is variability in clinical practice regarding the diameter of drills or needles used for CD and BMAC injection. Depending on the technique, multiple small-diameter drillings of approximately 3 mm or the use of larger instruments with diameters of 8–10 mm have been reported. In this study, simulations were performed using a needle diameter of 6.2 mm. While different diameters may yield quantitatively different results, the selected diameter represents a commonly used device and falls within an intermediate range that is neither extremely small nor excessively large. Therefore, the findings are considered to have reasonable generalizability across commonly employed techniques. Third, the sample size was relatively small, consisting of 40 hips with a balanced sex distribution. In addition, all participants in this study were Asian, who generally have a smaller body size and femoral morphology compared with Western populations. Finally, patients with advanced-stage ONFH were excluded to minimize confounding factors arising from femoral head deformities. Although this approach enhances internal validity, it may limit the applicability of the findings to patients with late-stage disease.

In conclusion, this study provides an anatomical basis supporting safe needle insertion during CD with BMAC injection in patients with ONFH. Six entry–target combinations that achieved a 100% needle passage rate were quantitatively identified as representing the safest options for CD with BMAC injection. Among these, the anterior center target was the most accessible, allowing safe insertion at three distinct entry levels (B, C, and D). Nevertheless, because distal insertion requires greater cortical removal, the most distal entry within the thin cortex is preferred. Morphological analysis demonstrated that a smaller minimum femoral neck diameter and greater femoral anteversion were associated with a narrower permissible insertion angle. These findings provide practical reference data for safer, morphology-based surgical planning and may guide the development of navigated or patient-specific systems for ONFH treatment.
