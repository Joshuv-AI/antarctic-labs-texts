Sports Cardiology Care in the United States
Authors: Bradley J Petek, Jonathan H Kim, Matthew W Martinez, Aaron L Baggish
Published: 2026

Fueled by the rapid global increase in competitive and recreational sports participation, sports cardiology has emerged as a distinct cardiovascular subspecialty. Sports cardiology now represents a key component of comprehensive cardiovascular service lines, but a fundamental unanswered question deserves immediate attention. What defines a sports cardiologist? At present, there are no formal training requirements, accreditation exams, mandated continuing medical education, or minimum required patient volumes. This commentary highlights gaps in current quality standards for sports cardiology in the United States and provides a roadmap forward.

The primary mission in sports cardiology is to provide high-quality cardiovascular care to people who prioritize sports and exercise for occupation (ie, competitive and tactical athletes), recreation, and personal health-based goals. Accordingly, patients who seek out sports cardiology include professional athletes, civil servant workers, and recreational “weekend warriors.” In caring for this heterogeneous population, sports cardiologists must possess a comprehensive understanding of the cardiovascular benefits and risks of high levels of exercise and accurately contextualize the benefit/risk balance individually.1,2 Athletes and highly active people may harbor or develop all forms of cardiovascular disease. In clinical practice, cardiologists with expertise in various other forms of cardiovascular subspecialties gravitate to sports cardiology based on their desire to work with athletes. At present, there are numerous entry portals into sports cardiology but no standardization or requirements.3 Future efforts to develop a unified path to becoming a competent sports cardiologist are imperative.

Exercise impacts cardiovascular physiology in many ways, and the hallmarks of exercise-induced cardiac remodeling manifest on all forms of cardiovascular diagnostic testing. Differentiating normal vs pathology among athletic patients is a fundamental sports cardiology core competency. This begins with the interpretation of the 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG), which is commonly used during pre-participation cardiovascular screening (PPCS) and symptom-driven clinical encounters.2 International consensus recommendations for the interpretation of the athlete’s ECG have been published.4 Nonetheless, significant heterogeneity persists in real-world provider interpretation. A study of 111,062 Italian athletes, an ethnically homogenous population in which the prevalence of high-risk cardiovascular disease was reported as 0.3%, documented an “abnormal ECG” rate across 26 participating physicians ranging from 0% to 7.1%.5 This provider inconsistency, emerging from a country with mandatory sports cardiology education, likely represents a best case scenario. The magnitude of this problem in the United States is likely greater. Practice variability extends to cardiac imaging modalities including transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging).6 Among 1597 athletes who underwent CMR during the COVID-19 pandemic, the reported prevalence of myocarditis, an extremely rare disease among young athletes, ranged from 0% to 7.6% across participating institutions.7 There is no plausible explanation for this reported incidence range other than problematic variability of CMR interpretation. The logical pathway to minimize clinical heterogeneity and ensure a consistent standard of care in sports cardiology includes educational initiatives and metrics to confirm competency. This will be most effective if buttressed by support from cardiovascular societies, integration of sports cardiology core competencies into fellowship training, and strengthened partnerships with professional sport leagues and sport federations.

A critical starting point for this initiative is professional sports, where changes here will have immediate impact and the potential for broader policy dissemination. Currently, there are no universal standards among U.S.-based professional sporting organizations to ensure competent sports cardiology care for their athletes. Most organizations stipulate only that a team cardiologist holds general cardiology board certification, and in some cases, that this person has at least 5 years of post-fellowship clinical experience. This model is problematic, as it provides no mechanism to ensure that the chosen cardiologist has ample experience with the care of athletes or the interpretation of PPCS ECG+/−TTE that is mandated by many sport organizations. The current U.S. model facilitates a “highest bidder” phenomenon. In this scenario, teams simply align with a hospital with sponsorship rights or one who will provide medical care for low expenditures, leaving the selection of the team cardiologist unregulated. Any cardiologist who is a sports fan would be excited about this opportunity. However, the biggest fan may not always be the best physician for the job, particularly once complex sports participation decisions arise for athletes with cardiovascular abnormalities. Effective sports cardiology requires the right skill set, substantial time commitment, and the assumption of significant medicolegal risk.8 Because of the constant evolution and complexities of the field, professional teams assume liability by having inexperienced sports cardiologists who may be prone to underdiagnosis and overdiagnosis of disease. It is essential that the field of sports cardiology organize itself and devise strategies to address these shortcomings.

The path forward necessitates the development of standardized requirements to practice as a sports cardiologist. This will be most effective if developed by experienced leaders in the field and implemented at both the medical societal level and across professional sporting organizations (Figure 1). This approach is not unique to cardiology. Sports medicine providers have both fellowship requirements and formal accreditation testing. The time has come for sports cardiology to adopt this model. Immediate future work should include the creation of formal requisites to practice as a sports cardiologist. These should include: 1) sports cardiology training opportunities; 2) certification that includes an accreditation test; 3) minimum athlete-patient volume for the determination of sport cardiology centers of excellence; and 4) ongoing continuing medical education requirements. Once in place, major sporting leagues and organizations should mandate these requirements for the men, women, and hospital systems charged with the cardiovascular care of their athletes. This work will take time and resources, but failure will lead to harm. In the interim, options exist. Leagues and organizations are encouraged to develop core networks of sports cardiology providers to review cases and provide clinical guidance. In addition, centralized interpretation of ECG and TTE data derived during PPCS may improve quality of care by reducing false negative and false positive results. Sports cardiologists take care of athletic patients who are always on the move. The time has come for us to follow suit and move the field in the direction of higher quality and universal competency.
