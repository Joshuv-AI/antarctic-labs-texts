A critical anthropological evaluation of digital media based interventions for mycetoma in Sudan; the case of “Hope for a Devastating Neglected Disease” documentary film
Authors: Osama Abu Zied Nogid El Nour, Papreen Nahar, Maya Semrau, Shahaduz Zaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMycetoma, a disfiguring and debilitating neglected tropical disease (NTD), long languished in obscurity. Prior to 2013, limited research, ineffective treatments, and inadequate global recognition plagued the disease. However, a pivotal 2013 meeting in Geneva marked a turning point. Recognizing advocacy as a critical missing link, the Drugs for Neglected Diseases Initiative (DNDi) spearheaded efforts to raise awareness and address knowledge gaps. Scientific symposia, media coverage, and research collaborations, including the DNDi trial, paved the way for mycetoma inclusion in the 2016 WHO NTD list. This recognition spurred further global attention, research, and media engagement. This study delves into the role of digital media interventions in raising awareness and combating mycetoma neglect in Sudan. Employing an anthropological lens, we critically evaluate the production and impact of these interventions, focusing on a short promotional documentary film titled “Hope for a Devastating Neglected Disease.” produced by DNDi in collaboration with a medical research center in Sudan; Produced in 2015 and made available on the DNDi’s official YouTube channel Drugs for Neglected Diseases initiative (DNDi). Through in-depth interviews with Sudanese film-makers and a public audience, we analyze the film’s content, visual representation, and perceived effectiveness in addressing social and cultural factors contributing to mycetoma neglect. Our analysis sheds light on the complex interplay between digital media, global health initiatives, and local communities in the fight against NTDs. By critically examining the narratives and power dynamics embedded within these interventions, we contribute to a nuanced understanding of their potential and limitations in addressing public health challenges in the Global South, especially in Sudan. Our study suggests that digital media alone cannot bridge the chasm of global health disparities. Unveiling the neglect requires deeper, culturally sensitive anthropological insights to guide interventions that empower communities and truly advocate for the voiceless.Keywords: Mycetoma, Sudan, Neglected tropical diseases, Social intervention, Film critique

Background

Although Sudan is notably recognized as the “homeland” of mycetoma [1], reporting the highest number of cases globally, prior studies on mycetoma in Sudan have predominantly focused on clinical management of the disease.

Mycetoma, a neglected tropical disease, is a significant public health issue in Sudan, particularly in rural regions such as in Gezira and Sennar states in central Sudan. The disease primarily impacts individuals involved in farming and herding, with delayed medical intervention often leading to severe deformities and disability [2].

Neglected Tropical Diseases (NTDs) refer to a group of diseases that prevail in tropical and subtropical areas. More than one billion people worldwide are reported to be affected by NTDs. They tend to occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including the least developed countries where poverty prevails and the health infrastructure is fragile [3]. The economic burden of NTDs is massive when considering the fragile economy of LMICs; billions of dollars every year are spent on treatment [4]. The concept of neglect can be considered a fundamental element of power dynamics, as most NTDs predominantly impact communities with limited political influence [5]. Furthermore, NTDs affect the world’s poorest populations and have historically received less attention compared to other diseases [6]. To avoid oversimplification, NTDs are termed “neglected” because, according to the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID), they are rarely fatal but can cause significant lifelong physical disabilities, such as blindness, fatigue, and disfigurement [7].

Mycetoma leads to disability, stigma, and social isolation through its chronic and progressive nature, causing severe physical deformities and functional impairments. The infection typically starts as a small, painless nodule but can spread to involve deep tissues, bones and joints, leading to large, disfiguring swellings and draining sinuses. This extensive tissue destruction can result in the loss of mobility, making it difficult for affected individuals to perform daily activities or continue working, particularly in labor-intensive occupations common in endemic areas [8]. The visible deformities and chronic nature of the disease often result in social stigma, as communities may mistakenly perceive mycetoma as a contagious [9] or divine punishment, leading to the marginalization of affected individuals [10]. Consequently, those suffering from mycetoma may experience social isolation, further exacerbated by the economic burden of the disease due to loss of income and the cost of prolonged medical treatment.

In Sudan, mycetoma is endemic in specific regions, including Sennar, Al Jazirah, and Khartoum states, where environmental factors such as proximity to water bodies, soil composition, and the presence of Acacia trees contribute to its prevalence [1].

Poor rural communities, particularly agricultural workers who are frequently in contact with soil and plant material, are at heightened risk [11]. A study conducted in the Eastern Sennar locality of Sudan reported a prevalence rate of 0.87%, with the majority of cases occurring in males at a productive age group ranging from 31 to 47 years [1]. The stigma associated with mycetoma in Sudan further increases the suffering of affected individuals, often leading to social isolation and psychological distress [11].

Late diagnosis due to limited healthcare access results in severe tissue damage, often necessitating surgical interventions such as amputations. This exacerbates the socio-economic burden on affected individuals and their families. Given the disease’s neglect and severe consequences, there is a pressing need for enhanced prevention, early detection, and integrated management strategies, particularly in endemic areas in Sudan [12]. Implementing targeted awareness campaigns is crucial to reducing stigma and improving health outcomes for those affected by mycetoma.

To tackle the issue of neglect concerning Mycetoma, various global health initiatives have been pursued. The initiatives led by DNDi are centered on research, funding, and media engagement. Consequently, there has been increased media coverage and development of media projects. The documentary film under scrutiny in this paper is a direct outcome of these initiatives.

The emphasis on clinical outcomes in research studies conducted in Sudan may explain why mycetoma research in Sudan have predominantly focused on clinical management, given the clinical nature of the research centres and the focus of the research efforts. In contrast, this study evaluates a global initiative as a social intervention, thereby exploring the social dimensions of mycetoma concurrently. Additionally, it seeks to address the oversight in incorporating social sciences in the context of Neglected Tropical Diseases (NTDs).

Evaluating the effectiveness of documentary films uploaded on social media platforms as social interventions for medical conditions is an emerging research area that has garnered attention due to the potential of these films to raise awareness and influence public health behavior. However, specific studies uniquely focusing on evaluating documentaries pertinent to NTDs are sparse, as it remains an emerging area of interest and often intersects with broader health communication strategies. Studies in this field typically examine how these films can raise awareness, educate the public, and influence health behavior for certain medical conditions. A few studies have been conducted to holistically evaluate endeavors of medical documentaries in ways that could advocate for patients by comprehending patients as an agency interacting with other societal entities.

This study aims to evaluate the documentary film under research.

A free listing exercise [13] for all social and media-based interventions was conducted prior to writing this manuscript. The free listing exercise encompassed initiatives including locally produced photography works, radio and television programs, and press articles. Additionally, educational materials targeting individuals with lived experience of mycetoma were identified. Notably, the documentary under investigation is a globally recognized initiative that aims to raise awareness and advocate for mycetoma awareness among a broader audience, having received international recognition through a prestigious award.

To achieve this, the study is structured into three main parts: assessment of the visual elements of the intervention (the documentary), analysis of its content, and evaluation of its perceived impact.

Methods

To critically evaluate the documentary, qualitative data were collected using focus group discussions (FGDs), in-depth interviews (IDIs), and participants observation. FGDs were conducted with two key participant groups: the public audience and academic researchers. Each group took part in two FGDs, resulting in four discussions total, with eight participants each (32 participants overall). All participants watched the film under study prior to the FGDs and IDIs. At the beginning of each session, a background of the study and its objectives was presented.

Following the FGDs, three participants from each group were recruited for in-depth interviews. Additionally, in-depth interviews were conducted with six film-makers to explore both the content and technical dimensions of the documentary. All data collection activities, including FGDs and IDIs, were conducted in Arabic by the lead author, who facilitated the discussions and interviews, while a trained research assistant was responsible for note-taking.

The data were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, coded, and sub-coded using thematic inductive analysis approach. Participant anonymity was maintained. The evaluation focused on three interrelated dimensions influencing the documentary’s potential to address mycetoma neglect: visual presentation, content quality, and perceived impact.

All focus groups were moderated by the lead author, with the assistance of a note taker who was appointed and trained specifically for this project. The interviews were conducted by the lead author. The researcher was not known to participants before the study in order not to have an influence on the participants’ responses.

Mycetoma places a significant burden on rural communities in Sudan [14]. Despite its impact, the disease remains under-addressed in global health, receiving limited funding and attention [15, 16]. Media advocacy tools like documentaries offer promising avenues to raise awareness and drive action [17]. This study evaluates the impact of a short documentary film on mycetoma awareness in Sudan using data collected from Sudanese participants in Khartoum.

Khartoum was selected as the study site due to its centrality, availability of academic and medical institutions, and relevance for studying local-global health intersections [18–20]. The study population comprised members of the public, academic researchers, and film-makers. Such a diversity of participants was intended to offer the evaluation experience a broader perspective of viewers from different backgrounds. Participants were purposively sampled to ensure information richness until thematic saturation was reached [21, 22]. Public audience selection criteria were flexible to include individuals from diverse socio-economic backgrounds.

Existence of previous knowledge of mycetoma was nither an exclusion or an inclusion criterion for participants.

While it was initially estimated that fewer than 40 individuals would participate, the final number reached 44: 32 participants in FGDs and 12 in IDIs (including 6 film-makers, 3 public audience members, and 3 researchers), (see Table 1). Academic researchers were selected through open calls in Khartoum-based research institutions such as the University of Khartoum and CEDEJ-Khartoum. This group included 16 males and 16 females, aged 27 to 65. Public audience participants were selected using a flexible, iterative method to ensure demographic diversity, including age [1, 11–13, 23–38], educational background, and economic status. Gender parity was achieved (50% female, 50% male).

Film-makers were selected based on their experience with documentary films addressing social stigma of medical conditions in Sudan. Due to the limited number of female professionals in the local film industry, only two females and four males (aged 29–47) were included. Recruitment was facilitated through Sudan’s largest film-making firm, known for its civil society collaborations on social and health issues. Methodological triangulation was achieved through the combination of FGDs, IDIs, and observation [39].

All FGDs and interviews were held at a research center in Khartoum as it was accessible for all participants.

The documentary evoked inquiries that remained unanswered, notably the absence of comprehensive information on disease prevention measures originating directly from the disease itself. Participants identified various deficiencies in the documentary, including incomplete geographical coverage of disease prevalence, the omission of successful recovery narratives, and the insufficient elucidation of prevention measures, disease onset stages, modes of transmission, contagiousness, and actionable directives. The descriptors ‘inadequate’, ‘insufficient’, and ‘incomplete’ were recurrent terms employed by participants to characterize the informational content presented in the documentary. While the documentary’s duration may have influenced the depth of information conveyed, filmmakers’ participants perceived the runtime as appropriate.

A key limitation of the study was the inability to include individuals with lived experience of mycetoma due to restricted access to endemic areas following the outbreak of war in Sudan in April 2023 between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces (RSF) over the integration of the RSF into the SAF [40], and subsequently over power leading to the worst humanitarian crisis [41] world wide, with over 25 million people in need of assistance. While the study did not aim to collect illness narratives, the perspectives of affected individuals could have added valuable insights into the documentary’s accuracy and relevance. Additionally, the gender imbalance among the film-makers may have limited the diversity of feedback due to the gendered nature of film-making and media reception.

The lead author’s positionality as part of the research team was challenging in a way that in each focus group discussion, and following the film’s watching experience, participants would normally pose questions to know more about mycetoma.

The study was collaboratively conceptualised and overseen by researchers from Sudan and the United Kingdom. Although data collection was conducted solely in Sudan by a Sudanese researcher—ensuring socio-cultural and linguistic fluency—the lack of in-person involvement from Global North researchers may have introduced subtle interpretive biases despite efforts to uphold reflexivity and methodological rigor.

The study was also granted research ethical clearance by the Faculty Research Board of the Faculty of Geographical and Environmental Sciences, University of Khartoum.

Ethics declaration

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the declaration of Helsinki.

Consent to participate

All study participants received a Participant Information Sheet (PIS), including the researcher’s information, affiliation, and contact details. The PIS explained the study title, objectives, organizers, study funders, participants’ expected roles, rights, and confidentiality of participants. Having understood and accepted the PIS, all participants signed a written consent form to take part in this study.

Results

Visual elements, emotional engagement, and narrative structure

In terms of visual elements, the filmmakers employ various techniques to convey its messages. It uses close-ups of patients’ faces to highlight their emotions and struggles [42]. The close-up shots contribute to the film’s narrative structure, creating a more intimate and engaging storytelling experience. This technique can be seen as a way to convey complex emotional and psychological states, providing a richer context for the audience to understand the patients’ journey while encouraging viewers to engage with their stories on a deeper, more empathetic level. Close-ups are utilized for the two main patients’ storytelling including a male; (00:48), and a female (00:56).

The close-up shots are a cinematic technique employed in the documentary to further emphasize those affected by mycetoma and their stories, allowing the audience to concentrate on the patients’ emotions, actions, or details. This can enhance the narrative by emphasizing what is most important in the frame. Mycetoma patients are placed inside the frame to be at the center of the audience’s attention to allow space for their narrative to be the focus. The close-ups are primarily applied during patient interviews reaching a climax at the time when a “split-screen” is used (01 min: 06 s) to emphasize the shared pain of the illness and its ramifications where the two close-up shots of the two patients’ (Al Sadik and Zeinab) faces, taken in different locations, are displayed simultaneously, each taking up half of the screen (see shape 2). Overall, using close-up shots is a powerful tool in visual storytelling to create a sense of intimacy, focus and importance around the subjects and their stories.

Color grading is another visual technique used to create a great effect. The color palette is muted and desaturated, which helps to create a somber and serious tone throughout the film. The use of warm tones in some scenes, such as when patients are receiving treatment, creates a sense of compassion and warmth [43]. The selective use of warm tones, particularly in scenes depicting care or compassion, can evoke feelings of warmth and empathy. Warm colors such as reds, oranges, and yellows are often associated with positive emotions and human connection [44]. In “Hope for a devastating Neglected Disease” patients are shown receiving treatment in warm tones (05:20). Such color choice humanizes the characters and creates a more intimate atmosphere, fostering a sense of hope and caring amidst a generally serious film [45]. Color grading is a powerful tool in filmmaking that helps to create specific moods and tones. By using a muted and desaturated color palette, filmmakers can evoke a somber and serious atmosphere, while the selective use of warm tones can inject scenes with compassion and warmth. Therefore, these color choices in the documentary film under study are not uniquely aesthetic decisions but are integral to the storytelling process, enhancing the emotional depth and audience connection to the narrative. By understanding and employing this technique, the filmmakers significantly influence how the stories in the film are perceived and felt by audiences, underscoring the importance of color in this visual storytelling arsenal.

Overall, the film does an excellent job of using visual elements to convey the emotional weight of the selected stories in this regard. The close-ups, split screen, and color grading all work together to create a powerful and moving visual experience. However, how the documentary is visually crafted remains contentious when other emotionally provoking sides of it are considered. The film is characterized by a profusion of visually impactful images dispersed throughout its narrative, spanning from the initial to the fifth minute. These visual elements encompass still archival close-ups (00 min, 17–24 s) and dynamic life shots captured in various settings, including the outpatient clinic waiting area, interior clinic scenes during medical examinations (00 min, 29–33 s, 05 min, 19 s), wound sterilization procedures, and patient interviews (1 min, 02 s). Given that viewer responses to visual stimuli significantly influence the efficacy of documentary communication, it is imperative to acknowledge that graphic imagery has the potential to evoke viewer discomfort, emotional distress, overload, avoidance, desensitization, reduced empathy, and audience alienation. Such imagery can overwhelm viewers emotionally, leading to feelings of unease, distress, or even aversion. Consequently, this emotional response may prompt viewers to disengage from the film [46], thereby missing the essential messages intended to raise awareness about mycetoma as a neglected disease.

“The documentary’s introductory sequence was frequently cited by participants as creating a sense of emotional and psychological distance, a finding that aligns with the anthropological concept of ‘othering’—the process of portraying a group as fundamentally different and separate from the viewer.

One filmmaker participant explicitly linked the visual framing to a direct invitation for disengagement:

The introductory part continues to show vast spaces. It already tells you not to engage (A 35 year female film-maker participant).

This perception of “distancing” was reinforced by another filmmaker who critiqued the portrayal of the setting as monolithic and miserable:

It starts with tough and miserable scenes from Omdurman and Khartoum outskirts in general… If I have to introduce Omdurman and Khartoum, I could film the whole Khartoum including the hotels and the River Nile behind…(A 41 year old male film-maker participant).

These responses indicate that the documentary’s choice of wide, ‘miserable’ shots framed the endemic community not as a place of everyday life with diverse realities, but as a singular, impoverished landscape. This visual strategy constructs an ‘otherness,’ presenting the community and its problems as a distant, foreign world to be observed from afar, rather than a relatable context with which the audience can connect.”

Expressing difficulty in focusing on the film due to the inclusion of graphic footage of severe wounds was shared across the focus group discussions.

to be honest, I couldn’t focus on the film as it included harsh wounds footage, I just couldn’t (26 years female participant in a focus group discussion).

.

The above quote stresses that anticipation of disturbing content may lead individuals to abstain from watching the documentary altogether, resulting in diminished reach and impact, particularly in the context of an online media platform where viewers can easily opt out. Furthermore, prolonged exposure to graphic content can engender desensitization, wherein viewers become less attuned to the depicted suffering, thereby undermining the empathetic response crucial for advocacy and awareness raising initiatives [23]. The documentary’s reliance on shock-inducing visuals poses a risk of diverting attention from the core issues it aims to illuminate. Instead of focusing on the systemic neglect and the imperative for enhanced healthcare, viewers may become fixated on the shock value of the images, potentially weakening the intended message. Moreover, the portrayal of physical afflictions in a sensationalized manner can inadvertently reinforce negative stereotypes about affected populations, accentuating their suffering rather than their inherent capabilities and resilience. Such depictions have the potential to contribute to the stigmatization of individuals or groups, particularly if they perpetuate negative stereotypes or prejudices [24].

The study participants-across all the focus group discussions -expressed aversion towards specific visual content, including depictions of “wounds,” “bruises,” and “uncovered affected body parts.” Descriptive terms such as “shocking”, “disgusting” and “painful” represented patterns employed by participants of the focus group discussions to convey the psychological distress induced by viewing these undesired documentary shots.

psychologically disturbing …I always feel disgusted having to watch wounds, allergies, and such kind of stuff…the suffering experience of patients was also painful to me .It might have been necessary to reflect the danger and severity of it but the pictures were shocking and disturbing (A 26-year-old female participant in a focus group discussion)

This quote articulated feeling “psychologically disturbed” and “disgusted” by the portrayal of open wounds, highlights the distressing nature of the viewing experience. Several participants shared similar sentiments. The use of such graphic content may lead to audience alienation, stressing the concept of the “otherness”, or further promotes desensitization, potentially undermining the documentary’s message.

Observations showed that participants demonstrated physical signs of discomfort, such as shielding their faces, averting their gaze, or closing their eyes when faced with graphic imagery, indicating a range of coping mechanisms in response to the challenging content presented. This observation was more common among female participants while male facial expressions showed the same but without the need of other ways to express their emotional reaction.

The medium through which the script content is conveyed to viewers comprises patients’ narrative (two patients), experts (two medical doctors) narrative, and predominantly the voiceover account of a medical doctor. Here, we explore the content through the medium by shedding light on the narrative, and how the disease context and patients’ experience are reflected in the documentary, and how that influences to (un)mask mycetoma neglect, including related contextual elements, social stigma, awareness raising, and advocacy.

The film narrates two distinct stories of Mycetoma, adopting a balanced gender approach in selecting patients as the evidence shows. However, the storytelling strategies and narrative arcs remain insufficiently assertive, overly brief, not wholly informative, and inadequately inclusive to convey the full human experience of living with a neglected disease like Mycetoma, particularly the social stigma associated with it.

Participants’ views in the in-depth interviews regarding visual elements of the films, shared the same concern of “distancing” the viewer. Most of the film-makers’ feedback in this regard went further to be more focused on the employed cinematic techniques causing this notion of “distancing “, “alienation”, and “ the invitation not to disengage” ; patterns used to enforce both “ the otherness” and the “ medical gaze” concepts embedded in the documentary.

This participant’s point of view regarding the film’s introductory part provides a subtle but critical entry point into analyzing how visual framing in this documentary film can reinforce a “medical gaze” and the construction of an “otherness” entity indirectly inviting audience not to engage in the “diseased” world, and to remain as “observers”.

While the film was visually appealing by using close-ups and color grading, it was simultaneously challenged by the opening shot techniques when it utilised wide shots in the introductory part as shown in the above participant’s quote. another participant noted;

It starts with tough and miserable scenes from Omdurman and Khartoum outskirts in general…there could be better ways; more art was needed better than the general shots where Omdurman, Sudan, and alike, are written…If I have to introduce Omdurman and Khartoum, I could film the whole Khartoum including the hotels and the River Nile behind through a drone or something (A 41 year old male film-maker participant).

By opening with visual imagery that avoids engagement and instead presents a vast, silent, miserable space, the film potentially preconditions the viewer to see the subject community not as individuals but as part of a distant health crisis—a visual terrain to be mapped, studied, and diagnosed. This risks sidelining narratives of agency, lived experience, and relational belonging, which are essential in decolonizing global health storytelling.

“The ‘medical gaze’—a mode of perception that reduces a person to their pathology—was not only embedded in the film’s narrative but was also reflected in the viewing experience of some participants with clinical backgrounds. While most participants expressed aversion to the graphic wound imagery, one respondent, a medical doctor, demonstrated a viewing perspective entirely focused on the disease as a clinical object.

I didn’t feel any discomfort watching open wounds, I wanted to see more on different stages, to witness the progression of mycetoma manifestations, just for educational purposes (A 30 year old female participant in an in-depth interview).

This response is a clear manifestation of the medical gaze in action. The participant’s desire to see more graphic content for ‘educational purposes’ underscores how the documentary’s visual approach can facilitate a clinical, de-personalized mode of looking. The patient’s body becomes a textbook illustration, and their subjective suffering is secondary to the pathological progression of the disease. This quote powerfully demonstrates how the film’s aesthetic can train the audience to adopt the gaze that the anthropological critique seeks to dismantle.”

As a medical doctor, the respondent’s experience was informed by a “medical gaze”, the gaze that minimizes the individual to a ‘diseased’ person, a patient, and the person who holds symptoms on his skin. She even thought it could have been more of a learning experience if an opportunity was given to her -as a doctor- to witness the skin condition progression.

Gender did not seem to be a determinant factor on viewers’ visual engagement with the documentary as aversion was common among both females and males among the study participants. The way aversion was expressed varied as females tended to either cover their faces using both hands in most cases whereas signs of discomfort were obvious on males facial expressions.

When analysing the above quotations, it becomes obvious that while the public audience and the academic researchers expressed the “discomfort” and the “distancing” feeling caused by the film’s visual elements, the film-makers participants, through the in-depth interviews went further explaining reasons behind this “distancing” through the film-makers experts lens, combined with their previous experience in the field.

Portrayal of patients’ experiences and social stigma

The account of the male patient; Al Sadik Mohamed (starting at 00 min, 48 s); a young man who lived with mycetoma for 19 years, focuses predominantly on his physical pain until his leg was amputated, at which point he reportedly felt “comfort.” This subtly suggests that amputation might be a viable solution, which is a potentially stigmatizing implication for other mycetoma patients. Moreover, Al Sadik’s narrative style lacked proper vocalization of his own pain, and his body language and facial expressions seemed incongruent with the narrative, indicating that he might not have been the most suitable patient to interview.

In contrast, the female patient; Zeinab Ibrahim (starting at 01 min, 06 s), who lived with the disease for 20 years, briefly recounts the issue of social stigma, expressing her hope for recovery to visit her neighbors. When it was her turn to discuss the psychological suffering, how she seized to be a genuine thread of her community’s social fabric following her illness, and have the stigma dimension unfold, the film shifted focus to a male family member who discussed her unwillingness to go out after the infection, her physical pain and the impact on her ability to perform household tasks.

“The power dynamics of the medical gaze were further evident in the control of patient narratives. The film’s attempt at a gendered balance was undermined when the story of the female patient, Zeinab, was abruptly taken over by a male family member as she began to touch upon the psychological and social dimensions of her illness. This narrative interruption silences the patient’s voice and re-centers the perspective on physical functionality and observable symptoms.

This editorial choice aligns with a paternalistic model of medicine where the patient’s subjective experience is less valid than an external, often male or clinical, account of their condition. It prevents the patient from being a ‘wounded storyteller’ and instead frames her lived experience through a lens that prioritizes biomedical and domestic functionality over her psychological and social world.”

This narrative approach interrupts the natural narrative flow of those with mycetoma lived experience, withdraws the claimed gendered balance, fails to provide a pluralistic perspective on illness experience, ignoring the role of comprehensive storytelling in coping with illness and challenging the dominant narratives about illness and recovery.

The documentary could have benefitted from deliberately utilizing storytelling as a craft to enable the two patients to make sense of their illness experiences and resist dominant medical narratives. This would have created a space for personal meaning and alternative perspectives, as proposed by narrative theory [25].

For the selected patients, to me, those who were in the film, talked in a good way, but I could have found patients who are more vocalized and could talk passionately in a natural way (A 35 year female film-maker participant).

This quote speaks to the importance of narrative and storytelling in shaping our understanding of illness experiences. The participant’s preference for patients who are “more vocalized” and can speak “passionately in a natural way” implies a desire for more authentic and compelling narratives that can resonate with audiences.

This resonates with anthropological theories of narrative and identity, which emphasize the role of storytelling in shaping individual and collective experiences of illness [47, 48]. By selecting patients who can articulate their experiences in a way that is both personal and relatable, documentary filmmakers can create more impactful portrayals of illness that resonate with diverse audiences.

Narrative style amplifying patients’ adaptability and stances of creative coping mechanisms overcoming the illness challenges were not showcased in the film. This tendency of focusing on the portrayal of individuals’ disease symptoms could reduce patients’ agency to their disease in a minimalistic manifestation of patients’ diverse human capacity [26].

The way the narrative handles social stigma of Mycetoma, a complex and multifaceted phenomenon influenced by various social, cultural, and historical factors, could have been more influential if addressed through characterization, patient storytelling, and accompanying research data used through the voiceover. The film briefly mentions the social stigma with a single phrase using the voiceover as a medium; “in fact, it is a social stigma” (min 02, Sect. 47). However, some study participants believe the film could have a subordinate impact on reducing Mycetoma-related social stigma through awareness raising elements included in the documentary by educating viewers about the disease itself.

the film didn’t address the stigma issue in a direct or even indirect way, never at all. Moreover, reducing social stigma is a big effort, but for this film, as a film, it did nothing in stigma reduction (A 35 year female film-maker Participant).

The above comment, made by an expert film maker who took part in various documentary film making dealing with social stigma of medical conditions, confirms the film under study’s limitation when dealing with stigma reduction. It even nullifies any space for the documentary in “attempting” to reduce the stigma.

in all the in-depth interviews conducted with film-makers, participants believe the documentary does not tackle the stigma issue as reflected on one of their similar responses.

It is crucial to recognize that reducing stigma is a complex and ongoing process requiring sustained effort from policymakers, healthcare workers, patients, and the broader public. Documentaries like “Hope for a Devastating Neglected Disease” can be an important tool in this process, but they are not a panacea.

It was noted that in all FGDs, females were more vocalized and more engaged with discussions related to stigma than male participants.

Content, narrative flow and power dynamics

The documentary’s content demonstrates a bounded exploration of the socio-economic, political ecology, and geopolitical factors that influence mycetoma as a neglected disease. It primarily emphasizes the biomedical aspects of Mycetoma, which are crucially significant, yet could benefit from a more profound examination of the social and political determinants that contribute to the disease neglect, spread and impact. An enriched narrative could encompass an analysis of economic, political, and environmental influences on disease propagation, alongside an evaluation of policies and practices contributing to the broader neglect of tropical diseases.

Critical ethnography contends that comprehending power dynamics, political ecology, and the politics surrounding the disease’s neglect necessitates scrutinizing power relations within the community depicted and between the film-makers and their subjects. It also advocates for amplifying marginalized perspectives [25].

Contrarily, “Hope for a Devastating Neglected Disease” fails to incorporate indigenous medical knowledge and local remedies utilized to address the disease and its psychological ramifications, including homemade treatments and spiritual healing practices. The documentary further asserts that traditional medicine lead to secondary infections without clarifying whether these alternative moralities underwent medical testing.

Because the drugs are very expensive, they adapt a local native treatment, and again that will lead to secondary bacterial infection and that will lead to poor outcome of the patient (min 04, Sect. 19).

In spite of the prevalent use of herbalist medicine and non-invasive modalities as the primary forms of traditional treatment for mycetoma [26], the documentary chose to visually depict unsettling imagery of alternative traditional practices. This portrayal included still images featuring sharp cutting implements and blood, emanating from the observed practice (from 04:22 to 04:32).

This unilateral conceptualization of mycetoma patients’ health seeking behavior constitutes an ineffectual approach to accommodate the biomedical challenges in the provision of a successful medical treatment addressed in the same documentary;

the available treatment for mycetoma are not very effective, the cure rate is about 27% (min 03, Sect. 33),

among other statements in the same film.

The second statement, among others in the same film like “first, the drug is very expensive, second, the drugs have severe side effects and the patient compliance said that daily I have a medication, I have a medication? and they become angry about that” (03: 49), for instance٫ came in a different context other than potential reasons of the health seeking behavior, which is the trial of a new mycetoma medication; that symbolizes the “hope” in the documentary title " Hope for a Devastating Neglected Disease “, the title which is not visually displayed on screen but rather explained on the film’s descriptive section on YouTube.

“Hope” to reduce the impact of disease neglect and devastation requires a pluralistic approach acknowledging local communities’ culture and knowledge.

I was very interested in the local ways of healing the disease because they talk about that as a bad way to do things, you know, as if the traditional healing is just something we need to fight, I can not agree with that if I don’t understand what they are doing. Anthropology have shown that traditional healing can be effective (A 41 year female researcher participant in an in-depth interview).

The above anthropologist note, not only resonates with how the medical gaze had shaped the documentary film but also reflects how mycetoma is handled throughout the film, overshadowing the indigenous knowledge, and the traditional healing pathways adopted by individuals with mycetoma lived experience. Informed by such a medicalised perception, the documentary might have broaden the chasm between the Western biomedical world, and the existed local traditional healing system thereby, indirectly contributing to further stigmatising those affected.

The entire dismissal of traditional knowledge can be regarded as paternalistic, undermining the patient’s autonomy and agency [27, 28]. This can result in patients feeling disempowered and less likely to engage actively in their healthcare [31]. Additionally, when patients seek out alternative practitioners due to dissatisfaction with conventional care, it can lead to fragmented care, where different aspects of the patient’s health are managed separately and less effectively [30]. Many traditional remedies have been found to have therapeutic benefits [32]. By understanding the what, why, and how of traditional practices, biomedical institutions can better evaluate their efficacy and safety, potentially integrating beneficial practices into mainstream medicine.

Ultimately, trust and rapport are essential for effective healthcare delivery. When the biomedical system exercises its own superior power over indigenous knowledge and beliefs, it risks alienating patients who feel that their beliefs and practices are not respected. This erosion of trust can lead to decreased patient engagement and less effective communication [33].

Outreach and perceived impact on advocacy and awareness-raising

To further explore the documentary’s contribution regarding advocacy and awareness-raising, it is critical to point out that while the two terms are often used interchangeably, their distinction lies in the target audience and the methodologies employed to achieve the intended impact. Advocacy typically focuses on influencing decision-makers and policymakers through a diverse set of strategies such as lobbying, policy analysis, coalition-building, litigation, and other strategic actions [35]. Conversely, awareness raising efforts aim to reach a broader audience encompassing both the general public and policymakers through educational campaigns, media engagement, public events, social media platforms, and other dissemination tools to ensure widespread information dissemination. In the assessment of the documentary’s impact on awareness raising, which seeks to cultivate a shared understanding and acknowledgment of an issue among the public and policymakers [34], study participants indicated that the documentary ‘Hope for a Devastating Disease’ provided them with novel insights and knowledge regarding Mycetoma.

When we talk about influence or impact, you intuitively talk about strategy. An impact strategy should include expectation, like for example, in year one, what do I expect in a reference to awareness raising or stigma reduction. What do I expect in three years, so I missed the strategy here (A 47 year old film-maker in an in-depth interview).

The above quote reflects the importance of a clear strategy for impact, which the participant -as an expert- could not grasp from the documentry he just watched as he was expecting. The quote also suggests that only by crafting a robust impact strategy, awareness raising and stigma reduction could be achieved.

The same participant further added.

The most I missed in what I have seen here is that I didn’t feel an integrated effort that includes social media with a long term plan, like saying that, these are the films of the first year (A 47 year old film-maker in an in-depth interview).

The expert in this quote emphasizes the significance of sustainable programmatic vision when tackling complex issues like mycetoma while aiming to achieve a long term impact.The quote speaks to the concept of the long term planning in order to have an impact for years to come. It indicates that this documentary film should be a part of a bigger and integrated endeavors to achieve a certain goal. This kind of a plan should be highlited right in the social media platform where the film is showcased to keep the audience engaged with the current efforts and the work to come in the future.

Despite the diverse backgrounds of study participants - encompassing public audiences, academics, and documentary filmmakers - all participants had not previously viewed the documentary and a substantial proportion exhibited limited prior awareness of the disease.

Quote

In the realm of advocacy, defined as the endeavor to endorse a cause or proposition to influence decision-makers and instigate systemic change [35], participants underscored aspects such as language utilization and contextual relevance. The decision to adopt English as the documentary’s production language, despite its divergence from the spoken language of the Mycetoma-affected community, catalyzed discussions on post-viewing individual action plans spanning from charitable contributions and awareness campaigns to field-specific initiatives and expanded research endeavors. The film-makers responses ranged from consideration of embarking on distinct cinematic ventures when invited, uncertainty, readiness for future collaborative film productions, to a neutral stance.

The impact and outreach of a documentary extends beyond language alone; other critical factors significantly influence its effectiveness. According to the filmmakers’ participants reaching a broader audience hinges on multiple considerations. Notably, excessive use of graphics can adversely affect viewership due to YouTube algorithms. Consequently, awareness and advocacy messages within the documentary may be diluted. As one film-maker respondent explained during an in-depth interview,

YouTube and Facebook algorithms employ filters that can inadvertently suppress content or misclassify videos. For instance, filters may identify disturbing shots of wounds, reducing the video’s visibility without removing it entirely (A 41 year male film-maker Participant).

This response highlighted the limitations of YouTube’s reach in Sudan. To effectively target Sudanese audiences, the documentary should be uploaded to alternative platforms like Facebook. Even on Facebook, discoverability remains a challenge.

The respondent suggested using strategic tags, awareness campaigns (such as a mycetoma week), and visually engaging elements (such as posters) to direct viewers to the content. Ultimately, attaching the documentary to frequently searched topics enhances its visibility.

In summary, the documentary’s impact relies on tailoring messages to the specific audience profile, considering both language differences and platform-specific strategies.

Discussion

Some previous related work has been conducted in this field. Mol [49] examines how medical documentaries represent illness and suffering, focusing on the distinctions between ‘exotic’ others and ‘us’. She critiques these representations for perpetuating stereotypes and ethnocentric views. Cartwright [50] analyzes medical cinema’s role in shaping the medical gaze, often reducing patients to objects of scientific inquiry. She argues that these films reinforced societal norms and prejudices, objectified patients, and perpetuated a voyeuristic gaze.

Organizations like Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) have also used documentaries to advocate for NTDs such as noma and snakebite [51, 52]. While these films are used for education and awareness [53, 54], a critical anthropological evaluation of their production and reception is lacking, a gap this study seeks to fill.”

A study reviewed interventions for NTDs among children and adolescents, incorporating educational tools like films to enhance awareness and promote health education [55]. The study found an increase in awareness and understanding of NTDs among participants. This highlights the potential of documentaries in raising awareness and promoting health education, particularly among vulnerable populations.

The concept of “medicalization” and the associated “clinical gaze” explains that power exercised by doctors over patients is not merely a result of their medical knowledge, but also through the way they observe, classify, and treat the patient. The “clinical gaze” refers to the process by which doctors transform the patient into a case, a set of symptoms to be examined and diagnosed, thereby exercising control and authority over the patient’s body and life [35]. The “Medicalization and Clinical Gaze” concept argues that medicalization and clinical gaze emerged in the late 18th and early 19th centuries as medicine transitioned from a practice based on individual patient narratives to one focused on systematic observation and classification [36]. This process of medicalization extends the power of the medical profession into various aspects of everyday life, influencing the health domain and how people think about health, illness, and the body. The medicalization concept is necessarily wedded to the idea of the “us” and the “exotic others”, sustaining this pure medical perception. This medical gaze is regarded as a mechanism of power that establishes a hierarchical relationship between doctor and patient, where the doctor is seen as superior due to his/her medical knowledge and expertise.

The “medicalized gaze” and the “otherness” concepts are strongly interrelated. Annemarie Mol [49] critically examined documentary films, scrutinizing their portrayal of illness and suffering. Mol specifically focused on the distinction drawn in documentaries between the ‘exotic’ others -referring to local communities’ knowledge in different parts of the world -and the ‘us,’ representing Western medical practices.

“Our findings critically illuminate how the documentary ‘Hope for a Devastating Neglected Disease,’ despite its intentions, inadvertently reinforces the very power dynamics and forms of neglect it seeks to combat. The analysis reveals three interconnected domains where this occurs:

First, the reinforcement of the ‘Medical Gaze’ and ‘Othering’: The documentary’s visual grammar, particularly the use of alienating wide shots in the introduction and the unflinching focus on graphic wounds, creates a distinct separation between the viewer (‘us’) and the subjects (‘the exotic other’). This aligns with Cartwright’s (2005) concept of the medical gaze, where the patient is reduced to a pathological object. The film often presents mycetoma patients as a collection of symptoms—a ‘disease map’ as highlighted in the Epilogue—rather than as individuals with full social lives and agency. This approach, as critiqued by Mol (1998), perpetuates a voyeuristic dynamic that can desensitize audiences and undermine empathetic engagement, ultimately distancing the viewer from the lived reality of the illness.

Second, the narrative silencing of patient voice and local knowledge: The documentary’s content, while biomedically focused, fails to leverage storytelling as a tool for empowerment. The interruption of the female patient’s narrative (Zeinab) by a male family member, and the lack of assertive, first-person narratives, strip patients of their voice. This contradicts Frank’s [25] notion of the ‘wounded storyteller,’ where narrating one’s illness is a form of meaning-making and resistance. Furthermore, the outright dismissal of traditional healing practices without anthropological exploration disregards the complex health-seeking behaviors of the community. As Kleinman (1980) and other medical anthropologists argue, such a paternalistic approach erodes trust and ignores potential synergies between local and biomedical systems, thereby widening the chasm between global health initiatives and the communities they aim to serve.

Third, the limitations of a ‘stand-alone’ intervention: Our data on outreach and impact underscore that a single documentary, especially one facing algorithmic visibility challenges and language barriers, is insufficient to drive systemic change. The feedback from filmmakers emphasized the lack of a clear, long-term impact strategy. This finding suggests that for digital media to be truly effective in advocacy and stigma reduction, it must be embedded within a sustained, multi-faceted program that includes community mobilization, social media campaigns with targeted hashtags, and policy advocacy. The current humanitarian crisis in Sudan, which has shattered healthcare infrastructure, makes this integrated approach not just ideal, but essential.

In conclusion, while the documentary succeeds in generating an initial emotional shock, its reliance on a medicalized, ‘othering’ framework and its failure to center patient narratives and local context limit its capacity to unveil the deep-seated neglect of mycetoma. Truly effective interventions must be informed by deeper anthropological insights that prioritize cultural sensitivity, narrative agency, and integrated, long-term strategic planning.”

‘Hope for a Devastating Disease’ shows delineation for the two related concepts in diverse cinematic techniques related to both the documentary’s visual elements and content and, thus, the perceived impact. The film follows a similar trajectory in its presentation of Mycetoma, emphasizing the “otherness” concept to create a dichotomy between two distinct worlds: the exotic others (mycetoma patients) and the “us” (the biomedical world). The two concepts shape all of the documentary stages; in its introductory segment, the documentary employs two distinct audio-visual cinematic techniques: wide shots and background music. The opening scenes feature expansive views of misery in Omdurman city, Khartoum; the capital of Sudan (see shape no.1). The psychological impact of these wide shots conveys a sense of viewer alienation, outsiderhood, and isolation from the subject matter. This reinforces the embedded notion of “otherness,” not only within the medical context but also among the audience. Scholars in cinematography and study participants from the filmmaking community have extensively discussed the alienating effect of wide shots in a film opening scene. The documentary therefore misses an opportunity for viewer engagement by exclusively relying on wide shots in its initial stages. To establish a more personal connection with the audience, close-up shots should be balanced with wide shots. Close-ups allow viewers to connect intimately with the subjects, a crucial element in the early scenes (36 ). Unfortunately, the documentary fails to incorporate medium or close shots during this phase. Although close up shots were utilized to convey patients’ emotion in a successful way, that only took place at a later stage but not in the inaugural part.

The selection of the introductory site in the documentary also raises questions, as it appears entirely disconnected from any other narrative elements included in the film. Despite this disconnection, the chosen location adds a heightened shocking effect. Moreover, it inadvertently contributes to romanticizing the affected community by emphasizing their misery within the context of the chosen locale.

Accompanying this visual choice is the use of dramatic background music, which further intensifies the emotional impact. However, this heightened emotional state may inadvertently reinforce the sense of misery depicted in the scene, ultimately contributing to the process of isolation. Filmmaker respondents in in-depth interviews expressed dissatisfaction with the music selection, describing it as “too dramatic” and incongruent with the desired tone for the documentary’s introduction. One respondent noted that the music discouraged viewers from continuing to watch, setting an unpleasant tone. The wider shots of Omdurman, Sudan, were also criticized as irrelevant by another participant. In summary, the documentary’s initial choices (both in location and music) shape the audience’s perception and emotional engagement, potentially hindering their willingness to engage further with the film.

As the documentary’s initial scenes fade, the narrative transitions into an in-depth exploration of medical research, enriched with archival images that highlight the scientific aspects of the subject. Notably, the film abstains from incorporating the relevant social context.

In its closing sequence, the documentary features smiling children to symbolize the medical “hope” embodied by the anticipated new pharmaceutical product, which is presented as a potential solution to the community’s suffering (see shape No.3). However, study participants critiqued this conclusion as incongruent with the rest of the film.

Overall, the way the documentary was ideologically sought (introduction, script, final scenes) reflects an esoteric conceptualization for the illness and its lived experience. The medical gaze that saturates the documentary does not seem to adequately assist in unveiling mycetoma neglect.

Conclusion

The evaluated documentary primarily relies on a combination of visual and auditory elements to create a powerful and emotional impact. Some of the employed cinematic techniques are powerful tools in conveying the depth of patients’ emotions. However, as an intervention to reduce the social stigma around the disease, the documentary had little to offer in this stance. The medical gaze of the documentary, devoid of the illness from its socio-economic, geopolitical, and environmental context, is deemed successful in introducing the neglected disease uniquely to those invited for a watching experience.

This context has become even more critical since April 15, 2023, when war erupted in Sudan between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces (RSF). The ongoing conflict has devastated healthcare infrastructure and displaced millions, leaving mycetoma patients - already marginalized by disease neglect - facing compounded vulnerabilities in inaccessible conflict zones.

The reach of messages to unveil the disease neglect is bound by various factors such as language barriers and limited search engagement, as mycetoma still remains a neglected disease and does not fall into contemporary searchable issues for those who are not aware of the disease. A documentary film production, right from the script writing, needs to be informed by deeper anthropological insights to convey the depth of the illness experience and amplify the patients’ narratives. Standing alone, a documentary film does not have the capacity to unveil the disease neglect or bridge the gap of global health disparities; it needs to be sustained by a series of other parallel endeavors and initiatives such as community mobilisation, community campaigns in endemic areas and social media campaigns using targeted hashtags, and outreach of global health initiatives considering mycetoma local context. Future research should explore social interventions targeting mycetoma, its neglect, the social stigma, and the media based advocacy efforts. Encouraging an integrated mycetoma management approach needs to be considered while planning intervention programmes, particularly in conflict-affected regions where mycetoma patients thrive amidst neglect and pervasive collapse of a fragile health system.

Although recruiting people with mycetoma lived experience as study participants was an important part of the study proposal, they were not included in the study. The eruption of war in Sudan in April 2023 made the planned field trip to endemic areas impossible. Inclusion of this group might have added a different perspective to the study and its results.

Epilogue

Unlike any of the study respondents, I found myself overwhelmed and shocked by a scene in the documentary where a doctor examines a patient. The patient, bare-chested, attempted to hide his identity by shielding his face with one arm. Meanwhile, the doctor explained to the audience where mycetoma manifests in the human body, using the patient’s body as a “disease map” and reducing him to a mere collection of symptoms. The patient’s face was unnecessary for this “body mapping” process.

For me, the solution was simple: film him without revealing his face, sparing him the effort of concealment, or digitally mask his face in post-production to preserve his dignity. This led me to question: Is a patient’s consent in a medical setting always a sufficient justification to expose them to the public and online indefinitely? Would I, with my different perception of “privacy,” give consent if I were in that patient’s position?

These questions haunted me. I wondered where this patient might be after the war that erupted in Sudan on April 15th, 2023. Has he survived? What about the other patients? Did they make it? How do they live now, deprived of access to healthcare, food, and basic necessities?

My plan to include mycetoma patients in the study failed due to the ongoing conflict. Fortunately, I completed data collection with other participants. As I write this, I feel a deeper connection to mycetoma patients than ever before. Fleeing my home and becoming an internally displaced person, then crossing an internationally recognized border to become a forced migrant, has profoundly changed me.

I used to write about the wellbeing of marginalized groups including internally displaced persons (IDPs), refugees and patients with neglected diseases, never imagining I would become one of them. I am still trying to understand this shift. What shocks me most is the silence of the media and the quiet of the international community in the face of daily atrocities and violations against innocent Sudanese citizens, but “Silence sometimes speaks.”
