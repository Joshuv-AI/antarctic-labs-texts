Prioritizing treatment targets for youths with disruptive behaviours and callous-unemotional traits: a modified Delphi study
Authors: Jennifer Diep, Ellfie Chen, Joyce H. L. Lui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundYouths with disruptive behaviours (DB) and callous-unemotional (CU) traits experience significant impairments and impose substantial costs on families and society. Treatment outcome studies of current DB treatments highlight the need for tailored treatments. Identifying priority treatment targets that integrate empirical evidence with stakeholder perspectives may inform more effective treatment tailoring.MethodA modified Delphi study was conducted to establish consensus on priority treatment targets for youths with DB and CU traits. A mixed panel of 86 experts (parents, clinicians, and researchers; Mage = 43.38, SD = 10.44, 76% female) rated the priority of candidate treatment targets across behavioural, emotional, cognitive, parental/home, peer, environmental/school, and temperament/personality domains. Ratings were completed separately for children (ages 3–12) and adolescents (ages 13–18). Experts also rated the feasibility of addressing candidate treatment targets within typical outpatient psychosocial treatment settings.ResultsOverall, 26 treatment targets for children and 30 for adolescents were identified as high priority; no targets reached consensus as low priority. Developmental differences emerged: parental/home targets were consistently prioritized for children, whereas adolescents showed broader priorities including emotional, cognitive, and peer targets. Among consensus priority targets, 13 child and 17 adolescent targets were rated as at least moderately feasible to address within outpatient care. Feasibility ratings did not differ across stakeholder groups.ConclusionsFindings identify stakeholder prioritized candidate treatment targets for youth with DB + CU, including both well-established and under-addressed targets. These results support consideration of treatment approaches that retain core DB components while extending attention to CU-relevant emotional, cognitive, and relationship processes, particularly for adolescents. By integrating lived experience, clinical expertise, and research expertise, this study provides a stakeholder-informed foundation for future treatment development, tailoring, and empirical evaluation for youths with DB + CU.

Background

Disruptive behaviour (DB) disorders are the most common reason for referral to child mental health services in North America [1] with an estimated lifetime prevalence of 9.5% in the United States [2] and global estimate of 8% [3]. DB disorders encompass conditions such as conduct disorder and oppositional defiant disorder, and are characterized by aggression, impulsiveness, defiance, hostile behaviours, and serious violations of rules [4]. Among youths with DB, a distinct subgroup exhibits elevated callous-unemotional (CU) traits, which are associated with heightened levels and more stable trajectories of DB [5]. Now included as a specifier for DB disorders in diagnostic classification systems (DSM-5 and ICD11; [4, 6], CU traits (also called “limited prosocial emotions”) are characterized by a lack of guilt, diminished empathy, reduced emotional expression, and uncaringness [7]. CU traits predict a range of negative outcomes, including earlier delinquency [8], increased reoffending [9], persistent concurrent and future antisocial behaviours [10], aggression [10, 11], and substance use [12]. Both DB and CU traits are associated with significant costs to families and society [13–15].

Currently available treatments

Early research has reported less positive treatment outcomes for youths with co-occurring DB and CU traits (henceforth DB + CU). Specifically, early studies report minimal or reduced improvements in DB symptoms following standard DB interventions [16–19]. Recent literature offers a more nuanced and optimistic picture [20–23]. In a comprehensive meta-analysis, Perlstein and colleagues found that DB + CU youths showed decreases in DB symptoms comparable to DB-only youths; however, symptom severity remained significantly higher at post-treatment for DB + CU youths [23]. Furthermore, there was no overall effect of treatment on CU traits, although reductions in CU traits were more likely in treatments that incorporated a parent management component [23]. Similarly, several studies found that youths with DB + CU participating in Summer Treatment Programs showed significant reductions in DB symptoms, although they continued to show impairment following treatment [24]. Collectively, these findings suggest that although existing treatments can effectively reduce DB symptoms in youths with DB + CU, current treatments may be insufficient to normalize functioning or reliably reduce CU traits. This pattern raises the possibility that existing treatments may not adequately target the factors most critical for optimizing treatment outcomes for DB + CU youths.

Efforts to adapt or tailor treatments to better meet the needs of youths with DB + CU often draw on identified risk factors and correlates associated with CU traits. In a seminal review, Frick and colleagues identified more than 19 factors uniquely associated with DB + CU that contribute to the development and maintenance of DBs [7]. These factors spanned cognitive, emotional, biological, temperament and personality, parenting, and peer domains. Additional risk factors have since been identified such as neighbourhood-level risk (e.g., exposure to delinquency, substance availability, violence, and poverty; [25]. Despite this expansive literature, empirical evidence guiding which factors should be emphasized in treatment for DB + CU remains limited. Notably, Perlstein et al. [23] did not find evidence of moderation of treatment effects based on whether treatment was adapted specifically for CU traits (i.e., adapted treatments yielded minimal or modest differential treatment effects compared to standard treatments), although they noted there were a small number of high-quality adapted treatment studies overall. Together, these findings raise an important question: among the many putative factors identified in the literature, which are essential and should be prioritized when tailoring or enhancing treatments for youths with DB + CU?

Understanding treatment priorities among stakeholders

Historically, psychosocial treatment development has not consistently involved those most affected by the treatments [26]. There is increasing recognition that incorporating the input of stakeholders throughout treatment development can enhance relevance, acceptability, and potential impact [27, 28]. The Delphi method [29], which is designed to systematically elicit expert consensus, is well suited to identifying treatment priorities for youths with DB + CU. The Delphi method is increasingly used for mental health research to establish research priorities [30–32], develop guidelines for treatments [33–35], and identify treatment and service priorities [36–39]. Two particularly important stakeholder groups are treatment recipients and treatment providers. Parents of children with DB + CU offer a unique perspective as they have observed their children across developmental stages and are often informed about their child’s behaviours by other adults, such as teachers. Additionally, parents are often central to treatment initiation and delivery and have direct experience in navigating real-world care contexts for their children and youth. Treatment providers contribute practice-based knowledge regarding feasibility, implementation constraints, and clinical decision making. Psychosocial treatment development grounded in human-centred designs emphasize the importance of engaging end users to enhance usability, identify foreseeable implementation challenges, and generate solutions that improve fit within routine practice settings and users’ needs [28, 40, 41].

In summary, youths with DB + CU experience significant impairments and impose substantial costs on families and society. Although existing treatments can reduce DB symptoms for youths with DB + CU, outcomes remain suboptimal, highlighting the need for more effectively tailored treatments. To inform treatment tailoring and enhancement, it is essential to identify which putative treatment targets should be prioritized, integrating empirical knowledge with stakeholder expertise. The current study used a modified Delphi method to establish expert consensus on priority treatment targets for youths with DB + CU. Identifying targets that stakeholders view as essential and relevant may enhance treatment fit, usability, and uptake.

Method

The present study adhered to ethical standards certified by Concordia University’s Human Research Ethics Committee (Certification Number: 30017315). Study design, procedures, and reporting followed the DELPHISTAR guidelines [42].

Participants

A mixed panel of experts with experience relevant to youths DB and CU traits was recruited, including (a) parents who self-identified as having a child with DB + CU, (b) clinicians who self-identified as providing treatments to youths with DB + CU, and (c) researchers who conduct treatment research with this population. A total of 86 participants completed Round 1 of the Delphi survey: 26 parents, 31 clinicians, and 29 researchers. Participants were between 24 to 76 years old (M = 43.38, SD = 10.44); 76% identified as female. Additional demographic characteristics are presented in Table 1. Researchers were identified through Perlstein et al.’s meta-analysis [23] of treatment outcome studies for youths with DB + CU. All authors from the meta-analysis were invited to participate in the current study. Of the 203 researchers contacted, 29 (14%) completed the Round 1 survey. Most researchers (62.1%) specialized in clinical psychology. Researchers demonstrated substantial and varied expertise related to CU traits. The majority reported extensive scholarly involvement, including first authorship (62%) and co-authorship (93%) on CU traits publications, and roles as authors (44.8%) or editors (13.8%) of CU traits-related books, chapters, or special issue journals. Many also contributed to CU traits research through grants, including serving as principal investigator (31%) or co-investigator (51.7%). On average, researchers reported 15.83 years (SD = 8.73) of experience working in the area of CU traits. Overall, 89.7% of researchers reported engagement in at least five of six professional activities assessed, indicating a high level of cumulative expertise within the panel.

Parents were recruited via Facebook support groups for parents of children with DB. Identification of DB + CU children relied on parent reports of child characteristics rather than standardized assessment of DB and CU traits. Interested parents completed an eligibility screening form in which they confirmed that their child had disruptive behaviours and CU trait, including low empathy or remorse, lack of care for others, or shallow emotions. Eligible participants were then provided a Qualtrics link to the consent form and Round 1 survey. Of the 77 parents who received the survey link, 26 (38%) provided verified, valid responses after checks for fraudulent or bot responses. All parents reported that their child had received a diagnosis of DB disorder, and 96% of parents endorsed their child as having at least two CU traits.

Clinicians were recruited through listservs of mental health clinics and professional organizations, and online directories of mental health providers (e.g., Association for Behavioral and Cognitive Therapies, Ontario Psychological Association, Ordre des Psychologues du Québec). Recruitment focused on clinicians practicing in North America, Europe, Australia, and New Zealand, reflecting regions with broadly comparable outpatient service delivery models. Interested clinicians completed an eligibility screen form in which they confirmed that they had at least two years of experience working with youths who show disruptive behaviours and CU traits, including low empathy, lack of guilt or remorse, insincere emotions, and lack of care about others. Eligible participants were then sent a Qualtrics link to the consent form and Round 1 survey. Of the 67 clinicians who received the survey link, 31 (46%) provided verified, valid responses after checks for fraudulent or bot responses. The most frequently reported discipline was clinical psychology (41.9%). Clinicians reported an average of 10.81 years (SD = 7.00) of experience working with youths with CU traits, and the majority (83.9%) were licensed providers who served this population, reflecting substantial practice-based expertise relevant to youths with DB + CU. Most clinicians had formal training in evidence-based psychosocial interventions commonly used to treat DB, including behavioural parent training (71%) and cognitive behavioural therapy (83.9%). A subset also reported training in family- and systems- oriented approaches, including attachment-based therapies (35.5%), Functional Family Therapy (9.7%), and Multisystemic Therapy (9.7%).

Modified Delphi method

The Delphi method is a structured, iterative approach for eliciting expert opinion to achieve consensus, or agreement, on a specific topic [43]. It involves multiple rounds of anonymous surveys with group summary feedback to allow participants to reconsider their responses in light of group responses. This method is suitable to our goal of identifying priority treatment targets because it gathers the opinions of researchers, clinicians, and parents. The current study used a modified Delphi method conducted online, with the primary objective of identifying treatment targets that experts viewed as priority for tailoring or enhancing psychosocial interventions for youths with DB + CU. The study was designed a priori to include a maximum of three rounds, based on a review of Delphi studies on similar topics [44–53]. Rounds 1 and 2 served as consensus-generating rounds. Because a large number of treatment targets reached consensus by the end of Round 2, Round 3 was used as a confirmation and feasibility round rather than an additional consensus-generation round.

Identification of candidate treatment targets

Candidate treatment targets were initially drawn from Frick et al.’s [7] review of factors associated with DB + CU. Identified factors spanned genetic, cognitive, emotional, biological, parental, peer, and temperament domains. For the purposes of the current study, genetic and biological factors were excluded, as the goal was to identify targets that could be directly targeted and/or reasonably translated into psychosocial intervention components. To capture more recent developments, a supplemental literature search covering 2014 to 2023 was conducted using the APA PsycNet database with the following search terms: “(risk* OR relation* OR association* OR correlation*) AND (callous unemotional OR “CU trait”) AND Peer-Reviewed Journals only”). This process yielded a final list of 42 candidate treatment targets (see Table 2), grouped into seven domains: behavioural, emotional, cognitive, environmental/school, parental/home, peer, and temperament/personality. Defining consensus

Consensus was defined descriptively as at least 75% agreement among experts, aligned with Diamond et al.’s systematic review [54]. Specifically, treatment targets were considered to have reached consensus if ≥ 75% of experts rated them as High or Very high priority (retained as priority targets) or as Very low or Low priority (rejected as not priority). Targets not meeting either criterion were considered equivocal and carried forward to subsequent rounds.

Round 1

In Round 1, experts were presented with definitions of DB and CU traits and reminded of the study’s goal to inform treatment tailoring and enhancement. They were then presented with the 42 candidate treatment targets, along with definitions and examples for each. Experts were asked to rate how much of a priority, or how essential it is, to address each of the 42 targets in treatment for youths with DB + CU, using a 5-point Likert scale (Very low – Very high). Ratings were made separately for children (ages 3–12) and adolescents (ages 13–18), as we anticipated different treatment foci based on age groups. Age categories were informed by evidence-based updates of psychosocial treatments for disruptive behaviours in children [55] and adolescents [56]. Our child category aligned with the age range used in the evidence-based update for children [55]. Although the adolescent evidence-base update used an age range of 12–19 years [56], adolescence was defined as 13–18 in the current study to avoid overlap with the child category. Open-ended questions invited experts to suggest additional treatment targets not represented in the initial list.

Round 2

Round 2 included all equivocal targets that had not reached consensus in Round 1. For each target, participants were presented with group feedback (i.e., the group median and a bar graph of response distributions) and their own previous rating. This feedback was aggregated across all experts. Experts were invited to re-rate each target, with the option to retain or revise their initial rating in light of group feedback. This round also introduced six new treatment targets suggested by experts in response to Round 1’s open-ended question.

Round 3

The original study intent was to have a third round of consensus ratings. However, by the end of Round 2, consensus had been reached on 26 targets for children and 30 targets for adolescents. Given the large number of treatment targets that had already reached consensus, and the study’s pragmatic goal of informing future psychosocial treatment development, we adapted Round 3 to focus on confirmation and feasibility rather than additional consensus generation. This shift was intended to balance comprehensiveness with practicality, as the number of consensus targets exceeds what could realistically be addressed within a single, time-limited psychosocial intervention. In Round 3, participants were presented with the list of consensus priority treatment targets and were asked to indicate their agreement with these targets as priorities for future treatment development for youths with DB + CU, using a 5-point Likert scale (Strongly agree – Strongly disagree). Ratings were made separately for children and adolescents. Open-ended questions invited experts to suggest therapeutic approaches or strategies relevant to each domain. Additionally, experts rated the feasibility of addressing each consensus priority treatment target within a typical outpatient psychosocial treatment context, using a 5-point Likert scale (Very low – Very high).

Surveys were carried out online from May 2024 to March 2025. Experts received a $25 gift card after the completion of each round. Responses to individual items were optional, and participants could opt out of answering items they felt fell outside their scope of expertise. All experts who completed a given round were invited to participate in the subsequent round. Of the 86 who completed Round 1 (26 parents, 31 clinicians, and 29 researchers), 57 completed Round 2 (16 parents, 25 clinicians, and 16 researchers), and 54 completed Round 3 (15 parents, 24 clinicians, and 15 researchers). Planned analysis

Quantitative ratings were analyzed descriptively in SPSS. Expert ratings were weighted equally because the aim of the study was to identify consensus across a mixed panel rather than to privilege one form of expertise over another. Parents, clinicians, and researchers were each considered to contribute distinct and relevant expertise regarding youths with DB + CU, including lived experience raising a child and navigating care in real-world contexts, clinical experience, and research experience, respectively. A one-way MANOVA was conducted to examine whether there were between-group differences in feasibility ratings across expert groups. Mean priority and feasibility ratings for consensus targets were plotted on a Go-Zone matrix to visually characterize targets with high perceived priority and feasibility. For open-ended response, a trained clinical psychology graduate student (JD) conducted initial independent coding using a content analysis approach, which involves identifying the frequency of key words or content and interpreting these findings [57]. Open-ended responses per round were extracted and reviewed in full. Responses were coded deductively based on commonly identified treatment family categories from the literature, and the frequency of each code was summarized. Coding was reviewed with supervising author (JL); no discrepancies emerged during the review process.

Results

Priority treatment targets

In Round 1, experts rated 42 candidate treatment targets. Of these, 26% of targets for children (11) and 24% of targets for adolescents (10) reached consensus as high priority and were retained. In the Round 2, experts re-rated the remaining equivocal targets for children (31) and adolescents (32), along with six new candidate targets suggested in Round 1. After Round 2, an additional 41% of child targets (15) and 53% of adolescent targets (20) reached consensus as high priority and were retained. Thus, across Rounds 1 and 2, a total of 54% (26/48) of candidate treatment targets for children (Table 2), and 63% (30/48) for adolescents (Table 3) were identified as consensus priority treatment targets. Feasibility of consensus priority targets

Overall, feasibility ratings for consensus treatment targets were moderate (M = 3.11, SD = 0.91). Of the 35 treatment targets (collapsed across age groups), 57% (20) were rated as having at least moderate feasibility (i.e., mean feasibility rating ≥ 3 on a 5-point scale; see Table 4). Targets within the behavioural, emotional, and cognitive domains were rated as more feasible. Feasibility ratings for parental/home targets were mixed, whereas environmental/school and peer targets were rated as having the lowest feasibility. A one-way MANOVA was conducted to examine whether feasibility ratings differed across expert groups. The omnibus multivariate test was not statistically significant, Pillai’s Trace = 0.34, F(12, 88) = 1.52, p = 0.13, indicating no overall difference in ratings between parents, clinicians, and researchers across treatment target domains. Exploratory univariate tests indicated a significant group difference in feasibility ratings only for emotional targets, F(2, 48) = 5.80, p = 0.006. Specifically, parents and researchers rated emotional targets as less feasible than clinicians, whereas parents and researchers did not significantly differ from one another. Suggested therapeutic strategies

Analyses of open-ended responses indicated the most frequently suggested treatment approaches across emotional, cognitive, peer, and behavioural domains were cognitive-behavioural therapy and third-wave cognitive-behavioural therapy (e.g., acceptance and commitment therapy, dialectical behavioural therapy, mindfulness-based therapy). To a lesser extent, emotion-focused therapy was suggested for the emotional domain, and social skills training was suggested for the peer domain. For the parental domain, the most common recommended strategy was behavioural parent training. For the environmental domain, the most common recommended strategies were coordinating with schools or delivering interventions in schools. Notably, within each domain, 7.32% to 15% of experts expressed treatment pessimism (i.e., 2.45% to 7.5% indicated no treatment would work, 2.32% to 7.5% indicated skepticism that effective treatments exist), or lack of knowledge regarding available treatments.

Discussion

Youths with co-occurring DB and CU traits experience elevated risk of negative outcomes and impose substantial costs on families and society. Evidence to date suggests that adapted treatments may confer some benefits [59–61], but that incremental gains beyond standard treatments are modest and not consistently demonstrated [23]. One explanation is that current treatments may not consistently target the factors most critical for optimizing outcomes among youths with DB + CU. The current modified Delphi study addressed this gap by integrating lived experience (parents), clinical expertise, and research expertise to identify consensus priority treatment targets for youths with DB + CU to inform treatment tailoring and enhancement, with the goal of maximizing relevance, fit and eventual uptake.

A clear developmental pattern emerged in the consensus priority treatment targets identified for children versus adolescents. For children, experts endorsed all parental/home targets (e.g., inconsistent parenting practices, low parental involvement, parents’ negative cognitions about the child) as priorities, alongside select emotional targets (e.g., deficits in empathy, emotional regulation difficulties), and some environmental/school targets. For adolescents, consensus priorities included nearly all parental/home targets, all peer related targets, all emotional targets, and several cognitive targets (e.g., endorsing deviant goals, impairments in moral reasoning).

Across both age groups, aggressive/defiant/conduct behaviours were consistently prioritized, supporting the relevance of behavioural symptom reduction as a core candidate treatment target for youths with DB + CU. This finding aligns with meta-analytic evidence demonstrating that youths with DB + CU show meaningful reductions in DB symptoms [23]. At the same time, several adolescent-specific priorities mapped closely onto processes uniquely implicated in CU traits [62–65]. Adolescents were more likely to have consensus priorities within the emotional and cognitive domains, including low motivation to change, deficits in empathy, difficulties processing emotions, endorsement of deviant goals/values, and impairments in moral reasoning. This pattern is consistent with developmental theory suggesting that adolescents may be better positioned than younger children to engage with more abstract socioemotional and cognitive concepts [66]. Notably, the small but growing number of tailored or adapted treatments for DB + CU youth tends to directly target these emotional and cognitive processes [23]. For example, Emotion Processing Skills Training [61], adaptations of parent–child interaction therapy for youths with comorbid CU traits [67, 68], virtual reality based interventions (e.g. Impact VR [69]), and emotion recognition training adjuncts [70] focus on improving emotion recognition, understanding, empathy, and moral emotions. Other approaches have targeted motivational and cognitive processes more explicitly. Salekin’s Mental Model framework emphasizes increasing positive emotional experiences, identifying strengths, and fostering future-oriented goal setting [71]. Similarly, PSYCHOPATHY.COMP for detained adolescents focuses on replacing aggressive motivational patterns with more compassionate motivations and promoting responsibility-taking [72]. Several of these approaches explicitly incorporate motivational enhancement techniques to address low motivation to change [61, 71], a consensus priority in the current study.

Developmentally, the distribution of priorities across relational contexts also aligns with theory and clinical practice. Parental/home targets were more strongly emphasized for children, supporting the continued centrality of parent-focused interventions for younger children. Peer factors (e.g., affiliation with delinquent peers, peer rejection, low quality friendships) were more often prioritized for adolescents, consistent with the increasing salience of peer relationships during adolescence [73]. This finding points to the potential of addressing peer functioning and social connectedness in adolescence, including for youths with DB + CU.

Integrating expert consensus about what should be prioritized with what is at least moderately addressable in typical outpatient psychosocial services, 13 child and 17 adolescent targets were identified. These targets were concentrated primarily in the parental/home domain, with a smaller number of behavioural, emotional, and cognitive targets. The Go-Zone matrix offered a more restrictive, secondary lens, which may support decisions about prioritizing emphasis when resources, sessions, or staffing are constrained. Using the above-average criterion, the set narrows further to 9 child and 11 adolescent targets. Across the two approaches, many of the treatment targets identified align with core components of established evidence-based treatments for DB. For children, parent behaviour therapy is a well-established treatment [55] that targets parenting practices and parent–child relationship processes central to the parental/home priorities identified in the current study (e.g., inconsistent parenting, negative parenting practices, low parental warmth). For adolescents, well-established family-based approaches for justice-involved youths (i.e., Functional Family Therapy, Multisystemic Therapy; [56]) target risk factors across individual, family, peer, and community contexts, including emotion regulation, parenting practices, peer affiliation, and school functioning. Attachment-based intervention approaches (e.g., the Connect Program; [74]) also align with the prominence of parent–child relationship targets in the current study. The present findings suggest that treatment planning for youth with DB + CU may require retaining these core DB treatment components while extending intervention targets to processes more specific to CU traits.

At the same time, several high-priority targets appear less routinely addressed in current popular treatments for DB, pointing to potential opportunities for treatment innovation. These include parents’ negative cognitions about the child, parental emotion socialization, risky or harmful online behaviours, and moral reasoning impairments. Risky online behaviours may be addressable by explicitly extending evidence-based strategies used to reduce antisocial or conduct behaviours. For example, behavioural parenting training routinely incorporates functional assessment and contingency management to reduce problem behaviours; these same principles could be applied explicitly to online contexts (e.g., consistent reinforcement for safe/prosocial online behaviours, consequences for rule violations). Making the focus on online behaviours more explicit may increase the relevance of existing intervention components for contemporary risk contexts. Parent emotion socialization represents a related opportunity. Although many parenting interventions implicitly address emotion socialization through coaching and modeling of parent behaviours, elevating this target as a more explicit treatment emphasis, such as in adapted PCIT for CU traits [68] may be suggested. Similarly, these findings suggest that parent negative cognitions about the child may require more direct attention than is typical in standard behavioural parent training. In addition to improving parenting practices, tailored protocols may benefit from specific modules designed to identify and modify maladaptive attributions about child behaviours and to strengthen caregiver empathy and reflective capacity, supporting more flexible appraisals. Finally, moral reasoning impairments could potentially be addressed through extensions of mentalization or perspective taking approaches already used in related intervention contexts [71, 75]. Thus, although treatment targets for DB and DB + CU overlap, the DB + CU profile may require additional tailoring to address the affective and interpersonal features that distinguish CU traits from DB alone.

Overall, feasibility ratings for consensus priority targets were moderate, with nearly half of the targets (46%) rated as low feasibility for outpatient treatment. Importantly, feasibility ratings did not systematically differ across parents, clinicians, and researchers. This pattern appears to reflect perceptions of service-context implementation challenges and beliefs about malleability of DB + CU behaviours. Targets related to peer (e.g., affiliation with delinquent peers, low quality friendships) and environmental/school domains (e.g., low involvement in school, low quality relationships with other adults) were rated as high priority but low feasibility, and experts provided fewer treatment suggestions in these domains. However, feasibility ratings should be interpreted as context dependent. In particular, lower feasibility ratings may reflect perceptions that office-based outpatient models are not optimally structured to influence peer networks, school engagement, or broader community environments. It is also possible that experts may have less familiarity with effective school-based interventions, or limited resources to implement school-based interventions. Notably, recent developments in delivery of PCIT in school settings support the feasibility of school-based service delivery [76]. Together, findings suggest the value of considering alternative service delivery models, such as school-based supports, and more intentional involvement of peers in treatment, which have been underemphasized in the DB + CU literature, despite evidence from Summer Treatment Programs demonstrating the potential value of peer-context interventions for youths with DB + CU [24].

Within the emotional domain, empathy deficits were rated as high priority but low feasibility. Given that low empathy is a core feature of CU traits that has partial genetic underpinnings [77], empathy may be perceived as less amenable to change. Clinically, aligned with Frick and Kemp’s argument [78], this raises the possibility that intervention goals may be more pragmatic when they emphasize reductions in antisocial behaviour and increases in prosocial behaviours even if empathy itself changes minimally. Nonetheless, beliefs about malleability matter, as past research indicates that beliefs about the malleability of empathy impacts empathic effort [79]. Psychoeducation may be relevant for shaping expectations and engagement among providers, parents, and youths.

Within the parental/home domain, feasibility ratings varied by target type. Targets related to parents’ own characteristics (e.g., parent antisocial behaviours and psychopathy) were perceived as least feasible to address in treatment, followed by family dynamics (e.g., family conflict, home chaos), whereas parenting behaviours and cognitions directed toward the child were perceived as feasible to address. This gradient likely reflects skepticism about the malleability of adult personality traits and entrenched family patterns. These findings point to the importance of realistic treatment planning, and when appropriate, linkage to adult services (e.g. contingency management, schema therapy, dialectical behaviour therapy; [80]), and psychoeducation regarding available supports.

Notably, temperament and personality related targets (e.g., fearlessness, thrill seeking, difficult temperament) did not reach consensus as priority targets for children or adolescents. Similarly, low sensitivity to punishment and high sensitivity to reward- well documented correlates of CU traits- were not prioritized. These results may reflect perceptions that such characteristics are biologically driven or not directly modifiable. Alternatively, stakeholders may have interpreted these targets as requiring direct intervention, rather than as factors that could inform treatment delivery (e.g. emphasising reinforcement strategies and de-emphasizing punishment strategies; [68, 81]). Future work can clarify whether certain correlates should be treated as direct intervention targets versus design parameters that shape treatment delivery.

A subset of experts (7.32% to 15%) expressed uncertainty or pessimism regarding effective treatments across domains. This may reflect broader stigmatizing beliefs about youths with DB + CU and skepticism regarding the malleability of CU traits. Parents of children with conduct problems often report experiencing stigma [82], and similar negative attributions may extend to CU traits. Such perceptions may be reinforced by earlier literature reporting limited treatment responsiveness in DB + CU youths [16–19]. Future research should directly examine attributions regarding CU traits, especially among treatment providers and recipients, including beliefs about stigma and malleability, as these beliefs likely influence treatment engagement.

Limitations and future studies

Results of the current study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, DB and CU traits in children were identified using parent and clinician self-report. Future studies should use standardized assessments to better identify and characterize the sample. Although the study included perspectives from parents, clinicians, and researchers (rather than a single stakeholder group), the sample lacked diversity with respect to socioeconomic status, race, parent gender, and professional discipline. Parents in our panel were predominantly mothers; individuals in other caregiver roles, such as fathers, may hold distinct views. Given the persistent underrepresentation of fathers in pediatric research [83], future research should prioritize recruiting fathers to better capture their perspectives. Our sample was also predominantly White, limiting generalizability. In light of documented racial disparities in the diagnoses of DBs in youths [84], future work should prioritize more diverse and representative samples. It is possible that treatment suggestions from our study does not adequately address and/or accurately reflect the needs of more diverse treatment recipients. In addition, most clinicians and researchers in our panel were trained in clinical psychology. This disciplinary concentration may have shaped perceptions of feasibility towards treatment targets commonly addressed within child- and family-focused outpatient models, potentially underestimating the feasibility of addressing broader systemic or contextual factors that may be more familiar to professionals trained in other disciplines (e.g., education, social work, public health). It would be beneficial to include a wider range of professional backgrounds to capture a broader set of implementation perspectives. Delphi methodology prioritizes group consensus and may obscure meaningful divergent perspectives; future studies could more deliberately explore minority viewpoints in greater depth. We also observed attrition between consensus-generating rounds, primarily among the researcher group, which may have influenced the reliability of the consensus ratings. Although all expert groups remained represented in Round 2 and participants who remained did not differ on most demographic or professional characteristics from those who discontinued, the decreased proportion of researchers (females in particular) may have influenced the final consensus findings. Of note, the Round 2 sample remained within recommended ranges for Delphi studies [85–87]. Also, while treatment targets in the Delphi rounds were informed by literature and suggestions of our stakeholders, additional factors that can impact treatment delivery and outcome (e.g. therapist-child alliance) were not considered. The identified targets should be interpreted as candidate targets for future treatment development and empirical evaluation, rather than as validated mechanisms of change or direct clinical recommendations. Furthermore, feasibility ratings were collected only for consensus priority targets, precluding identification of targets that may be highly feasible but considered of moderate priority. Such targets could nonetheless play an important role in early engagement or treatment momentum. Future studies may benefit from concurrently assessing priority and feasibility across all candidate targets. Additionally, future studies may explicitly examine why certain targets were perceived as less feasible, including structural, organizational, financial, and workforce-related constraints. Treatment development research would also benefit from considering the appropriateness of targeting specific processes within particular service contexts (e.g., outpatient clinics, schools, primary care), alongside considerations of cost, sustainability, and scalability.

Conclusion

This modified Delphi study integrated perspectives of parents, clinicians, and researchers to identify consensus priority treatment targets for youths with DB + CU. A developmentally differentiated set of priorities are suggested: emphasizing parental/home factors for all ages, and expanded peer, emotional, and cognitive targets for adolescents. Findings offer actionable guidance for treatment tailoring, highlighting both well-established and under-addressed targets, and the need to address stakeholder beliefs about malleability and stigma to support implementation. Although stakeholder-identified priorities represent only one component of treatment design, aligning interventions with shared priorities may enhance fit, acceptability, and buy-in from end-users. Together, findings represent an important step toward informing the development of tailored interventions for youths with DB + CU.
