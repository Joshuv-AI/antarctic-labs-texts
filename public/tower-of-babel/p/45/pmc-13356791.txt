Associations of accelerometer-derived physical activity with chronic kidney disease risk in a population with hypertension: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Hongguang Wu, Xiaoyan Li, Juda Hu, Peiyao Li, Xiaohong Li, Jing Chen, Haojian Dong, Yanqiu Ou, Petros Koutrakis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertension is a key risk factor for chronic kidney disease (CKD), but evidence on whether physical activity (PA) reduces CKD risk in patients with hypertension remains limited. This study investigates the association between PA intensity and CKD prevention in this high-risk group.MethodsThis prospective cohort study analyzed 37,908 patients with hypertension from the UK Biobank without pre-existing CKD who underwent accelerometer-based PA monitoring between 2013 and 2015. PA was classified into light (LPA), moderate (MPA), and vigorous (VPA) intensities, with weekly duration calculated for each. CKD incidents were determined via hospital admissions and death records. Polygenic risk scores were used to evaluate genetic susceptibility. Cox proportional hazards models and restricted cubic splines were applied to examine associations between PA levels and CKD, adjusting for socioeconomic status, comorbidities, and genetic risk.ResultsDuring the median follow-up period of 7.71 years, 1,131 participants (2.98%) developed CKD. Higher MVPA levels were significantly associated with reduced CKD risk (highest vs. lowest quartile: Hazard Ratio(HR) 0.62, 95% Confidence Interval(CI) 0.50–0.77). Independently, MPA and VPA showed protective associations (highest vs. lowest quartile: HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.50–0.98 and HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.54–0.85, respectively). Of particular note, we found that MPA levels surpassing current guideline recommendations lowered the risk of CKD by 65%(HR 0.35, 95% CI 0.21–0.58) compared with the most inactive group. LPA alone showed no preventive effect on CKD risk.ConclusionsIn patients with hypertension, more MVPA duration rather than LPA could benefit more renal protection. These findings highlight the value of objective PA monitoring and support precise, intensity-specific exercise prescriptions for CKD prevention in populations with hypertension.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-04941-x.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) represents a growing global health challenge, with hypertension recognized as a primary modifiable risk factor. From 1990 to 2021, CKD cases attributable to hypertension rose by over 60%, a trend expected to escalate as one-third of the global population currently suffers from elevated blood pressure [1–4]. While physical activity (PA) is a well-established protective factor against cardiovascular and metabolic diseases [5, 6], its specific role in renal preservation remains under-investigated in populations with hypertension.

Existing evidence from general and diabetic cohorts suggests that higher PA levels reduce CKD risk [7–9] and insufficient PA has been linked to huge burden of CKD annually [10]. Despite these findings, critical evidence gaps persist. First, most PA-CKD studies have focused on general or diabetic populations, with limited investigation of individuals with hypertension—another high-risk group for CKD. Second, current WHO PA recommendations for hypertension management (150–300 min/week moderate or 75–150 min/week vigorous activity) lack robust validation in CKD prevention. Third, reliance on self-reported PA assessment in existing studies introduces substantial recall bias [11], potentially obscuring true exposure-outcome relationships.

To address these limitations, we utilized objective, accelerometer-derived data from the UK Biobank. This study aims to elucidate the precise dose-response and joint associations of light (LPA), moderate (MPA), and vigorous (VPA) physical activity with CKD risk in patients with hypertension. Furthermore, we integrated polygenic risk scores to assess whether genetic susceptibility modulates these associations, providing a basis for precision lifestyle interventions.

Methods

Study population

The UK Biobank is a prospective cohort. Between 2006 and 2010, from 22 assessment centers across Scotland, England, and Wales, approximately 500,000 individuals aged 37–73 were recruited into this cohort, with their demographic characteristics, lifestyle, and health information recorded [12]. All participants provided informed consent and obtained ethical approval from the Northwest Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee.

This study utilized the population with hypertension with accelerometer data from the UK Biobank. The individuals with hypertension were defined as those meeting any of the following criteria: (1) individuals diagnosed with hypertension by a doctor as obtained from the medical record system; (2) individuals self-reporting as hypertensive; (3) individuals using antihypertensive medication; and (4) individuals with measured systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg. We applied strict inclusion and exclusion criteria to the data including: (1) accelerometer data with poor calibration; (2) accelerometer data not worn for at least 72 h; (3) individuals with accelerometer data spanning daylight saving time changes; (4) individuals with missing covariates; (5) individuals who withdrew from the UK Biobank; and (6) individuals with pre-existing CKD before the baseline. (Details in Figure S1)

Accelerometer-based PA assessment

From 2013 to 2015, 103,663 participants from the UK Biobank with valid email addresses were randomly invited to wear an Axivity AX3 wrist-worn triaxial accelerometer and triaxial acceleration data over 7 days were collected with a sampling frequency of 100 Hz and a dynamic range of ±8 g. The details of the data collection and processing of the accelerometers have been reported previously [13].

The time spent each week in LPA, MPA and VPA was defined as the minutes spent in activities with intensities of 30–125 milli-gravities(mg), 125–400 mg, and > 400 mg, respectively [14, 15]. Moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) and total PA (TPA) were expressed in weekly metabolic equivalent minutes (MET-min/week). MVPA was calculated as the sum of weekly MET-min of MPA and VPA, while TPA represented the sum of LPA, MPA, and VPA. The metabolic equivalents for different intensity activities were estimated as follows: LPA × 2, MPA × 4, and VPA × 8 [15, 16].

CKD assessment

In this study, incident CKD cases were identified through hospital records and death registry systems. CKD events were defined based on the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes (Table S1). The study’s baseline timepoint was established as the commencement date of accelerometer wear. To ensure the absence of CKD at baseline, we additionally applied the participant self-report events and supplemental diagnostic confirmation based on estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73m2 (calculated from serum creatinine levels measured at enrollment using the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) creatinine equation). During follow-up, the endpoint was defined as the first occurrence of CKD diagnosis through ICD-10 codes. The study observation period concluded on August 10, 2022. The follow-up time was determined based on whichever occurred first among the date of CKD diagnosis, date of death; or the end of the observation period (August 10, 2022).

Polygenic risk scores of CKD

To obtain the polygenic risk score (PRS) for CKD, we utilized imputed genetic data from the UK Biobank. We used 251 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) previously identified in a large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) by Gorski et al. following the methodology validated by Zhang et al. in the UK Biobank cohort [17, 18]. These SNPs were selected based on genome-wide significance and underwent linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruning (threshold: r2 < 0.1; window size: 250 kb) to ensure that the genetic variants were independent (Table S2). We extracted genotype dosage data for these SNPs from the UK Biobank’s annotated data (in BGEN format) using Bgenix and performed efficient data processing and score calculation with PLINK 2.0 [19].

The PRS was calculated by multiplying the number of risk alleles (coded as 0, 1, or 2) at each locus by the corresponding β-coefficient (logarithm of the odds ratio) from the GWAS, followed by standardization based on the sum of the β-coefficients across all included SNPs. A higher PRS derived from the extracted SNPs indicates a genetically predicted higher CKD risk. After standardizing the PRS, the PRS-z was obtained. In addition, we categorized the computed PRS for CKD into three groups by the tertile method: high genetic risk group, moderate genetic risk group, and low genetic risk group.

Covariates

Since the baseline of our study was defined as the date of accelerometer wear, the age of the study participants was calculated by subtracting their birth date from the accelerometer wear date. To maintain temporal relevance, all covariates were extracted from the most recent evaluation preceding the exposure measurement with a median interval time of 5.17 years (The details of time interval distribution in Figure S2). Body mass index (BMI) was derived from nurse-measured height and weight. Smoking status was self-reported via questionnaire and categorized as Never, Previous, and Current. Alcohol consumption frequency was also self-reported and classified as Never, Special occasions only, 1–3 times monthly, 1–2 times weekly, 3–4 times weekly, and daily or nearly daily. Income was based on self-reported annual household income, and originally collected in British Pounds (£), were converted to US Dollars ($) to facilitate international comparability. The conversion was based on the 2025 average exchange rate of £1 = $1.34 and the household income was categorized into <$24,120, $24,120–$41,539, $41,539–$69,679, $69,679–$134,000, and >$134,000. Ethnicity was classified as White or Others. Education level was categorized as low, medium, or high based on participants’ highest attained qualification. eGFR was calculated using serum creatinine levels measured by the 2021 CKD-EPI equation. Systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were measured by nurses using electronic blood pressure monitors combined with manual readings. Use of cholesterol-lowering and antihypertensive medications was determined by self-reported drug use. Diabetes status at baseline was assessed through insulin use, medical records, and self-reporting. Additionally, the presence of urolithiasis, coronary heart disease, heart failure, and stroke at baseline was identified via self-reports and medical records. Further details were provided in the Table S3.

Statistical analysis

In this study, quartiles of weekly TPA metabolic equivalent minutes were used for grouping and presenting baseline characteristics. For continuous variables, data were presented as mean ± SD if normally distributed, or as median and interquartile range if non-normally distributed. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages.

We employed Cox proportional hazards models to examine the associations between various PA levels (LPA, MPA, VPA, MVPA, and TPA) and CKD risk. We tested the proportional hazards assumption using Schoenfeld residuals and found no violations (Table S4). Comparisons were made across PA quartiles. Restricted cubic spline models were used to assess dose-response relationships between PA and CKD risk, with knots placed at the 5, 35, 65, and 95th percentiles for curve fitting.

In accordance with WHO recommendations for individuals with hypertension (150–300 min/week of MPA or 75–150 min/week of VPA), we further categorized MPA and VPA for deeper analysis. MPA was divided into < 150, 150–300, and > 300 min/week groups; VPA into < 75, 75–150, and > 150 min/week groups. We calculated joint effects of MPA and VPA on CKD risk and visualized them using risk matrices. Population attributable fractions (PAFs) were computed to estimate the potential reduction in CKD incidence if populations with hypertension achieved optimal PA levels(The method we used to calculate the PAFs was described in details in the supplementary materials). As WHO provides no specific recommendations for LPA duration, it was not included in this analysis.

All models were adjusted for sociodemographic factors (age, sex, BMI, smoking status, alcohol frequency, household income, ethnicity), comorbidities, medication use, and PRS to account for genetic predisposition. LPA, MPA and VPA were mutually adjusted to estimate the independent effect of each different physical intensity. We used the Pearson’s correlation coefficient to test the potential collinearity among different activity types and the correlation coefficients between LPA, MPA, and VPA were low (details in Figure S3). Subgroup analyses were conducted by sex (female/male), age (< 65/≥65 years), BMI (< 25/≥25 kg/m²), antihypertensive medication use (yes/no), and PRS (low/moderate/high). Several sensitivity analyses were performed: (1) Exclusion of participants developing CKD within two years to minimize reverse causation due to subclinical kidney dysfunction; (2) Re-analysis using the 2017 AHA hypertension criteria (SBP ≥ 130 or DBP ≥ 80 mmHg); (3) Another analysis by strictly defining the outcome using exclusively ICD-10 codes N18.0–N18.5, N18.8, and N18.9 to mitigate potential ascertainment bias related to hypertensive renal disease coding; (4) Using the EValue package to assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounding, with larger E-values indicating greater robustness of the observed associations; (5) In a subset with 8008 participants who had available Cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) data, CRF was additionally adjusted (CRF calculation method was described in supplementary materials) [20]; (6) To ensure temporal proximity, we restricted the analysis to participants with the shortest time intervals between covariate recording and baseline (top 50% of the distribution). All analyses were conducted using R 4.4.2, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Population characteristics

Among 103,663 UK Biobank participants with accelerometer data, 92,393 (89.1%) had complete and high-quality recordings available for analysis. After excluding individuals with pre-existing CKD at baseline or missing covariate data, 37,908 patients with hypertension were included in the final analysis (see flowchart in Figure S1). The median follow-up duration was 7.71 years, during which 1,131 incident CKD cases were identified, yielding a cumulative incidence rate of 2.98%.

Stratified by quartiles of TPA, patients with hypertension with higher activity levels were more likely to be female, younger, having lower BMI, lower rates of smoking and alcohol consumption, higher household income, lower prevalence of comorbidities, and higher baseline eGFR. (Table 1)

Genetic risk of CKD in a population with hypertension

In the population with hypertension, after standardization, each standard deviation increase in PRS was associated with a 7% higher risk of CKD (Hazard ratio(HR) 1.07, 95% Confidence Interval(CI) 1.01–1.14, p = 0.026). Furthermore, when analyzed by tertiles, the high genetic risk group also showed an increase in CKD risk compared to the low genetic risk group after adjusting for baseline eGFR (HR 1.13 95% CI 0.98–1.31) although the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.095). (Table S5)

PA effect on CKD risk in a population with hypertension

The associations between different PA levels and the outcome risk are presented in Fig. 1. Compared with the lowest quartile, higher levels of MPA, VPA, MVPA and TPA were significantly associated with reduced risk. MPA exhibited a protective trend, with the highest quartile showing a significant 24% lower risk (HR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.59–0.98). VPA demonstrated a dose-dependent inverse association, with the highest quartile displaying a 32% risk reduction (HR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.54–0.85). MVPA and TPA exhibited the strongest inverse relationships (p-trend < 0.001 for both). For MVPA, HRs progressively decreased from Q2 (HR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.67–0.91) to Q4 (HR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.50–0.77). Similarly, TPA showed consistent risk reductions (Q4 HR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.60–0.88). However, LPA showed no significant association. For LPA, no meaningful risk reduction was observed across quartiles.

Figure 2 and Figure S2 illustrate the dose-response relationships between different types of physical activity (LPA, MPA, VPA, MVPA, and TPA) and CKD risk. All PA types except VPA demonstrated nonlinear associations with CKD incidence. Specifically, MPA, MVPA and TPA exhibited reverse relationships with CKD risk, indicating that increasing time spent in these activities provided significant protective effects against CKD development. While VPA demonstrated a linear inverse association with the incidence of CKD (p for nonlinearity = 0.458), indicating that the risk of CKD in populations with hypertension progressively decreased with increasing weekly VPA duration.

In accordance with the WHO recommendations for physical activities, this study conducted a joint effect analysis of MPA and VPA. Figure 3 presents a risk matrix plot based on the joint effect results, with detailed data provided in Table S7. The risk matrix analysis revealed that compared to the least active group, individuals engaging in > 300 min/week of MPA exhibited a significantly lower risk of CKD (41%), even when VPA remained at a low level (< 75 min/week). Furthermore, among those maintaining high MPA levels (≥ 300 min/week), increasing VPA duration to 75–150 min/week was associated with an additional reduction in CKD risk (HR 0.35, 95% 0.21–0.58). And when the duration of VPA exceeds this range, there was still a relative protective effect on CKD (HR 0.41, 95% CI: 0.18–0.94) (Table S8).

Joint effects between PA and Genetic risk on CKD in population with hypertension

Both MPA and VPA demonstrated trends toward reduced health risks across all genetic risk groups. Specifically, among hypertensive individuals with low genetic risk, engaging in just 300 min of MPA per week was associated with an approximately 50% reduction in the risk of CKD (HR 0.53, 95% CI: 0.38–0.72). Increases in VPA did not show as pronounced an improvement in the moderate and high genetic risk groups as MPA did. However, among those with low genetic risk, performing more than 75 min of VPA per week was linked to a markedly significant reduction in CKD risk compared to the reference group with less than 25 min of VPA per week (HR 0.16, 95% CI: 0.04–0.64). Subgroup analysis and sensitivity analysis

No significant interaction effects were observed in subgroup analyses stratified by sex, age, BMI, antihypertensive medication use, or PRS (all interaction p-values > 0.05), with all subgroups demonstrating results consistent with the primary analysis. (Table S16)

We performed a series of sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings (Figures S4–S10, Tables S7, S9–S15, S17-S22). First, we compared the characteristics of the study populations altered by our inclusion criteria (Table S6). Participants excluded due to developing CKD within two years (to minimize reverse causation) were generally older, had lower socioeconomic status, lower baseline eGFR, and a higher burden of comorbidities compared to the original cohort. Conversely, additional individuals identified as hypertensive solely under the 2017 AHA criteria (SBP ≥ 130 or DBP ≥ 80 mmHg) tended to be younger, had higher socioeconomic status and baseline eGFR, and fewer comorbidities. Despite these demographic differences, the primary results demonstrated high robustness. Notably, in the analysis applying the 2017 AHA hypertension criteria, the high-risk PRS group (top tertile) continued to show a significantly elevated CKD risk compared to the low-risk group (HR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.01–1.33, p = 0.040), with consistent risk estimates observed per SD increase in PRS-z (HR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.01–1.13). Meanwhile, to address potential labeling bias inherent in a hypertensive cohort, we conducted a sensitivity analysis restricting the CKD outcome exclusively to ICD-10 code N18 (excluding I12.0, I13.1 and I13.2, n = 3, occupied 0.2% of all previous CKD events). The results remained robust and consistent with the primary analysis. Specifically, compared with the lowest quartile of MVPA, the highest quartile showed a significantly reduced risk of N18-defined CKD (HR: 0.62, 95% CI: 0.50–0.77), confirming that the observed benefits were not driven by the inclusion of hypertensive renal disease codes. Also, when restricting the analysis to participants with a shorter time interval between covariate assessment and baseline, the inverse association of MVPA with risk of CKD remained robust (Q4 vs. Q1, HR: 0.63, 95% CI: 0.47–0.86; P for trend < 0.001). Finally, additionally adjusting for CRF did not change the trends and E-value analysis indicated that substantial unmeasured confounding would be required to explain away the observed associations, further supporting the validity of our results.

Discussion

This study utilized device-measured PA to investigate the impact of different intensity levels of PA on the development of CKD in patients with hypertension. While increasing LPA alone did not consistently reduce CKD risk, MPA and VPA were associated with a lower risk of CKD in this population. Notably, the combined benefit of MPA and VPA was greater than that of either activity alone. And for the low genetic risk group, a lower weekly volume of PA is sufficient to achieve significant preventive benefits against CKD. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the relationship between accelerometer-measured PA and CKD risk in hypertensive individuals.

Previous research has primarily focused on the association between PA and CKD in the general population or among patients with diabetes, with limited attention given to hypertensive individuals. A prior self-reported PA study in patients with hypertension found an inverse relationship between weekly MVPA duration and CKD risk, with the lowest risk observed in those exceeding 300 min per week (OR 0.53, 95% CI: 0.37–0.76) [21]. However, the study probably underestimated PA levels as self-reported PA often captures intentional exercise while omitting incidental daily activity [14]. Furthermore, the previous research did not account for genetic factors. Our findings demonstrated that the PRS exhibited good and consistent predictive performance for CKD in population with hypertension, consistent with the finding that PRS constructed by the same set of 251 SNPs predicted CKD in broader populations [17]. Therefore, it is crucial to consider the potential impact of genetic risk in assessing the impact of PA on CKD. By adjusting PRS in our models and performing comprehensive subgroup analyses, although we observed no significant interaction between genetic predisposition and PA in CKD prevention among hypertensive individuals, for those populations with hypertension with low genetic susceptibility of CKD can get enough benefit with less MPA duration a week, which supports personal PA suggestions for different genetic risk groups. Additionally, prior studies did not separately analyze MPA and VPA. In contrast, our study utilized accelerometer-based measurements and enabled further exploration into the specific MPA and VPA impact. Therefore, our research tends to provide more precise evaluation of PA impact for specific populations.

UK Biobank participants met or exceeded WHO-recommended MPA duration (150–300 min/week), which have lower risk in CKD development than those with weekly MPA less than 150 min, yet additional MPA duration (> 300 min/week) still conferred benefits. Although increasing MPA from the lowest level to over 600 min/week only reduces the proportion of CKD patients by approximately 5%, it is important to note that individuals with MPA below 150 min/week account for only 5.4% of the total population. Therefore, the value of increasing MPA levels may be underestimated, and these findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution as PAF estimates are particularly susceptible to residual confounding and imbalances in population distribution [14, 22]. On the other hand, increasing VPA to > 75 min/week has provided modest benefits. Dose-response analysis also revealed that prolonged VPA exhibited a more protective effect against CKD incidence. However, the findings for high-volume VPA should be interpreted with caution, as the robustness of these estimates is limited by the small sample size in this extreme exposure group. The majority of participants (66.4%) performed < 25 min/week of VPA, with only a small proportion (7.3%) meeting the > 75 min/week threshold. Given that both MPA and VPA exceeded the recommended levels by the guidelines for only 673 individuals, and there were only 6 cases of CKD patients, further research is necessary to validate the safety of high-volume VPA and to refine guideline recommendations.

In our study, increasing the duration of LPA alone did not demonstrate a statistically significant association with a reduced risk of CKD in the hypertensive population. Nevertheless, current guidelines recommend integrating LPA into daily routines to displace sedentary behavior [23]. Previous research suggests that increasing LPA, while maintaining MVPA levels, may offer protective benefits against CKD in older adults [24]. And other studies have indicated that the risk-reduction effect of LPA is significantly less pronounced than that of MVPA [25]. Consequently, engaging in sufficient MVPA appears essential for CKD risk reduction, while the specific role of LPA in the hypertensive population warrants further investigation.

The mechanisms by which PA reduces the risk of CKD in patients with hypertension may be multifaceted. Firstly, aerobic exercise improves vascular endothelial function by promoting nitric oxide (NO) release, leading to vasodilation, blood pressure reduction, and decreased renal vascular resistance, thereby slowing the progression of glomerulosclerosis [26, 27]. Secondly, exercise modulates autonomic nervous function by suppressing sympathetic activity and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), reducing angiotensin II and aldosterone secretion, which alleviates renal hyperperfusion and proteinuria [28–30]. Additionally, exercise ameliorates metabolic syndrome-related indicators, such as reducing insulin resistance, visceral fat accumulation, and improving lipid profiles (e.g., elevating high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels), thus mitigating metabolic burden on the kidneys [31, 32]. Studies further demonstrate that exercise inhibits oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, attenuates renal tissue damage, and even reverses hypertension-induced microstructural abnormalities in the kidneys, exerting nephroprotective effects [26, 33]. Notably, certain microRNAs, such as miR-204, play a critical role in protecting against hypertension-related renal injury, and exercise training upregulates the expression of these miRNAs to enhance renal protection [34, 35].

Our study has several strengths. First, it is the first accelerometer-based study to assess the association between PA and CKD risk in patients with hypertension, providing a more accurate estimation of their relationship. Second, in addition to adjusting for conventional sociodemographic factors, we accounted for multiple disease-related and genetic factors, enhancing the robustness of our findings. Furthermore, we conducted sensitivity analyses, including excluding individuals who developed CKD within 2 years after baseline to minimize reverse causation due to subclinical kidney dysfunction, and applying an alternative hypertension definition to improve the generalizability of our results.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our sample was derived from the UK Biobank, which may introduce selection bias. Besides, participants who accepted accelerometer measurement in UK biobank might represent a healthier subset. However, evidence suggests that exposure-outcome associations in UK Biobank remain generalizable [36] and the baseline characteristics are generally comparable between those who accepted accelerometer measurement and those who did not [37]. Second, caution is warranted regarding causal inferences. Although we adjusted for numerous covariates, this remains an observational study. Unmeasured confounders, such as specific dietary factors, were not considered. It is also important to note that PAF estimates are similarly susceptible to bias from unmeasured confounding which needs to be examined with other researches. Moreover, covariate status such as medication adherence was assessed only at baseline, and changes during the follow-up period could not be captured. To address this, we calculated E-values to assess robustness. Third, despite our efforts to minimize temporal mismatch by utilizing covariate data from the visit closest to the accelerometer wear date and conducting sensitivity analyses restricted to participants with a shorter interval between assessments, we acknowledge that residual confounding and covariate misclassification may persist. Given that many covariates were measured years before the accelerometer assessment, this constraint cannot be entirely ruled out. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as observational associations, and caution is warranted when drawing causal inferences. Additionally, wrist-worn accelerometers may not perfectly distinguish LPA from sedentary behavior. Also, PA levels were derived from only 7-day measurements while prior evidence suggests these correlate well with long-term levels [38]. Besides, it should be noted that the small number of participants in the high VPA group may compromise the precision of the findings. Finally, relying on ICD-10 records may overlook sub-clinical CKD. However, according to the theory of Non-differential Misclassification, this situation is likely to have a limited impact on the results [39].

Conclusion

This prospective study provides robust evidence that higher levels of MVPA rather than LPA are independently associated with a reduced risk of CKD in individuals with hypertension. Our findings underscore the limitations of relying solely on total activity volume and highlight the specific protective value of physical activity intensity. Consequently, we advocate for the integration of objective, accelerometer-derived metrics into routine clinical risk assessments. Future public health guidelines and hypertension management strategies should explicitly prioritize MVPA as a key, modifiable intervention target to preserve renal function in this high-risk population.
