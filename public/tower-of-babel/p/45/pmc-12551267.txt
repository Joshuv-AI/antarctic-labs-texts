Evaluation of a new TyG indicator, TyHGB, in predicting non-alcoholic fatty liver disease: evidence from two independent populations
Authors: Enfa Zhao, Ruimeng Wang, Yiqing Chen, Hang Xie, Yuan Gao, Bingtian Dong, Chaoxue Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) becomes increasingly common and affects population health, simple and effective screening tools for NAFLD are essential. The triglyceride high-density cholesterol-glucose body index (TyHGB), a novel metabolic index, has shown promise for predicting metabolic disorders. The objective of this research was to assess TyHGB’s predictive capability for NAFLD across two distinct cohorts.MethodsThis retrospective study utilized data obtained from two independent cohorts: a Chinese hospital cohort (n = 181,241) and the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) cohort (n = 3,286). TyHGB was computed according to the following equation: triglyceride level/high-density lipoprotein cholesterol level + 0.7 × fasting blood glucose level (mmol/L) + 0.1 × body mass index (kg/m²). NAFLD was diagnosed using ultrasonography. The TyHGB-NAFLD association was evaluated using multivariable logistic regression analysis, restricted cubic spline methodology, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. To establish the added predictive capacity of TyHGB over the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were determined.ResultsIn both cohorts, participants with higher TyHGB values demonstrated a significantly higher prevalence of NAFLD. After full adjustment for potential confounders, the odds ratios for NAFLD on comparing the highest versus lowest quartiles of TyHGB were 12.22 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 10.73–13.93) in the Chinese cohort and 3.17 (95% CI: 2.25–4.46) in the NHANES cohort. Restricted cubic spline modeling demonstrated significant nonlinear associations between TyHGB values and NAFLD risk in both populations (P for nonlinearity < 0.001). TyHGB demonstrated superior discriminative ability for NAFLD in comparison with the TyG index, with area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.8410 versus 0.7995 in the Chinese cohort and 0.6492 versus 0.5952 in the NHANES cohort (both P < 0.001). Adding TyHGB to the baseline prediction models significantly improved risk discrimination, with greater improvements than those achieved by adding TyG (NRI: 0.0183, 95% CI: 0.015–0.0217; IDI: 0.0067, 95% CI: 0.0057–0.0076; both P < 0.001).ConclusionTyHGB demonstrated robust and superior performance in predicting NAFLD in comparison with the established TyG index across two diverse populations. Since TyHGB only requires routinely measured clinical parameters, it represents a practical and equitable tool for early NAFLD risk stratification across diverse healthcare settings, potentially reducing health disparities in liver disease detection and enabling cost-effective prevention strategies at the population level.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02742-z.Keywords: NAFLD, Insulin resistance, Triglycerides, BMI, Triglyceride high-density cholesterol-glucose body index

Introduction

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) has emerged as one of the most prevalent chronic Liver diseases worldwide, with a recent systematic review reporting a global prevalence of 30.05% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 27.88%−32.32%), and with an alarming increase of 50.4% from 25.26% in 1990–2006 to 38.00% in 2016–2019 [1]. Now termed metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), this disorder represents a continuum ranging from simple steatosis to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, affecting approximately 3%−5% of patients progressing to cirrhosis over 20 years and showing a subsequent risk of hepatocellular carcinoma [2, 3]. NAFLD’s global health burden progressively increases in conjunction with the expanding prevalence of obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and metabolic syndrome [4, 5]. However, NAFLD can manifest in non-obese persons, notably in Asian demographic groups, with occurrence rates of 10%−20% observed among individuals maintaining normal body mass index (BMI) levels, highlighting the heterogeneous pathomechanisms, including dysfunctional adipose tissue, dysregulated hepatokine secretion, and organ crosstalk, that contribute to disease development beyond simple adiposity [6, 7]. The substantial public health burden of NAFLD has necessitated the development of accessible and reliable screening tools to identify at-risk individuals early and enable timely interventions.

While insulin resistance (IR) is commonly considered a central driver of NAFLD pathogenesis, recent evidence has highlighted the substantial heterogeneity in the disease pathophysiology, with some patients developing hepatic steatosis through IR-independent mechanisms, including dysfunctional adipose tissue, subclinical inflammation, and dysregulated hepatokine secretion [7–9]. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, calculated as ln(fasting triglyceride [mg/dL] × fasting plasma glucose [mg/dL]/2), has been proposed as a simple, reliable, and cost-effective surrogate marker for IR, and has been associated with NAFLD risk across diverse populations [10–12]. Previous studies have established a negative correlation between high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) concentrations and NAFLD, while showing positive correlations between NAFLD and triglyceride (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and total cholesterol (TC) levels [13, 14]. However, the TyG index primarily reflects glucose and triglyceride dynamics, potentially overlooking other critical components such as the HDL-C level and body composition, which have also been implicated in NAFLD development [15]. Although TyG has shown utility in NAFLD prediction, it has several notable limitations: (1) it does not account for HDL-C, which plays a protective role against hepatic steatosis through its involvement in reverse cholesterol transport and its anti-inflammatory properties [16]; (2) it fails to incorporate body composition metrics, which are established independent risk factors for NAFLD; and (3) it could fail to comprehensively reflect the multifaceted relationships between diverse metabolic parameters that collectively contribute to NAFLD pathogenesis.

The triglyceride high-density cholesterol-glucose body index (TyHGB), a novel modification of the TyG index that incorporates the HDL-C level and BMI, was recently developed to enhance the predictive capability for IR-related conditions [17, 18]. This index is calculated using the following formula: TG/HDL-C + 0.7 × fasting plasma glucose (mmol/L) + 0.1 × BMI (kg/m²); the TyHGB was initially validated for predicting IR in patients with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) [17, 18]. TyHGB offers several potential advantages over the conventional TyG index: (1) it integrates the TG/HDL-C ratio, which has been demonstrated to be more strongly associated with IR and hepatic steatosis than either parameter alone [9]; (2) it directly incorporates BMI to address the critical role of adiposity in NAFLD pathophysiology; and (3) it offers a more thorough evaluation of metabolic dysfunction by capturing multiple parameters in a single metric. By integrating the HDL-C level and BMI with TG and fasting glucose levels, TyHGB can capture a more comprehensive metabolic profile. Additionally, TyHGB components are routinely measured in clinical practice, making them practical tools that require no additional testing beyond standard laboratory assessments. Given the shared metabolic underpinnings of NAFLD and GDM, namely, IR, dyslipidemia, and adiposity, TyHGB holds promise as a novel tool for NAFLD risk assessment. However, to date, no investigations have examined the performance of TyHGB in predicting NAFLD, nor have its predictive capabilities been compared to the established TyG index across different populations.

This investigation was designed to examine the diagnostic accuracy of TyHGB for NAFLD prediction by using data from two independent cohorts: a large Chinese hospital-based population and a National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) cohort. The study hypothesis proposed that TyHGB, by incorporating the HDL-C level and BMI alongside the established TyG components, would demonstrate superior predictive performance for NAFLD in comparison with the TyG index alone. Specifically, this study sought to (1) determine whether TyHGB offers incremental value over the established TyG index for identifying NAFLD risk, (2) evaluate the consistency of TyHGB’s performance across ethnically diverse populations, and (3) quantify the potential clinical utility of TyHGB as a practical screening tool for NAFLD in routine clinical practice.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective investigation utilized information from an extensive hospital-based health screening database developed by the Wenzhou Medical Center at Wenzhou People’s Hospital in China. To reduce selection bias, participant data from health examinations were gathered sequentially and without selection criteria from January 2010 to December 2014. This dataset has been previously described and employed in multiple publications investigating associations between various metabolic markers and NAFLD [19–21]. For the present analysis, we initially enrolled 181,241 adult participants who had undergone comprehensive health examinations including liver ultrasonography (Fig. 1). Participants were excluded on the basis of the following criteria: (1) substantial alcohol consumption (> 140 g/week for male and > 70 g/week for female); (2) presence of other chronic liver diseases, including viral hepatitis, drug-induced liver injury, or autoimmune hepatitis; (3) use of medications known to influence hepatic steatosis or metabolic parameters (including antihypertensive agents, antidiabetic agents, or lipid-lowering medications); (4) incomplete data necessary for calculation of TyHGB and the TyG index; and (5) missing liver ultrasonography results. The raw data were obtained from the DATADRYAD database (https://datadryad.org/stash) under DOI:10.5061/dryad.1n6c4. The research protocol obtained ethics approval from the Wenzhou People’s Hospital Ethics Committee, and informed consent was acquired from all study participants. For this secondary analysis, individual identifiers were deleted, and health examination numbers were used instead of personal information to ensure confidentiality.

Study flow diagram and participant selection process. Flow chart illustrating the selection of participants from two independent populations

The findings were validated in a varied population through analysis of NHANES data from the 2017–2018 cycle. From the initial NHANES 2017–2018 population, 3,286 adult participants with complete data for liver ultrasonography findings, TG, HDL-C, and fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels, and BMI required for the calculation of TyHGB were included (Fig. 1). Exclusion criteria similar to those used in the Chinese cohort were applied. Individuals with heavy alcohol intake, viral hepatitis, or other established liver pathologies were eliminated, as were those taking medications that may affect hepatic steatosis or metabolism. Patients with BMI > 25 kg/m2 were not excluded from the NHANES cohort, which served as a validation population with more diverse body composition profiles and allowed us to assess the generalizability of TyHGB across different body habitus representations. Thus, we could directly test whether the superior performance of TyHGB over TyG would persist in participants with a higher BMI (including obese participants), in participants with elevated LDL-C levels, and in a more metabolically diverse population. This approach strengthens our findings, since TyHGB maintained its superiority despite the increased heterogeneity. The National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board approved the NHANES protocols, and all participants provided written informed consent. Since this is a publicly available, de-identified dataset, further institutional review board approval was not necessary for this analysis.

Ultrasonographic diagnosis of NAFLD

In the Chinese hospital cohort, NAFLD diagnosis was established through ultrasonographic evaluation following guidelines from the Chinese Liver Disease Association [22]. Qualified technicians without access to participants’ clinical and laboratory information conducted the ultrasound evaluations. NAFLD was characterized by diffuse intensification of near-field echogenicity in the liver (exceeding that of kidney and spleen) with progressive far-field echo attenuation when accompanied by any of these characteristics: (1) indistinct intrahepatic vascular structure visualization, (2) mild-to-moderate liver enlargement with rounded, blunt margins, (3) diminished blood flow signals with preserved normal flow patterns, and (4) poor or incomplete delineation of the right hepatic lobe boundary and diaphragmatic interface. NAFLD was diagnosed when at least two experienced ultrasonographers agreed on the findings. When discordant findings occurred, a third senior ultrasonographer was engaged to establish consensus.

For the NHANES cohort, transient elastography via FibroScan model 502 V2 Touch was employed for hepatic steatosis assessment. The FibroScan device utilizes vibration-controlled transient elastography to generate liver stiffness measurements while concurrently assessing ultrasound attenuation associated with liver steatosis through the controlled attenuation parameter (CAP). In accordance with established diagnostic thresholds, NAFLD was defined as a CAP score ≥ 248 dB/m in the absence of significant alcohol consumption (defined as > 3 drinks/day in male and > 2 drinks/day in female) and other known causes of liver disease, including viral hepatitis (hepatitis B virus [HBV] or hepatitis C virus [HCV] infections) [23–25]. Participants with HBV or HCV infection were identified on the basis of positive serological test results or self-reported diagnoses.

Covariates

In the Chinese hospital cohort, comprehensive data for clinical and biochemical parameters were collected during health examinations. Blood specimens were obtained following a minimum 8-hour overnight fasting period and analyzed in a hospital laboratory using standardized methods. The demographic information included data for age and sex. Anthropometric assessments encompassed weight and height measurements, obtained while participants wore minimal clothing without footwear. BMI was computed as body weight in kilograms divided by height squared in meters (kg/m²). Blood pressure was recorded in a seated position after a 5-min rest using an automated sphygmomanometer. Laboratory assessments included liver function tests for measurement of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), gamma glutamyl transferase (GGT), total protein, albumin, globulin, total bilirubin, and direct bilirubin levels. Kidney function was evaluated on the basis of the blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, and uric acid levels. Lipid parameter evaluation included TC, TG, HDL-C, and LDL-C determinations. FBG levels were also measured. All biochemical analyses were conducted using an automated analyzer (Abbott AxSYM) and standard methods.

For the NHANES cohort, a comprehensive set of demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical variables was collected using standardized protocols, including questionnaires, physical evaluations, and laboratory measurements. Demographic information included data for age, sex (male, female), and race/ethnicity (categorized as Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American, and Others). Socioeconomic factors, such as education achievement (categorized as below high school, high school, and above high school), marital status (categorized as unmarried or married), and household income were also recorded. Anthropometric measurements included measurement of waist circumference and BMI. Clinical variables encompassed cardiovascular disease (CVD), characterized as participant-reported diagnoses of heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina, myocardial infarction, or stroke. Diabetes was defined as having been diagnosed by a doctor or having a fasting blood sugar level of ≥ 126 mg/dL. Hypertension was defined as blood pressure readings of 140/90 mmHg or above or a prior diagnosis of hypertension in participants not currently taking antihypertensive medications (since those on antihypertensive therapy were excluded from the analysis). Smoking patterns were grouped as never smoker, former smoker, or current smoker. Blood pressure assessment was conducted following a 5-minute rest period with individuals in a seated posture, and the mean of three measurements was utilized for analysis. Blood specimens were obtained following overnight fasting and analyzed at the NHANES Health and Nutrition Examination Center. Laboratory measurements included measurement of liver enzyme (ALT and AST), lipid profile (TC, HDL-C, LDL-C, and triglycerides), FBG, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels. Prescription medication usage, including blood pressure, diabetes, and cholesterol medications, was documented through self-reports and medication inventories. Study participants were eliminated if they demonstrated considerable alcohol consumption, viral hepatitis, additional confirmed liver disorders, or pharmaceutical therapy that could affect hepatic steatosis or metabolic function. Laboratory assessments for both study populations were standardized following their respective institutional guidelines. The TyHGB index was derived through the following calculation: TG/HDL-C + 0.7*FBG (mmol/L) + 0.1*BMI (kg/m²), while the comparison marker TyG index was computed as ln[TG (mg/dL) × FBG (mg/dL)/2].

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables were described using mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed data were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges [Q1, Q3]. Categorical data were displayed as frequency (percentage). Baseline characteristic variations among TyHGB quartiles were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed data and one-way ANOVA for normally distributed data, while categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests. Missing data were handled through k-nearest neighbor (KNN) imputation to maximize the utilization of available data. Prior to performing the primary analyses, multicollinearity evaluation among covariates was conducted using the variance inflation factor (VIF) methodology. Covariates with VIF values exceeding 5 were deemed to demonstrate substantial collinearity and underwent careful assessment for final model inclusion.

The TyHGB index was analyzed both as a categorical variable (by quartiles) and as a continuous variable. To examine the association between TyHGB and NAFLD, multivariable logistic regression models were utilized, and odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated. Three stepwise adjustment models were established: Model I (unadjusted); Model II (adjusted for sex, age, and BMI for the Chinese cohort; sex, age, BMI, and race/ethnicity for the NHANES cohort); and Model III (for the Chinese cohort: fully adjusted for sex, age, BMI, and GGT, ALT, TC, AST, LDL-C, total protein, total bilirubin, direct bilirubin, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, and uric acid; for the NHANES cohort: fully adjusted for sex, age, BMI, race/ethnicity, marital status, blood urea nitrogen, CVD, smoking status, AST, diabetes, albumin, hypertension, creatinine, ALT, uric acid, TC, and LDL-C levels). Linear trends were performed by incorporating the median value of each TyHGB quartile as a continuous variable within the regression analyses.

For investigating possible nonlinear associations between TyHGB and NAFLD risk, restricted cubic spline (RCS) modeling was conducted with knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the TyHGB distribution. The significance of nonlinear relationships was evaluated using likelihood ratio tests that compared linear-term models against spline-term models. The predictive performance of TyHGB for NAFLD was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with the area under the ROC curve (AUC) calculated with the 95% CIs. DeLong’s test was used to compare the AUCs of the TyHGB and TyG index. Net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) calculations were conducted without category constraints to quantify the incremental value of incorporating TyHGB or TyG into baseline risk assessment models.

The baseline prediction model for the Chinese cohort included sex, age, total protein, BMI, total bilirubin, GGT, blood urea nitrogen, ALT, direct bilirubin, AST, creatinine, TC, uric acid, and LDL-C levels. For the NHANES cohort, the baseline model included sex, age, BMI, race/ethnicity, marital status, CVD, smoking status, diabetes, hypertension, and ALT, AST, albumin, creatinine, uric acid, blood urea nitrogen, TC, and LDL-C levels. Data analysis was were performed using R software (version 4.1.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and EmpowerStats software (http://www.empowerstat.org/en/). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 across all analyses.

Baseline characteristics of NHANES individuals classified by quartiles of the TyHGB

In the Chinese cohort, age and sex distributions differed significantly across the TyHGB quartiles. Participants in the higher TyHGB quartiles were significantly older and included a significantly higher proportion of males, with male representation rising from 20.65% in Q1 to 77.83% in Q4 (P < 0.001). BMI increased significantly with increasing TyHGB quartiles (P < 0.001). Liver enzyme levels differed significantly across the TyHGB quartiles. The mean GGT level increased from 17.97 ± 11.07 IU/L in Q1 to 41.29 ± 41.56 IU/L in Q4, and the ALT level increased from 15.34 ± 14.25 IU/L to 25.28 ± 17.99 IU/L (both P < 0.001). Consistent patterns were identified for Lipid parameters, with the TC and LDL-C levels increasing from 4.37 ± 0.71 to 4.72 ± 0.73 mmol/L and 2.06 ± 0.45 to 2.40 ± 0.45 µmol/L, respectively (both P < 0.001). Creatinine and uric acid levels also increased significantly across TyHGB quartiles (P < 0.001).

The NHANES cohort exhibited comparable patterns, with older and predominantly male participants in the higher TyHGB quartiles (P < 0.001). Notably, the increase in BMI across TyHGB quartiles was more pronounced in the NHANES population, rising from 22.51 ± 3.02 kg/m² in Q1 to 34.49 ± 8.91 kg/m² in Q4 (P < 0.001). Liver enzyme levels also demonstrated similar trends to the Chinese cohort, with ALT levels rising from 15.78 ± 8.47 IU/L in Q1 to 29.90 ± 23.32 IU/L in Q4 (P < 0.001). Parameters of glucose metabolism also demonstrated significant deterioration across TyHGB quartiles, evidenced by HbA1c values rising from 5.29% ± 0.31% in Q1 to 5.92% ± 1.29% in Q4 (P < 0.001). Higher TyHGB quartiles were associated with significant socioeconomic disparities, including family income-to-poverty ratios (2.84 ± 1.57 in Q1 vs. 2.42 ± 1.46 in Q4, P < 0.001) and lower educational level (percentage with above high school education: 67.88% in Q1 vs. 52.49% in Q4, P < 0.001). The prevalence of cardiometabolic comorbidities increased significantly across the TyHGB quartiles in the NHANES cohort. Diabetes prevalence showed a substantial increase from 0.61% in the first quartile to 13.97% in the fourth quartile, whereas the prevalence of hypertension increased from 12.41% to 30.86% (both P < 0.001). Similarly, CVD was more prevalent in higher TyHGB quartiles (2.07% in Q1 vs. 4.25% in Q4; P = 0.014).

Nonlinear relationship between TyHGB and the risk of NAFLD

RCS analyses were performed to exam the dose-response association between TyHGB and NAFLD risk in both cohorts (Fig. 2). In the Chinese cohort, a notable nonlinear association was confirmed between TyHGB and NAFLD (P for nonlinearity, < 0.001; Fig. 2A). This relationship exhibited a J-shaped curve with minimal risk at lower TyHGB values, followed by a progressive increase in NAFLD risk as TyHGB values increased beyond a certain threshold. Specifically, the risk remained relatively flat at lower TyHGB values, but increased exponentially thereafter, with the steepest gradient observed at higher values. At the highest observed TyHGB values, the adjusted odds ratio for NAFLD exceeded 10.0, demonstrating the substantial discriminative capacity of this index. The NHANES cohort also showed a similar significant nonlinear relationship (P for nonlinearity < 0.001; Fig. 2B), but with a different pattern. The association in the NHANES cohort demonstrated an initial sharp increase in the NAFLD risk at lower TyHGB values, followed by a more gradual positive slope at higher values.

Associations between TyHGB and NAFLD

Table 3 presents the findings from the multivariate logistic regression analyses examining the association between TyHGB and NAFLD in both cohorts. In the Chinese cohort, TyHGB demonstrated a robust dose-dependent relationship with NAFLD, which remained significant following extensive adjustment for potential confounding variables. When examined as a continuous variable in the comprehensively adjusted model (Model III), each unit increase in TyHGB was associated with an 82% increase in the odds of NAFLD (OR = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.79–1.85, P < 0.0001). When grouped into quartiles, participants within the top TyHGB quartile showed markedly higher odds of NAFLD compared to those in the lowest quartile (OR = 12.22, 95% CI: 10.73–13.93, P < 0.0001), with significant positive associations also observed for the second (OR = 2.56, 95% CI: 2.24–2.92, P < 0.0001) and third quartiles (OR = 5.03, 95% CI: 4.42–5.73, P < 0.0001). A statistically significant linear pattern was evident across the quartiles (P for trend, < 0.0001), reinforcing the dose-dependent nature of this relationship. In the NHANES cohort, TyHGB exhibited statistically significant positive associations with NAFLD, though the strength of these associations was moderate. In Model III, every unit improvement in TyHGB was Linked to a 7% rise in NAFLD odds (OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.04–1.10, P < 0.0001). Quartile analysis revealed progressively increasing ORs across TyHGB categories, with participants in the second (OR = 1.62, 95% CI: 1.22–2.14, P = 0.0008), third (OR = 2.63, 95% CI: 1.94–3.56, P < 0.0001), and fourth quartiles (OR = 3.17, 95% CI: 2.25–4.46, P < 0.0001) demonstrating substantially increased odds of NAFLD compared to the lowest quartile. Similar to the Chinese cohort, a significant dose-response trend was demonstrated across the quartiles (P for trend < 0.0001).

Additive predictive capacity of the TyHGB index for NAFLD

The discriminative performance of TyHGB for NAFLD was evaluated using the ROC curve analysis (Fig. 3). In the Chinese cohort, TyHGB demonstrated superior predictive ability in comparison with the TyG index, with an AUC of 0.8410 (95% CI: 0.8385–0.8434) versus 0.7995 (95% CI: 0.7981–0.8050) for TyG (P < 0.001 by DeLong’s test, Fig. 3A). Similarly, in the NHANES cohort, TyHGB exhibited significantly better discriminative capacity for NAFLD, with an AUC of 0.6492 (95% CI: 0.6302–0.6681) in comparison with the TyG index, which had an AUC of 0.5952 (95% CI: 0.5737–0.6145) (P < 0.001, Fig. 3B). These findings indicate that TyHGB consistently outperformed TyG in identifying NAFLD across both populations, despite the overall lower AUC values in the NHANES cohort.

To further assess the potential clinical utility of these indices, their incremental value when added to established risk prediction models was examined (Fig. 4). In the Chinese cohort, the baseline model incorporating conventional risk factors yielded an AUC of 0.8173 (95% CI: 0.8144–0.8201). Adding the TyG index to this baseline model significantly improved the AUC to 0.8518 (95% CI: 0.8494–0.8545) (P < 0.001), while incorporating TyHGB further enhanced the predictive performance to an AUC of 0.8616 (95% CI: 0.8591–0.8640) (P < 0.001 in comparison with the baseline model and the baseline model plus TyG, Fig. 4A). Similarly, in the NHANES cohort, the addition of TyHGB to the baseline model (AUC: 0.7127, 95% CI: 0.6939–0.7319) resulted in a greater improvement in predictive performance (AUC: 0.7280, 95% CI: 0.7095–0.7466) in comparison with the addition of TyG (AUC: 0.7241, 95% CI: 0.7057–0.7443), with significant differences between all model comparisons (all P < 0.05, Fig. 4B).

The incremental value of TyHGB over both the baseline model and the baseline model plus TyG was further quantified using the NRI and IDI (Fig. 5). In the Chinese cohort, adding TyHGB to the baseline model yielded a significant NRI of 0.082 (95% CI: 0.0772–0.0868) and an IDI of 0.077 (95% CI: 0.0749–0.0792) (both P < 0.001), which were greater than the improvements observed with TyG (NRI: 0.0631, 95% CI: 0.0578–0.0684; IDI: 0.0704, 95% CI: 0.0685–0.0722; both P < 0.001). Notably, replacing TyG with TyHGB in the prediction model resulted in significant improvements in both NRI (0.0183, 95% CI: 0.015–0.0217) and IDI (0.0067, 95% CI: 0.0057–0.0076) (both P < 0.001), demonstrating the incremental value of TyHGB beyond TyG. Additionally, substituting TyG with TyHGB resulted in significant improvements in reclassification (NRI: 0.1179, 95% CI: 0.0434–0.1924, P = 0.00191; IDI: 0.0048, 95% CI: 0.0016–0.0081, P = 0.00371). These findings consistently demonstrated that TyHGB provides meaningful improvements in NAFLD risk prediction beyond conventional risk factors and the established TyG index across distinct populations.

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the influence of different exclusion criteria on our findings, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by applying the same BMI and LDL-C restrictions to the NHANES cohort. Among the 771 participants meeting these criteria, adding TyHGB to the baseline model improved the AUC from 0.7056 (95% CI: 0.6429–0.7660) to 0.7326 (95% CI: 0.6720–0.7891), showing a trend toward significance (P = 0.062). This improvement was more pronounced than that achieved by adding TyG, which increased the AUC to 0.7306 (95% CI: 0.6722–0.7836, P = 0.172 vs. baseline; Supplementary Fig. 1). Notably, the incremental value of TyHGB over the baseline model was substantial: the NRI of 0.3168 (95% CI: 0.1723–0.4612, P < 0.001) and IDI of 0.0245 (95% CI: 0.0066–0.0424, P = 0.00732) indicated significant improvements in risk classification and discrimination. When comparing TyHGB to TyG, statistical significance was not achieved (P = 0.415), likely due to the reduced sample size, the NRI (0.0276, 95% CI: −0.0681-0.1234) and IDI (0.0021, 95% CI: −0.0017-0.0058) showed consistent trends favoring TyHGB (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study presented a comprehensive evaluation of TyHGB as a novel predictor of NAFLD in two independent populations. The findings convincingly demonstrated that TyHGB exhibits superior predictive performance in comparison with the established TyG index in both the Chinese hospital and NHANES cohorts. A robust, dose-dependent relationship between TyHGB and NAFLD risk was consistently observed across both populations, with the highest TyHGB quartile being associated with substantially increased odds of NAFLD in comparison with the lowest quartile in both cohorts. TyHGB demonstrated superior discriminative performance for NAFLD compared to TyG, as evidenced by higher AUC values in both cohorts. Furthermore, adding TyHGB to the baseline prediction models yielded significant improvements in risk reclassification and discrimination metrics (NRI and IDI), with greater improvements than those achieved by adding TyG. These findings collectively suggest that TyHGB represents an advancement over the existing metabolic indices for NAFLD risk assessment.

The TyHGB index is an extension of the established TyG index that incorporates additional metabolic parameters, namely the HDL-C level and BMI, to improve its utility as a comprehensive assessment tool. The TyG index has proven to be a beneficial marker for metabolic dysfunction evaluation without the need for direct insulin measurement, thus providing a cost-effective approach for evaluating IR across various clinical environments [26, 27]. However, the TyG index has several important limitations that have prompted the development of more comprehensive indices. It fails to account for HDL-C, which plays a protective role against hepatic steatosis through its involvement in reverse cholesterol transport and its anti-inflammatory properties [16, 27, 28]. Because of the exclusion of this protective factor, the TyG index may not fully capture an individual’s metabolic risk profile, particularly related to lipid metabolism beyond TG levels. Additionally, the TyG index does not incorporate any measure of body composition or adiposity, such as BMI, despite the established independent relationship between body composition metrics and metabolic disorders, such as NAFLD [29, 30]. The novel TyHGB index incorporates two additional parameters, HDL-C level and BMI, to create a more comprehensive assessment of metabolic health. Rather than simply adding HDL-C as an independent factor, TyHGB incorporates it as part of the TG/HDL-C ratio, which has been found to demonstrate more powerful associations with IR and hepatic steatosis compared to TG or HDL-C alone [9, 31, 32]. This ratio effectively captures the balance between potentially harmful (TG) and protective (HDL-C) lipid fractions, offering deeper insights into lipid metabolism beyond that offered by triglycerides alone.

The present findings indicate that TyHGB demonstrates robust and superior performance in predicting NAFLD in comparison with the established TyG index across diverse populations. These results are consistent with and expand upon existing research on metabolic indices and NAFLD prediction. Previous research have established the value of the TyG index as a cost-effective and easily accessible alternative for NAFLD risk assessment [33]. The TyG index acts as a substitute biomarker for IR, a critical component in the development of NAFLD, and its predictive capability has been confirmed across various ethnicities [10, 34]. Studies have also explored the combination of TyG with other anthropometric measurements to improve the prediction of NAFLD. Notably, the TyG-BMI index provides superior predictive performance for NAFLD compared to TyG used independently [33]. In a study of non-obese patients, TyG-BMI exhibited enhanced performance relative to TyG alone in evaluating NAFLD risk [35]. Furthermore, TyG-BMI exhibited the highest AUC for predicting NAFLD in patients with type 2 diabetes [36]. Similarly, TyG-related parameters such as TyG-WC (waist circumference) and TyG-WHtR (waist-to-height ratio) have shown greater predictive capacity for NAFLD than the TyG index alone [33, 37]. The present findings extend this line of research by incorporating the HDL-C level and BMI with the TyG index to yield a single comprehensive metric. The consistent superiority of TyHGB over TyG in both cohorts suggests that the incorporation of additional metabolic parameters better captures the multifaceted metabolic determinants of NAFLD. This aligns with the expanding understanding that NAFLD represents more than just a hepatic component of metabolic syndrome but a complex condition influenced by interrelated metabolic pathways involving lipid metabolism, glucose homeostasis, and adiposity [38]. By integrating multiple metabolic parameters into a single index, TyHGB can serve as an efficient marker for NAFLD risk assessment that accounts for the interconnected nature of these metabolic factors.

The superior predictive ability of TyHGB for NAFLD is supported by its strong biological plausibility related to the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying NAFLD development. NAFLD demonstrates strong associations with metabolic dysfunction, and IR plays a central role in its pathogenesis. The TyG index, a component of TyHGB, is a well-established surrogate marker for IR, reflecting impaired glucose metabolism, which contributes to hepatic steatosis through enhanced newly synthesized fat production and impaired suppression of lipolysis [34]. The TyHGB includes the TG and HDL-C levels, both of which are critical indicators of lipid metabolism that directly influence hepatic fat accumulation [18, 39]. Elevated TG levels reflect increased substrate availability for hepatic fat deposition, whereas reduced HDL-C levels signify impaired reverse cholesterol transport, which normally helps remove excess cholesterol from peripheral tissues, including the liver. By incorporating both TG and HDL-C levels as a ratio, TyHGB provides a more complete assessment of dyslipidemia than TyG alone, which only accounts for TG. Obesity, which is quantified by BMI in the TyHGB formula, is a significant risk factor for IR and NAFLD [40]. Excess adipose tissue, particularly visceral fat, contributes to the pathogenesis of NAFLD through multiple mechanisms: (1) increased release of free fatty acids, which are taken up by the liver and contribute to hepatic steatosis; (2) production of pro-inflammatory adipokines that promote hepatic inflammation and fibrosis; and (3) induction of IR, which further exacerbates hepatic steatosis through dysregulated glucose and lipid metabolism. The inclusion of BMI in TyHGB accounted for the contribution of overall adiposity to NAFLD risk, thereby addressing a major limitation of the TyG index.

The distinct nonlinear patterns between the cohorts reflected population-specific metabolic thresholds. The J-shaped curve in the Chinese cohort, with a minimal risk below a TyHGB value of 7.5, indicates preserved metabolic flexibility at lower values: intact mitochondrial function, appropriate insulin signaling, and functional adipose tissue maintaining hepatic lipid homeostasis. Once this threshold is exceeded, these compensatory mechanisms become overwhelmed, leading to an exponential increase in the risk of NAFLD. This result agrees with studies demonstrating that East Asian populations maintain metabolic health at lower BMI levels, but metabolic health deteriorates rapidly beyond specific thresholds [41]. The NHANES cohort’s immediate risk elevation at low TyHGB values indicates preexisting metabolic inflexibility, likely due to chronic overnutrition and the dysfunctional adipose tissue characteristics in Western populations. The subsequent risk plateau may represent hepatic adaptation through the formation of less-lipotoxic large lipid droplets, rather than dispersed lipid accumulation [42]. These patterns suggest that East Asian populations may benefit from early interventions targeting a TyHGB threshold of 7.5, whereas Western populations require aggressive intervention even at lower TyHGB values. This population-specific risk stratification can guide personalized NAFLD prevention strategies.

Notable differences in the performance of TyHGB were observed between the Chinese hospital and NHANES cohorts, with higher AUC values in the Chinese population (0.8410 vs. 0.6492). Several factors may have contributed to this discrepancy. The greatest variation between study populations was observed in the extent of the TyHGB-NAFLD relationship. In the Chinese cohort, the odds ratio for NAFLD, when comparing the highest and lowest TyHGB quartiles, was substantially higher than that in the NHANES cohort. Thus, the relationship between metabolic parameters and NAFLD risk may vary across populations, potentially owing to differences in genetic susceptibility, environmental factors, or interactions between these elements [43]. Differences in baseline characteristics between the cohorts may partially explain the observed disparities in performance. The Chinese cohort exhibited a more homogeneous metabolic profile, potentially allowing for clearer differentiation between metabolic health states. In contrast, the NHANES cohort displayed greater heterogeneity in metabolic parameters, including a wider range of BMI values (particularly in the higher quartiles), which might have attenuated the discriminative capacity of TyHGB. Additionally, the NHANES cohort presented with greater comorbidity burden, particularly diabetes and hypertension, throughout all TyHGB quartiles, potentially confounding the relationship between TyHGB and NAFLD. The diagnostic criteria and methodologies for NAFLD assessment may also have contributed to the observed differences. Moreover, population-based differences in body composition and metabolic risk patterns likely account for the varying effectiveness of TyHGB. Asian populations typically exhibit metabolic dysfunction at lower BMI thresholds in comparison with Western populations, a phenomenon sometimes termed “metabolically obese normal weight” [44, 45]. This may explain why TyHGB, which incorporates BMI, demonstrated stronger associations with NAFLD in the Chinese cohort despite the lower average BMI values. Genetic factors influencing lipid metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and hepatic fat accumulation may further contribute to ethnic variations in the relationship between metabolic indices and NAFLD [46, 47]. Lifestyle and dietary factors that differ substantially between populations may influence the association between metabolic variables and NAFLD. Differences in dietary composition, physical activity patterns, and alcohol consumption between Chinese and American populations can influence both the prevalence of metabolic disturbances and their influence on hepatic steatosis [48, 49]. These factors may not have been fully captured by TyHGB or adequately controlled for in the analysis, potentially contributing to performance differences. Despite these differences, TyHGB consistently outperformed TyG in both populations, suggesting that the fundamental approach of incorporating multiple metabolic parameters provides improved NAFLD risk assessment across diverse demographic groups, even if the magnitude of benefit varies. The superior performance of TyHGB in lean individuals may reflect its ability to capture the lipodystrophy-like phenotype [50], which is characterized by impaired adipose tissue expandability and ectopic fat deposition despite normal BMI. The components of TyHGB, particularly the TG/HDL-C ratio, align with the atherogenic dyslipidemia pattern typical of this phenotype, potentially explaining why TyHGB shows stronger associations in BMI-restricted populations.

The superior discriminative ability of TyHGB in comparison with TyG suggests that healthcare providers may benefit from transitioning to this more comprehensive index for NAFLD risk assessment. The components required for the TyHGB calculation (triglycerides, HDL-C, FBG, and BMI) are routinely measured in standard clinical evaluations and do not require specialized equipment or expensive assays, making them practical options for various healthcare settings, including resource-limited environments. Prompt recognition of high-risk NAFLD individuals through TyHGB assessment may enable proactive preventive measure implementation before the occurrence of significant liver damage occurs. By detecting individuals at elevated risk who could benefit from intensified lifestyle approaches or enhanced monitoring, TyHGB could facilitate a more efficient allocation of healthcare resources and potentially decrease NAFLD-associated complication burden.

Beyond NAFLD prediction, TyHGB may also have broader applications in cardiometabolic risk stratification. As noted by Stefan et al. [51], while novel metabolic phenotyping approaches may not universally outperform established risk models, they show promise for specific populations. The strong performance of TyHGB in lean individuals suggests that it could help identify those with metabolically unhealthy normal weight, a group with a disproportionately high cardiometabolic risk despite a normal BMI. The comprehensive nature of TyHGB, which incorporates lipid ratios, glucose metabolism, and adiposity, captures multiple dimensions of metabolic dysfunction that collectively drive CVD and diabetes risks. Future longitudinal studies should evaluate whether TyHGB can predict incident cardiovascular events and diabetes and potentially act as a simple, unified tool for metabolic risk assessment that can guide both NAFLD screening and broader cardiometabolic risk stratification in clinical practice.

Study strengths and limitations

The study conclusions are supported by several strong design elements: the large Chinese cohort provided substantial statistical power for detecting meaningful associations; validation across two ethnically diverse populations enhanced the generalizability of our conclusions; our comprehensive statistical approach, including RCS, ROC curves, and NRI/IDI calculations, enabled a thorough assessment of TyHGB’s performance; and adjustment for potential confounders minimized the risk of spurious associations. Our sensitivity analysis also provided crucial insights into the performance of TyHGB under standardized conditions. When identical exclusion criteria were applied to the NHANES cohort, creating a population comparable to the Chinese cohort, TyHGB still demonstrated meaningful improvement over the baseline model (ΔAUC = 0.027, P = 0.062), with significant improvements in both NRI (0.3168, P < 0.001) and IDI (0.0245, P = 0.00732). These improvements were more substantial than those achieved by TyG (ΔAUC = 0.025, P = 0.172). However, several key limitations of this research deserve attention. The cross-sectional design of both cohorts prevented the establishment of temporal relationships and causal inferences. Selection bias may have influenced our findings, particularly in this hospital-based Chinese cohort study. The unavailability of blood pressure measurements and clinical diagnostic information in the publicly available Chinese cohort dataset prevented a direct comparison of the prevalence of comorbidities between the Chinese and NHANES populations, and may partially explain the performance differences observed between cohorts. The utilization of ultrasound imaging rather than hepatic biopsy for NAFLD identification could result in prevalence underestimation, and technical differences between cohorts may influence diagnostic consistency. The Chinese cohort data obtained from a publicly available database had preexisting exclusions for BMI > 25 kg/m² and LDL-c levels > 3.12 mmol/L. Although the analysis of participants outside these ranges was not possible in the Chinese cohort, validation in the unrestricted NHANES cohort, which included participants with a wide range of BMI and LDL-c values, demonstrated that TyHGB maintained superior predictive performance across diverse populations. Furthermore, the retrospective nature of the analysis may have introduced information bias. Despite extensive adjustments, unmeasured factors such as dietary habits, exercise patterns, and genetic susceptibility could have confounded the observed associations. Finally, although strong associations between TyHGB and NAFLD were established in this study, interventional studies are required to determine whether targeting TyHGB components can effectively reduce the risk of NAFLD or improve liver outcomes. Future studies should validate TyHGB in individuals with BMI > 25 kg/m² to explore whether differential utility exists across adiposity phenotypes.

Clinical implementation

On the basis of these findings, we propose practical TyHGB thresholds for clinical decision-making d. For East Asian populations, TyHGB < 7.5 indicates low risk (annual monitoring), 7.5-9.0 suggests intermediate risk (biannual assessment with structured lifestyle interventions), and > 9.0 warrants high-risk management (quarterly monitoring, hepatic imaging, and consideration for pharmacotherapy). Western populations, given their heightened metabolic susceptibility, require lower thresholds: low risk, < 6.0; intermediate risk, 6.0–8.0; and high risk, > 8.0. Implementation can be facilitated through automated calculation of electronic health records using routine laboratory values (TG, HDL-C, and FBG levels and BMI), with alert systems triggered when thresholds are exceeded. This risk-stratified approach enables efficient resource allocation, targeting intensive interventions for high-risk individuals while avoiding the overmedicalization of low-risk populations. The simplicity of the TyHGB calculation, which requires only standard metabolic panel components, makes it immediately implementable in primary care settings without additional costs. Healthcare systems can integrate TyHGB into existing cardiovascular risk assessment protocols to create comprehensive metabolic evaluation pathways. Prospective studies should validate these proposed thresholds and evaluate whether TyHGB-guided interventions can improve clinical outcomes in comparison with standard care.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that TyHGB, a novel metabolic index incorporating triglyceride, HDL cholesterol, and FBG levels as well as BMI, exhibited superior performance in predicting NAFLD in comparison with the established TyG index across two diverse populations. The nonlinear relationship between TyHGB values and NAFLD risk revealed population-specific metabolic thresholds that can guide targeted screening strategies. Given that TyHGB only requires routinely measured clinical parameters, it represents a practical tool for early NAFLD risk stratification in primary care settings. Beyond NAFLD prediction, the comprehensive metabolic assessment provided by TyHGB may prove valuable for broader cardiometabolic risk evaluation, particularly in lean individuals where traditional approaches may be inadequate. Future prospective studies should validate these thresholds and determine whether TyHGB-guided interventions can improve clinical outcomes.
