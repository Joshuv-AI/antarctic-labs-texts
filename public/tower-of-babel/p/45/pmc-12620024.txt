Postpartum Blood Pressure Screening in the United States Primary Care Settings: A Systematic Review
Authors: Emmanuel Adediran, Robert Owens, Carl Whittaker, Katherine T Fortenberry, Saskia Spiess
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDTimely blood pressure (BP) screening is not consistently performed for new mothers. Only 60% complete a 6-week postpartum visit, and even fewer are evaluated within 1 week. This timing is crucial since mortality is highest in the first 6 days. Primary care has a role to play in improving postpartum BP care. However, research has yet to fully explore primary care-driven strategies for postpartum BP screening.OBJECTIVEThis review aims to identify primary care postpartum BP screening strategies and to understand the characteristics of patients who are completing BP screenings within 1 week.METHODSWe used the PubMed and Web of Science databases to identify peer-reviewed studies published since 2010. Included studies were conducted in the United States and of English-language publications.RESULTSWe identified 13,452 articles and synthesized 32 studies. Of 11,270 postpartum patients, 40% (n = 4,790) identified as Black/African American race. For recruitment, 24 (75.0%) studies focused on high-risk patients, 5 (15.6%) compared high-risk to low-risk patients, and 3 (9.4%) studies recruited all patients with no restrictions. Studies often reported BP ascertainment within 1-week postpartum (53.1%; n = 17). Further, 12 (37.5%) studies incorporated both in-clinic and in-home settings for postpartum BP screening, 10 (31.2%) were in-clinic, and 10 (31.2%) were in-home.CONCLUSIONSWe found that primary care systems are evaluating BP within 1-week postpartum, leveraging remote BP monitoring and child-wellness checks. Those strategies, however, less often included low-risk patients. Updated guidelines are needed to cover postpartum BP screening for patients with uncomplicated pregnancies and the absence of other risk factors.Keywords: blood pressure, hypertension, postpartum, primary care

Maternal morbidity and mortality rates are concerningly high in the United States. In a recent snapshot, maternal mortality rates were 17.4 deaths per 100,000 live births in 2018, 20.1 deaths in 2019, 32.9 deaths in 2021, and 22.3 deaths in 2022.1 These outcomes are worse for Black/African American women. Notably, they have the highest mortality rates, with an estimated 49.5 deaths per 100,000 live births reported in 2022, compared to women identifying as White (19.0), Hispanic (16.9), and Asian (13.2).1

Importantly, more than 50% of pregnancy-related deaths occur in the postpartum period.2,3 Of these, 18.6% of deaths occur within 6-days postpartum, 21.4% of deaths within 42 days, and 11.7% of deaths within 1-year postpartum.3 Chronic health complications, resulting from hypertension, diabetes, and obesity, are contributing to this crisis.4 Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP), in particular, are among the leading causes of maternal mortality in the United States,5 and may go undiagnosed in the postpartum period, resulting in significant maternal morbidity and mortality.

Early blood pressure (BP) screening, occurring within 1-week postpartum, is optimal for assessing new-onset and progression of HDP and to prevent and manage complications like preeclampsia, eclampsia, and stroke.6 However, BP screening in this postpartum window is not routinely performed for all new mothers, and existing clinical guidelines only cover high-risk patients, such as those diagnosed along the spectrum of hypertensive disorders during pregnancy.7

Prior reviews make no mention of low-risk patients and have largely implemented and compared pharmacological strategies in the afflicted populations.8–10 For example, Magee et al. investigated the effectiveness of antihypertensive therapy to treat postpartum hypertension.11 Similarly, Cairns et al. compared the efficacy of therapeutical interventions in reducing postpartum BP.10 However, their findings are limited since they focused only on high-risk patients and those who have been diagnosed with HDP—with no mention of postpartum screening for previously normotensive patients. This limitation becomes important given how more than 12% of women, with no comorbidities, will experience new-onset hypertension post-delivery.12 Moreover, the incidence is likely underreported since health systems do not comprehensively capture postpartum outcomes in low-risk moms.12

Another research gap is the lack of focus on the role primary care can play in improving postpartum BP care and management. Primary care is uniquely suited for this role, given its focus on primary prevention, patient-centeredness, affordability, and holistic care.13 Research has yet to fully explore postpartum BP screening in primary care settings. Instead, studies have assessed screening strategies in overall healthcare settings (e.g., specialist care) with no mention of primary care specifically.

Therefore, we conducted this review to investigate innovative postpartum BP screening methods in primary care settings, including their implementation strategies, and potential differences by patients’ demographic and clinical characteristics.

METHODS

Protocol

Our systematic review was structured according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses checklist.14 We used the Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome (PICO) processes to develop the research questions, literature search strategies (Table 1), and study inclusion and exclusion criteria (Table 2). The review did not develop or publish a study protocol.

Search strategy

We queried the PubMed and Web of Science (WES) databases to identify eligible studies using the search terms provided in Table 1. The first group of database searches were conducted on 20 May 2024. The searches were limited to studies conducted in the US and English-language publications. Furthermore, to ensure representation with current guidelines and maternity care practices, we restricted our searches to those studies published after 2010.

Eligibility criteria

We excluded studies from the review if: (i) the intervention setting was a hospital; (ii) study design was retrospective; (iii) if study was conducted outside of the US or non-English language publication; and (iv) study was a commentary, review, or editorial (Table 2).

Study selection process

One author (E.A.) queried the PubMed and Web of Science databases to identify studies. Two authors (E.A., R.O.) independently screened the titles of studies retrieved from the database, with guidance from a third author (S.S.). Studies were excluded if the titles were not related to hypertension or cardiovascular health. The authors then met to review the results and address discrepancies. Abstract screening and full-text review then followed, using the exclusion and inclusion criteria in Table 2. We utilized the Catchii online program to manage the entire screening process.15

Data collection

We extracted the following items from studies meeting the inclusion criteria: author and year, location (state), study design, study aims, study settings, postpartum BP screening intervention, first reported postpartum BP screening, total postpartum timing, implementation clinician, correlative health factors, participants demographic characteristics, study inclusion and exclusion criteria, and study outcomes.

Risk of bias assessment

Two authors (EA and RO) used the Cochrane risk of bias tools to assess the quality of the randomized (RCT) and non-randomized control studies (non-RCT; SS-4).16

RESULTS

Study selection

We identified 13,452 articles from the PubMed (n = 7,827) and WES (n = 5,625) databases (Figure 1). We then removed 3,501 duplicates, leaving 9,951 records for screening. A combined title and abstract screening removed 9,014 records. Then, we conducted a full-text screening for the remaining 937 studies. We excluded 893 records for the following reasons: study not discussing BP screening in postpartum (n = 171), retrospective cohort studies (n = 120), systematic review (n = 28), international studies (n = 448), studies not focusing on mothers in postpartum (n = 32), studies with no indication of primary care settings (n = 42), additional duplicates (n = 15), conference presentation abstracts (n = 15), animal studies (n = 3), qualitative studies (n = 4), studies that have been retracted from publication (n = 2), case studies (n = 2), and studies published before 2010 (n = 13). Overall, 32 studies were synthesized, consisting of 31 studies after exclusions and one study retrieved that was published in 2025.

Risk of bias assessments

The review included 12 RCTs and 20 non-RCTs. Of the RCTs, four studies were judged to be of “some concern” due to: (i) a lack of information on the randomization process and concealment protocol in the allocation sequence and, (ii) higher likelihood that awareness of the intervention may have impacted assessment of the intervention outcome (Supplementary Material SS-3).

Of the non-RCTs, two studies were judged to be “moderate risk” due to the concerns that some aspects of the study outcomes (e.g., change in behavior) were likely influenced by knowledge of the intervention and were not controlled for. Eleven studies were considered “serious risk” of bias due to concerns that all study outcomes were likely influenced by participants’ knowledge of the intervention.

Population cohort

The included studies recruited a total of 11,270 women, with a mean age of 31.1 years (Table 3). Of these, 35.8% (n = 4,032) reported at least one prior pregnancy. Patients primarily identified as Black/African American race (40.0%; n = 4,790), followed by 33.8% (n = 2,843) White race, and 23.0% (n = 1,932) Hispanic/Latino ethnicity. Asian (3.2%; n = 272) and American Indian and Alaskan Native (n = 2) individuals were least represented.

Study characteristics

Overall, the review identified two study groups: those that completed BP screening “within 1-week” postpartum (n = 17) and those that reported a time frame “greater than 1-week” postpartum (n = 15; Table 3).

Of the 11,270 patients recruited, 65.0% (n = 7,327) completed BP screening within 1-week postpartum. Compared to the “greater than 1-week” group, patients most represented in the within 1-week postpartum group had prior pregnancies (59.4% vs. 40.6%; P = 0.5102), were often Asian race (72.1% vs. 27.9%; P = 0.2809), included among studies recruiting high-risk patients (62.5% vs. 37.5%; P = 0.2060), non-RCTs (65.0% vs. 35.0%; P = 0.0822), primarily focused on pharmacological (66.7% vs. 33.3%) or screening feasibility interventions (60.9% vs. 39.1%; P = 0.1115), leveraged in-home screening settings (80.0% vs. 20.0%; P = 0.0779).

Risk-based recruitment criteria

Studies varied in their population of interest. Of the included studies, 24 (75.0%) recruited “high-risk” women only, 5 (15.6%) studies directly compared “high- and low-risk” women, and 3 (9.4%) studies recruited “all women” with no recruitment restrictions.

Study aims

We observed that 71.8% (n = 23) of studies aimed to assess BP screening feasibility (Table 3, Supplementary Materials SS-1 and SS-2). For example, Burgess et al. implemented a mobile health intervention to improve postpartum BP monitoring.17 Conversely, 6 (18.8%) studies focused on health risk assessments and investigated health changes in the postpartum period. To illustrate, Rowan et al. explored the impact of physical activity on heart rate variability.18

Three (9.4%) studies focused on pharmacological interventions, investigating the effectiveness of medications, medication titration, or therapeutic procedures on postpartum BP control. For example, Fishel Bartal et al. compared the effectiveness of oral hydrochlorothiazide and lisinopril therapy with nifedipine in reducing postpartum BP.19

Postpartum BP screening strategy

Studies completed postpartum BP screening in a clinical setting (31.2%; n = 10), in-home (31.2%; n = 10), or combining both clinical and home settings (37.5%; n = 12) (Table 3, Supplementary Materials SS-1 and SS-2). Compared to studies with BP ascertainment more than 1-week postpartum, studies that reported within 1 week were most often in-home study settings (80.0% vs. 20.0%; P = 0.7120). Further, three in-clinic studies which all reported BP screening within 1 week specifically leveraged child-wellness visits.20–22

DISCUSSION

We synthesized 32 articles to identify primary care strategies for hypertension screening in the postpartum period. There were three key findings: First, studies recruiting high-risk patients more often involved earlier postpartum BP screenings than those recruiting low-risk patients. Second, patients who identified as Asian or American Indian/Alaskan Natives, were least represented in the postpartum BP management interventions. Third, interventions utilized in in-home settings were generally associated with earlier postpartum BP screenings than in-clinic settings alone.

Screening in high-risk vs. lower-risk patients

Studies focusing on high-risk patients—defined as those with an existing diagnosis of HDP—reported earlier postpartum BP evaluation than those that included low-risk patients. This is not surprising given that patients with preexisting HDP are generally at higher risk of postpartum complications, requiring more comprehensive and stringent BP evaluation protocols. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, for example, recommends BP screening within 72-hours postpartum for patients with severe hypertension.23

These findings highlight a critical gap that early BP screening guidelines are less clearly defined for postpartum patients without additional health comorbidities. Existing recommendations continue to be tailored to and prioritize high-risk populations, even in primary care settings. This is particularly concerning in light of research reporting that at least one in 10 women with normotensive pregnancies will develop hypertension within 12-months postpartum.12 We advise the need for universal postpartum BP evaluation guidelines tailored for normotensive patients, including low-risk primiparous and multiparous patients. We also recommend continuous BP monitoring for these patients prior to hospital discharge, as well as, early scheduling of postpartum BP screening appointments.

Screening differences across race and ethnic groups

Our review identified limited evidence of race and ethnic disparities in the early initiation of postpartum BP screenings in primary care. Except for patients identifying as American Indian/Alaskan Natives, we found that most of the study cohorts were in the within 1-week group, regardless of their race or ethnic identity. It is unclear why our review contrasts with prior research, which reports inadequate postpartum BP checkup encounters among racial and ethnic minority populations in primary care.24,25

A possible explanation is that studies specifically targeted the underrepresented racial and ethnic minority patients because they are perceived as less likely to complete timely postpartum BP evaluation. Although it is widely accepted that disparities exist in postpartum BP evaluation, further research is needed to explore drivers of racial and ethnic differences in the timing of postpartum BP evaluation, particularly after accounting for the social determinants of health, such as income, education, insurance coverage, and neighborhood deprivation.

Furthermore, it is noteworthy that patients with limited English proficiency, limited access to a cellular phone, and those identifying as American Indian/Alaskan Native were least represented in the interventions.

Most studies explicitly excluded non-English speakers from participation. This is alarming given that more than 25 million individuals in the United States are unable to read, write, or understand English.26 Importantly, data is scarce on the prevalence of currently pregnant or postpartum patients in the United States with limited English proficiency. Research, however, has consistently demonstrated that these populations are at increased risk of adverse maternal and infant outcomes.27 Thus, their exclusion from health-promoting research serves only to exacerbate those outcomes.

Furthermore, despite having one of the highest prevalence of HDP (16.4%) and twice as likely to die from pregnancy-related causes than their White non-Hispanic counterparts, American Indian/Alaskan Native patients continue to be less involved in maternal health research.28,29 Factors contributing to this disparity include inadequate access to maternity care services, historical trauma, impaired health literacy, and limited research funding streams.29 Consequently, we call for a new collaborative approach to better connect health systems, legislative bodies, and academic stakeholders to advocate for and intensify efforts to address the persistent maternal health burden in this population.

Screening in-home vs. in clinical settings

In-home intervention settings were generally associated with earlier reported postpartum BP screening compared to clinical settings alone. Primary care physicians leveraged at-home remote BP monitoring via telehealth, text messaging, phone calls, and fitness activity trackers to encourage continuous and consistent postpartum BP screening. This finding is not surprising, as remote BP monitoring is often regarded as a key strategy for hypertension management in the postpartum period.30 The adoption of remote monitoring is driven by the need to increase the convenience of care, reduce healthcare costs for patients, reduce the burden of post-discharge encounters, and improve early detection of post-pregnancy heart health complications.9,31

Home-BP monitoring is also highly accepted by postpartum patients. In fact, most home-BP screening feasibility studies in our review demonstrated successful postpartum BP ascertainment behaviors. For example, the Janssen et al. study, which evaluated an integrated text messaging and remote BP intervention, had a 96.5% (n = 192) participant engagement rate.32 Similarly, the Hoppe et al. telehealth integrated study showed an 86% (n = 39) participant preference for remote monitoring.33 Interestingly, the studies reviewed did not describe cost of implementation and reimbursement strategies. Presumably, integrating a home visiting program would require funding to support the required staff and equipment provided to patients. Similarly in home monitoring programs, the information needs to be reviewed in a timely manner and providers must be reimbursed for their expertise in reviewing data and managing postpartum BP abnormalities.

Regarding clinical settings, our study found evidence of leveraging well-child checks as an important postpartum BP screening strategy in primary care. Well-child checks—which typically occur a few days after hospital discharge, and again at frequent intervals though the first months—provide an opportunity for comprehensive evaluation of new mothers and their babies.34 However, current guidelines make no mention of maternal BP ascertainment during those visits. Integrating maternal BP screening as part of the well-child comprehensive care program reduces patient and physician burdens of having to schedule additional appointments for BP evaluation. More research is needed in this area, particularly, on the best practices for integrating maternal BP screening with well-child assessment protocols.

Strengths and limitations

The present review adds to the body of knowledge on postpartum BP screening strategies. To our knowledge, it is the first study focusing on primary care settings specifically. Our results revealed the need for early BP screening guidelines for normotensive postpartum patients, and additional research on disparities that may exist in the timing of postpartum BP ascertainment. These findings provide critical insights for future clinical work on improving postpartum BP screening in primary care.

A limitation of our study is the inclusion of all study designs and not restricting to RCTs. While the synthesis of RCTs and non-RCTs provided depth in understanding BP screening in primary care settings, it was challenging to assess overall intervention effectiveness, due to variations in reported outcomes and study aims. Furthermore, generalizability may be limited given our focus on primary care settings in the United States. Notably, our study observations may not apply to other healthcare settings like private or public-funded health systems or international health institutions.

Additionally, we described our results based on apparent differences observed between study groups. A further analysis, however, showed no statistically significant differences in any of the relationships we explored. It is possible that our sample sizes were too small to generate an associative effect. It is also possible that the cohort frequencies between study groups were too close to show any meaningful comparisons. Future studies with a larger population size will be needed to validate the findings.

Finally, we reported postpartum BP ascertainment timing based on what are described in the included studies. It is possible that BP screenings may have taken place with study participants as part of routine care and occurred earlier or later than stated in the studies. Thus, there is a risk that we are understating or overstating these strategies, since the reported timing may differ from those used in clinical practice outside of study contexts.

CONCLUSION

We found that primary care clinics are evaluating BP within 1-week postpartum and leveraging home monitoring and child-wellness checks. Those strategies, however, less often included low-risk patients. Evidence suggests the need to further expand maternal BP screening to well-child checks, given the lack of adequate postpartum visits. Updated guidelines are urgently needed to cover postpartum BP screening for patients with uncomplicated pregnancies and the absence of other risk factors.
