Dermatophytic Infections of the Eyebrows Due To Nannizzia Gypsea: A Case Series
Authors: Vincenzina Rubino, Elisa Margherita Difonzo, Luigi Pisano
Published: 2026

Introduction

Dermatophytic infection of the eyebrows is an uncommon condition, typically occurring secondary to scalp or facial infections and only rarely presenting as an isolated condition [1]. It typically presents as an erythematous-desquamative plaque with scaly crusts and partial or complete alopecia of the eyebrows [1]. The inflammatory response may be intense, with pustulation, potentially evolving into Kerion Celsi of the eyebrow region [2]. Due to its rarity, it is frequently misdiagnosed and treated inappropriately, resulting in prolonged ineffective therapies. Nannizzia gypsea (formerly Microsporum gypseum) is a geophilic dermatophyte with worldwide distribution, typically found in soil rich in keratinous debris [4]. Human infection is rare and usually follows contact with contaminated soil or animals, which can be asymptomatic carriers [3]. Eyebrow tinea caused by N. gypsea is exceptionally rare. Herein, we report three cases of this condition successfully treated with oral terbinafine and topical bifonazole.

Case Presentation

A 75-year-old female, living on a farm, presented with a four-month history of a lesion on the eyebrow, previously treated as pyoderma with topical antibiotics without improvement. Examination revealed an erythematous plaque with yellowish scaly crusts and near-complete eyebrow hair loss (Figure 1A).

Physical examination of the three patients: A) Erythematous lesion with clusters of yellowish, scaly crusts and near-complete loss of hairs; B) Erythematous lesion with follicular pustules, punctiform erosions, hairs loss, and few broken hairs; C) Raised, intensely erythematous and alopecic lesion accompanied by exudative erosions and scaly crusts; D) Clinical healing, in the patient from Case 3, after six weeks of oral terbinafine and topical bifonazole therapy.

A 65-year-old female, living with semiferal cats, presented with a two-month history of an erythematous lesion on the eyebrow characterized by follicular pustules, erosions, and broken hairs (Figure 1B). Previous treatment with topical corticosteroids for presumed insect bite reaction resulted in clinical worsening.

A 41-year-old gardener presented with a lesion on the eyebrow, appearing two months earlier after contact with chrysanthemum flowers, initially misdiagnosed as allergic contact dermatitis unresponsive to topical corticosteroids. On examination, there was a well-demarcated, oval alopecic plaque with intense erythema, erosions, and scaly crusts surrounding a few remaining hairs (Figure 1C). Due to suspicion of eyebrow tinea, mycological investigations were performed in all cases. Microscopic examination showed ectothrix invasion with abundant hyphae inside the hair shaft (Figure 2A). Skin scrapings revealed numerous hyphae. Cultures from hairs and scales on Sabouraud’s glucose agar produced powdery colonies of sand-colored surface with irregular margins (Figure 2B). The reverse side of the colonies was of yellowish-buff color (Figure 2C).

Mycological investigations: A) Microscopic examination of eyebrow: fungal spores outside the hair shaft; B) Culture from hair: powdery colonies with a sand-colored surface, irregular margins, and a woolly surface at the periphery; C) reverse side of the colonies: yellowish-buff color; D) Microscopic examination of cultures: fusiform macroconidia made up of four to six cells with rounded extremities.

Microscopic examination revealed fusiform, thin-walled macroconidia with rounded extremities divided into 4–6 cells, which are typical of N. gypsea (Figure 2D) [4].

All patients received oral terbinafine with topical bifonazole for six weeks, achieving complete lesion resolution, full eyebrow regrowth, and no recurrence at three months (Figure 1D).

Conclusions

Eyebrow dermatophytosis is an uncommon and often misdiagnosed condition that may mimic other inflammatory dermatoses, leading to delayed diagnosis or ineffective treatment. Inflammatory cases caused by N. gypsea can present with intense erythema, pustules, and rapid follicular involvement. Careful evaluation of environmental and occupational exposures, together with mycological examination, is essential for accurate diagnosis. Although griseofulvin remains the treatment of choice for Microsporum species [5,6], terbinafine proved effective in our cases, offering shorter treatment courses and better patient adherence.

Further studies are needed to optimize treatment regimens and evaluate its broader applicability.
