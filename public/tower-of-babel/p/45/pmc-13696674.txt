Rise and subsequent fall in neuro-behavioral coupling during learning a skilled reaching task is revealed by generative AI
Authors: Sean Tanabe, Michael Eckert, Hardeep Ryait, Ian Q. Whishaw, Masami Tatsuno, Artur Luczak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding complex relations between neuronal activity and animal behavior is central question in neuroscience. Rapid advancements in Artificial Intelligence (AI) methods offer powerful tools to investigate highly non-linear mapping between motor cortex activity and body movements. Here, we developed a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) that showed that detailed videos of behaving rats can be recreated from activity of just few selected neurons. This analysis also revealed that the predictability of behavior from neuronal activity (and vice versa) initially increases as a rat learns a new task. However, after the animal performance on the motor task achieves the required accuracy, then coupling between neuronal activity and behavior decreases, without degrading task performance. A plausible interpretation is that, as training progresses from Early to Mid training days, more neurons become engaged, forming a denser, broadly distributed representation, which then in the Late training days evolves into a sparse and more energy-efficient representation, with only a small subset of tuned neurons. Neuronal network simulations showed that such changes in coding strategy may be explained by neurons minimizing their energy use. Thus, our approach reveals a non-linear relationship between learning stages and neural-behavioral coupling, which is likely driven by energy efficiency.

Introduction

The motor cortex is organized into a “motor map” in which specific areas control the movement of body parts, as exemplified by Penfield’s motor homunculus1. Nevertheless, the way in which the motor cortex produces functional behavior remains debated2. Early experiments variously demonstrated that electrical stimulation of map regions could activate individual muscles or discrete movements of body parts, whereas more recent findings indicate that prolonged stimulation can evoke complex actions, such as walking, reaching, or bringing a hand to the mouth3–5. Complicating matters, studies especially on primates show that motor cortex regions active during movement are also implicated in other functions, including planning6, perception (e.g., mirror neurons7), and sensory integration8. To further investigate the function of neuronal activity in the motor cortex, we here employ new Machine Learning (ML) tools capable of complex input-output mapping. We suggest that this tool is useful for studying highly nonlinear relations between neuronal activity and behavior. Our motivation is to advance the understanding of skilled motor learning by moving beyond the assumption of predefined limb markers. The application of this advanced, data-driven machine-learning method allowed us to reconstruct behavioral videos directly from neural activity and then predict neuronal firing patterns from raw videos, revealing new insights into the dynamics of motor cortex contributions to skilled reach learning.

In this study, we apply Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) and Convolutional Neural Networks (ConvNets) to investigate the relationship between behavior and motor cortex single-cell activity. GANs produce synthetic data that closely mirrors real data by leveraging the competition between two neural networks: the Generator, which replicates the true data distribution, and the Discriminator, a binary classifier that differentiates between real and fake samples9. GANs, known for their flexibility and efficacy, have seen widespread application beyond image processing and computer vision, extending into healthcare, biology, astronomy, remote sensing, material science, and finance10. Conditional GANs (cGANs), which incorporate additional information, yield more realistic and diverse outputs suited to specific conditions, enhancing their applicability in areas such as image translation, text-to-image synthesis, and super-resolution11. Previous studies have also applied GANs to neural decoding, reconstructing natural images12,13 and decoding of faces14 from fMRI activity. This paper builds on that work by applying GANs to create behavioral videos generated from neuronal spiking activity.

ConvNet has revolutionized image recognition by extracting image features in a data-driven way15,16. Despite these advancements, interpreting what features the neural network model uses for predictions remains challenging. The Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME) model tries to provide an interpretable explanation of which parts of the image were most informative for model decisions17. This study uses ConvNets and the LIME explain model to decode movements associated with neuronal activity. This presents an innovative method for extracting receptive fields, thus enhancing our understanding of neural mechanisms underlying motor movements.

Here, we use raw videos for analyses, and the ML algorithms are allowed to explore which movement components are the most related to neuronal activity and vice versa. We demonstrated that this data-driven approach results in discovering how the relation between motor cortex activity and animal behavior changes as a skilled reaching task is learned.

Results

To investigate the relation between behavior and neuronal activity, we used data recorded from four rats learning a skilled reaching task for a food pellet (“Methods”)18. Predicting neural activity from behavioral videos

To investigate the relation between skilled reaching and neuronal activity, we first asked how well neuronal firing patterns can be predicted from videos of skilled reaching. Each video frame was processed by ConvNet to extract 2048 image features (Fig. 2A). For computational efficiency, we restricted analyses to 150 × 150 pixel window (denoted with a red rectangle in Fig. 1A). Next, we trained a shallow neural network to predict from the extracted features the activity of individual neurons (“Methods”). For those analyses, the spiking activity of each neuron was smoothed with a Gaussian kernel (σ = 100 ms), which is typical for relating neuronal activity to movement trajectories (e.g., ref. 23). From each recording day, we had 6–51 neurons with firing rate above 1 Hz (median 20 neurons) per animal. To quantify the similarity between actual and predicted activity patterns, we used Euclidean distance. For statistical consistency, we used only six neurons per rat/day, since this was the smallest number of neurons recorded in a single session. The distribution of distances during each behavioral training phase is shown in Fig. 2C. For comparison, we also calculated distances between the predicted activity of one neuron and the actual activity of a different neuron during the same trial. The distribution of distances for shuffled data has a significantly larger median distance as compared to original data (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Early/Mid/Late all P < 0.0001, the median is 1.30/1.08/1.23 AU, N = 660/720/660; to provide intuition about this distance metric, in Fig. 2B the distance between actual and predicted activity is 0.71 AU for Rat 1 and 0.67 AU for Rat 2, whereas a flat-zero prediction (y = 0) would give much larger distances of 2.47 AU and 2.45 AU, respectively; similarly, the distance between actual and an averaged across session predicted activity would be larger: 1.38 AU and 1.54 AU, respectively).

Interestingly, we observed that the distance values were the lowest during the mid-training days, a finding consistently observed in the results of all four rats (Fig. 2D, Friedman’s test, χ² = 6.5, P = 0.0388). This indicates that the predictability of neuronal activity from behavior is not continuously increasing as animals are learning the task, but rather the predictability peaks in Mid days (see “Discussion” how this may suggest a change in allocation of neuronal resources during learning phases).

To ensure robustness of our neuronal prediction results, we conducted additional control analyses. Specifically, we predicted neural activity on trial t + 1 based on videos from the previous trial t. This allowed us to test whether our model can also capture trial-to-trial variability. This alternative null model is known as the linear shift method, which controls for slow trends in the data24. We used leave-two-adjacent-trials-out cross-validation. We found that predicting neuronal activity from trial-matched videos (as in Fig. 2) was significantly better than predictions from trial-shifted videos (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P < 0.0001, the median is −0.0811 AU, N = 3672; Fig. 3A). Note that the difference between trial-matched and trial-shifted predictions (Fig. 3A) is much smaller compared to random control (Fig. 2C). This is because neuronal activities are similar to each other on consecutive trials, thus reducing the difference between distributions. Moreover, similarly to Fig. 2D, we found that trial-matched data showed the highest predictability between neuronal activity and behavior was in the Mid-training phase (Friedman’s test, χ² = 6.5, P = 0.0388; Fig. 3B). The same analyses were repeated for trial-shifted data, which also showed a significant V-shape: P = 0.0388 (additional control analyses are also presented in Supplementary Figs. 2–4). Having a model predicting neuronal activity from animal behavior can also be used to study how the activity of the motor cortical neurons is related to the movement components of skilled reaching. Specifically, we can apply “explain” methods to our model to find which parts of the video frames were the most important for predicting the activity of each neuron. This is similar to deriving a receptive field of a neuron. However, the method applied here allows for finding highly nonlinear relations between video frames and neuronal activity. Contrary to our expectations, obtained “receptive fields” were difficult to interpret as they showed that many parts of the image were relevant for predicting neuronal activity (Supplementary Fig. 5). This may mean that cortical motor activity is affected by diverse factors like sensory inputs25, cognitive processes26, or brain state27,28, thus, our model could be using global features (like body posture or evaluating arousal level) to better predict activity of a single cortical neuron. This result is consistent with modern machine-learning findings showing that a single artificial neuron often responds to many different features rather than one single feature29; see also discussion in Supplementary Fig. 5).

Reconstructing behavioral videos from neural activity

As a proof-of-concept, we also demonstrate how videos of a behaving animal can be reconstructed from neuronal activity. Advancements in AI allow users to generate images based on text prompts11. Here, we are using a similar approach, where instead of text, a neuronal activity is given as a “prompt” to generate a video frame. A conditional Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) was trained to reproduce video frames from smoothed neuronal activity (Fig. 4A, “Methods”). Sample frames from original and generated videos are shown in Fig. 4B and Supplementary Figs. 6. To focus on detailed movements of hands and nose, in the following analyses, we used GAN to reconstruct only the central part of frames as illustrated in Fig. 5A, Supplementary Fig. 7, and in sample videos provided at the GitHub repository (see Data availability section). Interestingly, we found that we could generate good-quality videos of skilled reaching based on the neural activity of only a few neurons, which had activity most predictive of videos (see analyses in Fig. 2A, B). To quantify the accuracy of reconstructed movements, we compared trajectories of selected body parts in the videos. We used the DeepLabCut neural network30, which was trained to track the position of the hands (forepaws) and nose (Supplementary Fig. 7; “Methods”). The sample trajectory of the right paw is superimposed on Fig. 5A, and it is illustrated in more detail in Fig. 5B, which shows how the x and y positions of the right hand changed in time in the actual and generated videos. To quantify the similarity between actual and generated trajectories, we calculated the Euclidean distance between trajectories in image pixel space (Fig. 5C). For comparison, we also calculated the distance between actual and generated trajectories shuffled in time. This allowed us to estimate what would be expected for the distribution of distances if our model were to only learn to reproduce images, but without learning how frames are related to neuronal activity. We found that actual and generated trajectories were significantly more similar to each other than shuffled data. This effect was consistent across training phases (Fig. 5C, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Early/Mid/Late all P < 0.0001). We also compared our model to an alternative null model using the linear shift method24, which controls for slow trends in the data. This confirmed that our network indeed captures trial-to-trial variability in reconstructed videos (Supplementary Fig. 8, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P < 0.0001, the median is −1.4910, N = 272). Consistent with analyses in Fig. 2D, we found that for all rats the distance values were the lowest during the Mid-training days (Fig. 5D, Friedman’s test, χ² = 6, P = 0.0498, and Supplementary Fig. 9), indicating the strongest relation between behavior and neuronal activity patterns during that learning phase.

As a control analysis, to test if our cGAN can capture trial-to-trial variability, we reconstructed video frames on trial t + 1 from neuronal activity on previous trial t (Fig. 5E; analogously to the linear shift method described in Fig. 3). We used leave-two-adjacent-trials-out cross-validation. The histogram in Fig. 5E illustrates that distances for trial-matched videos were significantly lower (better) than distances calculated from trial shifted-videos (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P < 0.0001, the median is −1.4910 AU, N = 272; see Supplementary Fig. 8 for more details). The difference between trial-matched and trial-shifted predictions in Fig. 5E is much smaller compared to random control (Fig. 5C), which is a result of high similarity between movement trajectories on consecutive trials (Supplementary Fig. 11). Moreover, the same type of plot as in Fig. 5D was calculated from trial-matched data from the analyses above (Fig. 5F). Consistent with Fig. 5D, it shows that the highest predictability between neuronal activity and behavior was in the Mid-training phase (Friedman’s test, χ² = 6, P = 0.0498). For trial-shifted data, the results were not significant: P = 0.1738. Thus, the fact that the trial-matched model outperformed the trial-shifted control in all the panels presented here indicates that our model is predicting trial-specific movement variability, rather than merely reproducing the average trajectory.

To investigate neuronal reorganization during learning in more detail, we conducted neural network simulations (“Methods”). Using a network with biologically plausible learning rule31,32, we found that the Early, Mid and Late phases of learning can be also observed in artificial networks (Fig. 6). Similarly, as in motor cortex, during learning a new task, initially the network develops dense coding (neurons participate in responses to multiple categories), and later, neurons specialize to respond to only selected categories (sparse coding). Interestingly, adding energy constraints, where neurons try to reduce their activity, results in more sparse coding in the Late phase, similar to that observed in the motor cortex data (Fig. 6E and Supplementary Fig. 12). Considering our results, we suggest the following plausible interpretation on how neuronal activity in the motor cortex reorganizes during learning: Early phase: Initially, the new skill is controlled by many neurons which are not well tuned to new movements. Thus, the activity of a random subset of those neurons has low predictability of the new behavior. Mid phase: As training advances, many neurons become tuned to the new movement (dense coding). Late phase: Neuronal representation becomes sparse with only a few neurons remaining tuned for the new behavior (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. 2). Consistent with this picture, population activity and hand trajectories showed the lowest variance during Mid days (Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11). This can be interpreted as the mid-phase requiring the most neuronal resources, and the Late phase the fewest neuronal resources.Fig. 7Schematic of how the neuronal representation of a motor skill may evolve over training.Left: During Early training days, when the animal’s task accuracy is low, many neurons show only moderate involvement in the movement. Discussion

Here, we developed novel AI methods capable of analyzing a highly nonlinear relationship between neuronal activity and animal behavior. This led to three main advancements: (1) We show that neuronal activity can be predicted directly from raw videos of animal movement. This removes human assumptions about which body parts are the most relevant for predicting neuronal activity and allows for fully automated and data-driven analysis. (2) We show for the first time that detailed videos of behaving animals can be recreated from the activity of just a few selected neurons. Using GANs, we predicted the position of multiple body parts (two forepaws and nose) in video frames from only 6 neurons. This finding has important implications for brain-computer interface (BCI) technologies as it reveals that far more information about movements can be extracted from neuronal activity than previously assumed. (3) Our AI tools allowed us to discover that as learning progressed, initially, a predictability between cortical activity and behavior increases; however, surprisingly, later, as an animal becomes proficient on a new task, this predictability decreases. Using neuronal network simulations with a biological learning rule31, we showed that this change in predictability can be explained by neurons minimizing their energy use. A plausible interpretation of our findings is that initially motor learning involves recruiting a large number of neurons, and later, as a task is learned, neuronal representation becomes sparse and energy-efficient.

Consequently, our findings demonstrate that the predictability of neuronal activity from behavioral data and vice versa is highest during the middle phase of training. This finding is consistent with other work showing that there can be a nonlinear relationship between learning stages and neural-activity33. We suggest that as learning occurs, the predictability between neuronal activity and behavior initially increases, likely due to the increased demand for precise motor control and coordination. However, once the rats achieve proficiency in the new motor skill, this predictability decreases even though task performance remains stable. This later decoupling may indicate a shift in the neural resources or strategies employed by the motor cortex, possibly reflecting a transition from a highly engaged learning state to a more automated execution state34. It is interesting to note that the V-shape in neuro-behavioral coupling is also present in trial-shifted control analyses in Fig. 3B. This is consistent with results in Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11 showing reduced trial-to-trial variability, which most likely contributes to increased predictability during Mid-training days.

Microelectrode electrical stimulation of the neocortex shows that the laboratory rat has a large region of frontal neocortex that can be defined as motor cortex, and within this region the forelimb area comprises its largest part35–37. The neurons in layer V of the rat forelimb area of motor cortex project to a number of brain targets as well as to the spinal cord. Within the spinal cord motor pools, they synapse with premotor neurons but not with motor neurons, as occurs in anthropoid primates38. Despite this difference in connectivity, the rat uses its forelimb for a wide variety of skilled motor behaviors, including reaching for food items with a hand for placement in the mouth for eating. These reaching movements are motorically similar to those used by anthropoid primates, including humans39,40. Both electrophysiological and anatomical evidence suggest that the motor cortex is involved in both the learning and performance of skilled reaching41,42. To further investigate the contribution of the motor cortex neurons to skilled reaching for food, we employed ML tools capable of complex input– output mappings that are useful for studying highly nonlinear relations between neuronal activity and behavior.

The use of GANs in the context of skilled motor learning is particularly noteworthy as it marks the first instance of behavioral videos being reconstructed from motor cortical activity that is presumably involved in skilled learning. This innovative application of GANs, typically used in image synthesis and enhancement, underscores their potential for generating contextually relevant synthetic outputs based on neural data. The ability to create accurate behavioral reconstructions from sparse neural recordings opens new avenues for understanding and interpreting motor cortex function.

We suggest that these results and insights have implications for the development of brain-machine interfaces (BMIs) such as those aimed at restoring movement in individuals with motor impairments. Our findings challenge the assumption that motor cortical signals remain stable over extended periods, highlighting the need for adaptive BMI systems that can accommodate changes in neural-behavioral predictability over time33.

Limitations

It’s important to acknowledge that due to the complexity of electrophysiological experiments, we had only a small number of animals (4). Thus, it could be difficult to show a change in neural representation convincingly based on data from this study alone. Nevertheless, our finding of a V-shape change in neural coding and behavioral predictability is consistent with results from other labs. For example, Peters et al.43 show that during motor learning, the fraction of movement-related neurons first increases and then decreases across later sessions. This was accompanied by a transient increase in spine density during middle sessions and followed by pruning/sparsening. Similarly, training on a visuomotor task sparsens and orthogonalizes cortical responses as the animal becomes proficient44. Finally, Hwang et al.34 reported that M1 population activity becomes less variable from early to middle learning sessions but then becomes more variable again from middle to late stages, similarly as observed in our data (Supplementary Fig. 10). Taken together, these studies provide independent support for the present results of a rise and fall in neuronal–behavioral predictability with sparsification of the neural code at the latest stage of training.

This sparsification raises the question: if in the Late phase there are fewer neurons in the motor cortex predictive of behavior, then how can it be explained that animal task performance is the best in this Late phase? In our data, we could not find a clear answer to this question. However, it has been suggested that at the expert level (i.e., in the Late phase), motor control may be transferred from M1 to subcortical motor structures34,45.

Another limitation of the present study is the inherent technical challenge of tracking individual neurons with electrophysiological recordings across multiple days. Therefore, it is likely that the neural populations recorded on successive days were not identical, which could introduce variability when interpreting longitudinal changes in neural activity during learning. We also acknowledge that our neural network simulation in Fig. 6 is a significant abstraction of the complex sensorimotor processes involved in skilled reaching. However, our findings are consistent with much more complex and realistic neural network models that showed that adding regularization constraints (similar to metabolic energy constraints) leads to increased sparsity46,47. Interestingly, such regularization also helps to prevent overfitting and improve network robustness and generalization48. Therefore, while the model itself is a simplification, it serves to demonstrate that energy constraints could explain code sparsening in the later phase of learning and “decoupling” between neuronal activity and behavior as observed in the motor cortex in Figs. 2D and 5D.

There are also other neuronal network models that predict neuronal activity from behavior49,50. In contrast to our method, those models require a pre-processing step to mark selected body parts, which can be subjective. Previous work has shown that using full videos could result in better detection of behavioral deficits (e.g., movement inaccuracies in animals with brain stroke) as compared to using only selected body markers20. However, if the most relevant body parts are correctly selected, methods with virtual body markers could be more efficient and accurate, due to the lower dimensionality of input data. To compare these approaches to our method, we trained a neural network to predict neuronal activity using only the coordinates of tracked body parts. We found that this marker-based model performed slightly better than our full-frame ConvNet approach (Supplementary Fig. 4). This suggests that our method can offer a comparable alternative for predicting neural activity directly from raw video, without the need for pre-selecting limb markers.

Five adult male Fisher–Brown Norway rats (Rattus norvegicus), aged between four and nine months and weighing 350–450 g, were used. The rats, housed under a reverse light cycle, underwent experiments during their dark cycle. They had continuous access to food and water, except during reaching task training, when their diet was limited to 85% of their body weight. After initial hand preference tests, electrodes were implanted in the primary motor cortex’s forelimb region, contralateral to the preferred paw (1.0 mm anterior, 2.5 mm lateral to bregma). Electrode tip locations were confirmed with Nissl staining to be in deep layers22. Post-surgery, rats received 3 days of analgesics (Metacam) and 5 days of antibiotics (Baytril), with a 1-week recovery before recording.

Recording and behavioral training

For electrophysiological recordings, we used digital Cheetah SX data acquisition software (Neuralynx, Bozeman, Montana). Signals were bandpass filtered (600–6000 Hz) and sampled at 32 kHz. A reference electrode in the drive was placed in the white matter below the cortex. Tetrodes were gradually lowered during a habituation period of one to two weeks to deep layers of the motor cortex. Adjustments were made during the training period to optimize cell yield.

The single pellet reaching task requires an animal to use one hand to reach for, grasp, and bring a single food item to its mouth, similar to a common behavior in both nonhuman primates and humans18. The task uses rectangular, clear Plexiglas boxes that allow filming from any angle. Their open design enables the animal to approach and leave the food location freely, choose a hand for reaching, and exhibit adaptive behavioral changes18,51,52. The single-pellet reaching task used a box with a 1.5 cm slot from which the rat retrieved a 45 mg sugar pellet (Bio-Serv, Frenchtown, NJ, USA), placed in a well 1.5 cm from the slot on a 3 cm high shelf.

High-speed infrared cameras recorded the task at 200 fps (Prosilica GigE). Habituation involved closed shelf doors, with the rat occasionally finding pellets on the floor. Rats, naive to skill training, underwent reach training with 60 trials per session, continuing until the asymptotic performance was achieved for three consecutive days (less than 5% increase in performance across 3 days). A trial was deemed successful if the rat grabbed, retrieved, and ate the pellet. All other outcomes, like misses or drops, were classified as unsuccessful.

Spikes were sorted using automated clustering53 and manually refined (MClust). Sorted unit quality was evaluated based on inter-spike interval histogram, cross-correlation with other units, waveform shape consistency, firing rate patterns, and L-ratio22. In our analyses, neurons with mean firing rates below 1 Hz were excluded, resulting in a median of 20 neurons per rat per day (range: 6–51). One of the five rats was excluded due to insufficient firing rates.

Predicting cortical activity from behavioral video

Our objective was to reconstruct motor cortical activity from behavioral videos using a pre-trained InceptionV3 deep convolutional neural network (ConvNet)54. This ConvNet has 25 million parameters, including 9 convolutional layers, 2 pooling layers, and a linear layer. It was originally trained on the ImageNet dataset, which includes images from 1000 classes such as “dog,” “car,” and “make-up”. We used this network because it previously demonstrated a strong performance on classifying videos of animals with neurological deficits20,55. This ConvNet, without any finetuning for our dataset, was applied to extract 2048 high-level visual features from each video frame, after it was converted to grayscale. We focused on reconstructing cortical neuron activity from −400 ms to 600 ms around reach initiation, in 50 ms increments, applying a Gaussian filter (500 ms length, 2.5 alpha). We used a 50 ms frame rate and this Gaussian kernel for smoothing the neural data, primarily due to computational efficiency, balancing temporal detail with processing demands. For each neuron (median 20 per rat/session, range 6–51; neurons with firing rates below 1 Hz excluded), a single-unit fully connected layer was trained on 2048 features extracted by ConvNet to predict the neuron’s activity. Training employed a leave-one-out cross-validation on 10 reach trials, thus for every session, 10 models were trained for each neuron. The single model for a single neuron was trained for 400 epochs, which took about 1 min on a single PC with Intel Core i7 11700 F processor and Nvidia GeForce GTX1650 GPU. The model was optimized with the Adam optimizer (learning rate: 0.001) using Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss.

Additionally, the LIME explain model was used to analyze the receptive field of neural activity as predicted by ConvNet on the cross-validation testing data. The LIME explanation model simplifies understanding neural network predictions, like ConvNet, by altering inputs slightly and observing the impact on the network’s predictions17. This involves making small changes to the data that the network processes, and then comparing these adjusted predictions to the original ones. The difference between the new prediction and the original, the explained value, tells us how important that specific piece of information was for the prediction. Through this comparison, the model learns about the network’s behavior. To quicken this learning process, we modified specific segments of the input, such as altering a 4 × 4 pixel section within a larger 150 × 150 pixel frame.

Reconstruction of behavioral video from cortical activity

We used a Conditional Generative Adversarial Network (cGAN)11 for synthesizing rat reach images from neural activity. The cGAN uses a generator and a discriminator. The generator input includes a 100-dimensional latent noise vector and a conditioning vector composed of normalized activity of six neurons (the same neurons used for ConvNet statistics). The network features four convolutional transpose layers. These layers upsample features from an initial 8×8 spatial dimension to generate 128 × 128 pixel, 3-channel (RGB) images. Each convolutional transpose layer applies 128 filters and a (2,2) stride. Leaky ReLU activations (α = 0.2) are used. The final layer uses a tanh activation. The discriminator receives either a 128 × 128 × 3 real image or a generated image, with the neural activity conditioning vector. The neural condition is processed via a dense layer and reshaped into a 128 × 128 × 1 spatial map, then concatenated with the image, forming a 128 × 128 × 4 input. The discriminator uses four convolutional layers, each with 128 filters and a (2,2) stride, downsampling features to 8 × 8. Leaky ReLU activations (α = 0.2) are applied. A dropout layer with a 0.4 rate is included. The output layer provides a single probability via a sigmoid activation. The generator consists of 19 layers, and the discriminator has 16 layers, for a combined total of 2,473,924 trainable parameters. Both the generator and discriminator models were trained from scratch and were not pre-trained. Training utilized the Adam optimizer (learning rate: 0.0002, β1: 0.5) for both the discriminator and the combined GAN model. We trained both models for 100 epochs with a 32-sample batch size, which took about 100 min for one rat/day. Both models were using a binary cross-entropy loss function.

To compare the accuracy of movement reconstruction, we applied the DeepLabCut neural network30 to track the position of selected body parts in actual and generated videos. Specifically, we traced the position of both hands and the nose (Supplementary Fig. 7). DeepLabCut combines object recognition and semantic segmentation, using pre-trained ResNets and deconvolutional layers. Specifically, it is a variation of ResNets trained on the ImageNet database that replaces its classification layer with deconvolutional layers to enhance visual information and generate spatial probability maps. These maps predict the likelihood of body part locations. For specific tasks, the network is fine-tuned with labeled images, adjusting its weights to accurately locate body parts. We threshold the position likelihood at 0.4 for the nose and hands. The DeepLabCut model was trained solely on actual video and then applied location prediction to both actual and GAN videos. This approach enabled us to quantify the precision of reconstructing behaviors from motor cortical activities by comparing the alignment of actual and GAN-produced limb positions.

Trial-shift control

The trial-shift (or linear shift) control is a more conservative null model as compared to the shuffling within-trial method used in Fig. 5C. However, the trial-shift result is more difficult to interpret as it also depends on the size of trial-to-trial variability. For example, if neuronal activity and hand trajectory on trial t and trial t + 1 would be identical, then a good model would predict the same trajectory in both: trial-matched and trial-shifted cases. This means that this shuffling control would show that trial-matched results are the same as trial-shifted (both distributions would completely overlap), showing no significant difference despite a perfect predictive model. This hypothetical scenario is not that far from the actual data, as neural activity and resulting reaching behavior are often highly stereotyped, meaning that adjacent trials (t and t + 1) are very similar (see provided videos). This also helps to explain why the difference between trial-matched and trial-shifted predictions (Figs. 3A and 5E) is much smaller as compared to random control (Figs. 2C and 5C). This is because activities themselves are similar to each other on consecutive trials, thus reducing the difference between distributions.

For trial-shifted analyses, we used leave-two-out cross-validation, where both excluded trials were used for calculating trial-matched prediction. Thus, there was a larger number of trials used in plots 3B and 5F as compared to Figs. 2D and 5D, where we used leave-one-out cross-validation repeated 10 times (10 trials out of n were used for each day/animal).

Neural network simulations

This network was previously described in refs. 31,32, and the code with all the details is provided below. Briefly, we created a recurrent neural network which had 420 input units, 50 hidden units, and 10 output units. Output units have recurrent (feedback) connections to the hidden layer (Fig. 6A). The network was trained on a handwritten digit recognition task MNIST56 (Fig. 6B). To reduce input signal dimensionality only 21 × 20 pixels from the center of each image were given as input to the network. First, only the input signal is presented to the network, and network activity settles into a steady-state pattern. During this unsupervised learning phase, each neuron learns to predict its steady-state activity level based on initial input. To make comparable analyses to actual data presented in Fig. 2D, where neuronal activity is predicted from behavior, we did the following: we assumed that the activity of output neurons is equivalent to a behavior; thus, we used the activity of output neurons to predict the activity of neurons in the hidden layer (representing the motor cortex). To account for the fact that in the original data we do not have full information about behavior (we only see the animal from one camera with limited information about movement details), thus we used only one output neuron to predict the rest of the neuronal activity. To compare how well the activity of a single hidden neuron can be predicted from output activity, we used a cosine distance between actual and predicted activity in response across 400 stimuli (distance = 0 means a perfect prediction). For visual consistency with other figures, we divided results into three learning periods: Early (learning epochs: 1–30), Mid (epochs 41–70), and Late (epochs 171–200). Statistics and reproducibility

All analyses were performed using custom Python and Matlab code, available at the repository listed below. Sample size: the in vivo experiments were conducted using n = 4 independent animals (adult male Fisher–Brown Norway rats). An initial cohort of 5 rats was surgically implanted, but one was subsequently excluded from all analyses due to insufficient neuronal firing rates (mean firing rates below 1 Hz). All procedures adhered to the Canadian Council for Animal Care and were approved by the University of Lethbridge Animal Welfare Committee. To ensure statistical consistency for varying cell yields across different animals and recording days, the neural prediction and video generation models utilized a fixed, matched sample of exactly 6 neurons per rat per session. Model reliability and reproducibility were validated through cross-validation. To evaluate changes across the three distinct training phases (Early, Mid, and Late), a non-parametric Friedman’s test was applied. For comparing actual data against chance-level shuffled data, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. For neural network simulations, learning curves display the average accuracy ± standard error of the mean (SEM) computed across ten independent simulations. Statistical significance was set at a threshold of P < 0.05.
