Efficient UAV object detection using spectro-spatial synergistic learning and implicit recursive refinement
Authors: Xiaoji Wei, Zhongbin Luo, Bilu Luo, JunHao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Object detection in UAV imagery faces severe challenges arising from drastic scale variations, pervasive background noise, and the dominance of tiny objects, further compounded by the computational constraints of edge devices. Existing lightweight detectors frequently suffer from feature degradation, in which low-frequency background information tends to submerge the high-frequency details of small objects. To overcome this limitation, we propose S3-Det, a novel Spectro-Spatial Synergistic Detector. Specifically, we design the Spectro-Spatial Synergistic Network (S3Net) as the backbone architecture. Subsequently, we design an Implicit Recursive Feature Aggregator to enhance feature semantics without explicitly increasing network depth. By leveraging implicit refinement units with shared weights, this module recursively refines the feature pyramid. This mechanism achieves deep representational capacity with the parameter efficiency of a shallow network. Finally, we propose a decoupled detection head incorporating large-kernel context regression and SIoU loss to effectively alleviate the misalignment between classification and localization for tiny objects. Extensive experiments demonstrate that S3-Det achieves an exceptional trade-off between accuracy and latency, surpassing state-of-the-art lightweight detectors to establish a new benchmark for real-time aerial surveillance.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) technology has catalyzed its widespread adoption across diverse domains, including disaster rescue, traffic surveillance, agricultural inspection, and military reconnaissance. As a fundamental component of UAV vision systems, object detection aims to accurately localize and identify targets of interest from aerial imagery in real time. However, compared to natural scenes, UAV aerial imagery presents distinct characteristics, including broad viewing coverage, complex background diversity, and drastic scale variations. Notably, statistics from authoritative benchmarks such as Vision Meets Drones1 and UAV-Benchmark2 reveal that aerial images contain a massive number of tiny objects with extremely low resolution, often exhibiting high-density distributions. The minute and dense nature of targets, coupled with the severe computational constraints of embedded devices, makes achieving high-precision detection under real-time requirements a critical challenge in UAV vision.

Deep Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have fundamentally revolutionized the field of object detection. Early two-stage frameworks, exemplified by Fast R-CNN3, achieved remarkable accuracy gains through novel architectural designs. However, the intensive computational cost inherently restricted their inference speed. Subsequently, the Feature Pyramid Network (FPN)4 emerged to address multi-scale detection challenges, leveraging multi-level feature fusion to enhance representation capabilities across different scales. To tackle the critical issue of positive-negative sample imbalance, Focal Loss5 was introduced, significantly propelling the evolution of one-stage detectors. Building upon these foundations, the YOLO series6 and EfficientDet7 attained an optimal balance between speed and accuracy by harmonizing network depth, width, and resolution. These milestones inaugurated a golden era for object detection. Consequently, the domain has experienced an unprecedented surge in development, predominantly anchored on the YOLO architecture. Anchor-free detectors, exemplified by YOLOX8, streamlined the intricate post-processing pipeline. Furthermore, recent iterations such as YOLOv99 and YOLOv1010 employ programmable gradient information and end-to-end optimization strategies, establishing new performance upper bounds for general object detection.Fig. 1Comparison between S3-Det and traditional frameworks. While the aforementioned general detectors excel on benchmarks such as COCO, deploying them directly to embedded UAV platforms presents significant challenges. The primary obstacle lies in the conflict between feature degradation and computational redundancy. In UAV aerial imagery, tiny objects occupy only a few pixels, and their discriminative information is predominantly encoded in high-frequency components, including fine-grained edges, corners, and textural details, rather than in low-frequency semantic content. However, it is well established that standard CNNs exhibit an inherent spectral bias, tending to prioritize the learning of low-frequency components while attenuating high-frequency signals11. Conventional lightweight architectures further exacerbate this issue by adopting aggressive downsampling strategies, such as large-stride convolutions and pooling operations, to minimize FLOPs. These operations progressively suppress high-frequency details across successive feature maps, causing the high-frequency cues critical for tiny object detection to be irreversibly lost as network depth increases. Consequently, the uppermost layers of the feature pyramid acquire rich semantics but suffer from a severe loss of spatial and high-frequency information. This degradation is particularly pronounced in UAV scenarios, where tiny objects already exhibit extremely low signal-to-noise ratios, rendering conventional lightweight backbones fundamentally ill-suited for preserving the high-frequency details upon which tiny object detection critically depends. Moreover, lightweight models designed for general scenarios, exemplified by MobileNetV312 and ShuffleNet V213, deliver impressive inference speeds but lack the capacity to explicitly model frequency-domain information, making them unable to distinguish weak signals of tiny objects from background noise under strict parameter constraints. Recent works have explored various directions to improve aerial object detection. For instance, multi-scale feature fusion combined with knowledge distillation has been investigated to enhance detection performance in aerial imagery14. Approaches integrating enhanced feature pyramids with expanded receptive fields have also demonstrated promising results for UAV small object detection15. Additionally, learning joint global and local representations from remote sensing modalities has emerged as a valuable direction16. Recent initiatives, exemplified by LUD-YOLO17, have sought to optimize network architectures for UAV environments, underscoring the necessity of domain-specific customization. However, effectively synergizing spatial and frequency information without incurring substantial computational costs remains an open challenge.Fig. 2Visual comparison of model accuracy and size for multiple lightweight models on the VisDrone dataset.

To overcome feature representation bottlenecks under stringent lightweight constraints, extensive efforts have focused on designing efficient neural network architectures. CSPNet18 enhances the learning capability of CNNs by truncating redundant gradient flows. Meanwhile, RepVGG19 introduces the structural re-parameterization paradigm. However, most existing methodologies primarily target spatial operation optimization, often overlooking the pivotal role of frequency-domain information in detecting small objects. We posit that an ideal UAV detector should possess spectro-spatial synergistic perception capabilities. Specifically, the model must simultaneously capture long-range dependencies in the spatial domain and adaptively enhance high-frequency details within the frequency domain. Additionally, conventional FPN architectures typically augment semantic depth by stacking convolutional layers, which incurs substantial parameter costs. Drawing inspiration from Deep Equilibrium Models, we leverage a recursive mechanism with weight sharing as an efficient pathway to achieve deep representational capacity with shallow parameter overhead.

Based on these insights, we propose S3-Det as an efficient detection network designed to address the dual challenges of feature degradation in tiny objects and computational constraints in UAV aerial imagery. Related work

Object detection in UAV images

Object detection in aerial imagery serves as a fundamental task for UAV vision systems. Unlike natural scenes captured from ground-level perspectives, UAV imagery exhibits distinct characteristics, including broad viewing coverage, cluttered backgrounds, and drastic scale variations. As highlighted by Zhu et al.17 and the VisDrone-DET2018 benchmark20, conventional algorithms frequently struggle with densely distributed tiny objects in high-resolution aerial imagery, leading to severe missed detections and false positives. These obstacles laid the groundwork for subsequent efforts aimed at addressing scale variations and background interference.

To address the challenges posed by arbitrary target orientation and dense distribution, early initiatives prioritized reinforcing feature representation and geometric modeling. Ding et al.21 introduced the RoI Transformer, which applies spatial transformations to Regions of Interest within a two-stage framework to enhance localization accuracy for oriented targets. Yang et al.22 developed SCRDet, leveraging feature alignment and attention mechanisms to suppress interference from complex background noise. Subsequently, Han et al.23 developed S2A-Net, employing feature alignment strategies to mitigate spatial misalignment between deep and shallow features. While these approaches rooted in complex geometric modeling yield remarkable accuracy gains, they often entail substantial computational overhead and high inference latency. These drawbacks render them unsuitable for direct deployment on edge UAV platforms, which mandate strict power efficiency and real-time processing.

The escalating demand for edge computing has directed significant attention toward efficient one-stage detectors, particularly variants based on the YOLO architecture. Specifically, Li et al.24 and Zhang et al.25 tailored YOLO frameworks for UAV scenarios. By leveraging network pruning and lightweight backbone designs, these approaches achieved substantial parameter reduction while maintaining competitive detection accuracy. Transformer architectures have been introduced to overcome the limitations of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) in capturing long-range dependencies. Notably, ViT-YOLO26 integrates the global receptive field advantage of Transformers with the computational efficiency of the YOLO framework. Subsequently, Drone-DETR27 and UAV-DETR28 leveraged an end-to-end set prediction paradigm to refine the detection pipeline for dense tiny objects. However, the inherent quadratic computational complexity of Transformers remains a critical bottleneck when processing high-resolution aerial imagery. This limitation impedes practical deployment and drives the pursuit of more streamlined hybrid architectures.

Recently, a surge of state-of-the-art methods has emerged, prioritizing ultra-lightweight architectures and interference robustness. For instance, CSPDNet29 achieves efficient feature extraction through cross-stage partial connection networks. To further boost the recall rate of tiny objects, LightUAV-YOLO30 and SS-YOLOv831 strengthen the propagation of shallow details by refining multi-scale feature fusion strategies. To mitigate illumination and occlusion challenges within complex environments, LAM-YOLO32 integrates specialized attention mechanisms to bolster feature robustness. Parallel to this, RemDet33 re-evaluates model design principles for UAV detection, highlighting the inference acceleration facilitated by structural re-parameterization. These works have achieved substantial progress in spatial feature fusion yet frequently overlook the spectral bias of neural networks. High-frequency details are disproportionately lost during downsampling processes. Effectively preserving these frequency cues under minimal computational overhead constraints remains an unresolved challenge.

Efficient neural architecture design

Despite substantial advancements in spatial feature fusion, these methods frequently overlook the spectral bias inherent in neural networks, where high-frequency details suffer disproportionate loss during downsampling. Consequently, effectively preserving these frequency cues while maintaining minimal computational overhead remains an unresolved challenge. The evolution of lightweight network architectures has consistently centered on three core objectives: minimizing parameter counts, reducing FLOPs, and optimizing representation capabilities. MobileNetV234 pioneered the systematic integration of efficient operators and topological design paradigms into mobile CNNs. By leveraging an innovative inverted residual structure coupled with depthwise separable convolutions, this architecture attains a stable balance between accuracy and latency. Furthermore, it effectively suppresses information degradation induced by non-linearities within narrow bottleneck layers. Building on these advancements, MNASNet35 propelled structural design from heuristic-driven paradigms toward automated neural architecture search under platform constraints. By jointly optimizing accuracy and real-world device latency, this framework transitioned lightweight networks from theoretical low-parameter models into solutions viable for practical deployment.

However, exclusively relying on separable convolutions remains limited by feature redundancy and representation efficiency. GhostNet36 pioneered the concept of generating redundant features through cost-effective operations. Specifically, it produces intrinsic feature maps using a small number of standard convolutions and subsequently generates additional ’ghost’ features via lightweight linear transformations. This strategy reconstructs the feature generation mechanism at the level of computational allocation, providing a structural basis for expanding the representation space at minimal cost. Similarly, structural re-parameterization methodologies, exemplified by RepVGG19 and RepViT37, decouple multi-branch topologies used during training from single-path architectures employed for inference. This paradigm offers a “heavy training, light deployment” solution, effectively bolstering feature extraction capabilities without incurring additional inference overhead.

The incorporation of Transformers into vision backbones has shifted the focus of lightweight architecture design toward low-cost global modeling. TinyViT38 utilizes pre-training distillation and efficient attention mechanisms to enable effective representation capabilities under restricted computational resources. EdgeNeXt39 and EfficientFormer40,41 investigate hybrid CNN-Transformer paradigms to strike a robust compromise between local inductive biases and long-range dependencies. Notably, recent advancements such as MobileMamba42 and MambaVision43 integrate state space models into vision backbones, leveraging multi-receptive field mechanisms to minimize sequence modeling costs. However, these architectures primarily focus on spatial aggregation. In the context of UAV detection, explicit frequency-domain modeling remains underexplored in lightweight backbones, despite its potential to enhance high-frequency components like edges and textures for mitigating feature blurring in tiny objects.

Equally critical is the efficiency of feature aggregation necks. Conventional approaches typically deepen semantic representations by stacking convolutional layers, a practice that inevitably incurs substantial parameter overhead. Drawing from Deep Equilibrium Models, recursive processing with weight sharing offers a mechanism to emulate infinite depth using fixed parameters. While FRR-Net44 and FMR-Net45 have explored multi-scale residual organization, incorporating implicit recursive refinement into lightweight feature pyramids remains an underexplored avenue for maximizing representational capacity per parameter.

Proposed method

In this section, we detail the proposed S3-Det framework. Tailored to address the intricate challenges of tiny object scales, background clutter, and computational constraints inherent to UAV aerial imagery, S3-Det integrates explicit frequency-domain enhancement with implicit recursive feature refinement. This design establishes a detection architecture that balances high efficiency with superior precision. As depicted in Fig. 3, the proposed framework comprises three integral components: the Spectro-Spatial Synergistic Network (S3Net) serving as the backbone, the Implicit Recursive Feature Aggregator (IRFA) for feature pyramid enhancement, and the decoupled Large-Context Regoupled Head (LCR-Head).Fig. 3Overview of the proposed S3-Det architecture. It employs stacked Spectro-Spatial Blocks (SSB) to dynamically enhance high-frequency details critical for small objects while capturing long-range dependencies via granular split attention. (2) The Implicit Recursive Feature Aggregator (IRFA) utilizes weight-sharing Implicit Refinement Units (IRU) for recursive on-demand fusion of multi-scale features. This design maximizes feature semantics without significantly increasing network depth. (3) The Large-Context Regoupled Head (LCR-Head) decouples classification and regression branches. Large kernel depthwise convolutions are integrated into the regression branch to expand the receptive field and mitigate localization bias for tiny objects.

Spectro-spatial synergistic network

Existing lightweight Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) typically exhibit low-pass filtering behaviors, prioritizing low-frequency structural information while suppressing high-frequency details. However, within UAV imagery, the critical features of tiny objects such as pedestrians and vehicles are predominantly concentrated in the high-frequency domain, manifested as edges and textures. To counteract such feature degradation, we propose S3Net. Constructed by stacking multiple Spectro-Spatial Blocks (SSBs), this architecture leverages a frequency-domain gating mechanism to dynamically enhance high-frequency representations while preserving spatial efficiency.

Implicit recursive feature aggregator

In the design of Feature Pyramid Networks (FPNs), increasing network depth typically enhances semantic representation at the cost of significantly higher parameter counts. To achieve the inference power of deep architectures while maintaining a lightweight footprint, we propose the Implicit Recursive Feature Aggregator (IRFA). The core design of IRFA leverages the principle of deep equilibrium models, employing a shared-weight Implicit Refinement Unit (IRU) to refine features through recursive processing. It is worth noting that to meet the strict real-time requirements of UAV applications, the “recursive” mechanism here denotes cross-level weight sharing rather than computationally expensive multi-step iterations at a single node. Specifically, in our 3-level pyramid structure, the shared IRU is sequentially applied 4 times per forward pass (twice during the top-down pathway and twice during the bottom-up pathway). Since no multi-loop iterations are introduced and the IRU is built upon lightweight depth-wise convolutions, IRFA does not impose excessive computational overhead (FLOPs) compared to standard FPNs. Meanwhile, the weight-sharing strategy effectively keeps the total parameter count highly compact while mimicking the representational power of a much deeper network.

Implicit refinement unit (IRU)

The IRU serves as a non-linear transformation module with shared parameters, denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\psi (\cdot ; \theta )$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document} represents the shared weights. Specifically, the IRU employs a depthwise convolution to extract local context and a pointwise convolution for channel mixing. Additionally, we integrate a gating mechanism to regulate the information flow as follows:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \psi (\mathscr {F}) = Sigmoid(\mathscr {K}_{pw}(\mathscr {B}N(ReLU(\mathscr {B}N(\mathscr {K}_{dw}(\mathscr {F})))))) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This unit is repeatedly invoked during each feature fusion process of the FPN. It simulates the implicit layer behavior of an infinitely deep network while maintaining a constant number of parameters.

Recursive feature fusion

IRFA adopts the bidirectional path structure of PANet, comprising both top-down and bottom-up streams. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_2$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_3$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_4$$\end{document} denote the output features from S3Net. We first generate the initial pyramid features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_2$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_3$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_4$$\end{document} via lateral connections. In the top-down path, the upsampled features from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{i+1}$$\end{document} are fused into the i-th layer features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_i$$\end{document}. Rather than employing a simple additive fusion, we treat \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_i$$\end{document} as a latent state and utilize the IRU to perform gated updates as follows:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} G_{td} = \psi (P_i; \theta _{shared}), \quad P_i^{td} = P_i + Upsample(P_{i+1}) \odot G_{td} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_{td}$$\end{document} serves as a dynamic forget gate, determining the balance between retaining current layer information and injecting high-level semantic cues. Similarly, in the bottom-up path, we reuse the same transformation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\psi$$\end{document} to fuse the downsampled features from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{i-1}$$\end{document} into the i-th layer features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_i$$\end{document} as follows:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} G_{bu} = \psi (N_i; \theta _{shared}), \quad N_i^{out} = N_i + Downsample(N_{i-1}) \odot G_{bu} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This recursive implicit aggregation strategy ensures that features across various scales undergo multiple stages of non-linear refinement during fusion. By iteratively mapping the feature space, this approach effectively mitigates feature aliasing for tiny objects, which typically occurs during repetitive upsampling and downsampling operations.

Large-context regoupled head

Traditional single-stage detection heads typically share convolutional weights between classification and regression branches, with receptive fields constrained by standard convolution kernels. However, classification tasks require translation invariance, whereas regression tasks necessitate translation covariance. Moreover, bounding box regression for tiny objects demands more extensive surrounding context to achieve precise localization. To address these conflicting requirements, we propose the LCR-Head.

The LCR-Head adopts a decoupled architecture. For the feature layer \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_i$$\end{document}, we construct a classification branch \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {B}_{cls}$$\end{document} and a regression branch \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {B}_{reg}$$\end{document} separately. We maintain a lightweight design for the classification branch by using stacked \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3\times 3$$\end{document} depthwise convolutions:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {F}_{cls} = DWConv^{3\times 3}(...(DWConv^{3\times 3}(P_i))) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}For the regression branch, we introduce large-kernel convolutions to expand the effective receptive field, specifically addressing dense object distributions and severe occlusions within the VisDrone dataset. We increase the kernel size to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5\times 5$$\end{document}, which can be expressed as follows:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {F}_{reg} = DWConv^{5\times 5}(...(DWConv^{5\times 5}(P_i))) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The larger receptive field enables the regressor to perceive the surrounding contextual information. Consequently, the environmental information is utilized to assist in inferring the bounding box positions when the inherent features of the targets are ambiguous.

Learning objectives

We adopt a composite loss function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {L}_{total}$$\end{document} to further improve the localization accuracy of small targets and accelerate convergence. This composite loss function comprises Quality Focal Loss (QFL), Distribution Focal Loss (DFL), and SIoU loss. The formulation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {L}_{total}$$\end{document} is expressed as follows:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {L}_{total} = \lambda _{qfl}\mathscr {L}_{QFL} + \lambda _{dfl}\mathscr {L}_{DFL} + \lambda _{bbox}\mathscr {L}_{SIoU} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Quality focal loss (QFL)

To resolve the inconsistency in quality estimation between training and inference phases, we employ QFL to jointly model classification scores and IoU quality. Given a category label \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y \in \{0, 1\}$$\end{document} and a predicted score s, the QFL is defined with a modulating factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta =2.0$$\end{document} as follows:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {L}_{QFL}(s) = -|y-{s}|^\beta ((1-y)\log (1-{s}) + y\log ({s})) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

SIoU loss

To mitigate the drastic IoU fluctuations caused by minor positional deviations in tiny objects, we adopt the SIoU loss. Traditional IoU metrics often overlook the directional orientation of regression vectors. By contrast, SIoU introduces an angle penalty term to redefine the distance loss. We first define the angle cost \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Lambda$$\end{document}. Let b and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_{gt}$$\end{document} represent the center coordinates of the predicted and ground-truth boxes. The horizontal and vertical offsets between these centers are denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_x$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_y$$\end{document}, with the Euclidean distance represented as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho$$\end{document}:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Lambda = 1 - 2\sin ^2 \left( \arcsin \left( \frac{\min ({d_x}, {d_y})}{\rho }\right) - \frac{\pi }{4}\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Next, we define the distance cost \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta$$\end{document}, which takes into account the effect of angle:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Delta = \sum _{t \in \{x,y\}} (1 - e^{-\gamma \rho _t}), \quad \text {where } \gamma = 2 - \Lambda \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The final SIoU loss combines the shape cost \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega$$\end{document} and IoU:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {L}_{SIoU} = 1 - IoU + \frac{\Delta + \Omega }{2} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}SIoU guides the predicted boxes to align rapidly with the axes of the ground truth boxes. This mechanism significantly reduces the degrees of freedom in regression. Such a reduction is particularly vital for small object detection because these tasks are highly sensitive to pixel-level errors.

Experiments

Experimental setup

Datasets

To evaluate the effectiveness and generalization of the S3-Det framework, we select two prominent benchmark datasets: VisDrone2019 and UAVDT. The VisDrone2019 dataset is widely recognized for its high scene complexity and the prevalence of tiny objects. It encompasses diverse environments ranging from urban to suburban areas, captured across varying altitudes and camera angles. This dataset comprises ten common categories, such as pedestrians and vehicles, providing a rigorous testbed for UAV-based detection. UAVDT is a large-scale dataset specifically designed for vehicle detection in complex traffic scenarios, characterized by multiple perspectives, various flight altitudes, and diverse weather conditions such as fog and night. Joint evaluation on these two benchmarks enables an objective and comprehensive verification of S3-Det for edge-side UAV applications. Specifically, this assessment validates the proposed framework across two critical dimensions: the perception accuracy for extremely small objects and the adaptability to cross-scenario environments.

Implementation details

We implement the architecture of S3-Det using the PyTorch framework. All experiments, including training and inference, are conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX 4060 GPU. We employ the AdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001 and a weight decay of 0.05. The learning rate follows a cosine annealing schedule with a minimum value of 5e-5. To ensure stability during the early stages of training, we apply a linear warmup for the first 500 iterations. The total training duration is 100 epochs. We develop a robust data augmentation pipeline tailored to the unique characteristics of UAV imagery. Specifically, we set the random scaling range to [0.5, 1.5] to simulate the drastic scale variations inherent to different flight altitudes. The pipeline also incorporates perspective transformation, geometric stretching, and color jittering. Finally, we resize all input images to a uniform resolution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$640 \times 640$$\end{document}. We employ S3Net (1.0x) as the backbone network. Within the detection head, the regression branch incorporates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5\times 5$$\end{document} large convolutional kernels to capture contextual information. Furthermore, we replace traditional GIoU and CIoU with SIoU loss, leveraging its angle-aware characteristics to accelerate regression convergence for tiny objects. We generate the final detection results through multi-class Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS). To maximize the recall of potential small targets, we set the IoU threshold to 0.6 and the confidence threshold to 0.01.

Conclusion

This paper proposes S3-Det to address the loss of high-frequency features and the constraints of edge-side computational power in UAV-perspective object detection. The framework introduces a frequency-spatial synergy network, termed S3Net, which dynamically enhances the feature representation of tiny objects by integrating high-frequency gating with granular attention. Simultaneously, we develop the Implicit Recursive Feature Aggregator (IRFA), which achieves recursive purification of deep semantics at a minimal computational cost through a parameter-sharing mechanism. By integrating a decoupled large-kernel context head and the SIoU loss, S3-Det effectively resolves the misalignment between classification and localization tasks. Experimental results demonstrate that our method achieves a superior trade-off between accuracy and efficiency on the VisDrone dataset compared to existing state-of-the-art lightweight models. Future work will focus on heterogeneous deployment optimizations for FPGA and NPU platforms. Additionally, we plan to investigate temporal correlation detection based on video streams to further improve detection stability.
