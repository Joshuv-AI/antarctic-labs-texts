Macrophage Notch1 drives hepatocyte ferroptosis via the exosomal miR-142a-3p/TIPE2 axis to promote MASH progression
Authors: Xiaoyu Dong, Mengya Zhang, Xiaoxing Huang, Fan Guo, Qintong Hu, Yanxue Feng, Lanxuan Jiang, Qinyong Zhang, Yue Ge, Xin Song, Junke Zhao, Kun Li, Jie Ping
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) is a serious chronic liver disease, in which ferroptosis has been identified as a crucial triggering event. However, the precise mechanisms of ferroptosis activation in MASH have not been fully disclosed. Herein, we found a positive association between macrophage Notch1 activation and hepatocyte ferroptosis in MASH patients and mice. Further, macrophage-specific Notch1 knockout (Notch1M-KO) mice showed ameliorative MASH symptoms, including less liver injury, lipid accumulation, inflammation, and collagen deposition. These mice also exhibited reduced hepatocyte ferroptosis, evidenced by decreased Fe2+ levels and expression of pro-ferroptosis genes (Hamp and Ptgs2) and higher expression of anti-ferroptosis genes (Gpx4 and Slc7a11), as well as improved mitochondrial structure. Moreover, hepatocytes that received exosomes from Notch1-deficient macrophages exhibited decreased ferroptosis, while the tail vein infusion of Notch1-activated macrophage exosomes aggravated ferroptosis and MASH symptoms, identifying the role of macrophage Notch1-exosomes in promoting hepatocyte ferroptosis under MASH. Mechanistically, we discovered that macrophage Notch1 activation increased the level of exosomal miR-142a-3p by miRNA sequencing and decreased its target gene TIPE2, and confirmed that the inhibition of miR-142a-3p upregulated the Notch1 activation-induced TIPE2 decrease. Besides, overexpression of TIPE2 in hepatocytes inhibited ferroptosis. Collectively, our findings uncover a novel mechanism by which Notch1 activation in hepatic macrophages promotes hepatocyte ferroptosis through the exosomal miR-142a-3p/TIPE2 axis in MASH. We revealed a novel insight into hepatocyte ferroptosis activation from the perspective of macrophage, and discovered a pivotal role of macrophage Notch1 in hepatocyte ferroptosis during MASH progression, highlighting a potential therapeutic target for MASH treatment.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13578-026-01621-z.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), formerly known as nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), is a severe stage of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) that progresses from simple steatosis to the disease of inflammation and fibrosis [1]. While the prognosis of simple steatosis is benign, the progression into severe MASH is a serious risk factor for cirrhosis and carcinogenesis [2, 3]. However, the molecular mechanisms that control progressive MASH have yet to be fully determined, highlighting the importance of exploring the pathogenesis of MASH to develop effective therapeutic approaches.

Hepatocyte death represents a central step in the transition from reversible simple steatosis to irreversible MASH and fibrosis. Thus, the effective control of hepatocyte death plays a critical role in halting the progression of MASH. Among different types of hepatocyte death, ferroptosis serves as the initial event in MASH pathogenesis, preceding other modes of cell death [4–8]. Ferroptosis is characterized by significant iron-dependent lipid peroxidation [9], which may cause hepatocyte swelling, inflammation, and fibrosis, thus speeding up the progression of MASH [8, 10]. Studies have found that the ferroptosis inducer can aggravate MASH severity, whereas the ferroptosis inhibitor can alleviate MASH [11, 12]. Even a previous study revealed that mice exhibited signs of ferroptosis in the liver as early as the 10th day of methionine- and choline-deficient (MCD) diet [11]. However, the precise mechanisms behind hepatocyte ferroptosis activation in MASH progression have not been fully disclosed.

Although hepatocytes are the primary iron storage cells in the body, maintaining hepatic iron homeostasis requires interactions with other cell types. Particularly macrophages, the most abundant immune cells in the liver, exhibit a strong association with hepatocyte ferroptosis activation [13]. Despite the direct link between macrophages and hepatocyte ferroptosis in MASH has not been reported, extensive researches have substantiated the close association of hepatic macrophages with lipid metabolism, inflammatory cytokines, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which are also the key triggers of ferroptosis [13–16]. Consequently, molecules targeting macrophages might be a prospective strategy for halting MASH progression by inhibiting hepatocyte ferroptosis.

Notch1 is a highly conserved signaling pathway regulating macrophage biological functions [17]. Studies have shown that the activation of Notch1 in macrophages promotes their pro-inflammatory property, leading to the release of large amounts of chemokines and cytokines that exacerbate inflammation and contribute to various chronic inflammatory diseases [18–20]. Notably, recent research has presented evidence indicating that Notch1 in adipocytes can modulate ferroptosis through mitochondrial pathways [21]. In the liver, studies have revealed that Notch1 signaling regulates ferroptosis in hepatic stellate cells and hepatocytes to participate in liver fibrosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [22, 23]. However, whether or how macrophage notch1 regulates hepatocyte ferroptosis during MASH progression remains to be elucidated.

In this study, we discovered a previously unrecognized molecular mechanism that triggered ferroptosis during MASH. We demonstrated that the activation of Notch1 in hepatic macrophages is closely related to the occurrence of ferroptosis in MASH. Using macrophage-specific Notch1 knockout (Notch1M-KO) mice and miRNA sequencing, we found that Notch1 signaling in hepatic macrophages modulated the Exosomal miR-142a-3p levels, which in turn affected TIPE2 in hepatocytes, thereby promoting the occurrence of ferroptosis during the progression of MASH. These findings provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms of ferroptosis in MASH and suggest a potential therapeutic target.

Results

Macrophage Notch1 activation is positively correlated with hepatocyte ferroptosis during the progression of MASH

To observe the involvement of hepatocyte ferroptosis in MASH initiation and progression, we first analyzed a publicly available database of clinical MASH patients (GSE260666). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway of liver samples of MASH patients suggests that ferroptosis is significantly elevated in MASH (Figure S1). Then, we analyzed the intrahepatic ferroptosis in MASH mice, as exhibited by significant macrovesicular steatosis and inflammatory cell infiltration within the hepatic lobule spaces under the MCD and HFD fed (Fig. 1A and E). As expected, hepatic Fe2+ concentrations were significantly increased in MCD and HFD mice (Fig. 1B and F). qRT-PCR revealed decreased mRNA levels of anti-ferroptosis genes Gpx4 and Slc7a11, and increased levels of pro-ferroptosis genes Hamp and Ptgs2 in the MASH liver (Fig. 1C and G). Additionally, the protein expression levels of GPX4 and SLC7A11 were reduced in the MASH groups (Fig. 1D and H).

To investigate the regulation of hepatic macrophage Notch1 in MASH and hepatocyte ferroptosis, we assessed Notch1 activation in hepatic macrophages. The immunofluorescence results from clinical liver tissue samples showed that compared with the control group, Notch1 activation in the liver of MASH patients was significantly increased and primarily localized in CD68+ macrophages (Figure S2). Then, we investigated the Notch1 activation in the above steatohepatitis mice. Flow cytometry analysis indicated that hepatic macrophage NICD (the activated form of Notch1) expressions were markedly increased in MASH mice (Fig. 2A and C). We further isolated hepatic macrophages from MASH mice to measure NICD expression, and the results showed NICD expressions were significantly upregulated in the isolated hepatic macrophages from MCD and HFD mice, compared with the MCS and NCD mice, respectively. (Fig. 2B and D). Importantly, the level of Notch1 activation in macrophages was positively correlated with hepatic Fe2+ concentration and the mRNA levels of Hamp and Ptgs2, in contrast, which was significantly negatively correlated with the mRNA levels of Gpx4 and Slc7a11 (Fig. 2E and F). These observations implied the positive associations between Notch1 activation in hepatic macrophages and the occurrence of ferroptosis in MASH.

Notch1 deletion in macrophages alleviates MASH in mice

To further determine the role of macrophage Notch1 in MASH, we constructed Floxed Notch1 (Notch1FL/FL) and Notch1M-KO mice (Figure S3). These mice were fed with either MCS or MCD for 2 weeks to focus on identifying the initial triggers of this process. As shown in Fig. 3A, Notch1M-KO mice exhibited reduced steatosis, less inflammatory cell infiltration, and decreased collagen deposition compared to Notch1FL/FL mice. In addition, serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) in MCD-Notch1M-KO mice were lower than those in MCD-Notch1FL/FL mice (Fig. 3B). Also, Notch1M-KO mice displayed significant decreases in hepatic levels of triglycerides (TG) and total cholesterol (TC) compared with Notch1FL/FL mice (Fig. 3C). When liver inflammatory status was examined, the mRNA expression levels of proinflammatory cytokines (Tnf-α, Il-1β, and Il-6) and chemokines (Mcp-1) were significantly lower in Notch1M-KO mice, indicating reduced inflammation in these mice (Fig. 3D). These observations indicate that the aberrant activation of macrophage Notch1 participates in MASH.

Notch1 deficiency in macrophages depresses hepatocyte ferroptosis in MASH

Next, to further determine whether the ameliorative effect of macrophage Notch1 deficiency is related to the hepatic ferroptosis in steatohepatitis. We observed the morphology of mitochondria by transmission electron microscopy. The results confirmed that the disruption of macrophagic Notch1 inhibited the reduction of mitochondrial cristae and alleviated damage to the outer mitochondrial membrane (Fig. 4A). Biochemical assays revealed that Notch1M-KO livers had decreased levels of malondialdehyde‌ (MDA), reactive oxygen species (ROS), and Fe2+, alongside increased superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione (GSH) levels compared to Notch1FL/FL livers (Fig. 4B–D). Additionally, the disruption of macrophagic Notch1 downregulated the mRNA levels of Hamp and Ptgs2 and increased the mRNA levels of Gpx4 and Slc7a11 compared with Notch1FL/FL livers (Fig. 4E). Correspondingly, Western blot analysis confirmed increased GPX4 and SLC7A11 protein expressions in Notch1M-KO livers (Fig. 4F).

Next, we further explored the direct regulation of Notch1 signaling in macrophages on hepatocyte ferroptosis. Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were obtained from WT mice and treated with LPS + PA, the primary stimulators of hepatic macrophages in steatohepatitis. Western blot analysis showed that NICD expression was increased in macrophages after LPS + PA stimulation (Figure S4A). Then, we established an in vitro BMDMs/primary hepatocytes co-culture system (Figure S4B) and harvested the hepatocytes after 24 h to measure intracellular Fe2+ concentration and the mRNA or protein expressions of ferroptosis-related genes. As shown in Figure S4C–E, Notch1 deficiency resulted in a diminished Fe2+ concentration and reduced mRNA levels of Ptgs2 and Hamp, while the mRNA and protein levels of Gpx4 and Slc7a11 were increased. Additionally, flow cytometry analysis revealed that Notch1 deficiency significantly downregulated ROS levels in hepatocytes co-cultured with LPS + PA-stimulated macrophages, indicating reduced oxidative stress (Fig. 4G). In contrast, NICD overexpression in macrophages further exacerbated ROS accumulation in hepatocytes (Fig. 4H). Collectively, these results indicate that aberrant activation of Notch1 in macrophages is essential for hepatic ferroptosis in MASH.

Exosomes (Exos) mediate the regulation of macrophage Notch1 on hepatocyte ferroptosis

Having demonstrated that the Exos pathway is an important mediator of intercellular communication in the liver [24], we speculated that macrophage Notch1 regulates hepatocyte ferroptosis via Exos. To confirm this hypothesis, we first treated BMDMs with GW4869, a commonly used chemical inhibitor of Exos biogenesis. The results showed that GW4869 treatment of macrophages stimulated with LPS + PA reduced the susceptibility of hepatocytes to ferroptosis, as indicated by decreased expressions of Ptgs2 and Hamp and increased expressions of Gpx4 and Slc7a11 (Fig. 5A). Western blot results also showed that GW4869 treatment increased the protein expression of SLC7A11 and GPX4 (Fig. 5B). Next, we isolated and characterized BMDMs-Exos. Transmission electron microscopy revealed the complete double-layer vesicle structures with diameters ranging from 30 to 120 nm in a cup or saucer shape (Fig. 5C). Additionally, Western blot confirmed these BMDMs-Exos expressed Exos marker proteins CD9 and TSG101, and showed no expression of negative control marker Calnexin (Fig. 5D). Further, to explore whether BMDM-Exos could be taken up by hepatocytes, BMDM-Exos were labeled with PKH26 (a red fluorescent dye) and co-cultured with hepatocytes. After 12 h, microscopy showed that BMDMs-Exos were effectively taken up by hepatocytes (Fig. 5E). To further examine whether Exos contribute to Notch1-mediated hepatocyte ferroptosis, BMDMs-Exos from Notch1FL/FL and Notch1M-KO mice were added into the culture medium of hepatocytes, respectively (Fig. 5F). As expected, the hepatocytes co-cultured with Notch1M-KO BMDMs-Exos showed a significant decrease in Fe2+ concentration and the mRNA levels of Ptgs2 and Hamp, an increase in mRNA levels of Gpx4 and Slc7a11, compared with the Notch1FL/FL BMDMs-Exos (Fig. 5G and H). Taken together, these results demonstrated that macrophage Notch1 mediates hepatocyte ferroptosis through Exos.

To further confirm the in vivo effects of macrophage Notch1-regulated Exos in MASH progression, Exos derived from NICD-overexpressed BMDMs were administered via the tail vein, followed by MCD feeding. Histological analysis revealed that mice received NICD-overexpressed BMDMs-Exos exhibited significantly increased hepatic steatosis compared to controls, as shown by H&E and Oil Red O staining. Additionally, Sirius Red staining revealed a notable increase in collagen deposition in the NICD-overexpression group compared to controls (Fig. 6A). Increased serum ALT and AST levels, as well as liver TG and TC levels, revealed severe steatohepatitis after NICD-overexpressed Exos treatment (Fig. 6B and C). Ferroptosis-related indicators, including hepatic Fe2⁺ concentrations, MDA, and ROS levels, were significantly elevated in these mice, while antioxidant markers such as SOD and GSH were markedly reduced (Fig. 6D and E). Western blot analysis further confirmed that the expression of GPX4 and SLC7A11 was significantly downregulated in the livers of mice receiving NICD-BMDMs-Exos, compared to controls (Fig. 6F). Taken together, these results demonstrate that macrophage Notch1 signaling mediates ferroptosis and exacerbates MASH progression in vivo through the secretion of Exos.

Notch1-activated macrophages affect hepatocyte ferroptosis via exosomal miR-142a-3p

To investigate the specific mechanism by which Notch1-activated macrophage Exos induce ferroptosis in hepatocytes, we performed miRNA-seq on Exos. We utilized volcano plot and heatmap analyses to illustrate differential expression patterns of miRNAs, effectively highlighting the distinct profiles between Notch1FL/FL-Exos and Notch1M-KO-Exos (Fig. 7A). Among the differentially expressed miRNAs, miR-142a-3p, miR-339-3p, and miR-340-3p were identified, all of which have been previously implicated in ferroptosis [25, 26]. We then validated the expression of these miRNAs in Notch1FL/FL and Notch1M-KO BMDM-Exos, as well as in hepatocytes co-cultured with these Exos. Notably, Notch1 deficiency in macrophages resulted in a significant reduction in miR-142a-3p levels both in BMDMs-Exos and in hepatocytes exposed to these Exos, rather than miR-339-3p or miR-340-3p (Fig. 7A and S5). Additionally, analysis of the GEO database (GSE206338 and GSE263875) indicated that miR-142a-3p is closely linked to MASH (Fig. 7B).

To further assess the functional relevance of miR-142a-3p in macrophage-derived Exos and hepatocyte ferroptosis, we transfected BMDMs with either miR-142a-3p mimic or negative control (NC) and then co-cultured these with hepatocytes following LPS + PA treatment (Figure S6A). The results showed that miR-142a-3p overexpression led to a decrease in the mRNA and protein levels of Gpx4 and Slc7a11, while an increase in the mRNA levels of Ptgs2 and Hamp, and Fe2+ concentration (Fig. 7C–E). Conversely, when BMDMs were transfected with miR-142a-3p inhibitor or NC and co-cultured with hepatocytes following LPS + PA treatment, the mRNA and protein levels of Gpx4 and Slc7a11 were elevated, and the mRNA levels of Ptgs2 and Hamp, along with Fe2+ concentration, were reduced (Fig. 7F–H). Furthermore, BMDMs overexpressed NICD and transfected with a miR-142a-3p inhibitor displayed increased protein levels of GPX4 and SLC7A11(Fig. 7I and S6B). These results confirmed that Notch1 activation in macrophages promotes hepatocyte ferroptosis via Exos-miR-142a-3p.

Notch1-induced miR-142a-3p promotes hepatocyte ferroptosis by targeting TIPE2

To explore the target genes of miR-142a-3p, we predicted its binding sites using miRWalk and identified Tipe2, a reported ferroptosis-related gene [27], as a potential target. In addition, analysis of public datasets from MASH patients revealed a significant correlation between TIPE2 expression and key ferroptosis-related genes (Gpx4, Slc7a11, Hamp, and Ptgs2) (Figure S7). Dual-luciferase reporter assays subsequently demonstrated that the miR-142a-3p mimic significantly reduced the activity of the wild-type TIPE2 3'-UTR compared to cells transfected with the NC (Fig. 8A). To further confirm that miR-142a-3p regulates TIPE2 in hepatocytes, we transfected hepatocytes with the miR-142a-3p mimic or inhibitor and measured TIPE2 levels. The results showed that miR-142a-3p significantly reduced Tipe2 levels compared to the NC group (Fig. 8B), confirming that TIPE2 is the target gene of miR-142a-3p in hepatocytes.

However, the role of hepatocyte TIPE2 in ferroptosis remains unclear. To investigate its regulatory effect, we overexpressed TIPE2 by transfecting hepatocytes with a TIPE2 plasmid and then co-culturing them with the supernatant of LPS + PA-stimulated BMDMs (Figure S6C). The results indicated that overexpression of TIPE2 significantly increased the expression of ferroptosis inhibitory genes and proteins (Gpx4 and Slc7a11) while downregulating the expression of ferroptosis-promoting genes (Ptgs2 and Hamp) and reducing Fe2+ concentration (Fig. 8C–E). These results suggest that TIPE2 inhibits ferroptosis in hepatocytes.

Next, we verified the expression of miR-142a-3p and TIPE2 in the liver. The results showed that miR-142a-3p was elevated, while TIPE2 mRNA and protein levels were significantly reduced in the MCD liver compared to the MCS liver. Importantly, Notch1 knockout in macrophages significantly reduced the MCD-induced increase of miR-142a-3p and prevented the decrease of TIPE2 caused by the MCD diet (Fig. 8F and G). These findings confirm that macrophage Notch1 upregulates miR-142a-3p and downregulates hepatocyte Tipe2.

Last, to determine whether miR-142a-3p mediates the effect of macrophage Notch1 on TIPE2 expression, hepatocytes were co-cultured with macrophages overexpressing NICD after transfection with the miR-142a-3p inhibitor. Results showed that macrophage overexpression of NICD led to a significant reduction in TIPE2 expression in hepatocytes. However, inhibition of miR-142a-3p effectively restored TIPE2 expression to levels compared with the control group (Fig. 8H). These findings support the conclusion that macrophage Notch1 signaling inhibits TIPE2 expression in hepatocytes through miR-142a-3p. Collectively, our results indicated that abnormal activation of Notch1 in macrophages promoted ferroptosis during MASH by inhibiting the expression of TIPE2 in hepatocytes through Exos-miR-142a-3p.

Discussion

Ferroptosis is considered the earliest form of cell death and the primary trigger of inflammation in the progression of MASH [8]. However, the mechanisms underlying the activation of hepatocyte ferroptosis in MASH progression are not completely understood. In this study, we confirmed that abnormal activation of Notch1 in macrophages was closely related to the occurrence of hepatocyte ferroptosis in MASH progression. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the disruption of Notch1 in macrophages reduced hepatic Fe2+ concentration, improved mitochondrial morphology, and decreased lipid peroxidation levels during MASH. Mechanistically, the abnormal activation of Notch1 signaling in macrophages promoted hepatocyte ferroptosis through the Exos-miR-142a-3p/TIPE2 axis. Collectively, these findings revealed a new regulatory mechanism of hepatocyte ferroptosis in MASH, providing new therapeutic strategies for the treatment of MASH.

Recent studies have highlighted the diverse regulatory mechanisms that control ferroptosis across different pathological contexts. For example, the EP300/YY1/GPX4 signaling axis has been shown to suppress ferroptosis while promoting proliferation and fibrosis in keloid fibroblasts [28]. Similarly, ferroptosis is activated in livers and kidneys in the early stage of non-obese diabetic (NOD) mice [29]. In the liver, maintaining iron homeostasis requires the coordinated interaction between hepatocytes and macrophages [13]. However, how macrophages regulate hepatocyte ferroptosis in MASH is not yet clear. In this study, we found elevated hepatocyte ferroptosis and activated macrophage Notch1 in MASH patients’ livers, and revealed their positive correlation in 2-week MCD and 12-week HFD induced MASH mice. Nevertheless, the roles of macrophage Notch1 in MASH and hepatocyte ferroptosis have not yet been elucidated. We further used macrophage-specific Notch1 knockout mice, and fed them with 2-week MCD to focus on identifying the activation mechanisms of ferroptosis in MASH initiation, as reported it begins as early as 10 days by MCD feeding [11]. We found that the deletion of Notch1 in macrophages significantly inhibited hepatocyte ferroptosis and improved liver vacuolation, reduced inflammatory cell infiltration during MASH initiation.

Substantial evidence has confirmed the role of Exos-mediated communication between macrophages and ferroptosis levels in other cells [30–32]. For example, tumor-associated macrophage-derived Exos have been reported to suppress ferroptosis in cervical cancer cells [33]. circFAM134B has been reported to regulate reticulophagy-mediated ferroptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma [34]. Exos from alveolar macrophages alleviate bleomycin-induced lung damage by inhibiting ferroptosis [35]. However, whether Exos mediate the communication of macrophages and ferroptosis in hepatocytes is not clear. Our study further demonstrated that macrophage-derived Exos could be taken up by hepatocytes. Moreover, the treatment with the exosome inhibitor GW4869 significantly reduced the ferroptosis of hepatocytes induced by pro-inflammatory macrophages, indicating that Exos mediate the regulatory influence of macrophages on hepatocyte ferroptosis. Additionally, we validated that deletion of Notch1 in macrophages significantly ameliorated Exos-induced hepatocyte ferroptosis. Furthermore, the intravenous infusion of Exos derived from Notch1-activated macrophages reinforced their role in driving ferroptosis progression in MASH. These findings highlight that aberrant activation of macrophage Notch1 contributes to hepatocyte ferroptosis via the Exos pathway.

The molecular cargo contained within Exos can be regulated by physiological and pathological states. In recent years, numerous studies have focused on the interactions among various cells mediated by hepatic macrophage-derived Exo-miRNAs [36, 37]. These miRNAs can fine-tune gene expression by binding to specific mRNA sequences, influencing mRNA degradation or translational inhibition. Research has shown that hepatic macrophages could regulate fibrosis progression in MASH through Exos-miRNA-223 [24]. In our study, Exos-miR-seq revealed that the activation of macrophage Notch1 significantly affected the levels of miR-142a-3p in Exos. Existing studies on the role of miR-142a-3p in liver diseases mainly focus on hepatocellular carcinoma [38, 39]. The roles and mechanisms of miR-142a-3p in MASH merit further investigation. Our research reveals that miR-142a-3p expression is significantly increased in the MASH livers and decreased in macrophage Notch1-deficiency mice. Further mechanism experiments confirmed that overexpression of miR-142-3p in hepatocytes significantly promotes ferroptosis. While our rescue experiment using the miR-142a-3p inhibitor demonstrated a significant reversal of macrophage Notch1-overexpression-induced hepatocyte ferroptosis. In the meantime, it is important to note that the reversal has not fully returned to normal levels, which implies that Notch1 activation likely engages additional, parallel mechanisms that cooperate with or amplify the exosomal signal. However, the magnitude of the effect supports the exosomal miR-142a-3p/TIPE2 axis as the dominant mechanism. As reported, Notch1 activation is a potent driver of pro-inflammatory cytokine production [18, 40], which can generate substantial ROS and sensitize cells to ferroptosis. Therefore, cytokine-mediated inflammatory milieu might play a synergistic role in macrophage Notch1-driven hepatocyte ferroptosis.

Furthermore, target prediction and luciferase reporter assays demonstrate that TIPE2 is a target gene of miR-142a-3p. A previous study has indicated that overexpression of TIPE2 inhibits the progression of MASH and improves hepatic steatosis, insulin resistance, inflammation, and fibrosis [41]. Moreover, TIPE2 knockout inhibits cardiomyocyte ferroptosis by restraining lipid peroxidation [42]. However, the role of hepatocyte TIPE2 in ferroptosis remains unclear. In this study, we found that overexpressing TIPE2 in hepatocytes significantly suppressed ferroptosis induced by Notch1-activated macrophages. During the occurrence of steatohepatitis, the deletion of Notch1 in macrophages reduced the levels of miR-142a-3p, increased the expression of TIPE2, and decreased the level of ferroptosis in hepatocytes. Moreover, analysis of publicly available human transcriptomic datasets revealed that miR-142a-3p expression is also altered in patients with MASH, suggesting potential translational relevance. While our data support a role for macrophage-derived exosomal miR-142a-3p in regulating hepatocyte injury, future studies using hepatocyte-specific inhibition of miR-142a-3p in vivo will be important to further define its causal contribution to MASH progression.

From a translational perspective, our findings suggest that macrophage-mediated intercellular communication through exosomal miRNAs may represent a potential therapeutic target in MASH. Modulation of macrophage Notch1 signaling or inhibition of exosomal miR-142a-3p transfer could potentially attenuate hepatocyte ferroptosis and limit disease progression. However, therapeutic targeting of these pathways may face challenges, including the essential roles of Notch signaling in immune homeostasis and potential issues related to delivery specificity and off-target effects of miRNA-based interventions. Future studies are therefore needed to develop cell-type-specific strategies and to evaluate the safety and efficacy of targeting macrophage-hepatocyte signaling in MASH.

In summary, our study elucidated an unrecognized regulatory mechanism whereby macrophage Notch1 activation promotes hepatocyte ferroptosis through the Exos-miR-142a-3p/TIPE2 axis and revealed a novel role of TIPE2 in hepatocyte ferroptosis during MASH progression. While our findings strongly support the involvement of the Exos-miRNA pathway, it is important to acknowledge that other mechanisms, including additional biologically active molecules derived from Exos, ligand-receptor interactions, and cytokines, may also contribute to macrophage regulation of hepatocyte ferroptosis. These findings enhance our understanding of the pathophysiology of ferroptosis in MASH and provide a rationale for the development of therapeutic strategies targeting ferroptosis modulation as a potential treatment for MASH.

Materials and methods

Animal treatment

Wild-type (WT) C57BL/6 J mice were sourced from the Hubei Medical Scientific Academy. Floxed Notch1 (Notch1FL/FL) mice (Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME) and Lyz2-Cre mice (Lyz2-Cre; Jackson Laboratory) were utilized to create macrophage-specific Notch1 knockout (Notch1M-KO) mice. Genotyping was conducted on tail DNA using PCR. The mice were bred under standard conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle. Mice aged 8 weeks were used for all experiments. The male mice were fed a methionine- and choline-sufficient diet (MCS) or methionine- and choline-deficient chow diet (MCD) for 2 weeks, or either normal chow diet (NCD) or high-fat diet (HFD) for 12 weeks. Male mice are known to be more susceptible to diet-induced MASH, whereas female mice are relatively protected due to estrogen-mediated metabolic effects. To reduce variability in disease induction and ensure a consistent MASH phenotype, experiments in this study were therefore performed using male mice only. All animal protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Wuhan University in China (Permit No: WP20220219). The animals were provided with humane care in accordance with the guidelines outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health.

Human liver samples

Liver sections of human subjects were collected at Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University (Wuhan, China). Control liver specimens were obtained from individuals without steatohepatitis who underwent partial hepatectomy for hepatocellular carcinoma. MASH samples were obtained from patients diagnosed with steatohepatitis based on imaging studies and liver biopsy. Informed consent was available for each patient. This study was approved by the medical ethics committee of Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University (Ethical Approval Number: 2023022). All research was conducted in accordance with both the Declaration of Helsinki and Istanbul.

Histopathological analysis

Paraffin sections were prepared from mouse liver samples that were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, and cut into sections of 4 μm thickness. The paraffin-embedded liver sections were stained with hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) or Sirius red to evaluate histological changes. Additionally, mouse livers were frozen, sectioned into thin slices of 5 μm thickness, and stained with Oil Red O to evaluate liver lipid accumulation.

Flow cytometry

Mice were anesthetized with 0.2 mL of 1% pentobarbital solution. The thoracic cavity was dissected, and a prewarmed preperfusion solution (D-Hanks's solution) was infused via the inferior vena cava. Once the liver turned white, a digestion solution (1% BSA, 0.5% phosphatase inhibitor, 0.05% collagenase IV, and 0.002% DNase I in Hanks solution) was introduced. After removing the gallbladder, the liver tissue was shredded and passed through a 200-mesh sieve. Hepatocytes were removed by centrifugation at 50 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min to obtain liver non-parenchymal cells after erythrocytes were removed.

To detect Notch1 in hepatic macrophages, non-parenchymal cells were blocked by CD16/32 for 10 min at 4 °C, and then stained with a surface antibody against F4/80-PeCy7 for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. After extensive washing, cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Foxp3 Fixation/Permeabilization working solution according to the manufacturer's instructions (00-5523-00, eBioscience). The cells were then incubated with an antibody specific to the intracellular domain of Notch1 for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Stained cells were analyzed using a BD FACS AriaTM III flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), and all data were processed using FlowJo software. The antibodies used in this study are listed in Table S2.

Isolation of hepatocytes and hepatic macrophages

Mouse liver was perfused with D-Hanks and the digestion solution mentioned above, then cell suspensions were got through the 200-mesh filter screen and centrifugated at 50 × g for 5 min to get hepatocytes. The supernatant hepatic non-parenchymal cells were obtained by centrifugation at 400 × g for 5 min at 4 °C after being cracked with red cell lysis buffer. Then non-parenchymal cells were layered onto a 50%/25% 2-step Percoll gradient (Sigma-Aldrich). After centrifugation, hepatic macrophages in the middle layer were collected and allowed to adhere to cell culture plates in DMEM with 10% FBS for 15 min at 37 °C. The purity of hepatic macrophages was determined by flow cytometry.

Isolation of bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs)

BMDMs were isolated following established protocols [43]. Briefly, bone marrow cells were isolated from the femur and tibia of mice, and then differentiated in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 30% L929 supernatant for 7 days. The purity of BMDMs was determined by flow cytometry.

Isolation and characterization of BMDMs-Exosomes (Exos)

The concentrated medium was processed by differential centrifugation to isolate Exos. To minimize protein degradation, all centrifugation steps and procedures were performed at 4 °C. Cells in the concentrated medium were removed by low-speed centrifugation at 300 × g for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was then sequentially centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min and at 10,000 × g for 30 min to eliminate any remaining cell debris or microvesicles. Subsequently, the solution underwent ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 70 min to pellet the Exos. The Exos were resuspended in PBS and stored at − 80 °C for future use.

Approximately 5 μL of the Exos suspension was placed on carbon-coated copper grids for 10 min at room temperature and negatively stained with a 2% phosphotungstic acid solution (pH = 6.5). The morphology of the Exos was observed using an HT7700 transmission electron microscope (TEM) (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Positive biomarkers, including TSG101, CD9, and Calnexin (listed in Table S2), were detected by Western blot.

Cell culture and co-culture system

BMDMs were treated with 100 ng/mL LPS and 250 µM PA for 12 h, followed by 24 h of culture in fresh medium without LPS and PA. The supernatant was collected and added to hepatocytes for 24 h to establish a co-culture system.

To investigate the role of Exos, BMDMs were cultured for 24 h in medium containing 5 μM GW4869 (Cat# D1692, Sigma Aldrich) after the initial LPS and PA treatment. The supernatant was then collected and added to hepatocytes for another 24 h. For the co-culture of Exos and hepatocytes, BMDM-Exos (30 μg/ml) were added to hepatocytes and co-incubated for 24 h.

Biochemical detection

For tissue samples, livers were perfused with normal saline to remove blood stains from the tissue surfaces. The tissues were then weighed, homogenized in PBS at 4 °C, and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C.

For cell samples, cells were washed three times with cold PBS. Next, they were homogenized in RIPA buffer containing a protease inhibitor, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C.

The supernatants were kept on ice for testing. A portion of the supernatants was used to measure relative levels of Fe2+ (Cat# E-BC-F108) using detection kits from Elabscience Biotechnology (Wuhan, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The activity of serum aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and the levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Cat# E004-1-1), hepatic reduced glutathione (GSH) (Cat# A006-2-1), superoxide dismutase (SOD) (Cat# A001-3-2), malondialdehyde (MDA) (Cat# A003-1-2), triglyceride (TG) (Cat# 110-1-1) and total cholesterol (TC) (Cat# 111-1-1) were detected by biochemical kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Exos labelling for cellular uptake

To investigate the uptake of BMDMs-Exos by hepatocytes, BMDMs-Exos were labeled with PKH26 red fluorescent labeling kit (Cat# PKH26GL, Sigma Aldrich). The labeled BMDMs-Exos were then incubated with hepatocytes for 12 h. Following incubation, the Exos-treated hepatocytes were fixed and imaged using confocal microscopy (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

miRNA sequencing and data analysis

miRNA sequencing of RNA from BMDM-Exos was conducted by Novogene (Beijing, China). Briefly, 2 μg of total RNA was used for miRNA library preparation after verifying RNA quality and integrity. Sequencing was performed using the TruSeq SR Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Illumina) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

For miRNA analysis, raw sequencing data were first normalized using a log2(x + 1) transformation. Differential expression analysis was then performed using the DESeq2 package. miRNAs with a p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. The results were visualized using heatmaps and ggplot2.

Bioinformatics analysis

Transcriptomic datasets were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus using the keywords “MASH” and “NASH”. Datasets were included based on the following criteria: (1) liver transcriptomic data, and (2) at least two samples per group to ensure compatibility with downstream differential expression analysis using the DESeq2 package. The datasets used in this study included GSE206338 (control = 2, MASH = 2) and GSE260666 (control = 6, MASH = 4). The diagnosis of MASH in these datasets was based on histological evaluation, including the presence of hepatic steatosis, lobular inflammation, and hepatocellular ballooning, as defined in the original study annotations provided in GEO.

Raw expression data were preprocessed using log2(x + 1) transformation for normalization. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package in R (version 4.4.1). Genes with an absolute log2fold change > 1 and a p-value < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was subsequently conducted using the clusterProfiler package based on the identified DEGs. Enriched pathways were ranked according to gene counts and statistical significance. The top 10 enriched pathways were visualized using the ggplot2 package. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and pathways with an adjusted p-value (p.adjust) < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was also performed using the clusterProfiler package to evaluate coordinated changes in predefined gene sets. GSEA results were visualized using ridge plots generated with the enrichplot package.

To reduce scale differences among datasets, selected expression data were normalized using a Log2(x + 1) transformation before downstream analyses. Correlation analyses were conducted using the Xiantao Academic online platform (https://www.xiantaozi.com/), which provides standardized statistical analysis and visualization tools.

Heatmaps were generated using the pheatmap package, and volcano plots were produced using the ggplot2 and ggpubr packages to visualize differentially expressed genes.

Transfection

BMDMs were transfected with the pEF-Flag-NICD plasmid, and hepatocytes were transfected with pHAGE-TIPE2 plasmid using the Lipo8000 transfection reagent (Cat# C0533, Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

The miR-142a-3p mimic and its negative control (NC), as well as the miR-142a-3p inhibitor and its NC, were obtained from RiboBio (Guangzhou, China). Hepatocytes were transfected with either 50 nmol/L miRNA mimic or 100 nmol/L miRNA inhibitor, along with their respective NC, using the Lipo8000 transfection reagent.

In vivo delivery of NICD-overexpressed exos

BMDMs were isolated from C57BL/6 J mice and transfected with pEF-Fag-NICD or control plasmids. After 48 h, the cells were treated with LPS (100 ng/mL) and PA (250 μM) for an additional 12 h. Exos were isolated and quantified using the BCA assay. Exos (200 μg/mouse) were then injected via the tail vein into mice fed an MCD diet for 1 week, followed by an additional week of MCD feeding.

Quantitative RT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent. cDNA was synthesized using either the miRNA 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Cat# MR201, Vazyme) for miRNA or the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Cat# RR902, Takara) for mRNA, following the manufacturer’s protocols. The expression levels of miRNA and mRNA were quantified using a real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, CA, USA) with SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Cat# Q712, Vazyme). β-actin and U6 served as internal controls. Data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Western blot

Cells or tissues were lysed with RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Following protein quantification using the BCA assay (Cat# P0010S, Beyotime), proteins were separated by gel electrophoresis and then transferred to membranes for blotting. The protein bands were visualized and analyzed using a gel imaging system (Peiqing, Shanghai, China). The antibodies used in this study are listed in Table S2.

Luciferase reporter assay

The wild-type (WT) and mutated (MT) sequences containing miR-142a-3p binding sites were amplified by PCR and cloned into the pmirGLO vector (Cat# E1330, Promega) to construct luciferase reporter plasmids. HEK293T cells at 70% confluence were then co-transfected with 100 ng of either WT or MT vectors and 50 nM miR-142a-3p using the Lipo8000 transfection reagent. After 48 h, cells were harvested, and firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Cat# DL101, Vazyme). Data were normalized by calculating the ratio of firefly luciferase activity to Renilla luciferase activity.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD. Prior to statistical testing, data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk normality test. Homogeneity of variance was evaluated using the Brown-Forsythe test. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed data with equal variances; otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. Differences between multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by one-way ANOVA (the data variances were homogeneous) or Dunnett T3’s (the data variances were heterogeneous) post hoc tests. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, indicated as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 7 software.
