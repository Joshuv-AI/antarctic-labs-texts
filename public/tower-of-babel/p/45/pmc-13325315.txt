Can manual gemcitabine instillation or tumor microenvironment-based selection enhance the efficacy of TAR-200 in bladder cancer?
Authors: Lei Chen, Chaozhao Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Gemcitabine Instillation Has Potential in Enhancing the Efficacy of TAR-200 in Bladder Cancer.•Tumor Microenvironment-Based Selection Could Select Proper Population for TAR-200.•Combining instillation potentiation with biomarker-guided selection may maximize TAR-200 benefit through drug intensification and patient optimization.Keywords: TAR-200, BCG, Non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer, PD-L1

LETTER TO THE EDITOR

Daneshmand et al.¹ recently reported the phase IIb SunRISe-1 study, demonstrating the remarkable efficacy of TAR-200 in patients with bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG)-unresponsive high-risk non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) who were ineligible for or declined radical cystectomy. In this study, TAR-200 monotherapy achieved a complete response (CR) rate of 82.4% (95% CI, 72.6 to 89.8) in patients with BCG-unresponsive carcinoma in situ (CIS), exceeding rates typically observed with manual gemcitabine instillation. As noted in a related review, TAR-200 sustains a high intravesical gemcitabine concentration over an extended period, whereas manual instillation maintains elevated levels for only several hours². However, the peak concentration achieved by manual instillation is higher and earlier than that by TAR-200².

It is well established that an immediate, postoperative gemcitabine instillation following transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) aims to eradicate residual tumor cells. A higher initial drug concentration may hypothetically be more effective for this purpose. Moreover, bladder cancer cells can shed from the tumor tissue and implant into the adjacent bladder wall. Hence, we speculated that combining a single manual gemcitabine instillation with TAR-200 may speculatively offer dual advantages: an immediate high concentration to eliminate shed or residual cells, followed by a sustained therapeutic level for continuous antitumor activity. We speculatively proposed that a one-time manual gemcitabine instillation administered concurrently with TAR-200 placement, requiring no additional invasive procedure, may hypothetically improve the CR rate. We admit that this has not yet been demonstrated and requires validation. Many factors can affect the CR rate and the proposed combined therapy strategy may not show the better CR rate than TAR-200 when TAR-200 have achieved maximal efficacy in eliminating tumor cells. In this case, the added manual gemcitabine instillation will not enhance the therapeutic efficacy, and future study may focus on how to avoid drug resistance, targeting the alternative signaling pathway, etc. In addition, because the manual gemcitabine installation is completed via the catheter, which is the same as the gemcitabine releasing device, and gemcitabine instillation can be completed along with the vesical placement of the gemcitabine releasing device. Hence, there will be no extra invasive procedure for the gemcitabine instillation. This approach may speculatively leverage the rapid, high-concentration exposure from manual instillation and the prolonged, maintained exposure from TAR-200, highlighting the complementary nature of these delivery methods.

However, we acknowledge that no direct evidence supports the synergy between continuous TAR-200 delivery and the bolus gemcitabine instillation, which is a hypothesis-generating idea, and more studies are warranted to validate the efficacy. Although current evidence has demonstrated that TAR-200 elevated the gemcitabine concentration later than a manual installation, its efficacy remained satisfied². The pharmacokinetic rationale of this combination has not been clinically validated, and a future study that elucidates the pharmacokinetic characteristics of the combination therapy is essential. Hence, although a higher peak concentration after TURBT might theoretically eliminate residual surface tumor cells before continuous drug delivery, the synergistic effects should be demonstrated by orthotopic bladder cancer model studies to test whether the combination improves the tumor cytotoxicity beyond TAR-200 alone. If animal experiments demonstrate the synergistic effects of gemcitabine installation plus TAR-200, pre-clinical and randomized clinical trials are warranted to further evaluate the potential synergistic effects on patients.

In addition, the possible local adverse effects, including chemical cystitis, bladder pain, hematuria, and increased urinary frequency, etc., should not be neglected when combined with manual gemcitabine instillation with TAR-200, which was one of the reasons causing gemcitabine installation intolerance. Future studies should aim to identify the optimal dose of manual gemcitabine instillation when combined with TAR-200 to minimize the side effects and maximize the therapeutic efficacy.

The study also evaluated TAR-200 combined with the PD-1 inhibitor cetrelimab. Notably, the combination did not yield a higher CR rate than TAR-200 monotherapy. The mechanisms of BCG involve direct cytotoxicity, induction of G1/S phase arrest, and promotion of apoptosis and necrosis³. BCG also remodels the bladder tumor microenvironment (TME), inducing secretion of cytokines, chemokines, and interferons that recruit and activate immune cells⁴. Conversely, BCG may simultaneously foster an immunosuppressive microenvironment, contributing to treatment failure³. These dual effects likely depend on tumor heterogeneity and the composition of the tumor microenvironment.

Mechanisms underlying BCG failure have been explored; for instance, 25–30% of tumors exhibit high PD-L1 expression before BCG therapy, and T-cell inhibition by tumor cells is another contributing factor³. BCG stimulation can further upregulate PD-L1 expression on dendritic cells via increased IL-6, IL-10, and STAT3 signaling in dendritic cells and macrophages⁵. Additionally, high PD-L1 expression has been observed in monocytes co-cultured with bladder cancer cells³. Elevated PD-L1 on both tumor and immune cells is a key driver of T-cell exhaustion. Therefore, patients unresponsive to BCG may have a pre-existing or BCG-induced immunosuppressive microenvironment. This may partially explain why adding cetrelimab to TAR-200 did not demonstrate superior efficacy compared with TAR-200 alone. Assessing whether the tumor microenvironment is immunosuppressive may help select appropriate patients for TAR-200 plus cetrelimab combination therapy. However, we have to admit that multiple alternative mechanisms may cause the lack of cetrelimab benefit, including intrinsic resistance (e.g., lack of pre-existing tumor-specific T-cells, defective antigen presentation), drug-specific limitations (e.g., inadequate PD-1 blockade at the tumor site or off-target effects), trial design issues (e.g., small sample size, short follow-up duration, patient heterogeneity), as well as the possibility that TAR-200 monotherapy already maximizes the therapeutic efficacies, leaving little room for additive PD-1 inhibition.

Apart from PD-L1, the additional TME components, including CD8+ T-cell infiltration density, regulatory T-cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and alternative checkpoints (LAG-3, TIGIT, TIM-3), may be the potential factors that affected the efficacy. Because TME is composed of multiple components, and PD-L1 may be one of the potential factors. In addition, PD-L1 expression alone may be unlikely to be a sufficient biomarker; a multi-parameter TME-related signature that includes several relevant and important parameters would be better to predict therapeutic efficacies. Currently, with the development of tissue sequencing technologies, multiplex immunohistochemistry, and spatial transcriptomics with standardized scoring, it is not difficult to detect the levels of PD-L1, immune cells, and assess TME status. For example, before treatment, detecting the markers of specific cells, such as CD8+ T cells, as well as PD-L1, PD-1, and LAG-3, could reflect the TME status in the transurethral resection/biopsy tissues, which may assist in identifying the appropriate patients to receive additional immunotherapy.

In conclusion, the strategies proposed here are hypothesis-generating and are not yet supported by clinical evidence. We call for animal experiments, preclinical studies, and prospective clinical trials to explore the combined effects, which may speculatively maximize therapeutic outcomes. Developing a TME - related predictor may help optimize outcomes for patients receiving TAR-200 combined with cetrelimab.
