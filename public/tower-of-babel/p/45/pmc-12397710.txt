Combining curriculum learning and weakly supervised attention for enhanced thyroid nodule assessment in ultrasound imaging
Authors: Chadaporn Keatmanee, Dittapong Songsaeng, Songphon Klabwong, Yoichi Nakaguro, Alisa Kunapinun, Mongkol Ekpanyapong, Matthew N. Dailey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe accurate assessment of thyroid nodules, which are increasingly common with age and lifestyle factors, is essential for early malignancy detection. Ultrasound imaging, the primary diagnostic tool for this purpose, holds promise when paired with deep learning. However, challenges persist with small datasets, where conventional data augmentation can introduce noise and obscure essential diagnostic features. To address dataset imbalance and enhance model generalization, this study integrates curriculum learning with a weakly supervised attention network to improve diagnostic accuracy for thyroid nodule classification.MethodsThis study integrates curriculum learning with attention-guided data augmentation to improve deep learning model performance in classifying thyroid nodules. Using verified datasets from Siriraj Hospital, the model was trained progressively, beginning with simpler images and gradually incorporating more complex cases. This structured learning approach is designed to enhance the model’s diagnostic accuracy by refining its ability to distinguish benign from malignant nodules.ResultsAmong the curriculum learning schemes tested, schematic IV achieved the best results, with a precision of 100% for benign and 70% for malignant nodules, a recall of 82% for benign and 100% for malignant, and F1-scores of 90% and 83%, respectively. This structured approach improved the model’s diagnostic sensitivity and robustness.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that automated thyroid nodule assessment, supported by curriculum learning, has the potential to complement radiologists in clinical practice, enhancing diagnostic accuracy and aiding in more reliable malignancy detection.

Introduction

Thyroid nodules represent a significant clinical finding, with incidence increasing alongside age and lifestyle factors, affecting a broad demographic (1,2). Accurate evaluation is critical due to the potential malignancy in a subset of cases, necessitating prompt and effective intervention (3,4). As the primary diagnostic modality, ultrasound imaging is favored for its non-invasiveness, cost-effectiveness, and high sensitivity in delineating thyroid anatomy and detecting pathological changes (5,6). In recent years, deep learning techniques, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have gained prominence in medical image analysis, showing promise in automating the classification and detection of abnormalities across a range of imaging modalities (2,7,8).

Numerous studies conducted between 2020 and 2024 have investigated the application of deep learning in the classification of thyroid nodules. For example, Kwon et al. (9) developed CNNs for classifying thyroid nodules using ultrasonographic images, with surgical pathology serving as the reference standard. Their model exhibited high sensitivity and specificity, highlighting its potential as a valuable diagnostic aid for clinicians in distinguishing between benign and malignant nodules. Zhao et al. (10) compared CNNs with radiologists in differentiating benign and malignant thyroid nodules on CT images. The ensemble CNNs model outperformed radiologists, showcasing the potential of deep learning in enhancing diagnostic performance. Tao et al. (11) explored the use of deep learning for diagnosing suspicious thyroid nodules based on multimodal ultrasound images. Their study highlighted the model’s auxiliary role for radiologists with varying degrees of experience, indicating improved diagnostic accuracy. Zhou et al. (12) proposed an all-optical diffraction neural network for classifying and detecting thyroid nodules. Their approach achieved high accuracy, demonstrating the feasibility of using optical neural networks in medical image processing with enhanced speed and efficiency. Du et al. (13) developed a deep learning radiomics model based on ultrasound images, demonstrating its capability to objectively evaluate thyroid nodules and assist physicians in improving diagnostic levels. The study emphasized the model’s potential in reducing interobserver variability and enhancing diagnostic consistency across different experience levels. To further highlight contributions to the field of automated thyroid nodule evaluation, Das et al. (14) provided a technical review of deep learning for thyroid nodule examination, discussing the advantages and limitations of recent deep learning techniques in classification, segmentation, and detection tasks.

Despite these advancements, constructing robust deep learning models for medical applications remains challenging, primarily due to the need for high-quality annotated datasets and the variability of ultrasound images (14). These challenges primarily stem from limited dataset size and class imbalance (5). Random data augmentation is frequently applied to address this issue, yet it can prove suboptimal. As observed by Chawla et al. (15), the random augmentation may exacerbate imbalance by disproportionately increasing instances of the majority class, limiting benefits to minority classes (16). Additionally, uncontrolled transformations such as extreme rotations or flips, risk distorting diagnostic features critical to model accuracy, thereby compromising performance (4). Preservation of anatomical and contextual integrity in medical images is paramount, as inappropriate augmentations may mislead models and undermine diagnostic reliability (6,17). These constraints highlight the need for more refined augmentation strategies, such as attention mechanisms, which have demonstrated effectiveness in enhancing model generalization, particularly approaches that integrate attention mechanisms and novel architectures, which have contributed significantly to the field of automated thyroid nodule evaluation. They are detailed in the following contributions.

Zhao et al. (18) developed a deep learning-based method for detecting and classifying ultrasound images of suspicious thyroid nodules. Their computer-aided diagnosis system utilized a multiscale detection network and an attention-based classification network, achieving superior performance compared to other state-of-the-art methods and experienced radiologists. Namsena et al. (19) introduced an artificial intelligence (AI) system incorporating the Weakly Supervised Data Augmentation Network (WSDAN) for thyroid nodule assessment. Their multicenter retrospective study in Thailand showcased the model’s proficiency in discerning nuanced features on ultrasound images, leading to improved classification metrics and reduced reliance on subjective interpretation. A key component of their approach was the integration of attention mechanisms, which enabled the model to focus selectively on the most diagnostically relevant regions within the ultrasound images. This targeted focus not only improved interpretability but also enhanced the model’s ability to differentiate between benign and malignant nodules, even in cases with subtle textural differences. Attention mechanisms are particularly valuable in medical imaging, as they mimic the decision-making process of experienced radiologists by prioritizing critical areas of interest while minimizing background noise. In addition, Yang et al. (20) provided a comprehensive review of deep learning applications in thyroid imaging, highlighting the integration of attention mechanisms and multimodal data to enhance diagnostic accuracy. Their findings underscore the potential of deep learning models to match or surpass expert radiologists in certain diagnostic tasks.

In addition to deep learning and attention mechanisms, curriculum learning has emerged as a promising strategy to further enhance model performance and generalization. Recent studies have explored the integration of curriculum learning within medical imaging tasks, demonstrating its potential to improve diagnostic accuracy by structuring the learning process from simple to complex cases. Sahiner et al. (21) discussed the challenges and strategies in applying deep learning to medical imaging and radiation therapy, emphasizing the importance of curriculum learning in addressing data scarcity and improving model generalization. Burduja and Ionescu (22) explored curriculum learning methods for training CNNs on three-dimensional (3D) medical image registration tasks. By starting with simpler training setups and gradually increasing complexity, they demonstrated improved performance in deformable image registration. Singh et al. (23) introduced a curriculum learning-based approach to train deep learning models on occluded medical images. Their method progressively introduces occlusion, allowing the model to first learn from clear images and gradually adapt to more complex, occluded scenarios, thereby enhancing diagnostic accuracy. Li et al. (24) proposed Dynamic Curriculum Learning via In-Domain Uncertainty (DCLU) for medical image classification. This approach utilizes uncertainty estimation to adaptively adjust the learning curriculum, leading to enhanced model performance on medical imaging datasets. Zhang and Qie (25) provided a comprehensive review of deep learning applications in medical imaging, highlighting the role of curriculum learning in improving model training and performance across various imaging modalities.

In response, this study employs WSDAN, as proposed by Hu et al. (26), which integrate attention mechanisms to guide the augmentation process, ensuring that the model prioritizes relevant features during training. This method improves generalization across diverse inputs, crucial in clinical settings where the appearance of nodules may vary considerably (4,27). Furthermore, the study applies curriculum learning, a pedagogical strategy inspired by human learning processes, wherein the model is exposed first to simpler cases before progressing to more complex examples (28-30). Applied to thyroid nodule assessment, this approach enables the model to discriminate effectively between benign and malignant nodules, thereby ensuring improved diagnostic precision and reliability (31,32).

This paper presents a comprehensive account of our methodology, experimental design, and findings, illustrating the efficacy of combining WSDAN and curriculum learning to advance diagnostic accuracy. By addressing dataset imbalance and enhancing model generalization, this approach contributes to the development of automated thyroid nodule evaluation through ultrasound imaging. We present this article in accordance with the CLEAR reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-24-2431/rc).

Methods

Dataset

This study analyzed 650 thyroid nodules (130 malignant, 520 benign) from ultrasound examinations conducted by experienced radiologists at a thyroid imaging center, utilizing high-frequency linear probes in transverse and longitudinal planes per American College of Radiology (ACR) standards. All images were archived in a picture archiving and communication system (PACS). The dataset demographics were previously reported by Arunrukthavon et al. (33). This retrospective study was approved by the institutional review board of Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University (SIRB Protocol No. 316/2566 [IRB2]). Individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived. Patient information and ultrasound images were anonymized before analysis. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Curriculum learning

Four annotators were involved in categorizing the images by difficulty level: (I) a professor-level radiologist with over 20 years of experience in head and neck ultrasound; (II) a fellow-level radiologist with 5–10 years of experience; (III) a radiology resident with 3–4 years of clinical ultrasound exposure; and (IV) a biomedical engineer (skilled trainee) with 3–4 years of experience in ultrasound image analysis, directly trained by the professor. The annotation was performed independently. In cases of uncertainty or disagreement, the professor’s annotation was considered the most reliable due to their extensive clinical experience. Although formal inter-observer variability metrics were not calculated, the annotation process incorporated informal consensus and repeated cross-validation to ensure consistency. The skilled trainee demonstrated diagnostic capability comparable to the fellow radiologist, as validated by the professor’s oversight.

Based on this annotation protocol, we developed a curriculum learning framework that ranks images by difficulty using annotator agreement. Images were categorized into four levels:

Organizing images by difficulty allows the model to learn progressively, similar to human learning, which typically starts at a beginner level and advances through intermediate to more complex stages. In deep learning, starting with less complex images and gradually progressing to more challenging cases enables the model to arrange the weights associated with features of benign and malignant nodules in a more structured and comprehensive way. As the model’s ability to discern features improves at each stage, it becomes increasingly adept at recognizing key characteristics, allowing for more accurate classification as it encounters more complex images.

Criteria for the four distinct difficulty levels

The complexity of classifying thyroid nodules as benign or malignant depends on the expertise of radiologists and evaluation standards such as ACR Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System (ACR-TIRADS) and Chinese TIRADS (C-TIRADS), which provide essential guidelines for accurately assessing nodule characteristics (34).

Criteria for classifying malignant nodules

The criteria commonly used to evaluate malignant nodules include:

❖ Echogenic foci: malignant nodules often exhibit echogenic shadows and calcifications due to irregular cell proliferation. This uneven cellular growth can lead to excess calcium formation, which disperses within the nodule. If microcalcifications are observed, the nodule is highly likely to be Malignant, as these are strongly associated with cancerous nodules.

❖ Shape: nodules that are “taller than wide” are typically classified as malignant, as this shape often indicates aggressive cellular growth within a confined area.

❖ Margin: irregular or clearly defined edges suggest that the nodule may be Malignant, as malignant cells often do not integrate well with surrounding tissue, leading to rough or uneven borders.

Based on these characteristics, if a nodule distinctly exhibits Malignant features, experts can confidently classify it as malignant, which would receive a high TIRADS score, typically TR5.

Criteria for classifying benign nodules

For nodules with the following characteristics, they are typically classified as Benign and assigned a low TIRADS score, such as TR1 or TR2:

❖ Edges: benign nodules generally have clearly defined, smooth edges, indicating uniform growth and integration with surrounding tissue.

❖ Color and composition: the color of the nodule matches other thyroid tissue or appears solid black, suggesting that it consists primarily of fluid or a cyst rather than solid tissue.

❖ Absence of calcifications: benign nodules lack the echogenic foci commonly associated with malignancy.

❖ Artifacts: benign nodules may exhibit artifacts such as a clear “comet tail” pattern, a type of reflection in fluid-filled areas that indicates the presence of liquid within the nodule. Because it lacks calcifications, the comet tail does not cast a shadow, but it does reflect light below the artifact.

If the nodule does not clearly exhibit typical Benign or Malignant characteristics, experts may experience uncertainty and need to consider additional attributes such as color, edge, shadow, reflection, and tissue consistency. This increases complexity according to the following aspects:

White spot without shadow: observing a white spot without a shadow can make it difficult to determine if it’s a microcalcification or merely a reflection from certain angles.

Nodules that fall within TIRADS scores TR3 and TR4 are typically the most challenging for experts to classify as benign or malignant. These nodules often possess both benign and malignant characteristics, requiring a more nuanced evaluation, which significantly increases diagnostic complexity. Deep learning techniques

The WSDAN (26) warrants closer examination due to its crucial role in classifying fine-grained features in this study. This approach is particularly valuable in medical image analysis, where subtle features may indicate specific conditions (27,31,35,36). WSDAN utilizes attention-guided data augmentation during training, enabling the model to generalize effectively across varying input conditions. This is crucial for managing real-world scenarios where object appearances can differ. An attention map Ak is randomly chosen to guide the data augmentation process and is normalized as the i-th augmentation map Ak∗∈RH×W, as illustrated in Eq. [1].

The normalization of Ak helps reduce the influence of individual samples on the overall dataset. Following this, attention cropping and attention dropping techniques are applied to enhance the robustness and effectiveness of data augmentation.

Attention cropping focuses on isolating the feature regions identified by the deep neural networks, enabling the extraction of more detailed local features. Specifically, for each element Ak∗(i,j), if its value exceeds the threshold θc∈[0,1], the corresponding value in the crop mask Ck(i,j), is set to 1; otherwise, it is set to 0. A bounding Bk is then generated to encompass the area defined by the crop mask Ck, expanding the selected region from the original image to serve as input data. Results

Implementation details

The proposed model and comparison algorithms were tested under controlled experimental conditions. All evaluations were performed using a Tesla P100 GPU, which supports FP16 and FP32 CUDA cores for efficient computation. The implementation relied on open-source software libraries, including Python 3.10, PyTorch 2.4.1, Torchvision 0.19.1, and Torchaudio 2.4.1. The hyperparameters for the proposed model and comparison algorithms are image size: 224×224, batch size: 10, pretraining dataset: ImageNet, and epochs: 300.

Comparison with the state-of-the-art methods

We selected four state-of-the-art CNN-based methods commonly used in recent thyroid nodule classification studies from ultrasound images (20,38-41) to provide a comprehensive comparison with our proposed approach, as illustrated in Figure 9. All models were implemented in PyTorch and represent notable advancements in lightweight and high-performance image classification. Discussion

The assessment of WSDAN across four curriculum learning schematics offers valuable insights into optimizing model performance for small and imbalanced datasets. Notably, curriculum learning schematic IV outperformed other approaches, indicating that a structured progression from easy to challenging images enhances the model’s feature extraction and classification capabilities (24,28,29). In contrast, schematic III yielded less favorable results than traditional single-stage training, suggesting that consecutive exposure to varied difficulty levels may hinder effective knowledge assimilation. This highlights the importance of designing curriculum learning strategies that enhance deep neural networks’ adaptability and accuracy (22,23).

The superior performance of schematic IV over schematic III can be attributed to its cumulative learning strategy, which enables repeated exposure to previously learned “easier” datasets alongside increasingly difficult examples. In contrast, schematic III does not revisit prior stages, which may cause the model to forget foundational patterns essential for recognizing complex diagnostic cues. This mimics human learning, where basic principles learned early often support higher-level reasoning. By retaining easier examples at each stage, schematic IV reinforces core features that enhance model stability and generalization throughout training.

The success of schematic IV supports the hypothesis that a well-structured curriculum, informed by annotator agreement, systematically enhances model generalization by improving feature prioritization. These results suggest that progressive, difficulty-based learning may offer significant advantages for clinical image classification. From a clinical perspective, this finding could inform radiologist training strategies, encouraging the use of mixed difficulty cases, ensuring basic diagnostic patterns are not lost in advanced training stages.

Nonetheless, this study has several limitations. The dataset is relatively small and unbalanced across difficulty levels. Specifically, the scarcity of “very hard” cases could limit the model’s ability to generalize and increase susceptibility to overfitting. In our implementation, we addressed this by reducing the number of easier cases in the later stages to balance exposure. Moreover, the model was evaluated only on internal data; future work should validate its performance on external datasets from different institutions to assess its broader applicability.

Future research should further refine curriculum learning frameworks and explore advanced attention mechanisms to enhance robustness and feature extraction across diverse clinical contexts (26,35). Expanding the dataset and validating across populations will also be essential to strengthen the clinical utility of curriculum-based diagnostic AI.

Conclusions

The integration of WSDAN’s attention mechanism with the proposed curriculum learning strategy has demonstrated significant improvements in the model’s ability to capture discriminative features, enhancing its differentiation between benign and malignant thyroid nodules. This attention-guided approach directs the model to prioritize relevant regions within ultrasound images, ensuring that key diagnostic features are emphasized during training. Additionally, the structured progression through multiple curricula strengthens the model’s capacity to assimilate increasingly complex data, contributing to improved diagnostic accuracy and reliability.

Future work will focus on refining curriculum learning strategies, particularly by investigating advanced methods for dataset partitioning and difficulty grading. The impact of various attention mechanisms within the WSDAN framework will also be explored to further enhance feature extraction and model generalization. Expanding the dataset to include a broader range of ultrasound images from diverse populations and healthcare settings will be essential to bolster the model’s robustness and clinical applicability across varied environments.
