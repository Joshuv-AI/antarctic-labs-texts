A case of surgical excision of cyanoacrylate-filled vein due to delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction following cyanoacrylate closure for lower-extremity varicose veins
Authors: Tomomi Nawa, Toshio Takayama, Masayuki Hirokawa, Katsuyuki Hoshina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypersensitivity reaction (HSR) is a known adverse event after cyanoacrylate closure (CAC) for varicose veins, and surgical excision is rarely required. We report the case of surgical excision of a cyanoacrylate-filled vein due to HSR after CAC. A 68-year-old woman developed erythema and swelling of the thigh 5 days after CAC of the great saphenous vein. Symptoms persisted despite antiallergic medication, and HSR was clinically suspected. At the patient’s request, surgical excision of the treated vein was performed. Histopathological examination showed dense infiltration of CD4-positive T cells. Surgical excision may be considered in selected cases of refractory HSR after CAC.Keywords: Cyanoacrylate closure, Hypersensitivity reaction, LTT

Hypersensitivity reaction (HSR) is a known adverse event following cyanoacrylate closure (CAC) for varicose veins. Typical symptoms include erythema, swelling, pain, and pruritus along the treated vein.1 Because HSR is a type IV allergic response, oral corticosteroids are generally recommended, and surgical removal is rarely required. We report a case of HSR after CAC in which the cyanoacrylate (CA)-filled vein was surgically excised because antihistamines were ineffective. HSR was diagnosed clinically despite a negative lymphocyte transformation test (LTT).

Case report

A 68-year-old woman underwent CAC of the great saphenous vein (GSV) at another institution. Five days later, she developed erythema and swelling of the thigh. On postoperative day 16, she experienced generalized urticaria and pruritus. Although treated with an antihistamine (bepotastine besilate) and an H2-receptor antagonist (famotidine) by a local dermatologist, her symptoms persisted. She revisited the original hospital and was instructed to continue the same medications. Because the urticaria did not improve, she visited our clinic 36 days after the procedure.

At presentation, no visible rash was noted on any part of the body, including the previously affected thigh. Ultrasound guidance showed complete occlusion of the right femoral GSV due to CA, which allowed clear identification of the treated segment. The LTT was negative.

Loratadine and famotidine were continued, but the generalized pruritus did not improve. We recommended oral steroids; however, the patient declined because of her pre-existing mild renal dysfunction, osteoporosis, and reluctance to retain a foreign substance in her body. Because she insisted on surgical treatment, we planned a CA-filled GSV excision 2 months after the initial procedure.

Surgical findings

The procedure was performed under local anesthesia, which was also used for hydrodissection. Four small skin incisions were made, and the proximal and distal stumps of the GSV were secured several centimeters away from the CA-filled segment. The CA-filled GSV was carefully removed en bloc without fragmentation. There was little adhesion, and perivenous dissection was relatively easy. Operative time was 74 minutes, and the resected vein measured approximately 40 cm in length (Fig 1).

The cyanoacrylate-filled great saphenous vein was removed through four small skin incisions.

Pathological findings

Histopathological examination revealed severe inflammatory cell infiltration involving the entire circumference of the venous wall, predominantly consisting of T lymphocytes, with a slight dominance of CD4-positive T lymphocytes over CD8-positive cells. CD68-positive macrophages and granuloma formation accompanied by a foreign body reaction were almost exclusively found in the intimal layer. Eosinophilic material, consistent with CA, remained in the vascular lumen. Intimal thickening and fibrin deposition were also noted (Fig 2).

Postoperative course

Compression therapy with elastic stockings was applied for 1 month. Urticaria recurred when antihistamines were discontinued, requiring temporary resumption. Four months after surgery, antiallergic medication was discontinued again, and the patient has remained symptom-free.

Discussion

Although CAC is a minimally invasive technique, phlebitis is one of the possible complications.2 Symptoms such as erythema, edema, pain, and pruritus along the treated vein have been reported a few days to 2 weeks postoperatively.

Historically, such reactions have been regarded as simple phlebitis. However, distinct features—including the delayed onset, positive CA patch tests, and the presence of T4 lymphocytes in the excised specimens—have led to their current classification as a HSR, specifically a type IV delayed-type allergy.1,3 The reported incidence of HSR after CAC ranges from 5.6% to 13.3% worldwide,4 whereas a large-scale questionnaire survey in Japan reported an incidence of 6.8%.5

The diagnosis of HSR is based on clinical symptoms, skin lesions, LTT, and histopathological examination. The most significant clinical symptom is a pruritic wheal. Although LTT can detect allergies to the glue and its additives, its sensitivity is low (60%-70%), and false-negative results are common, especially in the early stages.6,7 Histopathologically, the presence of inflammatory infiltration dominated by T lymphocytes confirms HSR, but this requires excision of the treated vein. In our case, LTT was negative, and HSR was diagnosed clinically based on chronological course and skin manifestations, with histopathological confirmation. Therefore, HSR should primarily be diagnosed clinically, with immunological tests serving only as supportive tools.

In this case, inflammatory infiltration was predominantly composed of CD4-positive lymphocytes in the intimal and medial layers, whereas CD68-positive macrophages were sparse and limited to the intimal layer. This differs from previous reports of late-phase lesions after CAC, in which macrophages were widely distributed from the lumen to the adventitia.8 First, the limited macrophage infiltration may be explained by the earlier timing of resection at 10 weeks after CAC.9 Second, inflammation was relatively localized compared with previous reports. Clinically, this case was mild and managed surgically based on patient’s preference, which may have contributed to the limited distribution.

Treatment for HSR follows a step-up approach. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and antihistamines are the first-line treatments, whereas systemic steroids are indicated for persistent or severe symptoms.4 Surgical treatment may be required for some severe, refractory cases only; in fact, direct excision of the affected vein after CAC-related HSR is extremely rare. According to a large-scale Japanese survey, only four out of 24,209 CAC procedures (0.017%) required vein resection due to localized or systemic HSR.5

In the present case, the patient opted for surgical excision because of coexisting medical issues and anxiety about the initial procedure, resulting in refusal of oral steroids. Her postoperative course was favorable, and she remained symptom-free after discontinuing antihistamines 4 months after surgery. This suggests that surgical excision may be an alternative treatment option for CAC-related HSR when systemic corticosteroids cannot be administered. In addition, prolonged symptoms may negatively affect patient trust and satisfaction; therefore, early consideration of systemic corticosteroids may be reasonable when HSR is clinically suspected. This approach is also pathophysiologically supported, as HSR is primarily a type IV (cell-mediated) reaction, for which corticosteroid therapy is generally considered effective.

Conclusions

We report a case of HSR after CAC that required excision of the CA-filled vein. Although oral steroid administration remains the first-line treatment for HSR, excision of the CA-filled vein may be a viable treatment option when oral steroids cannot be used.

The patient provided written informed consent for publication of this report and images. Institutional review board approval was not required for this single case report.
