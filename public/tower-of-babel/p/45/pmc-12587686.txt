Antimicrobial resistance in raw cow’s milk in Bwegera, Democratic Republic of the Congo: implications for food safety
Authors: Honoré Irenge Mitima, Lameck Zigashane Kawala, Jean-Baptiste Imani Kajiramugabi, Dominique Aganze Mulume, Jean-Roger Byumanine Ntabaza, Patrick Bazika Byemba, Daniella Ashuza Kagayo, Devote Sifa Byadunia, Théophile Mitima Kashosi, Fideline Nabintu Tchombe, Ghislaine Teganyi Baciyunjuze, Didier Mugisho Nyambwe, Orthence Kazige Kanyere, Adolphe Mweze Kwakanaba, Patrick Mafikiri Bakwanamaha, Marc Nyamugabo Ntavuna, Mushakulwa Waziri, Billy Paul Kaishusha, Louisette Wimba Kayange
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntibiotic resistance poses a significant threat to global food safety, particularly in unpasteurised dairy products. In the locality of Bwegera (South Kivu, DRC), livestock management practices promote the emergence of resistant bacterial strains, constituting a risk to public health.ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess the resistance profile of enteropathogenic bacteria isolated from raw cow’s milk in Bwegera, and to identify farming practices associated with this antibiotic resistance.MethodsA total of 144 milk samples were collected from 12 farms and analyzed following the standards of the International Organization for Standardization and the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute / European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing to identify bacteria belonging to the Enterobacteriaceae family and to determine their susceptibility to various antibiotics.ResultsThe analysis revealed a high prevalence of Escherichia coli (52.8%), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae (10.4%) and Citrobacter diversus (10.4%). Resistance was notably high for tetracyclines and vancomycin, affecting up to 95% of isolates, while amikacin remained fully effective (100% of isolates susceptible). Farming practices were characterised by frequent self-medication (83.3%) and a lack of awareness regarding antibiotic resistance (66.7%). Three farmer profiles were identified: one well-trained group misusing antibiotics, and another using antibiotics as feed additives. The majority of samples exhibited polymicrobial contamination (71.5%), with microbial loads often exceeding 300 CFU/mL.ConclusionInadequate sanitary and pharmaceutical practices foster resistant bacterial strains. Improved farmer education, stricter antibiotic regulations, and alternative interventions such as probiotics are needed within a ‘One Health’ framework.

Introduction

Dairy products are key contributors to global nutrition and health, providing essential nutrients such as calcium, high-quality proteins, vitamins A and B12, and minerals vital for proper physiological function [1]. These nutrients are important for bone development, prevention of osteoporosis, and overall metabolic health [2, 3]. Despite their nutritional value, dairy products are biologically perishable and highly susceptible to contamination by pathogenic microorganisms, posing a significant public health risk.

A major concern in this context is antibiotic resistance (ABR) the ability of bacteria to survive and multiply despite exposure to antibiotics that would normally kill them or inhibit their growth [4]. ABR threatens food safety and public health by making bacterial infections harder to treat, leading to increased illness and mortality. According to a 2021 study conducted by the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, as presented in reports by the World Health Organization and the UN Interagency Coordination Group on Antimicrobial Resistance, at least 700,000 deaths per year are attributable to antimicrobial-resistant diseases. Without urgent, coordinated action, this toll is projected to rise to 10 million deaths per year by 2050 [4].

In many regions, including Africa, milk and dairy products can be contaminated with foodborne pathogens such as Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Listeria monocytogenes, and Salmonella spp [5–8]. Some of these bacteria exhibit multidrug resistance, such as extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing E. coli, complicating treatment options [9]. In Africa, weak regulation of antibiotic use in livestock has facilitated the emergence of resistant strains, with penicillin- and aminoglycoside-resistant S. aureus reported in up to 73% of isolates [10].

In the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), dairy production reached approximately 8,424,000 tonnes in 2020, supported by a cattle herd of over one million head in 2015, mainly low-yield indigenous breeds [11]. In eastern provinces such as South Kivu and Tanganyika, dairy farming though economically promising remains largely artisanal, with milk processed and sold through informal value chains [11]. Studies of 302 milk samples from local producers, collectors, and vendors have revealed widespread contamination by Salmonella spp. and Staphylococcus spp., largely linked to inadequate infrastructure, insufficient hygiene practices, and lack of formal training [12, 13].

The misuse of antibiotics in livestock production further exacerbates the ABR problem in South Kivu, where rural farmers often administer antibiotics without veterinary guidance. This promotes the spread of resistant bacteria throughout the dairy supply chain [12]. Food safety risks are heightened in communities facing food insecurity and lacking proper sanitation, where consumption of raw or poorly stored milk is common [11, 14, 15]. Vulnerable groups such as children, the elderly, and immunocompromised individuals are particularly at risk of severe illness [7, 8].

The Bwegera region in South Kivu illustrates these challenges well. Dairy farming there provides both nutrition and income for rural households, but limited animal health management, informal antibiotic use, and insufficient regulatory oversight create favorable conditions for the emergence of resistant pathogens [11]. Given that raw milk is often consumed with minimal or no processing, it represents a direct route for transmitting these bacteria to humans [16]. Despite the seriousness of this issue, very few studies have examined the prevalence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria in dairy products in the DRC, highlighting an urgent need for targeted research and intervention.

This study therefore aims to fill this gap by examining the antibiotic resistance profile in raw cow’s milk produced in Bwegera. By identifying the pathogenic agents present in the milk, their susceptibility to commonly used antibiotics in the region, and in vitro efficacy, we seek to assess the potential public health risks and provide recommendations to improve food safety management and antibiotic regulation in dairy farming. The ultimate goal is to provide relevant data to support the development of effective food safety policies and sustainable veterinary practices.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area and design

This was a cross-sectional study conducted between July to October 2024 in the locality of Bwegera, located in the Kakamba groupement, Uvira Territory, within the South Kivu Province of the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC). This rural area is part of the Bafuliru chiefdom. Bwegera occupies a strategically important geographical position along National Road No. 5. It is bordered to the north by the village of Luvungi, to the south by Kisizi Kabumenge, to the east by Lake Tanganyika, and to the west by Rwiburure Kiringi. The climate is tropical, moderated by altitude and the influence of the lake, with two distinct seasons: a dry season from May to September, and a rainy season from October to April. Average temperatures range from 20 to 28 °C, creating conditions that are favourable not only for agriculture and livestock farming but also for the proliferation of pathogenic microorganisms.

The local economy is primarily based on artisanal fishing, subsistence farming, and dairy cattle farming. The latter holds particular significance in the context of this research, as traditional livestock rearing and milk processing practices, combined with environmental conditions conducive to contamination, may promote the development of pathogenic bacteria. With Z = 1.96 (corresponding to a 95% confidence interval), p = 0.5 (estimated prevalence proportion, used in the absence of prior data), and e = 0.08 (accepted margin of error, i.e. 8%), the theoretical sample size was estimated at 150 samples. However, a total of 144 samples were ultimately included in the study, due to the refusal of some farmers to provide the required samples.

sample collection

A total of 144 raw milk samples were randomly collected from 12 purposively selected farms, chosen based on the accessibility of the sites and the availability of livestock during field visits. Sample collection was carried out under strict aseptic conditions using pre-labelled sterile containers. They were collected aseptically and classified into three types: directly from teats, from milking vessels, and from storage containers. This ensured coverage of multiple contamination points in the milk handling chain.

For sample collection, the cows’ teats were carefully cleaned and disinfected using 70% ethanol, then dried with a single-use paper towel. The initial streams of milk were discarded to reduce the microbial load potentially present in the teat canal [18]. Milk was then aseptically collected into sterile jars. The samples were immediately placed in an insulated cooler and transported to the laboratory for microbial isolation and antibiotic susceptibility testing.

Laboratory analysis techniques

Bacteriological analysis of milk samples

The microbiological analyses of raw cow’s milk were conducted at the ISTM-Bukavu application laboratory, using selective media for inoculation and counting of characteristic colonies of Enterobacteriaceae, allowing for the assessment of the hygienic quality of the milk [19, 20]. Classical experimental methods, such as inoculation on selective media and the use of the API 20E identification system, were employed to rapidly identify pathogenic enterobacteria present in raw cow’s milk [20, 21].

Enumeration of total aerobic mesophilic flora

The total aerobic mesophilic flora was enumerated using Plate Count Agar (PCA) as the selective culture medium. The samples were first thoroughly homogenised using a vortex mixer for 30 s to obtain a uniform suspension. Serial decimal dilutions were then performed using sterile distilled water. For each dilution, 1 mL was spread on the surface of Petri dishes containing solidified PCA medium. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 72 h in a calibrated incubator, after which visible colonies were manually counted. The results were expressed as colony-forming units per millilitre (CFU/mL), taking into account the appropriate dilution factor [22, 23].

Isolation of Salmonella spp. in raw milk samples

The isolation of Salmonella spp. from raw milk was carried out using a non-selective pre-enrichment step in buffered peptone water, followed by selective enrichment in media such as Rappaport-Vassiliadis broth and selenite cystine broth, and subsequent inoculation onto selective Hektoen enteric agar [24]. For each sample, 25 mL of milk were inoculated into 225 mL of the corresponding enrichment broth. The cultures were incubated under controlled conditions at 37 °C for 24 h in the case of Rappaport-Vassiliadis broth. After incubation, subcultures were made onto solid Hektoen agar to isolate suspected bacterial strains [25, 26].

Isolation of other enterobacteriaceae

Following standard guidelines, including the revised ISO standards EN ISO 21528-1 and EN ISO 21528-2 by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO), we conducted two phases of isolation of Enterobacteriaceae and/or total coliforms present in raw milk samples. These procedures follow the horizontal method for the detection and enumeration of Enterobacteriaceae, frequently present in both human food and animal feed samples.

Enumeration of total coliforms in raw milk was conducted in accordance with ISO 4832:2006. Decimal dilutions were prepared using sterile buffered peptone water (ISO 6887-1), and 100 µL of each dilution was plated onto Violet Red Bile Lactose Agar (VRBL or VRBA) by surface spreading. Plates were incubated at 30 °C for 24 h to allow for the enumeration of purplish colonies (ISO 4832:2006). Decimal dilutions of raw cow’s milk (from 1:10 to 1:10⁴) were thoroughly homogenised prior to inoculation [27]. Each dilution (100 µL) was plated onto MacConkey agar and evenly spread using a sterile spreader. Cultures were incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h [28].

Due to the presence of neutral red dye, lactose-fermenting colonies (such as E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, and Citrobacter) appeared as pink to purplish-red, allowing them to be distinguished from non-lactose fermenters. Characteristic colonies were isolated (e.g. E. coli: small, pink, and flat; Citrobacter: pale pink, slow-fermenting; Enterobacter and Klebsiella: large, mucoid, and moist) and subcultured onto fresh MacConkey agar. A qualitative biochemical identification was then performed using the API 20E system for species confirmation [29, 30].

Gram staining and microscopic observation

Each bacterial isolate underwent Gram staining following the standard protocol to identify cell morphology and cell wall type (Gram-positive or Gram-negative) [31]. The sequential steps included heat fixation of smears, crystal violet staining for 1 min, mordanting with iodine solution for 1 min, decolorisation with alcohol for one second, and counterstaining with safranin for 30 s. Slides were observed under a light microscope at 100x oil immersion objective, allowing clear differentiation between Gram-positive bacteria (purple) and Gram-negative bacteria (pink) [29].

Biochemical identification of isolates

Precise identification of strains was performed using a series of standard qualitative biochemical tests. Kligler Iron Agar medium was used to assess carbohydrate fermentation and hydrogen sulfide production [29]. Simmons citrate agar evaluated the ability of bacteria to utilise citrate as the sole carbon source. The MIU medium (Motility-Indole-Urease) provided information on bacterial motility, indole production, and urease activity. Lastly, the lysine decarboxylase test detected the activity of this specific enzyme. All tests were incubated at 37 °C for 24 to 48 h before interpretation [29].

Antibiotic resistance analysis

Antibiograms were conducted using the standardized agar diffusion method recommended by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) [32]. Antibiotic discs representing five major therapeutic families were used: quinolones (ciprofloxacin), beta-lactams (amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, ceftazidime, ceftriaxone), aminoglycosides (amikacin), sulfonamides (cotrimoxazole), and tetracyclines (tetracycline). Bacterial strains were inoculated on Mueller-Hinton agar adjusted to a standard turbidity of 0.5 McFarland [33]. After antibiotic disc application and incubation at 37 °C for 18 to 24 h, inhibition zone diameters were precisely measured using a calibrated ruler. Results were interpreted according to the clinical breakpoints defined by the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) [34], classifying isolates as susceptible, intermediate, or resistant to each antibiotic tested.

Statistical analyses

Data from this study (bacterial counts, biochemical tests, and antibiograms) were entered into MS Excel 2019 and cleaned prior to analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (RStudio, version 4.1.3). Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies) were first computed to characterise microbial load, pathogen prevalence, and antibiotic resistance profiles. Farmer classification was conducted using a Hierarchical Ascending Classification (CAH) based on key variables describing farming practices, such as hygiene management, compliance with antibiotic dosages, diversity of antibiotic molecules used, and awareness of antimicrobial resistance. The resulting factor scores were subjected to hierarchical cluster analysis (Ward’s method) to group farmers into homogeneous classes, which were then characterized based on their specific practices. Chi-square tests and ANOVA were employed to validate associations between variables and compare microbial loads across groups.

The results reveal significant differences (p < 0.05) among the three farmer classes in farming practices and therapeutic treatment use. Class 1, the smallest group (N = 24), demonstrates full mastery of disease management (100%) and strong veterinary diagnostic knowledge (87.5%), alongside frequent antibiotic switching (95.8%). However, none adhere to recommended antibiotic dosages, indicating autonomous yet potentially risky medical practices. Class 2, the largest group (N = 94), shows no disease control (0%) and no diagnostic knowledge (100%), with mixed compliance to antibiotic dosing (48.9% non-compliant, 51.1% compliant) and moderate antibiotic variation (56.4%). Class 3 (N = 26) similarly lacks disease control and diagnostic knowledge (100%), but over half (53.8%) do not respect dosages, and very few (3.8%) vary antibiotics used. Disease prevalence did not differ significantly between classes (p = 0.3); mastitis was most common overall, especially in Classes 3 (42.3%) and 2 (38.3%), while enteritis and septicemia were slightly more frequent in Class 1. These profiles highlight distinct farmer categories: trained but antibiotic-risky practitioners (Class 1), empirical majority with inconsistent treatment practices (Class 2), and less engaged farmers with limited health management (Class 3).

Antibiotic use practices among farmers by class

The results of this table highlight profound and statistically significant differences (p < 0.001) between the three farmer classes regarding their antibiotic usage practices.

Class 1, although guided by veterinarians, showed 100% non-compliance with dosage, suggesting overconfidence or misapplication despite expertise. Their primary objective is disease treatment (100%), and treatments are systematically continued until the full recovery of the sick animal. Additionally, 91.7% of farmers in this class have good knowledge of antibiotics and antibiotic resistance. This class thus represents a model of rational and supervised practices, reflecting appropriate training or better access to veterinary services.

Class 2, the largest group, operates exclusively through self-medication (100%) without veterinary guidance, while mostly using oxytetracycline alone (87.2%). All treatments are also aimed at disease treatment (100%), and farmers report stopping treatment after recovery (100%). However, only a very small proportion (1.1%) are aware of the concept of AMR, indicating high vulnerability to inappropriate antibiotic use despite relatively consistent practices. This group reflects limited access to professional advice and a dependence on empirical habits.

Class 3 is quite distinct. Like class 2, its members apply antibiotic treatments without veterinary supervision, but are characterized by the exclusive use of an oxytetracycline and tylosin combination (100%). Unlike the other two classes, its antibiotic use is mainly non-therapeutic: 96.2% use antibiotics both for treatment and as feed additives, which is particularly concerning from a resistance management perspective. Moreover, nearly half of the farmers (46.2%) stop treatment before recovery, increasing the risk of resistance development. Paradoxically, however, 96.2% of farmers in this class claim to be aware of antibiotic resistance, which may reflect superficial knowledge or poor integration of this awareness into practice.

The results highlight significant differences among the three farmer classes in milking hygiene, microbial contamination, and bacterial culture types. Non-sterile container use is high overall (78.5%), especially in Class 1 (87.5%), whereas Class 3 uses sterile basins more frequently (38.5%), though this difference is not statistically significant. Container washing varies markedly, with only 50% of Class 1 farmers washing containers compared to 98.9% in Class 2 and 76.9% in Class 3 (p < 0.001), reflecting better hygiene in Classes 2 and 3. Most farmers collect milk directly (81.9%) with no difference between classes, but contamination levels differ significantly (p < 0.001): Class 3 has the highest proportion of low contamination samples (< 100 CFU/mL, 34.6%) and no intermediate contamination, while Class 1 shows a wider contamination range, including 20.8% of samples with very high contamination (> 400 CFU/mL). Class 2 mainly falls into moderate contamination levels, suggesting only partial hygiene effectiveness. Polymicrobial infections dominate overall (71.5%), particularly in Class 2 (76.6%), whereas Class 3 has more monomicrobial cultures (42.3%), indicating relatively better hygiene and less cross-contamination. Overall, despite some improvements in Class 3, milking hygiene remains insufficient, especially in Class 1, where poor practices are linked to higher contamination and frequent polymicrobial infections.

The results reveal a diversity of bacterial colony types and pathogenic species identified in the milk samples, with notable variations between the classes of farmers. Pink colonies (40.3%) and yellow colonies (38.9%) are the most frequently observed, reflecting the predominant presence of potentially enteropathogenic Gram-negative bacteria. The predominance of pink colonies in class 3 (61.5%) suggests a more pronounced or specific contamination in this group, while mucous colonies and small colonies are absent in this class but more present in classes 1 and 2, which may indicate differences in microbial profiles related to hygiene practices.

Regarding bacterial identification, Escherichia coli is by far the most prevalent species (52.8% overall), with a particularly high frequency in class 1 (79.2%). This situation could be explained by fecal contamination linked to deficient hygiene despite better knowledge of good practices in this class. The notably higher presence of Citrobacter freundii and Klebsiella pneumoniae in Class 3 suggests a different contamination profile, potentially linked to unprescribed antibiotic administration or inadequate sanitary practices. In contrast, species such as Salmonella spp. and Enterobacter aerogenes are only found in class 2, reinforcing the idea that this class is exposed to a broad range of potentially pathogenic bacteria, likely due to empirical antibiotic use and a lack of veterinary control.

Antibiotic resistance profile by farming class

The results show that antibiotic resistance profiles of bacterial isolates from milk vary significantly according to the farmer classes, highlighting several concerning trends. A very high resistance to vancomycin is observed in 95% of cases across all classes, indicating an almost complete loss of efficacy for this antibiotic. Similarly, tetracyclines exhibit an alarming resistance rate, also reaching 95%, suggesting excessive or inappropriate use of this antibiotic class within the sector. Amoxiclav, often used as a first-line treatment, shows resistance rates ranging from 60% to 90% depending on the groups, considerably reducing its therapeutic value.

The results reveal a predominance of β-lactam resistance in Class 1, which may be attributed to systematic veterinary prescriptions, notably of cephalosporins, in this group. Conversely, Class 3, characterized by more frequent self-medication, displays increased resistance to fluoroquinolones, likely linked to empirical and combined use of multiple molecules. These disparities clearly illustrate the impact of farming practices and treatment access modalities on the selection of resistant bacterial strains.

Discussion

This study in Bwegera highlights how variations in livestock management, antibiotic use, and hygiene practices directly shape contamination levels, bacterial diversity, and resistance profiles in raw milk. Three farmer profiles emerged, reflecting distinct combinations of knowledge, practices, and risk behaviours. These patterns underscore that awareness alone is insufficient to control antibiotic resistance (ABR) without systemic changes.

Antibiotic use and resistance development

Misuse of antibiotics whether through self-medication, molecule switching, use as growth promoters, or premature treatment interruption creates strong selective pressure that favours survival and multiplication of resistant bacteria. In this context, high tetracycline and vancomycin resistance (95%) likely results from prolonged, often unregulated use in livestock, consistent with reports from other African dairy systems [10, 16, 35, 36]. Even in cases where veterinary prescriptions were followed, heavy reliance on a single molecule (e.g., oxytetracycline) may accelerate resistance development. The detection of class-specific resistance profiles such as cephalosporin resistance in Class 1 and fluoroquinolone resistance in Class 3 reflects targeted selection pressures driven by farmers’ antibiotic choices. The high efficacy observed for Amikacin may be attributed to its limited use in veterinary and human medicine within the Bwegera region, suggesting a lower selective pressure for resistance development compared to more commonly used antibiotics.

Hygiene and bacterial contamination

Hygiene practices influenced bacterial diversity and prevalence but were not sufficient to eliminate contamination. Poor cleaning compliance and low sterilization rates in some farms created favourable conditions for bacterial proliferation [37, 38]. Even farms with high cleaning compliance experienced post-wash contamination, highlighting risks from recontamination during handling [39, 40]. The predominance of E. coli confirms its role as a fecal contamination indicator [41, 42], while occurrence of Salmonella spp. in specific farmer classes indicates distinct contamination routes [43]. The presence of Klebsiella pneumoniae and Citrobacter species in certain classes suggests that antibiotic driven selection may also shape microbial diversity.

One health implications

In Bwegera, where raw milk is widely consumed unpasteurized and human animal contact is frequent, irrational antibiotic use and poor hygiene create an enabling environment for ABR emergence and spread. This not only threatens animal health but also increases the risk of resistant pathogens transferring to humans, either directly through milk consumption or indirectly via environmental contamination. The findings illustrate the interconnectedness of animal husbandry practices, environmental hygiene, and public health core principles of the One Health approach.

Addressing ABR in Bwegera requires coordinated, multi-level action that bridges human, animal, and environmental health. Government authorities should strengthen regulation on veterinary antibiotic use, enforce withdrawal periods, and invest in farmer training to ensure responsible drug administration. Veterinary services and NGOs must expand outreach, improve access to diagnostics, and promote preventive measures such as vaccination and improved animal housing. At the community level, farmer groups should be mobilized to lead peer-based hygiene monitoring and encouraged to adopt pasteurization or other safe milk-handling practices. Such integrated interventions, tackling knowledge gaps, regulatory weaknesses, and structural constraints simultaneously, are essential to reducing ABR risk in rural dairy systems.

Limitations and future research

This study was limited to a cross-sectional survey of bovine farms in Bwegera, which may not capture seasonal variations in bacterial contamination and antibiotic resistance (ABR) patterns. Sampling focused on a single rural locality, limiting the generalizability of findings to other dairy systems in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Furthermore, while phenotypic resistance was assessed, molecular characterization of resistance genes and their transmission pathways was not performed, restricting our ability to identify specific genetic determinants of ABR.

Future research should extend surveillance to multiple regions and across different seasons, integrating molecular typing and whole-genome sequencing to better understand resistance mechanisms and potential zoonotic transfer. Longitudinal studies assessing the impact of targeted interventions such as farmer training, veterinary support, and regulatory enforcement on ABR prevalence in milk are also needed. Finally, interdisciplinary “One Health” studies incorporating environmental sampling, human health outcomes, and economic analyses would provide a more comprehensive understanding of ABR dynamics in rural dairy value chains.

Conclusion

This study revealed specific shortcomings in hygiene and antibiotic use in bovine farming in Bwegera, creating a tangible risk of antibiotic resistance (ABR) in milk a key component of the local diet. Limited veterinary access, insufficient farmer training, and misuse of antibiotics emerged as major drivers of resistant bacterial strains, echoing patterns documented in other sub-Saharan African dairy systems.

In line with the One Health framework promoted by the FAO, OIE, and World Health Organization (WHO), these findings call for a targeted package of interventions. Priority actions include subsidized veterinary services for smallholder farmers to improve disease diagnosis and treatment; a regulatory ban on antibiotics as feed additives; and routine ABR surveillance throughout the dairy value chain to detect and contain emerging resistance. Hygiene improvements should focus on mandatory container sterilization, prevention of post-wash contamination, and on-farm pasteurization where feasible.

Complementary measures should strengthen farmer education on rational antibiotic use, enforce veterinary oversight, and restrict over-the-counter antibiotic sales. Sustainable alternatives such as vaccination, probiotics, and improved herd management should be promoted to reduce reliance on antibiotics. Implementing these targeted, context-appropriate measures will be essential to lowering ABR rates, ensuring milk safety, and protecting both human and animal health in Bwegera and similar rural settings.
