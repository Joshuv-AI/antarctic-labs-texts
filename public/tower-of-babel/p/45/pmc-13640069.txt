Development and validation of a machine learning model for identifying liver fibrosis in MAFLD: a retrospective model development study
Authors: Chenyu Hu, Yu Qiu, Yuxin Zhong, Wei Zhou, Zhiqi Zhang, Yi Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), as the most prevalent chronic liver disease globally, is closely related to the prevalence of metabolic syndrome. Liver fibrosis is a core stage in the progression of this disease, significantly increasing the risk of liver cirrhosis, liver failure, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Early identification of high-risk patients is crucial for blocking disease progression. We developed and internally validated a machine learning model to identify MAFLD patients with VCTE-defined liver fibrosis using routinely collected clinical variables.MethodThis study selected 2728 research subjects from January 2020 to October 2025. Through the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, Boruta algorithm, and recursive feature elimination (RFE), eight key variables, namely ALT, AST, SBP, BMI, WHR, DBP, LDL-C, and GGT, were selected. Classification models employed logistic regression, decision trees, random forests, XGBoost, LightGBM, support vector machines (SVM), and artificial neural networks (ANN). Nomogram and SHapley additive exPlanations (SHAP) were used to explain the constructed model.ResultsThis study included 2728 patients with 1013 cases (38%) of liver fibrosis. XGBoost, LightGBM, and Random Forest demonstrated broadly comparable performance.ConclusionThe proposed machine learning model has clinical application potential in identifying liver fibrosis defined by VCTE in patients with MAFLD. External validation and prospective clinical evaluation are required before routine clinical implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-026-03638-3.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) refers to liver steatosis, a condition characterized by the presence of at least one metabolic risk factor and the exclusion of other competing causes that could lead to fatty liver [1, 2]. MAFLD has become the most common chronic liver disease globally [3]. Its prevalence increases with rising rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome [4]. MAFLD encompasses the traditional non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) spectrum and highlights its strong metabolic association [5, 6]. Characterized by hepatic steatosis, the disease can progress to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), liver fibrosis, or cirrhosis, ultimately raising risks of liver failure and hepatocellular carcinoma [7, 8].

Liver fibrosis refers to the pathological repair process in the liver where excessive deposition and abnormal distribution of extracellular matrix occur due to continuous stimulation from chronic damage [9]. It represents a pivotal stage in MAFLD progression. Early identification of high-risk patients is crucial for halting disease advancement and improving prognosis [10]. While liver biopsy remains the definitive diagnostic reference, its invasiveness, high cost, sampling variability, and procedure-related risks preclude its utility for population-level screening and longitudinal monitoring [11–13]. Consequently, non-invasive diagnostic tools are a major research focus. Liver stiffness measurement (LSM) and ultrasonography enable non-invasive fibrosis assessment, yet remain limited by biological heterogeneity and suboptimal early-stage sensitivity [14, 15].

Machine learning has demonstrated significant potential in medical data mining and identification modeling. Recently, studies have applied machine learning (ML) and explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) frameworks to the assessment of MAFLD. For instance, the MORIX framework employs a similar ML and XAI process to detect the severity of MAFLD patients. This has successfully demonstrated the value of model interpretability in clinical decision-making [16]. However, these studies mainly focused on the late-stage survival outcomes of the patients. Hepatic fibrosis is a crucial turning point in the development of MAFLD, and early identification of this stage is of vital importance for preventing the progression of the disease. Currently, some studies have explored the application of machine learning in this field, such as using a support vector machine algorithm based on ultrasound image features to assess the fibrosis progression in patients with diabetic MAFLD [17], or combining serum biomarkers with machine learning frameworks for the stratification of fibrosis severity [18]. Previous similar models have mainly focused on studying the prognosis and mortality prediction of MAFLD. Currently, there is still a lack of specialized research on using easily accessible multi-dimensional clinical data (including metabolic, anthropometric and lifestyle indicators) for the early identification of fibrosis in MAFLD.

In this context, this study conducted a retrospective cross-sectional analysis using a clinical database and constructed a machine learning model for identifying liver fibrosis in MAFLD. Since the established ML-XAI process is a standard practice, this study focused on clinical incremental transformation applications, concentrating on the early identification of liver fibrosis in MAFLD. And the application of SHAP analysis and nomogram techniques helps to clarify the contribution of each identification feature to the model output.

Method

Research population

This study was a single-center retrospective cohort study that continuously included patients diagnosed with MAFLD who visited the liver disease outpatient and inpatient departments of Chongqing Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine from January 2020 to October 2025. Diagnosis strictly adhered to the Chinese Consensus on Management of MAFLD (2023 Edition) criteria, with validation via Vibration-Controlled Transient Elastography (VCTE) (Controlled Attenuation Parameter (CAP) ≥ 238 dB/m) [19].

Exclusion criteria for this study encompassed: (1) Concurrent known chronic liver diseases—including viral hepatitis, drug-induced liver injury, autoimmune liver diseases, and alcohol-related liver disease (defined as alcohol intake > 20 g/day for females and > 30 g/day for males); (2) Absence of critical VCTE measurements (both CAP and LSM); (3)CAP value < 238 dB/m and not meeting the imaging inclusion criteria for MAFLD; (4) The missing rate of key variables related to the research (such as laboratory indicators and anthropometric data) exceeded 30%.

Study outcomes were defined as metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease with significant fibrosis determined by vibration-controlled transient elastography-measured liver stiffness values exceeding 7.4 kPa. It is a widely validated threshold derived from large-scale meta-analyses and recommended by the AASLD/EASL guidelines [20, 21]. This non-invasive assessment method is consistent with the recent research results, indicating that the liver stiffness measurement values are comparable to the histopathological fibrosis staging obtained from clinical studies [22]. To ensure the reliability of LSM values, all VCTE examinations were performed by experienced operators following standardized protocols. Only measurements with at least 10 valid acquisitions and an interquartile range/median (IQR/M) ratio of less than 30% were considered valid and included in the analysis. All eligible participants were consequently enrolled in the final retrospective cohort, forming the basis for developing and validating machine learning algorithms. The process is shown in Fig. 1.

Data sources and quality

This study constructed a multidimensional dataset based on the electronic medical record system. The dataset comprised demographics, medical history, laboratory results, patient-reported symptoms, and lifestyle factors. This research implemented a strict multi-level quality control process. The data was collected by two experienced liver disease doctors based on standardized procedures. Two other researchers independently verified the input data. Any discrepancies that arose during the review or verification process would be resolved by a third senior doctor in consultation with the research team.

All continuous variables were modeled by retaining their original data rounded to two decimal places. Categorical variables defined based on established clinical thresholds or standardized questionnaire assessments. For instance, the pace of lifestyle is classified according to the level of daily busyness, while clinical symptoms are graded based on the severity as reported by the patients.

Data preprocessing and dataset division

This study carried out the processing of missing values, identification and handling of outliers, data standardization, and dataset division on the original clinical data. Missing data patterns and mechanisms were assessed using visualizations (missingness matrix) and Little’s MCAR test. Variables with a missing rate exceeding 30% were excluded (Fig. 2). The dataset was first split into a training set and a validation set in an 80:20 ratio using stratified partitioning. The Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) imputation model was then fitted exclusively on the training set. This fitted model was subsequently applied to impute missing values in both the training and validation sets. For data outliers, we initially identify statistical outliers using the three-standard-deviation (3σ) principle. Subsequently, experienced clinical personnel review each of the measured values marked, retaining those that are confirmed as clinically true pathological manifestations, while deleting those that reflect measurement errors. All categorical variables were converted to factor type. Continuous variables were standardized using Z-score normalization, with standardization parameters estimated only from the training set. Categorical variables are divided into nominal variables and ordinal variables. The various categories of nominal variables do not have inherent size or order relationships, so one-hot encoding is adopted. The encoding strategy for ordinal variables varies depending on the model. In tree-based models (XGBoost, LightGBM), ordinal encoding is used. This is because tree models achieve node splitting through feature sorting and threshold division. Ordinal encoding naturally retains the inherent monotonic increasing or decreasing order in ordinal variables, guiding the model to make the optimal split. Nominal variables are encoded using one-hot encoding in logistic regression, ANN, and SVM. The mathematical basis of these three types of models is highly dependent on algebraic operations of numerical values. One-hot encoding expands them into independent binary features, allowing the model to learn independent weights for each level, thereby effectively avoiding the erroneous statistical assumption of equidistant intervals.

Model development and evaluation

This study uses Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, the Boruta algorithm, and recursive feature elimination (RFE) for systematic variable selection. All feature selection procedures were performed exclusively on the training set. LASSO regression utilized penalized least squares for variable shrinkage and determined the retained variables by selecting the optimal regularization parameter lambda within one standard error range via 10-fold cross-validation [23]. The Boruta algorithm, serving as an all-relevant feature selection method based on random forest importance, confirmed the statistical significance of variables by comparing original features with shadow features [24, 25]. RFE was combined with 5-fold cross-validation using the area under the curve as the evaluation metric to iteratively remove redundant features and search for the optimal feature subset [26]. Finally, the union of variables identified by the three methods was compiled as the candidate pool, which was then judiciously screened and refined by integrating clinical expertise to ascertain the final feature set. When experts select variables, they prioritize mechanism variables first, and then consider time variables.

In the model construction phase, this study systematically developed seven types of classification models, including logistic regression, decision tree, random forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, support vector machine (SVM), and artificial neural networks (ANN). All models employed a grid search strategy combined with 5-fold cross-validation, and hyperparameter tuning was conducted only within training-set cross-validation. Specifically, the training data were split into 5 stratified folds; 4 folds were used for training and 1 for validation in each iteration, and hyperparameters were selected based on the highest mean AUC. Regarding the constructed models, this study conducted a comprehensive and in-depth comparative evaluation across three dimensions, including discrimination capability, calibration ability, and clinical utility. The assessment of discrimination capability not only covered the calculation of the AUC and its 95% confidence interval but also comprehensively measured the diagnostic effects of models on different categories and the overall diagnostic performance through multiple quantitative metrics derived from the confusion matrix, such as accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score. Among them, the AUC was calculated for its confidence interval using the Delong method, while the other indicators were calculated using the Bootstrap method. The classification threshold was uniformly set at 0.3 based on empirical tuning aimed at optimizing the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity on our specific dataset. 14All confusion matrix metrics were independently calculated in the validation set. The assessment of calibration ability is mainly conducted by drawing calibration curves (DCA) to evaluate the model’s estimated probabilities. The clinical practicability assessment is carried out by using decision curve analysis to analyze the net benefits under different threshold probabilities, in order to evaluate the practical application value of the model in clinical practice. All data fitting processes are completed within the training set, while the validation set is only used for model evaluation.

Interpretation

To address the black box limitation of complex algorithms in clinical applications and enhance model interpretability, this study employed SHAP analysis and nomogram techniques based on different model characteristics. For the logistic regression model, given the statistical interpretability of its regression coefficients, a visual nomogram was constructed to facilitate clinical application transformation. The regression coefficients were converted into an intuitive scoring system, enabling clinicians to quickly calculate and quantify the probability of prevalent liver fibrosis of individual patients by simply adding the variable scores [27, 28]. For other complex machine learning models, such as Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM, given their highly nonlinear structure and complex integration mechanisms, the SHAP framework was introduced to conduct an in-depth analysis of the model’s outputs. The average absolute SHAP value was calculated to quantify and evaluate the global importance of features, thereby accurately identifying the key variables that contribute the most to the identification of fibrosis within the feature set [29–31].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses in this study were performed independently using R (version 4.5.1), Microsoft Excel 2016, and Python (version 3.10.4). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value < 0.05. Continuous variables were first assessed for normality via the Shapiro-Wilk test. Variables conforming to a normal distribution were analyzed with Student’s t-test, expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Non-normally distributed variables were summarized using medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and compared via the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were described as numbers and compared between groups using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

Results

Basic information

This study enrolled a cohort of 2728 patients with MAFLD. A train-validation split was implemented at an 8:2 ratio, comprising 2183 cases for model training and 545 for validation. The cohort included 1715 fibrosis-negative cases (62.86%) and 1013 fibrosis-positive cases (37.14%). The median age was 48 years (IQR, 19–86), with 1924 males (71%). The FIB-4 score averaged 1.32 points (IQR 0.11–6.46).

Fibrosis-positive patients demonstrated significantly older age, elevated weight, greater BMI, and increased waistline, waist-hip ratio (WHR), and Abdominal girth compared to the fibrosis-negative group. SBP and DBP were higher within this group, while percentage body fat values exhibited differential distributions. Hepatic enzyme concentrations were uniformly elevated in the fibrosis-positive cohort. ALT, AST, GGT, and ALP activities were significantly enhanced. Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) concentrations appeared higher, while TG, TC, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) maintained comparable levels across groups. Regarding categorical variables, the fibrosis-positive group demonstrated notably higher prevalence of leisurely lifestyles but had reduced representation of balanced lifestyles. Mild sigh symptoms occurred more frequently in this group, while moderate sigh presentation occurred less often.

Other parameters, including gender distribution, socioeconomic tiers, sleep-wake rhythms, physical exertion levels, dietary habits, key digestive disturbances (incorporating hypochondriac pain, abdominal bloating, fatigue), and hematological indices (WBC, Hb, PLT), revealed no statistically significant intergroup variances (Table 1).

Construction of ML models

The AUC values for logistic regression, decision trees, random forests, XGBoost, LightGBM, support vector machines, and artificial neural networks constructed on the training set were 0.762 (95% CI: 0.740–0.783), 0.790 (95% CI: 0.768–0.810), 0.843 (95% CI: 0.825–0.859), 0.896 (95% CI: 0.882–0.909), 0.847 (95% CI: 0.829–0.863), 0.764 (95% CI: 0.740–0.785), 0.846 (95% CI: 0.829–0.862), with XGBoost yielding the highest AUC value (Fig. 4A). Final model parameters are provided in Supplementary Material 2. The ROC curve analysis of the model in the validation set showed that the XGBoost model performed with an AUC value of 0.841 (95% CI: 0.803–0.878). The Random Forest model followed closely with an AUC of 0.839 (95% CI: 0.802–0.876). The Logistic model achieved an AUC of 0.816 (95% CI: 0.779–0.855), the Decision Tree model reached 0.807 (95% CI: 0.766–0.843), The SVM model achieved an AUC of 0.808 (95% CI: 0.769–0.846), the LightGBM model reached 0.840 (95% CI: 0.803–0.876), and the ANN model recorded 0.781 (95% CI: 0.739–0.823) (Fig. 4B). The ROC curves for all models lie above the diagonal line, indicating that their discriminatory capabilities are superior to random guessing.

Model calibration and reliability

Through the systematic application of calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA), we conducted a comprehensive evaluation of the model’s reliability and clinical utility. Detailed results are presented in Fig. 5.

This study assesses the calibration performance by using calibration curves and Brier score to measure the consistency between the predicted probabilities of the model and the actual results. In the training set, the Brier score of XGBoost was at 0.135 (95% CI: 0.127–0.142). And its calibration curve shows the closest fit to the ideal diagonal. LightGBM, Random Forest, and Artificial Neural Network (ANN) also demonstrated good calibration performance, whereas Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Logistic Regression exhibited systematic risk overestimation in the high-probability range. In the validation set, tree-based ensemble models maintained excellent robustness. LightGBM, Random Forest, and XGBoost ranked top three with Brier score of 0.149, 0.151, and 0.151. In contrast, ANNs showed a significant decline in calibration performance on the validation set, while SVMs consistently exhibited the poorest calibration accuracy.

The core associated features, ranked by their degree of influence, are BMI, systolic blood pressure, waist-to-hip ratio, LDL cholesterol, and liver function indicators. When multiple of these features coexist, a high total score (approaching 160 points) sharply elevates the calculated probability of liver fibrosis to nearly 95%, whereas a low total score corresponds to a probability below 0.05%.

By comparing SHAP bee swarm plots across six models—XGBoost, LightGBM, Random Forest, Decision Tree, ANN, and SVM—we observed high consistency in feature importance rankings among tree-based models. XGBoost, LightGBM, and random forest all identified diastolic blood pressure as the most influential variable, and the SHAP values had the largest range, demonstrating a linear trend where high blood pressure significantly increases the likelihood of fibrosis. Discussion

This study developed a machine learning model, which has the potential to serve as an auxiliary tool in clinical settings for detecting the presence of liver fibrosis in patients with MAFLD. Unlike previous studies that focused on advanced prognostic indicators such as mortality rates, this research shifted the analytical focus to the early identification of liver fibrosis. Liver fibrosis is a pathological stage that may be reversible, and therefore early intervention is emphasized as being crucial. The core models constructed by XGBoost, LightGBM and Random Forest algorithms demonstrate statistical discrimination performance. The AUC values on the validation set were higher than those of the clinically recognized FIB-4 scoring system and other comparison models. Compared with simple scoring indicators such as FIB-4, it can improve sensitivity and make up for the deficiency that FIB-4 often fails to identify early fibrosis cases in clinical practice. In clinical practice, we can use FIB-4 as a preliminary screening tool, while the machine learning model is used to handle uncertain patient groups as a secondary option. This model can be implemented by automatically embedding into electronic health records (EHR), enabling the application of this framework even in primary healthcare institutions and areas with limited resources. The model showed robust results across key metrics, including accuracy, precision, sensitivity, and F1 score. Its relatively high sensitivity suggests its utility in identifying patients with liver fibrosis. Through the SHAP technique, the model exhibited the relative contribution of each feature to the model’s predictions, thereby enhancing the transparency of the model’s decision-making process. This model demonstrates similar performance on both the training set and the validation set, indicating a low risk of overfitting and stable generalization ability.

From the perspective of biological explanations, the core variables have indicated the potential biological significance of MAFLD. However, these findings have not yet been directly confirmed, so they should be regarded as observational results that generate hypotheses. This study indicates that GGT, as a sensitive indicator of oxidative stress and bile stasis, may serve as a valuable marker for the identification of liver fibrosis. ALT and GGT can both reflect the activity of liver inflammation. From the perspective of the pathophysiology of MAFLD, liver inflammation and fibrosis are closely related processes. Chronic inflammatory damage is the main driving factor for fibrosis. Although the biomarkers of the model may be mixed with inflammatory effects and do not simply reflect structural fibrosis, given that chronic inflammation is the core driving force of fibrosis, these indicators can still identify patients who urgently need clinical intervention. Notably, SHAP analysis identified blood pressure and WHR as important predictors. While this finding aligns with the “hepatic-vascular axis” hypothesis proposed by Tan [34] and suggests potential vascular-metabolic interactions, they merely reflect statistical associations within the model rather than establish a causal “liver-vascular-metabolic axis”. Such pathophysiological implications remain speculative and require further mechanistic studies.

Based on these findings, the prevention and control strategies for liver fibrosis should focus on the overall regulation of metabolic abnormalities. Managing metabolic abnormalities appears to be a fundamental aspect for achieving this goal, with lifestyle modifications serving as a primary means of such management [35–37]. Previous studies have shown that metabolic syndrome, especially central obesity, hypertension, and dyslipidemia, are associated with an increased the risk of MAFLD patients progressing to liver fibrosis and even liver cirrhosis [38–40]. This aligns with our model’s emphasis on metabolic features and suggests that the management strategies for patients with MAFLD might consider looking beyond the liver itself to address underlying metabolic disorders, potentially adopting comprehensive measures to slow disease progression and improve patient outcomes. For the high-probability individuals identified by the model, targeted lifestyle interventions could theoretically be implemented for this group as part of clinical decision support. The intervention measures guided by such models still fall under the category of hypothetical clinical application value. The actual clinical effectiveness of these measures needs to be confirmed through further prospective intervention studies.

This study, through individualized SHAP profile analysis offers potential insights for clinical decision support regarding targeted intervention. At the individual patient level, the ALT level is the most significant contributing factor in elevating the estimated probability from the baseline to a high-probability status, followed by WHR, AST, and BMI. This result indicates that for a specific individual, the abnormality of a single indicator may act as an important variable linked to the progression of the disease. These findings contribute to the early identification of individuals with a high likelihood of fibrosis (especially those with metabolic risk factors), which could theoretically inform intensified monitoring and targeted lifestyle interventions for this group, although the actual impact on clinical outcomes would require further interventional studies to confirm.

Strengths and limitations

This study developed and validated a machine learning-based liver fibrosis identification model, which is applicable to the population with MAFLD. In addition to extending the detection window period to the potentially reversible early stage, this model has the following advantages compared to previous studies.

Our advantage stems from the data foundation. Our database undergoes strict quality control and integrates data from 43 clinical dimensions, including anthropometric data, physiological indicators, liver function, lipid metabolism, socioeconomic status, and lifestyle. Compared with studies using large public databases, the self-built database can more accurately capture the complexity of the disease conditions of MAFLD patients [41, 42]. Unlike traditional scoring systems (such as FIB-4) or machine learning models mainly relying on direct liver biomarkers [43, 44], our model establishes blood flow dynamics indicators (systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure) and anthropometric fat distribution (waist-to-hip ratio) as core identifying indicators. This provides clinicians with additional screening targets.

In clinical application, this study also has certain practical benefits. Compared with traditional linear regression models and single scoring systems such as FIB-4 [45, 46], our models are specifically designed to handle complex nonlinear interactions between variables, providing a tool for dynamic and personalized probability estimation. However, although our machine learning model has improved in diagnostic accuracy, the increase in AUC compared to FIB-4 is relatively limited, and the model requires more input variables, resulting in a higher computational complexity than simple non-invasive scoring methods. The DCA of this model indicates its potential to effectively identify early fibrosis cases, and it may be able to effectively prevent excessive false-positive referral pressure on specialized clinics in the future. However, it should be noted that while DCA demonstrates the theoretical net benefit, this study lacks a prospective clinical impact assessment. The actual improvement in patient outcomes and clinical decision-making efficacy necessitate validation through future randomized controlled trials.

This study still has limitations. The main drawback is the lack of an external validation cohort. The performance of this model was evaluated solely using the internal validation set; therefore, its generalizability across different geographical regions, ethnic groups, and medical environments remains undetermined. Since this study adopted a single-center, hospital-based research design, there is a potential for selection bias. The sample of this study came from the liver disease department, which usually receives patients with more complex conditions or those seeking specific comprehensive treatments. This referral bias implies that the disease spectrum and baseline characteristics of our study sample may not represent the general metabolic fatty liver disease patient population in primary care settings. Therefore, the prevalence of fibrosis and the estimated weights of certain characteristics may be artificially distorted. Furthermore, the lack of fairness considerations and the analysis of the performance of different subgroups are also another limitation. If we do not evaluate the performance of the model in different demographic subgroups (such as age, gender, or ethnicity), we will not be able to eliminate possible algorithmic biases, which may lead to health inequalities. To narrow the gap between this single-center study and clinical application, we have formulated a plan for multi-center validation in the future. Firstly, we plan to collaborate with primary care networks and comprehensive metabolic clinics to validate this model in groups with a lower prevalence of fibrosis. This will help evaluate its performance in a broader and more realistic MAFLD population. Secondly, to address potential racial and regional bias issues, external validation will be conducted in different regions. Thirdly, given that the samples in the current specialized wards may be biased towards severe cases, future external validation will be stratified by fibrosis stage to test the model’s true performance at different levels of disease severity. In clinical practical applications, this model can be realized by automatically embedding into electronic health records (EHR). This workflow eliminates the complex manual scoring steps and can be implemented in non-specialist tertiary care centers.

This study has certain limitations in defining the reference standards for the research results. Although the liver stiffness value obtained through VCTE measurement is a widely recognized non-invasive surrogate indicator [47], compared with the histopathological diagnostic standard of liver biopsy, non-invasive indicators have inherent risks of misclassification. Additionally, possible measurement variability in VCTE, influenced by operators or transient inflammatory states, may introduce noise into the labeling of our training data. The static characteristics of the cross-sectional variable factors limit the model’s ability to capture the disease trajectory. This model is constructed based on baseline clinical and laboratory parameters obtained at a single time point. MAFLD is a dynamic disease, and the progression of fibrosis is severely affected by longitudinal changes, such as weight fluctuations, differences in blood pressure control, and the cumulative duration of metabolic stressors. These data fail to reflect the temporal variations, which limits the application value of this model in longitudinal monitoring and assessing dynamic progression. Consequently, this study lacks temporal validation, meaning the model’s ability to predict future fibrosis progression in the same cohort remains untested. Furthermore, future studies should consider conducting sensitivity analyses using alternative LSM cutoffs (e.g., higher thresholds such as 9.1 kPa or 12.5 kPa for advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis, where the confounding effect of transient inflammation is relatively minimized) to validate whether the weights of inflammatory markers like ALT and GGT remain stable. This would further clarify whether our model is truly identifying structural fibrosis or merely acute inflammatory states.

At the method level, the internal validation strategy of this study has several limitations. First, the current study relies on a single random train-validation split, without employing repeated cross-validation or bootstrap optimism correction. This approach may not adequately account for the variance and optimistic bias in performance estimates. Second, we acknowledge the potential risk of information leakage; although feature selection was strictly limited to the training set to avoid data leakage, it was carried out as a fixed step before the hyperparameter tuning loop rather than being nested within it. Given the high data dimensionality and multiple feature selection steps involved, this separated approach fails to fully mitigate information leakage, which could lead to an overestimation of the model’s generalization ability and introduce optimistic bias in performance metrics. Therefore, to rigorously evaluate the model’s stability before any further validation, future research must adopt a fully nested and repeated cross-validation approach—treating feature selection as a hyperparameter within the inner loop—and consider incorporating bootstrap optimism correction.

Some potential influencing factors that were not included, such as detailed dietary and exercise data, as well as genetic data, may further enhance the discriminatory performance of the model. Although we excluded patients with major known causes of liver diseases through strict criteria, the potential confounding effects of complex comorbidities on the identification algorithm require more detailed investigation. Additionally, The SHAP explanation reveals the statistical correlations within the model’s behavior, rather than biological causal relationships or model stability. Therefore, SHAP explanations should not be overinterpreted as causal mechanisms, but should be viewed as observations that support the hypothesis.

Conclusion

The machine learning model developed in this study has clinical potential in identifying liver fibrosis in patients with metabolic-related fatty liver disease (MAFLD) as defined by VCTE. Before its routine clinical application, external validation and prospective clinical assessment are still required. To meet these requirements, a strict stepwise multi-center validation plan should be formulated in the future, covering different ethnic groups and grassroots medical institutions. Only after successfully completing these steps can this model become a reliable tool for personalized risk management.
