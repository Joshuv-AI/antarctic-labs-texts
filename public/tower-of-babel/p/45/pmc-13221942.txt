Beyond Monotherapy: Targeting the Triad of Insulin Resistance, Hyperandrogenism and Obesity in Polycystic Ovary Syndrome
Authors: Amal Shaharyar, Shakun Chaudhary, Vivek Jha, Sanjay Kumar Bhadada, Rama Walia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most common endocrine disorder affecting women of reproductive age group. It is a heterogeneous condition with multifactorial etiology and polygenic inheritance. Most prevalent current management of PCOS involves oral contraceptive pills (OCPs) with/without antiandrogens along with lifestyle modification. Given the multifaceted pathophysiology of PCOS, addressing it with a single pharmacologic agent makes long-term management difficult. The comprehensive management of PCOS must target the clinical symptomatology as well as underlying pathophysiologic mechanisms. Obesity, which is commonly observed in PCOS, contributes significantly in the pathophysiology of PCOS by exacerbating insulin resistance (IR) and hyperandrogenism. Women with Obesity and PCOS have poorer metabolic and reproductive outcomes than nonobese women. Although OCPs are widely used as first-line pharmacologic agents, they may worsen metabolic function and have multiple adverse effects and contraindications. Metabolic dysfunction is more prevalent in women with obesity and PCOS due to the additive effect of obesity and PCOS. Hence, there is an urgent need of a multipronged approach that targets both the pathophysiology and symptomatology in women with obesity and PCOS. Although combination therapies for management of PCOS have been explored in the past, an integrated approach addressing all 3 components of obese PCOS together—namely IR, hyperandrogenism, and obesity remains underutilized. A therapeutic strategy using a combination of insulin sensitizers for IR, antiandrogens for hyperandrogenism, and antiobesity medications for weight management, along with lifestyle modification offers a more holistic approach. Such a regimen is expected to improve both metabolic and reproductive outcomes in women with obesity and PCOS while avoiding the adverse effects of hormonal therapy.Key words: PCOS, OCP, obesity, monotherapy, triad, IR, hyperandrogenism

Highlights

First comprehensive review advocating beyond monotherapy approach in obese PCOS management.

Oral contraceptive pills as monotherapy fail to address pathophysiologic triad of insulin resistance, hyperandrogenism, and obesity

Combination therapy targeting all 3 core components shows superior metabolic and reproductive outcomes

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists emerge as promising anti-obesity agents with additional metabolic benefits in polycystic ovary syndrome

Paradigm shift from symptom-driven to pathophysiology-driven treatment approach needed for long-term care

Clinical Relevance

This comprehensive review presents compelling evidence for transitioning from traditional oral contraceptive monotherapy to a multipronged therapeutic strategy in obese women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS). By systematically analyzing the limitations of current symptom-oriented approaches and the interconnected pathophysiology of insulin resistance, hyperandrogenism, and obesity, this review provides clinicians with a roadmap for comprehensive PCOS management. The proposed combination therapy approach—integrating insulin sensitizers, antiandrogens, and anti-obesity medications alongside lifestyle modification—addresses the multifactorial nature of PCOS and offers potential for improved long-term metabolic and reproductive outcomes. This paradigm shift is particularly valuable for endocrinologists and gynecologists managing the growing population of obese women with PCOS, providing evidence-based rationale for personalized, phenotype-specific treatment strategies that target disease pathophysiology rather than merely managing symptoms.

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) affects ∼11% to 13% of women worldwide, spanning from adolescence to menopause, and imposes a significant clinical and economic burden on global health systems.1 In India, the reported prevalence of PCOS ranges from 3.7% to 22.5%, reflecting considerable variation based on population characteristics and the diagnostic criteria employed.2 Although condition was first described by Stein and Leventhal3 in 1935, the exact etiology of this syndrome still remains elusive. The predominant pathophysiologic features include hyperandrogenism and insulin resistance (IR).

tHyperinsulinemia potentiates ovarian and adrenal androgen production, thereby amplifying hyperandrogenic manifestations.4 Androgen excess, in turn, promotes visceral adiposity and adipocyte dysfunction leading to IR. Thus, perpetuating a vicious cycle of worsening IR and hyperinsulinemia.5 To break this cycle, both IR and hyperandrogenism are key treatment targets.

Obesity is more common in women with PCOS and it has a bidirectional relationship with the disease. Obesity is increased in PCOS, is causal of PCOS, and exacerbates PCOS.6 Obesity acts as a connecting link between hyperandrogenism and IR aggravating a vicious cycle.

The diagnoses of PCOS have evolved over time from its first description in 1935 to laying down of Rotterdam criteria in 2003.7 The Rotterdam consensus defines PCOS by ≥2 of following: (1) oligo/anovulation, (2) clinical/biochemical hyperandrogenism, and (3) polycystic ovaries, after excluding other causes.

Despite a high prevalence of disease, condition remains under described with controversies regarding its appropriate treatment. Even after substantial improvement in understanding of pathophysiology of PCOS, its treatment remains symptom oriented. Oral contraceptive pills (OCPs), which are first line of treatment, are associated with various side effects in long term. OCPs worsen cardiometabolic profile of patient and using them in individuals who are already at risk of metabolic complications remain an irony.

Thus, PCOS is a syndrome with multiple pathogenic mechanisms, requiring a multifaceted and personalized therapeutic approach for effective management. To achieve optimal outcomes in women with obesity and PCOS, it is essential to target all 3 interconnected pathways with combination therapy simultaneously along with addressal of both the symptoms and underlying pathophysiology for additive therapeutic benefits.

Management

Lifestyle modification is regarded as the cornerstone of all interventions in PCOS especially if overweight or obese.8 Lifestyle modification reduces IR and increases the serum levels of sex-hormone-binding globulins (SHBGs) when compared with metformin.9 It also improves ovulation as well as regularize menstrual cycles.10

Current evidence does not clearly support the superiority of any specific dietary pattern in PCOS. Therefore, ESHRE 2023 recommends women with PCOS to follow a balanced diet consistent with general population guidelines for healthy eating.

A meta-analysis done by Colombo et al11 concluded that there are insufficient randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing exercise modalities in PCOS to establish with certainty whether one form of exercise is superior to another. However ESHRE 2023 recommends 150 to 300 minutes moderate or 75 to 150 minutes vigorous aerobic activity weekly, plus muscle strengthening on ≥2 nonconsecutive days to maintain health and prevent weight gain.12

Although lifestyle modification is the cornerstone of PCOS management, adherence is often challenging and sustained clinical improvement is insufficient.13 Lifestyle modification frequently needs to be combined with pharmacotherapy to fetch goals of weight loss in the management of PCOS.

Why OCP as Monotherapy Is Not Enough?

Although OCPs are the first-line pharmacologic treatment they have limited impact on the metabolic features of PCOS. Due to the multifactorial nature of PCOS a single-agent approach is unlikely to address the full spectrum of symptoms; hence, a combination strategy is necessary, tailored to target multiple pathways involved.

OCPs suppress the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis and induce withdrawal bleeding, mimicking normal cycles. Estrogen-driven increase in SHBG lowers free testosterone, reducing hyperandrogenism.

OCP common side effects include nausea, headache, cramps, breast tenderness, and altered libido often improving with time but persistent in some.14 Moreover, estrogen-driven hepatic protein synthesis increases coagulation; moreover OCPs double venous thromboembolism risk from 2–5 to 7–10/10,000 woman-years.15

Another major limitation of OCPs is negative impact on metabolic profile. A meta-analysis by Amiri et al16 found that OCPs in PCOS significantly altered lipid profiles, with a rise in high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, mainly due to estrogen-driven hepatic lipogenesis. The metabolic effects of estrogens and progestins vary, with progestin-related metabolic outcomes largely influenced by their degree of androgenic activity.17 OCP use is linked to activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, mediated by estrogen-induced upregulation of angiotensinogen synthesis, which contributes to an increased risk of hypertension.18

OCPs also affect glucose tolerance, shown in a study by Godsland et al19 where OCP was associated with increased plasma glucose, insulin, and C peptide than in controls. In addition, the estrogenic component of contraceptives plays a significant role in modulating insulin sensitivity.20 Hence, OCPs need to be prescribed with caution in patients with risk factors for metabolic syndrome, hypertension, obesity, and cardiovascular disease.

OCPs are mainly taken by adolescents and young girls because this is the most affected age group in PCOS. The long-term OCP use in this population can compromise bone mineral acquisition and quality of bone. In a 1-year study, 79 adolescent girls (age 12-18 years) using low-dose OCP exhibited smaller mean gains in bone mineral density compared with nonuser controls (107).21 The spine bone mineral density of young adult women (age 19-22 years) taking low-dose OCP remained unchanged over 5 years whereas nonusers gained 7.8%.22 HPG axis suppression induced by OCP appears to be a key factor in contributing to reduced bone accretion in adolescents. Estradiol levels tend to remain consistently within the early follicular phase range, lacking the physiologic fluctuations typically seen throughout the normal menstrual cycle.

In another interesting study by Mes-Krowinkel et al,23 OCPs use led to significant reductions in androgen levels, including total testosterone, androstenedione, dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate, and anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), ovarian volume and follicle count, but after discontinuation of OCP therapy, a rebound phenomenon was observed, characterized by a rise in mean follicle count, ovarian volume, and circulating androgen levels as well as AMH. This highlights that OCPs pharmacologically mainly suppress HPG axis, rather than correcting the underlying pathophysiology.

Another significant limitation is that OCPs are not suitable for women who are planning to conceive thus limiting its applicability across the various stages of a woman's reproductive life. Since many women with PCOS present with infertility as their primary concern, using OCPs in them is not feasible.

Therefore, despite the advantage of symptomatic control with OCPs, their possible adverse metabolic effects, risk of venous thromboembolism, paradoxical worsening of disease, and poor bone health may pose a health threat on long-term use. Hence, various other combinations of drugs along with OCPs have been explored in women with PCOS.

OCPs With Antiandrogen

Efficacy of OCP in hirsutism is around 30% to 50% as shown in various trials and increases by 10% to 15% more when combined with antiandrogens.24 The combination of OCP with antiandrogens yields superior clinical outcomes compared to OCP monotherapy. Antiandrogen monotherapies such as flutamide, finasteride, and spironolactone (SP) demonstrate greater efficacy than placebo, with comparable effectiveness among them.24,25

Classical antiandrogen used in PCOS is SP, a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist.26 It functions as an androgen receptor antagonist, partially suppresses ovarian and adrenal steroidogenesis, and inhibits key enzymes including 5α-reductase, 17-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase, and 17–20 lyase; it also increases SHBG and aromatase activity. It is also recognized for its beneficial effects on metabolic parameters, including improvements in glucose tolerance, homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), and lipid profiles.27 Pleiotropic effect of SP along with a broad safety profile makes it an optimal drug for management of PCOS.

However, one major issue with use of SP is menstrual irregularity found in 20% to 50% of PCOS and the underlying mechanism is still not clear.28 SP acts as a selective progesterone receptor (PR) modulator in estrogenic phase. At low progesterone levels, SP may exert a progesterone receptor (PR) agonist effect, impairing endometrial receptivity. During the early luteal phase, its metabolite amrinone may act as a PR antagonist in the context of rising progesterone, potentially leading to breakthrough bleeding. Additionally, SP exhibits selective estrogen receptor modulator-like properties—acting as an agonist at pituitary estrogen receptors and an antagonist at endometrial receptors—which may further contribute to menstrual irregularities.29,30

To tackle this issue of menstrual irregularity SP can be prescribed either in combination with OCP or in a cyclical form that is administering on days 5 to 25 of the menstrual cycle, which reduces the side effect of intermenstrual bleeding.28 This leads to SP free time of 5 days in which adrenal and ovarian steroidogenesis will resume, which will kick start the HPG axis.

Clinical trials directly comparing SP with other antiandrogens such as flutamide and finasteride have consistently demonstrated a modest superiority of SP in terms of efficacy. In a meta-analysis by Ganie et al,31 SP was found to be superior to finasteride as well as flutamide in idiopathic hirsutism while had similar efficacy in PCOS. Finasteride is another antiandrogen, which is used in PCOS. It is a 5α-reductase 2 inhibitor, which is found predominantly in the male genitalia and inner root sheath of hair follicles. Dutasteride inhibits both type I and type II isoenzymes of 5α-reductase. Dutasteride exhibits approximately 3-fold greater potency than finasteride in inhibiting type I 5α-reductase and is nearly 100 times more potent in inhibiting type II 5α-reductase. The type I isoenzyme is predominantly expressed in the skin, particularly within hair follicles and sebaceous glands.32 Hence, dutasteride appears to be much more effective not only for androgenic alopecia but also for hirsutism. Although there is no head-to-head trial in PCOS, but given its high efficacy in androgenic alopecia it can be used.33 Earlier, another antiandrogen, flutamide, which is a nonsteroidal antiandrogen has also been used. It acts as a selective antagonist to the androgen receptor, but it is rarely used nowadays due to risk of hepatotoxicity.34

Major limitation of antiandrogen therapy is need for effective contraception due to the potential risk of feminization of male fetal genitalia. Moreover, the role of antiandrogen agents to improve menstrual frequency and metabolic parameters remains controversial. In a meta-analysis by Ramirez et al,35 hirsutism, total testosterone, free androgen index, and dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate were reduced with OCPs plus antiandrogens but there was no effect on metabolic parameters like HOMA-IR, body mass index (BMI), and fasting blood glucose (FBG). This highlights that although combination of antiandrogen and OCPs is effective for hyperandrogenism and hirsutism, the effect on pathophysiology of PCOS is minimal.

Thus, combination of OCPs with antiandrogens relies on old concept of symptom relief with minimal impact on pathophysiology of the disease. However, in current scenario we need to shift this treatment approach to target core defect associated with this disease, ie, IR.

OCPs With Insulin Sensitizer

It is paradoxical that OCPs, which are known to negatively impact metabolic health, are routinely prescribed to women with PCOS—a population already predisposed to IR, dyslipidemia, and obesity. IR is consistently found in PCOS irrespective of BMI.36 IR is present in approximately 65% to 70% of women with PCOS, affecting 70% to 80% of those who are obese and 20% to 25% of lean individuals.37 IR predisposes these women for obesity, diabetes, metabolic complications, cardiovascular disease, and sleep apnea.

In an Indian multicentric study of 1,224 women with PCOS, obesity (43%), dyslipidemia (92%), non alcoholic fatty liver disease (33%), metabolic syndrome (25%), impaired glucose tolerance (9%), diabetes (3%), and hypertension (8%) were reported.38 Hence, insulin sensitizers (IS) are a good choice to prevent these metabolic sequelae and improve long-term outcomes.

Metformin lowers insulin, reduces ovarian androgens, and improves ovulation. It restores cycles, reduces hyperandrogenism, offers modest weight loss, improves metabolic profile, and lowers type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) risk—especially in women who are overweight or have impaired glucose tolerance PCOS.1 Various trials have explored combining OCPs with metformin to provide both symptom relief and targeting the primary defect.31,39 A meta-analysis of 32 RCTs showed metformin improved BMI, HOMA-IR, waist-to-hip ratio, fasting glucose, lipids, C-reactive protein, plasminogen activator inhibitor-1, and cycle regularity, but not hirsutism.40 In another RCT by Lord et al,41 metformin improved menstrual regularity and insulin sensitivity but no significant changes were seen in androgen levels or weight.

Triple combination consisting of OCPs with antiandrogen and IS has also been tried in a study by Ibáñez et al42 (flutamide and metformin) in nonobese young women with PCOS. The combination led to improvements in insulin sensitivity, lipid profile, androgen levels, SHBG, and favorable changes in body composition.

Although IS are primarily used to offset IR and the metabolic complications associated with OCPs, they have limited efficacy in terms of symptom control. Thus, they need to be combined with either OCPs or an antiandrogen. Furthermore, some OCPs with androgenic progestins may worsen IR, counteracting metformin’s benefits—prompting exploration of non-OCP strategies in PCOS.

Insulin Sensitizers With Antiandrogens

IR and hyperandrogenism are the driving forces for all the manifestations of PCOS so targeting them together makes the combination of IS with antiandrogen, a rationale treatment approach. In a meta-analysis of 6 RCTs by Zeng et al,43 the combination of metformin and SP was found to be more effective than monotherapy in reducing BMI, androgen levels, fasting plasma glucose, and improving IR, without an associated increase in adverse effects. Systematic review evidence by Alesi et al44 suggests that adding an antiandrogen with metformin can improve biochemical and clinical hyperandrogenism outcomes in PCOS, particularly when OCPs are contraindicated or response is suboptimal.

In adolescent girls with PCOS, a 12-month trial comparing low-dose spironolactone–pioglitazone–metformin (SPIOMET) with oral contraceptives found that SPIOMET produced greater improvements in metabolic, inflammatory, and androgenic markers, with approximately threefold more ovulations after SPIOMET than after OCP treatment, suggesting SPIOMET may offer lasting, disease-modifying benefits.45

These trials show that multipronged therapy benefits women with PCOS, but weight change is modest, underscoring the need for anti-obesity drugs, since obesity both contributes to and worsens the syndrome. Moreover, weight reduction is shown to improve reproductive and metabolic consequences of PCOS.46

A holistic pharmacotherapy approach in obese women with PCOS should combine IS, antiandrogens, and weight-loss drugs alongside sustained lifestyle modification, which remains cornerstone of management. Targeting the Triad: Insulin Sensitizer, Antiandrogens, and Antiobesity Agents in Obese PCOS

In PCOS, a complex and self-perpetuating interplay exists between IR, obesity, and hyperandrogenism, forming a pathogenic triad that underpins both metabolic and reproductive dysfunction. Women with obesity with PCOS are metabolically worse and are associated with poorer reproductive and metabolic outcomes.47 Although obesity worsens outcomes in PCOS, it also offers an advantage because it is treatable, thus making it a manageable aspect of the condition.

Thus, phenotype-based personalized therapy is central to contemporary PCOS management. Given the heterogeneity of PCOS, not all phenotypes require multitarget pharmacotherapy. The proposed triad-based approach is best suited to obese, insulin-resistant, hyperandrogenic PCOS, in whom cardiometabolic risk is greatest. In contrast, lean or norm androgenic phenotypes may be effectively managed with lifestyle intervention or selected single-agent therapy.

Reproductive intent, age, and cardiometabolic risk should guide drug selection and sequencing. In women with high metabolic risk, a multitarget approach addressing IR, hyperandrogenism, and obesity may be initiated in parallel or sequentially based on disease severity and tolerability. Several anti-obesity medications have been evaluated in PCOS, each with varying effectiveness and side effect profiles. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) are approved for both weight reduction and management of type 2 diabetes.

GLP-1 RAs represent a paradigm shift in the management of obese PCOS by directly targeting obesity and IR, key upstream drivers of hyperandrogenism. Emerging evidence demonstrates significant weight loss, improved insulin sensitivity, and favorable metabolic remodeling, positioning GLP-1 RA as disease-modifying agents rather than just an adjunctive therapy.

As GLP-1 RAs have taken center stage in the management of obesity, their role in obese PCOS women has also been explored. In 65 overweight/insulin-resistant participants, liraglutide (1.8 mg, 26 weeks) improved anthropometrics and FBG vs placebo, but not Matsuda index or lipid profile.48,49 In women with obesity with PCOS unresponsive to lifestyle changes, semaglutide achieved weight loss in ∼80%, restored menstrual regularity (weight-loss independent), and improved basal insulin, FBG, and HOMA.50 Jensterle et al51 showed semaglutide improved weight, total/low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, fasting and oral glucose tolerance test glucose, and testosterone in PCOS.

Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), including its progressive form metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis, is increasingly recognized in women with PCOS, particularly those with obesity and IR, and PCOS is independently associated with a higher prevalence of MASLD even after adjustment for BMI.52,53 Shared pathogenic mechanisms—IR, visceral adiposity, chronic inflammation, and dyslipidemia—link PCOS and MASLD and contribute to heightened cardiovascular risk.54 Importantly, therapies targeting weight loss and IR, including GLP-1 RAs, have demonstrated improvements in hepatic steatosis and liver enzymes, suggesting potential synergistic metabolic benefits in obese women with PCOS.55

Limitation to use of GLP-1 RA in women with PCOS is need for reliable contraception during therapy and to allow for a washout period before trying to conceive. Another caveat with GLP1 RA is loss of muscle mass .However, one study observed that there was no significant difference in lean body mass between treatment and control groups when liraglutide was combined with lifestyle modifications.56 This suggests that incorporation of resistance training and structured exercise alongside GLP-1 RAs can help preserve lean body mass and support long-term weight maintenance.57

Current evidence for use of GLP-1 RA in PCOS is limited. With increase in popularity of GLP-1 RA further clinical trials are essential to validate them. Given the fact that PCOS is a lifelong condition, pharmacologic treatment options targeting IR–obesity–hyperandrogenism cross-talk, represent significant therapeutic implications. PCOS, much like T2DM, is a multifactorial and heterogeneous disorder. Over the years, the therapeutic approach to T2DM has evolved from initial monotherapy with metformin to a strategy that targets multiple pathophysiologic mechanisms simultaneously. Monotherapy is no longer the standard of care. Similarly, managing PCOS with a single-drug approach is unlikely to be effective in most cases. An early, aggressive, and multitargeted intervention may be necessary to optimize long-term reproductive, metabolic, and psychological outcomes—potentially inducing a “legacy effect” in patients with PCOS akin to that observed in T2DM. Future Directions

Future research should focus on large, well-designed prospective trials to establish the long-term safety and efficacy of emerging therapies, while also elucidating them molecular and metabolic effects across diverse PCOS phenotypes. In particular, there is a critical need for data on long-term cardiovascular outcomes, given the elevated cardiometabolic risk associated with obese PCOS. Additional research should also evaluate reproductive and pregnancy-related outcomes, including fertility, gestational complications, and maternal–fetal safety with multitarget pharmacotherapy.

Ongoing challenges include optimizing drug combinations, minimizing adverse effects, and tailoring interventions to the heterogeneous phenotypes of PCOS. High-quality prospective studies are needed to determine the comparative and cost-effectiveness of polypharmacotherapy, especially in resource-limited settings, where pill burden and long-term affordability may influence adherence. Despite the availability of multiple pharmacologic options, no single regimen effectively addresses both the underlying pathophysiology and clinical manifestations of PCOS. A holistic approach combining IS, antiandrogens, and anti-obesity agents alongside lifestyle modification addresses the multifactorial nature of PCOS (Fig. 2). Future studies should also examine weight regain following discontinuation of antiobesity therapies, an important consideration that may inform the need for long-term or maintenance treatment strategies in obese women with PCOS.

Conclusion

Although oral contraceptives remain the first-line therapy for managing symptoms in women with PCOS, their long-term use is associated with unfavorable cardiovascular and metabolic risks. Since PCOS is a chronic condition spanning the entire reproductive lifespan, there is a pressing need for a safe and effective long-term treatment strategy that addresses its multifactorial pathophysiology. An integrated approach that combines lifestyle modification, anti-obesity pharmacotherapy, IS, and antiandrogens offers a more comprehensive solution in obese women with PCOS. This paradigm shift from symptomatic to pathophysiology plus symptom-driven approach may significantly enhance quality of life and mitigate long-term metabolic sequelae in women with PCOS.
