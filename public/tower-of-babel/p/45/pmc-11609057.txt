Effect of Itraconazole, a CYP3A4 Inhibitor, and Rifampin, a CYP3A4 Inducer, on the Pharmacokinetics of Vatiquinone
Authors: Lucy Lee, Martin Thoolen, Jiyuan Ma, Diksha Kaushik, Lee Golden, Ronald Kong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractVatiquinone is a small molecule inhibitor of 15‐lipoxygenase in development for patients with Friedreich's ataxia. The objective of this analysis was to determine the effect of a cytochrome P450 isoform 3A4 (CYP3A4) inhibitor and inducer on vatiquinone pharmacokinetics (PKs). The coadministration of 400 mg of vatiquinone with 200 mg of itraconazole (a CYP3A4 inhibitor) resulted in increased maximum observed concentration (Cmax) of vatiquinone and systemic exposure (AUC0‐inf) by approximately 3.5‐ and 2.9‐fold, respectively. The coadministration of 400 mg of vatiquinone with 600 mg of rifampin (a CYP3A4 inducer) resulted in decreased vatiquinone Cmax and AUC0‐inf by approximately 0.64‐ and 0.54‐fold, respectively. The terminal half‐life of vatiquinone was not affected by itraconazole or rifampin. These clinical study results confirm the in vitro reaction phenotyping data that shows that CYP3A4 plays an important role in vatiquinone metabolism. The result of this analysis together with phase 3 efficacy and safety data, population PK analysis, and the exposure‐response relationship will determine if the extent of vatiquinone changes in the presence of CYP3A4 inhibitors and inducers are considered clinically relevant.

Vatiquinone (PTC743, EPI‐743, alpha‐tocotrienol quinone) is an orally bioavailable small molecule being developed by PTC Therapeutics (PTC) for the treatment of Friedreich's ataxia and other disorders characterized by high levels of oxidative stress and dysregulation of energy metabolism. 1 Vatiquinone crosses the blood‐brain barrier, where it functions as a potent inhibitor of 15‐lipoxygenase (15‐LO), an oxidoreductase enzyme that controls the process of inflammation, oxidative stress, glutathione (GSH) depletion, and a type of cell death involving lipid peroxidation known as ferroptosis. By inhibiting 15‐LO, vatiquinone alleviates oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, thereby preventing the neuronal dysfunction and cell death (ferroptosis) that underpin the development of Friedreich's ataxia. The activity of 15‐LO is particularly enhanced in patients with diseases that are caused by oxidative stress, which ultimately leads to depletion of endogenous antioxidants such as GSH. 2 , 3 In clinical studies, vatiquinone has been found to increase GSH levels and decrease the ratio of oxidized GSH to reduced GSH (GSSG/GSH), correlating with clinical improvement in disease symptoms and arrest or reversal of disease progression. 4 , 5 The critical role of 15‐LO as a key enzyme in the biological process of ferroptosis has been implicated in a number of central nervous system diseases. 6 , 7

Clinically, vatiquinone has been studied in children and adults with Friedreich's ataxia and other disorders characterized by high levels of oxidative stress, such as Leigh syndrome. Data from these studies demonstrate a long‐term improvement in neurological and neuromuscular function in patients with Friedreich's ataxia. Clinical safety data suggest that vatiquinone is well tolerated, with no dose‐limiting toxicities reported. 8

Vatiquinone is orally administered either in capsule form or as a solution. To enhance oral bioavailability, vatiquinone should be administered with food. It is recommended that patients take vatiquinone with low‐fat meals that consist of 25% to <50% fat by caloric content. With an estimated effective half‐life of approximately 9 hours, a 3 times daily dosing regimen may be required to maintain therapeutic drug exposures. The pharmacokinetics (PKs) and pharmacodynamics (PDs) of vatiquinone have been evaluated in healthy volunteers and in phase 2/2b studies in pediatric and adult patients. Clinical studies have utilized an increase in GSH and decrease in GSSG/GSH as a “redox signature” and PD marker of 15‐LO inhibition. Targeting a dose that achieves PK exposures that maximize reduction of the GSSG/GSH ratio was a strategy that aimed to maximize vatiquinone potential clinical effect. Preliminary PK modeling indicated that body weight correlates with drug clearance. Accordingly, a body weight‐based dosing regimen was developed to allow patients across a broad age range to achieve consistent vatiquinone PK exposures.

A human mass balance and metabolism study demonstrated that hepatic elimination is the main route of drug elimination. Vatiquinone was extensively metabolized, but no major metabolite (>10% of drug‐related substance) was found in the circulation. 9 Mitochondrial disorders can be clinically diverse and therefore treated with a variety of medications for disease management. To this end, it has been important to evaluate vatiquinone both as a victim and as a perpetrator for cytochrome P450 (CYP) and transporter‐mediated drug‐drug interactions (DDIs). In vitro studies showed vatiquinone to be a weak inhibitor of CYP2B6, CYP2C8, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4/5, but not an inhibitor of CYP1A2 (data not shown). Clinical studies demonstrated a lack of clinically relevant effect of vatiquinone on CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and BCRP substrates. 10 , 11 At therapeutic doses, vatiquinone is not expected to exhibit clinically meaningful inhibition of CYP2B6, CYP2C8, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4/5.

An in vitro CYP reaction phenotyping study showed that vatiquinone metabolism was mainly mediated by CYP3A4 in human liver microsomes (PTC internal data). The objective of the current study was to evaluate the effect of a CYP3A4 inhibitor (itraconazole) and an inducer of CYP3A4 (rifampin) on the PK of vatiquinone.

Methods

Ethics

The study was conducted at QPS MRA Clinical Research, Miami, FL. The study followed the International Council on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice Guidelines and the Code of Federal Regulations. The Principal Investigator and the subinvestigators provided written commitments to conduct the study in full conformance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and in accordance with the protocol stipulations. The investigator obtained written informed consent from each individual participating in the study after fully explaining the aims, methods, and objectives of the study and potential hazards of the study drug to the participant. The investigator also explained that the participants were completely free to refuse to enter the study or to withdraw from it at any time for any reason. Appropriate forms for documenting written informed consent were provided to the site prior to the study. The protocol, amendments, and participant informed consent forms were approved by the Institutional Review Board (IntegReview Board, Austin, TX, USA) and competent authority according to national and international regulations.

Study Participants

The study enrolled male and female participants who were between 18 and 45 years of age, inclusive, with a body mass index of 18.5‐30 kg/m2. Both male and female participants agreed to use birth control for the duration of the study and for at least 1 month after the last dose. Participants were determined to be healthy based on medical history, physical examination, laboratory assessments, 12‐lead electrocardiogram (ECG), and vital signs at screening. Participants must not have participated in any other investigational trial within 28 days prior to study drug administration or have known hypersensitivity to the study drugs or any of their excipients. CYP3A4 inhibitors/inducers were prohibited 1 week prior to and during the study. Other key exclusion criteria included participants with any current condition or history of significant endocrine, hepatic, renal, hematologic, pulmonary, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, urological, immunological, or neurological disorders.

Study Design

As shown in Figure 1, this was a phase 1, 2‐part, open‐label, fixed‐sequence, single and multiple oral dose study aimed to assess the impact of a CYP3A4 inhibitor (Part 1, itraconazole) and a CYP3A4 inducer (Part 2, rifampin) on the single‐dose PKs of vatiquinone. On day 1 of each part, participants received a single dose of vatiquinone 400 mg alone, followed by a washout of 7 days. In Part 1, participants received itraconazole on days 8 through 11, and they received vatiquinone coadministered with itraconazole on day 12 followed by a last dose of itraconazole on day 13. In Part 2, participants received rifampin on days 8 through 21, and they received vatiquinone coadministered with rifampin on day 22 followed by a last dose of rifampin on day 23. Participants came to the study center on the day before the first administration of vatiquinone (day −1) for baseline assessments and to reconfirm eligibility. Blood samples for bioanalysis of vatiquinone were collected predose and at specified time points postdose. Participants returned to the clinic approximately 7 days after the last administration of vatiquinone for a follow‐up visit.

Study design schematic.

Itraconazole and rifampin doses used in this study were chosen based on the clinical tolerability of the drugs and the ability to reliably assess their PK parameters. The drugs have been on the market for many years and are used as chronic treatments for the mentioned indications. The selected dose is expected to have an adequate margin in the event of a potential PK DDI.

Vatiquinone, itraconazole, and rifampin were taken with 240 mL of water when administered alone and when coadministered. On PK sampling days, participants had an overnight fast of at least 10 hours and were not allowed to drink water/other fluids between 1 hour predose and1 hour postdose. On vatiquinone dosing days, participants consumed low‐fat meals and 8 oz of whole milk within 15 minutes followed by administration of drug within 30 minutes of completing the meals.

Bioanalytical Methods

Sample Collection: Three milliliters of human blood specimens for vatiquinone were collected in 3‐mL lithium heparin vacutainers under the protection of white light. On collection, blood vacutainers were covered with amber‐colored bags and harvested to plasma by spinning the blood samples at 1600 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C or ambient temperature. All drawn blood samples were processed and centrifuged within 45 minutes of blood collection based on the established stability of the PK assay in blood. At least 0.8 mL of plasma from each centrifuged blood sample tube was transferred, using a clean disposable pipette (1 new pipette per sample), into 2 appropriately labeled amber‐colored plasma cryovials and stored in a freezer maintained at ≤−70°C, and in an upright position, until analysis.

Bioanalysis: Method involved extraction of 50 µL of lithium heparin human plasma and 50 µL of the internal standard (IS), vatiquinone‐d4, by a protein‐precipitation extraction procedure using cold methanol across a quantitation range of 5.00‐1000 ng/mL. The mixture was vortexed and centrifuged, and the supernatant layer was transferred to a clean 1‐mL 96‐well plate where 100 µL of methanol:water (50:50, v/v) was added to all wells and the plate vortexed and centrifuged. A 5‐µL sample of the final extract was injected into a high‐performance liquid chromatography system and quantified using tandem mass spectrometry. The chromatographic separation was performed using an Acquity UPLC BEH phenyl column (1.7 µm, 2.1 × 50 mm; Waters Corporation) with water:acetonitrile:1 M ammonium acetate:acetic acid (95:5:0.2:0.2, v/v/v/v) as mobile phase A and acetonitrile:1 M ammonium acetate:acetic acid (100:0.2:0.2, v/v/v) as mobile phase B under gradient conditions.

The multiple reaction monitoring transition for ion transitions were set to m/z 423.4→165.1 and m/z 427.4→169.1 for vatiquinone and the IS (vatiquinone‐d4), respectively. Analyte‐to‐IS peak area ratios for the standards were used to obtain a linear calibration curve using 1/x2 weighted least‐squares regression analysis. Owing to the photolabile nature of vatiquinone, the method was validated in yellow light conditions (in the absence of white light). Specimens were stored deep frozen (≤−70°C) until analysis. The validation study demonstrated that the procedures for the determination of vatiquinone in human lithium heparin plasma were specific, accurate, and reproducible.

NCA PK Analysis and PK Assessments

Blood samples for vatiquinone PK analysis were taken on day 1 (vatiquinone alone), on day 12 (itraconazole concomitantly with vatiquinone), and on day 22 (rifampin concomitantly with vatiquinone), and the collection time points were 0 (predose), 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 12, 24, and 36 hours.

The PK parameters for vatiquinone were computed using Phoenix WinNonlin 6.3 or higher and employing a noncompartmental analysis of plasma concentration–time profiles based on actual blood sampling times (hours). PK parameters derived from the analysis included maximum observed concentration (Cmax), time to peak concentration (Tmax), terminal elimination rate constant (λz), terminal elimination half‐life (T1/2), and area under the concentration‐time curve from time 0 to the last measurable concentration (AUC0‐t), and from time 0 to infinity (AUC0‐inf). A regression analysis was performed on the terminal linear phase of the semilogarithmic plots of individual concentration‐time data. During the analysis, regressions using the last 3 points with non‐0 concentrations, then the last 4 points, and the last 5 points, and so forth, were repeated. Points prior to Cmax (including Cmax) were not used.

Safety Assessments

The safety set, which included participants who received the study drug and had at least 1 safety assessment postbaseline, was employed in the analysis of tolerability and safety variables. Drug safety was evaluated by predose and postdose assessments including vital signs (temperature, blood pressure, respiratory rate, and heart rate), ECGs, clinical laboratory values (chemistry, hematology, and urinalysis), physical examinations, and monitoring of adverse events (incidence, severity, and relationship). A follow‐up visit occurred 7 days after the last clinic day.

Statistical Analysis

The sample size was based on empirical considerations. For each part, a mixed‐effects model with treatment as a fixed effect and participant as a random effect was performed on the following log‐transformed PK parameters of Cmax, AUC0‐t, and AUC0‐inf. The point estimate was calculated as the geometric least‐squares mean ratio (GMR) for each PK parameter, for the test treatment (vatiquinone + itraconazole or rifampin) relative to the reference treatment (vatiquinone alone) and expressed as a percentage. Geometric least‐squares mean ratios of PK parameters and 90% confidence intervals (CIs) for GMRs were derived from the model. If 90% CIs for GMRs of PK parameters fell entirely within the equivalence range of 80.00%‐125.00%, it was concluded that there was no statistically significant DDI effect.

Safety and Tolerability

Three participants (13.04%) reported 4 treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs). There were no treatment‐emergent serious adverse events (TESAEs). Two participants had temporary clinically significant elevations in hepatic enzymes. The TEAEs were within the Gastrointestinal System Organ Class (SOC) and, for 1 participant, also within the Nervous System SOC. All TEAEs were mild and resolved by the end of the study or shortly thereafter. In the Principal Investigator's opinion, all the TEAEs were unrelated to the administration of vatiquinone, although the elevated alanine transaminase and aspartate transaminase enzymes were considered possibly related to the administration of itraconazole.

Conclusion and Discussion

In the current study, vatiquinone dose was selected based on the dose level that would achieve the target therapeutic drug exposures. Both itraconazole and rifampin are commonly used in healthy volunteer DDI studies as a CYP3A4 inhibitor and inducer, respectively. The selected dose for itraconazole was 200 mg once daily for 6 days. The T1/2 is about 18 hours, and maximum plasma concentrations are reached about 2‐5 hours after dosing. The selected dose for rifampin was 600 mg once daily for 16 days, which provides adequate time for maximum CYP3A4 induction. The T1/2 is about 3‐5 hours, and maximum plasma concentrations are reached about 2‐4 hours after dosing.

The coadministration of 400 mg of vatiquinone with 200 mg of itraconazole (a CYP3A4 inhibitor) resulted in an increase in vatiquinone Cmax and systemic exposure (AUC0‐inf) by approximately 3.5‐ and 2.9‐fold, respectively. The T1/2 of vatiquinone was unaffected by itraconazole. Similarly, the coadministration of 400 mg of vatiquinone with 600 mg of rifampin (a CYP3A4 inducer) resulted in a decrease in vatiquinone Cmax and AUC0‐inf by approximately 0.64‐ and 0.54‐fold, respectively. The T1/2 of vatiquinone was not affected by rifampin.

A human mass balance and metabolism study demonstrated that the metabolism of vatiquinone in human participants is extensive and the major metabolic pathways include reduction, oxidation/β‐oxidation, dehydrogenation, sulfation, glucuronidation, and taurine conjugation. 9 This may explain why although CYP3A4 plays a major role in vatiquinone metabolism in human liver microsomes, competitive metabolic pathways in vivo that include both phase I and phase II metabolizing enzymes resulted in a moderate vatiquinone AUC increase (<5‐fold) by itraconazole, a CYP3A4 inhibitor, and a small vatiquinone AUC decrease (<50%) by rifampin, a CYP3A4 inducer, in this study. Based on the current results, the impact of moderate CYP3A4 inhibitors and inducers on vatiquinone metabolism is expected to be low in the clinic.

The clinical study results confirm that the CYP3A4 enzyme plays an important role in vatiquinone metabolism and the effect on vatiquinone PK when coadministered with a potent CYP3A4 inhibitor or inducer. Additional work was performed to understand the impact of moderate CYP3A4 inhibitors and inducers on vatiquinone exposures via physiologically based pharmacokinetic modeling and will be presented elsewhere.

The results of this analysis together with phase 3 efficacy and safety data, population PK analysis, and the exposure‐response relationship will determine if the extent of vatiquinone changes in the presence of CYP3A4 inhibitors and inducers are considered clinically relevant.
