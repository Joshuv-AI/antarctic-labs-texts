Efficacy of anterioposterior weight-shift training with visual biofeedback for step length asymmetry and functional mobility in chronic stroke: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Jong Weon Lee, Yea Jin Jo, Yoon Su Baek, Seeun Kim, Jung Hoon Kim, Dae Hyun Kim, Jong Hyun Choi, Jong Bum Park, Deog Young Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStep length asymmetry is a specific and persistent characteristic of hemiparetic gait in patients with chronic stroke, typically resulting from impaired weight bearing and reduced propulsive force on the paretic side. This asymmetry leads to reduced walking efficiency and an increased risk of musculoskeletal complications. This study investigated the efficacy of an anterioposterior weight-shift training (AP training) system utilizing visual biofeedback to improve gait asymmetry in patients with chronic stroke.MethodsThis randomized controlled trial enrolled 34 patients with chronic hemiplegic stroke who exhibited step-length asymmetry (step-length ratio > 1.1). Participants were randomized into an AP training group or a control group. Both groups received conventional gait training for 6 weeks, and the experimental group received additional AP training sessions (30 min, 3 times/week). The AP training system provided real-time visual biofeedback on the Center of Pressure trajectory and weight distribution. The primary outcome was the Step Length Asymmetry Index (SLAI). The secondary outcomes included plantar pressure distribution, temporospatial parameters, 3D kinematic/kinetic gait analysis, and energy consumption.ResultsThe AP training group demonstrated significant improvements in the SLAI compared with the control group (p < 0.05). Significant between-group differences favoring the intervention were also observed for swing time asymmetry, forefoot contact area, and pressure (p < 0.05). Three-dimensional gait analysis revealed an increased ankle dorsiflexion angle and hip extension moment on the affected side in the training group. While the self-selected walking speed showed no significant difference, the maximum safe walking speed significantly improved in the training group (p < 0.05). No significant between-group differences were observed in energy consumption (oxygen cost or oxygen rate).ConclusionAP training may effectively improve step length asymmetry and paretic limb mechanics in patients with chronic stroke. Background

Stroke is a leading cause of long-term disability, with gait impairment being the primary contributor to reduced quality of life and community participation [1, 2]. Temporal and spatial asymmetry, specifically characterized by a shorter step length on the non-paretic side and reduced stance time on the paretic side, is a hallmark of the post-stroke hemiparetic gait [3, 4]. This asymmetry is mechanically linked to impaired forward propulsion and insufficient weight bearing on the paretic limb [5, 6]. In the chronic phase of stroke, asymmetric patterns often become entrenched due to learned non-use and compensatory strategies, such as circumduction or hip hiking, which may increase the metabolic cost of walking and the risk of falls [7, 8]. Furthermore, persistent asymmetry is associated with long-term musculoskeletal complications, including reduced bone density in the paretic limb and joint damage in the non-paretic limb due to excessive loading [9, 10].

Traditional rehabilitation often utilizes verbal cueing to encourage weight shifting; however, this method lacks precision and real-time objectivity [11]. Visual biofeedback systems have emerged as promising adjuncts that offer patients immediate and tangible information about their performance, which is particularly beneficial for motor learning in patients with cognitive deficits [12]. While previous studies have indicated that visual biofeedback can improve balance and walking speed, few randomized controlled trials have specifically targeted the correction of step-length asymmetry in patients with chronic stroke [13, 14].

Our research group previously developed an anteroposterior weight-shift (AP) training designed to restore the “ankle rocker” mechanism–controlled forward progression of the tibia over the foot–by providing visual biofeedback on the Center of Pressure (CoP) trajectory [15]. While previous study demonstrated the efficacy of this system in subacute patients [15], it remains unclear whether chronic patients, who exhibit more established pathological gait patterns, can derive similar benefits. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of AP training on gait symmetry, plantar pressure distribution, gait kinematics, and functional mobility in patients with chronic stroke.

Methods

Study design and participants

A total of 34 patients with chronic stroke were enrolled and randomly assigned to the AP training group (n = 17) or the control group (n = 17) (Fig. 1a). The sample size was determined a priori using G*Power 3.1 software. Based on the effect size observed in the previous subacute study [15], with an alpha of 0.05 and a power of 0.80 for a two-group repeated-measures design, a minimum of 15 participants per group was required. We enrolled 17 per group to account for potential dropout. Patients with chronic stroke who were admitted to a single tertiary hospital were included in this study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: [1] first-ever hemiplegic stroke with onset > 6 months prior; [2] independent ambulation for at least 10 m without assistive devices or orthoses; and [3] a Step Length Ratio (SLR) > 1.1, indicating clinically significant asymmetry. The exclusion criteria were as follows: [1] quadriplegia or double hemiplegia; [2] major musculoskeletal or other neurological disorders affecting gait; or [3] severe cognitive or perceptual deficits precluding participation.

a Flow diagram of the study participants; b Study design

Participants were randomly assigned to either the AP training or control group (Fig. 1b). Both groups received conventional gait training (30 min/day), three times per week for 6 weeks. Conventional gait training was conducted by a licensed physical therapist and included overground walking practice, stretching of the lower extremity muscles, strengthening exercises targeting the paretic lower limb, and balance training. The specific content of each session was individualized according to the patient’s functional level, but the overall structure and duration were standardized across both groups. The experimental group additionally underwent 30 min of AP training three times per week for 6 weeks. The control group received education regarding weight shifting using the paretic lower limb, but did not receive visual biofeedback training.

For all patients, step length, behavioral parameters, and plantar pressure analysis parameters were assessed at baseline (T0), during training (3 weeks after training, T1), after training (6 weeks after training, T2), and at the 6-week follow-up (12 weeks after training, T3). Three-dimensional (3D) gait analysis and energy consumption were evaluated at T0 and T2. All assessments were performed on the same day in a standardized order: [1] 3D gait analysis [2], plantar pressure analysis after a minimum 30-minute rest period, and [3] energy consumption testing last, following an additional 30-minute rest period. The assessors who conducted the AP training sessions was not involved in any outcomes assessments. All behavioral assessments were performed by a separate occupational therapist who was blinded to group allocation, and the 3D gait analysis and plantar pressure data were processed by another researcher also blinded to group assignment.

This protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our hospital (IRB No. 4-2013-0270) and registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02207933). All of the participants provided written informed consent.

Anterioposterior weight shift training using visual biofeedback

In this study, the AP training system used a customized visual biofeedback interface integrated with a plantar pressure analysis system (F-Scan; Tekscan Inc., Boston, MA, USA) developed by our study group [15]. Unlike conventional weight-shift biofeedback systems that monitor only the center of pressure on a force plate and train weight shifting in multiple directions, our system has three distinguishing features: [1] dual-sensor verification using pelvic and ankle motion trackers to ensure forward weight shifting is achieved through genuine trunk anterior progression rather than compensatory hip flexion or knee bending; [2] exclusive anteroposterior focus, deliberately constraining training to the sagittal plane to specifically target the ankle rocker mechanism [16]; and [3] individualized threshold calibration based on each patient’s maximal CoP trajectory. The patients stood with the paretic foot advanced 30 cm and both legs shoulder-width apart. They performed maximal anterior weight shifts ten times, and the system calculated the target threshold by adding 5% of the mean CoP trajectories (Fig. 2).

Anterioposterior weight shift training using visual feedback. a Plantar pressure distribution and trajectory of center of pressure during training session. b Gamified visual biofeedback from the archery target interface

The training sessions were gamified using an archery target interface. During training, the patients were instructed to shift their weight forward onto the paretic limb to move a virtual arrow toward the bullseye of the target. Successful trials required two conditions: [1] the CoP trajectory reached the individualized target, and [2] the pelvic sensor confirmed forward translation beyond the ankle sensor. Successful trials triggered positive audio-visual reinforcement (“Good job”), while failure to shift weight sufficiently or the use of compensatory posture triggered corrective cues (e.g., “Try harder,” “Keep posture correct”) (Fig. 2). Each session consisted of two 10-min blocks of active training separated by a 5-min rest interval.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome

The step length asymmetry was quantified using the SLR and Step Length Asymmetry Index (SLAI), both of which were calculated using data from a high-resolution pressure mat (body pressure measurement system HMER4 model, Tekscan Inc., Norwood, MA, USA). The participants walked across the mat ten times, and the average step length was extracted. The SLR was defined as the paretic step length divided by the non-paretic step length [3]. Secondary outcomes

Three-dimensional gait analysis

Temporospatial, kinematic, and kinetic data were obtained by recording the participants walking 8 m at a self-selected speed using the VICON MX-T10 Motion Analysis System (Oxford Parameters Inc., Oxford, UK) equipped with six infrared cameras operating at a sampling frequency of 100 Hz. A total of 16 reflective markers were placed on the lower limb bilaterally (8 markers per limb) according to the Plug-in gait model based on the Helen Hayes marker set [17]. Kinematic data were smoothed using a Woltring quintic spline filter with mean squared error mode at a smoothing level of 10. The average of five representative gait cycles was used for analysis. The temporospatial parameters included cadence (steps/min), step time (s), single support (s), double support (s), step length (m), and walking speed (m/s). The kinematic parameters included the angles of the pelvis, hip, knee, and ankle in the 3D plane. The kinetic data included the maximal moment, power generation, and power absolution of the hip, knee, and ankle in the sagittal plane. 3D gait analysis was performed at T0 and T2.

Plantar pressure analysis

Using in-shoe pressure sensors (F-Scan), the contact area (mm2), contract pressure (103kPa), peak pressure (103kPa), and CoP trajectory length for the forefoot, midfoot, and hindfoot were measured during a 30 m walk. Pressure variables were averaged across all complete steps captured during the walk. Plantar pressure data were collected at T0, T1, T2, and T3.

Energy consumption

Oxygen cost (mL/m/kg) and oxygen rate (mL/min/kg) were measured using a telemetric gas analysis system (KB1-C, Aerosports Inc., Ann Arbor, MI, USA) during a 5-min continuous walk at a self-selected speed on a 20-m oval track. Energy consumption was measured at T0 and T2 and the average value from 3 to 5 min was used for the analysis.

Behavioral parameters

The behavioral parameters included the Functional Ambulation Category (FAC) [18], lower extremity Fugl-Meyer assessment (FMA-L) score [19], Medical Research Council (MRC) grading of muscle strength [20], self-selected walking speed (SSWS) [21], and maximal safe walking speed (MSWS) [21]. The SSWS and MSWS were assessed over a 10-m walkway, with timing performed over the central 8 m to minimize acceleration and deceleration effects. Each condition was repeated three times, and the mean value was used for analysis. All patients were evaluated by an experienced occupational therapist at T0, T1, T2, and T3.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical variables were compared using a two-sample t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, or chi-square test, as appropriate. All variables were analyzed using repeated-measures analysis of variance (RMANOVA) with a post-hoc test and Bonferroni correction. As supplementary analyses, Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) was performed for the primary outcome with baseline FAC, ankle dorsiflexor MRC, and ankle plantar flexor MRC as covariates to control for baseline imbalances. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Baseline patient characteristics

The AP training group had a mean age of 56.31 (10.50) years, while the control group had a mean age of 58.06 (11.13) years (Table 1). Males were predominant in both groups (76% of the training group vs. 62% of the control group). There were no statistically significant differences between the groups in age, sex, lesion side, lower extremity motor function, walking speed, or step length asymmetry at baseline (all p > 0.05). However, the control group had a significantly higher baseline FAC score than the training group (4 ([3–4]) vs. 3 ([3–3]), p = 0.021).

Gait asymmetry

At baseline, the SLAI did not differ significantly between the AP training and control groups (0.61 (0.49) vs. 0.58 (0.41), p = 0.890) (Fig. 3). The RMANOVA revealed a significant time × group interaction for the SLAI (F = 12.543, p < 0.001). The AP training group showed significant improvements in SLAI at T1 (0.39 (0.37)), T2 (0.25 (0.30)), and T3 (0.29 (0.28)) compared to baseline (all p < 0.01), whereas the control group did not show significant within-group changes at any time point. The ANCOVA results, adjusted for baseline FAC and ankle MRC scores, confirmed the significant time x group interaction of SLAI (F = 11.284, p < 0.001). From the temporospatial parameters of the 3D gait analysis, significant interactions favoring the AP training group were also found for asymmetry indices of swing time (F = 16.983, p < 0.001), step length (F = 10.691, p = 0.003), and intra-limb swing/stance time ratio (F = 8.364, p = 0.007) (Supplemental Table 1).

Plantar pressure analysis

The baseline parameters from the plantar pressure analysis did not differ significantly between the two groups (all p > 0.05) (Table 2). Compared to the control group, the AP training group demonstrated significant increases in the paretic forefoot contact area (F = 4.211, p = 0.008), forefoot contact pressure (F = 3.340, p = 0.024), and total contact area (F = 3.980, p = 0.010). Post-hoc analysis revealed that, compared to baseline, the forefoot contact area, forefoot contact pressure, and total contact area on the affected side were significantly increased at T1, T2, and T3 in the AP training group (all p < 0.05). In addition, significant improvements in the mediolateral trajectory were observed at T2 and T3 compared with baseline in the AP training group.

Three-dimensional gait analysis

The baseline temporospatial gait parameters did not differ significantly between the two groups, except for affected side step length (0.24 (0.07) vs. 0.31 (0.10), p = 0.038) and both sides cadence (affected side 61.10 (14.57) vs. 78.85 (28.06,0 p = 0.028, unaffected side 60.03 (16.03) vs. 78.44 (28.01), p = 0.026) and walking speed (affected side 0.19 (0.08) vs. 0.31 (0.15), p = 0.018, unaffected side 0.19 (0.08) vs. 0.30 (0.14), p = 0.015) (Table 3). Compared with the control group, the AP training group demonstrated a significant decrease in the unaffected-side double support time (F = 4.511, p = 0.042) and an increase in the unaffected-side step length (F = 7.263, p = 0.011) and walking speed (F = 6.248, p = 0.018) post-training (T2).

None of the baseline kinematic gait parameters differed significantly between the two groups (all p > 0.05). On the affected side, the AP training group showed significantly increased maximal ankle dorsiflexion at stance (F = 10.870, p = 0.002) and reduced minimal hip flexion angle at stance (F = 6.842, p = 0.014) post-training (T2) compared with the control group. On the unaffected side, the AP training group showed a significant increase in the maximal hip flexion angle at initial contact (F = 7.505, p = 0.010), the maximal hip flexion angle at swing (F = 4.233, p = 0.048), and the maximal pelvic external rotation angle (F = 5.566, p = 0.025), whereas the minimal hip flexion angle at stance showed a significant reduction (F = 5.705, p = 0.023).

None of the baseline kinetic gait parameters differed significantly between the two groups (all p > 0.05). On the affected side, the AP training group showed significantly increased maximal hip extension moment (F = 4.822, p = 0.036) and maximal knee power generation (F = 12.110, p = 0.002) post-training (T2) compared to the control group.

Behavioral parameters

The baseline behavioral parameters did not differ significantly between the two groups, except for FAC (3.18 (0.39) vs. 3.63 (0.62), p = 0.021) and MRC of ankle dorsiflexor (1.64 (0.60) vs. 2.43 (0.62), p < 0.001) and plantar flexor (1.70 (0.58) vs. 2.43 (0.62), p = 0.002) (Supplemental Table 2). The MSWS showed a significant time × group interaction (F = 3.32, p = 0.024). The AP training group improved MSWS by an average of 0.05 m/s compared to 0.01 m/s in the control group. However, no significant interactions were observed for the FAC, FMA, or MRC scores.

Energy consumption

There were no statistically significant differences in oxygen cost (F = 0.573, p = 0.455) or oxygen rate (F = 0.046, p = 0.832) between the AP training and control groups (Supplemental Table 3).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first randomized controlled trial to evaluate AP training with visual biofeedback, specifically in patients with chronic stroke with step-length asymmetry. The results demonstrated that this targeted intervention significantly reduced the step length asymmetry and improved the mechanics of forward propulsion, even in patients who were more than 6 months post-stroke. While our previous study reported the efficacy of AP training in patients with subacute stroke [15], this study extended these findings by showing significant improvements in step length asymmetry, forefoot contact area, and pressure in survivors of chronic stroke.

According to previous studies, providing visual feedback on motion or performance in the context of computer games or virtual environments is a valuable complement to conventional therapy for balance or gait training in patients with Parkinson’s disease, cerebral palsy, and stroke [22–24]. Bishop et al. [22] reported significant improvements in swing time asymmetry following assisted weight-shift training with visual feedback in stroke patients. Similarly, Nunzio et al. [25] reported that stroke patients who underwent weight-shift training with audiovisual biofeedback demonstrated a significantly reduced CoP index of asymmetry. In line with these previous studies, the gait symmetry improvement observed in this study likely resulted from the addition of visual biofeedback, which provided a real-time 2D representation of weight distribution and CoP movement, thereby facilitating self-controlled motor learning induced by AP training.

The improvement in the SLAI in the AP training group validates the principle that restoring the “ankle rocker” mechanism is crucial for gait symmetry [26, 27]. In contrast to previous weight-shift training protocols that focused on shifting the body weight in various directions from a standing position [8, 28, 29], this study utilized weight shifting exclusively in the anteroposterior direction. This approach avoids diverting attention to directions that are unnecessary for ankle-rocker mechanisms. The AP training focused on the repeated implementation of the ankle rocker to assist active tibial progression over the supporting foot during the gait cycle. Increasing the number of repetitions is considered a key determinant in acquiring skilled performance, particularly in stroke patients who exhibit relatively slow motor learning [30, 31].

Mechanistically, the improvement in symmetry appears to be driven by indirect evidence of enhanced paretic limb propulsion. The AP training group demonstrated significantly increased forefoot contact area and pressure on the affected side, indicating improved weight transfer onto the paretic forefoot during the stance phase. These findings are consistent with restoration of the ankle rocker mechanism, which facilitates controlled tibial progression during mid-stance, positioning the limb for effective terminal stance extension. The increased maximal ankle dorsiflexion during stance further supports improved tibial advancement, which in turn allows for greater hip extension, as reflected in the reduced minimal hip flexion angle at stance and increased hip extension moment. This mechanical chain—from improved ankle rocker to enhanced forefoot loading and terminal stance limb extension—increases the effective push-off distance.

We also observed significant increases in forefoot contact area and pressure on the affected side, along with increased step length and decreased double support time on the unaffected side. Choo et al. [32] highlighted the importance of ankle dorsiflexion in the paretic limb for step length, stride length, and confident gait. Moreover, Padmanabhan et al. [33] demonstrated that a combination of ankle power generation and increased paretic limb extension is essential for paretic propulsion. Contrary to earlier studies suggesting a limited capacity to alter established gait patterns in patients with chronic stroke, the changes in gait parameters observed in this study confirm that locomotor patterns remain trainable, a finding that is consistent with recent literature [34, 35].

The temporospatial gait symmetry and propulsive force in the paretic leg are closely linked to walking speed, and this study confirmed an increase in walking speed [36, 37]. Wonsetler et al. [38] showed that gait speed is strongly associated with hip extension, whereas Mao et al. [39] reported that body weight support training significantly improved gait speed with concurrent increases in the hip extension angle and hip flexion peak moment. Consistent with these studies, the AP training group in this study showed an increased maximal hip extension moment and decreased minimal hip flexion angle at stance, thereby increasing MSWS. Park at el [40]. demonstrated that an improved weight shift toward the paretic side may elicit enhanced activation of the paretic leg muscles, particularly the hip extensors and ankle plantar flexors. Furthermore, knee joint stabilization during the stance phase of the paretic leg is related to an improved weight shift toward the paretic side [41, 42]. Thus, the improvement in walking speed, coupled with improved motor function of the paretic lower limb, suggests that AP training is effective in enhancing the walking ability in stroke patients.

Interestingly, while the gait quality (symmetry and maximum speed) improved significantly in the AP training group, the motor impairment scores did not show parallel changes, likely due to the distinction between impairment- and task-level functional performance. The FMA and MRC primarily assess isolated voluntary motor control and muscle strength and are relatively insensitive to improvements in intersegmental coordination, timing, and dynamic weight shifting, all of which are critical for efficient gait [43]. In chronic stroke, neurological deficits, such as weakness, abnormal synergies, and spasticity, often persist owing to established corticospinal damage; however, the capacity for motor adaptation and task-specific learning remain relatively preserved [44]. Accordingly, intensive and task-oriented gait training may improve functional gait parameters through enhanced coordination and sensorimotor integration, even without measurable changes in impairment-based motor scores. Park et al. [45] stated that although neurological deficits persist in chronic stroke, biomechanical gait profiles can be improved with appropriate gait training. Moreover, Cherry-Allen et al. [46] demonstrated that split-belt treadmill gait training with auditory biofeedback improved step length asymmetry and other spatiotemporal features without concurrent changes in lower-extremity muscle strength, supporting the notion that locomotor learning can occur independently of muscle strength or motor recovery in chronic stroke.

Given that gait asymmetry increases energy consumption, a decrease in energy consumption (O2 rate or O2 cost) is expected following AP training [47, 48]. In this study, the change in energy consumption between the groups was not statistically significant. Wutzke et al. [49] argued that the long-term correction of spatiotemporal asymmetry may reduce energy consumption, while Awad et al. [47] showed an improvement in energy costs only after 12 weeks of locomotor training. The 6-week training duration employed in this study may have been too short to demonstrate a significant reduction in energy consumption.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size of this study was relatively small, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Future large-scale, multi-center clinical trial will be needed to validate these findings across broader populations. Second, although an improvement in balance ability was expected, specific balance evaluations were not included. Future research should include standardized measures, such as the Berg Balance Scale or the Timed Up and Go test, which are commonly used in similar studies. Third, this study did not address the participants’ cognitive function or capacity for sustained attention. Because visual biofeedback requires active cognitive engagement, variations in attentional capacity could have influenced the training outcomes. Fourth, the anterior component of the ground reaction force was not extracted as a separate variable during data processing; future studies should include this direct measure such as ground reaction force to strengthen the evidence for improved propulsion. Fifth, although adequate rest periods were provided between assessment sessions, the effects of fatigue from performing multiple evaluations on the same day cannot be entirely excluded. Finally, the study did not control for the participants’ daily physical activity levels outside the prescribed therapy sessions, which may have acted as a confounding variable influencing functional recovery.

Conclusions

This randomized controlled trial investigated whether AP weight-shift training with visual biofeedback could improve gait asymmetry and some functional mobility in patients with chronic stroke. It suggested that 6 weeks of AP training significantly reduced step length asymmetry, improved paretic forefoot loading and propulsive mechanics, and increased maximal safe walking speed compared with conventional gait training alone, with effects maintained at 6-week follow-up even in the chronic phase. AP training with visual biofeedback may offer a targeted therapeutic option for chronic stroke patients with established gait asymmetry. Future large-scale, multi-center clinical trials with longer training durations should include direct measures of propulsive force (e.g., ground reaction force), balance assessments, and cognitive profiling to further elucidate the mechanisms and optimize patient selection for this intervention.
