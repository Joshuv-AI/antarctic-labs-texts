Background

Hypocalcemia can cause seizures as an acute, reversible metabolic disturbance [1, 2]. However, when hypocalcemia results from chronic conditions such as hypoparathyroidism, persistent calcium-phosphate imbalance may lead to basal ganglia calcification [1–3]. These calcifications can act as a long standing epileptogenic focus and may also produce a range of neuropsychiatric manifestations, such as seizures, mood disorders like anxiety and even cognitive impairment [1–3].

This constellation of clinical and radiological features is collectively known as Fahr syndrome, a rare condition with an estimated prevalence of less than 1 per 1,000,000 population [4]. We report one such case of Fahr syndrome who presented to our center with generalized tonic–clonic seizures along with a known history of anxiety disorder.

This case report has been prepared in accordance with the CARE reporting guideline, and the completed CARE checklist has been provided as a supplementary file.

Case presentation

A 36-year-old Nepalese female with a history of anxiety disorder, on sertraline 100 mg and amitriptyline 25 mg daily, presented with the sudden onset of generalized tonic–clonic seizures lasting approximately 5 min, followed by up-rolling of the eyes and postictal altered consciousness lasting about 20 min. There was no associated tongue biting or involuntary passage of urine or feces. She had no prior history of similar episodes, and her family history was unremarkable.

On physical examination, her vital signs were stable. She was drowsy but had a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15/15. Deep tendon reflexes were normal, and both Chvostek and Trousseau signs were positive. No focal neurological deficits were identified. The remainder of the systemic examination was unremarkable.

Laboratory evaluation revealed normal complete blood count, renal and liver function tests, arterial blood gas analysis, and urinalysis. Electrocardiography showed normal sinus rhythm with a normal QT interval. Antinuclear antibody (ANA) testing was negative. The biochemical profile demonstrated severe hypocalcemia with markedly suppressed PTH levels, consistent with hypoparathyroidism. The patient was initially treated with 10% calcium gluconate (10 mL) administered intravenously, containing 1 g of calcium gluconate (equivalent to 90 mg of elemental calcium), diluted in 50 mL of normal saline and infused over 10 min. No further seizure episodes occurred in the Emergency Department. She was subsequently admitted to the Intensive Care Unit and started on a continuous intravenous calcium infusion, prepared by adding 11 ampoules (10 mL each) of 10% calcium gluconate (equivalent to a total of 1,000 mg of elemental calcium) to normal saline to make a final volume of 1,000 mL, yielding a solution containing 1 mg/mL of elemental calcium. This infusion was administered at a rate of 50 mL/h.

In addition, she received intravenous magnesium sulfate at a dose of 4 g over 12 h. Repeat serum calcium measurement at 6 h showed improvement to 4.0 mg/dL, which increased further to 4.5 mg/dL the following day. Serum magnesium also improved to 1.6 mg/dL. The intravenous calcium infusion was continued, and by the fourth day of admission, serum calcium had risen to 8.0 mg/dL. The infusion was then discontinued, and the patient was transitioned to oral calcium and calcitriol supplementation.

No recurrent seizure episodes occurred during hospitalization. She was discharged on oral calcium carbonate 1,000 mg and calcitriol 0.5 µg twice daily. At 1-month follow-up, her serum calcium levels remained within the normal range, and she reported no recurrence of symptoms. At the 6-month follow-up, she also reported no recurrence of symptoms.

Discussion and conclusions

Neuropsychiatric manifestations associated with bilateral basal ganglia calcification may occur either idiopathically, termed Fahr disease, or secondary to an underlying condition, termed Fahr syndrome [2, 3, 5]. Fahr syndrome, although radiographically similar to Fahr disease, refers to secondary intracranial calcification due to underlying conditions such as hypoparathyroidism or pseudohypoparathyroidism [2, 5, 6]. The differential diagnosis of bilateral intracranial calcifications includes Fahr disease, infectious conditions such as congenital toxoplasmosis or cytomegalovirus infection, metabolic disorders including pseudohypoparathyroidism, mitochondrial disorders, and certain genetic neurodegenerative syndromes. However, the presence of profound hypocalcemia with markedly reduced PTH levels, absence of infectious history, and characteristic symmetrical calcifications involving the basal ganglia and cerebellum strongly supported hypoparathyroidism-related Fahr syndrome in our patient [7]. In hypoparathyroidism, chronic disturbances in calcium-phosphate homeostasis may promote ectopic intracranial calcium deposition over time, resulting in perivascular calcification within the basal ganglia, cerebellum, and other brain regions, as observed in our patient [2, 6, 8].

Fahr syndrome may present with a heterogenous spectrum of neurological and psychiatric manifestations, such as seizures, movement disorders, mood disorder or psychotic symptoms. Seizures are a well-documented presenting feature [2, 3, 5]. It may arise acutely from hypocalcemia that increases neuronal excitability and can produce a generalized or focal events, often accompanied by signs such as tetany or paresthesia [1, 2]. On other hand, chronic basal ganglia calcification may serve as a persistent epileptogenic focus that may cause seizures even after biochemical correction [2, 3, 5]. In our patient, the presence of positive Chvostek and Trousseau signs, severe hypocalcemia, and rapid cessation of seizures following calcium correction suggest that acute metabolic derangement played a predominant role in seizure precipitation. However, the extensive intracranial calcifications likely represent a chronic process, highlighting the dual pathophysiological mechanisms involved.

Our patient also had a prior diagnosis of anxiety disorder. Psychiatric manifestations including anxiety, depression, and behavioral changes have been described in patients with basal ganglia calcification, likely related to disruption of neural circuits involved in emotional regulation [3, 5, 8, 9]. In some reports, psychiatric symptoms have preceded neurological manifestations by several years. However, because anxiety disorders are common in the general population, the coexistence of anxiety and intracranial calcification in this patient should be interpreted cautiously, and a direct causal relationship cannot be established from this case alone.

Hypoparathyroidism, in this case, may have several possible etiologies. Hypomagnesemia can impair both secretion and peripheral action of parathyroid hormone, resulting in functional hypoparathyroidism [10]. Additionally, the coexistence of elevated TSH with normal FT4 raises the possibility of an underlying autoimmune endocrine process affecting multiple glands. Idiopathic hypoparathyroidism also remains a potential explanation since no history of neck surgeries and radiation exposure was present.

There are currently no standardized guidelines regarding the use of antiepileptic drugs in Fahr syndrome associated with hypocalcemia [11]. Seizures in this setting often respond poorly to anticonvulsant therapy alone and instead improve with correction of the underlying metabolic abnormality, particularly normalization of serum calcium levels [11, 12]. Calcium supplementation with active vitamin D, or parathyroid hormone replacement when appropriate, is effective in resolving seizures and other neurological manifestations, while psychiatric symptoms may also improve following metabolic correction [3, 5, 8]. In our patient, seizures resolved promptly after calcium correction without the need for long-term antiepileptic therapy, highlighting the central role of metabolic management in hypoparathyroidism-related Fahr syndrome.

The prognosis of Fahr syndrome remains variable and unpredictable and does not appear to correlate with the extent of intracranial calcification [13]. Early recognition and timely treatment of the underlying metabolic disorder, as demonstrated in this case, can result in excellent symptomatic control and may prevent recurrence and progression of neurological and neuropsychiatric manifestations.

This case adds to the literature of Fahr syndrome by highlighting the complex metabolic, endocrine, neurological, and possible neuropsychiatric manifestations associated with hypoparathyroidism-related intracranial calcification. The coexistence of long-standing anxiety preceding neurological symptoms raises the possibility of an association with underlying basal ganglia dysfunction; however, causality cannot be inferred from a single case. Additionally, seizure as the presenting neurological manifestation with resolution following correction of hypocalcemia, underscores the role of reversible metabolic abnormalities in precipitating neurological symptoms despite structural calcification. This case therefore emphasizes the importance of metabolic and endocrine evaluation in patients presenting with neuropsychiatric symptoms and basal ganglia calcification.

This case highlights an unusual presentation of Fahr syndrome with seizure precipitated by hypocalcemia due to hypoparathyroidism, which improved following correction of metabolic abnormality. Early identification of this condition allows prompt correction of metabolic abnormalities with calcium and active vitamin D supplementation, which can result in complete seizure control without the need for long-term antiepileptic therapy. Timely diagnosis and targeted metabolic management are essential to prevent recurrence of seizures and progression of neurological and neuropsychiatric complications.
