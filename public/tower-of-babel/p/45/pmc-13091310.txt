CSPG4 Mediates Inflammatory, Cell Death, and Senescence Responses in Enteric Glia Exposed to Clostridioides difficile Toxins
Authors: Francisco M. S. Marques, Deiziane V. S. Costa, Conceição S. M. Rebouças, Gerly A. C. Brito, Cirle A. Warren, Renata F. C. Leitão
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) is a persistent public health problem worldwide. C. difficile toxins A (TcdA) and B (TcdB) can affect enteric glial cells, an important component of the enteric nervous system. Here, we aimed to identify the profile of C. difficile toxin receptors expressed by enteric glia and investigate the contribution of the chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan receptors (CSPG4) in mediating inflammatory response, cell death, and senescence in enteric glia exposed to TcdA or TcdB. We found that human, mouse, and rat enteric glia express all of the previously recognized C. difficile toxin receptors. However, in vitro, C. difficile toxins significantly increased CSPG4 expression in enteric glia. In vivo, CDI also increased CSPG4 expression in colonic and cecal tissues. Blockage of CSPG4 significantly decreased IL‐6 and S100B gene expression, as well as reduced the nuclear translocation of NFκB and STAT3 in enteric glia exposed to TcdB, but not TcdA. Interestingly, blockade of CSPG4 also decreased senescence‐associated beta‐galactosidase (SA‐β‐galactosidase) activity and other senescence markers (γH2AX, p16 and p21) in enteric glia exposed to TcdA or TcdB. However, CSPG4 blockade reduced cell death promoted by TcdA, but not TcdB. Taken together, these findings show that enteric glia, by expressing all known C. difficile toxin receptors, can respond directly to TcdA or TcdB. However, CSPG4 seems to play an important role in cell death and inflammatory response promoted by TcdA and TcdB, respectively, as well as senescence induced by both toxins.

Introduction

Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) is a major public health problem, traditionally seen in hospital settings, but with a significant number of community‐acquired cases [1, 2]. The clinical disease ranges from mild diarrhea to fulminant disease or death [3]. C. difficile toxins A (TcdA) and B (TcdB) are the main virulence factors produced during infection. These toxins induce gut inflammation and cell death, which can be exacerbated by a persistent host immune response [4]. In addition to affecting intestinal epithelial cells, several studies have shown that TcdA and TcdB also impact the enteric nervous system (ENS) cells, including enteric glia and neurons, which play a crucial role in regulating essential gut functions such as intestinal motility, secretion, and permeability [3]. As a result, several intestinal complications have been reported as possible GI sequelae of CDI, including irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), functional diarrhea, dyspepsia, and constipation [5, 6, 7]. Symptoms such as abdominal pain, constipation, diarrhea, IBS, and dyspepsia can persist for up to 2 years after the infection resolves [7].

Enteric glia that survive the cytotoxic effects of TcdB can remain metabolically active and enter the senescence process [8]. Senescence is characterized by an irreversible cell cycle arrest, changes in metabolism, morphology, and gene expression, as well as the occurrence of DNA modifications and damage [9]. There is yet no evidence that TcdA can promote a senescence state in enteric glia. It is also unclear how the C. difficile toxin receptors contribute to persistent cellular dysfunction post‐ C. difficile toxin exposure.

Recent findings indicate that TcdB is more strongly associated with disease severity, exhibiting a cytotoxic potential 100‐ to 1000‐fold greater than that of TcdA [10], highlighting TcdB's clinical relevance. Currently, the only known receptor for TcdA is LDLR (low‐density lipoprotein receptor) [11]. TcdB has several recognized receptors, including Frizzled (1, 2, and 7) [12], Poliovirus‐like receptor 3 (PVRL3) [13], Tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI) [14, 15], Low‐density lipoprotein receptor‐related protein 1 (LRP1) [16], and Chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan 4 (CSPG4) [17, 18]. Among these TcdB receptors, CSPG4 has been shown to facilitate toxin effects, and its loss in vivo is protective against CDI [19]. However, the mechanism behind this protection remains to be further elucidated.

The present study aimed to identify the distribution of C. difficile toxin receptors in enteric glia and investigate the role of the CSPG4 receptor in modulating the expression of inflammatory mediators, cell death, and senescence induced by TcdA and TcdB in these cells.

Materials and Methods

Samples From Mice and Humans

The paraffin‐embedded samples from the large intestine of uninfected and C. difficile ‐infected humans and mice used in this study were previously described [20]. Importantly, the large intestine samples (cecum and colon) from mice were collected on day 3 post‐infection, which corresponds to the peak of clinical symptoms and intestinal damage, as previously reported [20].

Rat Enteric Glial Cell Culture and Treatment

Immortalized rat enteric glial cell line PK060399egfr (ATCC CRL‐2690, VA, USA) was cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM, Gibco) and supplemented with 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA), 1% antibiotics (100 μg/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin, Gibco), and 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco) at 37°C in a humidified incubator under 5% CO2 for no more than 24 passages. For all experiments, enteric glia cells were released using 0.05% trypsin–EDTA for 5 min.

To investigate the role of the CSPG4 receptor in the response of enteric glial cells to TcdA (50 ng/mL) or TcdB (1 ng/mL), cells were pretreated for 1 h with 0.001 μg/mL of anti‐CSPG4 or 0.001 μg/mL of isotype control. The anti‐CSPG4 concentration was selected based on a cell death assay (Figure S1). After 18 h of incubation with the TcdA or TcdB, the cells were harvested for analysis of the study parameters.

For the senescence studies, cells were incubated with TcdA (50 ng/mL) or TcdB (1 ng/mL) for 24 h, and fresh media were replaced every other day. Cells were harvested for analysis after 48, 72, 96, 120, and 144 h (6 days) following the initial toxin challenge. In some experiments, cells were pretreated with anti‐CSPG4 or an isotype control as mentioned above.

The concentrations of TcdA (50 ng/mL) and TcdB (1 ng/mL) were selected based on previous studies conducted by our research group [20, 21, 22, 23]. Purified TcdA and TcdB, derived from C. difficile strain VPI 10463, were obtained from TechLab (Virginia, USA).

Assessment of Expression of C. difficile Toxin Receptors in Enteric Glial Cells From Humans, Mice, and Rats

An initial screening was conducted using the publicly available single‐cell RNA sequencing dataset (“the human and mouse enteric nervous system at single cell resolution”) [24] available in the Single Cell Portal (https://singlecell.broadinstitute.org/single_cell), where we assessed the expression profiles of multiple C. difficile toxin receptors (Cspg4, Pvrl3, Fzd1, Lrp1, Fzd2, Tfpi, Fzd7, and Ldlr) in colonic enteric glia cells from humans and mice. Enteric glia cells were screened based on the expression of specific markers known for these cells, including S100 calcium‐binding protein B (S100B), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), SRY‐box transcription factor 10 (SOX10), and proteolipid protein 1 (PLP1). The expression of all the C. difficile toxin receptors in rat enteric glia (PK060399egfr, ATCC CRL‐2690) was evaluated by qPCR. To validate the most expressed receptors in myenteric glia from human and mouse, as well as in rat enteric glia, we performed immunofluorescence staining.

Immunofluorescence in Colonic and Cecum Tissues

Sections (4 μm thick) were prepared from paraffin‐embedded cecum and colon tissues from uninfected and C. difficile ‐infected mice, as well as colonic tissues from humans (uninfected and infected with C. difficile ), as previously described with modifications [21]. Following deparaffinization, antigen retrieval (sodium citrate pH 6.0) was performed by incubating the slides in citrate buffer solution (Abcam) for 10 min at 95°C. To reduce non‐specific binding, slides were then incubated in a blocking solution containing 5% donkey serum and 0.1% Triton X‐100 in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: anti‐CSPG4 (Abcam, ab275024, 1:400), or anti‐nectin 3 (PVRL3, Invitrogen, PA5‐51095, 1:100) in combination with GFAP (Abcam, ab4674, 1:500) to assess colocalization in enteric glial cells. Following primary antibody incubation, the slides were washed three times in PBS with 0.05% Tween‐20 for 5 min and then incubated for 2 h at room temperature with species‐specific secondary antibodies: donkey anti‐rabbit 594 (Abcam, ab150064, 1:400) and donkey anti‐chicken 488 (Abcam, ab150169, 1:400). To decrease the autofluorescence, samples were incubated with Vector TrueView solution (Vector labs, SP‐8400) for 1 min at room temperature. Slides were washed with PBS (5 min) and incubated with 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole, dihydrochloride (DAPI, Invitrogen, D1306; 1:1000 in PBS) for 5 min. The DAPI solution was then refreshed and incubated for an additional 5 min. Finally, the slides were washed three times in PBS (5 min each) and coverslipped using 30 μL of mounting medium (Vector Laboratories, Vector TrueVIEW Autofluorescence Quenching Kit). Fluorescence images were captured using a ZEISS fluorescence microscope.

Immunohistochemistry in the Cecum and Colon Tissues

Sections (4 μm thick) were prepared from paraffin‐embedded mouse cecum and colon tissues. Following deparaffinization, antigen retrieval was performed by incubating the slides in citrate buffer Target retrieval solution at low pH (K8005, Dako) for 20 min at 95°C in PTLink (PT10027, Dako). To reduce non‐specific binding, endogenous peroxidase was blocked by incubating with 3% H2O2 for 10 min. The sections were then incubated for 4 h with an anti‐CSPG4 (Abcam, ab275024, 1:400), followed by a 30‐min incubation with polymer HRP (K8000, Dako). The antibody binding sites were visualized by incubating the samples with diaminobenzidine‐H2O2 (DAB, Dako) solution. Sections incubated with antibody diluent without a primary antibody were used as negative controls. The brown coloration identified positive staining. Immunohistochemistry images were captured using a light microscope equipped with a digital camera and the LAZ 3.5 acquisition system (LEICA DM1000, Germany). To quantify the CSPG4‐immunostained area, Adobe Photoshop 8.0 was used to determine both the total tissue area and the immunostained area. The percentage (%) of the stained area was then calculated using the following formula: (Immunostained area [pixels] × 100)/Total area [pixels] [25].

qPCR Analysis

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed to assess how exposure to (50 ng/mL) or TcdB (1 ng/mL) affects the expression of Cspg4, Pvrl3, Fzd1, Lrp1, Fzd2, Tfpi, Fzd7 and Ldlr in enteric glial cells (6 × 105 cells/well) at different incubation time points (2, 12, and 18 h). For this time‐course experiment, control cells were incubated with culture medium only. In addition, qPCR was performed to evaluate the participation of CSPG4 in TcdA and TcdB‐induced changes in the expression of Bcl2, S100B, and IL‐6 in enteric glia cells. To assess the contribution of CSPG4 in the expression of Bcl2, S100B, and IL‐6 in enteric glial cells (6 × 105 cells/well) exposed to TcdA (50 ng/mL) or TcdB (1 ng/mL), cells were then treated with anti‐CSPG4 antibody (0.001 μg/mL) or isotype control for 1 h, followed by incubation with TcdA (50 ng/mL) or TcdB (1 ng/mL) for 18 h. After incubation time, total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) on the QIAcube automated system. RNA concentration and purity were measured, and samples were treated with DNase I (Invitrogen) to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. Subsequently, RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio‐Rad). qPCR amplification of genes was performed using the CFX Connect Real‐Time PCR Detection System (Bio‐Rad) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s. Reactions were carried out using iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio‐Rad). Gapdh was used as a housekeeping gene. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [26].

Cell Death (Phosphatidylserine‐Annexin V Binding Assay)

To investigate the involvement of the CSPG4 receptor in C. difficile toxin‐induced cell death, a real‐time phosphatidylserine (PS)–annexin V binding assay was performed on live enteric glial cells, following the manufacturer's instructions (Glo‐Annexin V Apoptosis Real‐Time Assay, Promega, JA1011) and as previously described [21, 23]. First, enteric glial cells (104 cells/well) were cultured and plated in white, opaque 96‐well plates (Falcon). After 24 h, the cells were incubated with 0.001 μg/mL of anti‐CSPG4 or isotype control. After 1 h of incubation, TcdA (50 ng/mL) or TcdB (1 ng/mL) was added. Then, 100 μL of 2× detection reagent (2 μL of the annexin substrate NanoBit, 2 μL CaCl2, 2 μL annexin V‐SmBit and 2 μL annexin V‐LgBit in 1000 μL of supplemented DMEM pre‐warmed to 37°C) was added to each well, and incubated at 37°C in a humidified incubator under 5% CO2 for 18 h. Luminescence was measured using a luminometer (Infinite 200 PRO, TECAN). To obtain the Relative Luminescence Unit (RLU), the luminescence signal of all samples was subtracted from the intrinsic luminescence of the detection reagent (a well containing only DMEM supplemented without cells) and normalized to the control group (cells exposed only to the supplemented culture medium).

Caspase‐3/7 Activity

Caspase‐3/7 activity was measured using the Caspase‐Glo 3/7 Assay (Promega, G8090), according to the manufacturer's instructions and as previously described [21]. Briefly, enteric glial cells (104 cells/well) were seeded into white, opaque 96‐well plates (Falcon) and cultured for 18 h. Cells were then treated with anti‐CSPG4 antibody (0.001 μg/mL) or isotype control (0.001 μg/mL) for 1 h before the addition of TcdA (50 ng/mL) or TcdB (1 ng/mL). Control cells were incubated with medium only. After 18 h of toxin exposure, 100 μL of Caspase‐Glo 3/7 reagent (substrate reconstituted in buffer and equilibrated to room temperature) was added to each well. The plate was shaken for 30 s at 500 rpm and then incubated in the dark at room temperature for 2 h. Luminescence was measured using a microplate luminometer (Infinite 200 PRO, TECAN). Relative luminescence units (RLU) were calculated by subtracting the background signal (wells containing only Caspase‐Glo 3/7 reagent and supplemented DMEM, without cells) from the total signal of each sample and normalized to the control group (cells exposed only to the supplemented culture medium).

Measurement of S100B

To evaluate the involvement of the CSPG4 receptor in S100B release, the concentration of S100B in the supernatant of enteric glial cells (6 × 105 cells/well) was measured after 18 h of exposure to TcdA or TcdB, in the presence or absence of anti‐CSPG4 or isotype control. The measurement was performed by ELISA using the DuoSet S100B kit (R&D Systems), according to the manufacturer's instructions and as previously described [20]. Absorbance was read at 450 nm using an Epoch plate reader (BioTek).

Immunofluorescence to Evaluate CSPG4, LRP1, PVRL3, γH2AX, p16 and p21 as Well as NFκB and pSTAT3 Nuclear Translocation in Enteric Glia

To evaluate the expression of CSPG4, LRP1, and PVRL3, enteric glia cells were treated with TcdA or TcdB for 18 h. To evaluate the expression of markers of senescence gamma H2A histone family member X (γH2AX), 16‐kilodalton protein (p16), and p21, also known as CDKN1A, enteric glia cells were incubated with TcdA or TcdB for 24 h and harvested after 96 h of initial toxin exposure. To investigate the contribution of CSPG4 to the expression of γH2AX, p16, and p21, as well as to the nuclear translocation of NFκB and pSTAT3 promoted by TcdA or TcdB, enteric glial cells were pretreated with an anti‐CSPG4 antibody (0.001 μg/mL) or an isotype control 1 h before incubation with the toxins. After incubation with toxins (18 h for NFκB and pSTAT3 or 96 h for senescence markers), cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 30 min at room temperature. Then, cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X‐100 and 3% BSA in PBS for 5 min at 4°C. Cells staining with nectin‐3 (PVRL3) or LRP1 were not permeabilized. Non‐specific binding was blocked using 5% normal donkey serum in PBS for 40 min at room temperature. After washing, cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against CSPG4 (Abcam, ab275024, 1:400), nectin‐3 (PVRL3, Invitrogen, PA5‐51095, 1:200), LRP1 (Abcam, ab92544, 1:100), NFκB p65 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc‐372, 1:100), or pSTAT3 (R&D Systems, AF4607, 1:100). For senescence markers (γH2AX, p16, and p21), cells were incubated for 10 h at 4°C with primary antibodies against γH2AX (Abcam, ab81299, 1:200), p16 (Abcam, ab211542, 1:200), or p21 (Invitrogen, 14‐6715‐81, 1:100). After three washes with PBS, cells were incubated for 2 h at room temperature with AlexaFluor 488 donkey anti‐rabbit secondary antibody (1:400, Abcam, ab150073) or AlexaFluor 594 donkey anti‐rabbit secondary antibody (1:400, Abcam, ab150064). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000 in PBS) for 10 min. Slides were mounted using antifade mounting medium containing phalloidin (Vector Labs, H1600) to visualize the actin cytoskeleton. Fluorescent images were acquired using a Zeiss fluorescence microscope. ImageJ software was used to quantify the fluorescence intensity of CSPG4, LRP1, PVRL3, and p21. The percentage of cells showing nuclear immunoreactivity for NFκB p65, pSTAT3, γH2AX, or p16 was calculated from 100 cells per condition.

Cellular Senescence‐Associated Beta‐Galactosidase (SA‐β‐Galactosidase) Analysis

β‐galactosidase activity was measured using the CellEvent Senescence Green Detection Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, C10850), following the manufacturer's protocol. Cells were fixed for 20 min, washed twice with PBS containing 1% BSA, and incubated with the detection solution for 2 h at 37°C. After three washes, nuclei were stained with DAPI (Invitrogen, D1306, 1:1000 in PBS) for 10 min, followed by final washes in PBS. Slides were mounted with VECTASHIELD HardSet antifade mounting medium (Vector Laboratories, H‐1400‐10). Images were acquired with a ZEISS fluorescence microscope. Quantification of senescence‐associated β‐galactosidase (SA‐β‐gal) was performed using ImageJ software at 200× magnification from three fields per sample.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad software 10.0 (San Diego, CA, USA). The data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Student's t‐test or one‐ or two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Tukey test was used to compare means. p < 0.05 was considered to indicate significance.

Results

Human, Mouse and Rat Enteric Glia Express All Known C. difficile Toxin Receptors

The distribution of C. difficile toxin receptors varies according to the host cells and across species [19]. Which toxin receptors are present in enteric glia is not well defined. For that, we mined a scRNA‐seq dataset publicly available, which was generated from sorted myenteric cells from human and mouse colonic tissues [24], to identify which receptors for TcdA (Ldlr) and TcdB (Cspg4, Tfpi, Pvrl3, Lrp1, Fzd1, Fzd2, and Fzd7) are expressed in enteric glia cells under physiological conditions. We also identified the expression of these receptors in the rat enteric glia cell line (PK060399egfr), which has been broadly used for mechanistic studies [8, 20, 21, 22, 23, 27, 28]. We found that human, mouse, and rat enteric glia express all known receptors for TcdA (Ldlr) and TcdB (Cspg4, Tfpi, Pvrl3, Lrp1, Fzd1, Fzd2, and Fzd7) (Figure 1). The most expressed receptors for TcdB in mouse and human enteric glia were Pvrl3, Lrp1, and Cspg4 (Figure 1). Pvrl3, Fzd1, Fzd2, and LRP1 were also the most abundant in the rat enteric glia cell line (Figure 1). From a protein perspective, we also validated the expression of some of these receptors (CSPG4, PVRL3, and/or LRP1) in GFAP‐positive enteric glia from human and mouse colonic tissue, as well as in rat enteric glia cell line (Figure S2).

Gene expression of these receptors in rat enteric glia (PK060399egfr/ATCC) cells was measured by qPCR.

Challenge With TcdA or TcdB Modifies the Expression of C. difficile Toxin Receptors in Enteric Glia

Next, we investigated whether C. difficile toxins could change the expression of the receptors in enteric glia. No significant changes were observed at 2 h of incubation with either toxin (Figure 2A). Interestingly, enteric glia cells challenged with TcdA or TcdB exhibited a pronounced and consistent upregulation of CSPG4 from 12 h of incubation (p < 0.0001, Figure 2A). Minimal upregulation (Tfpi and Ldlr) or downregulation (Ldlr, Pvrl3, Lrp1, Fzd1, and Fzd2) of other receptors in response to C. difficile toxin challenge was also observed in enteric glia in a time‐dependent manner (Figure 2A). Given that CSPG4 exhibited the most substantial change, we focused next on this receptor specifically in the subsequent studies.

Additionally, the enteric glia marker GFAP co‐localizes with CSPG4 in cecal (Figure 3B) and colonic tissues (Figure 3C).

Taken together, enteric glia express all known receptors for TcdA and TcdB. Under C. difficile toxin challenge, the expression of these receptors undergoes upregulation or downregulation in a time‐dependent manner. In addition, CSPG4 is increased in enteric glia under C. difficile toxin challenge in vitro and in a preclinical model of CDI.

CSPG4 Facilitates NFκB Nuclear Translocation and IL‐6 Expression in Enteric Glial Cells in Response to TcdB, but Not TcdA

We have previously reported that both TcdA and TcdB activate the transcription factor NFκB in enteric glial cells, and that its activation regulates IL‐6 expression [20]. Next, we investigated whether CSPG4 could participate in the activation of NFκB/IL‐6 in enteric glia exposed to TcdA or TcdB.

CSPG4 blockade partially decreased the percentage of enteric glia with immunostaining positive for NFκB in the nucleus, suggesting reduced translocation to the nucleus in response to TcdB (p < 0.0001, Figure 4A,B). CSPG4 blockade also decreased IL‐6 gene expression in enteric glia challenged with TcdB (p = 0.004, Figure 4C). Interestingly, CSPG4 inhibition did not attenuate TcdA‐induced NFκB nuclear translocation and IL‐6 upregulation in enteric glia.

Given that IL‐6 is known to promote STAT3 activation and consequently nuclear translocation in other cells [29], we investigated whether CSPG4 blockade, which decreased IL‐6 expression in enteric glia challenged with TcdB, could affect STAT3 activation under TcdA or TcdB challenge. We found that TcdA or TcdB increases the percentage of enteric glia with immunostaining positive for pSTAT3 in the nucleus (p < 0.0001, Figure 5A,B), whereas CSPG4 inhibition significantly decreased pSTAT3 nuclear translocation in enteric glia exposed to TcdB, but not TcdA (Figure 5A,B).

CSPG4 Mediates S100B Gene Expression in Enteric Glia Challenged With TcdB, but Not TcdA

In our previous study, we also reported that TcdA or TcdB upregulated S100B in enteric glia [20]. Given that S100B has been shown to play an important role in the regulation of inflammatory response during CDI [20], we investigated whether CSPG4 blockade could modulate the expression of these cytokines in enteric glia under TcdA or TcdB challenge. We found that blocking CSPG4 decreased S100B gene expression in enteric glia exposed to TcdB (p = 0.03, Figure S3A), but not TcdA. Interestingly, CSPG4 blockade did not reduce S100B levels in the enteric glia supernatant (Figure S3B), suggesting that CSPG4 inhibition may not prevent the early increase in S100B.

CSPG4 Mediates Enteric Glia Death Induced by TcdA, but Not TcdB

C. difficile toxins have been shown to promote enteric glia death in vitro [21, 22, 23, 27, 28] and in vivo [21]. One of the mechanisms of cell death promoted by TcdA or TcdB involves caspase 3 and 7 activation [21, 22, 23].

Blockade of CSPG4 decreased TcdA‐induced, but not TcdB‐induced, enteric glia death (Figure S4A). Interestingly, CSPG4 blockade did not prevent TcdA or TcdB‐induced increased caspase‐3/7 activation (Figure S4B). Bcl2 expression also remained unchanged across groups exposed to C. difficile toxins, indicating no modulation of antiapoptotic pathways by CSPG4 inhibition (Figure S4C).

CSPG4 Modulates TcdA‐ and TcdB‐Induced Senescence in Enteric Glial Cells

Previous studies showed that enteric glia surviving TcdB exhibit a senescence phenotype [8]. We investigated whether CSPG4 blockade prevents senescence in enteric glial cells challenged with TcdA or TcdB using an immunofluorescence assay with the senescence‐associated β‐galactosidase (SA‐β‐Gal) marker and other markers (γH2AX, p16, and p21). Our time‐course analysis (48 h, 72 h, 96 h, 120 h, and 144 h) revealed that TcdA or TcdB induced a significant increase in SA‐β‐Gal staining in enteric glia compared to control cells, beginning at 96 h (Figures 6A and S5). We also found that the senescence study in enteric glia at time points beyond 120 h is not feasible due to increased senescence in control cells (Figures 6A and S5). Blockade of CSPG4 decreased SA‐β‐Gal staining in enteric glia exposed to TcdA (p = 0.01, Figure 6B,C) or TcdB (p = 0.0002, Figure 6B,C) after 96 h of initial challenge. In addition, blockade of CSPG4 also reduced other senescence markers, γH2AX (p < 0.0001, Figure 6D,E), p16 (TcdA: p < 0.0001, TcdB: p = 0.02, Figure 6D–F), and p21 (p < 0.0001, Figure 6D–G) in enteric glia exposed to TcdA or TcdB after 96 h of initial challenge.

Discussion

Here, we found that enteric glia in both humans and mice, as well as in rats, express all known receptors for TcdA (LDLR) and TcdB (CSPG4, FZD‐1, FZD2, FZD7 2, PVRL3, TFPI, and LRP1). Previously, FZD7 expression was reported in human enteric glia using an available scRNA‐seq dataset, and CSPG4 was not detected in enteric glia expressing PLP1, which was detected by using a dual reporter mouse, under physiological conditions [19]. In contrast, we validated our findings by labeling enteric glia with GFAP, thereby improving our ability to detect cells expressing CSPG4. In a mouse preclinical model of CDI, infected mice with global CSPG4 deletion showed less severe disease and decreased colonic damage [17]. Similarly, decreased inflammation was also detected in cecal tissues of CSPG4‐depleted mice intoxicated by TcdB [19]. Together, these previous data highlighted that CSPG4 plays an important role in the pathogenesis of CDI.

Although PVRL3 and LRP1 were the most expressed TcdB receptors in human, mouse, and rat enteric glia under physiological conditions, the expression of CSPG4 was the most upregulated under C. difficile toxin challenge. Our data suggest that the upregulation of CSPG4 is induced by both C. difficile toxins, TcdA and TcdB, in enteric glia in an equivalent manner. In a preclinical model of colitis induced by dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) in mice, CSPG4 was decreased in damaged colonic tissues [30]. Non‐infectious and infectious colitis can employ distinct mechanisms to promote inflammation, which may explain differences in CSPG4 expression. As shown in pancreatic cancer cells, hypoxia positively regulates CSPG4 expression [31]. Upregulation of hypoxia‐related genes has been previously reported in tissues exposed to C. difficile toxin [32].

Another important finding was that CSPG4 blockade decreased nuclear translocation of the transcription factors NFκB and STAT3, as well as IL‐6 gene expression in enteric glia challenged with TcdB, but not TcdA. Several studies have shown that the participation of different receptors on enteric glia in response to TcdA and TcdB can vary [21], which could partially explain the differences found in our study. IL‐6 is one of the most relevant pro‐inflammatory cytokines, as it plays a significant role in regulating various physiological and pathological processes, particularly in acute and chronic inflammation, infectious diseases, and neoplastic diseases [33]. IL‐6 upregulation primarily occurs through activation of the NFκB pathway. Previously, our research group showed that this pathway was crucial for IL‐6 expression in enteric glia challenged with TcdA or TcdB [20]. Once activated, NFκB migrates to the cell nucleus and binds to the IL‐6 gene promoter, triggering transcription of this interleukin [34]. Secreted IL‐6, in turn, leads to the activation of STAT3. Once activated, STAT3 can drive increased IL‐6 production, creating a positive feedback loop that further amplifies the inflammatory response [29]. More studies are needed to better understand the biological relevance of the IL‐6 and CSPG4 signaling relationship in cells exposed to TcdB.

S100B is a key modulator of inflammation during CDI and of IL‐6 synthesis promoted by C. difficile toxins in enteric glia [20]. In our study, CSPG4 was shown to be important in modulating S100B gene expression in enteric glia challenged with TcdB, but not with TcdA. Blocking it promoted the downregulation of S100B expression after 18 h of challenge. However, blocking CSPG4 did not prevent the TcdB‐induced increase in S100B secretion at 18 h. In a previous study, S100B gene expression in enteric glial cells and S100B secretion into the extracellular medium began to increase after 6 h of incubation with TcdB [20]. These findings may explain why S100B levels remain elevated in the supernatant, as they were quantified at 18 h of incubation with the toxin. Still, the protein may have accumulated earlier.

CSPG4 inhibition also decreased enteric glial death promoted by TcdA, but not TcdB. In enteric glia, TcdA and TcdB have been shown to promote cell death via apoptosis [21, 23], as also shown in this study. However, the attenuation of cell death promoted by CSPG4 blockade in enteric glia exposed to TcdA is unlikely to occur due to the decrease in caspase 3 and 7, which are known effectors of apoptosis [35]. In other cells, such as peritoneal macrophages, TcdA and TcdB induced cell death via pyroptosis [36]. On the other hand, TcdB can also promote cell death via autophagy [37] and necrosis [38].

Interestingly, our findings also showed that both TcdA and TcdB induce a robust senescence phenotype in enteric glia, characterized by SA‐β‐Gal activity, DNA damage (γH2AX), and elevated p21 and p16 expression. When used in combination, these markers are widely recognized as indicators of cellular senescence [39, 40]. Senescence in enteric glia post‐TcdB challenge was previously reported [8]. TcdB at a concentration of 10 ng/mL induced senescence in enteric glial cells that survived the action of the toxin, which predominantly induces death by apoptosis/necrosis after 6 days of exposure. According to the authors, TcdB‐induced senescence in enteric glia primarily triggers irreversible cell cycle arrest in G0/G1 and G2/M, mediated by the overexpression of p27 associated with the downregulation of cyclin‐dependent kinase 1 (CDK1) and cyclin B1, persistent DNA damage, downregulation of c‐myc, and hypophosphorylation of phosphorylated retinoblastoma protein (pRb) [8]. Cellular senescence is characterized by irreversible cell cycle arrest, changes in cellular metabolism, and alterations in cellular morphology [41]. It can be caused by antitumor drugs, irradiation, oxidative stress, and aging that damage DNA [42]. As shown in our study, senescence can also be caused by bacteria‐derived toxins such as colibactin from Escherichia coli [43] and pyocyanin from Pseudomonas aeruginosa [44]. Here, we show that CSPG4 signaling contributes to persistent damage in enteric glia exposed to C. difficile toxins. However, the underlying mechanisms remain to be further elucidated.

In our study, we found that analyzing persistent damage in cells in vitro at time points beyond day 6 post‐ C. difficile toxin challenge is not feasible due to the physiological senescence process in non‐challenged cells. In fact, cells maintained at confluence can exhibit senescence‐related activity due to cellular aging [45]. Previous studies revealed that cellular senescence is involved in several gastrointestinal diseases, such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and colorectal carcinogenesis [46, 47], and even irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) [48]. GI dysfunction has been previously reported post‐CDI in humans [5, 6, 7] and mice [6]. However, the relationship between GI dysfunction and senescence in ENS cells remains to be elucidated in the context of CDI using in vivo models.

In summary, our findings suggest that CSPG4 plays an important role in mediating the inflammatory response induced by TcdB in enteric glia by partially mediating upregulation of S100B and the nuclear translocation of the transcription factors NFκB and STAT3, which in turn increase the expression of IL‐6, a known important marker associated with mortality in CDI patients [49]. Although CSPG4 does not contribute to the inflammatory response promoted by TcdA in enteric glia, the activity of this receptor contributes to cell death. Finally, our findings also showed an important participation of CSPG4 in promoting senescence in enteric glia exposed to TcdA or TcdB. Future studies should extend these findings using preclinical models and human tissues from patients recovering from CDI to better define the temporal dynamics of senescence, identify the broader cellular network involved, and determine whether targeting CSPG4 can reverse senescence following infection.
