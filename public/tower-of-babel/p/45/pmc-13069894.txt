Oscillatory alpha and aperiodic neural correlates of visual working memory and attention
Authors: Molly A Erickson, Charlotte Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo date, the physiological mechanisms that support visual working memory (WM) remain poorly understood. One reason is that a typical WM task is unable to distinguish between multiple underlying sub-processes (e.g. perception, attentional control, consolidation/storage), which map onto different neural signals. We used a modified change detection task sequence to examine electroencephalography (EEG) correlates of perception, attentional engagement, and memory consolidation/storage in a sample of 42 individuals. Additionally, we applied a novel analysis approach designed to disentangle the aperiodic and oscillatory components of the EEG signal during the prestimulus and the poststimulus period. We observed: (i) aperiodic parameters were significantly elevated during the pretrial period of the memory consolidation/storage task variant compared to the perception and attention task variants; (ii) prestimulus aperiodic parameters and oscillatory alpha were significantly associated with WM capacity and attentional control, respectively; and (iii) unexpectedly, higher oscillatory alpha was associated with better attentional engagement across participants, as well as within participants. Poststimulus aperiodic parameters and alpha oscillations showed a load-dependent effect in the poststimulus period; however, these parameters were not associated with task performance. This work suggests that aperiodic and alpha oscillatory elements of the EEG signal may serve unique roles in WM capacity and attention.Keywords: alpha, aperiodic, attention, EEG, visual working memory

Introduction

Visual working memory (WM) is a cognitive domain that is associated with intellectual functioning (Engle et al. 1999; Cowan et al. 2005; Fukuda et al. 2010) and academic achievement (Aronen et al. 2005). However, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. One reason for this is that many paradigms fail to examine neural correlates of sub-process that unfold at different stages of a given task. For instance, previous literature has demonstrated that fluctuations in attentional state during a change detection paradigm significantly impact performance (Unsworth and Robison 2016). Alpha (9 to 13 Hz) oscillations within the electroencephalogram (EEG) have been identified as a marker of attentional state, with elevated power corresponding to states of low cortical excitability ideal for suppressing information and low power corresponding to states that are optimal for processing sensory input (Klimesch et al. 2007; Jensen and Mazaheri 2010; Morrow et al. 2023). A plausible hypothesis that emerges from this work is that elevated alpha power prior to the onset of a sample array will produce an attention lapse on that trial. Indeed, higher alpha power has been linked to attention lapsing during a language comprehension task (Boudewyn and Carter 2018) and poor performance on visual discrimination tasks (Ergenoglu et al. 2004; van Dijk et al. 2008; Paoletti et al. 2019).

Recent work also suggests that alpha oscillations are critical for item consolidation and storage once the trial has begun. Indeed, “suppression” of alpha power after the onset of the sample array has been linked to successful WM storage among psychiatrically healthy individuals (Sauseng et al. 2009; Bashivan et al. 2014; Chen et al. 2015; Fukuda et al. 2015; Pavlov and Kotchoubey 2017; Zammit et al. 2018; Erickson et al. 2019). Furthermore, evidence implicates poststimulus alpha suppression in the consolidation and storage aspects of WM; specifically, its association with WM capacity appears distinct from perceptual and attentional processes (Erickson et al. 2019), and cognitive effort (Kardan et al. 2020). Fukuda and colleagues additionally found that alpha suppression occurs during recall, suggesting that this process plays a role in bringing previously stored items back online into the WM buffer (Fukuda and Woodman 2017).

Altogether, this work indicates that fluctuations in alpha underlie critical aspects of WM task performance; however, open questions remain. For example, it is unknown whether the observed effects are truly due to oscillatory alpha, or if they are due to nonoscillatory, or “aperiodic,” components of the EEG signal. Traditional time–frequency analysis methods such as those utilizing Morlet wavelets generally cannot disambiguate alpha amplitude, which has been linked to attentional states and memory storage, from the amplitude and slope of the full spectrum, which has been linked to cortical excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) balance (Poil et al. 2012; Gao et al. 2017) and overall cognition (see Lu 2025 for a review). Following the application of a novel analysis approach (Donoghue et al. 2020, 2022) to a spatial WM task, we described separable contributions of oscillatory alpha and nonoscillatory broadband activity to task performance in a large sample of healthy individuals and people with serious mental illness. Surprisingly, we found that trial-by-trial fluctuations in prestimulus aperiodic parameters predicted attention lapsing during this task whereas prestimulus oscillatory alpha did not (Erickson et al. 2024). Such findings underscore the need for sophisticated analysis approaches to better understand which elements of the EEG map on to task performance.

In the present study, we used a modified change detection task sequence that permitted separate measurement of the electrophysiological responses to passive perception, active attentional engagement, and memory consolidation/storage of the sample arrays (Erickson et al. 2019). We first sought to determine the impact of task demands (perceive, attend, remember) on oscillatory and nonoscillatory parameters. Second, we aimed to replicate our previously published and unexpected effect in which aperiodic parameters were associated with behavioral measures of attention lapsing, whereas oscillatory alpha was not (Erickson et al. 2024). Third, we examined poststimulus suppression of oscillatory alpha and aperiodic parameters and their association with behavioral measures of memory capacity. Finally, we examined the specificity of the observed effects to the alpha frequency band by additionally measuring theta (4 to 8 Hz), beta (14 to 30 Hz), and gamma (31 to 40 Hz).

Materials and methods

Participants

Forty-three psychiatrically healthy individuals completed the present study. One participant was removed from analysis following artifact rejection procedures, for a final sample of 42 individuals (21 male; 21 female). All participants were between the ages of 18 and 60 (mean age = 36.05 years, SD = 11.23) and were free from a history of serious mental illness or mood disorder. They had no current substance use disorder, which was operationalized as meeting diagnostic criteria for the condition at any point in the past 3 months, and no history of significant neurologic injury. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, which was verified using the Ishihara test for colorblindness and the Snellen eye chart. To estimate overall cognitive ability, the 4-subtest version of the Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence (WASI-2; Wechsler 2011) was used (mean IQ = 104.56; SD = 12.96). All participants gave informed consent to participate, and all study procedures were approved by the University of Chicago Institutional Review Board (IRB19-1704).

Experimental paradigm

All participants completed three variants on a visual task, which is depicted in Fig. 1. The stimuli were presented on an LCD monitor with a gray background (190,190,190) and a continuously visible central fixation cross at a nominal viewing distance of 100 cm. For all three task variants, each trial began with a blank prestimulus period of 1,500 ms, followed by a sample array containing 1 to 5 colored squares that appeared for 300 ms. The squares were arranged around an invisible circle with a radius of 4.1 degrees, and each square measured 0.66 × 0.66 degrees visual angle. The colors of the squares in the array were highly discriminable and selected randomly and without replacement from the following list of colors: dark brown (140,81,10), tan (216,179,101), magenta (197,27,125), light pink (233,163,201), yellow (255,255,0), sage (161,215,106), teal (77,146,33), midnight blue (1,102,94), and sky blue (90,180,172). This array was followed by a blank delay period of 1,700 ms.

Example task sequence at set size 4. During the task, participants passively viewed the sample array A); actively attended to the array to make a button-press response when a target color was present within the sample array B); and actively stored items in working memory, making a button-press response when a probe appeared to indicate whether the color of the square had changed or stayed the same C).

In the Passive (WM−) task variant (Fig. 1A), participants were instructed to passively view the sample arrays and make no response. To ensure adequate engagement, participants were given an additional, irrelevant task in which they were intermittently asked to rate neutral images on a pleasantness scale from 1 to 5. In the Active (WM−) task variant (Fig. 1B), participants were instructed to attend to the sample array and press a button when they detected a target color appear, which occurred on 10% of trials. The target color was randomly selected for each participant and remained constant throughout the Active (WM−) condition. To eliminate any memory requirements on this task, the continuously present fixation cross was changed to the target color for the full duration of the Active (WM−) condition. To eliminate the impact of motor activity on the measurement of periodic and aperiodic variables during the delay period, only target-absent (ie 90%) of the Active (WM−) trials were included in the final analysis. Finally, in the Active (WM+) task, the 1,700-ms delay period was followed by the presentation of a single square from the sample array (Fig. 1C). On 50% of trials, the square changed to a new color not previously represented in the sample array for that trial; on the other 50% of trials, the color of the square remained the same. Participants were asked to indicate by button-press response whether the color of the square remained the same. All set sizes were equally possible and randomized within each of the 4 blocks per condition. The order of the conditions was also randomized across participants, with 32 trials of each set size in each condition (480 trials total).

E‌EG recording and analysis

The EEG was recorded from 61 scalp electrodes at a rate of 1,000 Hz using a Brain Products active electrode ActiCHamp system with a cascaded integrator-comb antialiasing filter (half-power cutoff at 280 Hz) and a reference at P9. Vertical and horizontal electro-oculograms were also recorded by placing one electrode underneath the right eye and two electrodes lateral to the external canthi. Offline data processing was conducted in MATLAB (The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA) using the EEGLAB (Delorme and Makeig 2004) and ERPLAB (Lopez-Calderon and Luck 2014) toolboxes. Briefly, the data were high-pass-filtered at 0.05 Hz and re-referenced to the average of P9/P10, which is similar to an average mastoid reference. Bad channels were identified using visual inspection and marked for interpolation, and independent component analysis (ICA) was performed using the unmarked channels to remove blink and eye movement artifacts, as described in Luck (2021). The EEG was then segmented into 4-s epochs (1,500 ms prestimulus) and baseline adjusted to the average prestimulus voltage. Epochs containing (i) amplitudes exceeding ±200 μV or (ii) peak-to-peak amplitudes exceeding 150 μV within a 200-ms moving window were marked for rejection using ERPLAB artifact rejection algorithms. Finally, a visual inspection of the data was conducted to remove any remaining artifacts, and all participants retaining fewer than 50% of trials (n = 1) were removed from further analysis. On average, participants retained an average of 81.756%, 82.488%, and 84.791% of trials for the Passive WM−, Active WM−, and Active WM+ conditions, respectively. Mean, minimum, and maximum trial counts per condition, per set size, can be found in the supplementary materials.

Extraction of periodic and aperiodic variables

The magnitude of six EEG parameters (described below) was measured during the prestimulus time period (−1,500 to −37 ms), as well as the poststimulus time period (50 to 1,463 ms). To do this, the data from each epoch were first segmented into 20 overlapping windows of 513 samples beginning at −1,500 to −987 ms and ending at −550 to −37 ms with 463 ms overlap (prestimulus period) and 19 overlapping windows of 513 samples beginning at 50 to 563 ms and ending at 950 to 1463 ms with 463-ms overlap (poststimulus period). This was done separately for each epoch and for each electrode. A fast Fourier transform (FFT) was then applied to each of the Hamming-tapered segments, which were subsequently averaged to create a single spectrum representing the prestimulus period and a single spectrum representing the poststimulus period, separately for each epoch and each electrode. This was done to optimize the signal-to-noise ratio of the single-trial spectra, thereby improving subsequent model fits; average R2 values for all parameters were >0.92 (see supplementary materials for goodness-of-fit statistics by parameter). Periodic and aperiodic parameters were extracted from these single-trial spectra using the Spectral Parameterization Python toolbox (Donoghue et al. 2020; see Supplementary Materials for model details and illustration of how the parameters were extracted from the individual trial spectra). Two aperiodic measures were extracted from each spectrum: (i) offset, or the uniform shift in power across all frequencies, and (ii) exponent, or the negative slope of the power spectrum, which captures the balance of high- to low-frequency amplitude. The dashed blue lines in Fig. S1 indicate the model fit of these aperiodic parameters, with offset reflecting the y-intercept in log-transformed space, and exponent reflecting the steepness of the slope as the frequency increases. Areas of the spectra where the solid blue lines (full model fit) rise above the dashed blue lines indicate the presence of oscillations within that band of interest. Four oscillatory measures were extracted from each segment, which reflects the amplitude of the power spectrum in the appropriate frequency band after adjusting for the aperiodic components: theta (4 to 8 Hz), alpha (9 to 13 Hz), beta (14 to 30 Hz), and gamma (31 to 40 Hz). The shaded blue area between the full model and aperiodic curve in Fig. S1 illustrates example trials with (Fig. S1A and C) and without (Fig. S1B) alpha oscillations. Figure S1D shows the average of all trials with an alpha oscillation present (solid blue line) compared to the average of all trials in which no alpha oscillation was present (dotted blue line).

If no peak was identified for a given frequency band, the parameter was set to zero. For the prestimulus period, peaks were detected for 49.84% (SD = 20.31%), 77.81% (SD = 19.04%), 91.29% (SD = 8.21%), and 37.50% (SD = 18.42%) of trials for theta, alpha, beta, and gamma, respectively. For the poststimulus period, peaks were detected for 39.97% (SD = 17.57%), 76.76% (SD = 15.53%), 90.18% (SD = 6.74%), and 42.18% (SD = 19.37%) of trials for theta, alpha, beta, and gamma, respectively. Poststimulus change (Δ) in parameter magnitude was calculated by subtracting the average prestimulus parameter magnitude from the average poststimulus parameter magnitude.

Statistical analyses

To quantify performance in the Active (WM+) condition, two parameters were estimated from the full set of behavioral responses across set sizes, separately for each participant: KMAX, or WM storage capacity, and Attn, or the probability that the participant was paying attention on a given trial (Rouder et al. 2011). The formula for calculating these two parameters can be found in the supplementary materials. Additionally, d’ was calculated from the Active (WM−) task using the formula d’ = z(H) − z(F), where H represents the hit rate and F represents the false alarm rate.

To examine the impact of task condition on EEG measures, repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVAs) were conducted and follow-up t-tests were conducted where appropriate. As nonnormal distributions were observed in some of the behavioral parameters (d’, Attn), Spearman correlation coefficients were conducted to examine the association between EEG measures and overall behavioral performance. Finally, we were interested in the degree to which trial-by-trial modulation of EEG parameters was associated with behavioral performance. To examine this, we applied a logistic linear mixed-effects regression model using the lme4 package in R, with standardized parameter magnitude as the continuous predictor variable and accuracy (0/1) as the outcome variable. All continuous predictor variables were transformed into Z-scores using the scale() function in R. The model included random intercepts for participant and trial. We performed separate logistic regression analyses for sub-capacity (ie set sizes 1 and 2) and super-capacity (ie set sizes 4 and 5) arrays. Across all participants, there were 178/1,625 inaccurate/accurate sub-capacity trials, and 511/1,301 inaccurate/accurate super-capacity trials used in the analysis. For the Active (WM−) regression analysis, there were 49/5,535 miss/correct rejection trials across all participants.

Results

Task performance

Overall, participants had an average KMAX of 2.80 items (SD = 0.93) and an average Attn parameter of 0.88 (SD = 0.13), indicating that they were estimated to be attentive on 88% of trials. The Active (WM−) task was designed to elicit near-ceiling performance, and accordingly, the average d’ value for this task was 4.13 (SD = 0.78). There were no task-relevant behavioral measures from the Passive (WM−) task variant.

Prestimulus EEG parameters by task condition

As the set sizes were intermixed within the condition block, the EEG signal for the prestimulus period was averaged across set sizes and is depicted in Fig. 2. The prestimulus signal for each parameter was measured at electrode sites in which the signal was maximal across all conditions, which are depicted in white. A repeated-measures ANOVA revealed that there was a significant effect of condition for the two aperiodic parameters (offset, exponent; F2,82’s > 10.85; P’s < 0.001; ηp2 > 0.20), with significantly larger values in the Active (WM+) condition compared to the Passive (WM−) and Active (WM−) conditions (P’s < 0.001; Cohen’s d > 0.59), the latter two of which were statistically similar to one another for both parameters (P’s = 0.238–0.797; Cohen’s d < 0.19). Similarly, prestimulus beta power differed significantly across conditions (F2,82 = 16.14; P < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.28), with post hoc analyses revealing that the amplitude of prestimulus beta oscillations was significantly larger in the Active (WM+) condition compared to the other two conditions (P’s < 0.001; Cohen’s d > 0.58), which were statistically similar to one another (P = 0.189; Cohen’s d = 0.21). Finally, prestimulus theta power also differed across conditions (F2,82 = 3.94; P = 0.023; ηp2 = 0.09), with significantly lower amplitude in the Active (WM+) condition compared to the other two conditions (P’s < 0.037; Cohen’s d > 0.33), which were not significantly different from one another (P = 0.587; Cohen’s d = 0.08). Neither prestimulus alpha (F2,82 = 1.85; P = 0.163; ηp2 = 0.04) nor prestimulus gamma (F2,82 = 2.13; P = 0.125; ηp2 = 0.05) differed significantly by condition.

In the Active (WM−) task, d’ was significantly associated with the exponent parameter (r = 0.34; P = 0.028), but not offset (r = 0.01; P = 0.974) or any of the oscillatory parameters (|r|’s < 0.12; P’s > 0.475). We were next interested in the question of whether trial-by-trial modulation in aperiodic and periodic parameters during the pretrial period predicted attention lapsing and memory storage during the trial. Of note, this is a different question from whether prestimulus parameters are associated with performance “on average,” across participants. Attention lapsing was operationalized in two ways: (i) an incorrect response (ie a miss or a false alarm) on Active (WM−) trial types and (ii) an incorrect response at sub-capacity set sizes (set sizes 1 and 2) on Active (WM+) trial types. Memory storage was operationalized as accuracy on super-capacity set sizes (set sizes 4 and 5) for Active (WM+) trial types. In all three cases, a logistic linear regression model was applied, with normalized prestimulus parameter magnitude as the continuous predictor variable and accuracy (0/1) as the outcome variable. All participants with a KMAX of < 2 (n = 7) were eliminated from the second of the three models, and all participants with a KMAX > 4 (n = 2) were eliminated from the third of the three models. This was done to ensure that the fundamental assumption of the analysis—that set sizes 1 & 2 were sub-capacity and set sizes 4 & 5 were super-capacity—was met. We observed that none of the prestimulus EEG parameters predicted attention lapsing on Active (WM−) trial types (all |β|’s < 0.23; all P’s > 0.219). However, greater prestimulus oscillatory alpha significantly predicted greater accuracy for sub-capacity set sizes on Active (WM+) trial types (β = 0.444; SE = 0.153; P = 0.004; see Fig. 4). By contrast, only prestimulus exponent predicted subsequent performance on super-capacity set size trials (β = 0.135; SE = 0.067; P = 0.044). The full sample showed a similar pattern (see Fig. S2).

Taken together, these results suggest that individual differences in aperiodic elements of the EEG signal are associated with WM capacity; however, the trial-by-trial modulation of those aperiodic parameters does not predict task performance within an individual. By contrast, elevated alpha power during the prestimulus period appeared to be associated with better sustained attention both across individuals and within individuals, as evidenced by a significant association with Attn parameter and significant β parameters following the trial-level logistic regression.

Post-stimulus EEG parameters by task condition

Post-stimulus change in parameter magnitude (Δ) was calculated by subtracting the prestimulus parameter magnitude (measured during the 1,500- to 37-ms prestimulus period) from the poststimulus parameter magnitude (measured during the 50- to 1,463-ms poststimulus period). The poststimulus signal for each parameter was measured at electrode sites in which the change in signal was maximal across all conditions, which are depicted in white, and averaged across time bins within the poststimulus period. Of note, offsetΔ and exponentΔ did not exhibit any spatial specificity, and therefore all scalp channels were used to calculate the average for these parameters. Additionally, given that several of the parameters exhibited significant effects of condition in the prestimulus interval, the effect of condition on poststimulus Δ in parameter magnitude largely mirrors that from the prestimulus analysis. Poststimulus EEG parameters and task performance

We next turned to examining the association between Δ parameters and measures of attention lapsing and WM capacity. To reduce the impact of prestimulus parameter magnitude on estimates of the association between poststimulus Δ parameters and task performance, we calculated the slope of each Δ parameter as a function of set size, separately for each participant. In doing so, we aimed to isolate the magnitude of modulation as a function of load, which cannot be attributable to prestimulus effects given that set sizes were randomly intermixed within blocks. Spearman correlations between Δ parameter slopes and behavioral measures of sustained attention (d’ from the Active [WM−] task, Attn parameter from the Active [WM+] task) and WM capacity (KMAX from the Active [WM+] task) were then calculated.

Broadly, we observed that although oscillatory alphaΔ and the two Δ aperiodic parameters exhibited a significant linear increase as a function of set size, none of the slopes were significantly associated with KMAX or Attn parameters from the Active (WM+) task (|r|’s < 0.20; P’s > 0.206; see Fig. S3 for scatterplots of rank–order associations between these variables). Similarly, none of the slopes from these three parameters were significantly associated with d’ from the Active (WM−) task (|r|'s < 0.07; P’s > 0.665). Given that neither thetaΔ, betaΔ, nor gammaΔ exhibited a significant effect of set size, significant correlations between slopes from these oscillatory parameters and behavioral markers were neither expected nor observed (|r’s| < 21; P’s > 0.194).

Finally, logistic linear regression models were again applied to examine the association between within-subject fluctuations in Δ parameters and behavioral performance measures on the Active (WM−) task and Active (WM+) task. Here, we found that thetaΔ was significantly associated with accuracy on the Active (WM−) task, with larger suppression of theta in the poststimulus period associated with better accuracy (β = −0.31; SE = 0.14; P = 0.028). None of the other poststimulus Δ parameters predicted attention lapsing on Active (WM−) trial types (all |β|’s < 0.21; all P’s > 0.24). Somewhat unsurprisingly given the observed effects from pretrial alpha, we found that larger alphaΔ was significantly associated with better accuracy for sub-capacity set sizes on Active (WM+) trial types (β = −0.332; SE = 0.137; P = 0.015; see Fig. 6). None of the Δ parameters were significantly associated with performance on super-capacity set size trials (all |β|’s < 0.087; all P’s > 0.118). The full sample showed a similar pattern (see Fig. S4).

Discussion

The present study was designed to examine the association of aperiodic and periodic elements of the EEG signal with cognitive sub-processes that support visual WM task performance. We found that aperiodic parameters (offset and exponent) were significantly elevated during the pretrial baseline period in the Active (WM+) condition compared to the other two conditions, along with elevated oscillatory beta and reduced oscillatory theta. Furthermore, larger (ie more positive) aperiodic parameters during the baseline period were significantly associated with higher WM capacity across participants, which is consistent with previous work showing a positive association between aperiodic parameters and cognitive functioning (Thuwal et al. 2021; Finley et al. 2024), as well as task difficulty (Lu et al. 2024). We speculate that there are two possible explanations for this observed association: First, it may be that trait-like, individual differences in the aperiodic elements of the EEG signal reflect a generalized cortical E/I balance that covaries with overall cognitive capacity; such an interpretation would be consistent with observations that aperiodic parameters are associated with cognitive functioning even in the absence of an overt cognitive task (ie during the resting state; Finley et al. 2024; McKeon et al. 2024). Alternately, and in line with the present findings that pretrial offset and exponent parameters were larger in the Active (WM+) task condition, it is possible that there is a cognitively meaningful, anticipatory “shift” in cortical E/I balance that enhances performance under demanding task conditions. Future studies will be needed to adjudicate between these two possible explanations.

Although pretrial alpha power was not significantly impacted by task condition, we observed a significant association with attention such that higher pretrial alpha was associated with higher estimates of sustained attention on the WM task across participants. This association between higher alpha power and greater attentional control was also observed within participants: a logistic regression analysis revealed that trial-by-trial modulation of pretrial alpha power was significantly associated with behavioral evidence of attentional control (accuracy at set sizes 1 and 2) but not memory storage (accuracy at set sizes 4 and 5). Of note, this same relationship between alpha and attentional state was not observed for the Active (WM−) task. We believe that the absence of this relationship is due to the fact that the Active (WM−) task was intended to produce near-ceiling performance; as such, there were very few “miss” trials to enter into the logistic regression (49 total across all participants). By the same token, there was little behavioral variability in overall d’ rates across subjects, which hinders the ability to detect a relationship between attentional state and alpha amplitude. We expect that more cognitively challenging tasks, such as the Active (WM+) task, are needed to reveal such relationships.

Overall, this pattern of results suggests that (i) individual differences in aperiodic elements of the baseline EEG signal have important implications for understanding cognitive functioning, and (ii) contrary to expectations, higher alpha power is associated with “fewer” attention lapses, not more. This work expands upon our previous observation that elevated pretrial oscillatory alpha was not associated with measures of attention lapsing during a spatial WM task (Erickson et al. 2024)—a finding that was unexpected at the time. Here, we demonstrate a significant “benefit” of elevated pretrial alpha with respect to sub-capacity task performance, further underscoring a dissociation between predictions emerging from previous studies of alpha as a marker of attentional state and evidence emerging from visual WM tasks.

One possible explanation for this dissociation is that paradigms demonstrating a relationship between elevated alpha and attention lapsing tend to utilize stimuli that require subtle visual discrimination or do not otherwise capture attention. For example, van Dijk et al. (2008) asked participants to make subtle distinctions between briefly presented (16-ms) gray disks with and without a target contrast. These authors found that pretrial alpha power was higher for trials in which this faint contrast target was not detected. Similarly, during a listening task, spontaneous elevations in alpha power over the course of an extended period were associated with self-reports of attention lapsing when participants were retroactively probed to report their attentional state (Boudewyn and Carter 2018). In both the present study and our previous study, the onset of each trial was salient and unambiguous; the duration of the sample stimuli (300 to 500 ms) and the elimination of the need to make subtle distinctions between the visual items in the sample array may have obscured a causal relationship between pretrial alpha and attention lapsing. Yet another possibility is that the use of the spectral parameterization analysis approach fundamentally alters the relationship between task performance and alpha power that was previously captured using more traditional time–frequency analysis. Although a direct comparison between traditional time–frequency analysis and spectral parameterization is beyond the scope of the present study, future work may directly test this hypothesis using tasks that are optimally designed to capture subtle variance in attentional state from trial to trial. A final intriguing possibility is that elevated alpha power during the pretrial period is somehow adaptive under certain contexts. For example, it may serve to clear the contents of the WM buffer in anticipation of the next trial or to provide a baseline for optimal poststimulus suppression of oscillatory alpha once the trial has begun. Future studies may directly test these interesting possibilities.

We next examined poststimulus suppression of aperiodic and periodic EEG parameters. Here, we observed that only the two aperiodic parameters and oscillatory alpha exhibited a significant effect of set size or interaction between condition and set size. That is, as set size increased, the magnitude of poststimulus suppression also increased in the Active (WM+) task condition and in the Active (WM−) condition in the case of oscillatory alpha. These observations indicate a functional role for poststimulus suppression of these parameters in WM consolidation/maintenance, specifically: Given that aperiodic parameters were not modulated by set size during the Passive (WM–) nor the Active (WM–), it may be concluded that offsetΔ and exponentΔ are associated with the effortful consolidation and/or storage of items in memory and are not reflective of lower-level sensory or attentional processes. Similarly, alphaΔ increased as a function of task demands, with Active (WM+) showing the largest change from baseline. This replicates previous findings from our work (Erickson et al. 2019), as well as others’ (eg Fukuda et al. 2015) using more traditional time–frequency analysis measures. However, unlike in previous studies, we did not find a significant association between the magnitude of poststimulus suppression and task performance. Only trial-by-trial alphaΔ exhibited a significant association with better task performance at sub-capacity arrays—a mirror of the effects observed from the pretrial period. Given that the analysis approach cannot disentangle prestimulus alpha from alphaΔ, it is not possible to determine the unique importance of poststimulus suppression of oscillatory alpha for successful WM storage from the present work. However, using creative experimental approaches, others have found that the magnitude of alpha suppression during the poststimulus period is associated with the effortful allocation of attention (Chen et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2021; Woodman et al. 2022; although see Kardan et al. 2020)—a finding that dovetails with our own work showing increased alpha suppression in the Active task conditions only.

Conclusion

Altogether, the present work suggests that both oscillatory alpha and aperiodic elements of the EEG are meaningfully associated with sustained attention and WM capacity in psychiatrically healthy individuals. These associations are most robustly demonstrated in the pretrial period. It must be noted, however, that although the prestimulus measurement windows were identical for all three conditions, the Passive (WM−) and Active (WM−) conditions had longer delays in between the onset of sample arrays as a result of the task structure. Thus, residual activity from the preceding trial may have impacted the beginning of the delay period from the Active (WM+) task in ways that would not have impacted the other two task conditions. Despite these measurement challenges, the elevated offset and exponent parameters observed during the pretrial interval appear to reflect a cognitively meaningful, anticipatory effect, which may explain the association with WM capacity in the present study and positive associations with cognition in other studies (Thuwal et al. 2021; Finley et al. 2024). Future work may consider systematically varying the ability to anticipate the onset of the sample array to determine the impact of expectation on aperiodic parameters.

A related issue is the fact that the impact of poststimulus suppression of the EEG parameters on WM is difficult to estimate due to its conflation with pretrial activity. Although the between-subjects association between task performance and poststimulus parameters could be separated from the prestimulus parameter magnitude by examining slope as a function of set size, this was not possible for the trial-by-trial mixed linear-effects modeling approach. Accordingly, the within-subjects association between task performance and poststimulus Δ parameters mirrored the effects from the prestimulus period. When attempting to include both parameters (pre and post) within the same logistic regression model, much of the variance was accounted for by prestimulus parameters and the specific contributions of poststimulus Δ parameters could not be isolated. This problem is not unique to the spectral parameterization approach, as more traditional time–frequency analyses (eg those using Morlet wavelets) are challenged by the same methodological issues. Nevertheless, the fact that there was a load-dependent effect on offsetΔ, exponentΔ, and alphaΔ in the Active (WM+) condition strongly suggests that Δ parameters play a functional role in consolidation and storage of items in memory. That is, given that set sizes were intermixed within block, as well as the absence of a similar effect in the Passive (WM−) and Active (WM−) conditions, this effect can only be explained by memory-related cognitive demands. Although the slopes of these effects were not associated with attention or memory in the present study, future experiments may be designed to disentangle the contributions of preparatory EEG modulation and poststimulus suppression on the sub-processes that support visual WM in healthy adults.
