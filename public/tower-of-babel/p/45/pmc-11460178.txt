Cryptococcal invasion: a comprehensive case of adrenal and systemic infection amid immunosuppression
Authors: Supeng Tai, Chaozhao Liang, Junyue Tao, Jun Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report a case of disseminated cryptococcosis, an uncommon fungal infection predominantly affecting the lungs and central nervous system, with the rare involvement of adrenal cryptococcosis, compounded by meningitis and pneumonia. The patient, previously diagnosed with primary myelofibrosis and undergoing oral Ruxolitinib treatment, exhibited immunosuppression. Imaging via chest and abdominal CT scans revealed inflammation in the right lung’s middle lobe, splenomegaly, a splenic lesion, and a left adrenal mass, initially prompting considerations of pheochromocytoma. However, unilateral adrenalectomy and subsequent pathological examination disclosed extensive infiltration by inflammatory and multinucleate giant cells, with Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining confirming the diagnosis. The identification of adrenal cryptococcosis was further supported by positive adrenal pus culture and significantly elevated capsular antigens in both serum and cerebrospinal fluid, at titers of 1:2560. Following a month of oral antifungal treatment, marked reductions in capsular antigen levels were noted, to 1:640 and 1:160 in serum and cerebrospinal fluid, respectively. The patient was discharged on a regimen of oral amphotericin B, flucytosine, and fluconazole, with regular outpatient follow-ups showing no signs of recurrence or dissemination.Keywords: Disseminated cryptococcosis, Adrenal cryptococcosis, Immunosuppress, Adrenalectomy

Introduction

Cryptococcosis is an infection caused by a spherical, encapsulated, yeast-like fungus. This condition predominantly afflicts immunocompromised individuals [1], such as those with HIV/AIDS, hematologic cancers, severe liver diseases, or those undergoing chemotherapy or immunosuppressive therapy, often spreading from the primary site to other organs including the lungs, central nervous system, skin, liver, spleen, lymph nodes, and pancreas. Disseminated cryptococcosis typically arises due to significant impairments in cell-mediated immune surveillance, such as from AIDS, immunosuppressive treatment, or organ transplantation [2]. Common presentations include meningitis and pneumonia, with patients frequently exhibiting symptoms like headaches, fatigue, weight loss, anorexia, and dizziness [3]. Adrenal cryptococcosis, however, is an uncommon clinical finding. Reports suggest that primary adrenal insufficiency symptoms such as hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, and skin pigmentation predominantly characterize adrenal cryptococcosis [2]. Humans are generally infected through direct or indirect contact with pigeon droppings or the inhalation of fungal spores. This report details a case of adrenal cryptococcosis accompanied by meningitis and pneumonia in a patient with a decade-long history of primary myelofibrosis and three years of oral Ruxolitinib treatment, highlighting immunosuppression as a contributing factor. Despite atypical symptoms, the patient underwent unilateral adrenalectomy and received oral medication, resulting in effective management of the disease. This case underscores the importance of considering fungal infections in immunocompromised patients, even when presenting symptoms are non-standard.

Case report

A 58-year-old female, previously diagnosed with myelofibrosis and under Ruxolitinib treatment for three years, was discovered to have a left adrenal gland mass during routine follow-up examinations, leading to her admission for further management. She presented with symptoms including fatigue, headache, left epigastric pain, fever, and chest oppression. Physical examination upon admission noted her vital signs as within normal limits, except for epigastric tenderness and distension.

Radiological evaluations present a patchy high-density shadow and air bronchogram sign in the middle lobe of the right lung, splenomegaly and a 65 mm×37 mm adrenal mass(Fig. 1A). Laboratory findings revealed elevated lactic dehydrogenase (LDH) levels, with normal serum potassium, sodium, creatinine, and glucose levels. Hematological analysis indicated moderate anemia, with reduced erythrocyte and platelet counts. Endocrinological assessments showed normal serum cortisol, adrenocorticotropic hormone, and aldosterone levels, suggesting intact adrenal endocrine function(Table 1).

Following admission, the patient experienced a fever spike to 39 °C, with further decline in leukocyte, erythrocyte, and platelet counts. Initial anti-infective treatment with piperacillin-tazobactam was escalated in dosage pre-surgery. Despite positive T-SPOT results, sputum cultures and mycobacterium smears returned negative.

A unilateral adrenalectomy was performed, revealing a 7.5 cm x 5 cm x 4 cm tumor with significant pus accumulation. Pathological examination highlighted extensive inflammatory and multinucleate giant cell infiltration with positive Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining, indicative of cryptococcal infection(Fig. 1B, C, D and E). Serum cryptococcal capsular antigen testing confirmed a high titer of 1:2560. Given the patient’s neurological symptoms, lumbar puncture was conducted, uncovering meningitis based on cerebrospinal fluid analysis and high cryptococcal capsular antigen levels.

The patient underwent the treatment of liposomal amphotericin B at 15 mg/d, Flucytosine at 2 g/d and Fluconazole at 0.6 g/d, resulting in significant reductions in cryptococcal antigen levels in both serum and CSF. The review of Computer tomograph scan of chest after two-weeks period of treatment showed the patchy high-density shadow had been absorbed. Despite initial treatment success, further adjustments in amphotericin B dosage were made based on recurring symptoms and disturbing laboratory tests 3 weeks later, and the dose of liposomal amphotericin B were increased to 20 mg/d. Under the treatment of liposomal amphotericin B at 20 mg/d, Flucytosine at 2 g/d and Fluconazole at 0.6 g/d for another fourteen days, ultimately, the patient was discharged on oral medications with regular follow-ups, demonstrating no recurrence signs(Fig. 1F).

This case underscores the necessity of considering fungal infections in immunocompromised patients, even with atypical presentations, and highlights the complexities of diagnosing and managing disseminated cryptococcosis with adrenal involvement.

Discussion

Cryptococcus, a spherical encapsulated yeast-like fungus primarily found in soil contaminated by pigeon or other bird droppings, poses varying clinical manifestations based on the host’s immune status. In immunocompetent individuals, symptoms such as cough, expectoration, chest tightness, and fever are common and nonspecific. Conversely, in immunocompromised hosts, cryptococcus infections often become unmanageable, leading to disseminated cryptococcosis, predominantly affecting the lungs and central nervous system and less frequently invading the adrenal glands, skin, liver, spleen, lymph nodes, and pancreas. The inaugural case of adrenal cryptococcosis was documented by A.J. Rawson in 1948, with merely 30 reported instances we could find since. Among these [2, 4–15], detailed treatment and prognosis are scarce, with satisfactory outcomes primarily through oral or intravenous medication in a minority. Notably, adrenal insufficiency was prevalent. Of the 30 articles we found, 16 described adrenal function, 13 of which had hypoadrenal function, and only 3 patients did not present the decreased function of adrenal glands. Surgical intervention was occasionally necessitated by inadequate drug response, generally leading to improved prognosis. (Fig. 2)

In our current case, the patient’s immunocompromised state, due to primary myelofibrosis and Ruxolitinib treatment, significantly contributed to her susceptibility to infection. Primary myelofibrosis, a hematologic disorder, can cause splenomegaly and hematocytopenia [16], reflecting an impaired immune system. Ruxolitinib, a JAK inhibitor, further suppresses various immune cells, underscoring the patient’s pronounced immunodeficiency [17]. Despite nonspecific clinical presentations and normal adrenal function tests, the case underscores the importance of vigilance for opportunistic infections in patients with immunosuppressive factors and suggests surgical exploration for definitive diagnosis and treatment. Both Akira Takeshita and Misa Ito have highlighted the adrenal glands as potential foci for fungemia, advocating for surgical intervention for more effective management [2, 9]. Previous reports primarily recommend adrenalectomy, which, in our case, significantly reduced serum and CSF cryptococcal antigen titers post-operation. Normal hormone level challenging the necessity of hormone replacement therapy post-unilateral adrenalectomy, especially considering potential risks of steroid treatments in fostering adrenal gland cryptococcosis [5]. Our approach, combining surgical resection of the primary lesion with pharmacotherapy, underscores the efficacy of integrated treatment strategies in managing disseminated cryptococcosis, as evidenced by the patient’s stable condition and absence of significant abnormalities upon six-month follow-up.
