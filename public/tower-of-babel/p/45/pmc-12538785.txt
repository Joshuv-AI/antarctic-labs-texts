FGF10 ameliorates epileptic seizures and related cognitive dysfunction via the FGFR2/CALB1 signaling pathway
Authors: Linqian Zhao, Shihao Chen, Xing Jin, Yuqing Xiao, Xu Zhang, Yaoyao Lin, Cheng Liang, Huiqin Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTemporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) is frequently associated with cognitive impairments, such as memory deficits, attention disorders, and executive dysfunction. Given that these cognitive deficits are closely linked to neuronal loss in TLE, fibroblast growth factor 10 (FGF10), a molecule recognized for its neuroprotective properties, has emerged as a promising therapeutic candidate. The kainic acid (KA)-induced epilepsy model can replicate key pathological features of TLE. The study aims to investigate the potential role of FGF10 in TLE, using the KA-induced model as an experimental framework.MethodsWe induced epilepsy in mice using KA and administered intranasal FGF10 over 14 days or delivered an AAV virus to overexpress FGF10. Seizure activity was monitored via video-electroencephalography (EEG), and behavioral tests were conducted to assess spatial cognition, anxiety-related behaviors, and depressive-like behaviors. Neuronal damage was evaluated using Nissl staining and TUNEL staining. To explore the molecular mechanisms underlying FGF10’s effects, we performed RNA sequencing, followed by validation with Western blotting and qRT-PCR. Additionally, we generated FGFR2 conditional knockout (cKO) mice to investigate the role of FGF10-FGFR2 signaling.ResultsFGF10 treatment significantly reduced seizure frequency and improved epilepsy-related cognitive deficits. It also exerted neuroprotective effects, as evidenced by reduced neuronal death in KA-induced epileptic mice. RNA sequencing revealed decreased CALB1 levels in the hippocampal dentate gyrus of epileptic mice, which were restored following FGF10 administration. Crucially, the therapeutic benefits of FGF10 were abolished in FGFR2-cKO mice, indicating that FGFR2 is essential for FGF10’s effects.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that FGF10 alleviates seizures and cognitive dysfunction in epilepsy, likely through FGFR2-dependent mechanisms involving CALB1 modulation. These results highlight FGF10 as a potential therapeutic target for epilepsy, offering a novel strategy for improving treatment outcomes in patients with TLE.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07185-9.Keywords: FGF10, Temporal lobe epilepsy, Epilepsy-related cognitive dysfunction, CALB1, Kainic acid

Introduction

Epilepsy is one of the most prevalent neurological disorders, accounting for approximately 1% of the global disease burden[1]. Temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE), the most common drug-resistant form in adults, typically originates in medial structures such as the hippocampus or amygdala. It often develops following acquired brain injuries caused by neuroinflammation, malformations, or prolonged seizures[2]. Clinically, TLE is characterized not only by recurrent seizures but also by frequent cognitive comorbidities—including deficits in memory, attention, and executive function—which substantially impair patients’ quality of life. Unfortunately, current treatment strategies provide inadequate control over seizures and offer limited benefit for associated cognitive impairments, highlighting an urgent need for novel therapeutic approaches.

Among the various neuropathological substrates of TLE, hippocampal sclerosis (HS) is one of the most common etiologies, particularly in adolescent and adult populations[3]. HS is characterized by neuronal loss, gliosis, neuroinflammation and synaptic reorganization within the hippocampus—a region crucial for memory formation and storage[4]. These pathological changes contribute not only to ictogenesis but also to cognitive decline. Importantly, underlying mechanisms such as neuronal apoptosis and neuroinflammation are increasingly recognized as shared pathways driving both seizure activity and cognitive dysfunction[5–7].

In animal studies, the kainic acid (KA)-induced mouse model is widely used to investigate the pathological mechanisms associated with TLE. This model recapitulates several key features of human TLE, including spontaneous recurrent seizures, unilateral hippocampal atrophy, gliosis, and neurodegeneration[8, 9]. Overall, the KA-induced mouse model serves as a vital tool for elucidating TLE pathogenesis and developing novel therapies targeting both seizures and cognitive deficits.

Fibroblast growth factor 10 (FGF10), a paracrine signaling molecule belonging to the FGF7/10/22 subfamily, plays crucial roles in development and tissue repair[10–12]. Emerging evidence indicates that FGF10 also confers neuroprotection under pathological conditions. It has been shown to promote axonal regeneration and functional recovery after peripheral nerve injury[13], and to inhibit neuroinflammation and apoptosis in models of cerebral ischemia[14] and traumatic brain injury[15]. Most recently, FGF10 was reported to improve neurological and cognitive function in Alzheimer’s disease models via activation of the FGFR2/PI3K/AKT pathway[16]. Given that neuroinflammation and apoptosis represent key mechanisms linking HS to both epileptogenesis and cognitive deficits in TLE, we hypothesized that FGF10 may exert beneficial effects in this disorder. Based on this rationale, the present study aims to evaluate the therapeutic potential of FGF10 in a KA-induced epileptic mice, with focus on its modulation of epileptic seizures and comorbid cognitive impairment.

Materials and methods

FGF10 ELISA analysis

Blood samples were collected from patients diagnosed with TLE at the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University, along with age-matched normal individuals. The samples were centrifuged at 2000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C (Ethical approval number: 2021-zx-030). Subsequently, serum samples were collected and stored at -80 °C until further analysis. The levels of FGF10 were measured according to the procedures outlined in the ELISA kit provided by the manufacturer (CSB-E14965h, Cusabio, China).

Animals

All animal experiments and methodologies conducted in this study adhered to ethical guidelines for animal research and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Wenzhou Medical University. Adult (8-week-old) male C57BL/6 J mice were purchased from Beijing Sibeifu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. To generate conditional knockout mice for FGFR2, LoxP sites were inserted flanking key exons of the FGFR2 gene using gene editing techniques, thus creating FGFR2-flox mice. FGFR2-flox mice were then crossed with Cre recombinase transgenic mice to obtain Cre-positive mice with homozygous knockout of the FGFR2 gene (Cre/ + ; FGFR2-flox/flox). The insertion of the Cre gene and LoxP sites was verified by RT-PCR, and the knockout efficiency of the FGFR2 gene was confirmed in target tissues, designating the successfully generated conditional knockout mice as FGFR2-CKO mice. The breeding of transgenic mice followed mendelian inheritance, maintaining an equal sex ratio. All mice were acclimatized in a controlled environment room for 4–6 days before experimentation. All animals were housed under standard laboratory conditions at a temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, with relative humidity maintained at 50 ± 15%, and were subjected to a 12-h light/dark cycle, with free access to food and water. This study was conducted exclusively in male mice to eliminate potential confusion, as estrogen fluctuations in females are known to have therapeutic effects that could mask the drug’s efficacy in the KA-induced mouse model[17].

Establishment of TLE model

To induce temporal lobe seizures, we employed a kainic acid (KA) administration model targeting the hippocampal region[18, 19]. Eight-week-old mice (either purchased or bred in-house) were acclimatized for one week and then randomly divided into three groups: the sham group, the KA group, and the KA + FGF10 group. Prior to seizure modeling, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of a pentobarbital sodium solution at 50 mL/kg. KA was injected into the hippocampal region at the following coordinates: anterior–posterior (AP): -2.00 mm, medial–lateral (ML): -2.20 mm, dorsal–ventral (DV): -1.80 mm. Using a microinjection syringe, 2 µL of KA (200 ng/µL) was administered over a period of 2 min. After injection, the needle was maintained in place for an additional 5 min to prevent backflow along the injection track. Mice in the Sham group received 2 µL of saline at the same coordinates. Finally, the mice were placed on a heating pad until they fully recovered. The severity of seizures was assessed using the Racine scale: stage 1, immobility and facial twitching; stage 2, head nodding; stage 3, forelimb clonus; stage 4, rearing; and stage 5, rearing and falling[20]. The onset of seizures was defined as the stages 4–5, and these mice were subsequently included in further studies.

Intranasal administration of FGF10

Recombinant human FGF10 (C4042, Sigma, USA) was freshly prepared in saline (concentration: 1 mg/mL). Mice in the KA + FGF10 group received daily intranasal administration of recombinant human FGF10 (0.5 mg/kg) starting from day 1 post-KA injection, continuing for 14 days. Mice in the sham surgery and KA groups received an equivalent volume of saline. At the end of the treatment period, behavioral assessments were conducted for each group of mice, or cardiac perfusion was performed to extract hippocampal tissues from the KA-injected side for subsequent molecular biology experiments. All necessary precautions were taken to minimize mouse suffering, in compliance with the ethical guidelines established by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Wenzhou Medical University.

EEG assessment

Three cortical electrodes were implanted for EEG recording. Two active recording electrodes were positioned at coordinates (AP: + 1.37 mm, ML: -1.23 mm) and (AP: -2.15 mm, ML: -1.98 mm) relative to bregma. The third electrode, located at (AP: -6.14 mm, ML: -0.85 mm), was used as the reference[21]. The implanted electrodes were secured with dental cement. Following surgery, mice were allowed a recovery period of 7 days before continuous EEG recording for 7 days to assess the development of spontaneous recurrent seizures. EEG assessments were performed using LabChart 8 software for analysis and quantification[22]. Seizures are defined as high-amplitude, rhythmic EEG activity lasting at least 10 s, with an amplitude exceeding 3 to 5 standard deviations above baseline, typically accompanied by frequency progression or behavioral abnormalities such as twitching, running, or tonic stiffness. Synchronous video recordings were also collected using a night vision high-definition digital camera for comparative monitoring of mouse seizure behaviors, during which the mice were allowed to move freely within the collection cage and access food and water.

Behavioral test

In this study, we employed a series of behavioral experiments including Novel Object Recognition (NOR), Y-Maze, Morris Water Maze (MWM), Open Field Test (OFT), and Elevated Plus Maze (EPM) to assess the cognitive function and anxiety-related behaviors of mice. These behavioral assessments were conducted following a 14-day treatment period.

The NOR test is a widely used behavioral assay for investigating various aspects of learning and memory. Mice were introduced to a new environment containing two identical objects, allowing them to explore for 5 min. Twenty-four hours after the training trial, one of the objects was replaced with a novel object (differing in color, shape, and size), and mice were permitted another 5-min exploration session. The discrimination index (DI) was calculated as follows: DI = Time spent exploring the novel object / (Time spent exploring the novel object + Time spent exploring the familiar object).

The Y-Maze was utilized to estimate short-term memory. This apparatus consists of three arms forming a metal Y-shape, each arm measuring 35 cm in length and 5 cm in width, extending from a central platform at an angle of 120 degrees. Each mouse was placed at the end of one arm and allowed to move freely within the maze for 1 min, with the criterion for entering an arm being the complete entry of all four limbs. Mice were recorded for their sequential entries into the three different arms as a measure of spontaneous alternation. The spontaneous alternation rate (%) was computed as follows: % Spontaneous Alternation = Actual number of spontaneous alternations / (Total entries into arms − 2) × 100.

The MWM test assessed the learning abilities and visuospatial memory of the animals. The circular water pool used in this study had a diameter of 100 cm, and it contained a submerged platform (diameter 12 cm) that was painted white and situated in the target quadrant of the pool. Over the next four days, mice underwent three trials daily, with the platform remaining in its original position. For each training trial, mice were placed in the water facing different walls of the pool at random. The maximum allotted time for each trial was 60 s. Once a mouse located the platform, it was permitted to remain there for 10 s. If a mouse failed to find the platform within 60 s, it was gently guided to the platform and allowed to stay there for 10 s. The average escape latency for each mouse across the four-day testing period was recorded as an indicator of acquisition or learning. On the fifth day, the platform was removed, and the mice were allowed to swim for 60 s to search for the previously learned information. The number of crossings into the area where the hidden platform had been located was recorded to assess spatial cognitive function.

The OFT was utilized to evaluate the anxiety levels and exploratory behaviors of the experimental animals, particularly the mice. Exploration behavior may be affected when experimental animals suffer from cognitive deficits; thus, the OFT also serves as an assessment of cognitive function. Prior to the experiment, mice were acclimated to the open field area (60 cm × 60 cm) for 5 min to mitigate the impact of environmental novelty on the experimental outcomes. At the start of the trial, a mouse was placed in the center of the testing box to initiate the assessment, typically lasting for 5 min. The center area of the open field was defined as a 20 cm × 20 cm square located in the center of the experimental area. The movement trajectories of the mice and the percentage of time spent in the center square were recorded and analyzed.

The EPM is one of the behavioral assessment methods used to study anxiety-related disorders. In the current study, the EPM employed featured arms measuring 50 cm in length, 5 cm in width, and 50 cm in height, with two arms enclosed by walls to form a protected area; the other two arms were open, representing an anxiogenic space. Each mouse was placed in the central region and allowed to explore freely. The movement trajectories and time spent in each arm were recorded and analyzed in conjunction with video recordings.

All behavioral analyses and data processing were conducted using SMART 3.0 software.

Western blotting

Mouse hippocampal tissues or cells were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer containing PMSF and protease inhibitors. Subsequently, protein samples were prepared by adding an appropriate volume of loading buffer and heating at 100 °C for 10 min. As previously detailed, Western blotting was performed to quantify protein changes in the hippocampus[23]. Briefly, samples were separated using 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked at room temperature for 1.5 h with 5% non-fat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 0.1% (TBST), followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with appropriately diluted primary antibodies. Following three washes with TBST, the membrane was incubated at room temperature with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. Detection was performed using an enhanced chemiluminescence assay kit (New Cell & Molecular Biotech, China). The protein bands were quantified using Image J software, and the data were normalized. Primary antibodies included CALB1 (1:5000, Proteintech) and FGF-10 (1:1000, Sigma-Aldrich); secondary antibodies included goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (1:10,000, Zen-Bioscience) and goat anti-mouse IgG-HRP (1:10,000, Zen-Bioscience).

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from mouse hippocampal tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), and RNA concentration was quantified using Nanodrop spectrometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) to ensure that the OD260/OD280 ratio was between 1.8 and 2.1. cDNA was synthesized following the manufacturer’s guidelines using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (RR037A, TaKaRa, Japan), and reverse transcription was conducted in a final reaction volume of 10 μL using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA). The relative expression levels of each gene were quantified using the 2−∆∆CT method and normalized to β-actin levels. The specific primer sequences used for qRT-PCR in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Immunofluorescence staining

Fourteen days after the induction of epilepsy in mice, 50 mg/kg pentobarbital sodium was injected intraperitoneally to achieve deep anesthesia. Following routine PBS perfusion, the mice were perfused through the heart with 4% paraformaldehyde. The brains were fixed overnight at 4 °C in 4% paraformaldehyde and then dehydrated for three days in a 30% sucrose solution before being embedded in OCT and sectioned at 25 μm on a cryostat. Brain sections were blocked at room temperature with 5% BSA for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies in PBS containing 1% BSA (NeuN antibody, 1:500, Cell Signaling Technology; CALB1 antibody, 1:400, Cell Signaling Technology). The next day, after washing, sections were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 1 h with corresponding secondary antibodies (donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488, 1:800, Abcam; donkey anti-goat Alexa Fluor 647, 1:800, Abcam). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI and mounted, and imaging was performed using a confocal microscope (Leica DMI8, Germany).

Nissl staining

Nissl staining is utilized to detect neuronal cell damage. Frozen sections from each group were treated with Nissl staining solution (C0117, Beyotime, China) at room temperature for 10 min. After staining, the sections were washed, differentiated, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Neurons in the hippocampus exhibiting a significant reduction in Nissl bodies or alterations in cellular structure were assessed as damaged neurons, indicating cellular impairment.

TUNEL staining

For the detection of neuronal apoptosis, we employed the TUNEL (Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling) detection kit (C1089, Beyotime, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Paraffin-embedded brain Sects. (5 μm) were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by treatment with proteinase K solution at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, the sections were incubated in a reaction buffer containing terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase at 37 °C in a humid chamber for 30 min. Cell nuclei were lightly stained with DAPI. The sections were observed under a confocal microscope (Leica DMI8, Germany) to identify TUNEL-positive cells.

Production and purification of AAV

The production and purification of AAV were based on previously established protocols[10]. Initially, we constructed a plasmid, pAAV-hSyn-mFGF10, containing the target gene FGF10. AAV particles were then produced in 293 T cells, which were cultured under high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. To maintain cell health, cells were passaged every 3–4 days using 0.25% trypsin. To produce replication-deficient AAV particles, we achieved co-transfection of three plasmids into 293 T cells using polyethyleneimine (PEI). These plasmids included a plasmid containing the target gene FGF10 (pAAV-hSyn-FGF10), a packaging plasmid (pAAV-RC) containing AAV rep and cap genes, and a helper plasmid (pHelper) containing adenoviral helper genes. After transfection, cells and supernatants were harvested after 120 h. Cells were collected, and viral particles in the supernatant were concentrated by precipitation with 8% polyethylene glycol 8000 (PEG8000). Subsequently, cellular debris was removed by centrifugation at 5000 g for 15 min, clarifying the lysate. The viral fraction was then separated using iodixanol gradient centrifugation (15%, 25%, 40%, and 60%), with AAV particles predominantly concentrated in the 40% layer. Finally, concentrated viral particles were further purified through PBS and a 100 K cutoff concentrator. The viral titer was determined using qRT-PCR protocol provided by Addgene, yielding pAAV-hSyn-mFGF10-T2A-EGFP for subsequent experiments.

Viral injection

The previously purified pAAV-hSyn-mFGF10-T2A-EGFP and the virus rAAV-hSyn-EGFP-P2A-CRE-WPRW-bGH pAAV2/9 which purchased from BrainVTA, Wuhan, China (titer: 5.90 × 10^12 VG/mL), were aliquoted and stored at -80 °C for future use. Briefly, two weeks prior to administration, the mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of a pentobarbital sodium solution at a dosage of 50 mL/kg and positioned in a stereotaxic apparatus. The AAV virus was injected into the hippocampus at predetermined sites, with a total volume of 0.5 µL of the viral solution administered at a rate of 0.05 µL/min. After the injection, the syringe was left in place for 10 min to allow for viral diffusion. Finally, the mice were placed on a heating pad until they fully recovered. The expression pattern of the virus was confirmed through immunohistochemical fluorescence staining and imaging using a confocal microscope (Leica DMI8, Germany).

Calcium ion measurement

In this study, we employed a calcium ion assay kit (Product No.: C004-2–1, Nanjing Jiancheng) to determine the calcium ion levels in the hippocampal tissues of mice from the Sham group, KA group, and KA + FGF10 group. Briefly, hippocampal tissues from each experimental group were collected post-modeling and processed according to the microplate assay protocol outlined in the kit instructions. This protocol included sample preparation, reagent addition, and establishment of reaction conditions. Subsequent analysis was performed using a microplate reader to record the optical density values of each sample. By comparing these values with a standard curve, we calculated the calcium ion levels in the hippocampal tissues of the different mouse groups.

RNA sequencing

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was conducted at the Beijing Genomics Institute (Shenzhen, Guangdong, China). Total RNA was isolated using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen). The obtained clean data from the sequencing of the extracted RNA was aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.2.1)[24]. The clean data was further aligned to the reference gene set using Bowtie2 (v2.4.5)[25]. Gene expression quantification was conducted using RSEM (v1.3.1)[26], and differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.34.0)[27]. Information regarding the RNA-seq data is provided in Supplementary Data 3. KEGG pathway and Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment analyses for differentially expressed genes were carried out using the clusterProfiler R package, with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Fiber photometry

We followed the experimental protocol established by Meng-Juan Sun et al.[28]. The specific procedures are illustrated in Fig. 6H-I. First, we induced epilepsy in mice using KA as previously described, while simultaneously injecting 300 nL of rAAV-hSyn-GCaMP6s into the dentate gyrus (DG) region (stereotactic coordinates: AP: -1.25 mm; ML: -2.06 mm; DV: -1.75 mm). Following viral injection, we implanted a ceramic ferrule with an optical fiber (diameter: 200 μm, 0.30 NA, Reward Life Science Technology) approximately 0.2 mm above the DG region, then secured the fiber to the skull using dental cement. After recovery, mice received daily intranasal administration of either FGF10 or an equivalent volume of saline. Two weeks later, we employed fiber photometry to record calcium transients in neurons. Specifically, we used a commercial fiber photometry system (Thinker Tech Nanjing Biotech) to record GCaMP signals. The system operated in dual-wavelength excitation mode: 470 nm excitation light (40 mW at fiber tip) specifically activated GCaMP6s fluorescence signals, while 410 nm reference light (20 mW at fiber tip) was used to acquire calcium-independent background signals. Real-time ratio calculation (470 nm/410 nm) was performed to eliminate motion artifact interference. On recording days, head-fixed epileptic mice from both groups were connected to the fiber photometry system for observation. When tonic–clonic seizures (Racine scale ≥ stage IV) were detected, they were marked accordingly. Finally, normalized GCaMP fluorescence (ΔF/F0) was used to evaluate calcium ion signals.

Statistical analysis

We performed the Shapiro–Wilk test for normality and Levene’s test for homogeneity of variances. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired t-test was utilized when normality and homogeneity of variances were met. For multiple group comparisons, one-way ANOVA was applied if both normality and homogeneity were satisfied; otherwise, Welch’s ANOVA was used if only normality was met. When the data did not conform to normal distribution, the Kruskal–Wallis test was employed. Results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

FGF10 improves cognitive dysfunction in KA-induced epileptic mice

To investigate whether the levels of FGF10 are dysregulated in patients with epilepsy, we initially assessed the serum FGF10 levels in epileptic patients using ELISA. The results indicated a significant decrease in serum FGF10 levels among epileptic patients compared to the age-matched control group (Fig. 1A). Subsequently, we utilized a mouse model to simulate TLE induced by KA and measured the expression of FGF10 protein in the hippocampal tissues 14 days post-modeling. Notably, the expression levels of FGF10 in the hippocampus of KA group mice were significantly lower than those in the Sham group, and intranasal administration of FGF10 resulted in an observable increase in FGF10 levels (Figs. 1B-C). This finding suggests that nasal delivery can effectively facilitate the transport of FGF10 across the blood–brain barrier into the hippocampal region of the brain. To evaluate the impact of FGF10 on epilepsy-related cognitive deficits, we conducted several behavioral tests: the Novel Object Recognition Test, Y-Maze, and Morris Water Maze, to assess hippocampal-dependent spatial learning and short-term memory performance. We observed that KA-induced epileptic mice spent significantly more time with the familiar object, leading to a markedly reduced recognition index, indicating impaired recognition capabilities. However, following treatment with FGF10, this cognitive function was restored (Fig. 1E). Y-Maze results demonstrated a significant decrease in the spontaneous alternation rate of KA group mice compared to those in the sham-operated group, whereas FGF10 treatment induced an improvement in the alternation rate (Fig. 1F), suggesting amelioration of KA-induced short-term memory deficits. Additionally, the Morris Water Maze test revealed that the average escape latency of each experimental animal decreased with training; however, the KA group exhibited a significantly prolonged escape latency, indicating a marked decline in spatial learning and memory abilities compared to the sham-operated group. Following FGF10 treatment, escape latency was notably reduced (Fig. 1H). On day five, following four days of training, the spatial probe test showed that KA mice crossed the target platform fewer times than sham-operated mice; however, FGF10 treatment increased the number of crossings and improved the exploratory trajectories of the mice (Fig. 1G, I). Furthermore, there were no significant differences in swimming speed among the groups during the testing period (Fig. 1J), indicating that KA-treated mice did not exhibit motor deficits. Collectively, these results demonstrate that FGF10 effectively ameliorates cognitive dysfunction in KA-induced epileptic mice.

In this study, we also assessed the effects of 14-day intranasal administration of FGF10 on anxiety and depressive-like behaviors in mice through the open field test and elevated plus maze test. The results showed that KA-induced epileptic mice spent significantly less time in the central area of the open field compared to wild-type mice (Figs. 1K-L). Concurrently, KA group mice exhibited prolonged time spent in the closed arms and entered the closed arms more frequently; however, FGF10 treatment reversed these anxiety and depressive-like behaviors (Fig. 1M), suggesting a potential ameliorative effect of FGF10 on anxiety behaviors in KA-induced epileptic mice.

FGF10 ameliorates seizure activity and neuronal death while effectively suppressing neuroinflammation in KA-induced epileptic mice

We utilized a modified Racine scale to investigate the efficacy of FGF10 in alleviating seizure-like behaviors in KA-induced epileptic mice[29]. Compared to the sham-operated group, mice in the KA group exhibited a range of seizure-like behaviors, including generalized limb twitching, uncontrollable rearing, and falling (Fig. 2B). The time point of electrode implantation in mice is shown in Fig. 2A. During electroencephalogram (EEG) recordings, KA group mice displayed characteristic abnormal EEG waveforms during seizure episodes. The frequency of seizures was significantly increased in the KA group compared to the sham-operated controls; however, this effect was markedly reversed in the KA + FGF10 group (Figs. 2C-D). Notably, there were no significant differences in the duration of seizures between the two groups (Fig. 2E).

Subsequently, we explored the potential neuroprotective effects of FGF10 in the KA-induced mouse model. Results from the TUNEL assay indicated a significant loss of neurons in the hippocampal region, particularly within the CA1 and CA3 subregions, in KA group mice. However, treatment with FGF10 appeared to reverse this trend of neuronal loss (Figs. 2F-H). Similarly, Nissl staining confirmed substantial neuronal death in the CA3 region of the hippocampus in KA-treated mice, characterized by disarrayed cell arrangements, nuclear degeneration, and a marked reduction in Nissl bodies. However, these damages were effectively salvaged following FGF10 treatment (Figs. 2I-J). Furthermore, we collected hippocampal tissues from the mice to determine whether expression levels of relevant inflammatory cytokines were altered. The results demonstrated that FGF10 treatment significantly downregulated the expression of pro-inflammatory factors (including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) induced by KA-triggered epileptogenesis, while upregulating anti-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-4, IL-10, and TGF-β) (Fig. 2K). These findings indicate that FGF10 treatment ameliorates KA-induced local inflammatory responses.

Collectively, these results suggest that FGF10 exhibits promising potential as a neuroprotective agent for epilepsy treatment.

Endogenous overexpression of FGF10 ameliorates cognitive dysfunction and anxiety behaviors in KA-induced epileptic mice

In this study, we constructed a viral vector, AAV9-hSyn-mFGF10-T2A-EGFP, which is capable of effectively crossing the blood–brain barrier (BBB) to achieve satisfactory transduction efficiency. By employing stereotactic injections, we facilitated the overexpression of FGF10 in the hippocampal region of epileptic mice. To evaluate the protective effects of endogenous FGF10 against epilepsy, we referred to previous experimental protocols. Twenty-one days post-injection of the AAV9 virus, we established a KA-induced epilepsy model in mice and divided them into three groups: AAV-GFP group, AAV-GFP + KA group, and AAV-mFGF10 + KA group. Fourteen days following KA induction, we conducted a series of behavioral experiments, including the open field test, novel object recognition task, Morris water maze, Y-maze, and elevated plus-maze, after which we collected brain tissue for further analysis.

Western blot analysis revealed that, compared to the AAV-GFP group, the AAV-mFGF10 + KA group exhibited a significant increase in hippocampal FGF10 expression levels (Fig. 3A-B). Utilizing GFP labeling, we visualized viral transduction, which demonstrated high-intensity fluorescence imaging in the neurons of the hippocampal region following AAV-mFGF10 administration (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, EEG results showed that mice with endogenous FGF10 overexpression exhibited similar effects in alleviating seizures as those administered exogenous FGF10. (Fig. 3D-F).

Subsequently, we assessed the cognitive performance of the three groups of mice using the novel object recognition task, Morris water maze, and Y-maze. Compared to the AAV-GFP + KA group, the AAV-mFGF10 + KA mice demonstrated a significantly prolonged exploration time for the novel object during the novel object recognition task, accompanied by a higher discrimination index (DI) (Fig. 3G). In the Y-maze experiment, the spontaneous alternation scores of the AAV-mFGF10 + KA mice were also significantly enhanced (Fig. 3H). In the spatial memory probe test of the Morris water maze, the AAV-mFGF10 + KA mice exhibited a significantly shortened escape latency and an increased number of platform crossings, with no notable differences in swimming speed (Fig. 3I-K, M).

Moreover, results from the open field and elevated plus-maze tests indicated that mice injected with AAV-mFGF10 + KA demonstrated significant improvements in anxiety and depressive behaviors compared to those in the AAV-GFP + KA group (Fig. 3N-O). Collectively, these findings suggest that the overexpression of endogenous FGF10 effectively ameliorates seizure activity, cognitive dysfunction, and anxiety behaviors in KA-induced epileptic mice.

FGFR2 conditional knockout inhibits the therapeutic effects of FGF10 on cognitive dysfunction in KA-induced epileptic mice

FGF10, an important member of the fibroblast growth factor family, is recognized in numerous studies for its ability to bind to the FGFR2 receptor, thereby activating its tyrosine kinase activity [30]. This activation initiates downstream signaling pathways that regulate a series of critical biological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, and migration[31–33]. Consequently, we constructed FGFR2 cKO mice for receptor-related investigations (Fig. 4A). The efficiency of FGFR2 gene knockout was confirmed through Western blotting and immunofluorescence staining. The results from Western blot analysis indicated that the protein expression of FGFR2 in the knockout group was significantly lower than that in the wild-type mice (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated the accuracy of the viral injection site and the successful spread of infection (Fig. 4C), further confirming the success of FGFR2 gene conditional knockout mediated by the Cre viral vector.

Our specific experimental procedure is outlined in Fig. 4D. Initially, EEG results indicated that in FGFR2 cKO mice, there were no significant changes in seizure frequency and duration between the KA treatment group and the KA + FGF10 treatment group (Fig. 4E-G). Subsequent behavioral experiments revealed that FGF10 did not exhibit therapeutic effects on cognitive impairments and anxiety behaviors in KA-induced FGFR2 cKO mice. Specifically, in cognitive function assessments, there were no significant differences in the performances of the FGFR2 cKO + KA group and the FGFR2 cKO + KA + FGF10 group in tests such as the novel object recognition, Y-maze, and Morris water maze (Fig. 4H-L, N). Additionally, the activity levels and anxiety behaviors of the FGFR2 cKO + KA + FGF10 group in the open field and elevated plus-maze tests did not differ significantly from those of the FGFR2 cKO + KA group (Fig. 4M, O-P). These results indicate that following FGF10 treatment, KA-induced FGFR2 cKO mice continue to exhibit cognitive impairments and anxiety behaviors characteristic of epilepsy, suggesting that the therapeutic effects of FGF10 are blocked in the absence of FGFR2.

RNA-seq analysis reveals key differences in gene expression profiles

To investigate the specific molecular mechanisms underlying FGF10 treatment, we performed RNA-seq analysis on the hippocampal tissue of mice that either successfully exhibited KA-induced seizures or received intranasal FGF10 treatment for 14 days post-injection. The results identified 449 differentially expressed genes that were significantly altered in the epileptic mice compared to the sham-operated group, and further changes were observed after the administration of exogenous FGF10 (Fig. 5A-C). The specific differentially expressed genes can be found in Supplementary Tables S2–S3. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that pathways related to calcium ion signaling, such as cellular calcium ion homeostasis, calcium ion transport, and regulation of cytosolic calcium ion concentration, were enriched. Additionally, entries related to cognition and memory were also highlighted (Fig. 5D). Moreover, we conducted enrichment analyses of differentially expressed genes between the Sham and KA groups, as well as between the KA and KA + FGF10 groups, which also indicated that the calcium signaling pathway was prominently enriched (Fig. 5E-F).

To further elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we queried the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) using "calcium ion homeostasis" as a keyword, which yielded 1,000 related genes. Simultaneously, using "cognitive dysfunction" as another keyword, we retrieved another set of 1,000 relevant genes (Fig. 5G). We subsequently intersected the 449 differentially expressed genes obtained from sequencing with those related to cognitive dysfunction and calcium ion signaling, and constructed a protein–protein interaction network. The results indicated that five genes, primarily CALB1, may influence cognitive impairment in epilepsy through calcium ion signaling pathways (Fig. 5H-I). The specific gene list can be found in Supplementary Table S4. Correlation analyses showed a significant relationship between CALB1 and FGF10 in both the Sham and KA groups (Fig. 5J). Sequencing data also indicated that the gene expression levels of FGF10 and CALB1 were significantly downregulated following KA modeling, whereas CALB1 expression was reversed after FGF10 administration (Fig. 5K). Finally, we assessed the expression levels of the five genes using qRT-PCR (Fig. 5L). The results demonstrated that CALB1 significantly decreased at the mRNA level after KA induction but rose to levels comparable to the sham-operated group following FGF10 treatment. Additionally, the mRNA levels of other genes within the interaction network, such as BDNF, HTR2A, HTR3A, and MMP9, were also altered after KA treatment.

The role of CALB1 signaling pathway in calcium homeostasis maintenance and neuronal death in KA-induced epileptic mice

The CALB1 signaling pathway plays a crucial role in maintaining calcium ion homeostasis in KA-induced epileptic mice. Following RNA-seq analysis, we shifted our focus to the role of the CALB1 gene in the mechanisms underlying epilepsy-related cognitive impairment. To further investigate the subcellular localization of CALB1 within the hippocampal region, we utilized the PanglaoDB database to obtain a single-cell RNA-seq dataset from the hippocampi of C57BL/6 mice[34]. Additionally, we accessed an RNA-seq dataset regarding eight different purified mouse cell types from the GEO database (GEO: GSE52564). The results indicated that CALB1 expression levels in neurons were significantly higher than in other cell populations, suggesting its potential physiological significance in neuronal function (Supplementary Fig. 1B).

Moreover, our immunofluorescence staining experiments further confirmed that CALB1 protein is highly expressed in the neurons of the hippocampal dentate gyrus (DG) (Supplementary Fig. 1C), aligning with previous research findings. We also observed morphological changes in the DG area of the mouse hippocampus following KA treatment (Supplementary Fig. 1C), suggesting that neuronal damage in this region may lead to structural remodeling, including neuronal loss, reduced neurogenesis, and increased proliferation of glial cells. These changes could mediate aberrant synaptic remodeling, ultimately resulting in epilepsy-associated cognitive deficits. The hippocampal DG region plays a vital role in cognitive functions[35], particularly in spatial and contextual memory[36]. Therefore, we speculate that the specific protein CALB1 may play a significant role in the therapeutic effects of FGF10 on epilepsy-related cognitive dysfunction.

Subsequently, we performed CALB1-related immunofluorescence staining experiments on previous groups of mice. The results revealed that the fluorescence intensity of CALB1 in the DG region of the KA group was significantly lower than that of the control group (Fig. 6A), indicating a reduction in protein expression levels under epileptic conditions. Notably, both exogenous administration and endogenous overexpression of FGF10 restored the expression levels of CALB1 (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Fig. 1D). However, this therapeutic effect of FGF10 was significantly blocked in FGFR2 conditional knockout (cKO) mice (Fig. 6B). The results of Western blotting corroborated our immunofluorescence findings, showing a significant downregulation of CALB1 protein after KA treatment, which could be reversed by FGF10 treatment in vivo experiments (Fig. 6C, E).

Given that prior sequencing results indicated significant activation of the PI3K-AKT pathway, we also assessed this pathway. The results demonstrated that exogenous administration of FGF10 activated the AKT pathway, leading to increased phosphorylation levels (Fig. 6C, E). In FGFR2 cKO mice, this activation was blocked and failed to regulate CALB1 levels (Fig. 6D, F). Since CALB1 plays a pivotal role in calcium ion regulation, we conducted an evaluation using a calcium ion detection kit, revealing that calcium ion levels were significantly elevated in the hippocampal tissues of KA-induced epileptic mice. However, following exogenous application of FGF10, calcium ion levels were significantly reduced (Fig. 6G), preliminarily confirming the role of FGF10 in calcium homeostasis regulation. We subsequently measured alterations in Ca2⁺ signals within DG neurons using fiber photometry. Following baseline correction and motion artifact removal, we observed that the ΔF/F0 values during epileptic seizures were significantly lower in FGF10-treated epileptic mice compared to saline-treated controls (Fig. 6K). Further analysis of peak Ca2⁺ signal amplitudes during seizure episodes revealed that the maximal fluorescence changes in the FGF10 group were also markedly reduced relative to untreated epileptic mice (Fig. 6L). These findings demonstrate that FGF10 can effectively normalize abnormal Ca2⁺ dynamics in DG neurons during seizure activity. In summary, these findings suggest that the CALB1 signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in regulating calcium homeostasis in KA-induced epileptic mice.

Discussion

This study aims to investigate the potential therapeutic effects of FGF10 on epilepsy-associated cognitive impairment. Our results demonstrate that kainic acid-induced epileptic mice exhibit CALB1-mediated calcium dyshomeostasis, which may represent one of the underlying mechanisms contributing to cognitive deficits in epilepsy. Furthermore, we speculate that CALB1-mediated regulation of calcium homeostasis may represent one of the mechanisms through which the FGF10/FGFR2 signaling axis alleviates temporal lobe epilepsy and its associated cognitive dysfunction.

Epilepsy is a common chronic disorder of the central nervous system characterized by recurrent seizures. Its pathological mechanisms involve imbalances between neuronal excitation and inhibition[37], neuroinflammatory responses[38], apoptosis[39], and abnormal neural network remodeling[40]. Notably, studies indicate that temporal lobe epilepsy patients are more prone to cognitive impairment, particularly in episodic memory, compared to those with other focal epilepsies[41]. White matter microvascular alterations in temporal lobe epilepsy have been found to closely correlate with associated cognitive deficits[42]. Moreover, patients with mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (mTLE) frequently exhibit impaired awareness during seizures and extensive neurocognitive impairments, likely due to seizure-induced disruption of subcortical structures involved in arousal regulation and their long-range connectivity[43]. At the molecular level, many researchers attribute this cognitive impairment to neuroinflammation and neuronal loss. Elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α during neuroinflammation have been shown to impair memory mechanisms[44]. Blockade of peripheral regulatory T cells can reduce hippocampal TNF-α levels and improve surgery-induced cognitive dysfunction in aged mice [45]. A comprehensive meta-analysis encompassing 170 studies further confirmed the pivotal role of inflammatory factors in mild cognitive impairment pathogenesis, with MCI patients showing significant alterations in multiple inflammatory markers (e.g., IL-6, IL-8) in peripheral blood and CSF[46]. Neuronal loss is widely recognized as a key pathological substrate for cognitive decline in various neurological disorders. Compared to "positive" pathological features like amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles, the "negative" pathology in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), particularly neuronal and synaptic loss, demonstrates more robust and direct correlation with cognitive deterioration[47]. In this study, we employed the well-established KA-induced mouse model to simulate temporal lobe epilepsy, followed by comprehensive behavioral assessments to evaluate cognitive function. Our research aims to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying epilepsy-associated cognitive impairment and identify novel therapeutic targets.

FGF10 is a mesenchymal signaling molecule predominantly expressed in epithelial tissues. Current research has primarily focused on its role in promoting epithelial cell proliferation and differentiation[48], highlighting its therapeutic potential for cardiac repair[49] and lung injury [50]. Additionally, FGF10 has demonstrated neuroprotective effects in neurological disorders. However, whether FGF10 exerts therapeutic benefits in epilepsy, particularly its potential to improve epilepsy-associated cognitive impairment, remains largely unexplored. In epilepsy and other neurological diseases, the blood–brain barrier poses a significant obstacle by restricting CNS access of therapeutic compounds. Therefore, BBB permeability represents a critical consideration when selecting biological agents for epilepsy treatment. To address this challenge, we utilized intranasal delivery—a non-invasive, safe, and BBB-penetrating administration method that avoids systemic side effects while ensuring efficient CNS drug delivery.

Calcium homeostasis plays a pivotal role in the nervous system, regulating neuronal growth, synaptic plasticity, and neurotransmission [51, 52]. By modulating normal and abnormal neuronal function, calcium ions directly influence cognitive processes like learning and memory. Growing evidence indicates that calcium dysregulation represents a key pathological feature of Alzheimer’s disease and other cognitive disorders[53–55]. Studies have established clear associations between calcium dyshomeostasis and epilepsy, with abnormal neuronal firing during seizures often accompanied by aberrant calcium fluxes[56]. However, the precise relationship between calcium imbalance and epilepsy-associated cognitive impairment remains incompletely understood. In this study, we identified calcium dysregulation in the hippocampus of KA-induced epileptic mice, which was ameliorated by exogenous FGF10 administration.

CALB1, a crucial calcium-binding protein, is well-characterized for its roles in maintaining intracellular calcium balance[57], regulating neuronal signaling[58], and modulating cellular senescence[59]. Research has shown that Calb1 circuits in the posterior basolateral amygdala (IL-pBLA) may alleviate chronic unpredictable mild stress-induced anxiety and depression-like behaviors through reward generalization[60]. CALB1 also plays important roles in cognitive function. Differential expression of calcium-binding proteins in layer III pyramidal neurons of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex influences L-type calcium channel activation and neuronal firing patterns, contributing to cognitive vulnerability[61]. Similarly, calcium-binding proteins are essential for hippocampal function and cognition. Recent studies demonstrate that ΔFosB induction in hippocampal neurons downregulates Calb1 transcription, driving persistent cognitive deficits in AD and epileptic mouse models[62]. Given its ability to prevent calcium overload [63], CALB1 exhibits significant neuroprotective properties, particularly under excitotoxic conditions where it may serve as a potential therapeutic target for certain neurological disorders. Our previous RNA-seq and Western blot results suggested that FGF10 may exert its therapeutic effects in epilepsy through a potential mechanism of action centering on CALB1-mediated regulation of calcium homeostasis. To further evaluate calcium dynamics, we employed fiber photometry for real-time monitoring of calcium levels in hippocampal DG neurons. Consistent with previous reports showing rapid calcium elevation in CA1 neurons after KA injection[28], we observed abnormal Ca2⁺ signals in DG neurons during seizures, which were normalized by FGF10 treatment. The results of this study indicate that in the treatment of temporal lobe epilepsy and related cognitive dysfunction, the FGF10/FGFR2 signaling pathway may involve the upregulation of CALB1 expression and the maintenance of intracellular calcium ion homeostasis, both of which collectively contribute to the neuromodulatory processes of this pathway.

Using Western blotting, we confirmed successful BBB penetration and brain distribution of intranasally administered FGF10. Cognitive deficits are highly prevalent among epilepsy patients [64] and significantly impact quality of life. Subsequent behavioral tests demonstrated that FGF10 treatment markedly improved learning and memory impairments in KA-induced epileptic mice. We hypothesize that cognitive decline in epileptic mice may result from aberrant neuroinflammation and neuronal loss, as elevated inflammatory cytokines are known to impair memory mechanisms[44]. Through EEG monitoring, TUNEL assay, and Nissl staining, we evaluated seizure susceptibility and hippocampal neuronal damage. Our results indicate that FGF10 exerts neuroprotective effects via FGFR2 binding, attenuating KA-induced seizures. qRT-PCR analysis further revealed FGF10’s ability to mitigate KA-triggered neuroinflammation. These findings position FGF10 as a potentially safe and effective therapeutic agent for alleviating epilepsy-related cognitive deficits.

In summary, this study demonstrates for the first time a critical link between disrupted calcium homeostasis and neuronal injury following intrahippocampal KA-induced spontaneous recurrent seizures. We also initially discovered that the therapeutic effects of the FGF10/FGFR2 signaling pathway are accompanied by altered CALB1 expression and calcium homeostasis regulation. Under this intervention, both the severity of epileptic seizures and cognitive dysfunction were significantly ameliorated, highlighting its potential therapeutic value. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the direct interaction between FGFR2 and CALB1 remains unverified. Second, our investigation of FGF10’s effects on CALB1-mediated calcium dysregulation was confined to the KA-induced epilepsy model. Additionally, the precise mechanisms and potential involvement of other pathways require further exploration. Future studies should address these gaps and conduct more extensive clinical trials to validate FGF10’s therapeutic efficacy in epilepsy patients. Finally, video-EEG monitoring was conducted over a 7-day period, which is shorter than the often-cited two-week "gold standard" for seizure monitoring in chronic animal models. While this duration was sufficient to capture a robust dataset and reveal significant differences between experimental groups, it may have missed some clustered episodes of spontaneous recurrent seizures.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our experimental results demonstrate that FGF10 administration was significantly associated with the activation of the FGFR2/CALB1 signaling pathway, upregulation of CALB1 expression, maintenance of intracellular calcium homeostasis, along with the observation of neuroprotective effects. Following FGF10 treatment, the severity of KA-induced epileptic seizures was markedly reduced, and cognitive impairment also showed an improving trend. Collectively, our research elucidates a novel molecular mechanism for the treatment of epilepsy and its related cognitive behavioral disorders, offering fresh perspectives on potential therapeutic targets.
