Regiodivergent C3 and C4 Amination of Quinolines via Radical and Ionic Pathways
Authors: Ye‐Eun Kim, Jieun Kim, Juyeon Lee, Sungwoo Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTControlling the site of amination on N‐heteroarenes is pivotal for rapid exploration of structure–activity relationships, yet a single‐precursor platform that toggles between C3 and C4 amination of quinolines has remained elusive. Here we report a regiodivergent method that channels quinoline amination to C3 or C4 through orthogonal radical and ionic manifolds. Under visible‐light, donor‐assisted electron‐donor–acceptor (EDA) conditions, homolytic N─N cleavage of N‐aminoquinolinium salts generates N‐centered radicals that selectively install amino groups at C3 via capture by an enamine intermediate (radical pathway) formed through traceless nucleophile‐induced dearomatization. In the absence of light and donor, the same quinolinium salt undergoes a two‐electron ionic process: SNAr‐type addition of amines at C4, followed by base‐promoted rearomatization to furnish C4‐aminated products. The method proceeds under mild conditions, accommodates a broad range of quinolines and amine partners, and enables late‐stage diversification. Mechanistic experiments support an EDA‐initiated origin for the C3 manifold and an ionic mechanism for C4, establishing condition‐gated control over quinoline C─N bond formation.

Introduction

Precise positional editing of N‐heteroarenes is central to modern medicinal chemistry because single‐site modifications can remodel binding modes, pKa, and physicochemical properties without altering the core topology [1, 2, 3]. Among these scaffolds, quinoline stands out as a privileged framework that permeates pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, natural products, and ligand architectures [4, 5]. Within this context, amino groups provide versatile hydrogen bond donor/acceptor capability and a basic handle for downstream diversification, making aminoquinolines recurrent motifs in antimalarials and kinase inhibitor chemotypes (Figure 1a) [6, 7, 8, 9]. The synthetic question is therefore not merely how to install an amine group, but where to place it on the quinoline ring to enable site‐specific control over structure–activity relationships (SAR) in medicinal chemistry. Classical solutions to quinoline amination—transition metal–catalyzed coupling of prefunctionalized halides or pseudohalides [10, 11, 12, 13], reduction of nitro precursors [14, 15, 16], and directed or innate C─H functionalization [17, 18, 19, 20]—have delivered valuable outcomes, yet they typically hardwire the site of installation or require separate precursors to access alternative ring positions.

Design and development of switchable C3 and C4 amination of quinolines.

Recent N‐activation strategies in azines have enabled milder reaction conditions and selective functionalization by embedding a leaving group on the ring or nitrogen [21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. What remains underdeveloped is a unified platform that switches the amination site between C3 and C4 on demand without changing the core precursor. Such switchable regiocontrol from a single quinoline species would streamline analogue preparation and unlock orthogonal substitution vectors on the same scaffold—capabilities that are central to efficient SAR exploration in both medicinal and discovery chemistry [26, 27]. However, achieving regiodivergent amination is nontrivial because quinoline's innate reactivity disfavors C3 under conventional two‐electron manifolds: electrophilic substitution preferentially engages the benzannulated sector (C5/C8)—for example, nitration typically furnishes 5‐ and 8‐nitroquinolines—whereas C3 remains intrinsically unreactive [28].

Crucially, the N‐activation playbook that unlocks C3 on pyridines does not translate to quinolines. In pyridines, N‐activation renders the ring susceptible to nucleophile‐induced ring opening to a Zincke–imine intermediate, which then recloses at C3 to deliver C3 amination [29]. By contrast, the fused benzenoid ring in quinolines imposes a substantial dearomatization penalty and geometric constraint on analogous ring opening [30, 31, 32], making formation of a Zincke‐type intermediate from quinoline derivatives energetically disfavored and experimentally inaccessible (Figure 1b, top). Consequently, this otherwise powerful ring‐opening/reclosure strategy fails to enable C3 amination in quinolines, as Zincke‐type reactions cannot proceed at this position.

To achieve C3 amination, we therefore sought an alternative approach: installing an amine assembly unit as an N‐activating group to serve dual roles as an N‐radical precursor [33, 34, 35, 36] and as a site‐selective directing group [37, 38]. Guided by this hypothesis, we envisioned N‐aminoquinolinium salts as a bifunctional platform, serving both as activated quinoline precursors and as N‐centered radical precursors. A nucleophile adds to C4 rapidly yet reversibly, acting as a temporary activator to establish a pre‐equilibrium, dearomatized enamine‐like intermediate (Figure 1b, bottom). The embedded N─N unit forms an EDA complex with a suitable donor [39, 40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45]; visible‐light excitation cleaves the N─N bond to reveal an N‐centered radical. The pre‐equilibrium dearomatized intermediate exhibits electronic enrichment at the C3 position, enabling selective capture of the electrophilic N‐radical. Subsequent rearomatization releases the nucleophile, affording the C3‐aminated product. In the absence of light and electron donor, the same quinolinium activation polarizes the ring and channels nucleophilic addition at C4 to give a 1,4‐dihydroquinoline (DHQ) intermediate. When the nucleophile is an amine, this intermediate is productive rather than transient: base‐promoted rearomatization commits the adduct and directly furnishes the C4‐aminated quinoline. Here we report that this regime switching—open‐shell radical versus polar two‐electron—enables predictable, on‐demand site selectivity across diverse quinoline substitution patterns and amine partners, and is directly applicable to late‐stage functionalization of pharmacologically relevant scaffolds. Mechanistic experiments delineate the radical origin of C3 selectivity and the ionic nature of C4 functionalization, establishing a general blueprint for regiodivergent C─N bond formation on quinoline cores.

Results and Discussion

We posited that a traceless nucleophile could endow the N─N quinolinium salt with dual functionality: (i) formation of an EDA complex under visible light, which induces N─N homolysis to release a sulfonamidyl radical and (ii) transient C4 dearomatization to a 1,4‐DHQ manifold for capture at the C3 position. Guided by this design, we surveyed nucleophiles (Table 1, entries 1–4). Methanol and p‐cyanobenzamide furnished no product, consistent with insufficient nucleophilicity to access the key dearomatized intermediate. By contrast, sulfinates enabled the desired regime switch: NaSO2Et delivered C3‐aminoquinoline 3a in 63% yield, whereas sodium benzenesulfinate was competent but less effective, indicating that sulfinate electronics modulate the efficiency of C4 addition [46]. Real‐time 1H NMR monitoring revealed rapid C4 addition to form the 1,4‐DHQ intermediate (see the Supporting Information for details), which then serves as the platform for C3 functionalization; the sulfinate is expelled upon rearomatization. On this basis, NaSO2Et was selected as the nucleophile of choice. Stoichiometry proved important: 1.2 equiv. of sodium ethyl sulfinate delivered optimal reactivity (Table 1, entries 4 and 5), consistent with its multifaceted role as (i) the donor component of the EDA complex under visible irradiation, (ii) a transient dearomatizing nucleophile, and (iii) the leaving group that promotes rearomatization. No desired product was observed in the dark (entry 6), underscoring the requirement for light in the open‐shell pathway. To delineate the orthogonal two‐electron manifold, we next examined amine nucleophiles under dark conditions. Inclusion of base accelerated deprotonation of the σ‐adduct and promoted SNAr‐type C4 addition, followed by base‐promoted rearomatization to furnish 4a. Sufficient basicity was critical; KO t Bu was optimal among the bases tested (entries 7–9). Among the solvents examined, DMF proved optimal for the ionic pathway, delivering the highest yields (entry 10). Further optimization details are provided in the Supporting Information.

We next examined the scope of the C3‐selective amination with various sulfonamide nucleophiles and quinoline substrates under the optimized visible‐light EDA conditions (Figure 2). The transformation proceeded smoothly under mild conditions to deliver a broad range of C3‐aminoquinolines—valuable scaffolds that are otherwise difficult to access and highly sought for meta‐addressable library synthesis in medicinal chemistry. Quinoline derivatives bearing diverse substituents on the benzo‐fused ring (C5─C8) were well tolerated. Halogenated substrates such as 6‐fluoro (3b), 6‐bromo (3c), 6‐chloro (3d), 7‐bromo (3e), 7‐chloro (3f), and 5‐chloro (3l) furnished the desired products while preserving reactive aryl halides for subsequent cross‐coupling. Electron‐donating groups such as 6‐methoxy (3a), 7‐methyl (3g), 7‐tert‐butyl (3h), 8‐methyl (3i), 6‐isopropyl (3j) and 5‐methyl (3k) also proved compatible, indicating that the transient dearomatization and EDA activation are not hindered by increased electron density. Substitution at the 5‐ and 8‐positions was likewise tolerated, and even multiply substituted scaffolds such as 5,7‐dichloroquinoline afforded 3m. Extended π‐systems, including 2‐phenylquinoline and benzoquinoline, reacted efficiently to yield 3n and 3o, further highlighting the versatility of the protocol. Variation of the sulfonamide partner revealed similarly broad generality. N‐methyl‐p‐toluene sulfonamide afforded 3p, establishing this substrate as a reliable benchmark. The reaction tolerated both electron‐donating and electron‐withdrawing substituents on the aryl sulfonyl unit: p‐methoxy (3q) and bulky p‐tert‐butyl (3r) sulfonamides reacted efficiently, whereas p‐trifluoromethyl (3s) and p‐chloro (3t) analogues delivered comparable yields, indicating minimal sensitivity to electronic effects on the sulfonyl arene. The N‐methyl‐N‐phenylsulfonamide also participated, affording 3u. Moreover, heteroaryl sulfonamides such as 5‐chlorothiophene‐2‐sulfonamide gave 3v, underscoring tolerance toward heteroatom‐containing sulfonyl groups. Increasing steric demand at nitrogen was permissible, as shown by N‐ethyl‐p‐toluenesulfonamide furnishing 3w with only a slight decrease in yield. Sulfonamidyl radicals derived from bioactive sulfone scaffolds [47, 48, 49], including valdecoxib, sildenafil, and valproic acid derived quinoline smoothly furnished the desired product 3x, 3y and 3z, underscoring the broad applicability of this protocol. These results collectively demonstrate that the traceless nucleophile strategy is robust to both steric and electronic perturbation, an important prerequisite for late‐stage diversification and parallel analogue synthesis. The complementary C4‐amination under ionic conditions (Figure 3) also displayed broad substrate compatibility. Quinoline derivatives bearing substituents at the C6 positions on the benzo‐fused ring (methoxy (4a), methyl (4b), bromo (4c), chloro (4d), and fluoro (4e)) afforded the corresponding products. Substituents at C7, including 7‐methyl (4f) and the strongly electron‐withdrawing 7‐trifluoromethyl (4g), were equally well tolerated. Even 6,7‐disubstituted (4j) and ester group (4h) were well tolerated. Remarkably, 2‐aryl‐substituted quinolines underwent smooth conversion to 4i and 4k, likely facilitated by enhanced electrophilicity at C4 through extended conjugation. 3‐methylquinoline and unsubstituted quinoline coupled efficiently with 4‐cyanoaniline to afford 4l and 4m. These findings establish the ionic regime as a complementary manifold that provides orthogonal, para‐amination. A wide variety of amines proved suitable nucleophiles. Aniline derivatives bearing electron‐neutral, electron‐rich, or electron‐deficient substituents—including aniline (4n), 2‐fluoroaniline (4o), 3‐trifluoromethylaniline (4p), 3‐methylaniline (4q), and 4‐methoxyaniline (4r)—all afforded the corresponding C4‐aminoquinolines in good yields. Heteroaromatic amines such as 2‐aminopyridine furnished 4s efficiently. Aliphatic amines, both primary (4t) and cyclic, including piperidine (4u), azepane (4v), and morpholine (4w), reacted smoothly to give near‐quantitative conversions, demonstrating insensitivity to steric hindrance around nitrogen. N‐heterocycles such as imidazole were also compatible (4x). Acyclic secondary amines—methyl‐tolyl‐amine (4y), dimethylamine (4z), and diallylamine (4aa)—underwent clean coupling, and the incorporation of diethanolamine (4ab) underscores the method's tolerance toward polar, hydroxyl‐bearing substrates, a valuable feature for medicinal chemistry applications. Specifically, complex amines such as phenylquine and aminofluorene were selectively installed at the C4 site of the quinoline, affording the corresponding products 4ac and 4ad. Overall, the two complementary regimes provide predictable, condition‐gated access to C3‐ and C4‐aminated quinolines across a wide substitution space while accommodating diverse functional groups. Leveraging this platform, we rapidly assembled a mini‐library of aminoquinolines for VEGFR2 profiling [50, 51]; screening identified a tractable hit bearing dimethoxy and 4‐chlorophenyl substituents (4j, IC50 = 8.8 µM, Figure 3). Although modest in potency, the scaffold is optimization‐ready and illustrates how programmable C3/C4 amination unlocks orthogonal growth vectors for SAR. Molecular docking into the VEGFR2 ATP site (PDB: 3EWH) places the quinoline hinge motif in canonical H‐bonding with Glu917, and Cys919 with Phe918; the dimethoxy groups are positioned for auxiliary H‐bonding with Gly922/solvation contacts, and the 4‐chlorophenyl substituent occupies a proximal hydrophobic pocket with favorable van der Waals complementarity. To gain mechanistic insight, we conducted a series of control experiments. First, a crossover experiment employing two distinct quinoline precursors (1m and 1d’) furnished four cross‐coupled products, consistent with pathways that proceed via the proposed intermolecular processes (Figure 4a). We next probed the postulated photoactive EDA complexes between quinolinium salts and potential electron donors (sulfinate anion). To suppress rapid 1,4‐DHQ formation via sulfinate addition (see the Supporting Information), UV–vis studies were conducted with a C4‐blocked salt, 4,6,8‐trimethylquinolinium (1aa). A pronounced bathochromic shift was observed upon mixing quinolinium 1aa with sodium sulfinate (Figure 4b; Supporting Information), consistent with EDA complex formation. Light–dark experiments indicated a partial chain pathway: notably, the yield of product 3p continued to increase during the dark intervals, suggesting that a light‐independent radical propagation component operates to some extent. We also delineated the ionic C4 pathway. In the absence of base, no desired C4 product was detected, indicating that deprotonation of the σ‐adduct (1,4‐DHQ intermediate) is required to drive rearomatization. Under the reaction conditions, N‐methyl‐p‐toluenesulfonamide was observed by crude 1H NMR, consistent with the deprotonation competence of the corresponding nitrogen anion (see the Supporting Information). On the basis of these observations, we propose the mechanism in Figure 4d. Initially, a soft nucleophile reversibly adds at C4 of the quinolinium salt to give a dearomatized 1,4‐DHQ‐type intermediate (Int A). Under irradiation, a donor‐assisted EDA complex between the quinolinium salt and sulfinate promotes SET, generating an in situ sulfonamidyl radical A that is captured at C3 of Int A to form radical intermediate B. Subsequent N─N homolysis and rearomatization release sulfinate and furnish the C3‐aminated product 3 (radical regime). In the complementary ionic regime, amine addition yields Int B, which undergoes sequential deprotonation followed by base‐promoted rearomatization to deliver the C4‐aminated product 4.

To demonstrate the synthetic utility, we carried out modifications of the sulfonamide motif (Figure 5). The tosyl group was readily removed using either a Hantzsch ester as an electron and hydrogen‐atom donor [52] or a phosphine anion [53]. The resulting aminoquinolines were subsequently coupled with pharmaceutically relevant motifs via standard amide coupling reactions. For example, ibuprofen (3aa) and probenecid (3ab) were successfully conjugated to the C3‐aminoquinoline scaffold. These transformations highlight the amenability of the sulfonamide handle to downstream derivatization and underscore the potential of this method for late‐stage functionalization and drug‐oriented molecular diversification. In addition, site‐selective functionalization of menthol‐derived quinolines was achieved using the C3 (radical) and C4 (ionic) protocols, delivering the corresponding products (3ae and 4ae) [54, 55].

Conclusion

We disclose a regiodivergent platform that unifies radical and ionic pathways to achieve on‐demand C3 and C4 amination of quinolines. N‐Aminoquinolinium salts undergo nucleophile‐induced dearomatization that can be directed along two orthogonal manifolds. Under electron‐donor‐assisted, visible‐light EDA activation, a sulfinate transiently generates a reversible enamine intermediate electronically enriched at C3, enabling selective capture of an N‐centered radical to furnish C3‐aminated products. In contrast, in the absence of light and donors, sterically and electronically biased SNAr‐type amine attack commits the reaction to C4 functionalization, followed by base‐promoted rearomatization. The protocol is operationally simple, proceeds under mild conditions, and accommodates a broad array of quinoline substitution patterns and amine classes, providing a general platform for the rapid assembly of aminoquinoline libraries and late‐stage diversification of bioactive scaffolds. Collectively, these results establish a versatile blueprint for regiodivergent C─N bond formation and programmable N‐heteroarene editing.
