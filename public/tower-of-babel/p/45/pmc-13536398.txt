Dental treatment and oral care for patients with severe mental illness receiving psychiatric home-visit care: A narrative review with an illustrative case
Authors: Takayuki Suga, Trang Thi Huyen Tu, Yuji Gamo, Takafumi Asakura, Shigeru Iida, Akira Toyofuku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with severe mental illness (SMI) often have untreated caries, periodontal inflammation, tooth loss, xerostomia-related risk, dental anxiety, and delayed dental attendance. This narrative review aimed to synthesize evidence relevant to dental treatment planning for patients with SMI receiving psychiatric home-visit care and to clarify when domiciliary dental assessment and repeated professional oral care may be indicated. Targeted literature searches and citation tracking were used to identify reviews, epidemiological studies, intervention studies, qualitative syntheses, and special care dentistry guidance. The evidence indicates that oral disease in SMI reflects not only limited knowledge but also avolition, cognitive impairment, psychosis, sedation, medication-induced dry mouth, shame, transport difficulty, and inability to sustain appointments. Domiciliary dental care for selected patients can include brief oral examination, identification of pain, swelling, residual roots, denture trauma and aspiration or nutritional risks, plaque and calculus removal as tolerated, topical or prescription fluoride, xerostomia and diet review, reinforcement of one feasible hygiene action, and referral for radiographs, restorations, extraction, periodontal or prosthetic treatment when clinic or special care settings become possible. In the illustrative home-visit case, severe avolition and somnolence prevented independent toothbrushing; therefore, dentists provided professional oral care every two weeks. During 7 months of follow-up, the patient had no acute dental symptoms, and no obvious clinical deterioration of carious lesions or periodontal inflammation was observed within the limits of domiciliary assessment. Home-visit dentistry should be considered for selected psychiatric home-care patients when self-care and clinic attendance are unreliable, before dental problems deteriorate into emergency-driven treatment.Keywords: Dental care for disabled, , House calls, Mental disorders, Oral health, Oral hygiene

Introduction

Severe mental illness (SMI) is commonly used to describe schizophrenia spectrum disorders, bipolar disorder, and other psychotic or disabling mood disorders that cause long-term functional impairment. For dentistry, the relevance of SMI is not the diagnostic label alone, but the way psychiatric symptoms, medication effects, social adversity, and access barriers can allow treatable oral disease to accumulate before dental care is reached. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have shown higher burdens of tooth loss, untreated caries, periodontal disease, and poorer oral self-care among people with SMI or schizophrenia than among the general population.1–3

The clinical problem is therefore an access and maintenance problem as well as a disease-burden problem. A recommendation to attend a dental clinic or improve toothbrushing may be correct but ineffective when avolition, somnolence, cognitive impairment, dental fear, shame, or transport difficulty prevents the patient from using ordinary pathways. Intervention reviews and consensus work have called for more active oral health support for people with SMI, but dental descriptions of how to start care for patients already receiving psychiatric home-visit services remain limited.4,5

Patients receiving psychiatric home-visit care are a particularly important subgroup because home-based psychiatric care may indicate severe functional impairment, social withdrawal, inability to organize appointments, or dependence on family concern rather than self-initiated help. This review summarizes evidence relevant to dental treatment planning for such patients and uses an illustrative case to clarify when domiciliary dental assessment and scheduled professional oral care may be a realistic first dental intervention.

Materials and methods

A targeted narrative review with systematic search elements was conducted to support clinical dental decision-making rather than pooled estimation of prevalence or treatment effect. The review question was: what oral disease patterns, self-care barriers, medication-related risks, and service barriers should guide treatment planning for patients with SMI, particularly those receiving psychiatric home-visit care?

Peer-reviewed English-language literature was identified through targeted database searches and citation tracking. Search concepts included severe mental illness, serious mental illness, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, psychosis, oral health, dental caries, periodontal disease, tooth loss, oral hygiene, toothbrushing, dental utilization, dental anxiety, xerostomia, antipsychotic medication, special care dentistry, domiciliary dentistry, home-visit dentistry, and psychiatric home care. Priority was given to systematic reviews, meta-analyses, scoping reviews, qualitative syntheses, consensus statements, population-based studies, intervention-development studies, and studies directly relevant to access to dental care.

Articles were selected when they informed one of four dental decisions: whether dental disease was likely to be present, why self-care or attendance might fail, which medication-related oral risks required prevention, and what first intervention could be repeated in a home or special care pathway. Evidence was critically interpreted by study type, population, outcome, and applicability to domiciliary dental care. Formal risk-of-bias scoring and meta-analysis were not performed because the included questions and study designs were heterogeneous.

The illustrative case was included to show a common decision point in home-visit dentistry: extensive dental need in a patient whose psychiatric symptoms made clinic attendance and independent plaque control unrealistic. The case was reviewed and approved by the relevant institutional ethics committees, and informed consent for publication of the clinical information and accompanying image was obtained.

Results

Oral disease burden and self-care failure

The oral disease burden in SMI is broad rather than confined to a single lesion. Reviews report excess edentulism, greater decayed, missing, and filled teeth burdens, periodontal treatment need, and poorer oral hygiene.1–3 Population- based studies also link poorer mental health with worse oral health status or lower dental care utilization.6,7 In daily dental practice, this burden may appear as accumulated plaque and calculus, untreated caries in multiple teeth, residual roots, gingival inflammation, broken restorations or prostheses, denture trauma, halitosis, pain, swelling, or presentation only after emergency deterioration.

The mechanisms behind this accumulation are often practical rather than informational. Serious mental illness can disrupt initiation, sequencing, memory, motivation, sensory tolerance, dietary regularity, and appointment keeping. Studies in chronic schizophrenia and intervention-development work indicate that brushing may fail even when the patient knows it is desirable, because executive function, habit formation, cost, social support, or tolerance of brushing procedures is insufficient.8,9 Qualitative work in psychosis similarly shows that oral health is shaped by quality of life, identity, support, stigma, and daily functioning, not only by knowledge of technique.10

For the dentist, this means that failure to brush should not be interpreted simply as non-compliance. A patient with avolition may agree to brush but not initiate the action. A patient with cognitive impairment may forget the sequence. A patient with autism-related sensory intolerance may reject toothpaste flavor, brush texture, mirror use, or prolonged mouth opening. A patient with somnolence may sleep through the time when hygiene would normally occur. The care plan should identify the specific limiting barrier and choose the smallest intervention that can be repeated.

Medication-related oral risk

Medication history should be part of dental risk assessment. Psychotropic and other long-term medications can contribute to xerostomia, sialorrhea, dysgeusia, bruxism, tardive dyskinesia, extrapyramidal symptoms, appetite or thirst change, and reduced alertness. Reviews of medication-related salivary dysfunction and antipsychotic exposure report salivary changes, caries, periodontal disease, oral infection, dysgeusia, orofacial pain, and temporomandibular outcomes relevant to SMI.11,12 Dry mouth associated with drugs used for depression, anxiety, schizophrenia, and bipolar disorder can lower the threshold at which plaque and frequent sugar exposure become active disease.13,14

These risks do not replace ordinary dental diagnosis, but they should change prevention. Xerostomia supports closer caries-risk review, fluoride varnish or high-fluoride toothpaste when indicated, saliva-aware diet advice, avoidance of sugar-containing drinks or lozenges when possible, and early attention to mucosal discomfort. Gingival enlargement should prompt review of plaque control and medication history. Bruxism, dyskinesia, and reduced manual dexterity may influence prosthetic design and the choice of hygiene aids.

Access barriers and psychiatric home-visit care

Dental attendance can be delayed by cost, transport, shame, dental anxiety, stigma, limited oral health literacy, previous frightening healthcare experiences, and difficulty organizing appointments. Qualitative synthesis has identified financial constraints, anxiety, medication side effects, limited awareness, stigma, and inadequate mental health training among dental professionals as recurring barriers. 15,16 Dental professionals also perceive anxiety as a major barrier for patients with mental illness.17 These factors shift dentistry from prevention to crisis response: the patient may appear only after pain, swelling, tooth fracture, infection, or prosthetic failure.

Patients receiving psychiatric home-visit care may represent a subgroup in whom this failure of access is concentrated. Studies of dental utilization among people with SMI or schizophrenia support the concern that care may be delayed even when dental services exist.18,19 A preliminary study of 22 patients receiving home-based psychiatric services in Tokyo found untreated caries, residual roots, poor oral hygiene, long gaps since previous dental visits, and substantial unmet dental needs.20 Earlier outreach work similarly suggested that patients with mental conditions who are difficult to reach by ordinary dental pathways may have unrecognized dental needs.21

For this subgroup, domiciliary dental assessment should be considered when visible oral neglect, pain behavior, diet change, denture problems, halitosis, family concern, long absence from dental care, or inability to brush is present. The first home dental visit does not need to reproduce a complete clinic examination. It can identify obvious pain, swelling, residual roots, mucosal trauma, denture problems, number of functional teeth, visible plaque and calculus, carious cavitation, periodontal inflammation, mouth-opening tolerance, gagging, instrument acceptance, and the patient’s capacity to follow one oral-care instruction. These observations allow the dentist to classify immediate urgency and decide whether home maintenance, clinic referral, or special care treatment is the next realistic step.

Delayed entry into care also changes the kind of dentistry that becomes necessary. Early caries that might have been managed with fluoride, monitoring, or minimally invasive restoration may progress to pulpal disease or residual roots. Gingivitis may progress to periodontal breakdown, mobility, and tooth loss. Denture problems may become mucosal trauma, reduced intake, or complete discontinuation of prosthesis use. The dental priority is therefore to shorten the period in which oral disease remains unobserved and to distinguish emergency need from high chronic risk.

Practical dental management and illustrative case

The first dental goal for patients with SMI should be both acceptable and clinically meaningful. A practical sequence is to identify pain, swelling, infection, or inability to eat; estimate caries and periodontal risk; simplify the hygiene target; address fluoride and xerostomia prevention; remove plaque and calculus as tolerated; and decide whether radiographs, restorations, extractions, periodontal treatment, or prosthetic care require a clinic or special care setting. When plaque control is unstable and recall is uncertain, immediate routine restorative dentistry may be less realistic than stabilizing attendance and biofilm control. In selected non-urgent lesions, fluoride measures can be a rational first step while cooperation and plaque control are improved.22,23

When professional maintenance is selected, the visit can include brief oral inspection, checking for new pain or swelling, removal of accessible plaque and deposits, reinforcement of one hygiene action, topical or prescription fluoride when indicated, xerostomia review, denture assessment, and reassessment of whether the patient can tolerate a more definitive procedure. The interval should reflect disease activity and feasibility. In severe self-care failure, short frequent visits may be more effective than a comprehensive appointment that cannot be repeated. Special care dentistry algorithms can support individualized planning when anxiety, cognitive impairment, medication effects, or cooperation problems complicate ordinary care.24

The illustrative case involved a 63-year-old man receiving psychiatric home-visit care who lived alone. His sister requested dental care because of concerns about his health and functioning. His psychiatric diagnoses included bipolar disorder and autism spectrum disorder, with past diagnoses of schizophrenia and early-onset Alzheimer-type dementia. He was often bedridden, had marked avolition, autistic-like withdrawal, and strong somnolence, and did not practice toothbrushing.

At the first home dental assessment, the dominant barrier was not acute refusal but absence of sustained self-care behavior. Intraoral examination showed extremely poor oral hygiene, heavy plaque accumulation, multiple carious teeth, calculus deposition including on occlusal surfaces, and gingival enlargement that was considered possibly related to antihypertensive medication (Fig. 1). The home setting did not allow the same diagnostic detail as a clinic, but no finding suggested that emergency treatment was more urgent than establishing plaque control, observation, and a workable dental route.

The initial options were clinic-based examination and treatment, home-based oral hygiene instruction alone, or recurring professional oral care at home with later referral if restorative, surgical, radiographic, periodontal, or prosthetic procedures became necessary. The patient could not attend a clinic, and instruction alone did not overcome avolition. The plan was therefore changed to scheduled professional oral care once every two weeks. Each visit aimed to remove plaque and deposits as tolerated, reinforce a simple hygiene message, monitor caries and gingival condition, and judge whether transition to clinic-based or special care dental treatment had become possible. Home-based professional oral care was continued for 7 months. During this follow-up period, the patient did not report acute dental symptoms such as spontaneous pain or swelling, and no obvious deterioration of carious lesions or periodontal inflammation was clinically observed within the limits of domiciliary assessment. The follow-up care focused on scheduled home-based professional oral care and clinical monitoring, but the patient eventually discontinued dental care because of financial constraints. This clinical course suggests that scheduled domiciliary oral care may help maintain short-term oral stability and keep oral disease under observation, although it should not be interpreted as evidence of definitive disease resolution.

Discussion

This review highlights a dental problem that can be underestimated: some patients with SMI do not lack dental need, but they lack a route into dental care that can survive psychiatric disability. High oral disease burden, self-care disruption, medication-related dry mouth or motor effects, dental anxiety, stigma, and transport or organizational barriers can combine to delay care until the patient presents with advanced disease. Psychiatric home-visit care identifies a subgroup in which this combination is likely and ordinary clinic-based access may fail silently.

The illustrative case clarifies why the first dental intervention may need to be modest and repeated. The patient could momentarily understand instruction, but severe avolition and somnolence prevented conversion of instruction into daily brushing. In that situation, scheduled professional oral care every two weeks was not merely supportive; it was the first intervention that could be delivered reliably. The relevant clinical question was not only which dental treatment was ideal, but which step could be repeated often enough to reduce risk and keep oral disease visible to dental care. The 7-month follow-up in this case should therefore be interpreted as short-term clinical stabilization under scheduled domiciliary oral care, rather than as definitive evidence of disease resolution.

Dental clinicians should therefore treat inability to brush, visible oral neglect, long absence from dental care, and psychiatric home-visit status as signals for screening. The first domiciliary visit can identify urgent disease, reduce plaque burden, provide fluoride and xerostomia advice, assess mouth opening and instrument tolerance, and determine whether radiographs, restorations, extractions, periodontal therapy, or prosthetic care require a clinic or special care dental setting. This approach preserves dental priorities while adapting the setting and pace to psychiatric disability.

Oral hygiene advice should be deliberately narrow. In severe SMI, too many simultaneous instructions may function as no instruction at all. A useful target may be one brushing site, one time of day, high-fluoride toothpaste, a caregiver-visible oral care item, a brief desensitization step, or professional plaque removal at fixed intervals. The first target should follow the limiting barrier, whether fear, avolition, sensory intolerance, xerostomia, pain, prosthetic failure, or inability to attend a clinic.

This does not mean that every patient with SMI needs home-visit dentistry. Many patients can receive ordinary clinic-based care when access, cooperation, and self-care are sufficient. The point is that psychiatric home-visit care identifies a group in whom the default pathway may fail silently. A responsive dental pathway should therefore use home-visit status and visible oral neglect as triggers for screening, followed by the least intensive intervention that can be repeated reliably.

Future studies should measure outcomes that fit hard-to-reach SMI populations, consistent with calls for clinically grounded research on mental disorders and oral diseases.25 DMFT, periodontal indices, and number of completed procedures remain important, but early gains may include successful oral examination, reduction in visible plaque burden, acceptance of instruments, fluoride use, fewer emergency presentations, restoration of a dental referral pathway, or transition from home maintenance to special care treatment. These intermediate outcomes may determine whether definitive dental treatment later becomes possible.

Limitations should be noted. This is a narrative review, not a systematic review or outcome study. The illustrative patient had complex psychiatric and cognitive disability, so the case should not be interpreted as representing SMI alone. The case also lacks longitudinal oral indices, radiographs, detailed periodontal charting, and quantified change after maintenance care. Nevertheless, the review and case identify a practical decision point: when self-care and clinic attendance cannot be established, domiciliary dental assessment and scheduled professional oral care may be required before definitive treatment can be planned.

In conclusion, patients with SMI receiving psychiatric home-visit care may have preventable and treatable oral disease that ordinary pathways do not reach. For selected patients, home dental assessment, risk-based prevention, simplified hygiene targets, plaque and calculus removal as tolerated, xerostomia-aware care, and scheduled professional maintenance can provide a practical access route before dental problems progress to emergency-driven treatment.
