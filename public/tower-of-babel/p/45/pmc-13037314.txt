Reactive thrombocytosis in hospitalized pneumonia reflects intense inflammation and confers context-dependent mortality risk
Authors: Cheng Liu, Lili He, Yuanhao Guo, Yang Su, Yao Wei, Yongxin Li, Xiaolong Ma, Rui Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBeyond hemostasis, platelets’ immune and inflammatory role is recognized and thrombocytosis is generally regarded as a marker of the inflammatory response. However, the immune functions of platelets remain beneath the surface, and previous studies have reported conflicting findings regarding the significance of thrombocytosis in infection-related diseases. Can reactive thrombocytosis be viewed as a “higher-is-worse or higher-is-better” predictor of clinical outcome?MethodsWe analyzed 2754 patients discharged from the respiratory and infectious-disease wards between 1 January 2021 and 11 June 2025, and patients from the MIMIC-IV 3.1 database were used as validation cohorts, grouped by peak platelet counts (< 90, ≥ 400, 90–399 × 10⁹/L). Cox regression and multivariate logistic regression were performed to analyze the relationship between platelet count and in-hospital mortality. Multivariate linear regression was used to analyze the relationship between platelet count and length of hospital stay (LOS).ResultsPatients with reactive thrombocytosis had longer hospital stays (13 vs. 10 days; p < 0.001) and higher inflammatory markers than those with normal platelet counts CRP (90.2 vs. 28.6 mg/L; p < 0.001), PCT (0.195 vs. 0.092 ng/mL; p < 0.001), WBC (12.6 vs. 8.4 × 10⁹/L; p < 0.001). Despite these differences, mortality (3.4% vs. 4.4%) did not differ, nor did the rates of bloodstream, fungal, or polymicrobial infections. Clustering analyses confirmed comparable overall microbial spectra between the two groups. The impact of thrombocytosis on mortality (HR, 0.52; 95% CI, 0.27–0.99; p = 0.047) is context-dependent: it trended toward higher mortality in non-septic patients (OR, 1.42; 95% CI, 0.64–2.86, p = 0.357) but toward lower mortality in those with sepsis (OR, 0.33; 95% CI, 0.05–1.51, p = 0.18). The same opposing pattern and the interaction of sepsis and thrombocytosis re-emerged (OR, 1.70; 95% CI, 0.95–2.92, p = 0.063 vs. OR, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.34–0.96, p = 0.04) in MIMIC-IV data. Thrombocytopenia is an independent risk factor for mortality (HR, 2.08; 95% CI, 1.48–2.93; p < 0.001) and has good combined predictive ability.ConclusionsThrombocytosis trended toward higher mortality in non-septic patients yet toward lower mortality in those with sepsis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04206-1.

Background

Platelets, traditionally viewed as hemostatic cells, are now increasingly recognized for their immune and inflammatory roles, especially in the post-COVID-19. Recognition of platelets’ pivotal role in thrombosis has enabled clinical applications in drug development and risk assessment: (1) antiplatelet agents targeting platelet activation are widely used for coronary artery disease and ischemic stroke; (2) platelet-function assays quantify thrombotic ability; (3) thrombocytopenia elevates bleeding risk; and (4) studies report that reactive thrombocytosis may be associated with thrombotic risk [1–4].

Correspondingly, with platelets’ inflammatory and immune roles now established, the next goal is to apply this insight to clinical inflammation assessment and to develop platelets as anti-inflammatory targets [5]. The concepts of thromboinflammation and immunothrombosis [6, 7] have provided a unified framework that markedly enhances our understanding of the crosstalk among platelets, immunity, inflammation, and thrombosis. Platelets not only exert immune functions during infection, but also trigger intense inflammatory responses that cannot be overlooked [8]. Maintaining the balance between inflammation and immunity is therefore critical, yet how to perform clinical assessment and intervention based on platelet counts remains a challenge. As the lungs are now recognized as a site of platelet biogenesis [9], investigating infection-induced pulmonary platelet release may offer novel insights into the immune and inflammatory roles of platelets. Therefore, after reviewing prior work, this study aims to determine whether reactive thrombocytosis carry prognostic significance for patients with pneumonia and, if so, whether a simple “higher-is-worse” or “higher-is-better” rule can serve as a readily applicable predictor of clinical outcome.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective study enrolled 2754 consecutive patients (age 15–105 years; 1,708 men, 62%) discharged from the Departments of Respiratory and Infectious Diseases in our hospital between 1 January 2021 and 11 June 2025, with a discharge diagnosis of pneumonia or lung infection. 16 patients who experienced both thrombocytosis and thrombocytopenia during hospitalization were excluded. All laboratory data generated during hospitalization were used for analysis. Comorbidities were classified into 29 disease categories based on ICD-10 codes at discharge, and the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index was calculated for subsequent multivariable adjustment. This retrospective study was approved by our institution. In parallel, we extracted from the MIMIC-IV 3.1 database adult patients whose first hospitalization and first-listed diagnosis were pneumonia, excluding those with cancer (cohort 1, n = 1444; 1555 before exclusion of records with missing data or concurrent thrombocytosis and thrombocytopenia) and patients whose pneumonia was among the top two discharge diagnoses (cohort 2, n = 3264; 3467 prior to the same exclusion).

Platelet counts and other covariates

All laboratory tests were performed in the routine clinical laboratories of our hospital. Complete blood counts were obtained with a Sysmex XN-2800 analyzer, and biochemical parameters were measured on a Beckman Coulter AU5800; both instruments met daily calibration and quality-control standards. Peak and nadir platelet counts during hospitalization were recorded. On the basis of clinical practice and prior literature, reactive thrombocytosis was defined as any platelet count ≥ 400 × 10⁹/L and thrombocytopenia as < 90 × 10⁹/L; the dates of first rise above 400 and first fall below 90 were recorded [1, 4, 18]. Peak values for c reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), white blood cell (WBC), D-dimer, and nadir PaO₂ were recorded to capture the maximal inflammatory burden and worst oxygenation during hospitalization. Etiologic evidence was derived primarily from bacterial cultures, with a minority confirmed by molecular assays. Pathogens were enumerated and stratified by culture source. The MIMIC-IV-derived cohort was likewise stratified by peak and nadir platelet counts; covariates comprised the Charlson Comorbidity Index, age, gender, and WBC.

Outcome

In our cohort, the primary outcome was in-hospital mortality, and the secondary outcome was length of stay (LOS). Death was ascertained from the medical record, and LOS was calculated as (discharge date − admission date + 1). ICU admission was determined from transfer records. For the MIMIC-IV-derived cohort, the outcome was 30-day mortality.

Cox, multiple linear and logistic regression

After platelet grouping, univariate analyses compared LOS and mortality across the three groups. Subsequently, gender, age, peak CRP, peak D-dimer, sepsis, nadir PaO₂ and Elixhauser index were included as covariates. Owing to 15% missing data, we performed five multiple imputations and used the fifth imputed dataset. Multivariable linear regression adjusted the effect of platelet levels on LOS after excluding patients who died, and Cox regression adjusted its effect on in-hospital mortality. After obtaining the peak platelet values during hospitalization for all patients, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was employed to further validate the relationship between platelet levels and mortality risk. Stratum-specific logistic regression (stratified by sepsis status) and logistic modelling of the platelet–sepsis interaction were performed.

ROC curves and DCA

Candidate variables included platelet levels, peak CRP, peak PCT, peak WBC, peak temperature, peak D-dimer, nadir PaO₂, age, gender, smoking and Elixhauser index. Univariable receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated for each predictor and a multivariable ROC curve by the Ridge Logistic; all three-variable combinations were then screened, and the triplet with the highest Area under Curve (AUC) was selected. Using these three variables, a mortality-prediction logistic model was constructed. Model reliability was evaluated with bootstrap-smoothed logistic-calibration and non-parametric calibration curves, together with mean error, maximum error, calibration slope, calibration intercept and Brier score. Finally, decision-curve analysis (DCA) for the same three-variable model was performed across clinically relevant risk thresholds (1–40%) to assess net clinical benefit.

Statistical analysis

Statistical comparisons among groups were performed with SPSS Statistics 27. Normality was assessed for each variable; parametric data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA, and non-parametric data by the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn–Bonferroni correction. Categorical variables were compared using Chi-Square Test with Bonferroni correction. Data cleaning, multiple imputation, Elixhauser Comorbidity Index calculation, multivariable linear regression, Cox and logistic models, ROC curves, Kaplan–Meier plots, and heat maps were carried out in R 4.3.3 (R Core Team, 2024) using the packages survival, survminer, mice, rms, car, pROC, ComplexHeatmap, comorbidity, icd, glmnet, ggplot2, broom, gtsummary, forestmodel.

In contrast, the thrombocytopenia group not only had a further prolonged LOS and higher inflammatory indices than the normal platelet values group, but also presented lower nadir PaO₂ (66.5 vs. 47.6 mmHg; p < 0.001) and higher mortality (3.4% vs. 32.0%; p < 0.001), and significantly higher proportions of bloodstream infection and polymicrobial infection. The thrombocytopenia group also carried the highest proportions of respiratory and cardiac failure and highest Elixhauser index (17 vs. 8, p < 0.001).

For thrombotic events, we screened all in-hospital imaging reports (ultrasound, CT and CTPA) and identified mainly lower-extremity deep-vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PVT). No excess of thrombosis was observed in the thrombocytosis group, whereas the thrombocytopenia group showed a higher incidence (30.2% vs. 17.3%, p < 0.001); however, after adjustment for the timing of thrombocytopenia this association was attenuated. One caveat is that such adjustment may misclassify events occurring during a rapid fall in platelet count (e.g., from 300 to 100 × 10⁹/L), thereby weakening the observed association.

Thrombocytosis independently lowers mortality depending on clinical context, a trend in sepsis

Both thrombocytosis and thrombocytopenia independently prolonged LOS by multivariate linear analysis, with no collinearity; model metrics are in supplementary Fig. 1. Timing of platelet rise and fall is listed in supplementary Table 1. After adjustment for gender, age, Elixhauser index, peak CRP, peak D-dimer, nadir PaO₂ and sepsis, Cox analysis showed that thrombocytopenia independently increased mortality risk (HR, 2.08; 95% CI, 1.48–2.93; p < 0.001), whereas thrombocytosis reduced it (HR, 0.52; 95% CI, 0.27–0.99; p = 0.047), as shown in Fig. 3. Using the peak platelet counts during hospitalization for all patients, RCS again showed no U-shaped curve, shown in Fig. 3D.

Prior studies on thrombocytosis in pneumonia have reported inconsistent outcomes: some indicate harm, others no difference, and some a protective effect, as shown in Table 1. Surprisingly, when we used a cohort from MIMIC-IV (n = 1444) to validate this protective effect, we obtained inconsistent results (OR, 1.34; 95% CI, 0.65–2.65, p = 0.408), supplementary Figure 2.

To resolve this inconsistency, we examined inter-study population differences and re-evaluated the significance of thrombocytosis after adjusting for stratification factors. Interestingly, stratifying by sepsis revealed that thrombocytosis exerts opposite trend on mortality in non-septic (OR, 1.42; 95% CI, 0.64–2.86, p = 0.357) versus septic (OR, 0.33; 95% CI, 0.05–1.51, p = 0.18) patients (Fig. 4A), while age, Elixhauser index, CRP, and PCT showed no such reversal (data not shown). Sepsis and thrombocytosis also showed an interaction (OR, 0.15; 95% CI, 0.02–0.92, p = 0.051).

To validate these findings, we drew cohort 2 from MIMIC-IV: first two discharge diagnoses of pneumonia, n = 3264. We further stratified by sepsis, this inverse trend re-emerged and became more pronounced in non-septic (OR, 1.70; 95% CI, 0.95–2.92, p = 0.063) versus septic patients (OR, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.34–0.96, p = 0.04), as shown in Fig. 4B. This indicates that the overall OR derived from multivariable logistic regression is unreliable; only the sepsis-stratified ORs reflect the true association.

Thrombocytopenia is an independent risk factor for mortality and has good predictive value

Our cohort and two MIMIC-IV-derived cohorts suggested thrombocytopenia consistently increased mortality risk. Therefore, we evaluated thrombocytopenia as a predictor of mortality. After generating ROC curves for each variable, we used Ridge Logistic to construct an overall ROC curve (Fig. 5A). Individually, PCT (AUC = 0.88) and D-dimer (AUC = 0.867) demonstrated good discriminatory ability, whereas thrombocytopenia (AUC = 0.717) showed modest predictive power. However, when three-variable combinations were evaluated, the model combining thrombocytopenia, WBC, and nadir PaO₂ achieved the highest AUC (AUC = 0.911; Youden index = 0.686)—second only to the fully fitted model (AUC = 0.936; Youden index = 0.753)—as shown in Fig. 5B. Bootstrap-smoothed calibration revealed a mean absolute error of 5.9%, indicating good overall calibration; the largest deviation (15.6%) occurred only at extreme predicted probabilities (< 5% or > 95%), which is clinically acceptable (Fig. 5D). Using these three variables, we derived a mortality-risk prediction formula: 1/[1 + exp(–(–1.36111044044563 + − 0.043831 × PaO₂ + 0.061555 × WBC + 1.694077 × PLT < 90 × 10⁹/L))]. Discussion

In this retrospective study, pneumonia patients with either thrombocytosis or thrombocytopenia exhibited higher inflammatory markers and longer LOS than those with normal platelet counts, and thrombocytopenia emerged as an independent risk factor for death. However, the effect of thrombocytosis (≥ 400 × 10⁹/L) depended on clinical context: after stratifying by sepsis, the trend reversed—raising risk in non-septic patients while lowering it in those with sepsis. This reversal was consistent across both our institutional cohort and the MIMIC-IV-derived cohorts. This divergence explains the conflicting findings reported in previous studies on thrombocytosis and mortality. In addition, the potential significance of thrombocytopenia for the prognosis of patients with pneumonia may inform future clinical risk stratification [19].

Our initial Cox model suggested that thrombocytosis lowered mortality in pneumonia—an effect opposite to the reports by Mirsaeidi [10] and Prina [11] and to the unstratified MIMIC-IV cohort 1 data, supplementary Fig. 3. However, the previous studies show no consensus on thrombocytosis and death in pneumonia, Table 1, underscoring that reactive thrombocytosis should not be interpreted as a straightforward “higher-is-worse” or “higher-is-better” signal. Two points guide interpretation: (1) platelets are intrinsically equipped with both immune and inflammatory functions in infection, and (2) infection-related thrombocytosis is a secondary response whose meaning must be re-weighted as overall severity changes. After stratifying by sepsis status, we observed directionally opposite signals: thrombocytosis carried higher mortality risk in non-septic pneumonia, but lower risk once sepsis was present. Upon expanding the sample size with MIMIC-IV data, this inverse trend reappeared and reached borderline statistical significance. Collectively, these lines of evidence attest to the robustness of our findings. The consistent reversal across datasets indicates that, before sepsis develops, rising platelets mark severe infection, whereas in established sepsis they reflect a preserved immune reserve. This also offers a partial explanation for why studies on conventional antiplatelet drugs in COVID-19 patients did not improve outcomes [20]. Developing medications that suppress inflammation without compromising hemostatic and immune functions remains our ultimate goal. Larger prospective cohorts enriched for septic pneumonia are needed to confirm this context-dependent prognostic switch.

Platelets engage in immune and inflammatory responses through multiple, complex mechanisms in chronic inflammation disease, infection, and cancer [6]: interaction with leukocytes to form aggregates and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) [21]; activation yielding procoagulant subsets [22]; crosstalk with immunoglobulins and complement [23]; platelet microparticles (PMPs) that propagate inflammation [24]; and release of damage associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and other inflammatory mediators [25]. In infection, they exert both immune and inflammatory effects. In Streptococcus pneumoniae infection, pneumolysin (PLY) activates the NLRP3 inflammasome to trigger platelet activation, and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) serves as an additional activator [26]. Activated platelets engage neutrophils to form NETs that ensnare pathogens, yet NETs are also implicated in thrombosis [21]. Recent reports that platelets originate in the lung have drawn attention [9], and the Looney group has since demonstrated the distinct immune profile of splenic platelets with CD40 in sepsis [8], offering new evidence for platelet-mediated immunity.

This study also captured in-hospital thrombotic events. Kwok [2] reported that ICU patients who developed thrombocytosis during admission carried higher post-discharge thrombotic risk and mortality, and Warny [4] linked high platelet counts to thrombosis. In contrast, we found that elevated platelets were not associated with thrombosis during hospitalization for pneumonia; this conclusion applies only to the inpatient period. Conversely, thrombocytopenic patients experienced more thrombosis, possibly reflecting consumptive loss after platelet activation.

This single-center retrospective study has inherent limitations; thus, the findings cannot establish causal relationships. Limited samples left stratified comparisons under-powered, yet consistent findings across cohorts give us confidence. As a single-center retrospective study, the generalizability of our findings regarding the clinical significance of thrombocytosis—particularly whether it is modified by sepsis—requires validation through multi-center research. Furthermore, we underscore the need for prospective studies to definitively elucidate the role of platelets and thrombocytosis in patients with infections, especially sepsis. Because treatment information could not be standardized, it was not included in the regression analyses for this study. Since formal severity scores (e.g., PSI, CURB-65, SOFA) were not available [19], we sought to control for confounding by including a wide range of clinical and laboratory parameters in our multivariate models. These included age, sex, CRP, PCT, PaO₂, D-dimer, and a comorbidity index (Charlson or Elixhauser) to approximate disease severity and strengthen the reliability of the estimated associations between platelet counts and clinical outcomes. Data were missing for ~ 15% of cases, but multiple imputation did not alter the direction of multivariable findings. Absence of follow-up precludes assessment of long-term post-discharge outcomes. Although hematologic or other malignancies were not routinely excluded, they were few in number and fully captured by the Elixhauser index included in the multivariable models; analyses excluding all tumor cases did not substantially alter the statistical results (supplement Table 2).

Conclusions

In this single-center analysis, sepsis served as a straightforward contextual anchor. It indicated a potential reversal in the thrombocytosis-mortality association across cohorts. These results could offer a potential explanation for inconsistent reports on reactive thrombocytosis in pneumonia. The divergence suggests a hypothesis for the dual role of platelets, highlighting a need for future prospective research to confirm these exploratory findings.
