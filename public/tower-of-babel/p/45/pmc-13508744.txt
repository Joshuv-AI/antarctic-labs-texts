Giant Anisotropy and High Second‐Order Nonlinearity of 3R‐MoS2 for Multifunctional Photonics
Authors: Georgy Ermolaev, Dmitriy Grudinin, Liudmila Klimova, Adilet Toksumakov, Mikhail Gubin, Anton Minnekhanov, Aleksandr Slavich, Mikhail Mironov, Arslan Mazitov, Dmitry Yakubovsky, Nikolay Pak, Andrey Vyshnevyy, Ivan Kruglov, Gleb Tselikov, Alexei Prokhorov, Aleksey Arsenin, Kostya S. Novoselov, Valentyn Volkov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe miniaturization of photonic circuits is impeded by the difficulty of combining active nonlinear frequency conversion with passive subdiffractional light guiding. Here, we establish a rhombohedral 3R‐polytype of MoS2 (3R‐MoS2) as a multifunctional platform resolving this challenge. Unlike its centrosymmetric 2H counterpart, 3R‐MoS2 preserves broken inversion symmetry in the bulk, enabling scalable second‐order nonlinearity. Challenging the assumption of identical linear properties between polytypes, we reveal that 3R‐MoS2 not only possesses a giant optical anisotropy similar to 2H, but also exceeds it. By determining the full dielectric tensor, we demonstrate that this anisotropy enables extreme skin‐depth (e‐skid) waveguides capable of subdiffractional light confinement, achieving mode sizes nearly 20% below the diffraction limit. Furthermore, we harness the combination of our measured giant in‐plane refractive index and high optical nonlinearity to tune the Fabry–Pérot (FP) resonator and enhance second‐harmonic (SH) generation at a given wavelength, yielding an effective nonlinear response of 1.2 nm/V. These findings bridge the gap between active and passive functionalities, positioning 3R‐MoS2 as a solution for high‐density multifunctional nanophotonic integration.

Introduction

The development of advanced spectroscopy [1], quantum information processing [2], and all‐optical signal routing [3] is increasingly reliant on the dense integration of photonic circuits [4]. This drive toward miniaturization presents a grand challenge: to identify a single material platform capable of performing active nonlinear frequency conversion [5] and passive subdiffractional light guiding [6]. Conventional materials, however, are often forced to settle at a compromise between these functionalities [7]. For example, Si and Si3N4 possess centrosymmetric crystal lattices that strictly forbid efficient second‐order nonlinear processes, necessitating complex heterogeneous integration for active functionality [8]. Conversely, traditional bulk nonlinear crystals, such as LiNbO3 9 or GaP [10], offer robust nonlinear coefficients but usually require macroscopic interaction lengths [11], thereby imposing a hard limit on device miniaturization. It should be noted that, in nonlinear optical waveguides, the formation of a waveguide mode and realization of propagation regimes requires relatively large propagation lengths, and efforts are aimed at reducing optical losses using special methods of their fabrication [12]. In the opposite case, thin films made of highly nonlinear materials under normal incidence of pump wave are not suitable for the wave propagation regime; however, they can be employed for increasing the efficiency of nonlinear optical processes through the enhancement of the near field at the pump wavelength and/or parametric wavelength.

In this context, van der Waals (vdW) crystals have emerged as a transformative materials platform [6, 13, 14, 15]. Defined by strong in‐plane covalent bonding and weak out‐of‐plane interactions [6], these materials enable an easy integration in photonic design, free from the lattice‐matching constraints of epitaxial growth and compatible with standard lithography processes [16, 17]. Among them, transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs) are the most promising materials thanks to their high refractive index (n ≈ 4) [18, 19, 20] and exceptionally strong light‐matter interactions [21, 22, 23]. However, the field has historically faced a materials dichotomy [24]: the thermodynamically stable 2H‐polytype of TMDCs, including 2H‐MoS2, becomes centrosymmetric in its bulk form, causing its second‐order nonlinearity to vanish. This restricts its utilization to atomically thin monolayers [25], where the interaction volume is insufficient for high‐efficiency conversion [26] or ultracompact waveguide mode [27].

The rhombohedral 3R‐polytype of MoS2 (3R‐MoS2) offers a crystallographic solution to this bottleneck [17, 23, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34]. Unlike its 2H counterpart, 3R‐MoS2 preserves broken inversion symmetry even in the bulk form, allowing the second‐order nonlinear response to scale constructively with the number of layers [28]. For instance, recent breakthroughs [28] have leveraged this property to demonstrate periodically poled vdW microstructures capable of macroscopic frequency conversion efficiencies within a thickness of only a few micrometers, which is orders of magnitude thinner than traditional nonlinear elements. Yet, despite this great interest [17, 23, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34] in the nonlinear capabilities of 3R‐MoS2, a critical gap remains in understanding its linear optical properties. A dominant assumption in the literature [17, 23, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34] is that the linear optical properties of the 3R‐polytype are identical to those of the 2H‐phase. However, this assumption ignores the profound impact of interlayer stacking order on the electronic wavefunction delocalization along the c‐axis [35, 36].

In this work, we challenge this assumption and demonstrate that the distinct stacking sequence of 3R‐MoS2 results in fundamentally different out‐of‐plane dielectric response, giving rise to a giant optical anisotropy in the transparency window. By combining far‐ and near‐field spectroscopies with ab initio calculations, we provide the full anisotropic dielectric tensor of 3R‐MoS2. We reveal that this intrinsic anisotropy is not merely a correction to the 2H‐phase, but a powerful design parameter. For example, we harness it to realize extreme skin‐depth (e‐skid) waveguides, which exploit the giant birefringence to guide light beyond the diffraction limit, outperforming the alternative vdW materials. Furthermore, we show that accurate knowledge of the optical constants allows for the engineering of intrinsic Fabry–Pérot (FP) cavity resonances that boost second‐harmonic (SH) generation efficiency without the need for nanostructuring. As a result, our findings establish 3R‐MoS2 as a multifunctional platform for the next‐generation linear and nonlinear nanophotonics.

Results

Polytype‐Dependent Optical Constants of MoS2

Although 2H‐MoS2 and 3R‐MoS2 share an identical intralayer stoichiometry and hexagonal lattice constants, their interlayer stacking order is fundamentally different. As illustrated in Figure 1a,b, 2H‐MoS2 polytype adopts an alternating AB–AB stacking sequence with a space group P63/mmc and inversion symmetry, while 3R‐MoS2 follows a sliding ABC–ABC stacking sequence with a space group R3m, maintaining a noncentrosymmetric structure. Moreover, photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy in Figure 1c and Note S1 reveals a noticeable difference between the optical properties of these two phases. In addition to a larger PL signal (acquired under identical conditions on flakes of comparable thickness of ∼20 nm), the spin‐orbit coupling (ΔAB) between the A‐ (EA2H = 1.831 eV, λA2H = 677.2 nm and EA3R = 1.828 eV, λA3R = 678.3 nm) and B‐ (EB2H = 2.019 eV, λB2H = 616.9 nm and EB3R = 1.968 eV, λB3R = 630.1 nm) excitonic transitions is noticeably reduced in the 3R‐phase (ΔAB3R = EB3R−EA3R = 140 meV) compared to 2H‐phase (ΔAB2H = EB2H−EA2H = 188 meV), serving as an indication of the modified optical properties in 3R configuration with respect to 2H‐MoS2. Raman spectroscopy of the samples further corroborates the 3R polytype assignment: under 532 nm excitation, the low‐frequency interlayer shear mode observed in 2H‐MoS2 is absent in 3R‐MoS2, consistent with prior reports [37]. Additional excitation‐dependent spectral differences are observed under 633 nm resonant conditions (Notes S1and S2). Furthermore, the band structure calculations in Figure 1d,e show the difference in electronic dispersion with the richer structure in the 3R‐phase owing to larger numbers of atoms in the elementary cell (Figure 1a,b).

To quantify this 2H‐3R difference, we determine the full anisotropic optical constants of 3R‐MoS2 (Figure 1f,g) using broadband spectroscopic ellipsometry (Notes S1, S3 and S4) and compare them with 2H‐MoS26. As anticipated, from the peaks in the extinction coefficients in Figure 1g, we observe the 2H‐3R difference in the spin‐orbit coupling: ΔAB3R = 144 meV and ΔAB2H = 169 meV, which is close to our PL observations in Figure 1c, where we obtain ΔAB3R = 140 meV and ΔAB2H = 188 meV. Moreover, the refractive index of 3R‐MoS2 is reduced compared to 2H‐MoS2 (Figure 1f). This reduction originates from the distinct interlayer stacking in 3R‐MoS2, which leads to stronger interlayer coupling and broader electronic band dispersion (Figure 1d‐e), thereby lowering the overall dielectric response [38]. Furthermore, because this modified coupling is fundamentally driven by out‐of‐plane orbital overlap, it predominantly alters the out‐of‐plane refractive index (nc ) while leaving the in‐plane refractive index (nab ) largely unaffected. Figure 1f confirms this conclusion since nab of 3R‐MoS2 closely mirrors that of the 2H‐phase, whereas nc differs significantly. As a consequence, 3R‐MoS2 has a higher birefringence (∆n = nab − nc ) than 2H‐MoS2, as seen in Figure 1h. To properly contextualize the magnitude of this optical anisotropy, we benchmarked the birefringence of 3R‐MoS2 against values reported for other highly anisotropic vdW materials currently explored in nanophotonics (Note S3). Therefore, we expect even more pronounced anisotropic effects in 3R‐MoS2 from this giant optical anisotropy.

We validate these 3R‐MoS2 optical constants (Figure 1f,h) via two independent checks. First, ab initio calculations based on the Bethe–Salpeter equation (BSE) coincide well with the complex refractive index of 3R‐MoS2 (Note S5). Second, as shown in the inset of Figure 1g, transfer‐matrix method (TMM) calculations based on our newly derived 3R‐MoS2 optical constants accurately reproduce the experimental reflectance for a 143 nm‐thick 3R‐MoS2 flake, whereas calculations based on standard 2H‐MoS2 optical constants do not reproduce the reflectance spectrum. Therefore, Figure 1 establishes the precise optical properties of 3R‐MoS2.

Waveguiding Properties of 3R‐MoS2

To demonstrate the capability of 3R‐MoS2 for subwavelength light guiding [39] and to strictly validate our anisotropic dielectric tensor (Figure 1f,g), we perform real‐space imaging of propagating waveguide modes using scattering‐type scanning near‐field optical microscopy (s‐SNOM, Figure 2a). This technique overcomes the diffraction limit of conventional far‐field characterization, allowing us to directly visualize and quantify the optical eigenmodes confined within the vdW crystal [40]. We investigate the waveguiding response across a broad spectral range using exfoliated 3R‐MoS2 flakes on standard SiO2(300 nm)/Si substrates (Figure 2b). For a 165 nm‐thick flake (Figure 2c), the near‐field amplitudes and phase maps in Figure 2d reveal high‐contrast interference fringes. We analyze these oscillations (Figure 2e) via Fourier transform (Figure 2f) to isolate the constituent optical modes. The Fourier spectra in Figure 2f reveal sharp, distinct peaks corresponding to the momenta of transverse‐magnetic (TM) modes (TM0 and TM1). Notably, the observation of TM1 mode in a flake as thin as 165 nm (< λ/6 for λ = 1000 nm) highlights the strong optical confinement enabled by the high refractive index of 3R‐MoS2 (Figure 1f), allowing higher‐order guided modes to be sustained in an ultrathin vdW geometry.

Extending this analysis to the technologically vital telecom band (1500–1600 nm), we image a 210 nm‐thick flake and resolve both fundamental transverse‐electric (TE0) and transverse‐magnetic (TM0) modes (Note S6). The simultaneous observation of TE and TM modes allows us to probe the material's full anisotropic tensor directly, as TE modes are primarily sensitive to the in‐plane refractive index, while TM modes are strongly influenced by the out‐of‐plane component. In turn, we extract the effective indices of these modes from the experimental momenta and compare them against the energy–momentum dispersion relations calculated by the TMM, based on the generalized 4 × 4 matrix formalism for stratified anisotropic media, in Figure 2g (for 165 nm‐thick flake) and Figure 2h (for 210 nm‐thick flake). This approach accounts for the uniaxial optical tensor of 3R‐MoS2 and allows both TE‐ and TM‐like guided modes to be modeled within the same multilayer framework [41, 42, 43]. As expected, the experimental data points for all observed modes (TM0, TM1, and TE0) across all measured wavelengths and flake thicknesses agree with the theoretical predictions. This agreement serves a dual purpose: it confirms the accuracy of our anisotropic optical constants of 3R‐MoS2 and establishes 3R‐MoS2 as a platform for waveguiding applications.

Subdiffraction Confinement Enabled by 3R‐MoS2

The discovery of giant optical anisotropy in 3R‐MoS2 (Figure 1h) offers an opportunity to overcome the fundamental diffraction limit of light in waveguides, traditionally taken as λ/(2n), where λ is the free‐space wavelength of light and n is the refractive index of the waveguide core. This constraint limits dense photonic integration and has hindered footprint scaling compared with Moore's‐law trends in electronics [44]. Indeed, when the waveguide thickness is reduced, the optical mode increasingly leaks into the low‐index (n clad) cladding (Figure 3a), causing the field to extend over large distances and limiting photonic circuit miniaturization (Figures 3a‐b). This leakage arises because, in isotropic waveguide cladding, the evanescent decay constant (κiso) of the field outside the core is strictly coupled to the effective index (n eff) of the waveguide mode as κiso∝neff2−nclad2. Anisotropic materials enable a fundamentally different regime of confinement, often termed as e‐skid waveguiding (Figure 3c,d) [6, 45, 46]. In this regime, the evanescent decay constant (κani) is governed by the tensor nature of dielectric permittivity as κani∝nabncneff2−nc2 for configuration in Figure 3d. As a result, owing to the giant optical anisotropy, 3R‐MoS2 is an ideal material for e‐skid waveguiding. Simulations of electric field distributions (Figure 3e) and normalized waveguide mode size (Figure 3f) of the 3R‐MoS2/Si/3R‐MoS2 structure confirm these predictions. In fact, waveguides with 3R‐MoS2 provide even better confinement compared to classical 2H‐MoS2 and 2H‐WS2 (Figures 3e,f). To assess the corresponding confinement–loss trade‐off, we also calculate the propagation length, Lprop, for the 3R‐MoS2‐based e‐skid waveguide. The mode size reaches the diffraction limit at λ = 744 nm, and near the maximum anisotropy of 3R‐MoS2, the calculated mode area becomes nearly 20% smaller than the diffraction‐limited value, with a propagation length on the order of ∼10 µm. At longer wavelengths, the mode remains strongly confined while the propagation length increases substantially, reaching the millimeter scale near λ = 1001 nm. This behavior reflects the e‐skid mechanism: the anisotropic MoS2 cladding reduces field leakage into the cladding and concentrates most of the optical energy in the Si core, such that the propagation loss is governed primarily by the material absorption weighted by the modal overlap, with the Si core providing the dominant contribution in the present geometry.

To demonstrate e‐skid waveguiding experimentally, we deposited a ∼300 nm‐thick Si layer on top of the 165 nm‐thick 3R‐MoS2 flake, forming an Air/Si/3R‐MoS2/SiO2/Si stack. s‐SNOM measurements from 1500 to 1600 nm (Figure 3g and Note S7) reveal clear propagating fringes corresponding exclusively to the fundamental TM0 mode. Fourier analysis of the near‐field profiles yields a single, sharp momentum peak at each wavelength (Figure 3h and Note S7), consistent with the strongly confined e‐skid mode predicted for this anisotropic structure. The extracted experimental momenta show excellent agreement with the TMM‐calculated dispersion (Figure 3i), providing strong evidence that 3R‐MoS2 enables e‐skid confinement in a realistic integrated geometry. These results identify 3R‐MoS2 as an effective natural anisotropic material for compact, deeply subwavelength photonic waveguides and open a path toward high‐density, low‐crosstalk photonic integration.

FP Enhancement of SH Generation Efficiency in 3R‐MoS2

To benchmark the nonlinear optical potential of 3R‐MoS2, we first compare its refractive index and second‐order nonlinear susceptibility (χ(2)) with other well‐known nonlinear crystals and two‐dimensional (2D) materials in Figure 4a (see Note S8 for details) at the widely used 1064 nm. 3R‐MoS2 occupies a promising position in this plot with a high refractive index and high χ(2). Moreover, its second‐order nonlinear susceptibility χ(2) can reach up to 800 pm/V at shorter wavelengths (Figure 4b). Another route to enhance second‐order processes in 3R‐MoS2 is the FP enhancement mechanism (Figure 4c), thanks to the high refractive index of 3R‐MoS2. For example, SH emission can be significantly enhanced if the pump wavelength corresponds to the FP resonator maxima of a thick 3R‐MoS2 flake. This leads to an additional increase in the amplitude of the harmonic function (2) with increasing flake thickness. However, the nonlinear susceptibility decreases with increasing pump wavelength in the infrared region of the spectrum, which does not allow full utilization of the nonlinear potential of the 3R‐MoS2 material for longer wavelengths (Figure 4b). Thus, the used pump at the wavelength 1064 nm corresponds to a sufficiently large coefficient χ(2), and additionally allows for the SH wavelength to be within the region of exciton resonances of the material, which contributes to its more efficient generation. As a result, the measured maximal effective nonlinear response (taking into account the FP) takes a giant value χEF(2)=χcal(2)W=1.2nm/V with W = 6.12 for the flake D, while we can achieve up to 1.8 nm/V in our system, which is orders of magnitude greater than in conventional, lithium niobate (LiNbO3) and KBBF (KBe2BO3F2) crystals, see Figure 4a, and is also almost five times greater than in strongly nonlinear GaAs crystal [52].

Discussion

This work establishes 3R‐MoS2 as a multifunctional optical material platform whose stacking order reshapes both linear and nonlinear optical properties, opening physical mechanisms for subdiffraction light confinement and efficient nonlinear conversion in van der Waals photonics. By combining ellipsometry with first‐principles calculations and near‐field measurements, we obtain the full dielectric tensor of 3R‐MoS2 that helps resolve a pervasive ambiguity in the literature [17, 23, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34], where different MoS2 polytypes are often treated interchangeably. A central implication of the extracted tensor is that 3R‐MoS2 hosts record optical anisotropy outperforming 2H‐MoS26, while preserving a high in‐plane refractive index similar to 2H‐MoS2. As a result, this giant optical anisotropy enables e‐skid waveguiding, allowing subdiffractional light confinement demonstrated in Si/3R‐MoS2 heterostructures.

Beyond the near‐field applications, the similar in‐plane refractive index and the distinct out‐of‐plane refractive index of 3R‐MoS2 relative to 2H‐MoS2 enable numerous far‐field applications. For example, their combination can serve as an anti‐reflection coating [53] or as an efficient polarizer [54] based on 2H‐MoS2/3R‐MoS2 heterostructures (for the polarizer demonstration see Note S10). In particular, anisotropy‐engineered vdW heterostructures have recently been shown to enable ultrathin polarization optics: most notably with biaxial crystals such as GeS2 in the visible–UV range [54]. The same strategy applies to the 2H/3R‐MoS2 system, because the two MoS2 polytypes have similar optical properties. As a result, the 2H/3R‐MoS2 heterostructure placed on a high‐index prism operates as a compact polarizer whose extinction ratio is set entirely by the polytype contrast (Note S10). Moreover, we find that the combination of large second‐order nonlinearity and high refractive index of 3R‐MoS2 results in the record‐high effective nonlinear response of 1.2 nm/V due to FP resonances. By comparison, this value is higher than the reported giant nonlinearity of γ‐NaAsSe2 (∼590 pm/V) [55] and comparable to few‐layer SnS (∼1.35 nm/V) [56]. Therefore, the obtained linear and nonlinear record optical properties of 3R‐MoS2 complement those of 2H‐MoS2, giving the additional control knob in photonic design via phase replacement in vdW heterostructures. Note that the use of a FP resonator with dual resonance at pump and SH wavelengths will further enhance the nonlinear optical conversion efficiency. However, this requires additional optimization of the system in order to achieve high nonlinearity and minimal absorption at both of these wavelengths being integer multiples of one another.
