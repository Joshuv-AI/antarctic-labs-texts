Plasticity of the cytotoxic Nep1-like protein enables promiscuity in binding to its lipid receptor glycosylinositol phosphorylceramides
Authors: Nika Žibrat Kalanj, Andreja Prešern, Katerina Naumoska, Tina Snoj, Jana Aupič, Vesna Glavnik, Alen Albreht, Mojca Mally, Jure Derganc, Peter Greimel, Alessandra Magistrato, Gregor Anderluh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Interactions between plants and pathogens pose a major challenge for plant health and agriculture. One type of threat comes from widespread cytolysins necrosis- and ethylene-inducing peptide 1–like proteins (NLPs) secreted by microbial pathogens. NLPs disrupt plant membranes through interactions with glycosylinositol phosphorylceramides (GIPCs), but the specificity of these interactions remains unclear. We studied binding and pore formation of NLPs in model membranes with GIPCs derived from dicotyledonous or monocotyledonous plants. Using the cytotoxic NLP 2 from fungus Moniliophthora perniciosa (MpNEP2), we show that membrane binding and damage extend beyond previously studied oomycetal NLPs. Furthermore, NLPPya from the oomycete Pythium aphanidermatum exhibits promiscuous binding with a marked preference for branched GIPC series B lipids. Molecular dynamics simulations demonstrated that the structural plasticity of NLPPya provides a rational basis for its interaction with a variety of GIPC headgroups. These results clarify the molecular basis of NLP cytotoxicity and emphasize their role in broad-spectrum pathogenicity.

INTRODUCTION

Plant diseases caused by pathogens pose a considerable threat to the consistent and reliable provision of agricultural products on a global scale (1). A plethora of microbial pathogens have been found to secrete effectors that have the capacity to damage plants through a multitude of pathways (2, 3). Necrosis- and ethylene-inducing peptide 1 (Nep1)–like proteins (NLPs) represent a class of microbial effectors that are secreted by a variety of taxonomically unrelated microorganisms, namely, bacteria, fungi, and oomycetes, with the capacity to target a wide range of agriculturally important plants (4). One of the most prominent examples is Phytophthora infestans, a causative agent of the Irish potato famine, which encodes 27 toxic and nontoxic NLP genes (5). The precise function of NLPs remains to be fully elucidated, but the expansion of NLP genes in plant pathogens of both necrotrophic and biotrophic lifestyles indicates their importance (4). A more detailed understanding of the mechanisms of action of NLPs at the molecular level is imperative for the development of better, wide-ranging (bio)pesticides, which have the potential to improve agricultural productivity and ensure food security worldwide (6, 7).

To date, NLPs are known for their ability to permeabilize the plant plasma membrane (8–10) and trigger the plant immune system through the immunogenic 20– to 24–amino acid–long sequence also known as nlp20/nlp24 peptide (11–16). However, the interplay between the two mechanisms of action and their codependency remains unexplained. These 24-kDa proteinaceous effectors share a conserved three-dimensional structure characterized by a central β-sandwich surrounded by flexible loops and three α helices (Fig. 1A and fig. S1) (8, 17, 18). They exhibit a similar structure to actinoporins, a family of pore-forming toxins from marine organisms (8, 19). Analogous to actinoporins, which recognize sphingolipids such as sphingomyelin as their receptor (19, 20), NLPs have been shown to interact at the plasma membrane of plant cells with a specific class of plant sphingolipids, glycosylinositol phosphorylceramides (GIPCs) (Fig. 1B) (9). The flexibility of the loops surrounding the crevice, where the binding occurs, has been suggested to be important for their cytolytic ability. The dynamic properties of the loops L2 and L3, in conjunction with the smaller loops Lc1 located near the C-terminal and Lc2 near the N-terminal region of NLPs, largely determine their capacity to interact with its GIPC receptor (17). In addition, molecular modeling and mutational studies revealed that several amino acid residues located in these loops are important for metal ion and sugar coordination and are essential for cytotoxicity and membrane binding (8, 9, 17). Following the interaction with the lipid receptor, NLPs remain stably attached to the membrane and form small, heterogenous protein oligomers that eventually lead to the formation of transient membrane openings, permeable to small molecules. Given that NLPs do not insert deeply into the membrane, the openings may result from extensive membrane rearrangements caused by NLP binding to GIPCs and oligomerization (Fig. 1C) (10, 21). While most of NLPs exhibit toxic activity, some appear to lack the capacity to damage plant cells and/or bind to GIPCs (4, 17). As shown for nontoxic NLP 3 from Hyaloperonospora arabidopsidis (HaNLP3), this might be explained by the limited conformational plasticity of the GIPC-binding crevice in nontoxic NLPs, which hinders binding to GIPCs (17).

Although GIPCs have been known for a long time (22), their exact headgroup structures remain poorly understood (23). GIPCs are known to be involved in a range of biological functions, such as cell wall–plasma membrane attachment (24), can act as precursors for signaling molecules (25), or sense extracellular concentration of sodium ions (26). Their core structure consists of a ceramide moiety linked to an inositol–glucuronic acid (GlcA) headgroup via a phosphodiester bond. The headgroup may undergo further modification with a variety of carbohydrates, primarily on the GlcA moiety (Fig. 1B) (23). The GIPC composition is plant and tissue specific (27–31). In case of eudicot plants, the predominant species belongs to GIPC series A, the core headgroup of which is modified with one terminal carbohydrate residue. By contrast, in monocot plants, the predominant species belongs to GIPC series B, carrying two additional carbohydrate residues on their core headgroup (9, 31, 32). NLPs were shown to interact with GIPCs from both dicot and monocot plants (9). However, it is widely accepted that NLPs inflict damage on eudicots but not on monocot plants (8, 9, 33–35). For example, plasma membrane–derived vesicles from the monocot Commelina communis exhibited resistance against the membrane cytolytic activity of NLPs derived from the phytopathogenic oomycetes Pythium aphanidermatum (NLPPya) and Phytophthora parasitica (NLPPp), as well as bacterium Pectobacterium carotovorum (NLPPcc). In contrast, all three NLPs were able to permeabilize vesicles prepared from the dicot Arabidopsis thaliana (8, 9). Furthermore, protoplasts from a dicot parsley and A. thaliana were sensitive to NLP-induced cell death, while protoplasts from a monocot maize remained intact (14). In addition, NLPPya was observed to induce necrosis in the monocot Phalaenopsis amabilis, which predominantly contains GIPC series A lipids, resembling a membrane composition analogous to dicots (9). As GIPC series B headgroups were commonly reported as linear structures (27, 28, 31), it was hypothesized that the further extended sugar headgroup in case of monocot GIPCs obstructed the crucial NLP interaction with the plasma membrane, impeding effective pore formation (9). However, recent reports indicate that several species of monocots are sensitive to cytotoxic action of NLPs and that this observed sensitivity does not correlate with the GIPC series A:B ratio (32, 36). Establishing a direct connection between lipid composition and the effects of NLPs is challenging because studies used natural systems that contain numerous additional components, which may influence NLP binding and subsequent membrane perforation.

To comprehend the discrepancy in NLP toxicity toward eudicot and monocot plants, it is essential to acquire a more detailed understanding of the NLP-GIPC interaction from a molecular perspective. Here, we show that unrelated cytotoxic NLPs bind and permeabilize membranes containing GIPCs derived from monocots or dicots. Furthermore, we present a markedly improved experimental procedure for the separation and isolation of GIPCs based on their headgroup differences, facilitating a more comprehensive characterization of their identity and the structural arrangement of their sugar headgroups. The binding analysis demonstrates that NLPPya binds to GIPCs from distinct series and establishes preferential binding of NLPPya to the branched GIPC series B lipids. In addition, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations with GIPCs from different series docked into the NLPPya binding crevice rationalize the experimentally observed binding promiscuity as a consequence of protein plasticity.

RESULTS

Cytotoxic NLPs bind membranes containing dicot or monocot GIPCs

We wanted to assess the ability of NLPs to form pores in model lipid membranes composed of GIPCs purified from dicot (tobacco) or monocot (leek) plants. For functional studies, we used two unrelated cytotoxic NLPs, namely, NLPPya and MpNEP2. NLPPya is produced by an oomycete P. aphanidermatum and is the most studied NLP (8–10), while MpNEP2 is present in a pathogenic fungus Moniliophthora perniciosa that causes the Witches’ Broom disease in cacao (fig. S1) (18). The sequence identity between NLPPya and MpNEP2 is 38.5% (fig. S1A). First, the recombinantly expressed NLPPya and MpNEP2 were confirmed to retain their cytotoxicity by inducing necrosis of tobacco plant cells (fig. S2, A and B). Next, binding of the recombinant proteins to multilamellar vesicles (MLVs) was assessed with the sedimentation assay. The vesicles were composed of 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC) and purified GIPCs from either tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum; Nt-GIPCs) or leek (Allium porrum; Ap-GIPCs) plants. In addition, plant sterols were added to the lipid mixture (37) (equimolar mix of stigmasterol, campesterol, and β-sitosterol), and the binding was compared to the vesicles that did not contain any sterols. The concentration of NLPs was 2.6 μM, yielding NLP:lipid of approximately 1:1800. Both proteins bound to all tested membranes containing GIPCs but not to control POPC membranes with or without sterols (fig. S2, C and D). The presence of sterols generally enhanced the binding of both proteins, as previously observed for NLPPya (10).

NLPs disrupt planar lipid bilayers comprising Nt-GIPCs and Ap-GIPCs

Next, we used the planar lipid bilayer approach to directly monitor the formation of pores in real time by measuring the ionic currents through the membrane (38). Using this highly sensitive method, NLPPya was recently shown to induce the formation of transient pores in a lipid bilayer mimicking the tobacco plasma membrane composition (10). Here, we used 1,2-diphytanoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DPhPC) and Nt-GIPCs at a molar ratio of 6:4 to form stable planar lipid bilayers (Fig. 1D). For a period of up to several minutes following the addition of NLPPya at a final concentration of 5 μM, transient increases in ion current were observed. These observed increases manifested as fluctuations in the amplitude of the electric signal. In the majority of cases, these fluctuations returned to their initial level, indicating the closure of the pores (Fig. 1D). Repeated opening and closing of pores were observed until the open pores gradually stabilized, which resulted in the bilayer structure of the membrane being overwhelmed and leading to its collapse (see the inset in Fig. 1D). Similar changes in ion currents were observed in the presence of Ap-GIPCs, giving rise to predominately noisy traces after addition of NLPPya (Fig. 1, E and F). Under both conditions, immediate membrane collapse after addition of the protein occurred (Fig. 1F) in a limited number of cases (<10% of cases). The delay between the addition of the protein and the first discernable fluctuations in the ion current is indicative of the efficacy of the protein-GIPC interaction, given its reliance on how this interaction affects the individual phases of the pore-forming process. This time delay was shorter in presence of Nt-GIPCs compared to Ap-GIPCs upon NLPPya activity (Fig. 1G), suggesting that NLPPya appears to be more effective on dicot GIPCs containing membranes.

The observed range of frequency and amplitudes of fluctuations of the ion current are indicative of a nonuniform distribution of the pore architecture and size. This finding is consistent with previous observations that oligomeric NLPPya assemblies that lead to pore formation are heterogeneous in size and do not traverse the lipid membrane in an ordered structure, in contrast to more typical pore-forming toxins (10, 21, 39). One such example is the pore-forming toxin lysenin, which forms well-defined β barrel pores with a diameter of ~2 nm (40). The insertion of lysenin pores into planar lipid bilayers containing both Nt-GIPCs or Ap-GIPCs is detected as a sudden, step-wise increase in electric current (Fig. 1H). The high degree of stable discrete insertion of lysenin pores in membranes containing GIPCs in contrast to NLPPya (Fig. 1, F versus I) serves as a good quality control, confirming the formation of stable bilayers before protein addition.

In case of MpNEP2 addition at a final concentration of 20 μM, similar fluctuations of the ion currents (transient pore formation) to NLPPya were observed (fig. S3, A and B), while immediate membrane collapse was observed in about 5% of the cases (fig. S3C). The delay time after protein addition until observation of the first fluctuations in ion currents was similar for both Nt-GIPCs and Ap-GIPCs containing membranes (fig. S3D). In summary, both NLPs were able to form qualitatively similar pores in both types of membranes.

Cytotoxic NLPs permeabilize giant unilamellar vesicles comprising Nt-GIPCs or Ap-GIPCs

A microfluidics setup with giant unilamellar vesicles (GUVs) as model membranes was used (fig. S4) to observe membrane binding and pore formation of NLPs in real time (41). We prepared GUVs from POPC, GIPCs, and plant sterols in a molar ratio of 1:2:1, encapsulating the fluorescent probe Alexa Fluor 594 (A594). We labeled NLPs with fluorescent probe Alexa Fluor 488 (A488) and confirmed that labeled NLPs retained cytotoxicity (fig. S5).

To characterize the respective GUV binding and pore-forming activity, we monitored the adsorption of NLPPya-A488 and the leakage of encapsulated A594 (movies S1 and S2). Addition of 1.5 μM NLPPya-A488 to Nt-GIPCs containing GUVs resulted in a strong fluorescent signal associated with the GUV membrane within a few minutes and subsequent release of the encapsulated A594 (Fig. 2, A, B, and E). In the case of Ap-GIPCs, binding to the GUV membrane occurred at similar timescales (Fig. 2, C, D, F, and G). However, the duration required for the complete release of the encapsulated fluorophore was prolonged in the presence of Ap-GIPCs compared to Nt-GIPCs (Fig. 2, E, F, and H). No probe leakage from GUVs without GIPCs was observed after the addition of 1.5 μM NLPPya-A488 [control 1 (C1); Fig. 2, E and F, and fig. S6, A and B]. Similarly, no probe leakage from GUVs incorporating either GIPCs upon treatment with buffer instead of NLP solution was observed [control 2 (C2); Fig. 2, E and F, and fig. S6, C and D]. Following NLPPya binding, 91% of Nt-GIPC–containing and 61% of Ap-GIPC–containing GUVs exhibited morphological changes, resulting in membrane deformations, formation of hair-like structures associated with shrinking of the vesicle size, and eventual complete vesicle collapse (Fig. 2, A, C, E, and F).

We performed the same experiments using labeled 12 μM MpNEP2 (movies S3 and S4). Similar to NLPPya, MpNEP2-A488 also bound to GUV membranes and induced pore formation, resulting in A594 probe leakage and emptying of the vesicles (fig. S7, A to F). The binding of MpNEP2-A488 was slower in case of Ap-GIPC compared to Nt-GIPC containing membranes (fig. S7G), although A594 leakage was not found to be significantly slower (fig. S7H). In contrast to NLPPya, MpNEP2-treated vesicles maintained their structural integrity after emptying and did not collapse (fig. S7, A, C, E, and F). Addition of 12 μM MpNEP2-A488 to GUVs in the absence of GIPC did not result in probe leakage, as observed also in NLPPya-treated GUVs (fig. S6B).

GIPC series B headgroups from tobacco and leek are branched

GIPC extracts contain a variety of GIPC series, each featuring a distinct number and type of carbohydrates in their headgroups (23, 31, 42). To obtain a more homogenous GIPC sample composed of individual species for functional studies, we separated GIPC extracts using high-performance thin-layer chromatography (HPTLC). After HPTLC separation of Nt-GIPCs, four distinct chromatographic zones containing GIPCs were identified by mass spectrometry (MS) and named Nt1-GIPCs to Nt4-GIPCs (Fig. 3A). Plant-derived impurities were observed above the GIPC-rich chromatographic zones, with lower molecular masses compared to GIPCs (Fig. 3A and fig. S8). The Nt1-GIPC to Nt4-GIPC fractions were individually extracted from HPTLC plates for further analysis and functional studies. Approximately two-thirds of the Nt-GIPCs were comprised by Nt3-GIPCs and Nt4-GIPCs (Fig. 3B). Full-MS spectra of these fractions showed that both Nt3-GIPCs and Nt4-GIPCs corresponded to GIPC series A. Nt2-GIPC fraction contained a mixture of GIPC series B to E, while Nt1-GIPCs were composed of a mixture of GIPC series D to F (Fig. 3C and table S1).

Tandem MS analysis of GIPC series A from Nt3-GIPC fraction (fig. S9 and table S1) and Nt4-GIPC fraction (fig. S10 and table S1) resulted in the highly similar fragmentation pattern, with the exception that fragments Y2 and Z2 resulted from a loss of either hexoamine (HexN) in Nt3-GIPCs or N-acetylhexosamine (HexNAc) in Nt4-GIPCs. Fragments 0,2X1 and Y1 confirmed that hexuronic acid (HexA) is linked to inositol, therefore identifying the lipids as HexN-HexA-inositol phosphorylceramides and HexNAc-HexA-inositol phosphorylceramides. Tandem MS analysis of GIPC series B (present in Nt2-GIPC fraction) revealed that it contains HexN as a terminal sugar (according to fragments Y2α, Z2α, [Z2α-CO2]−, [C3/1αPO3]−, [C3/1αPO3-CO2]−, and [B3/C1α-CO2]−) and also a terminal hexose (Hex; fragment Y1β) (Fig. 3, D and E, and table S1). According to fragments 0,2X1α, Y1α, Z1α, [B3/2αPO3]−, and [C3PO3/B2α]−, HexN is linked to HexA. Additional minor fragments further confirmed the presence of the second branch, since both HexA (fragments [Z2α/Y1β -CO2]− and [0,2X1α/Y1β]−) and Hex (fragments Y1α, Z1α, [B3/2αPO3]−, and [C3PO3/B2α]−) were shown to be linked to inositol (Fig. 3, D and E, and table S1). The precise locations of the two branches on the inositol ring remain undefined because of the lack of appropriate MS standards. GIPC series C from Nt2-GIPC fractions is most likely linear, since only a pentose (fragment Y4) but not a Hex [no signals at a mass/charge ratio (m/z) of 1392 or 1374] was observed as a terminal sugar (fig. S11 and table S1). The amounts of GIPCs associated with series higher than C were too low to determine their exact headgroup structures. In addition, all GIPCs belonging to series B or higher featured HexN, while HexNAc was not detected (table S1).

Given that GIPC series B represents the predominant GIPC species in leek (31, 42), this fraction was prepared (Fig. 3F) and analyzed by MS (Fig. 3G and table S1). Although the individual molecular masses of GIPC series B from leek differ from those of tobacco (Fig. 3, C and G, and table S1), the fragmentation pattern was similar, confirming that both HexN and Hex were present as terminal sugars (fig. S12). As a consequence, the observed mass differences between GIPC series B from leek and tobacco are attributable to variations in their acyl chain patterns.

NLPPya binds preferentially to GIPC series B

Next, binding of NLPPya to Nt1-GIPCs to Nt4-GIPCs was evaluated using binding assays incorporating washing steps. NLPPya bound to Nt2-GIPCs and very weakly to Nt4-GIPCs, but not to Nt1-GIPCs and Nt3-GIPCs, based on immunodetection after TLC separation of Nt-GIPCs (Fig. 4A). For dot blot analysis, we applied lipid mixtures on TLC plate as spots and transferred them to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. Strong NLPPya binding was detected on all samples that contained Nt2-GIPCs (Nt-GIPCs, Nt2-GIPCs, and mix of all isolated Nt-GIPC fractions) in presence of sterols, while all other fractions (Nt1-GIPCs, Nt3-GIPCs, and Nt4-GIPCs) resulted in very weak signals with low reproducibility (Fig. 4B). In the absence of sterols, practically no NLPPya binding was detected.

To test NLPPya binding to GIPCs incorporated in lipid bilayers, we prepared MLVs composed of POPC and GIPCs with or without sterols. We confirmed binding of NLPPya to vesicles containing Nt2-GIPCs, and the binding was found to be statistically significant in the presence and absence of sterols, as compared to non-GIPC vesicle controls (Fig. 4, C and D). Furthermore, binding of NLPPya to vesicles composed of Nt2-GIPCs and sterols was found to be significantly increased compared to the sterol-free vesicles (Fig. 4D). In contrast, NLPPya did not bind to vesicles prepared from fractions Nt1-GIPCs, Nt3-GIPCs, and Nt4-GIPCs even in the presence of sterols. This demonstrated that NLPPya presentably binds to Nt-GIPC fractions containing series B.

Performing the same dot blot experiment, as in Fig. 4B, with leek samples resulted in the same pattern of NLPPya binding (Fig. 4E). Similar to Nt2-GIPCs results, NLPPya bound to GIPC series B fraction (ApB-GIPCs) containing MLVs (Fig. 4, F and G).

NLPPya binds small unilamellar vesicles with distinct fractions of GIPCs

Using surface plasmon resonance (SPR), binding of NLPPya to small unilamellar vesicles (SUVs) was monitored in real time (43, 44). We used SUVs composed of POPC:GIPCs:sterols (ratio of 3:4:3, n:n:n) as a test surface and SUVs without GIPCs as a control surface (POPC:sterols, 7:3, n:n). NLPPya bound to all Nt-GIPCs fractions in a concentration-dependent manner. However, a marked variation was observed in the binding magnitude and dissociation from the surface of SUVs across the tested fractions (Fig. 5, A to E). The binding to SUVs containing Nt-GIPCs, Nt2-GIPCs, and Nt4-GIPCs was substantial, while binding to SUVs containing Nt1-GIPCs was considerably reduced, although concentration dependency was retained. The interaction of NLPPya with SUVs containing Nt3-GIPCs was found to be negligible; thus, further analysis of these results was omitted.

The observed sensorgram shapes were not consistent with a 1:1 binding model, thus rendering analysis using commonly used models unfeasible. This suggests that the recorded sensorgrams represent a sum of multiple interactions, an expected consequence given the heterogeneity of the GIPC samples. Even the purified fractions contained several different GIPC species differing in acyl chain length (i.e., Nt3-GIPCs, Nt4-GIPCs, and ApB-GIPCs) and, additionally, in their headgroup composition (i.e., Nt1-GIPCs and Nt2-GIPCs) (Fig. 3). The corresponding sensorgrams thus represent NLPPya interaction with different GIPC species in the SUVs. The dissociation data were fitted using a double exponential function according to Eq. 1 (table S2), which indicated a complex relationship between different fractions and NLPPya dissociation. Notably, the SUVs containing Nt-GIPCs and Nt2-GIPCs exhibited a slower rate of dissociation in comparison to the SUVs containing Nt1-GIPCs and Nt4-GIPCs. SUVs composed of Nt-GIPCs and Nt2-GIPCs were associated with a higher degree with the slower dissociation component (kd2 of ~3 × 10−2 s−1), while SUVs containing Nt1-GIPCs were predominately associated with the faster dissociation component, which is an order of magnitude faster (kd1 of ~5 × 10−1 s−1). Both components were of similar value in Nt4-GIPCs, resembling the predominant value of Nt1-GIPCs (kd1 and kd2 of ~5 × 10−1 s−1; table S2). Most of sensorgrams reached steady state during the association phase, allowing the estimation of equilibrium dissociation constants using steady-state response data (Fig. 5F and table S3). Affinity plots can be described by a two-state binding function according to Eq. 2. According to these results, SUVs composed of Nt-GIPCs and Nt2-GIPCs contain a high proportion of binding sites that yield a KD value of ~3 × 10−8 M, while most of binding sites from Nt1-GIPCs and Nt4-GIPCs result in notably lower KD values of ~1 × 10−6 M. Despite the fit of the dissociation phase and steady-state response levels to two-component functions, the sensorgrams did not fit standard two-state heterogeneity models, suggesting the coexistence of several similar interactions. Performing the same SPR analysis with SUVs containing leek-derived GIPC extract (Ap-GIPCs) and ApB-GIPCs (Fig. 5, G and H) revealed similar NLPPya binding patterns in shape, magnitude, and kinetic parameters to Nt-GIPCs and Nt2-GIPCs (Fig. 5I and tables S1 and S2), further supporting the importance of GIPC series B for efficient binding.

NLPPya binding crevice can accommodate different GIPC series

To obtain an atomistic insight into the impact of the chemical nature of the GIPC headgroups on the binding of NLPPya to the membrane surface, we used MD simulations. First, the different identified headgroups were docked into the Mg2+-containing NLPPya central crevice. Specifically, we modeled four different headgroups: GIPC series A with the terminal N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc), corresponding to Nt4-GIPCs; GIPC series A with the terminal glucosamine (GlcN), representing Nt3-GIPCs; and GIPC series B and C that make up Nt2-GIPCs. Identities of terminal sugars and GlcA as HexA were assumed according to previous data (42, 45–47). After docking, all GIPCs assumed a similar binding orientation in the central crevice, with the shared inositol-GlcA moiety held in place by Asn194, Asn196, Lys206, and the Mg2+ ion (fig. S13). The obtained GIPC:NLPPya complexes were then anchored into POPC:GIPC (1:1; n:n) lipid bilayers. In each simulated system, the membrane contained a single GIPC type, and MD trajectories were collected over 500 ns for each system in triplicates.

NLPPya remained bound to the membrane during MD simulations for all POPC:GIPC membrane types (Fig. 6, A to D). The GIPC sugar headgroups were stably anchored in the central crevice and largely maintained the initial docked conformations (fig. S14). NLPPya remained well folded with highest positional fluctuations observed in the loop regions L1, L2, L3, and Lc1, surrounding the GIPC binding crevice of NLPPya (Fig. 6, E to H, and fig. S15). This inherent flexibility, in turn, allowed the size of the binding cavity to flexibly adapt to the different headgroups of GIPCs (Fig. 6, E to H, and fig. S16). The contact site of the NLPPya on the membrane surface differed between the tested lipid bilayers (fig. S17), suggesting that NLPPya can assume different orientations on the membrane. This is in line with the results of previous metadynamics simulations showing that NLPPya wobbles on the POPC:GIPC membrane surface, assuming different metastable binding modes (10).

Analysis of interactions between GIPC headgroups and NLPPya identified Asp93, Glu106, His128, Ser129, Asp158, Asn194, Asn196, and Lys206 as the most contributing residues to GIPC headgroup binding (Fig. 7, A to E, and fig. Calculations of the average total contact number between NLPPya and docked GIPCs revealed that GIPC series A with the terminal GlcN interacted with NLPPya substantially less than other GIPC types (Fig. 7F). On the other hand, GIPC series A with the terminal GlcNAc formed the highest number of contacts and was engaged in hydrogen bonding (Fig. 7G). Last, we calculated the binding free energies with the molecular mechanics Poisson-Boltzmann surface area (MM-PBSA) method in combination with the heterogeneous dielectric implicit membrane model. GIPC series A with the terminal GlcNAc was characterized by the largest binding free energy (−45 ± 9 kcal/mol), calculated by averaging over three simulation replicas, indicating favorable binding. Binding free energy for GIPC series B and C was −37 ± 2 and −29 ± 2 kcal/mol, respectively, while GIPC series A featuring terminal GlcN displayed the lowest binding affinity (−23 ± 3 kcal/mol), consistent with the contact analysis.

Such a pronounced difference between GIPC series A lipids was quite unexpected, since the headgroups contrast only in the type of the functional group located on position 2 carbon of the terminal 2-deoxy-d-glucopyranose. While GlcNAc contains the acetamido group at that position, GlcN instead presents the positively charged amino group. Comparing the binding poses of GIPC series A with terminal GlcNAc or GlcN after the MD simulations, we noticed that in case of GIPC series A, with the terminal GlcN, the GIPC phosphate group moved closer to the GlcN amino group, changing the conformation of the sugar headgroup and pulling the inositol ring out of the cavity (fig. S14). This movement could explain the decreased binding affinity. In the case of GIPC series B, which also contains the terminal GlcN, we observed a similar positioning of the phosphate group and inositol ring (fig. S14). However, GIPC series B contains an additional d-glucose (Glc) moiety attached to the inositol ring, which can make additional contacts with the NLPPya protein, counteracting the reduction in affinity due to the loss of interaction between the inositol ring and NLPPya.

Together, MD simulations suggest that the experimentally observed capacity of NLPPya to bind different GIPC types can be attributed to the conformational plasticity of the GIPC binding crevice of NLPPya, allowing it to accommodate different GIPC headgroups. Regardless, the extent of interactions stabilizing the GIPC:NLPPya binding varies across GIPC types, explaining the experimentally observed differences in binding affinity.

DISCUSSION

Cell death and pore-forming activity have been known traits of NLPs (4, 10), but the underlying determinants governing binding and pore formation on plant plasma membranes remain poorly understood. Here, we report the direct assessment of the binding and pore-forming activity of NLPs in model membrane systems containing GIPCs from tobacco or leek.

Experiments on planar bilayer membranes (Fig. 1) and on GUVs using microfluidics (Fig. 2) showed that NLPPya can bind to membranes containing both GIPC extracts effectively and form transient pores. For GUVs, the binding time until pore formation (τbind) was not significantly different, while the duration of probe leakage (τrelease) was longer in GUVs composed of Ap-GIPCs when compared to Nt-GIPCs (Fig. 2, G and H). The difference in τrelease in GUVs was consistent with the slower appearance of pores in planar lipid bilayers composed of Ap-GIPCs (Fig. 1G). This difference in pore formation rate could be attributed to a slower oligomerization process in membranes containing Ap-GIPCs, as pore formation by NLPPya has been reported to be a multistep process composed of binding, oligomerization, and pore formation (10).

To determine whether the pore-forming ability of GIPC-rich membranes represents a common characteristic of toxic NLPs, fungal MpNEP2 was used as a second representative. It was demonstrated that MpNEP2 also binds to GIPCs derived from both tobacco and leek and that sterols promote binding (fig. S2). MpNEP2 has been shown to have the same capacity for pore formation as NLPPya, a finding that was substantiated using both planar lipid bilayers (fig. S3) and GUVs composed of Nt-GIPCs and Ap-GIPCs (fig. S7). However, in contrast to NLPPya, the presence of Nt-GIPCs or Ap-GIPCs significantly altered the τbind of MpNEP2, whereas τrelease was not affected (fig. S7). Following NLPPya attachment and pore formation, most GUVs, composed of both Nt-GIPCs and Ap-GIPCs, displayed morphological changes, such as blebs and protrusions that ultimately resulted in vesicle collapse. Similar morphological effects were not observed upon MpNEP2 treatment (fig. S7). In case of NLPPya, these morphological effects were reported to be protein concentration dependent (48) and only occurred following complete binding and depletion of the encapsulated probe (Fig. 2). This prolonged delay of morphological changes of membranes renders them less biologically relevant.

These results suggest that although toxic NLPs of different phylogenetic origin share the multistep mechanism of pore formation, the detailed process of binding, oligomerization, pore formation, and other effects on lipid membranes may vary between different toxic NLPs. These effects may depend on the structural features and physicochemical properties of the individual NLP (fig. S1). For example, although both proteins used in this study share a conserved fold and loop configuration, their surface charges differ substantially; specifically, the binding crevice of MpNEP2 is more negatively charged than that of NLPPya (fig. S1, B and C). This may affect the accommodation of negatively charged GIPC headgroups, differences in binding affinity to GIPCs, and the depth of insertion into the membrane. Collectively, these differences can influence membrane organization and give rise to distinct morphological changes of GUVs.

The lack of precise knowledge regarding the GIPC headgroup structures constitutes an important challenge in the study of the biology of plant lipid membranes, particularly in relation to NLP-membrane interactions. Here, we reported the separation of GIPCs from tobacco and leek. GIPCs derived from tobacco exhibited the expected mixture of series A to E (Fig. 3), with the predominant GIPC series A matching their known structure (49). Higher GIPC series headgroups are commonly referred to as linear structures (27, 28, 31), which was the case for series C (fig. S11). However, tandem MS analysis of GIPC series B from both tobacco and leek revealed the presence of the same branched headgroup structures with the same carbohydrate composition. The two branches were identified as a disaccharide HexN-HexA and a single Hex unit, which is consistent with two previous reports on tobacco GIPC series B (46, 50). Contrary to the literature, this is the first mention of branched GIPC series B in leek (28, 31). The same two branches were found in GIPC series B from maize, with a Hex, more specifically a mannose (Man) attached to the second position of inositol [Man(α1→2)inositol] and the disaccharide attached to the sixth position of inositol [GlcN(α1→4)GlcA(α1→6)inositol] (45). Considering that in the known structure of GIPC series A from tobacco, the disaccharide subunit is bound to position two of the inositol ring, it seems, from a biosynthetic point of view, more likely that GIPC series B from tobacco features the same disaccharide at position two (49). The branched sugar headgroup of tobacco GIPC series B has been previously described as the minor tetrasaccharide from tobacco leaves, whereas the major tetrasaccharide was reported to be linear (46). On the basis of our results, the branched GIPC series B headgroup is predominant, if not the only, tetrasaccharide in tobacco and leek leaves (Fig. 3, fig. S12, and table S1). Similar branched GIPC series B structures have also been found in other plants, namely, Erodium moschatum (a dicot), Physcomitrella patens (a moss), Polytrichum commune (a moss), and Pteridium aquilinum (a fern) (50). In conclusion, it appears that branched GIPC series B is widespread in the plant kingdom.

A more in-depth understanding of NLP preference for GIPC series was obtained by focusing on the association of NLPPya with different Nt-GIPC fractions. The results suggest that NLPPya can associate with different GIPCs (Figs. 5 to 7); however, branched GIPC series B serves as the major receptor of NLPs. In branched GIPC series B, the longest branch exhibits the same length as that found in GIPC series A headgroups, while the additional Hex unit of the second branch seems to improve the stability of the GIPC:NLPPya complex. The enhanced stability of branched GIPC series B:NLPPya complexe has been demonstrated to result in a reduced dissociation of the complex, as observed in membranes containing GIPC series B (Fig. 5). This observation was further emphasized by washing steps in end-point assays (Fig. 4), which effectively removed NLPPya from vesicles containing Nt1-GIPC, Nt3-GIPC, and Nt4-GIPC fractions. These results were in line with the same GIPC fractions exhibiting rapid dissociation in the SPR assay (Fig. 5). The slow dissociation from membranes upon interaction with a highly specific lipid receptor is a common phenomenon observed in pore-forming toxins (51). The influence of minor GIPC series in Nt1-GIPC and Nt2-GIPC fractions (i.e., series C to E) is likely to further influence NLPPya binding to model membranes. The MD simulations suggested that the size of the binding crevice of NLPPya is sufficiently large and flexible to also accommodate and recognize GIPC series C (Figs. 6 and 7). However, we hypothesize that these higher GIPC series do not play an important role in efficient binding of NLPPya, given their low observed amounts (Fig. 3) and considerable rarity of GIPC series C in plants, with its presence documented in only 7 of 23 plant species (31).

The anchoring of GIPC:NLPPya complex into GIPC-containing membrane showed that the loops around the binding site are highly flexible (Fig. 6 and fig. S15) (17) and adapt to different headgroups of GIPCs. MD simulations identified several residues that might be important for GIPC recognition, and some of them have already been tested for their role in NLP toxicity. The three residues that appear to interact with all four GIPC series are His128 positioned on Lc1 and Asn194 and Asn196 on Lc2. These residues are placed on the entry to the binding crevice, and thereby their interaction with GIPCs in MD trajectories is expected. Asp93, Asp104, Glu106, and Asp158 are important for Mg2+ coordination in the presence or absence of the sugar ligand (9), and mutational analysis of these residues in NLPs resulted in the absence or diminished toxicity (8, 18, 52). Asp104 and Glu106 are part of a highly conserved heptapeptide motif, positioned on the β strand, and are essential for the cytotoxicity (4, 6). Residues Ser126, Ser129, Gly130, and Tyr131 that interacted with GIPC series A and B are positioned on Lc1 surrounding the binding pocket where the sugar is docked. No reports were made on their role the toxicity; however, in NLPPcc, an NLP homolog from P. carotovorum, the mutation of Ser126 to Ala did not diminish the necrotic and plasma membrane–disrupting ability (8). The C-terminal helix, harboring residues Lys206 and Lys209, interacts with GIPC series A–GlcNAc and GIPC series B but not GIPC series A–GlcN and GIPC series C in MD simulations. It has been reported for NLP from P. parasitica that truncating the C-terminus prevents cytotoxic action (12). Together, MD simulations explain the interaction with all GIPC samples observed with SPR and provide further details on how different headgroups of GIPCs are bound by NLPPya.

Together, our results demonstrate that two unrelated NLPs can bind and form pores in model membranes containing GIPCs from both dicotyledonous and monocotyledonous plants. While GIPC series B appears to be the preferred receptor of NLPPya, both NLPs were able to form pores in membranes containing monocot-derived GIPCs, which predominantly consist of series B, as well as in membranes containing tobacco-derived GIPCs, where the majority, ~68% in our study (Fig. 3), are of series A. Although the specific role of different GIPC series in the pore-forming process could not be established, our results suggest that different GIPC series could have different roles in pore-forming mechanism, i.e., lipids of series A could be relevant to later stages of pore formation, or different GIPC series could contribute synergistically to different steps of pore formation.

Furthermore, the model systems used were deficient in membrane proteins and other molecules readily found in plant cells that could influence NLP activity. Recently, lysophospholipase, a peroxisome-localized enzyme produced in cotton, has been reported to counteract the toxic effect of NLP from the fungus Verticillium dahliae by binding to its NLP protein (53). In contrast, plant-derived factors such as leucine-rich repeat (LRR) proteins have been reported to be recruited by NLPs upon membrane binding. One such LRR protein, NTCD4, facilitates the oligomerization of NLPs from Magnaporthe oryzae and Botrytis cinerea and promotes their toxicity (54). The observed differences in NLP toxicity in plants are therefore likely the result of a complex interplay between the composition of the plasma membrane, the plant immune system, and other factors that can either block or promote NLP activity. In addition, GIPC content can vary between even different cultivars within the same species, meaning that model membranes containing GIPCs from other plants need to be tested in the future (32).

In summary, biophysical approaches were used to study NLP-membrane interactions, and it was demonstrated that NLPs from microorganisms from two distinct phyla can bind GIPCs and form transient pores in membranes containing monocot- and dicot-derived GIPCs, despite substantial differences in their GIPC series A and B composition. In addition, it was shown that NLPPya exhibits promiscuous binding to different GIPC series, while the most prominent NLPs receptor was branched GIPC series B. This branched GIPC species has been reported to be present throughout the plant kingdom, including dicots and monocots, which can explain recent reports that monocots were also susceptible to NLPs, regardless of their GIPC series A to B ratio (32, 36). The exact roles of NLPs in the infection cycle of plant-associated microbes remains largely unknown. While there is evidence that NLP-induced immune responses occur in both monocotyledonous and dicotyledonous plants (4, 32), the role of immunological and cytolytic effects of NLPs in plant damage remains uncertain. Further, in vivo studies are required to elucidate the precise function and mechanism of GIPC binding and pore formation in plant colonization and infection.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

A488 N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) ester and A594 (758.79 Da) were from Molecular Probes (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For HPTLC analysis and isolation of GIPCs, tetrahydrofuran [high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) grade, ≥99.9%] and methanol (LC-MS grade, ≥99.9%) were purchased from Honeywell (Seelze, Germany). Methanol [(ultra)HPLC grade] was acquired from J.T.Baker (Gliwice, Poland). Formic acid (for analysis, 98 to 100%), ammonia solution (32%), chloroform (for analysis), potassium carbonate (for analysis), and HPTLC silica gel 60 plates were obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Primuline, orcinol (97%), and acetone (for analysis, ≥99.5%) were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Ultrapure water (18 megohm·cm) was obtained from the Milli-Q water purification system, Millipore (Bedford, MA, USA). For binding experiments, TLC silica gel 60 F254 plate and PVDF membrane were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany), primary antibodies (Penta-His antibody) were obtained from QIAGEN (Hilden, Germany), and secondary antibodies [anti-mouse immunoglobulin G (whole molecule)–peroxidase] from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Sensor chip L1 was from Cytiva (Marlborough, MA, USA). For gel electrophoresis, mPAGE gels from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany) and ProBlue Safe from Giotto Biotech (Florence, Italy) were used.

Expression and purification of NLPPya and MpNEP2

We expressed and purified the NLPPya protein as described previously (8–10). Briefly, the gene for the protein without the signal peptide with a histidine tag on the C terminus was cloned into pET-21c and transformed into Escherichia coli BL21(DE3). The bacteria were grown in Terrific Broth medium at 37°C until they reached an optical density of 0.8. After induction with 0.5 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside, the protein was expressed at 20°C overnight. Bacterial culture was separated from the liquid medium by centrifugation and was resuspended in lysis buffer containing 8 M urea, 0.1 M NaH2PO4, and 10 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and sonicated on ice for 21 min (1-s on and 2-s off). The lysate was centrifuged at 50,000g for 45 min at 4°C. The supernatant was filtered through 22-μm filter and loaded onto Ni–nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA) column (QIAGEN, Germany). After loading, the bound sample was washed three times with three buffers containing 0.1 M NaH2PO4, 0.02 M imidazole, and 0.01 M tris-HCl (pH 6.3) and decreasing concentrations of urea, which were 2, 0.5, and 0.1 M. The final step was done with 0.05 M Na2HPO4/NaH2PO4, 300 mM NaCl, and 300 mM imidazole (pH 8.0). Last, the protein was eluted from the column with 6 M guanidinium hydrochloride and 0.2 M acetic acid. The protein was refolded four times by dialysis against water and lastly against 20 mM MES and 150 mM NaCl (pH 5.8) at 4°C.

We expressed and purified the MpNEP2 protein by modifying the published procedure (18, 55). Briefly, gene for MpNEP2 (GenBank accession number EF114673.1) without signal peptide was cloned into a modified pET28a vector with a histidine tag and a tobacco etch virus (TEV) cleavage site on the N-terminus. The protein was expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) after induction with 0.5 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside. After overnight expression at 20°C, we harvested the cells and sonicated for 21 min on ice (1-s on and 2-s off). The lysate was centrifuged at 50,000g for 45 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was filtered through 22-μm filter and was loaded onto Ni-NTA column (QIAGEN, Germany). The sample was dialyzed in 30 mM tris-HCl and 100 mM NaCl (pH 8.0), and TEV protease was added in a molar ratio of 1:60 to the protein for cleavage overnight at 4°C, followed by another 2 hours at 23°C, before performing Ni-NTA. The unbound fraction contained some cleaved protein, while the fraction eluted with 30 mM imidazole contained most of the cleaved protein. Final purification was done with gel filtration chromatography on Superdex 75 with a final buffer of 30 mM tris-HCl and 50 mM NaCl (pH 8.0). The purity was checked with SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE).

Infiltration assay

The National Institute of Biology, Ljubljana, Slovenia, provided the tobacco plants (N. tabacum “White Burley”) for purchase. We infiltrated the protein solution (50 μl in ultrapure water) using blunt-ended syringe pressure infiltration. The area on the upper side of the leaf was labeled and checked for the presence of necrosis after 72 hours. The level of leaf necrosis was estimated using Fiji software (56) by calculating the ratio of the necrotic area to the whole infiltrated area, both measured in squared pixels.

Extraction of GIPCs

We extracted and purified GIPCs as previously described (9, 10, 28). Briefly, tobacco (N. tabacum White Burley) leaves or whole leek plants (A. porrum) were blended with cold 0.1-N aqueous acetic acid and filtered through miracloth. The pellet fraction containing GIPCs was extracted with hot 70% ethanol/0.1 N of HCl and filtered through miracloth. Soluble fraction was transferred immediately to −20°C overnight, and precipitated lipids were then washed with cold acetone and diethyl ether to yield a whitish precipitate. The precipitate was dried completely under the stream of N2, dissolved in tetrahydrofuran:methanol:water (4:4:1, v/v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid and dried again. Lipid film was redissolved in butan-1-ol:water (1:1, v/v) to undergo phase partition. The upper, GIPC-containing butanol phase was dried completely and dissolved in tetrahydrofuran:methanol:water (4:4:1, v/v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid. After freezing at −20°C, the extract was filtered through 22-μm polytetrafluoroethylene filter.

Liposome sedimentation assay

We prepared MLVs from POPC, GIPCs, and, in certain cases, equimolar plant sterol mixture of stigmasterol, β-sitosterol, and campesterol. The molar ratios and buffers varied and are described separately for each experiment. The lipids were dried in vacuum to yield a thin film. Glass beads were added to the rotation flask, and the film was resuspended in the appropriate buffer by vortexing vigorously. The suspension of lipids was transferred into an Eppendorf tube and incubated at 42°C for 30 min, with shortly vortexing every 10 min.

We performed liposome sedimentation assays with incubating 2.6 μM NLPPya or MpNEP2 with MLVs (5 mM final concentration) on a rotating platform (600 rotations/min) for 30 min at room temperature. The buffer used was 20 mM MES (pH 5.8), with either 20 mM NaCl (Nt-GIPCs and Ap-GIPCs) or 50 mM NaCl (Nt1-GIPCs to Nt4-GIPCs and ApB-GIPCs). The mixture of protein and MLVs was centrifuged 21,000g for 30 min at 23°C. MLVs were washed twice with thorough resuspension of the pellet in the same buffer and centrifugation at the same conditions for either 20 min (Nt-GIPCs and Ap-GIPCs) or 30 min (Nt1-GIPCs to Nt4-GIPCs and ApB-GIPCs). The samples were then run on SDS-PAGE, followed by ProBlue Safe Stain (Giotto Biotech, Italy) staining according to the manufacturers’ protocol. For densiometric quantification of the protein bands, we used Fiji (56) for GIPC extract experiments or iBright analysis software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for GIPC fraction–related experiments. The percentage of binding was determined by dividing peak area of the pellet, which contained the bound protein, to the summed areas of the pellet and the supernatant, which contained unbound protein. Three independent measurements were used for the statistical analysis.

Ion current measurements

We performed the planar lipid bilayer experiments on Nanion Technologies Orbit 16 TC platform with Ionera 100-μm MECA recording chips, which enable the automated formation of 16 lipid bilayers and their simultaneous recording. We collected the data with the Elements Data Reader 4 (EDR4; Elements) recording software at a working range of 2 nA, a sampling frequency of 20 kHz, and a constant temperature of 22°C. DPhPC and GIPCs were dissolved to 5 mg/ml at a ratio of 6:4 (n:n) in ultrapure octane (analytical standard; Sigma-Aldrich, USA.) The chip was filled with 200 μl of recording buffer [20 mM MES and 100 mM NaCl (pH 5.8)], and the provided wetting tool was used to force the solution into 16 circular microcavities with microelectrode on the bottom. A voltage of +30 mV was applied, which triggered all the channels that were unobstructed and successfully filled to go to overload. The magnetic stir bar was added to the chip, and 0.2 μl of lipid solution was applied directly to the surface of the chip at two locations, both of which were in the stir bar trajectory toward the cavities. By rotating the stir bar several times at a medium speed, the lipids were spread over the edges of each cavity, covering them with the lipid membrane, which caused the currents to permanently drop back to 0 pA. In certain instance, more lipids in 0.2-μl steps had to be applied to achieve this. The successful generation of membranes was verified by using the resistance capacitance (RC) estimation function in EDR4 to determine the capacitances of the membranes. Only membranes with a capacitance of >10 pF were used in further experiments. A lower capacitance indicated an incompletely thinned and therefore nonfunctional bilayer, and these channels were switched off. The voltage was switched to −100 mV, and the membranes were recorded for 10 min without toxins to assess their stability, indicated by no electric current flowing through them. The NLPPya and MpNEP2 were added to the chip at a final concentration of 5 and 20 μM, respectively. The recording was 30 min long, followed by a short and strong electrical pulse to impair the stability of the toxin-unaffected membranes. To further ensure the quality of the GIPC-containing bilayers, preformed pores of lysenin (mutant RL1) (40) were added and recorded at +50 mV and a final concentration of 40 pM. Data were analyzed using Axon pCLAMP 11 software (Molecular Devices) and filtered using a Butterworth (eight-pole) low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 1000 Hz.

GUV preparation

We prepared GUVs used in the microfluidic experiments with a modified electroformation method, as described in (10, 41). Concentrations of lipid mixture spread over electrodes for electroformation (GIPCs:POPC:sterols, 2:1:1, n:n:n) was 1 mM. The concentration of A594 used for encapsulation was 8 μM, dissolved in sucrose (207 milliosmol/liter).

Labeling of NLPPya and MpNEP2 with A488

We labeled the proteins with A488 NHS ester according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The reaction buffer for labeling needs to be slightly basic, so for 2 ml of NLPPya (3.2 mg/ml), we added 0.5 ml of 1 M sodium bicarbonate. We dialyzed MpNEP2 to phosphate-buffered saline [10 mM Na2HPO4, 1.8 mM KH2PO4, 2.7 mM KCl (pH 7.4), and 140 mM NaCl] and concentrated to 3 mg/ml. A488 NHS ester was freshly diluted in dimethyl sulfoxide at a concentration of 15.5 mM and immediately added to the protein by drops while stirring to avoid the protein denaturation. The ratio of the protein to A488 was 3:1 (n:n). After 1-hour incubation at 22°C in the dark with gentle stirring, the free probe was separated from the labeled protein with PD-10 desalting column packed with Sephadex G-25 resin (Cytiva, USA). The degree of labeling was calculated by measuring absorbance at 280- and 495-nm wavelengths. Molar absorption concentration of 48,025 M−1 cm−1 for NLPPya, 52,940 M−1 cm−1 for MpNEP2, and 71,000 M−1 cm−1 for A488 and a correction factor of 0.11 for A488 were used. Degree of labeling was ~0.7 for NLPPya and ~1.2 for MpNEP2. The quality of the proteins after labeling was inspected by SDS-PAGE. MpNEP2-A488 was dialyzed to 1.2 mM Hepes (pH 7.4) and 14 mM NaCl, and NLPPya-A488 was dialyzed to 2 mM MES (pH 5.8) and 15 mM NaCl.

Experiments with GUVs in microfluidics

We studied the interaction of NLPPya labeled with A488 with individual GUVs in real time in a microfluidic system, as performed in (10, 41). The microchannel in a microfluidic chip is a 100-μm-wide and 40-μm-deep trough. The flow can be hydraulically regulated by the height adjustment of the exit reservoir, and the solution that flows through the system can be changed by introducing a fresh drop at the entrance reservoir (fig. S4). The diffusion chamber (100 μm by 250 μm by 40 μm) is a dead-end channel extending sideways from the main channel (fig. S4). The chamber is essentially flow free, and, therefore, the exchange of solutes between the main channel and the experimental chamber only occurs via passive diffusion (57). This enables gentle and gradual introduction of the protein to the vesicles.

The microfluidic system was placed on an inverted microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti, Japan) equipped with confocal microscopy unit (Confocal.nl, RCM1, The Netherlands), optical tweezers (Tweez, Aresis, Slovenia; laser wavelength of 1064 nm), and a 60× water immersion objective (near-infrared apochromat; numerical aperture, 1.0). A scientific complementary metal-oxide semiconductor camera (Zyla 5.5, Andor, UK) was used to record the experiments.

After preparing the fresh microfluidic chip for each experiment, we flushed the channels with a glucose solution, which was iso-osmolar to the solution used for electroformation of GUVs. After, we introduced the suspension of GUVs into the main channel, and the flow rate was set to ~20 μl/s. GUVs without visible membrane protrusions and deformations were selected from the main channel and individually transferred by optical tweezers into the diffusion chamber. Then, the system was flushed with an iso-osmolar glucose solution (203 milliosmol/liter) without a fluorescent marker, so that the fluorescent signals originated only from A594 trapped inside GUVs. This step also served to check the stability of the vesicles: The vesicles that changed in any way during the flushing with an iso-osmolar glucose solution were removed from the chamber. Last, an iso-osmolar solution of glucose with 1.5 μM NLPPya-A488 or 12 μM MpNEP2-A488 was introduced into the main channel, which led to diffusion toward GUVs in the diffusion chamber. The experiments were recorded by RCM1 confocal microscopy using Micro-Manager (58) in 3-s time lapses in green (for NLPPya-A488 and MpNEP2-A488) and red (for A594) fluorescence channels.

The images recorded were analyzed with Fiji (56). The intensities of three fluorescent signals were measured for each individual GUV: A594 in the vesicle interior, NLPPya-A488 or MpNEP2-A488 in the solution directly surrounding GUVs, and NLPPya-A488 or MpNEP2-A488 on the GUV membrane. All fluorescent signals were corrected for interchannel cross-talk and fluorescent marker bleaching. The NLPPya-A488 and MpNEP2-A488 signal on the membrane was determined as the difference between the average intensity on the membrane subtracted for the intensity in the surrounding solution. The A594 signal in the vesicle was calculated as the average A594 intensity in the vesicle interior. To quantify the dynamics of NLPPya and MpNEP2 binding and membrane permeabilization for each vesicle, the signals of the protein on the membrane and A594 inside the vesicles were fitted by a logistic sigmoidal curve by a custom-made Mathematica code 52. τbind was determined from the fitted curve as the time needed to reach from 10 to 90% of the final protein signal on the membrane (green). τrelease was determined from the fitted curve as the time needed to reach from 90 to 10% of the initial A594 signal (red) inside the vesicles (fig. S4C). For negative controls, GIPC-containing GUVs with the addition of buffer:glucose mixture were used instead of a protein solution (C1 in Fig. 2 and figs. S6, C and D, and S7, E and F), or A488-labeled proteins were added to GUVs without GIPC (POPC:sterols, 3:1, n:n) (C2 in Fig. 2 and figs. S6, A and B, and S7, E and F).

HPTLC analysis and isolation of GIPC fractions

We used HPTLC silica gel 60 plates for tobacco GIPC fractionation. The plates were first predeveloped with methanol:formic acid (10:1, v/v) and subsequently with methanol (both steps are important for TLC-MS analysis) (59). After each step, the plates were dried using a hairdryer. The double predeveloped plates were then placed in an oven at 110°C for 30 min.

The volume of the applied extracts was 100 μl for HPTLC analysis and 200 μl for HPTLC-MS, both as 8-mm bands, while for isolation of GIPC fractions, we applied 2250 μl as 180-mm band (Linomat 5, VisionCATS software, version 3.1, Camag, Switzerland, and Hamilton syringe 100 or 500 μl, Hamilton, Switzerland). The developing solvent was composed of chloroform:methanol:4 N of ammonium hydroxide (aqueous) (9:7:2, v/v/v) (37). The plates were developed up to 9 cm in twin trough chambers for 20 cm–by–10 cm plates with 10 ml of the developing solvent added to one trough of the chamber. Alternatively, automatic developing chamber (ADC2, Camag, Switzerland) that enabled humidity control (15 min before plate development using 12 M potassium carbonate solution; 48% relative humidity) was used for plate development. In this case, the plates were developed up to the maximal migration distance enabled by the ADC2 (8.5 cm) and dried for 5 min. For plate derivatization, primulin dissolved in 80% acetone (aqueous) (5%, w/v) was used (37). For GIPC visualization, entire plates were immersed in primulin solution using the TLC immersion device III (Camag, Switzerland), while for isolation purposes, only 2.5-cm-wide areas on both the left and right edges of the plate were derivatized. Plate documentation was performed at 254 and 366 nm using the Camag TLC Visualizer (Camag, Switzerland).

According to MS analysis, Nt-GIPC separation resulted in four chromatographic zones (fractions) of interest named Nt1-GIPCs to Nt4-GIPCs. Separation of Ap-GIPCs resulted in several chromatographic zones that contained different glycolipids, and ApB was shown to contain a homogenous GIPC series B. Nt1-GIPCs to Nt4-GIPCs or ApB-GIPCs were labeled by a pencil under 366 nm (DigiStore 2 Documentation System, Camag, Switzerland) and scratched off of the underivatized part of the plate using a spatula, and the removed adsorbent was suspended in 25 ml of tetrahydrofuran:methanol:water (4:4:1, v/v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid, vortexed, and filtered through 0.2-μm hydrophilic polytetrafluoroethylene filter (φ = 25 mm; Macherey-Nagel, Germany) mounted to a glass gas tight syringe (Fortuna Optima, Poulten & Graf GmbH, Germany). The extraction of compounds from the sorbent was repeated once more, and the filtrates were combined. The isolates were then dried under a stream of nitrogen flow, and the solid residues were dissolved twice in 500 μl of tetrahydrofuran:methanol:water (4:4:1, v/v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid before being quantitatively transferred to preweighted gas chromatography glass vials. The solvent was again evaporated to calculate the mass of the final isolates and then redissolved in tetrahydrofuran:methanol:water (4:4:1, v/v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid. Masses of isolated lipids exceeded the mass of the starting material, which we can explain by the presence of trace silica and other potential impurities derived from the HPTLC plate. GIPC content in isolated fractions was therefore estimated on the basis of primuline staining by the comparison of GIPC chromatographic zone intensities with chromatographic zone intensities of known quantities of POPC. For semiquantitative estimation of the compounds in bands Nt1-GIPCs to Nt4-GIPCs relative to the total GIPC content, three independent Nt-GIPCs were separated on the same HPTLC plate, captured at 254 nm after primuline staining, and their fluorescence was analyzed by the VideoScan software (version 1.02.00, Camag).

HPTLC–(−)electrospray ionization–MS of isolated fractions

For HPTLC–(−)electrospray ionization–MS (Accela 1250 coupled to LTQ Velos, Thermo Xcalibur software, version 2.1.0, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), the elution of the chromatographic zones of interest was done using the TLC-MS interface (Camag, Switzerland), with the same solvent used during the isolation [tetrahydrofuran:methanol:water (4:4:1, v/v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid], but with methanol of MS grade. The flow rate was 0.3 ml/min. The MS tune parameters, optimized for GlcN (Thermo Tune Plus software, version 2.6.0, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were as follows: heater and capillary temperature of 300° and 250°C, respectively; sheath, auxiliary, and sweep gas flow rate 46, 40, and 0 arbitrary units (a.u.), respectively; spray voltage of 3 kV; and S-Lens radio frequency level of 69%. Collision energy used for tandem MS experiments was 30 to 40%.

Lipid blotting and immunodetection

For dot blot immunoassay, we blotted ~16 μg of GIPCs, 7 μg of POPC, or 3.75 μg of sterols onto a TLC silica gel 60 F254 plate and dried under a stream of nitrogen. We prepared lipid mixtures before blotting and contained summed quantities of lipids. For TLC blotting, TLC silica gel 60 F254 plate was heated for 30 min at 120°C and held under a stream of nitrogen for 10 min. Approximately 200 and 70 μg of lipids were manually applied to the plate for TLC blotting and primulin staining, respectively, and separated in a saturated twin trough chamber. As developing solvent, chloroform:methanol:4 N of ammonium hydroxide (aqueous) (9:7:2, v/v/v) was used (37). When the solvent front almost reached the top of the plate, the plate was taken out and dried completely under a stream of nitrogen. TLC plate with smaller lipid quantities was dipped in 1% primuline in acetone:water (4:1, v/v) and dried under a stream of nitrogen, and the obtained chromatograms were visualized under ultraviolet light.

For transfer of lipids, a piece of PVDF membrane was dipped in a blotting solvent (isopropanol:0.2 M CaCl2:methanol, 40:20:7, v/v/v) for 1 min, placed over the developed TLC plate, and covered by a glass fiber filter paper. The assembly was heated for 1 min, and the PVDF membrane containing the transferred lipids was dried under the stream of nitrogen.

PVDF membrane was blocked overnight at 4°C in blocking buffer [20 mM tris, 50 mM NaCl, (pH 7.4), and 4% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA)]. Then, NLPPya (10 μg/ml) was incubated in blocking buffer for 2 hours at room temperature. Unbound protein was washed with washing buffer [20 mM tris and 50 mM NaCl (pH 7.4)] at room temperature, incubated for 1 hour with the primary anti-4His antibody (diluted 1:3000 in blocking buffer) at room temperature and washed again with washing buffer. For the dot blot, both washing steps consisted of three 10-min washes, while for TLC blotting, both washes lasted for 15 min. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated with secondary anti-mouse antibody tagged with horseradish peroxidase (diluted 1:40,000 for TLC blotting and 1:50,000 for the dot blot, both in the blocking buffer). Unbound secondary antibodies were washed three times with the washing buffer, and bound protein was detected using a Pierce ECL Western blotting substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

SUV preparation

To yield SUVs, we sonicated 0.1 mM MLVs [20 mM MES and 50 mM NaCl (pH 5.8)] on ice for a total of 20 min (10-s on and 10-s off) at 38% amplitude using Vibracell Ultrasonic Disintegrator VCX 750 (Sonics and Materials, Newtown, CT, USA). After sonication, we centrifuged the SUVs for 10 min at 16,100g and estimated their diameter with dynamic light scattering, using Zetasizer HT (Malvern Panalytical, UK). The molar ratio of POPC:GIPCs:sterols was 3:4:3 (n:n:n). GIPC-free vesicles were used as a negative control (POPC:sterols, 7:3, n:n).

Surface plasmon resonance

Real-time binding of NLPPya to GIPC-containing SUVs was monitored on Biacore X100 (Cytiva, USA), using sensor chip L1 (44, 60). For SUVs capturing, 20 mM MES and 50 mM NaCl (pH 5.8) were used as running buffer. After priming the system in the running buffer, the chip was regenerated with three 1-min injections of isopropanol:50 mM NaOH (2:3, v/v). Control SUVs (POPC:sterols, 7:3, n:n) were injected over the first flow cell, until the immobilization level reached about 2000 to 2200 response units (RU). The same was repeated for GIPC-containing SUVs, which were injected over the second flow cell. Loading of the vesicles was done at 5 μl/min. After immobilization, running buffer was changed for 20 mM MES, 50 mM NaCl (pH 5.8), and 0.5% BSA (w/v). When the baseline stabilized, increasing concentrations of NLPPya diluted in running buffer containing BSA were injected for 20 s, followed by a 3-min dissociation time. Because of fast protein dissociation, no regeneration was needed. The flow rate was 60 μl/min. To determine kinetic parameters, we used Origin 2018. For dissociation rate constants, we fitted dissociation regions of sensorgrams of 4 μM protein injection with the following equationRt=R1e−tkd1+R2e−tkd2(1)where Rt represent response at time t during the dissociation phase, R1 and R2 represent signal levels at the end of the association phase for interactions 1 and 2, t represents time during the dissociation phase, and kd1 and kd2 represent dissociation rate constants for interactions 1 and 2. For equilibrium dissociation constants, we created steady-state affinity plots and fit the data with the following equationReq=Rmax1[A]KD1+[A]+Rmax2[A]KD2+[A](2)where Req represents response at equilibrium for a given analyte concentration, Rmax1 and Rmax2 represent maximal binding responses for interactions 1 and 2, [A] represents analyte concentration, and KD1 and KD2 represent the dissociation equilibrium constants for interactions 1 and 2. Sensorgrams that did not reach steady state, were excluded from the analysis (Fig. 3).

Molecular modeling

To model how membrane adsorption of NLPs depends on GIPC type, we first constructed a GIPC:NLPPya complex for each GIPC type using molecular docking. NLPPya was modeled on the basis of the experimental structure determined by x-ray diffraction [Protein Data Bank (PDB) ID 3GNZ] that contained a Mg2+ ion in the sugar binding cavity (8). Missing loop residues were inserted with MODELLER (61). Determination of the protonation states of the protein residues and preparation of the protein for docking was done with Maestro software from the Schrodinger Suite (Schrödinger LLC, New York, NY, 2021). Polar headgroups for each studied GIPC type were then built with the same software. Sugar linkages of the GIPC A headgroup were chosen on the basis of the available experimental data (49). For GIPC B, we assumed that the first branch is positioned the same as in GIPC A, while the second branch resides on the inositol position 6, similar to corn GIPC B (45). Headgroup structure of GIPC C was also hypothesized on the basis of matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization–time-of-flight data. Next, LigPrep tool was used to generate the different possible ionization and stereoisomer states. Last, Glide was used for molecular docking using the extra precision protocol (62). To achieve the optimal docking pose, the polar headgroups were allowed to sample different conformations (i.e., flexible docking), and van der Waals radii were scaled by 0.5. Threshold for ring retention was set at 2 kcal/mol, and cutoff for pose retention was set at 200 kcal/mol. A constraint was imposed requiring that the carboxyl group from the GlcA, present in all investigated GIPCs, is coordinated to the Mg2+ ion with at least one oxygen atom.

Next, the best-scoring docked poses were used to perform GIPC membrane MD simulations. In each case, the system was prepared as follows. First, appropriate phosphoceramide chains were added to the sugar headgroups to construct a full GIPC molecule. Next, the simulation box was constructed with the packmol program (63). The molecular bilayer was constructed from 1 GIPC:NLPPya complex, 154 POPC lipid molecules, and 154 GIPC molecules. Last, water molecules and Na+ ions were added to solvate the system and reach a neutral charge, respectively. Topology was generated with the tLEaP tool from the AmberTools22 package (64). The protein and POPC lipids were described with the ff14SB and Lipid21 Amber force fields, respectively (65, 66). Transferable intermolecular potential with 3 points (TIP3P) model was used for water. Joung-Cheatham and Li-Merz parameters were used for Na+ and Mg2+ ions, respectively (67, 68). Sugar headgroups of GIPC molecules were described with the GLYCAM force field (69), while parameters for the inositol phosphoceramide (IPC) part were derived inhouse. Briefly, to obtain partial atom charges, Gaussian 16 (70) was used for geometry optimization of the IPC molecule with the Hartree-Fock method (71) and 6-31G* basis set (72), followed by Merz-Kollman restrained electrostatic potential calculations (73). Antechamber tool was then used to convert Gaussian 16 output files into tLEaP readable format and determine atom types according to the general amber force field (GAFF2) for organic molecules (74).

Classical MD simulations were run with the GROMACS software package (version 2022.3) (75). Energy minimization was performed with the steepest descent method until the maximum force in the system was below 1000 kJ/(mol nm). Then, simulated systems were slowly annealed from 0 to 300 K over 1 ns in the isothermal-isobaric (NPT) ensemble, while keeping the pressure at 1 bar. After reaching 300 K, the equilibration was continued for 9 ns. Newton’s equations of motion were integrated using the leap-frog algorithm and a 2-fs time step. Electrostatic interactions were calculated using the particle mesh Ewald method (76). Temperature was controlled with the V-rescale thermostat (τt = 0.1 ps) (77), while the Parrinello-Rahman barostat (78) was used for maintaining pressure (τp = 5 ps). During equilibration, position restraints were imposed on protein heavy atoms and on the z component of the position of phosphorous atoms in the lipid bilayer. After equilibration, restraints were removed, and trajectories were collected for 500 ns. All simulations were performed in triplicates.

Hydrogen bond and van der Waals contact formation was calculated with gmx hbond and gmx mindist tools from the GROMACS software package, respectively. Similarly, root mean square fluctuation was calculated with the gmx rmsf tool. Cavity size was determined with the MDpocket method (79). Interactions between NLPPya and GIPC were analyzed with the ProLIF Python package (80). Binding free energies between NLPPya and docked GIPCs were estimated with the MM-PBSA method (81) using the MMPBSA.py program (82) from the AmberTools22 package. Calculations were carried out over 625 frames, extracted at regular intervals from the MD trajectories. To mimic the membrane environment, we used the heterogeneous dielectric implicit membrane model (83). Salt concentration was 0.15 M. Average binding free energies and SDs were calculated over three replicas.

Statistical analysis

We statistically evaluated the results with two-sample t test for means in OriginLab (version 2018, OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA). For results of experiments with planar lipid bilayers (Fig. 1 and fig. S3) or GUVs (Fig. 2 and fig. S7), membranes with Nt-GIPCs or Ap-GIPCs were compared and tested for null hypothesis. In sedimentation assay (fig. S2), MLVs containing Nt-GIPCs or Ap-GIPCs in the presence and absence of plant sterols were compared and tested for the null hypothesis. For sedimentation assay with different GIPC fractions (Fig. 4), we tested the null hypothesis for each GIPC-containing MLVs compared to its non-GIPC counterpart, as well as for each GIPC-containing sterol and sterol-free pair. For all analyses, we set the significance level to 0.05, and equal variance was assumed.
