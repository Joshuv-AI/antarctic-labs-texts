Readability of patient information leaflets for commonly used medicines in the UK, Germany and Türkiye: a cross-sectional document analysis
Authors: Funda Örge Yaşar, Okan Yaşar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth literacy is a key determinant of individuals’ ability to use medications safely and correctly. The readability of patient information leaflets (PILs) plays a critical role in patient safety and treatment adherence. Inadequate readability may limit patients’ understanding of medication instructions and increase the risk of misuse. This study aimed to comparatively examine the readability levels of patient information leaflets for medications commonly used in the United Kingdom, Germany, and Türkiye.MethodsA cross-sectional design was used in the study. The research was conducted using a document analysis approach. Patient information leaflets for 20 medications with identical names, dosages, and pharmaceutical forms in the United Kingdom, Germany, and Türkiye were analyzed. The sections “What is X and what is it used for?”, “What you need to know before using X”, “How to use X”, “Possible side effects”, and “How to store X” were examined separately. Readability was assessed using the Flesch Reading Ease formula for English texts, the Amstad formula for German texts, and the Ateşman formula for Turkish texts.ResultsMean readability scores corresponded predominantly to the “difficult” or “very difficult” readability categories in four of the five analyzed sections in Germany and Türkiye and in three sections in the United Kingdom. Texts under the headings “What you need to know before using X” and “Possible side effects” were most frequently classified as “difficult” or “very difficult,” largely due to long sentence structures and dense technical terminology. Although variations in readability scores were observed between countries, the overall findings suggest that the extensive use of long sentence structures and technical terminology may limit the comprehensibility of the leaflets for the general public.ConclusionDespite existing regulatory requirements, patient information leaflets prepared in different linguistic and healthcare system contexts are not sufficiently accessible to the general public. Simplifying leaflet content and restructuring it in a more user-centered manner—both linguistically and structurally—is essential to promote safe medication use and protect public health. These findings may guide regulatory authorities and pharmaceutical companies in improving policies and practices related to leaflet readability.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14822-6.Keywords: Health literacy, Patient information leaflets, Readability, Medication safety, Public health

Introduction

In health communication, individuals’ literacy levels are regarded as a determining factor in the effective implementation of information processes, the assurance of patient safety, and the enhancement of treatment adherence [1]. Health literacy is a multidimensional concept encompassing individuals’ abilities to access, understand, evaluate, and apply health-related information in their daily lives [2]. Today, health literacy is recognized as a critical public health component in the prevention and effective management of non-communicable diseases, not only in high-income countries but also in low- and middle-income countries [3]. In this context, health literacy enables individuals to make informed decisions regarding their own health and simultaneously has a direct impact on the effectiveness and sustainability of health systems [4].

Individuals with higher levels of health literacy are better able to accurately interpret health information and to maintain medication use in a safe manner [5, 6]. In contrast, in many countries, a substantial proportion of the population is reported to have limited health literacy. In the United States, approximately 90 million adults are reported to experience difficulties in understanding and applying health-related information [7], and a similar situation is observed in European countries. It has been reported that approximately 62% of the adult population in Belgium [8], 60% in Canada [9], 44% in Germany [10], and 43% in England [11] have limited levels of health literacy. Low health literacy is associated with the misinterpretation of medical information, non-adherence to treatment instructions, and serious health problems, particularly those resulting from the incorrect use of medications [12]. Indeed, it is reported that approximately 237 million medication errors occur annually in England; a substantial proportion of these errors are preventable and result in serious clinical and economic consequences for the National Health Service (NHS) [13]. These findings clearly demonstrate the critical importance of preparing written health materials provided to patients in accordance with the population’s literacy levels, in terms of patient safety and public health.

Patient information leaflets (package inserts) are official technical documents included within medication packaging that provide written information on essential aspects of the medicinal product [14, 15]. The primary purpose of these leaflets is to support patients in using medicines correctly, safely, and consciously, aiming to convey information that is critical for treatment safety—such as indications, instructions for use and dosage, contraindications, warnings and precautions, potential drug interactions, and adverse effects—to patients in a clear and understandable manner [16, 17]. In this respect, package inserts are not merely informative texts but also fundamental patient education tools that support patients’ active participation in the treatment process [18, 19]. Recent studies indicate that clear and user-friendly patient information materials can positively influence patients’ attitudes toward medications and their levels of treatment adherence [20–22].

Directive 2001/83/EC of the European Union defines it as a legal requirement that package inserts be prepared in plain, comprehensible, and accessible language, enabling users to act appropriately when necessary, with the support of healthcare professionals [14]. Accordingly, package inserts should be structured in a manner that allows individuals from different age groups, educational backgrounds, and levels of health literacy to use them with a shared understanding. However, package inserts that are linguistically complex, contain long sentence structures, and include dense technical terminology may create substantial barriers to patients’ access to and comprehension of information. In contrast, the clear presentation of information regarding a medicine’s description, method of use, potential adverse effects, and storage conditions supports the safe and rational use of medicines and enhances treatment adherence [25].

A substantial body of literature indicates that medication package inserts largely exhibit linguistic and structural characteristics that exceed the average literacy level of the general population [26–31]. Quantitative analyses conducted in Ireland and the United Kingdom have shown that long and complex sentence structures, along with dense technical terminology, significantly reduce the readability of package inserts [32]. In a study conducted in Türkiye, it was determined that individuals would need an average of 13–15 years of education to be able to understand the package inserts examined [33]. Similar findings have also been reported in studies carried out in Saudi Arabia [34]. Nevertheless, comparative studies examining the readability of patient information leaflets across different countries and languages remain relatively scarce, with most existing research focusing on a single national or linguistic context [32, 34–36].

The United Kingdom, Germany, and Türkiye, despite belonging to different language families and healthcare system structures, provide patient information leaflets for medicinal products under broadly comparable regulatory frameworks. In the European Union, including Germany, patient information leaflets are governed by Directive 2001/83/EC and related European Medicines Agency (EMA) guidelines, which require that information be clear, understandable, and tested for readability [15, 16]. Following Brexit, the United Kingdom continues to apply similar standards through the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA), largely aligned with former EU legislation [37]. In Türkiye, patient information leaflets are regulated by the Turkish Medicines and Medical Devices Agency (TİTCK) in accordance with national legislation that is harmonized with EU acquis, including requirements for clarity, comprehensibility, and user testing [38]. This shared regulatory emphasis provides a meaningful basis for cross-country comparison of readability.

The aim of this study is to examine the readability levels of patient information leaflets for 20 medications commonly used in the United Kingdom, Germany, and Türkiye through document analysis and to comparatively evaluate whether the package inserts are user-friendly across different country and language contexts. Accordingly, the package inserts were analyzed using the Flesch readability formula for English texts, the Amstad readability formula for German texts, and the Ateşman readability formula for Turkish texts. This study seeks to determine the scores and readability levels of the following sections in selected medication package inserts from the UK, Germany and Türkiye according to the Flesch, Amstad and Ateşman readability formulas:

Method

Research design

This study was designed as a cross-sectional study aiming to examine the readability levels of patient information leaflets for medications commonly used in the United Kingdom, Germany, and Türkiye. Document analysis was employed in the study. Document analysis is defined as a structured research process based on the systematic examination and evaluation of printed or electronic materials [39]. Within the scope of this study, medication package inserts were systematically analyzed in accordance with predefined content headings [40]. The readability levels of the leaflets were quantitatively evaluated using the Flesch readability formula for English texts, the Amstad readability formula for German texts, and the Ateşman readability formula for Turkish texts.

Study material

The study material consists of a total of 60 patient information leaflets belonging to 20 medications selected from those available under the same brand name, pharmaceutical form, and dosage in the United Kingdom, Germany, and Türkiye. The medications were identified to include those commonly used prescription and over-the-counter drugs in the respective countries. Medications meeting the inclusion criteria were identified consecutively, and the first 20 eligible medications were included in the study to ensure cross-country comparability. The medications examined, listed alphabetically, are as follows: Abilify 10 mg tablet, Buscopan 10 mg coated tablet, Canesten 1% cream, Eliquis 5 mg film-coated tablet, Humalog KwikPen 100 U/ml injection pen, Lantus 100 U/ml solution for injection, Lipitor 20 mg film-coated tablet, Neurontin 600 mg film-coated tablet, NovoRapid FlexPen 100 U/ml injection pen, Nurofen 200 mg tablet, Otrivine 1 mg nasal spray, Ozempic 0.25 mg injection pen, Seroquel 25 mg film-coated tablet, Singulair 10 mg film-coated tablet, Ventolin 100 mcg inhalation suspension, Viagra 100 mg film-coated tablet, Xalatan 50 µg/ml eye drops, Zovirax 5% cream, Zyprexa 10 mg film-coated tablet, and Zyrtec 10 mg film-coated tablet. The package insert texts were accessed through publicly available online sources representing different types of information providers. For Germany, data were obtained from Apotheken Umschau, a widely used patient-oriented health information platform. For the United Kingdom, texts were retrieved from the Electronic Medicines Compendium (eMC), a centralized repository of medicines information aligned with regulatory approvals. For Türkiye, data were obtained from the Turkish Medicines and Medical Devices Agency (TİTCK), the national regulatory authority responsible for the authorization and dissemination of official medicinal product information. Detailed information on the package inserts included in the study is provided in the supplementary file.

Data collection instruments

In the study, readability levels were assessed using the Flesch Reading Ease formula developed for English texts, the Amstad Readability Formula adapted for German, and the Ateşman Readability Formula adapted for Turkish. These formulas are widely used readability assessment tools designed to evaluate the linguistic complexity of texts based on quantitative indicators such as word and sentence length. The Flesch Reading Ease formula was originally developed by Rudolf Flesch in 1948 and later adapted into German by Toni Amstad in 1978 and into Turkish by Ender Ateşman in 1997. In all three formulas, wl represents the average number of syllables per word, whereas sl represents the average number of words per sentence. Readability scores were calculated according to the respective formulas and used for subsequent analyses [41–44].

Among the most commonly used readability measures in healthcare research are the Flesch Reading Ease [32, 35, 45–48], Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level [32, 35, 45–49], Fry Readability Graph [32, 46, 47], Simple Measure of Gobbledygook (SMOG) [32, 35, 45–51], Gunning Fog Index [32, 35, 46–48], and Dale–Chall [32, 47, 48] Readability Formula [48, 52]. However, as this study covers patient information leaflets for 20 medications presented under the same brand name, formulation, and dosage in the United Kingdom, Germany, and Türkiye, it necessitated the use of formulas that are accepted as valid and directly adapted for each language. Therefore, the Flesch (1948) readability formula was used for English texts, the Amstad (1978) readability formula for German texts, and the Ateşman (1997) readability formula for Turkish texts.

Although the Ateşman formula was not specifically developed for health texts, it has been widely used in the evaluation of patient information materials and informed consent forms. Previous studies have shown that the formula has been effectively applied to informational materials related to various clinical conditions, including breast cancer [53], acute rheumatic fever [54], amblyopia [55], laryngeal cancer [56], congenital hearing loss [57], female pelvic floor disorders [58], diabetes [59], hypothyroidism [60], and varicose veins [61]. Similarly, the readability of patient information texts used in the fields of emergency surgery [62], urological and general surgery [63], cardiovascular surgery [64], and oral and maxillofacial surgery [65] has been evaluated using the Ateşman formula. In addition, the readability of online materials related to oral and dental health—including toothache [66], bruxism [67], restorative dentistry [68], dental abscesses [69], and periodontal diseases [70]—has also been determined using the same formula. Likewise, the readability of digital informational content addressing various health conditions such as vertigo [71], rhinoplasty [72], strabismus [73], voice disorders [74], carpal tunnel syndrome [75], and fibromyalgia [76] has been analyzed using this formula. Furthermore, within the scope of written health documents related to diagnostic, interventional, and surgical procedures, the readability levels of informed consent forms [77], as well as consent forms for intravenous and intramuscular injections [78] and radiological procedures [79], have also been evaluated using the Ateşman formula. The findings of these studies indicate that the Ateşman readability formula is a valid, consistent, and functional evaluation tool for different types of content and presentation formats in the field of health.

The Amstad Readability Formula is also observed to have a wide range of applications in the healthcare field. In particular, studies conducted in the areas of urology [80, 81], trauma and accident surgery [82], online health information [83], psychiatry [84], midwifery and the childbirth process [85], and obstetrics and gynecology [86] demonstrate that the Amstad formula is a valid and reliable tool for evaluating the readability of medical content and patient education materials.

Data collection

The data collection process consisted of interrelated and systematic stages. In the first stage, the criteria for counting sentences, words, and syllables in the package leaflet texts were determined. In this context, structures ending with a period (.), ellipsis (…), question mark (?), or exclamation mark (!) were considered as sentences. All expressions separated by spaces were treated as words, while numerical expressions and symbols were calculated based on their written equivalents. Syllable counts were conducted by taking into account the phonetic structure of the relevant language. In the final stage, the numbers of sentences, words, and syllables were determined separately for the sections titled “What X is and what it is used for,” “What you need to know before you take X,” “How to take X,” “Possible side effects,” and “How to store X” in the package leaflets.

Data analysis

To ensure the internal validity of the study, the analysis process was structured in a clear and systematic manner. To enhance the reliability of the research, sentence, word, and syllable counts were performed independently by the researchers. The first count was conducted simultaneously, and the second count was repeated after a certain period of time. One of the researchers is bilingual in German and Turkish, and both researchers have advanced proficiency in English. The data obtained by the same researcher at different times were compared, and a high level of consistency was observed between the counts. The limited discrepancies identified were reviewed jointly and resolved through consensus.

To ensure the accurate transfer of the data into the formulas and to verify the calculations, the computation process was also carried out independently by both researchers, and the results were cross-checked. These procedures were considered as measures supporting the reproducibility of the measurements and the overall reliability of the study.

Discussion, conclusion, and recommendations

In this study, the readability levels of a total of 60 patient information leaflets belonging to 20 medications that are widely used in all three countries and obtained from the official websites of the Electronic Medicines Compendium in the United Kingdom, Apotheken Umschau in Germany, and the Turkish Medicines and Medical Devices Agency of the Ministry of Health of the Republic of Türkiye were examined. Medication package inserts are fundamental informational texts intended to support patients in using medicines safely, correctly, and effectively. Therefore, it is of great importance that these texts be prepared in a clear, simple, and comprehensible manner, taking into account the reading comprehension abilities and informational needs of the target user population. However, the findings of the study reveal that a substantial proportion of the package inserts examined fall within the “difficult” and “very difficult” readability levels and require advanced literacy skills for comprehension by a broad user population.

The findings obtained are largely consistent with previous studies conducted in different countries and healthcare contexts. For example, a study examining package inserts for over-the-counter medications in China found that these texts had lower readability levels compared with international standards and were difficult for the public to understand [87]. Similarly, a study focusing on patient information texts for antibiotics used in the fully oral 6- and 9-month regimens recommended by the World Health Organization for the treatment of drug-resistant tuberculosis compared package inserts used in high-incidence countries such as Rwanda, Malaysia, and South Africa with those used in low-incidence countries such as the United Kingdom, Ireland, and Malta. That study reported a mean Flesch Reading Ease score of 47.4 for the materials examined, corresponding to a “difficult” readability level. It further emphasized that package inserts used in high-incidence countries tended to have lower readability levels, which could negatively affect patients’ ability to understand and correctly interpret treatment-related information, as well as their adherence to treatment [35]. Indeed, it is well established that the complex and technical language commonly used in health communication limits individuals’ access to health information and makes it more difficult for them to correctly interpret and apply such information [88–91].

The literature frequently highlights that the complexity of the language used in medication package inserts may lead to adverse public health outcomes. It has been reported that dense medical terminology and long, complex sentence structures significantly reduce the readability and accessibility of patient information texts [92]. The findings of the present study support this view. In the package inserts examined, the average sentence length was approximately 17 words in the United Kingdom samples, 16 words in the German samples, and 15 words in the Turkish samples, while the average word lengths were 1.6 syllables in the United Kingdom, 2.2 syllables in Germany, and 2.9 syllables in Türkiye. These findings indicate that long sentence structures and multisyllabic words are widely used in package insert texts and that these linguistic characteristics constitute key factors limiting the readability and comprehensibility of the texts. Such linguistic structures make it particularly difficult for individuals with low levels of health literacy to understand information accurately and completely, thereby hindering the effective communication of medication-related information. Previous studies have shown that individuals with inadequate health literacy experience difficulties in understanding both prescription and over-the-counter medication labels and package insert information, which can lead to incorrect medication use and serious health outcomes [89–91].

One of the original contributions of this study is the detailed, section-based, and comparative examination of the readability levels of the different sections included in patient information leaflets. The findings demonstrate that package inserts do not exhibit linguistic and structural homogeneity; rather, certain sections have markedly lower readability levels, particularly when the characteristics of the target user population are taken into account. In this context, the sections titled “What is X and what is it used for?” were predominantly classified as having a “difficult” readability level, with mean scores of 32.02 for Germany, 43.64 for the United Kingdom, and 44.44 for Türkiye. In these sections, the average word length ranged from 1.73 syllables in the United Kingdom to 2.24 syllables in Germany and 2.86 syllables in Türkiye, while the average sentence length varied between 15 and 17 words. Similarly, the statements under the heading “What you need to know before using X” were also evaluated as having a “difficult” readability level, with mean scores of 30.49 for Germany, 32.97 for Türkiye, and 44.07 for the United Kingdom. In these sections, the frequent use of long and complex sentence structures, along with multisyllabic and conceptually dense words, substantially reduces text comprehensibility. Yet, these sections constitute the most critical parts of package inserts, as they contain essential warnings related to the safe and appropriate use of the medication. Therefore, simplifying the language and shortening sentence structures, particularly in these sections, should be regarded as an important requirement for enhancing the functionality of patient information texts.

In contrast, the information presented under the heading “How to use X” demonstrates mean readability scores of 47.49 for Germany, 50.30 for Türkiye, and 64.98 for the United Kingdom, corresponding to a “difficult” level in the German samples and a “moderately difficult” level in the Turkish and United Kingdom samples. This finding indicates that information directly guiding medication use is presented in a clearer, more explicit, and more user-oriented language compared with other sections, thereby relatively facilitating readers’ access to this information. However, this relative clarity does not extend to all critical sections. The “Possible side effects” section consistently falls within the “difficult” readability level, which is particularly concerning from a public health perspective. In this section, the high density of technical terminology and long, complex sentence structures may lead to substantial comprehension difficulties, especially among older adults, individuals with limited health literacy, or those with reading impairments. Such barriers to understanding may result in the misinterpretation of potential risks, heightened treatment-related anxiety, or non-adherence to recommended medication use. By contrast, the section titled “How to store X” generally falls within a “moderately difficult” readability level, suggesting that shorter sentence structures and simpler vocabulary contribute to improved comprehensibility. To improve readability in sections related to side effects, pharmaceutical companies and regulatory authorities could adopt plain language principles, reduce sentence length, avoid unnecessary medical terminology, and provide information in shorter and more direct statements. For example, a statement such as “Discontinue administration immediately and consult your healthcare professional if severe hypersensitivity reactions occur” could be simplified as “Stop using the medicine and contact your doctor immediately if you develop a serious allergic reaction.” Such revisions may substantially improve the accessibility and comprehensibility of patient information leaflets for the general public.

Despite the insights provided by these section-based analyses, it should be noted that this study offers a readability assessment solely at the textual level. While readability formulas provide valuable indicators of linguistic complexity, they do not offer direct evidence of how users actually comprehend or apply the information in real-world contexts. Readability should therefore be regarded as a necessary, but not sufficient, condition for understanding. Future research would benefit from incorporating complementary user-centered methods—such as comprehension testing [89], cognitive interviews [93], think-aloud protocols [94], or eye-tracking analyses [95, 96]—to more accurately capture the relationship between textual readability and cognitive processing and to strengthen the evidence base for improving patient information materials. These approaches have also been applied in health communication research to examine how individuals attend to, interpret, and process health information [97, 98].

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study has several strengths. First, the comparative examination of patient information leaflets for medications commonly used across three countries with different linguistic, cultural, and regulatory contexts—namely the United Kingdom, Germany, and Türkiye—enhances the contribution of the findings to the international health communication literature. Moreover, the study not only assessed the overall readability levels of the package inserts but also employed a section-based analytical approach, enabling a more detailed identification of linguistic challenges in information areas that are critical for patients. The combined evaluation of multiple readability indicators, including average sentence length, word length, and standardized readability scores, further strengthens the comprehensiveness and robustness of the analyses.

However, the study also has certain limitations. First, the analyses rely solely on textual readability formulas. While these formulas provide important indicators of linguistic complexity, they do not offer direct evidence regarding the extent to which users comprehend the information or are able to apply it correctly in real-world settings. Second, the inclusion of only 20 medications commonly used in the three countries limits the generalizability of the findings across all drug categories and therapeutic areas. Additionally, factors that may influence comprehensibility—such as visual design, typography, layout, spacing, and the use of visual aids—were not considered within the scope of this study. Finally, the package inserts were obtained from official online sources, and potential differences in format or presentation in printed versions were not examined.

Public health and policy implications

The findings of this study indicate that the readability of patient information leaflets is not merely a linguistic issue but also a structural concern with direct implications for public health. Poorly readable leaflets can hinder patients’ accurate understanding of essential medication information, potentially leading to medication errors, non-adherence to treatment, and the misinterpretation of possible adverse effects. This situation is particularly concerning for vulnerable populations, including individuals with limited health literacy, older adults, and patients with chronic conditions, as it may exacerbate existing health inequalities.

In this context, it is crucial for regulatory authorities and pharmaceutical companies to approach patient information texts not only from a legal compliance perspective but also through a user-centered health communication lens. In the European Union, including Germany, Directive 2001/83/EC and European Medicines Agency (EMA) guidelines require patient information leaflets to be written in a clear, comprehensible, and readability-tested manner. Similar standards continue to be applied in the United Kingdom through the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA), while in Türkiye, patient information leaflets are regulated by the Turkish Medicines and Medical Devices Agency (TİTCK) in accordance with EU-harmonized legislation. However, despite these regulatory requirements, the findings of the present study indicate that many leaflet sections still fall within the “difficult” or “very difficult” readability categories. Incorporating target user profiles into the development of package inserts, adopting plain language principles, and routinely applying readability assessments as quality indicators may represent important steps toward enhancing patient safety. In particular, simplifying language and sentence structure in critical sections such as “What you need to know before using X” and “Possible side effects,” and supporting these sections with clear explanations or examples where appropriate, could substantially improve accessibility.

Furthermore, these findings offer important insights for health policymakers. Establishing clear readability standards for patient information leaflets at national and international levels and integrating such standards into regulatory review and oversight processes may contribute to strengthening medication safety. In parallel, policies aimed at improving population-level health literacy, alongside regulatory efforts to simplify patient information materials, may jointly enhance individuals’ access to health information and their capacity to use medications safely and effectively.

According to national regulations and institutional policies, ethical approval was not required for this type of study. Therefore, approval from an ethics committee or institutional review board was not sought. Informed consent to participate was not applicable, as the study did not involve human subjects.
