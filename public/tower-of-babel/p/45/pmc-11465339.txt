Acute middle cerebral artery occlusion due to a small internal carotid aneurysm cavity with blood stagnation: illustrative case
Authors: Minami Uezato, Hiroyuki Ikeda, Genki Kimura, Takuya Osuki, Yasunori Yokochi, Masanori Kinosada, Yoshitaka Kurosaki, and Masaki Chin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDIn cerebral large vessel occlusion, even when an aneurysm is close to the target vessel, if the aneurysm is relatively small and angiography shows no thrombus within the aneurysm, it is difficult to identify the aneurysm as the embolic source.OBSERVATIONSA 67-year-old man with a history of two left-sided cerebral infarctions developed a small left-sided cerebral infarction. On hospital day 3, he developed a left M2 occlusion and underwent thrombectomy with retraction of a stent retriever into an aspiration catheter at the proximal end of the thrombus. A red thrombus was retrieved, and M2 recanalization was successfully achieved. Angiography revealed a large partially thrombotic aneurysm with a small cavity with blood stagnation in the left cervical internal carotid artery, which was considered the embolic source. Stent-assisted coil embolization was performed on the aneurysm, and no recurrent cerebral infarction was observed after embolization.LESSONSIn cerebral large vessel occlusion, an aneurysm cavity with blood stagnation may be the embolic source. In cases in which thrombectomy was performed for acute cerebral large vessel occlusion, 2.9%–3.7% of cases had concomitant aneurysms in the target vessel.1, 2 These aneurysms leading to cerebral infarction are rare, occurring in only 0.3% of cases.1 Although there are reports of thrombectomy for large vessel occlusion due to an aneurysm proximal to the occlusion,3–9 it is still difficult to determine whether the aneurysm is the embolic source if it is relatively small and does not have a contrast defect indicating the presence of a thrombus.3 We present a case of large vessel occlusion in which an internal carotid aneurysm with blood stagnation and no contrast defect was the embolic source.

Illustrative Case

History and Examination

A 67-year-old man had a history of angina pectoris, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and two left-sided cerebral infarctions. Twenty years earlier, he had experienced an acute cerebral infarction in the left cerebral hemisphere and underwent a left carotid endarterectomy for severe stenosis of the left cervical internal carotid artery (ICA). Since then, he had been taking aspirin 100 mg/day. Five years earlier, he had again experienced an acute cerebral infarction in the left cerebral hemisphere. A partially thrombotic aneurysm in the left cervical ICA was noted, but it was not considered to be the embolic source. The aneurysm was not present before the carotid endarterectomy but was determined to be unrelated to the carotid endarterectomy due to its location away from the surgical site. Antithrombotic therapy with aspirin 100 mg/day was continued.

This time he collapsed at home and was brought to the hospital by ambulance. On arrival, he had right hemiparesis and sensory aphasia and scored 6 on the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS). Blood tests revealed a hemoglobin A1c level of 5.1%, a low-density lipoprotein cholesterol level of 104 mg/dL, which was within normal limits, and an elevated brain natriuretic peptide level of 156.2 pg/mL. D-dimer was slightly elevated to 1.1 μg/mL, while the activated partial thromboplastin time was 31.4 seconds, which was within normal limits. Electrocardiography showed normal sinus rhythm. Head magnetic resonance imaging showed an acute cerebral infarction around an old infarction in the left frontal lobe (Fig. 1A and B). Magnetic resonance angiography showed no occlusion of the main cerebral arteries but a partially thrombotic aneurysm measuring 19 × 15 × 13 mm in the left cervical ICA (Fig. 1C–E). Computed tomography angiography showed calcification along the aneurysm wall, and stenosis at the origin of the cervical ICA was ruled out (Fig. 1F–H). Antithrombotic therapy with aspirin 100 mg/day was continued, and a search for the embolic source was planned.

On hospital day 3, the patient’s right hemiparesis, sensory aphasia, dysarthria, and right facial palsy worsened, and his NIHSS score increased to 14 points. Computed tomography showed a hyperdense sign in the left M2 inferior trunk without new ischemic changes compared to magnetic resonance imaging on hospital day 1 (Fig. 1I). Computed tomography angiography showed a proximal occlusion of the left M2 inferior trunk (Fig. 1J). Thrombectomy was initiated 176 minutes after the last known normal state before symptom exacerbation during hospitalization.

Endovascular Surgery

A 9-Fr balloon guide catheter (Optimo EPD, Tokai Medical Products) was placed at the origin of the left cervical ICA via the right femoral artery with the patient under local anesthesia. Left ICA angiography showed occlusion of the left M2 inferior trunk and slight contrast inflow into a partially thrombotic aneurysm in the cervical ICA (Fig. 2A). A microguidewire, microcatheter, and 5-Fr aspiration catheter (Sofia Flow, Terumo) were carefully advanced through the cervical ICA to reduce stress on the aneurysm. A red thrombus was retrieved in one pass by retracting a stent retriever (Tron FX II, 4 × 40 mm, Terumo) into the Sofia catheter navigated at the proximal end of the thrombus using a combined technique (Fig. 2B); 16 minutes after the start of the procedure, left ICA angiography showed recanalization of the left M2 inferior trunk and occlusion of the peripheral portion of the left angular artery (Fig. 2C). Angiography from the origin of the left ICA showed contrast stagnation throughout the cavity of a partially thrombotic aneurysm in the left cervical ICA (Fig. 2D and E). Three-dimensional angiographic imaging of the left ICA showed an aneurysm cavity with a neck of 3.6 mm, a dome of 3.7 × 2.6 mm, and a height of 4.3 mm (height/neck [aspect] ratio 1.2); the ICA diameter was 5.1 mm proximal to the aneurysm and 4.4 mm distal to the aneurysm (Fig. 2F). There was no contrast defect indicating the presence of a thrombus within the aneurysm. The occurrence of two left cerebral infarctions in a short period of time and the left cerebral infarction 5 years earlier, as well as blood stagnation within the aneurysm, suggested the partially thrombotic aneurysm in the left ICA as the embolic source. Stent-assisted coil embolization for the partially thrombotic aneurysm after thrombectomy was planned to prevent recurrent cerebral infarction.

After cone-beam computed tomography confirmed the absence of intracranial hemorrhage associated with the thrombectomy, clopidogrel 300 mg was administered via a gastric tube. A 4-Fr Fubuki dilator kit (Asahi Intecc) was navigated via the left femoral artery to the origin of the left ICA for coil insertion. A microguidewire (Chikai 14, Asahi Intecc) navigated from the 9-Fr Optimo EPD (Tokai Medical Products, Inc.) was used as the axis to deploy the distal end of a 6 × 22–mm Carotid Wallstent (Boston Scientific) from the distal side of the aneurysm, covering most of the aneurysm neck to prevent thromboembolism during intra-aneurysmal manipulation. A steam-shaped microcatheter (Phenom 17, Medtronic) navigated from the 4-Fr Fubuki dilator kit was inserted into the aneurysm (Fig. 2G). The aneurysm neck was completely covered with the Carotid Wallstent, and the coils were filled into the aneurysm using a semijailing technique (Fig. 2H). The distal end of the Carotid Wallstent was slightly displaced caudally after stent expansion (Fig. 2I). Due to inadequate coverage of the distal neck of the aneurysm, a Precise stent (6 × 30 mm, Cordis, Johnson & Johnson) was added from the petrous segment of the ICA to partially overlap the distal side of the Carotid Wallstent (Fig. 2J). Additional coils were inserted into the aneurysm neck using a jailing technique (Fig. 2K). The coil mass extended beyond the aneurysm cavity. The procedure was terminated after left ICA angiography confirmed complete occlusion of the aneurysm, ICA patency, and the absence of new intracranial vessel occlusion (Fig. 2L and M).

Postoperative Course

No new neurological symptoms were observed postoperatively. Diffusion-weighted imaging on the day after surgery revealed a new partial cerebral infarction in the left middle cerebral artery (Fig. 3A). Magnetic resonance angiography confirmed patency of the left ICA and middle cerebral artery (Fig. 3B). Antithrombotic therapy with aspirin 100 mg/day and clopidogrel 75 mg/day was continued. Transthoracic echocardiography and Holter electrocardiography showed no cardiogenic emboli. T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging showed no hyperintense plaques in the aortic arch and left common carotid artery (Fig. 3C). Hematoxylin and eosin staining of the retrieved red thrombus showed abundant red blood cells with platelet aggregation and neutrophils mainly on the periphery of the thrombus (Fig. 3D and E). No organic thrombus or plaque was observed. Angiography 15 days postoperatively confirmed complete occlusion of the aneurysm (Fig. 3F). The right hemiparesis and sensory aphasia gradually improved, and the patient was transferred to a rehabilitation facility with a modified Rankin Scale score of 2 on day 47 postoperatively. Three months postoperatively, aspirin was discontinued but clopidogrel 75 mg/day was continued, and the patient was discharged from the rehabilitation facility with a modified Rankin Scale score of 1. Magnetic resonance angiography and imaging at 9 months postoperatively and computed tomography angiography at 1 year postoperatively showed no change in the aneurysm size compared to preoperative images (Fig. 3G–I). Angiography 1 year postoperatively showed complete occlusion of the aneurysm (Fig. 3J). Outpatient follow-up magnetic resonance imaging showed no evidence of recurrent cerebral infarction.

Discussion

Observations

In this case, angiography after thrombectomy revealed a small cavity with blood stagnation within a large partially thrombotic ICA aneurysm, which was determined to be the embolic source. The coil mass extended beyond the aneurysm cavity after embolization, indicating fresh thrombus formation around the aneurysm cavity. Because of the recurrent left-sided cerebral infarctions and the absence of an embolic source in the aortic arch, ICA origin, or heart, a large partially thrombotic ICA aneurysm was considered to be the embolic source. The aneurysm was thought to be a pseudoaneurysm based on its morphology, location, and behavior during coiling. Even if the cause of the cerebral infarction was a pseudoaneurysm, the recurrence of cerebral infarction within a short period of time led us to conclude that endovascular surgery was necessary. To our knowledge, this is the first report of an embolic source of large vessel occlusion due to blood stagnation within an aneurysm identified by angiography.

Thrombus formation has been reported in 48% of giant aneurysms and 76% of large aneurysms, and the greater the size of the aneurysm, the greater the likelihood of intra-aneurysmal thrombosis.10 However, unruptured giant or large aneurysms rarely become an embolic source of cerebral infarction.11 Intra-aneurysmal thrombosis can be caused by intra-aneurysmal blood stagnation, low wall shear stress at the aneurysm tip, endothelial injury due to turbulent intra-aneurysmal blood flow, and increased coagulability.11–14 Intracranial unruptured aneurysms with an aspect ratio greater than 2.5 have been reported to be prone to intra-aneurysmal thrombosis.15 In the present case, the aneurysm size was large with a maximum diameter of 19 mm, but the aneurysm cavity size was small with a maximum diameter of 4.3 mm due to partial thrombosis. Although the aspect ratio of the aneurysm cavity was 1.2 at the time of thrombectomy, it was considered the embolic source due to blood stagnation.

In cases of large vessel occlusion in which the proximal aneurysm is the embolic source, the aneurysm is usually identified before thrombectomy. Care must be taken to avoid mechanically stimulating the aneurysm when guiding a microguidewire, microcatheter, or aspiration catheter.2 Retraction of a stent retriever into a guide catheter placed in the neck may mechanically stimulate the aneurysm and cause distal embolism due to intra-aneurysmal thrombus. A direct aspiration first pass technique (ADAPT)16 or techniques that retract a stent retriever into an aspiration catheter17 may be useful to avoid interference between the aneurysm and the stent retriever.

In acute cerebral infarction, if the aneurysm is the embolic source, coil embolization can cause thrombus migration and result in a new cerebral embolism. Therefore, coil embolization after thrombus dissolution within the aneurysm by antithrombotic therapy may be useful for preventing embolism during the procedure.3 However, there is no definitive consensus on whether antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy is beneficial for thrombus regression in aneurysms.7 In the present case, the cerebral infarction occurred due to thrombus formation within the aneurysm cavity despite medication with aspirin. Therefore, antiplatelet drugs may not be sufficient to inhibit thrombus formation within the aneurysm. If a patient has a relatively high risk of recurrent cerebral infarction in the early stage of antithrombotic therapy, it may be acceptable to treat the aneurysm during the acute phase of the cerebral infarction.

In cases of ICA aneurysms, good occlusion results have been reported with stent monotherapy using covered stents, flow diverters, and carotid artery stents.5, 18, 19 Although the CASPER carotid artery stent (Terumo) is designed to treat carotid artery stenosis, it is a dual-layer micromesh stent that has a flow diversion effect.5 The risk of cerebral embolism is low with stent monotherapy because there is no intra-aneurysmal manipulation that can allow thrombus migration from the aneurysm into the bloodstream. The newer generation of balloon-mounted cardiac stents, such as the Papyrus covered stent (Biotronik) and the neurospecific Willis covered stent (MicroPort), have the advantage of immediate aneurysm occlusion and the disadvantages of stiffness and difficulty of delivery.20, 21 In our case, the Carotid Wallstent and the Precise stent, which are readily available for emergency surgery at our institution, were used. Since the flow-diverter effect of these stents is relatively low, coil embolization within the aneurysm was added. Because coil embolization could have allowed thrombus migration from the aneurysm into the parent vessel, the aneurysm neck was covered with carotid stents to prevent distal embolism.

Lessons

In cerebral large vessel occlusion, an aneurysm with blood stagnation may be the embolic source. Using an aspiration catheter alone or retracting a stent retriever into an aspiration catheter may be a useful technique and help to avoid interference between the aneurysm and the stent retriever.
