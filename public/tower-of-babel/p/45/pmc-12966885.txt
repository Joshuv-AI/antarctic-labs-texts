Key Factors for Quality End‐of‐Life Care for People With Intellectual Disabilities. A Critical Interpretive Synthesis Review Using a ‘Best‐Fit’ Framework Approach
Authors: Margaret Haigh, Philip McCallion, Mary McCarron, Pavithra Pavithra, Martin McMahon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe aim of this review was to develop a theoretical framework to guide understanding of the key factors in quality end‐of‐life care for people with intellectual disabilities.MethodA systematic review was conducted using a critical interpretive synthesis methodology and a ‘best‐fit’ framework approach to develop a new theoretical framework. Databases were searched using a wide‐ranging search strategy. Broad eligibility criteria were applied. Data were extracted from a purposive sample of relevant papers for the synthesis.ResultsOf 1270 retrieved records, 40 papers were selected for inclusion in the synthesis. From this, a new theoretical framework was developed comprising three themes—‘a personal approach to care’, ‘an involved network’ and ‘an enabling infrastructure’.ConclusionThe theoretical framework presented reflects the integration of a range of perspectives and provides a more developed understanding of the key factors in quality end‐of‐life care for people with intellectual disabilities.Keywords: critical interpretive synthesis, end‐of‐life care, intellectual disability, quality care

Introduction

It is well documented that people with intellectual disabilities are more likely to have poorer health than those without intellectual disabilities (Brennan et al. 2023; Emerson 2021; Emerson et al. 2016; Hughes‐McCormack et al. 2018; Liao et al. 2021; McCarron et al. 2013). The poorer health of this population can be attributed in part to greater prevalence rates of health problems than exist in the general population (McMahon and Hatton 2021). Furthermore, there is a younger onset of chronic illness than in the general population (van den Bemd et al. 2022). Liao et al. (2021) conclude that the health profile of people with intellectual disabilities is ‘complex’.

In addition to poorer health, people with intellectual disabilities are faced with other significant disadvantages which contribute to their poorer health outcomes. There is growing evidence that some of these inequalities in health are avoidable, such as the significant barriers faced in accessing effective healthcare (Chapman et al. 2024; Emerson 2021). These barriers can result from communication difficulties (Whittle et al. 2018); inequality in screening practices, particularly in relation to cancer (Cuypers et al. 2022); stigmatising attitudes among mainstream health professionals towards people with intellectual disabilities (Pelleboer‐Gunnink et al. 2017), and inadequate knowledge among doctors and other healthcare professionals about the health needs of people with intellectual disabilities leading to diagnostic overshadowing and ultimately delayed diagnosis and treatment (Iezzoni 2022).

Despite the complex health issues and disadvantages experienced, life expectancy in people with intellectual disabilities has been increasing for some time (Patja et al. 2000) and continues to increase (Landes et al. 2021; NHS Bristol North Somerset and South Gloucestershire 2023). This has been attributed to improved healthcare, including screening and other initiatives addressing lifestyle behaviours and health risks (O'Leary et al. 2018). However, as life expectancy increases, people with intellectual disabilities are living longer with more complex needs. The challenges experienced throughout life, such as physical and mental health comorbidities, hyper polypharmacy and communication difficulties, are often exacerbated by age (Brennan et al. 2023). In addition, there may be complex environmental and social issues associated with aging, such as the need for residential moves to meet increased care needs, often resulting in the fracturing of interpersonal relationships. Such challenges exist within the context of life‐long institutionalised care for many, with few opportunities to exercise personal choice in how they live their lives, let alone prepare for end‐of‐life (McCarron et al. 2017).

These multi‐faceted challenges associated with increased life expectancy highlight the complexities of providing this aging population with care at end‐of‐life. This period is defined as a time when death is imminent and life expectancy is limited (Health Service Executive 2012). The distinction between end‐of‐life care and palliative care must be noted as the latter encompasses a broader approach, encouraging early intervention to improve quality of life for people facing the challenges associated with a life‐limiting condition (World Health Organization 2023). End‐of‐life care should be viewed as a continuum of palliative care.

Compounding difficulties experienced by the person, some healthcare professionals—nurses, doctors and social care workers in particular (Bekkema et al. 2014; Gray and Kim 2020) have reported feeling inadequately trained to provide good end‐of‐life care to people with intellectual disabilities. These deficits have been confirmed more recently by professionals from various disciplines (Voss et al. 2023). A lack of understanding of the respective role of palliative care professionals and intellectual disability staff is also reported (Cross et al. 2012). The poorer quality end‐of‐life care reported for people with intellectual disabilities in comparison to the general population, notably in relation to hospital care, serves to underline these inequalities (Hunt et al. 2020).

As with the general population, compassionate and person‐centred care is required for people with intellectual disabilities at end‐of‐life (McCarron et al. 2017; McNamara et al. 2020) to ensure a ‘good death’ (McCarron et al. 2022, 2010; Tuffrey‐Wijne 2009). Good practice guidance and frameworks on dying, death and people with intellectual disabilities have started to emerge to guide the delivery of such care (Burke et al. 2017; NHS England PCPLD Network 2017; Tuffrey‐Wijne and Davidson 2018; Tuffrey‐Wijne and McLaughlin 2015). However, much of this evidence reflects the perspectives of individual stakeholder groups. There is an absence of a broader understanding of what the key factors are in quality end‐of‐life care for people with intellectual disabilities drawn from the integrated findings across all stakeholder groups. This review set out to address this deficit by appraising the evidence base to develop a comprehensive, systematic, well‐organised theoretical framework representing the views of a wide range of stakeholders. It is anticipated that the holistic framework developed will serve as a benchmark, providing guidance to support the implementation of best practice. From this, it will be possible to identify where policies, research and education are required to support the delivery of quality care.

Research Aim and Objectives

The research question which addressed the aim of this literature review was—What are the key factors in quality end‐of‐life care for people with intellectual disabilities?

The objective of the review was to develop a theoretically informed understanding of this research question.

Methods

Design

In the absence of a single, universally recognised guideline, this literature review sought the most suitable approach, informed by the findings of an initial review of the literature. The initial review was undertaken by the lead author and consisted of a search of the EMBASE electronic database based on the key concepts of ‘end‐of‐life’ and ‘people with intellectual disabilities’. The results of this review confirmed that the body of evidence in relation to end‐of‐life care for people with intellectual disabilities is diverse and complex. The evidence comprises empirical and non‐empirical works, including primary research, literature reviews, book chapters, reports and editorials. While conventional systematic review methodology is ‘ill‐suited’ (Dixon‐Woods et al. 2006, 2) to the challenges presented by a review of such diverse material, a critical interpretive synthesis (CIS) approach allows for all evidence that offers insight into the topic under investigation to be considered (Dixon‐Woods et al. 2006). It is therefore compatible with research questions which are likely to draw on literature that is heterogenous in nature (Wilson et al. 2014), as in the current study. CIS supports an inclusive approach by promoting a method that is interpretive and incorporates an element of quality appraisal. This is in contrast to an aggregative approach found in traditional systematic reviews (Dixon‐Woods et al. 2006). Furthermore, CIS allows for iterative theory generation (Depraetere et al. 2021) which is compatible with the objective of the study to develop a theoretically informed understanding of the question under review. For these reasons, this literature review adopted a CIS approach to address the research question.

To support the study objective, principles from a ‘best‐fit’ framework approach were borrowed (Carroll et al. 2011, 2013). This entailed using an existing published model (Tuffrey‐Wijne and Davidson 2018) (Table S1) to guide the initial coding process. The a priori framework was based on examples of good practice proposed by healthcare professionals and this specific focus is acknowledged. It was selected as a basis for this study because of its potential to serve as a starting point from which to develop a new theoretical framework (Carroll et al. 2011) in response to findings emerging from the literature, rather than as an instrument from which to maximise opportunities for new theory generation.

To enhance transparency, this systematic review was registered with PROSPERO and is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Page et al. 2021). Full details of the review methodology were outlined in the published protocol which was developed prior to the initiation of the review.

Eligibility Criteria

While conventional systematic reviews aim to identify and include all relevant literature meeting fixed inclusion criteria, interpretive reviews set out to identify potentially relevant papers close to the topic of interest (Dixon‐Woods et al. 2006). Though boundaries are less well‐defined in interpretive reviews, there is still a requirement to identify eligibility criteria to ensure the review remains focused on the topic under consideration, in this case end‐of‐life care for people with intellectual disabilities. Accordingly, broad eligibility criteria for this review were set at the outset. It was acknowledged that as the review progressed, these criteria may need to be refined to ensure that the insights emerging aligned with the aims of the review (Wilson et al. 2014).

The criteria for inclusion of literature in the preliminary stage of the review, that is title and abstract screening, were as follows:

Search Strategy

In line with a CIS approach, conventional systematic principles were applied in the development and application of a search strategy. A broad search strategy, based on the key concepts of ‘care at end‐of‐life’, ‘people with intellectual disabilities’ and ‘end‐of‐life’ was developed in consultation with subject librarians. The Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) checklist was utilised to improve the quality of the literature (McGowan et al. 2016). In line with the eligibility criteria, no restrictions were set on study design, publication type or time frame. Studies not written in English were excluded. The search utilised identified keywords with a combination of database specific control language. Similar search strings were performed across five electronic databases—CINAHL, EMBASE, MEDLINE, PsycINFO and Web of Science. Other sources were explored for grey literature. This involved searching supplementary databases including Cochrane Library, Global Index Medicus (WHO), Google Scholar and Proquest Dissertations and Theses. All papers identified as part of the systematic search were imported into EndNote X21 for deduplication. Details of the search strategies adopted, and subsequent results, are shown in Table S2.

Retrieved papers were exported to Covidence for title and abstract screening which was carried out by two members of the research team. As anticipated, and consistent with the iterative approach promoted by CIS (Wilson et al. 2014), preliminary assessment indicated that refinements to the initial eligibility criteria were necessary to exclude some papers returned which were not sufficiently specific to the topic in question. Exclusion criteria were introduced, namely where the focus of the literature was on any of the following topics:

From this screening process, papers which were identified as ‘eligible’, that is, classified as either meeting the inclusion criteria or unclear whether they should be included, were allocated for subsequent full‐text screening. Also included for full‐text screening were papers where the abstract was not available. Ongoing discussions took place between the reviewers on the eligibility of papers. Any uncertainty regarding inclusion was resolved by consultation.

Quality Appraisal

The full text of all non‐excluded papers was retrieved and reviewed independently by each reviewer to decide whether they should be included in the review. In line with CIS methodological guidance, a high tolerance was applied when conducting this ‘quality appraisal’ stage, the objective being to maximise the inclusion of a wide range of literature that provided conceptual insights, rather than to focus on ‘methodological specificities’ (Dixon‐Woods et al. 2006).

The preliminary step in the quality appraisal stage was to appraise empirical papers and to exclude any that may be classed as ‘fatally flawed’. These were papers which did not meet the following criteria (Dixon‐Woods et al. 2006).

Are the aims and objectives of the research clearly stated?

Are the aims and objectives of the research clearly stated?

Is the research design clearly specified and appropriate for the aims and objectives of the research?

Is the research design clearly specified and appropriate for the aims and objectives of the research?

Is a clear account provided of the process by which findings were reproduced?

Is a clear account provided of the process by which findings were reproduced?

Is sufficient data displayed to support interpretations and conclusions?

Is sufficient data displayed to support interpretations and conclusions?

Is the method of analysis appropriate and adequately explained?

Is the method of analysis appropriate and adequately explained?

As the final decision about inclusion in the review depended on a subsequent assessment of relevance, a low threshold was maintained when applying the above criteria. This was because some ‘weak’ papers were considered to have potentially high relevance and warrant further examination (Dixon‐Woods et al. 2006).

All remaining empirical papers, that is those found not to be ‘fatally flawed’, and other material which included grey literature, were then appraised for their relevance. This process constituted the second step in the quality appraisal stage. Relevance was based on the ability of each paper to generate concepts, theories or insights in relation to the research question (Dixon‐Woods et al. 2006). The following broad question was used to guide this stage of the appraisal process: Does the paper provide a clear understanding of what is good quality end‐of‐life care for people with intellectual disabilities? This approach adhered to the aim of a CIS review ‘to treat the literature as warranting critical scrutiny’ (Dixon‐Woods et al. 2006, 6). It is this critical approach which is a key feature of the CIS process, and one that distinguishes it from other types of interpretive synthesis.

As with any qualitative analysis, the ‘creative, interpretive processes involved’ (Dixon‐Woods et al. 2006, 6) limit full transparency. To address any potential bias based on a single perspective, the steps outlined above to appraise each paper were completed by two research team members. Papers deemed by consensus to be ‘relevant’ were considered for inclusion in the synthesis.

Data Extraction and Synthesis

The quality appraisal stage identified a sizeable body of relevant papers. Unlike traditional systematic reviews, CIS does not require all relevant literature which meets inclusion criteria and minimum quality standard to be included in the analysis (Wilson et al. 2014). Rather than aiming for comprehensive identification and inclusion of all relevant literature, the purpose of the searching phase in a CIS approach is to identify potentially relevant literature to provide a ‘sampling frame’ from which literature is selected for analysis (Dixon‐Woods et al. 2006). Sampling is justified as the focus in an interpretive synthesis is on the development of concepts and theory rather than on an exhaustive summary of all data (Wilson et al. 2014).

Following the approach set out by Bullock et al. (2021), a sample was drawn from this sampling frame for the initial data extraction and critique. A purposive sampling approach was used to select papers, based on the unique insights they provided to the topic under investigation (Wilson et al. 2014). These papers were identified from the quality appraisal stage as having a clear and unambiguous focus on quality end‐of‐life care. A broad range of material was selected to ensure that a variety of publication types, methodology, study design, and publication year were represented in the purposive sample.

An excel file served as the tool for data extraction and analysis. Descriptive data were extracted from each paper within the purposive sample as a first step. Further data were extracted and subsequently analysed using the Framework Method (Ritchie and Spencer 1994), adapting the process set out by Gale et al. (2013) as required. Both deductive and inductive approaches were adopted for the purpose of data analysis following the steps set out below.

An in‐depth familiarisation process was undertaken with all papers repeatedly read. The next step involved initial coding to identify the overarching main themes. Where possible, the a priori framework (Tuffrey‐Wijne and Davidson 2018) acted as the analytical framework with data for analysis extracted from the findings of each paper and coded deductively according to the categories and constituent codes contained within the four themes of the framework. These themes were ‘individual and organisational commitment’; ‘working together in collaboration’; ‘the person's story is at the heart of care’ and ‘developing tools and staff training’ (further details of these themes and constituent codes are contained in Table S1). A separate excel worksheet was created for each of the four themes. Data was organised as a framework matrix, with each row of the excel spreadsheet representing a paper and each column a code within the theme. For data which did not ‘fit’ within a pre‐defined code, but which added greater understanding to other key factors necessary in the provision of end‐of‐life care, additional codes were identified inductively using data‐driven thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke 2006). This data was captured in a framework matrix contained in a fifth spreadsheet.

From this emergent coding scheme, common themes and concepts were identified and a synthesising argument emerged in the form of a new theoretical framework. This framework, developed from the a priori framework, included both original and modified sub‐themes and those generated by the inductive thematic analysis of the extracted data (Carroll et al. 2011). Evidence from the literature comprising the purposive sample continued to be integrated into the developing theoretical framework until all evidence had been examined and a final coding scheme was established (Carroll et al. 2013). In line with CIS methodology, the processes of data extraction, critique and synthesis were conducted as ‘iterative, interactive, dynamic and recursive rather than as fixed procedures’ (Dixon‐Woods et al. 2006, 9).

To ‘test’ the framework and assess if further detail was required, three additional papers were drawn from the sampling frame and data extracted. When examined, it was evident that these papers yielded no further insights into the framework, indicating that saturation had been reached (Bullock et al. 2021).

Data was extracted by the primary author who developed and presented a proposed framework. This was critically considered by all reviewers and adapted until agreement was reached.

Results

Literature Selection

The literature selection process is outlined in Figure 1. The figure shows that a total of 1270 records were retrieved for title and abstract screening. This process resulted in 171 papers being retrieved for full text review. This ‘quality appraisal’ stage of the process excluded 120 papers. These excluded papers were comprised of two papers deemed to be ‘fatally flawed’, as per the criteria set out earlier; 65 papers which were considered not relevant to the research question in that they did not provide a clear understanding into what is good quality end‐of‐life care for people with intellectual disabilities; and 53 conference abstracts or poster presentations which did not provide sufficient insight into the topic under investigation. Details of these 120 excluded papers are contained in Table S3.

PRISMA flow diagram.

Following the exclusion of these papers, 51 relevant papers remained which constituted the ‘sampling frame’. A sample of 40 papers was selected from this sampling frame for extraction and synthesis, purposively drawn to reflect the diverse nature of the overall body of evidence.

Literature Characteristics

Details of the 40 papers included in the synthesis are summarised in Table 1. Twenty‐six related to empirical studies. These were conducted by researchers based in Ireland (n = 8), Australia (n = 8), the United Kingdom (UK) (n = 7), the Netherlands (n = 2), and the United States (US) (n = 1). Included studies adopted a range of approaches—quantitative, qualitative, and mixed‐methods. The studies explored a range of topics centred on end‐of‐life care for people with intellectual disabilities. Though efforts were made to ensure that a variety of participants were represented in the selected studies, this was constrained by the published evidence. For example, none of the participants with intellectual disabilities were themselves receiving end‐of‐life care. Furthermore, most of the bereaved caregivers were staff members, with few family members participating.

In addition to empirical studies, the following papers were also included in the synthesis—four literature reviews, four feature articles, three book chapters, a Delphi study, a consensus statement and a guide for delivering quality end‐of‐life care. This wide range of literature was purposively selected for inclusion in the synthesis to reflect the diversity of the overall body of evidence on this topic. This literature was also selected to acknowledge and to include the original contributions made by the authors. This is in keeping with key principles of CIS, which supports the inclusion of a theoretically rich sample of heterogeneous literature. Likewise, some early literature was purposively included to examine whether trends over time emerged. The inclusion of this literature also allowed for seminal works to be considered.

Developing the Framework

Data was extracted from the literature and coded initially according to the categories and codes contained within the four themes of the a priori framework (Tuffrey‐Wijne and Davidson 2018). In line with CIS methodology (Dixon‐Woods et al. 2006), analysis and synthesis were conducted simultaneously and iteratively, as recurring themes were identified. During this process, it became clear that while there was evidence to support the inclusion of some a priori sub‐themes in an emerging framework, there was little or no evidence to support others and thus they were not included. This lack of evidence may have been because these topics were not considered to play an important role in the delivery of good end‐of‐life care or because exploration of these topics has yet to occur. Findings from this deductive analysis, outlining the a priori sub‐themes which justified inclusion (some in a revised manner), are summarised in the following sub‐section. The subsequent sub‐section includes details of the new inductive sub‐themes. These were created thematically from extracted data which did not ‘fit’, but which provided valuable insights.

A Priori Sub‐Themes

Physical Care

The primary objective of end‐of‐life care was identified as the promotion of comfort (Egan et al. 2022). One aspect of this multi‐factorial phenomenon was symptom management (Tuffrey‐Wijne et al. 2016), with pain management identified as a priority (McNamara et al. 2020; Wark et al. 2022). Physical care also included effective management of nutrition and hydration (McCallion et al. 2017; McCarron et al. 2022; McNamara et al. 2020). Those well known to the person were identified as being important to support understanding of expressions of discomfort (Tuffrey‐Wijne 2002, 2003; Tuffrey‐Wijne et al. 2016).

Advance Care Planning

Advance care planning, encompassing all aspects of the person's care wishes, was reported to be an important component of end‐of‐life care and was evident from an early point in the literature (Amara 2023; Bailey et al. 2016; Bekkema et al. 2016; Diaz et al. 2023; Kingsbury 2005; McCallion et al. 2017; McCarron et al. 2022, 2017; McNamara et al. 2020; Tuffrey‐Wijne et al. 2016). Planning was noted to be particularly important where transfer to another care setting was required (Cithambaram et al. 2020). Early discussions and recording of wishes were advised to ensure wishes were known before any deterioration (Morton‐Nance and Schafer 2012; NHS England PCPLD Network 2017; Watchman 2005) and to avoid having to make important decisions later at a potentially more stressful time (Voss et al. 2021). Opportunities for end‐of‐life decision making were particularly valued by people with intellectual disabilities (Cithambaram et al. 2020; Diaz et al. 2023) who felt that they should be supported to engage in the process (McCallion et al. 2017; Tuffrey‐Wijne et al. 2016).

Enabling to Live as Desired

Allowing the person to live as desired was promoted as a means of providing end‐of‐life care (Amara 2023; Forrester‐Jones et al. 2017; McCallion et al. 2017; NHS England PCPLD Network 2017; Tromans et al. 2019). Among the strategies suggested included fulfilling the person's last wishes (Bekkema et al. 2016) and maintaining previously valued experiences, such as going for a walk (Wark et al. 2022), listening to favourite music (McCarron et al. 2010), and putting on make‐up (McCarron et al. 2017).

Continuity of Care

Continuity of care—not to be confused with ‘dying in place’ (Tuffrey‐Wijne 2009)—emerged as fundamental to quality of end‐of‐life care (Bekkema et al. 2015; Diaz et al. 2023; Grindrod and Rumbold 2017; McCarron et al. 2022, 2017, 2010; McLaughlin et al. 2014; McNamara et al. 2020; Voss et al. 2021; Wiese et al. 2012). This extended to keeping links with familiar people and possessions, especially when transitions for the purpose of care were involved (NHS England PCPLD Network 2017; Todd 2013). Continuity of care was noted to be of particular importance to those living with dementia (McCarron et al. 2022; Watchman 2005).

Regular Training

Training for intellectual disability service staff, particularly in relation to pain and symptom management (McCarron et al. 2010; McLaughlin et al. 2014; Tromans et al. 2019), was a consistent theme emerging (Adam et al. 2020; Bekkema et al. 2015; Diaz et al. 2023; Dunkley and Sales 2014; Grindrod and Rumbold 2017; Ryan 2022; Voss et al. 2021). This was also found in early works (Tuffrey‐Wijne 2003). Where there was end‐of‐life training, intellectual disability service staff felt more confident in their ability to provide high‐quality care (McCarron et al. 2017, 2011). Upskilling palliative care staff to be able to provide end‐of‐life care to people with intellectual disabilities was also encouraged (McCallion et al. 2017; McNamara et al. 2020; Tuffrey‐Wijne et al. 2016).

Collaboration Between Intellectual Disability Service and Palliative Care Staff

The value of intellectual disability service and specialist palliative care staff working together in the provision of end‐of‐life care was acknowledged in recent literature (Adam et al. 2020; McCarron et al. 2011, 2010; McNamara et al. 2020; Ryan 2022; Tuffrey‐Wijne 2002; Wark et al. 2017) and in earlier works (Tuffrey‐Wijne 2002, 2003). This collaboration helped to address gaps in service provision (Grindrod 2021), facilitated the sharing of expertise (Bailey et al. 2016; Ferretti et al. 2022; Kirkendall et al. 2012; McCallion et al. 2017), and enabled the person to remain in their place of care (McLaughlin et al. 2014). Collaboration was also beneficial in ‘dispelling fear and providing mutual support’ (McLaughlin, 2014, 1217). High satisfaction levels with the care provided were reported where collaboration occurred (McCarron et al. 2017). One important outcome of services working together was enabling the person to remain in their place of care, with continuity of care by familiar people (McLaughlin et al. 2014).

Collaboration Between All Healthcare Professionals

Collaboration between healthcare professionals across all care settings emerged as a pre‐eminent theme (Bailey et al. 2016; Cithambaram 2021; Dalton 2019; McCarron et al. 2022, 2011, 2010; McLaughlin et al. 2014; Morton‐Nance and Schafer 2012; NHS England PCPLD Network 2017; Tuffrey‐Wijne 2002; Voss et al. 2021; Welch and Moreno‐Leguizamon 2016). This was identified as key to the successful provision of palliative care (Tuffrey‐Wijne et al. 2016), particularly when end‐of‐life care was being given in the home (Tuffrey‐Wijne 2009). Communication was identified as being key to effective collaboration (McNamara et al. 2020; Wiese et al. 2012).

Collaboration With Families

The importance of involving the family in all aspects of end‐of‐life care, including post death rituals (McNamara et al. 2020), was recognised (McCarron et al. 2022; Ryan 2022; Tuffrey‐Wijne 2002; Tuffrey‐Wijne et al. 2016; Welch and Moreno‐Leguizamon 2016). The family should be viewed as important informants considering that they may know the person best (Bailey et al. 2016; McCarron et al. 2017). They should be considered as part of the ‘team’ (NHS England PCPLD Network 2017).

Supporting Families

Emotional support must be provided to families as part of quality end‐of‐life care (McNamara et al. 2020; Ryan 2022; Tuffrey‐Wijne et al. 2016) and built into end‐of‐life care planning (Tromans et al. 2019). Providing post‐death, bereavement support to families was also identified as a key consideration in good practice (NHS England PCPLD Network 2017).

Collaboration With Peers

Peers should be encouraged to participate in end‐of‐life care (Grindrod and Rumbold 2017; McCarron et al. 2010; Welch and Moreno‐Leguizamon 2016) to provide information on the care needs of the person at end‐of‐life (NHS England PCPLD Network 2017) and to identify important relationships (McCarron et al. 2017; Tuffrey‐Wijne et al. 2016). People with intellectual disabilities recognised the importance of being involved with their friends' care (Cithambaram 2021).

Leadership

Leadership was identified as being key to successful end‐of‐life care (Bailey et al. 2016; Forrester‐Jones et al. 2017; Grindrod and Rumbold 2017). Effective leadership was described as creating an environment which supported people to develop their own end‐of‐life plan (Cithambaram et al. 2020) and creating opportunities for choice (McNamara et al. 2020). Furthermore, leadership promoted a collaborative environment (McCarron et al. 2010; NHS England PCPLD Network 2017) and ensured adequate resources in terms of funding (McNamara et al. 2020) and appropriately trained intellectual disability service staff (Ryan 2022).

Policies

Evidence in relation to policies for quality end‐of‐life care centred on the importance of equity of access to palliative care (McCallion et al. 2017; McCarron et al. 2022; NHS England PCPLD Network 2017; Tuffrey‐Wijne et al. 2016). This has long been called for (Tuffrey‐Wijne 2003). Equitable access to mainstream palliative care services would support people with intellectual disabilities to remain in their own home at the end of life, should they wish (Ryan 2022).

New Sub‐Themes

An Empowered Partner in Care

Having the person involved, to the extent that they can influence their own end‐of‐life care, emerged strongly from the literature as a pre‐requisite for quality end‐of‐life care (Bekkema et al. 2016; Cithambaram et al. 2020; Diaz et al. 2023; Dunkley and Sales 2014; Kirkendall et al. 2012; McCallion et al. 2017; Welch and Moreno‐Leguizamon 2016). People with intellectual disabilities should be supported to enable their values and preferences to play a central role in end‐of‐life planning (McCarron et al. 2010; McNamara et al. 2020; Ryan 2022; Tromans et al. 2019) even if their capacity to make decisions is limited or communication is difficult (Voss et al. 2021). The introduction of the Assisted Decision Making (Capacity) Act 2015 in Ireland reflects this move towards empowerment as it promotes the inclusion of people with intellectual disabilities in decision making across all aspects of their lives, including end‐of‐life care (Dalton 2019).

Emotional Support

Providing emotional support to the person to offer reassurance was identified as vital to quality end‐of‐life care (Cithambaram et al. 2020; Diaz et al. 2023; McCarron et al. 2010; McNamara et al. 2020; Wark et al. 2022; Welch and Moreno‐Leguizamon 2016). The importance of ‘being there’ for the individual through the dying journey was noted (Todd 2013). Providing words of comfort (Bekkema et al. 2016), reading to the person (Wiese et al. 2012), and watching television together (Bekkema et al. 2016) were other examples of emotional support. People well known to the person were identified as being best placed to provide this emotional support (Cithambaram 2021).

Spiritual Care

Understanding and addressing the spiritual needs was observed as an essential component of end‐of‐life care (McCarron et al. 2010; McNamara et al. 2020; NHS England PCPLD Network 2017; Ryan 2022; Tromans et al. 2019; Voss et al. 2021), bringing peace and reducing fear (Bekkema et al. 2016). This could involve looking back on the person's life or preparing for imminent death (Bekkema et al. 2016).

Communication

There must be open communication with the person, and those identified as being important to them, for end‐of‐life care to be effective (Adam et al. 2020; Cithambaram 2021; Cithambaram et al. 2020; McCarron et al. 2017; Tuffrey‐Wijne et al. 2016; Voss et al. 2021; Welch and Moreno‐Leguizamon 2016). Appropriate communication regarding diagnosis and treatment options was highlighted as a means of empowering the person (Morton‐Nance and Schafer 2012). There should also be opportunities to participate in conversations about death and dying (Morton‐Nance and Schafer 2012) to help the person prepare for their own death (NHS England PCPLD Network 2017). It is important to remember that because a person does not speak, they do not communicate (Voss et al. 2021). Appropriate support measures should be put in place where necessary, for example by involving people well known to the person (Amara 2023; Kirkendall et al. 2012; McNamara et al. 2020; Ryan 2022; Tuffrey‐Wijne 2002).

Supporting Staff

The literature indicated that quality end‐of‐life care extended to appropriate support being put in place, both for intellectual disability service and palliative care staff (Bailey et al. 2016; Grindrod and Rumbold 2017; Ryan 2022). This included emotional and practical support (Bekkema et al. 2015; McCarron et al. 2011; Tuffrey‐Wijne 2009), training on self‐care (Tuffrey‐Wijne et al. 2016), and bereavement support (McNamara et al. 2020; NHS England PCPLD Network 2017).

Supporting Peers

Similarly, support for peers was recommended (NHS England PCPLD Network 2017). Strategies included acknowledging the importance of the relationship (McCarron et al. 2010) and supporting peers to participate in all aspects of the dying process (Ryan 2022), such as post‐death rituals (Watchman 2005; Wiese et al. 2012) and bereavement support (Tuffrey‐Wijne et al. 2016).

Capacity to Care

Appropriate resources (in terms of staff and equipment) were identified as pre‐requisites for quality end‐of‐life care (Bekkema et al. 2016, 2015; Cithambaram 2021; McCarron et al. 2017, 2010; McNamara et al. 2020; Ryan 2022; Voss et al. 2021; Wiese et al. 2012). These were particularly important if care were to be provided in the home (Bekkema et al. 2015; Grindrod and Rumbold 2017; McCarron et al. 2010). Capacity to care applied both to intellectual disability services (Grindrod 2021) and to palliative care organisations (Tuffrey‐Wijne et al. 2016). Staff must have the required training, expertise (Dunkley and Sales 2014; Welch and Moreno‐Leguizamon 2016), inter‐personal skills (Bailey et al. 2016), and dedication (Forrester‐Jones et al. 2017; Grindrod and Rumbold 2017; Todd 2013).

Theoretical Framework

During the framework synthesis, synergies emerged between the sub‐themes allowing over‐arching themes to be identified. The first theme reflected the need for a personal approach to care, addressing all aspects of care required to maintain comfort. This entailed treating the person holistically, by attending to their physical, emotional, social, and spiritual needs. With the person supported to express their will and preference, care should be delivered in a person‐centred manner. The second theme highlighted the need for an involved network around the person. These networks should be as extensive as possible, comprising staff from intellectual disability services, palliative care, the wider healthcare professional network, family and friends, all working together to provide care to the dying person, with they themselves supported. The third theme noted the importance of an enabling infrastructure with adequate capacity to care. This brought together all elements necessary for care, including the need to have an appropriately trained workforce. In addition, policies should be in place to enable equity of access to palliative care for people with intellectual disabilities. To co‐ordinate and implement these inter‐dependent factors, effective leadership is required.

This exercise did not yield any further insights, confirming that no amendments were required to the framework, and saturation had been reached with the purposive sample of 40 papers.

Discussion

This CIS review used principles from a ‘best‐fit’ framework approach to address the question: ‘What are the key factors in quality end‐of‐life care for people with intellectual disabilities?’ Using an a priori framework as a scaffold (Carroll et al. 2011) to integrate a diverse body of evidence, a theoretical framework was developed which provides additional clarity to the question under review.

In addition to empirical studies in which participants were healthcare professionals, studies in which participants were people with intellectual disabilities and family members were also included in the synthesis. In these studies, the following sub‐themes emerged strongly, implying a desire for autonomy and a focus on the softer aspect of caring—‘an empowered partner in care’ (Bekkema et al. 2016; Cithambaram et al. 2020; Diaz et al. 2023), ‘emotional support’ (Bekkema et al. 2016; Cithambaram 2021; Diaz et al. 2023), ‘spiritual care’ (Bekkema et al. 2016; Cithambaram 2021) and ‘communication’ (Cithambaram 2021; Cithambaram et al. 2020). These new sub‐themes reflect the broader focus of this new framework which was not previously present (Tuffrey‐Wijne and Davidson 2018).

As with the introduction of evidence from people with intellectual disabilities and family members, including the evidence of acknowledged leaders in the field, with their awareness of structural needs, has also contributed to the addition of new sub‐themes. The value of this material, represented in book chapters, a consensus statement and Delphi study, is evident from the new ‘enabling infrastructure’ theme, specifically in the modified sub‐theme relating to policies for ‘equity of access’ (McCallion et al. 2017; McCarron et al. 2022; Tuffrey‐Wijne et al. 2016) and the new, all‐encompassing sub‐theme called ‘capacity to care’ (McCallion et al. 2017; Ryan 2022; Tuffrey‐Wijne et al. 2016). Furthermore, the practical solutions to real‐world challenges, found in the feature articles and care guide, complement the value offered by empirical studies and expert insights. The addition of these perspectives extends the depth and breadth of the framework providing a more complete overview of the factors necessary for quality end‐of‐life care.

Some of the original sub‐themes, where evidence was present in the literature but not to a significant degree, for example ‘going beyond the call of duty’ and ‘mutual learning’, were integrated into other sub‐themes, namely ‘capacity to care’ and ‘collaboration between intellectual disability service staff and palliative care staff’ respectively. Where no evidence was found to justify inclusion, for example ‘charisma’, ‘working groups’, ‘professionals open to being challenged’ and ‘conferences’, these sub‐themes were not included. It is worth noting that the sub‐themes that were either excluded or amalgamated due to their secondary importance applied to staff providing end‐of‐life care. The wider range of contributors to this new framework, which led to other important factors emerging, may have diluted the significance of these staff‐related factors.

A key strength of this review is the methodological approach adopted. A critical interpretative synthesis has the advantage of being inclusive of all types of research. This allowed a broad sample of literature to be considered and resulted in a balance of perspectives being represented in the new, more inclusive, framework. In promoting the concept of sampling (Dixon‐Woods et al. 2006), the CIS approach supported the creation of a purposive sample to concentrate on the development of concepts and theory rather than on an exhaustive summary of all data. Furthermore, the simultaneous and iterative approach promoted by CIS (Dixon‐Woods et al. 2006) was utilised in the development of the synthesis allowing for a more insightful interpretation of the evidence. An additional strength of this review was the integration of key principles from the ‘best‐fit’ framework approach. The a priori framework selected provided a solid base from which to develop the emerging theory. The result is a theoretical framework, developed from the literature, which positively contributes to policy, research and practice.

By presenting a framework that incorporates key factors for quality end‐of‐life care, guidance is provided regarding best practice. This sets a benchmark against which end‐of‐life care in practice can be measured, highlighting where gaps may occur and improvements are required. The literature identified some shortcomings, particularly in relation to training (Diaz et al. 2023; Grindrod and Rumbold 2017; Voss et al. 2021). These deficits need to be confirmed and others identified by exploring the extent to which everyday end‐of‐life care for people with intellectual disabilities aligns with the key principles set out in the theoretical framework. This will indicate where progress is needed and policies, research, and education are required to support the delivery of quality care.

As is clear from this review, most of the evidence in relation to end‐of‐life care for people with intellectual disabilities is from the perspective of healthcare providers. While some research involving people with intellectual disabilities as active participants has been conducted (Bekkema et al. 2016; Cithambaram 2021; Cithambaram et al. 2020; Diaz et al. 2023), there is a paucity of research exploring the experiences of this cohort who are themselves approaching end‐of‐life. The inclusion of this cohort in future research may provide a deeper understanding of the challenges experienced by this population and of their priorities and perspectives regarding what constitutes quality end‐of‐life care. Additionally, a more comprehensive research agenda, which includes families as bereaved caregivers, is required. It is unclear whether the current dearth of evidence is due to a lack of family involvement in end‐of‐life care, or whether this is an under‐explored area of research.

Limitations

As not all relevant literature was included in the synthesis, there is a possibility that a key component of the new framework may have been missed. However, by testing whether additional material yielded further insights into the framework, the risk of this occurring was reduced. Furthermore, the suggestion that CIS lacks transparency (Depraetere et al. 2021), particularly around the methods used to generate the synthesising argument, has been addressed by clearly outlining how these stages of the review were conducted. In addition, the contribution of each relevant paper in the generation of the synthesising argument has been clearly noted in the development of the synthesis.

Conclusion

The present work is a major contribution to the knowledge base around quality end‐of‐life care for people with intellectual disabilities, providing a more developed understanding of the requirements. It provides an opportunity to examine the adequacy of, and to build upon, current practice, thus improving understanding of where further work is needed in terms of policy, research, and education. This new theoretical framework unites a range of perspectives from a variety of sources, meeting the needs of a cross‐section of stakeholders. This development of a cohesive framework demonstrates that the value offered by this integrated structure exceeds the sum of its individual parts.

The development of the framework was facilitated by adopting a methodology which supported the inclusion of a theoretically rich sample of heterogeneous literature and allowed for a critical approach to existing evidence. This review has demonstrated how a novel approach can use research‐based evidence to develop a framework, supporting guidance to the implementation of best practice.

The new framework contains three new well‐defined themes—‘a personal approach to care’, ‘an involved network’ and ‘an enabling infrastructure’. This demonstrates that quality end‐of‐life care for people with intellectual disabilities is multi‐faceted and is broader than ensuring that dedicated, well‐trained professionals are in place. It requires the involvement of all caregivers, appropriately supported and working together in an enabling environment to provide personalised care according to the wishes of the individual.
