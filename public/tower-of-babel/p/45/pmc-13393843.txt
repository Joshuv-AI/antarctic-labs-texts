Non-invasive cerebral oxygenation and blood pressure neuromonitoring characterizes dynamic cerebrovascular autoregulation following cardiac arrest
Authors: Rachel Beekman, Christine Nguyen, Lily Pwint Thinzar, Mark Warman, Noah Kim, William McCarthy, Akhil Khosla, Shyoko Honiden, Dawn Cooper, Guido J. Falcone, Jessica Magid-Bernstein, Stefanie P. Cappucci, Jennifer A. Kim, Kevin N. Sheth, Nils H. Petersen, Emily J. Gilmore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and purposeCerebrovascular autoregulation (CVAR) is impaired following cardiac arrest. We describe near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS)-derived CVAR parameters following cardiac arrest and characterize their evolution over time.MethodsThis was a retrospective analysis of a prospectively collected clinical and physiological cardiac arrest data repository at a single academic medical center between March 2022 and October 2023. Eligible patients were connected to neuromonitoring within 72 h of cardiac arrest, had greater than four hours of monitoring, and had no confounding neurologic diagnosis. NIRS derived cerebral oxygenation index (COx) and limits of autoregulation were computed using the linear correlation between time-synchronized regional cerebral oxygenation (rSO2) and continuous mean arterial pressure (MAP). CVAR limits [inclusive of optimal MAP (MAPOPT), lower limit of autoregulation (LLA), and upper limit of autoregulation (ULA)] were determined using a fitted COx versus MAP parabolic curve with a COx threshold of 0.3.ResultsOf 60 eligible patients, 56 underwent neuromonitoring, and 55 were included in this analysis. Mean (standard deviation [SD]) age was 53.4 (15.4) years, and the majority had high severity of post-cardiac arrest syndrome. The median (interquartile range) time from cardiac arrest to first recorded COx was 14.2 (10.1–21.2) h; median monitoring duration was 49.1 (26.6–85.3) h. Mean (SD) MAPOPT and limits of autoregulation were 86.0 (13.1) mmHg and 74.6 (11.3)–96.8 (13.0) mmHg, respectively. The LLA was ≥65 mmHg in 85.5% (n = 47) of patients. All patients had at least one epoch of impaired or dysfunctional CVAR. Thirteen patients (23.6%) survived to hospital discharge. Survivors had a higher MAP (91.6 vs 82.7 mmHg, p = 0.039) and lower COx (0.05 vs 0.15, p = 0.041) than non-survivors.ConclusionImpaired CVAR was common and dynamic over at least 120 h following cardiac arrest. If a population-based MAP threshold of ≥65 mmHg were applied to our cohort, it may have resulted in significant time burden below the LLA. While it was feasible to determine CVAR limits, further work is needed to identify if CVAR-based treatment approaches influence outcomes.

Introduction

Hypoxic-ischemic brain injury (HIBI) is a common and often devastating consequence of cardiac arrest. Following return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), patients remain at risk for secondary brain injury, which may be modifiable. As the fundamental driver of brain perfusion, adequate blood pressure ensures that oxygen and glucose delivery match the metabolic demands of the injured brain. Hypotension, defined as MAP < 65 mmHg, following ROSC occurs in over two-thirds of patients and is associated with worse neurologic outcomes and a lower likelihood of survival.1, 2, 3, 4 Despite consensus on the importance of blood pressure monitoring in critically ill brain-injured patients,5, 6 considerable practice variability around optimal monitoring methods exists, and data-driven treatment thresholds are lacking. Advanced neuromonitoring tools, including near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS), can be used to identify personalized rather than population-based blood pressure thresholds. Emerging evidence in acute brain injury suggests that deviation from personalized blood pressure limits is associated with increased mortality and worse functional recovery.7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12

Cerebrovascular autoregulation (CVAR), the brain’s intrinsic ability to maintain stable blood flow despite changes in cerebral perfusion pressure, is impaired after cardiac arrest.1, 13, 14 A joint American Heart Association (AHA) and Neurocritical Care Society (NCS) consensus statement published in 2024 recommended targeting a mean arterial pressure (MAP) over 80 mmHg or a MAP at or near the predicted optimal MAP, when advanced cerebral monitoring is used.11 However, the most recent AHA guidelines in 2025 recommended targeting a MAP ≥ 65 mmHg.15 The discrepancy between expert panel consensus and guidelines, based on evidence GRADE, highlights that defining optimal blood pressure and how and when to use individualized monitoring in the post-arrest phase of care are critical, unanswered knowledge gaps.

While intact CVAR after cardiac arrest is associated with reduced mortality, optimal blood pressure targets in patients with intact or impaired autoregulation and their impact on outcome remain unknown.1, 13, 14, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23 Cardiac arrest patients are a heterogeneous population, and CVAR is a dynamic process. Amongst cardiac arrest survivors, hemodynamic augmentation to higher MAP thresholds has not resulted in improved outcomes.24, 25, 26, 27 The use of population-based thresholds rather than individualized targets may contribute to neutral trial results. Trials utilizing individualized goals have been small with variable methods, given that the optimal approach for determination of CVAR is unknown.28, 29

We performed a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected real-time NIRS-derived optimal blood pressure parameters after cardiac arrest. We characterize the evolution of CVAR limits and evaluate the frequency of CVAR dysfunction and impairment based on different cerebral oxygenation index (COx) thresholds.30

Methods

Study design and patients

This was a retrospective analysis of a prospectively collected clinical and physiological cardiac arrest repository at a single academic medical center. Eligible patients included comatose cardiac arrest survivors between March 2022 and October 2023 who had CVAR monitoring with both NIRS and arterial line acquired continuous blood pressure. Eligible patients were connected to neuromonitoring within 72-h of cardiac arrest, had greater than four hours of monitoring, and had no confounding neurologic diagnosis. Review of our quality database identified 60 patients with cardiac arrest admitted to an eligible intensive care unit during the 19-month timeframe. This study was approved by the Yale University Institutional Review Board (HIC 2000030242).

General management

Post-arrest management was guided by institutional protocols. At our institution, all cardiac arrest patients who achieve ROSC have a “chill alert”, which prompts immediate evaluation by neurocritical care and cardiology. The purpose of this alert is to expedite early post-resuscitation management, including decisions about temperature control, cardiac catheterization, and brain-centered hemodynamic goals. All patients who do not follow commands and do not have an exclusion for temperature control, such as hemodynamic instability requiring multiple pressors or active life-threatening bleeding, are treated with hypothermic targets ranging from 33°C to 36°C. Target temperature was selected based on local protocols, influenced by initial core temperature. Non-invasive continuous autoregulatory monitoring was performed in eligible patients admitted to the medical or neurological ICU. Due to technical limitations, this type of monitoring was not available in the cardiac ICU. All patients were followed by the neurosciences ICU consult team, familiar with the use of this data to guide clinical recommendations. CVAR monitoring is considered standard of care in acute ischemic stroke, subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), and traumatic brain injury (TBI) patients admitted to the Yale neurosciences ICU. CVAR data were analyzed in real-time by ICM+ software (Cambridge Enterprise, UK) and were available to the primary and consulting teams throughout the monitoring period, but interpretation and use were at the discretion of the clinical teams. Clinical neuromonitoring protocols outlined the populations who may benefit from monitoring but did not guide the frequency of assessment or recommended treatment thresholds. To incorporate these individualized goals, the clinical team had to review the bedside monitor (laptop with ICM+ software) and communicate a change in hemodynamic goals to the clinical team. The electronic health record was reviewed to identify neurocritical care recommended MAP targets, including nursing documentation to evaluate if these recommendations were enacted by the clinical team.

Ascertainment of CVAR

Regional cerebral oxygenation (rSO2) was assessed with NIRS using ForeSight tissue oximetry sensors (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine CA, United States) attached to the patient’s forehead, connected to the CASMED Fore-Sight Elite Monitor (formerly CAS Medical Systems, Branford CT, United States). Continuous invasive blood pressure was measured using an arterial line and imported into a laptop from the patient’s bedside monitor (Phillips Intellivue) and synchronized with the NIRS data using ICM+ software (Cambridge Enterprise, Cambridge, United Kingdom). The ICM+ software calculated and displayed cerebral oxygenation autoregulatory index (COx), limits of CVAR, and the optimal mean arterial pressure (MAPOPT) in real-time. In addition to collecting data on the mean COx, MAP, and MAPOPT, we also collected data on the median recording duration, time to first COx measurement, total hours COx recorded, proportion and absolute time with COx ≥ 0.3 threshold, proportion and absolute time a MAPOPT was calculated, proportion and absolute time above and below the limits of CVAR, area outside of the limits of CVAR, percent of time spent outside of CVAR-derived MAPOPT ± 5 mmHg, and mean (standard deviation) deviation of MAP from MAPOPT. Lastly, we assessed the area (mmHg × h) with MAP below CVAR by multiplying the amplitude of the deviation below LLA (mmHg) by the time spent below this threshold (h). Clinical data were collected and extracted in a standardized fashion from the Yale cardiac arrest data repository, as previously published.31, 32

Covariates

Variables extracted from the electronic health record included: demographics, medical history, details of the arrest (location of arrest, witnessed arrest, bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), initial non-perfusing rhythm, post-arrest variables (initial core temperature, temperature control use, initial lactate, initial pH, vasopressor doses, neurologic examination), and clinical outcomes (withdrawal of life sustaining therapy (WLST), time from cardiac arrest to WLST, survival to hospital discharge, Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) score33 at hospital discharge, and death by neurologic criteria (DNC)). If a variable was not documented, it was entered as not available. Age-adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was used to classify premorbid medical status.34, 35 The Full Outline of UnResponsiveness (FOUR) score was used to characterize the immediate post-ROSC neurologic examination.36 Pittsburgh Cardiac Arrest Category (PCAC) and revised post-cardiac arrest syndrome for therapeutic hypothermia (rCAST) scores were used to characterize post-cardiac arrest syndrome severity.36, 37, 38 In all patients, we assessed the use of vasopressors during the recording duration and the peak simultaneous vasopressor dose in norepinephrine equivalents (NEE)39 within the first 24 h from cardiac arrest.

Outcomes

While this was a descriptive analysis, the survival to hospital discharge and Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) score were used to characterize groups. The CPC is a 5-point category ordinal score where 1 represents good cerebral performance (able to work) and 5 represents death, inclusive of DNC.40 CPC was ascertained through chart review of speech pathology, occupational therapy, and physical therapy notes by a blinded investigator (RB) as previously described.31, 36, 41

Data processing

Given the global nature of HIBI, the average rSO2 values between right and left hemispheres were used.42 COx was calculated as a moving Pearson correlation coefficient of 30 consecutive, ten-second averaged values of MAP and rSO2. MAP values were divided into bins of five mmHg, for which each corresponding COx was averaged. COx was computed using a rolling window of datapoints that updated every ten seconds to generate a continuous time-trend of CVAR. Mean COx values were derived for each patient on day 1–3 post-CA. Although a COx threshold for CVAR dysfunction has not been established in cardiac arrest patients, we used a COx value of 0.3 to define CVAR limits, consistent with prior publications.43, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48 COx ranges from −1 to +1 with higher values reflecting increased correlation between cerebral blood flow and MAP; this pressure passive cerebral circulation indicates a loss of CVAR. To understand degrees of CVAR, we defined CVAR as maintained if the COx value was ≤0, impaired if the COx value was >0 but <0.3, and dysfunctional if the COx value was ≥0.3. We graphically represented changes in CVAR over time using two-hours windows, where COx threshold was achieved for more than half of the recording period.30 Two-hour windows were selected as shorter windows provide dynamic information that has better correlation with CVAR indices.49, 50 A parabolic curve was fitted to a MAP-COx plot, with its nadir denoting the MAP value with the lowest associated COx value (MAPOPT). The limits of CVAR were determined by identifying the MAP intersecting a COx threshold of 0.3. The lower limit of autoregulation (LLA) was defined as the MAP value corresponding to where the fitted COx versus MAP parabolic curve crossed a COx value of 0.3 at the lower bounds, while the upper limit of autoregulation (ULA) was defined by the upper bounds.16, 20 A continuous time trend of MAPOPT and the limits of autoregulation were generated using a moving, multi-window exponentially weighted algorithm that recalculated values every minute.43, 51 Values were computed every minute and averaged for the recording; we compared each patient’s mean (SD) MAP to their CVAR-derived MAPOPT over the recording duration.

Data cleaning

The ICM+ software was used to monitor, analyze, and store CVAR and invasive continuous blood pressure data. The waveform data was first manually cleaned for artifact removal, followed by automated removal of blunted or flat ABP waveforms with pulse pressure <20 mmHg, and down-sampling to 10-s averages of data. The cleaned data was summarized into a usable format for analysis using Python version 3.8.10 (2021).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics and outcomes were reported as either n (%), mean (SD), or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Normally-distributed continuous variables were compared using a t-test. Categorical variables were compared using a chi-squared test with continuity correction. Ordinal variables or non-normal continuous variables were compared using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant. For secondary analyses, we stratified into three discrete time periods: Day 1 (0–24 h), Day 2 (24–48 h) and Day 3 (48–72 h) post-ROSC. We created figures using a continuous time series to help visualize trends across the full recording duration. The difference between patients’ actual MAP and MAPOPT was calculated as ΔMAPOPT, defined as MAP-MAPOPT at each minute, averaged for the recording duration. A scatterplot with a linear regression line was generated to visualize the relationship between patient temperature and COx. Analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1 (2023).

Results

Patient characteristics

We identified 56 patients with available neuromonitoring data, of which 55 were included in our analysis, Fig. 1. The mean (SD) age was 53.4 (15.4) years. Of the 55 patients, 35 (63.6%) were male and 27 (49.1%) were white. The median (IQR) CCI was 2 (1–6). Most of the cohort (n = 43, 78.2%) experienced out-of-hospital CA, had a non-shockable rhythm (n = 46, 83.6%), and a witnessed arrest (n = 39, 70.9%). Few patients had bystander CPR performed (n = 14, 25.5%). Post-cardiac arrest syndrome severity scores were high with a median (IQR) Pittsburgh Cardiac Arrest Category (PCAC) score of 4 (3–4) and revised post-Cardiac Arrest Syndrome for Therapeutic hypothermia (rCAST) score of 10.5 (8.0–14.5). Most patients were treated with temperature control (n = 53, 96.4%). The mean (SD) initial lactate was 9.5 (5.1) mmol/L and the mean (SD) initial pH was 7.22 (0.17). Vasopressor use was common, occurring in 60.0% (n = 33) of the cohort (Table 1). Amongst the cohort, 16 (29%) patients had individualized MAP thresholds specified in neurocritical care notes. In all cases, neurocritical care notes were consistent with nursing orders. Of those with individualized targets, the recommended lower limit of autoregulation ranged from 70 mmHg to 100 mmHg. Characterization of CVAR

Out of the 41,787 5-min averaged COx measurements, 8366 (20.0%) were above a COx threshold of 0.3. The median (IQR) time from cardiac arrest until the first measurement of COx was 14.2 (10.1–21.2) h. The median (IQR) duration of monitoring was 49.1 (26.6–85.3) h. MAPOPT was identified in all patients, for a median (IQR) of 82.7 (62.4–93.7)% of the recording duration. For most patients (n = 40, 72.7%), the MAPOPT and limits of CVAR were calculated on the first day of monitoring. The remaining patients all had limits of CVAR calculated before 73.5 h. The duration of recording for each patient is shown in Supplement Fig. 1.

Mean (SD) MAP and MAPOPT were significantly higher than the population-based threshold of 65 mmHg [83.1 (11.7) mmHg and 86.0 (13.1) mmHg, respectively]. Likewise, the LLA was on average 10-points higher than that recommended by AHA guidelines [74.6 (11.3) mmHg]. MAPOPT was dynamic through the recording duration (Fig. 2A). Most patients had a mean MAPOPT ≥ 75 mmHg (n = 43, 78.2%) and half had a mean MAPOPT ≥ 85 mmHg (n = 27, 49.1%). The LLA was ≥65 mmHg in 85.5% (n = 47) patients. The limits of CVAR over three days and beyond are shown in Fig. 2B. The median (IQR) time spent with LLA above 65 mmHg was 84.9 (68.5–96.4)% of the recording duration. Most patients were maintained within their calculated limits of CVAR, spending a median (IQR) of 25.6 (14.1–43.2)% of the recorded duration outside of these limits. This time was divided equally above [10.1 (6.2–15.1)%)] and below [10.8 (6.3–23.0)%] the limits of CVAR. Time spent with COx ≥ 0.3 was variable, with few (n = 9, 16.4%) patients spending ≥50% of the recording above these thresholds (Supplement Fig. 2).

Trend of CVAR in cardiac arrest patients

The CVAR data in survivors and non-survivors for the first 120 h are shown in Table 2. The MAP was higher amongst survivors (91.6 vs 82.7 mmHg, p = 0.039) and the COx was lower amongst survivors (0.05 vs 0.15, p = 0.041). There were no statistical differences in MAPOPT, ΔMAPOPT, or time spent outside CVAR, however, we were not adequately powered for these exploratory analyses (Table 2). Discussion

Our study demonstrates the feasibility of successfully obtaining and integrating dynamic individualized blood pressure targets using continuous CVAR monitoring into real-time clinical care of cardiac arrest patients. We were able to identify CVAR in all patients. The LLA was well above the population-based threshold of MAP ≥ 65 mmHg. Importantly, limits of CVAR were dynamic over the first 120 h following ROSC. While our findings are hypothesis generating, they suggest that targeting MAP of 65 mmHg15 may result in a significant portion of time outside of the limits of autoregulation, especially beyond day two. While these findings support the AHA/NCS consensus statement for CVAR monitoring in patients resuscitated from cardiac arrest,6 further work is needed to identify if MAPopt guided therapy improves neurologic outcome.

Our results are consistent with prior publications suggesting that CVAR may be right shifted following cardiac arrest, necessitating higher blood pressure targets to sustain cerebral blood flow and brain perfusion.14, 19 However, multiple prospective trials of higher blood pressure targets following cardiac arrest have failed to improve outcomes.25, 26, 27, 52 Possible reasons for this discrepancy include (1) the use of population-based rather than individualized thresholds; (2) a focus on blood pressure augmentation primarily during the initial 48 h following ROSC; and (3) methodological differences, such as the use of mean values rather than the percentage of time or area above or below a target value. In a post-hoc analysis of the COMACARE study, patients with poor neurologic outcome had impaired CVAR overall, however this finding did not apply to the 12–24-h period following ROSC.16 Given the known trajectory of cerebral edema after HIBI, it makes sense that optimal blood pressure would rise from day 2–5; this has implications on the optimal time period for hemodynamic augmentation.53 Future studies should extend the time window for augmentation beyond the initial 48 h.

Importantly, our data suggests that all patients have periods of impaired CVAR, which may place them at risk for secondary brain injury. We cannot treat CVAR as an all or none phenomenon and need to identify disease specific thresholds and evaluate the impact of time spent in critical ranges. Our feasibility study contributes to the emerging resuscitation literature supporting the use of real-time bedside monitoring of moving correlation coefficients to identify and characterize clinically useful and dynamically changing individualized hemodynamic goals.

Limitations

Given the global nature of HIBI, we averaged NIRS signals from both hemispheres, consistent with prior analyses,42 however it is possible that this approach contributed to error. Our study highlights methodological challenges of identifying individualized blood pressure targets in cardiac arrest patients. Unlike previous studies in acute ischemic stroke, the limits of CVAR and MAPOPT were calculated for a shorter duration.8 Post-cardiac arrest syndrome and shock may result in decreased blood pressure variability. As the evaluation of CVAR is dependent on the range of blood pressure observations, use of vasopressors may result in delay to identification of CVAR limits.

While we provide a descriptive analysis of patients with CVAR monitoring following cardiac arrest, generalizability may be limited given the high severity of post-cardiac arrest syndrome and low occurrence of cardiac or shockable rhythm arrest. As we did not monitor patients in the cardiac ICU, our data underrepresented Utstein cardiac arrest survival, with higher mortality rates and lower CPC 1–2 outcomes. This is important, as our results may not be applicable to cohorts with lower post-cardiac arrest illness severity.

Most importantly, interpretation of CVAR data was not protocolized, and as such, we cannot verify how often CVAR data was reviewed and if MAP recommendations were adjusted based on monitoring data. While we did perform a chart review, identifying that approximately 30% of the cohort had individualized MAP recommendations, we do not know if these recommendations were static or dynamic or how frequently they were updated. Despite the use of a population-based threshold for the majority of the cohort (N = 39), MAP was frequently titrated above this parameter. It is possible that verbal recommendations to optimize blood pressure targets influenced nursing titration of vasopressors, despite a standard order for MAP ≥ 65 mmHg. Additionally, while our standard neurocritical recommendations focus on brain-centered resuscitation goals including partial pressure of carbon dioxide 35–45 mmHg and partial pressure of oxygen 80–150 mmHg, we did not review changes in ventilatory parameters which can impact CVAR.

Lastly, cardiac arrest patients represent a population with unique physiology, which can be characterized by diffusion limitation, potentially impacting NIRS-based CVAR parameters.28, 54, 55 A COx threshold of 0.3 has been used to characterize the upper and lower limits of CVAR across multiple disease states43, 44, 45, 46 and is generally accepted across the literature, however, it is adopted from preclinical studies and clinical studies in cardiopulmonary bypass.47, 56, 57 A preclinical study aimed at detecting loss of autoregulation during hypotension in piglets found that COx had a sensitivity of 92% and specificity of 63% for pressure passive cerebral blood flow when the COx was ≥0.36.58 Physiologic studies defining COx thresholds for pressure passive cerebral blood flow have not been performed in cardiac arrest patients.

Conclusion

Our study supports prior cardiac arrest literature in which CVAR impairment is common and dynamic over five days following ROSC. If a population-based MAP threshold of ≥65 mmHg were applied to our cohort, it may have resulted in significant time burden below the LLA. Further work is necessary to validate COx thresholds in cardiac arrest patients and identify the impact of time outside of individualized CVAR with outcomes. Advancing precision perfusion in resuscitation science holds incredible promise as the next step towards defining salvageable phenotypes and improving outcomes for cardiac arrest survivors.
