Connection of smoking with risks of dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension among adults in Northeast China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Qian Zhao, Liquan Deng, Jikang Shi, Yong Li, Yi Cheng, Yawen Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe high smoking prevalence and growing burden of chronic metabolic diseases have been threatening public health in Northeast China. This study aimed to investigate the association of smoking/quitting status with hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia in residents in Northeast China.MethodsA total of 78,674 adults aged 35–75 years old were enrolled from a cross-sectional study conducted in Northeast China in 2020. Demographic data were collected, and biochemical indices and anthropometric indices were measured. Associations of smoking/quitting status with hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia were investigated using logistic regression. Restricted cubic spline analysis was performed to assess the dose-response relationship between smoking/quitting status and prevalence of dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension.ResultsA total of 16,401 (20.85%) smokers, 25,805 (32.80%) adults with dyslipidemia, 16,812 (21.37%) adults with diabetes, and 33,539 (42.63%) adults with hypertension were identified in this study. The risk of diabetes, dyslipidemia, and hypertension in current heavy smokers and former heavy smokers were higher than that in never-smokers (all P < 0.001). The risk of dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension elevated with the increase of cigarette amounts from “1–5 cigarettes per day” to “≥31 cigarettes per day” and smoking duration from “1–18 years” to “19–31 years”. However, excluding quitting smoking for 2–3 years (OR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.01–1.67, P = 0.041) and 7–20 years (OR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.12–1.83, P = 0.004) enhancing diabetes risk, quitting duration was not associated with the risk of dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension (all P > 0.05).ConclusionSmoking status, smoking amount, and smoking duration, rather than quitting duration, are associated with the risk of dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension among adults in Northeast China.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26700-1.

Introduction

There are 1.245 billion current tobacco users aged 15 years or older in the world [1]. Globally, China is the largest tobacco producer. Moreover, China has approximately 350 million smokers, making it the largest tobacco consumer in the world and leading to nearly 700 million non-smokers exposed to second-hand smoke [2]. In 2018, the prevalence of smoking among Chinese adults aged 18–69 years exceeded 25% [3, 4], with a significant sex disparity (47.6% among males versus 1.9% among females) [5]. In addition, cigarette consumption in China reached 2.5 million metric tons in 2013, exceeding the combined total of the following 40 highest-consuming countries [6]. The high prevalence of tobacco consumption elevates its health and financial harms to the scale of a major societal burden, affecting the entire population rather than only individual smokers.

Tobacco consumption leads to one million deaths in China in 2010. Among Chinese males aged 40–79 years, approximately one-fifth of all deaths were attributable to tobacco consumption. Of note, tobacco-attributable male deaths were substantially higher in urban areas than those in rural areas [7]. Furthermore, tobacco-attributable deaths are projected to increase from approximately 1 million in 2010, to 2 million in 2030, and to 3 million in 2050 [7]. Death from tobacco use is primarily mediated by chronic diseases, including cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and various cancers [8].

The high prevalences of hypertension (27.5% [9]), diabetes (10.9% [10]), and dyslipidemia (33.8% [11]) in China make them as major risk factors for CVD. Cigarette smoking is a well-recognized modifiable risk factor for the three diseases [12–14]. Notably, Northeast China is characterized by a persistently high smoking prevalence and a regional concentration of smoking-related risks, with Jilin Province’s smoking rate reaching 31.8% [15]. This region exceeds national averages in active smoking [15]. Furthermore, the cultural linkage between tobacco and alcohol consumption in Northeast China elevates the local smoking prevalence [16]. Thus, we investigated the association of smoking/quitting status with hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia among residents in Northeast China in this study.

Methods

Study population

We performed this cross-sectional study on the basis of data from the Project on Early Screening and Comprehensive Intervention for Population at High Risk of Cardiovascular Disease in Jilin Province in 2020. A total of 62 representative community health centers/hospitals were selected from the ten cities/prefectures (Changchun, Jilin, Siping, Liaoyuan, Tonghua, Baishan, Songyuan, Baicheng, Meihekou, and Yanbian Korean Autonomous Prefecture) in Jilin province according to population proportion, ethnicity, location, and economic development level. We collected data from residents born between January 1, 1945, and December 31, 1985, who had lived at least six months in the above cities/prefectures, identifying 78,674 residents with known smoking status from 81,005 residents with complete data. The response rate was 97.12%.

Questionnaire survey and health examination

A questionnaire was developed to collect information on demographic characteristics (age, sex, ethnicity, education, occupation, household income [unit: Chinese Yuan, CNY] , and marital status), health status (dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension), and smoking status (Supplementary material 1). After an overnight fast, capillary blood samples were collected from the fingertips of residents to measure fasting blood glucose (FBG) and lipid indices (total cholesterol [TC]; high density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C]; low density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C]); and triglycerides [TG]). Seated blood pressure (BP) was measured using a standardized upper-arm blood pressure monitor after a five-minute rest. A height and weight scale was employed to measure participants’ height and weight. Obesity, overweight, normal, and underweight statuses were determined using the body mass index (BMI) standard according to the National Health and Family Planning Commission of China [17].

Definition of smoking

A never-smoker was defined as a resident who had smoked less than 100 cigarettes ever. A current smoker was defined as a resident who had smoked in the last 30 days, and a former smoker was defined as a resident who had quit smoking for at least one month. A heavy smoker was defined as a resident who had smoked at least four days a week, and a light smoker was defined as a resident who had smoked less than three days a week.

Definition of dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension

Dyslipidemia was defined on the basis of the Chinese Guidelines on the Prevention and Treatment of Dyslipidemia in Adults (2007): TG ≥ 2.26mmol/L (200 mg/dL); TC ≥ 6.22mmol/L (240 mg/dL); LDL-C ≥ 4.14mmol/L (160 mg/dL); or HDL-C < 1.04mmol/L (40 mg/dL) [18]. Diabetes was defined according to the Chinese Guidelines on the Prevention and Treatment of Type 2 Diabetes (2017): FBG ≥ 7.0 mmol/l [19]. Hypertension was defined on the basis of the Chinese Guidelines on the Prevention and Treatment of Hypertension (2018): systolic BP (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic BP (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg [20]. In addition, residents who had been diagnosed by a registered medical practitioner or had taken medication for these diseases within two weeks were considered as patients with dyslipidemia, diabetes, or hypertension.

Statistical analysis

The normality of continuous variables was examined using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed data were displayed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and abnormally distributed data were given as median (25th, 75th percentiles). Student’s t -test was used to identify differences in the prevalence of smoking or chronic diseases (dyslipidemia, diabetes, or hypertension) between age groups. Chi-square (χ²) tests were used to examine differences in the prevalence of smoking and of chronic diseases (dyslipidemia, diabetes, or hypertension) by sex, ethnicity, education, occupation, income, marital status, and BMI categories. Logistic regression was utilized to analyze the odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension according to smoking status. Adjusted ORs were calculated by controlling for variables such as sex, age, ethnicity, marital status, education, occupation, income, and BMI. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was performed to assess the dose-response relationship between smoking status (smoking amount, smoking duration, and quitting time) and the prevalence of dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension. All analyses in this study were performed using R software (version 4.1.2), and P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 78,674 residents aged 56.20 ± 9.75 were enrolled in this study (31,793 [40.41%] males and 46,881 [59.59%] females). We found that 16,401 (20.85%) residents were smokers. There were significant differences in demographic characteristics (age, sex, ethnicity, education, occupation, income, and marital status) and health status (dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension) between smokers and never-smokers (all P < 0.001)(Table 1). The prevalence of smoking in males was significantly higher than that in females (40.02% versus 7.85%, P < 0.001). Moreover, smokers were more commonly found among residents with primary and junior high school education or those engaged in agriculture, forestry, and fishing (P < 0.001) (Table 1). Notably, compared with never-smokers, smokers were predisposed to dyslipidemia, diabetes, or hypertension (all P < 0.001) (Table 1).

General characteristics of chronic metabolic diseases among participants

Association of smoking status with dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension

Compared with never-smokers, current heavy smokers, former light smokers, and former heavy smokers had significantly high diabetes and hypertension risk (for diabetes: OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.05–1.15, P < 0.001; OR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.11–1.92, P = 0.006; OR = 1.71, 95% CI: 1.55–1.90, P < 0.001; for hypertension: OR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.28–1.38, P < 0.001; OR = 1.40, 95% CI: 1.10–1.79, P = 0.007; OR = 1.78, 95% CI: 1.62–1.96, P < 0.001) correspondingly. Additionally, compared with never-smokers, current heavy smokers, current light smokers, and former heavy smokers had significantly high dyslipidemia risk (OR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.26–1.37, P < 0.001; OR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.09–1.42, P = 0.001; OR = 1.47, 95% CI: 1.34–1.62, P < 0.001) correspondingly. After adjusting for potential confounders, smoking status remained an independent risk factor for diabetes, dyslipidemia, and hypertension (for diabetes: current heavy smokers OR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.08–1.20, P < 0.001; former heavy smokers OR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.25–1.54, P < 0.001; for dyslipidemia: current heavy smokers OR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.26–1.38, P < 0.001; former heavy smokers OR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.17–1.43, P < 0.001; for hypertension: current heavy smokers OR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.32–1.44, P < 0.001; former heavy smokers OR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.22–1.50, P < 0.001) (Table 3; Fig. 1A, B and C).

Association of smoking amount with dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension

The risk of dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension increased with the increase in cigarette amounts from “1–5 cigarettes per day” to “≥31 cigarettes per day” after adjusting for potential confounders (“1–5 cigarettes per day” versus “≥31 cigarettes per day”: for diabetes, [OR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.00–1.34 versus OR = 1.61, 95% CI: 1.32–1.95]; for hypertension, [OR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.13–1.46 versus OR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.27–1.82]); for dyslipidemia, [OR = 1.05, 95% CI: 0.92–1.20 versus OR = 1.81, 95% CI: 1.51–2.15]) (Table 3; Fig. 1D, E and F). Multivariable-adjusted spline regression model showed a non-linear dose-response association of smoking amount with dyslipidemia (P for non-linearity = 0.001), diabetes (P for non-linearity < 0.001), and hypertension (P for non-linearity = 0.007) after controlling for potential confounders (Fig. 2D, E and F).

Association of smoking duration with dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension

The risk of dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension increased with the increase in smoking duration from “1–18 years” to “19–31 years” after adjusting for potential confounders (“1–18 years” versus “19–31 years”: for diabetes, [OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 0.91–1.31 versus OR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.25–1.47]; for hypertension, [OR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.15–1.52 versus. OR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.34–1.53]); for dyslipidemia, [OR = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.26–1.64) versus. OR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.50–1.70]). However, the risk of dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension decreased with the increase in smoking duration from “32–40 years” to “≥52 years” after adjusting for potential confounders (“32–40 years” versus “41–51 years” versus “≥52 years”: for diabetes, [OR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.25–1.44 versus OR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.89–1.03 versus OR = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.63–0.91]; for hypertension, [OR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.33–1.50 versus OR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.15–1.31 versus OR = 1.12, 95% CI: 0.95–1.32]); for dyslipidemia, [OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.19–1.35 versus OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.01–1.15 versus OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.82–1.16]) (Table 3; Fig. 1G, H and I). Multivariable-adjusted spline regression model showed a non-linear dose-response association of smoking duration with dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension after controlling for potential confounders (all P for non-linearity < 0.001) (Fig. 2A, B and C).

Association of quitting duration with dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension

Excluding quitting duration for 2–3 years (OR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.01–1.67, P = 0.041) and 7–20 years(OR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.12–1.83, P = 0.004) which enhanced diabetes risk, quitting duration was not associated with the risk of dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension after adjusting for potential confounders (all P > 0.05) (Table 3; Fig. 1J, K and L). We also found no non-linear dose-response association of quitting duration with dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension using multivariable-adjusted spline regression model after controlling for those potential confounders (all P > 0.05) (Fig. 2G, H and I).

Discussion

Risks of CVD and diabetes are high in Northeast China [21, 22]. In this study, we documented that smoking status, smoking amount, and smoking duration, rather than quitting duration, were associated with the risks of dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension among adults in Northeast China. Our findings are particularly relevant to regions with high smoking prevalence and similar socioeconomic or cultural contexts, supporting the generalizability of smoking-CVD risk associations across China, as the biological mechanisms linking smoking with metabolic and cardiovascular abnormalities are consistent nationwide.

Chronic diseases and lifestyles exhibit a geography-related distribution pattern [21, 23]. The habits of residents in Northeast China differ remarkably from those in other areas of China: the long, harsh winter and monotonous leisure activities lead to a high prevalence of smoking in this population. In 2012, we conducted the Project on Present Situation and Change Forecast of Disease Spectrum, revealing a smoking prevalence of 30.8% among adults aged 18–79 years in Northeast China [12]. In this study, we found that the prevalence of smoking in adults aged 35–75 years was 20.8% in this region in 2020. In particular, China’s family planning policy has led to a sharp decline in the young population, especially in Northeast China. Therefore, the prevalence of smoking in adults tended to be reduced from 2012 to 2020 in Northeast China.

Similarly, Mei Zhang et al. revealed that the prevalence of smoking in adults aged 18–69 years in China was estimated at 26.7% in 2018 [3]. Zhang Guoting et al. also found a declining trend of smoking prevalence in China from 30.30% in 2010 to 28.69% in 2018, indicating that the Chinese government has made effective tobacco control measures over the years [24].

Demographic features identified in this study are similar to those in previous studies [24–26]. Men have a higher smoking rate than women. Smoking was more prevalent among individuals with primary and junior high school level or those engaged in agriculture, forestry, and fishing. The low rate of smoking in people with high education may be associated with their health awareness and health-related behaviors.

There is an association between smoking and dyslipidemia [27–29]. In this study, we found that, compared with never-smokers, current heavy smokers, current light smokers, and former heavy smokers had significantly high dyslipidemia risk. This result is consistent with the findings of Myung HaLee et al. [30]. W Y Craig et al. [31] and D S Freedman et al. [32] found that the effects of smoking amount on dyslipidemia are dose-dependent. We confirmed the results of W Y Craig et al. [31] and D S Freedman et al. [32]: the risk of dyslipidemia increases with the increase of smoking amount before and after adjusting for confounding variables on the basis of a population in 2012 [12] and that in 2020 which was investigated in this paper. In fact, no association between quitting duration and dyslipidemia was found after adjusting for confounders in this study; however, we previously found that quitting duration (> 6 years) is necessary for reducing dyslipidemia risk [12]. Indeed, quitting smoking for a short period is associated with adverse health consequences (weight gain, impaired glucose metabolism, and metabolic syndrome), but these adverse effects tend to be mitigated after several years of non-smoking [33, 34]. Moreover, levels of TC, TG, LDL-C, and HDL-C increase after smoking cessation [35].

Smoking is also a well-recognized risk factor for diabetes [36, 37]. Shamima Akter et al. [38] and Akiko Morimoto et al. [39] found that current and former smokers have an increased risk of diabetes compared with non-smokers. Similarly, our results also showed that, compared with never-smokers, current heavy smokers and former heavy smokers had a significantly high diabetes risk after adjusting for the confounding variables. In this study, we corroborated that smoking amount is positively associated with diabetes risk [37]. For smoking cessation, AkikoMorimoto et al. [40] conducted a 9.2-year cohort study including 2070 men aged 40–69 years without diabetes, finding that the risk for diabetes peaks within 3 years of quitting and persists at 5 years post-cessation among overweight individuals. AkikoMorimoto et al. [40] also identified an elevated diabetes risk among former smokers who had quit > 9 years, regardless of normal or overweight status. Subsequent post-smoking-cessation weight gain may offset some beneficial effects of smoking cessation and lead to smoking resumption. Weight gain can temporarily exacerbate diabetes and impair glycemic control and metabolic profiles [41]. Similarly, we found that quitting durations of 2–3 years and 7–20 years were associated with an increased diabetes risk even adjusting for BMI. The results of our cross-sectional study suggest that weight gain alone may not fully explain the elevated risk; other factors such as long-term smoking-induced pancreatic β-cell dysfunction, residual metabolic memory of insulin resistance, or unmeasured confounders, such as dietary patterns, physical activity changes post-cessation, may contribute [42–44]. Notably, the meta-analysis of 88 prospective studies [43] reported that diabetes risk gradually decreases with smoking cessation duration (new quitters < 5 years: RR = 1.54, long-term quitters ≥ 10 years: RR = 1.11) without a fluctuating elevation. The discrepancies between this meta-analysis and our findings may be attributed to distinct study designs (cross-sectional versus prospective), different stratification of cessation duration, variations in population characteristics, and confounding factor adjustment. We further found that smoking durations of 19–31 years and 32–40 years were associated with an increases diabetes risk after adjusting for confounding variables (OR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.25–1.47; OR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.25–1.44). Clinically, this discovery implies that smoking cessation programs should not only focus on weight management but also include long-term metabolic monitoring, especially for former heavy smokers with an extended smoking history.

Smoking is also implicated in hypertension [45–47]. Robert C. Kaplan et al. [47] conducted a 6-year follow-up study of US Hispanic adults aged 18–76 years, finding that current smokers appear to have an increased risk of hypertension. Sung Hoon Kim et al. [48] performed a cross-sectional study of 25,150 Korean adults aged ≥ 29 years, identifying that cigarette smoking is modestly associated with an increased risk of hypertension in women. In this study, we found that, compared with never-smokers, current heavy smokers and former heavy smokers had a significantly high hypertension risk. Robert C. Kaplan et al. [47] also found that a relatively modest cumulative dose of smoking—greater than 5 pack-years of exposure—raises the risk of hypertension by 30% or more; interestingly, the risk of hypertension did not increase further with higher pack-year levels. However, we validated that there was the statistically significant positive association between smoking amount and the risk of hypertension in this study. This discrepancy may be due to the lack of a consensus on the definition of smoking amount. Robert C. Kaplan et al. [47] categorized smoking amount by lifetime pack-years of smoking exposure; by contrast, we categorized smoking amount by daily cigarettes consumption. Because the associations between smoking duration and hypertension has been less investigated, we documented a non-linear association between these two factors, with elevated hypertension risk observed across multiple duration strata (1–18 years, 19–31 years, 32–40 years, and 41–51 years). This may reflect cumulative vascular damage from smoking: short-to-moderate durations initiate endothelial dysfunction, while longer durations lead to irreversible arterial stiffness or renin-angiotensin system activation, thereby contributing to sustained hypertension risk [49, 50].

Epidemiological studies have revealed associations of smoking with the risks of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Mechanistic investigations have provided evidence to underpin these associations. Cigarette smoking induces cardiovascular mitochondrial oxidative stress, thereby triggering endothelial dysfunction and hypertension [45]. Smoking acutely impairs glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity, leading to the development of diabetes [51]. Moreover, cigarette smoking enhances lipid peroxidation, leading to dyslipidemic dysregulation of lipid metabolism and subsequent alterations in serum lipoprotein profiles [52, 53]. These mechanistic studies confirm a causal link of cigarette smoking with the risks of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia.

In this study, we explored the association of smoking/quitting status with the risks of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia among residents in Northeast China using large-scale, representative data from Jilin Province. This study also has several limitations. First, recall bias associated with self-reported data constitutes an inherent limitation. Second, it is difficult to infer causal relationships in this cross-sectional study design; thus, subsequent longitudinal cohort studies are warranted.

In conclusion, smoking status, smoking amount, and smoking duration, rather than quitting duration, are associated with the risks of dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension among adults in Northeast China. This study provides data to inform tobacco control policies in Northeast China. Furthermore, longitudinal cohort studies involving multiple follow-up visits are necessary to better evaluate the effects of temporal trajectories in smoking status on the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidemia.
