Macrophage NLRP3 activation and IL-1β release drive osimertinib-induced antitumor immunity
Authors: Haiyang Yu, Xin Sun, Yan Li, Jing Pan, Xuemei Liu, Hongbin He, Haibo Wu, Yubei Sun, Yueyin Pan, Xiaojun Qian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the clinical efficacy of epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs) in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), patient outcomes vary even among those with identical EGFR mutations. This study investigates whether osimertinib, a third-generation EGFR-TKI, activates the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor protein-3 (NLRP3) inflammasome in macrophages to drive antitumor immunity and explores its mechanistic basis.MethodsUsing bone marrow-derived macrophages from wild-type and gene-deficient mice, human peripheral blood mononuclear cells, and a Lewis lung cancer murine model, we assessed osimertinib-induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation, interleukin (IL)-1β secretion, pyroptosis, and tumor microenvironment (TME) remodeling. Mechanistic studies evaluated lysosomal dysfunction, calcium overload, mitochondrial damage, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. Clinical correlations were analyzed in patients with NSCLC treated with EGFR-TKIs.ResultsOsimertinib triggered NLRP3 inflammasome activation in macrophages via lysosomal dysfunction-induced calcium overload, leading to mitochondrial damage and ROS production, which acted as damage-associated molecular patterns to activate NLRP3. This process promoted IL-1β release, pyroptosis, and CD8+ T-cell activation while suppressing regulatory T cells in the TME. In murine models, osimertinib’s antitumor effects were abrogated by NLRP3 inhibition (MCC950) and enhanced by recombinant IL-1β (rIL-1β) co-administration (p<0.01). Clinically, high NLRP3 and IL-1β expression in tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) correlated with prolonged progression-free survival (p<0.01) and overall survival (p<0.01) in EGFR-TKI-treated patients with NSCLC.ConclusionsOsimertinib exerts off-target immunomodulatory effects by activating the tumor-extrinsic NLRP3 inflammasome, linking mitochondrial-lysosomal crosstalk to antitumor immunity. NLRP3 and IL-1β in TAMs emerge as predictive biomarkers for EGFR-TKI efficacy, while rIL-1β combination therapy represents a novel strategy to enhance clinical outcomes.Keywords: Lung Cancer, Macrophage, Immune modulatory, Cytokine, Tumor microenvironment - TME

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs), including third-generation agents like osimertinib, are standard therapies for patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) with activating EGFR mutations. However, clinical outcomes remain heterogeneous even among patients with identical EGFR mutations, suggesting the involvement of non-canonical mechanisms. Prior studies established that certain TKIs (eg, gefitinib, imatinib) activate the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor protein-3(NLRP3) inflammasome in macrophages, potentially contributing to adverse inflammatory effects. Beyond TKIs, other antitumor therapies (eg, chemotherapy, radiotherapy) are known to activate NLRP3 in immune cells, triggering interleukin (IL)-1β-driven antitumor immunity. NLRP3 activation in myeloid cells, such as dendritic cells or macrophages, enhances CD8+ T-cell responses, while in immunosuppressive myeloid-derived suppressor cells, it may paradoxically dampen immunity.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Despite these insights, the role of NLRP3 in TKI efficacy and its mechanistic basis in reshaping the tumor microenvironment (TME) remained unexplored. This study elucidates a novel off-target immunomodulatory mechanism of osimertinib, linking lysosomal-mitochondrial crosstalk to NLRP3 inflammasome activation and antitumor immunity. Osimertinib induces lysosomal dysfunction in macrophages, triggering transient receptor potential mucolipin 1-mediated calcium efflux, mitochondrial damage, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. These ROS act as damage-associated molecular patterns to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, driving caspase-1-dependent IL-1β secretion, pyroptosis, and CD8+ T-cell activation while suppressing regulatory T cells in the TME. High NLRP3 and IL-1β expression in tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) correlates with prolonged progression-free survival and overall survival in patients with EGFR-mutant NSCLC treated with osimertinib, establishing these markers as predictive biomarkers.

Introduction

Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) are widely used in patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) with driver gene mutations.1,3 However, the progression-free survival (PFS) of patients who receive TKI therapy ranges from 3 to 24 months, despite harboring the same sensitive gene mutation.4 Osimertinib was initially approved for second-line treatment in epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-positive patients with T790M-resistant mutations, and the AURA3 Trial showed that the PFS of osimertinib was extended by more than 11 months, with an overall response rate of 70%.5 Osimertinib, in comparison to first-generation TKIs, has been demonstrated to significantly prolong both PFS and overall survival in first-line therapy.6 Nevertheless, the emergence of drug resistance in these patients is unavoidable. Research has demonstrated that oncogenic signaling resulting from EGFR mutations can lead to the upregulation of programmed cell death ligand 1 expression in cancer cells, facilitating immune evasion through T-cell exhaustion. This process ultimately contributes to the development of resistance to TKIs.7 In our previous study, we identified that the activation of the tumor chemotherapy-induced nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor protein-3 (NLRP3) inflammasome and antitumor immunity is critical.8 Nonetheless, the function of NLRP3 in the context of TKI treatment remains to be elucidated. TKIs directly target the cellular signaling pathway driven by target genes in tumor cells; however, little is known about off-target antitumor mechanisms. In addition to pharmacological mechanisms, some antitumor therapies simultaneously enhance antitumor immunity by regulating the immune microenvironment. For example, radiotherapy and chemotherapy, such as doxorubicin, oxaliplatin and taxane, activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, triggering interleukin (IL)-1β-mediated antitumor immunity.9,11 Surprisingly, NLRP3 knockout blocks tumor shrinkage caused by radiotherapy and drug-induced cytotoxicity. TKIs such as the EGFR-TKI gefitinib12 and the c-Kit-TKI imatinib13 have been shown to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome. Therefore, if TKIs activate the NLRP3 inflammasome to regulate immune cells and cause organ toxicity, then by extension, inflammasomes might play a role in drug efficacy in the therapeutic setting.

Inflammasomes are protein complexes in the cytoplasm of mainly monocytes,14 macrophages,15 dendritic cells,16 granulocytes17 and other myeloid cells, as well as epithelial cells18 and osteoblasts,19 to eliminate damaged cells or pathogens recognized by sensor proteins.20 The NLRP3 inflammasome is an oligomeric protein complex comprising the sensor protein NLRP3, which binds with apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a caspasedomain (ASC) via its pyrin domain (PYD) domain and procaspase-1 via its caspasedomain (CARD) .21 After procaspase-1 self-cleavage, proteolytic caspase-1 cleaves GSDMD to promote pyroptosis, releasing IL-1β and IL-18, which are also cleaved by caspase-1. This process not only promotes innate immunity but also remodels adaptive immunity, causing inflammatory diseases.22 However, in the tumor microenvironment (TME), NLRP3 senses pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) in different immune cells to induce different types of antitumor immunity.23 NLRP3 inflammasome activation in dendritic cells(DCs)9 23 and macrophages24 promotes CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor responses, whereas in myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), it induces IL-17 secretion by CD4+ T cells, thereby subverting antitumor immunity.25 TKIs, as systemic therapeutic agents, have been reported to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome in macrophages and are suspected to participate in interstitial pneumonitis and other adverse effects.12 13 However, it is still unknown whether TKI-induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation reshapes the TME.

NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and activation are triggered by the recognition of PAMPs and DAMPs, the latter of which participate in numerous inflammatory diseases.26 After antitumor therapy, the release of DAMPs, including ATP, HMGB, cathepsin B, and extracellular vesicles (EVs), leads to NLRP3 inflammasome activation.911,13 25 The classical mechanisms of DAMP release include (1) K+ efflux, which recruits pore-forming proteins to allow DAMP influx;27 28 (2) mitochondrial damage, which induces reactive oxygen species (ROS) production to facilitate thioredoxin interacting protein binding and NLRP3 activation;29 30 (3) lysosomal damage, which induces multiple cathepsins to activate NLRP331; and (4) calcium overload, which promotes NLRP3 inflammasome assembly.32 TKIs damage mitochondria and generate ROS, thereby activating the NLRP3 inflammasome; however, investigations on the mechanism by which TKIs elicit mitochondrial damage are limited. Inflammasome activation induces macrophage pyroptosis and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18.33 34

Here, we explored the role of EGFR-TKIs in inducing NLRP3 inflammasome in drug efficacy and explored its underlying mechanism. We demonstrated that the third generation EGFR-TKI osimertinib, a previously unrecognized activator, induced antitumor immunity by eliciting IL-1β release from macrophages in an NLRP3 inflammasome-dependent manner. Osimertinib triggered lysosomal dysfunction, causing calcium overload, which damaged mitochondria, leading to the release of ROS, which functioned as DAMPs sensed by NLRP3, resulting in cell lysis and IL-1β release and ultimately activating antitumor immunity. Clinically, the expression of macrophage NLRP3 and IL-1β in the TME was correlated with better survival in patients treated with osimertinib, providing new evidence for predicting the efficacy of EGFR-TKIs.

Cell cultures

Lewis lung cancer (LLC) lung carcinoma cells (from C57BL/6 mice) were purchased from the cell bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) (RRID: CVCL_5653). The cell line was cultured at 37°C under 5% CO2 in dulbecco's modified eagle medium (DMEM) (BI) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, BI) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (HyClone).

BMDM and PBMC preparation and stimulation

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from the bone marrow of 6–8 weeks old mice and cultured for 4–5 days in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin (HyClone and 20 ng/mL macrophage colony-stimulating factor(M-CSF)). Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from fresh peripheral blood using Lymphocyte Separation Medium and cultured in roswell park memorial institute (RPMI) 1640 medium containing 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 1 mM sodium pyruvate and 2 mM L-glutamine. To activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, BMDMs (5×105 cells/mL) and PBMCs (1×106 cells/mL) were seeded in 12-well plates and cultured overnight. The cell culture medium was subsequently replaced with Opti-MEM containing 50 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS). After 3–6 hours of priming with LPS, osimertinib was added to the culture medium to stimulate BMDMs, which were preincubated with or without various inhibitors (MCC950, CY09, MnTBAP, BAPTA-AM, ML-SI3, 2-APB, or Baf-A1) for 30 min. To induce AIM2 inflammasome activation, BMDMs were transfected with Lipofectamine 2000-assisted poly(A/T) (0.5 g/mL) for 0.5 hours. To induce Pyrin inflammasome activation, BMDMs were stimulated with C3 toxin (0.5 µg/mL) for 6 hours. To induce NLRC4 inflammasome activation, BMDMs were infected with S. typhimurium (multiplicity of infection=10) for 4 hours. The supernatant and cell lysate were collected after stimulation.

Bacterial culture

S. typhimurium was seeded in trypticase soy broth (TSB) medium and cultured overnight at 37°C with shaking at 200 rpm. The suspension was diluted and coated on solid TSB media to assess the bacterial density.

Confocal microscopy

BMDMs (2×105/mL) were seeded on coverslips (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in 24-well plates and incubated overnight. After 3 hours of priming with LPS, osimertinib was added to the culture medium to stimulate the BMDMs, which were preincubated with or without various inhibitors (MCC950, CY09, MnTBAP, BAPTA-AM, ML-SI3, 2-APB, or Baf-A1) for 30 min. 30 min before the end of stimulation, the BMDMs were stained with MitoTracker Red (50 nM), MitoSox (5 µM) or LysoTracker Green (200 nM). Then, the supernatant was removed, and the cells were washed three times with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for 15 min. Then, the cells were washed with phosphate buffered saline with tween (PBST) three times and stained with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI).

For live cell staining, BMDMs (2×105 cells/mL) were seeded in confocal microscopy culture dishes. After priming with LPS for 3–6 hours, the cells were stimulated with osimertinib for 1.5 hours. 25 min before the end of stimulation, Fluo-4 or LysoTracker Green was added to the cell culture medium. Five minutes before the end of stimulation, Hoechst 33342 was added to stain nuclei. Then, the culture medium was replaced with ice-cold PBS, and the cells were observed under a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 980).

ELISA

Mouse IL-1β, IL-6, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), IL-18 and human IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, IL-18 were measured in cell culture supernatants according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blot

Protein in culture supernatant was extracted via the methanol-chloroform method and resuspended in 1.5×sample buffer. To extract protein from adherent cells, 1.5×sample buffer was added directly to cells to obtain a cell lysis solution. Extracted protein was denatured by boiling at 105°C for 10–20 min. Proteins were separated by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis at 80 V for 30 min and then at 120 V for 1 hour. Then, proteins were transferred from the gel to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane at 90 V for 1 hour. The PVDF membrane was incubated at 4°C overnight with a primary antibody, washed three times with PBST, and then incubated with a conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 hours. The membrane was washed three times and then incubated with a reagent to visualize the protein bands. Protein bands were observed with Bio-Rad and analyzed with Image Lab software.

ASC oligomerization assay

BMDMs were cultured in 6-well plates and treated with osimertinib for 1.5 hours. The cells were subsequently lysed in tris-buffered saline (TBS) buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl (purchased from GCRF, China), 150 mM NaCl, and 0.5% Triton X-100 (purchased from Sigma-Aldrich), pH 7.4) at 4°C for 30 min. Then, the cells and buffer were collected and centrifuged at 6,000×g/4 °C for 15 min to obtain a pellet. The pellet was washed two times with TBS buffer, followed by resuspension in TBS buffer containing 2 mM fresh disuccinimidyl suberate (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cross-linking was carried out at 37°C for 30 min, with the tubes inverted every 10 min. The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 6,000×g/4 °C for 15 min. The cross-linked pellet was resuspended in 25 µL of 3× sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) loading buffer and boiled at 100°C for 10 min. The samples were analyzed by immunoblotting with an anti-ASC antibody.

Immunoprecipitation

For endogenous immunoprecipitation (IP) assays, BMDMs in 6-well plates were stimulated with osimertinib or nigericin, followed by lysis with NP-40 lysis buffer containing a complete protease inhibitor cocktail. Primary antibodies and Protein G Mag Sepharose were added to the cell lysates, followed by incubation overnight at 4°C. Antibody-bound proteins were precipitated with protein G beads and subjected to western blot analysis.

Drug affinity-responsive target stability assay

The drug affinity-responsive target stability (DARTS) assay was performed to analyze the direct binding of protein–drug interactions. BMDMs were treated with LPS (50 ng/mL) for 3 hours. After the supernatant was removed, the cells were lysed with NP-40 lysis buffer for 30 min. The lysates were then centrifuged at 12,000×g for 10 min at 4°C to remove cell debris. Osimertinib was added to the lysates at specified concentrations, followed by incubation overnight at 4°C with shaking. Pronase (Sigma) was then added to the lysates, which were subsequently incubated for 30 min at room temperature. The reaction was stopped by adding 3×SDS loading buffer, and the samples were denatured and subsequently analyzed by immunoblotting.

siRNA-mediated gene silencing in BMDMs

BMDMs (3×105 cells/mL) were seeded in 12-well plates. siRNA (50 nM per well) was transfected into the cells using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The siRNA sequence used was as follows: siTrpml1: 5′-GCCGCCTCAAGTACTTCTTTA-3′.

Quantitative PCR with reverse transcription

Total RNA was extracted using TsingZol Total RNA Extraction Reagent (Tsingke) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Specifically, after the cells were lysed with TsingZol Total RNA extraction reagent, chloroform was added, leading to the separation of the homogenate into three layers: a clear upper aqueous layer containing RNA, an interphase layer, and a pink lower organic layer containing DNA and protein. RNA was precipitated from the upper aqueous layer with isopropanol. Then, the RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using an M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Invitrogen). Quantitative PCR of cDNA was performed in a StepOne Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using SYBR Green premix (Takara Bio). The sequences of the primers used were as follows: mGapdh-forward: 5′-GGTGAAGGTCGGTGTGAACG-3′; mGapdh-reverse: 5′-CTCGCTCCTGGAAGATGGTG-3′; mTrpml1-forward: 5′- GTCGGTGTCATTCGCTACCT-3′; mTrpml1-reverse: 5′- TTGCCAGATGTGGGGCTATC-3′.

Tumor transplantation and in vivo treatments

For the subcutaneous models, 5×105 LLC cells were engrafted subcutaneously into the flanks of 6–8 weeks old sex and age-matched C57BL/6J mice. Tumor growth was assessed by caliper measurements every 3–5 days. Tumor volume was calculated as 0.5×length×width×width. 5–7 days after tumor cell inoculation, the mice were randomly assigned to receive 30 mg/kg osimertinib via intragastric treatment once a day, 10 mg/kg CY-09 via intraperitoneal injection once every 2 days, or recombinant IL (rIL)-1β (200 ng), rIL-18 (1 µg) via peritumoral injection once every 2 days.

Orthotopic lung tumor model

For orthotopic lung tumor model, 2×106 LLC cells are injected into the left lung of 6–8 weeks old sex and age-matched C57BL/6J mice. The IMAGING 100 Micro CT imaging system (Raycision, Hefei) uses a microfocus X-ray tube and a high-resolution flat panel detector to realize the rapid scanning imaging of the detached lung tumor and the calculation of the volume of mouse lung tumors through cone beam CT scanning and three-dimensional image reconstruction technology.

Tumor-infiltrating immune cell collection

To obtain tumor-infiltrating cells, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation and tumors were carefully removed and cut into small pieces, which were subsequently digested in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 1 mg/mL collagenase type IV and 0.1 mg/mL DNase I for 30 min in a shaking incubator (220 rpm) at 37°C. Leukocytes were obtained via suspension in 40% Percoll, density gradient centrifugation and filtration using a cell strainer (70 µm). The isolated cells were used for various assays.

Flow cytometry analysis

For tumor-infiltrating immune cells, isolated single cells were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies. To stain cytokines such as interferon (IFN)-γ, the cells were first incubated for 4 hours with phorbol-12-myristate-13-acetate (PMA) (50 ng/mL, Sigma-Aldrich), ionomycin (1 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) and monensin (2.5 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) at 37°C in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS. After being labeled with surface antibodies, the cells were then fixed with fixation/permeabilization buffer for 40 min, followed by staining with anti-cytokine antibodies. After washing, the cells were analyzed by flow cytometry and FlowJo V.10 software.

For BMDMs, flow cytometry was used to analyze the mitochondrial mass and calcium flux. To determine the mitochondrial mass, BMDMs were stained with MitoTracker Green and MitoTracker Deep Red (50 nM) for 30 min at 37°C. For calcium flux, BMDMs were incubated with Fluo-4 AM working solution (2 µM) for 30 min at 37°C in the dark. After washing with PBS, the unbound dye was removed, and the cells were analyzed by flow cytometry. The gating strategies used in all flow cytometry experiments are depicted in online supplemental figure 1.

Multiplex immunofluorescence analysis

To assess the expression of NLRP3 inflammasome-related proteins in the TME, multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) analysis was performed to detect the colocalization of CD68+NLRP3+ and CD68+IL-1β+ cells, indicating the expression of NLRP3 and IL-1β in macrophages. mIF staining was performed at Servicebio Technology (Wuhan, China). A total of four markers, namely, CD68, NLRP3, IL-1β and DAPI, were assessed. Formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections from NSCLC tissues were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Then, epitope retrieval was performed with Tris-EDTA buffer (pH=6; G1202, Servicebio Technology). Then, a circle was drawn around the tissue sections with an immunohistochemistry pen (G6100, Servicebio Technology). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H2O2, and non-specific antibody binding sites were blocked with goat serum (G1209 Servicebio Technology). Three antigens were then labeled via circular staining, including incubation with a primary antibody, secondary antibody, and tyramine signal amplification (TSA) reagent and the removal of the TSA-antibody complex in Tris-EDTA buffer (pH=6; G1202, Servicebio Technology) in a microwave. After three cycles of staining, the sections were counterstained with DAPI (G1012, Servicebio Technology) for 10 min. After treatment with an autofluorescence quencher, the sections were mounted with antifade mountant (G1401, Servicebio Technology). All sections were viewed using a fluorescence microscope (NIKON ECLIPSE C1, Japan) and scanned (SLIDEVIEW vs200, Olympus Japan). Digital images were visualized using qupath pathology software V.0.5.0. More than three fields per section were selected by a pathologist on the basis of tissue size for multispectral imaging analysis. The primary antibodies used included anti-CD68 (Abcam, AB955), anti-NLRP3 (19771–1-AP, Proteintech), and anti-IL-1β (16806, Servicebio Technology), and DAPI (28718-90-3, Sigma-Aldrich) was used to stain nuclei.

Results

Osimertinib promotes IL-1β production and caspase-1 maturation in macrophages

The third-generation EGFR-TKIs are widely used nowadays; however, researchers have not determined whether they also activate macrophage inflammasomes in a manner similar to first-generation,12 since TKIs activate inflammasomes independent of their target kinase.13 To determine the role of the inflammasome in antitumor efficacy treated by TKI, the classic third-generation EGFR-TKI osimertinib was introduced to BMDMs derived from mice that were primed with LPS (figure 1A). IL-1β secretion and caspase-1 maturation increased dramatically with increasing osimertinib concentration (figure 1B and C). Similarly, osimertinib also induced lactate dehydrogenase release and pyroptosis in BMDMs (figure 1C). Unlike LPS, osimertinib did not induce IL-6, TNF-α or IL-18 secretion (figure 1D and E, online supplemental figure 2A), which suggested that osimertinib itself could not induce cytokine production. Moreover, we investigated whether this phenomenon is induced by osimertinib in humans (figure 1G) and found that osimertinib specifically induced IL-1β secretion (but not IL-6, TNF-α or IL-18) in a concentration-dependent manner in human PBMCs (figure 1H–K, online supplemental figure 2B). To rule out an important role of IL-18 signaling in the antitumor activity of osimertinib, we next tested the combination antitumor therapy of osimertinib and rIL-18 with no inhibitory or synergistic effect against tumor growth (online supplemental figure 2C-E). These results indicated that osimertinib promoted IL-1β production and caspase-1 maturation in both mouse and human macrophages.

Osimertinib activates the NLRP3 inflammasome

Inflammasome activation induces caspase-1 self-shearing and activation and thus promotes the cleavage and secretion of IL-1β and IL-18.35,37 In this study, the relationship between IL-1β secretion and inflammasome activation was investigated using Casp1−/− and Pycard−/− murine BMDMs, and the results revealed that compared with wild-type (WT) BMDMs, in Casp1−/− and Pycard−/− murine BMDMs, IL-1β maturation and secretion were completely blocked (figure 2A–D). Moreover, to identify the functional nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptors (NLRs) that form inflammasomes after osimertinib treatment, BMDMs from Nlrp3−/−, Aim2−/−, Mefv−/− and Nlrc4−/− mice were analyzed. We found that IL-1β secretion and caspase-1 activation were dramatically suppressed in BMDMs from Nlrp3−/− mice (figure 2E and F). However, this inhibition was not observed in BMDMs from Aim2−/−, Mefv−/− or Nlrc4−/− mice (figure 2G–L). In addition, osimertinib-induced inflammasome activation was significantly attenuated by the NLRP3 inhibitors CY-0938 and MCC950,39 suggesting that osimertinib functions as an activator of the NLRP3 inflammasome, similar to nigericin (figure 2M–P). NLRP3 inflammasome complex assembly, including ASC oligomerization, NLRP3-ASC binding and NEK7-NLRP3 binding, induces the self-shearing and activation of caspase-1.40 41 Using disuccinimidyl suberate to promote ASC cross-linking, we found that osimertinib strengthened ASC oligomerization (online supplemental figure 3A). Coimmunoprecipitation revealed that osimertinib stimulated NLRP3-ASC binding and NEK7-NLRP3 binding (online supplemental figure 3B and C). These results demonstrated that osimertinib is a specific NLRP3 inflammasome activator.

Osimertinib mediates NLRP3 inflammasome activation through mitochondrial damage and reactive oxygen species production

NLRP3 inflammasome activation mechanisms have not been fully elucidated.42 For chemotherapy and irradiation, ATP and EVs derived from dying tumors activate inflammasomes in dendritic cells and macrophages.11 43 In comparison, gefitinib and imatinib are known to induce mitochondrial damage and lysosomal membrane destabilization, respectively, which ultimately activate inflammasomes in macrophages.12 13 To further explore the mechanism of inflammasome activation, the DARTS assay was performed to analyze the direct binding of protein-drug interactions.44 Protein levels decreased after pronase treatment, except for proteins that combined with other molecules. Unlike NLRP3 binding to the positive control MCC950,45 no binding was detected between NLRP3 and osimertinib (online supplemental figure 4A). Next, we investigated potassium (K+) efflux in osimertinib-induced inflammasome activation. We found that IL-1β secretion was not inhibited, but K+ efflux was inhibited by increasing the amount of extracellular KCl (online supplemental figure 4B). Next, we assessed mitochondrial damage and ROS production, and compared the effects of osimertinib with the effects of gefitinib. The results showed that osimertinib caused mitochondrial aggregation and damage as well as ROS production (figure 3A–D). The production of ROS, well-known DAMPs, was inhibited by MnTBAP,46 resulting in dramatically decreased IL-1β secretion and caspase-1 activation (figure 3E–H). Osimertinib-induced mitochondrial damage and ROS production were independent of NLRP3, suggesting the occurrence of events upstream of NLRP3 activation (figure 3I–K).

Osimertinib induces mitochondrial damage through lysosomal calcium flux

Mitochondrial dysfunction is observed during the activation of many inflammasomes, but the mechanisms involved are poorly understood.47 Intracellular Ca2+ reportedly causes mitochondrial dysfunction after influx into mitochondria via uniporters, leading to paraptosis.48 49 We hypothesized that osimertinib‐induced inflammasome activation and cell lysis are associated with Ca2+ overload. The Ca2+ concentration was measured via Fluo-4 staining and flow cytometry. As expected, osimertinib dramatically increased the cellular Ca2+ concentration, an effect that was attenuated by BAPTA-AM,50 a classic Ca2+ chelator (figure 4A–C). Additionally, BAPTA-AM inhibited osimertinib-induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation (figure 4D and E). These results indicate that osimertinib induces mitochondrial damage through intracellular Ca2+ accumulation. The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and lysosomes are the major Ca2+ storage reservoirs, which release large amounts of Ca2+ into the cytoplasm when ER stress or lysosome acidification occurs.51 We investigated the physiological source of Ca2+ via the ER Ca2+ channel inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptor (IP3R) inhibitor 2-APB52 and the lysosomal Ca2+ channel transient receptor potential mucolipin (TRPML) inhibitor ML-SI3.53 Osimertinib-induced Ca2+ accumulation was inhibited only by pretreatment with ML-SI3 (figure 4F–H, online supplemental figure 5A and B). Osimertinib-induced inflammasome activation was blocked by pretreatment with ML-SI3 but not 2-APB (figure 4I and J, online supplemental figure 5C and D). Moreover, only ML-SI3 attenuated mitochondrial damage and ROS production (online supplemental figure 6A-C). ML-SI3 is a potent lysosome TRPML channel inhibitor that blocks both TRPML1 and TRPML2. Osimertinib-induced inflammasome activation was measured after TRPML1 and TRPML2 knockdown, and only TRPML1 knockdown blocked IL-1β release (figure 4K and L). These results indicated that lysosomal calcium flux acted as an initial signal of mitochondrial damage and contributed to osimertinib-induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation.

Calcium overload is caused by osimertinib-induced lysosomal dysfunction

Previous research revealed that osimertinib induced cytoplasmic vacuole formation in various cancer cell lines.54 Here, we found that cytoplasmic vacuoles also appeared in macrophages, and LysoTracker Green staining confirmed that they were lysosomal vacuoles (figure 5A and B). These lysosomal vacuoles were not present after pretreatment with the lysosomal acidification inhibitor bafilomycin A1 (BAF-A1) (figure 5A and B). After lysosomal acidification was inhibited, the degree of intracellular Ca2+ accumulation and the degree of mitochondrial damage induced by osimertinib were attenuated (figure 5C–E). Additionally, IL-1β secretion and caspase-1 activation were blocked by BAF-A1 pretreatment (figure 5F and G). These results revealed the source of Ca2+ accumulation induced by osimertinib.

Osimertinib-mediated suppression of tumor progression is dependent on NLRP3

Osimertinib was designed to treat NSCLC by targeting EGFR; however, EGFR-independent antitumor mechanisms of osimertinib remain unknown. We sought to determine whether osimertinib-induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation participated in tumor shrinkage. Mouse LLC cells were implanted subcutaneously into Nlrp3−/− mice, which were then treated with osimertinib every day for approximately 4 weeks. Compared with WT mice, Nlrp3−/− mice presented rapid tumor growth under similar conditions (figure 6A–C). Previous studies have reported that Nlrp3−/− mice exhibit several inflammatory diseases, including peritonitis and experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis. We next analyzed intratumoral inflammasome activation and the TME in osimertinib-treated Nlrp3−/− mice. Consistent with the role of NLRP3 in osimertinib-induced tumor suppression, osimertinib-induced intratumoral caspase-1 activation and IL-1β release were not observed in the absence of NLRP3 (figure 6D and E), and there was no osimertinib-induced increase in the number of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the TME of Nlrp3−/− mice (figure 6F and G). The flow cytometry results were similar to the percentage normalized to tumor weight rather than CD45 cells (online supplemental figure 7A), in addition to the fact that there were no significant differences between groups for other types of immune cells in the supplementation results (online supplemental figure 7B and C). Moreover, the percentages of IFN-γ+CD4+ T cells and IFN-γ+CD8+ T cells in the TME were low in Nlrp3−/− mice after treatment with osimertinib (figure 6H1). These phenomena were also observed when osimertinib was combined with CY-09, an NLRP3 inhibitor (figure 6J–P, online supplemental figure 7D). In all other labeled immune cell types, an altered natural killer cell ratio was found (online supplemental figure 7E and F). As in Nlrp3−/− mice, CY-09 blocked NLRP3 inflammasome activation, resulting in a failure to release IL-1β, which is important for triggering an antitumor T-cell response. To confirm the essential role of IL-1β signaling in the antitumor activity of osimertinib, we next tested the combined antitumor therapy of osimertinib and rIL-1β, which had synergistic effects on antitumor growth (figure 6Q and S). Additionally, the combination increased the percentages of CD4+ T, CD8+ T, IFN-γ+CD4+ T and IFN-γ+CD8+ T cells in the TME (figure 6T–W, online supplemental figure 7G and H). In addition, we observed that polymorphonuclear myeloid derived suppressor cells (PMN-MDSCs) levels decreased following treatment with osimertinib and rIL-1β but subsequently increased in response to NLRP3 inhibition (CY-09b). These findings implicate IL-1β in suppressing PMN-MDSCs (online supplemental figure 7F and I). It has been reported that IL-1β production can induce Th17 cells,55 but our further flow cytometry results did not find this (online supplemental figure 8A). In order to further enhance the convincing effect of rIL-1β on antitumor, we further validated the lung orthotopic tumor model and obtained similar conclusions to subcutaneous tumorigenesis (online supplemental figure 8B-D). In summary, these findings suggest an off-target antitumor mechanism of osimertinib that is dependent on the NLRP3 inflammasome.

The NLRP3 inflammasome affects osimertinib outcomes

Patients with the same EGFR mutation usually present very different outcomes after osimertinib treatment. We next investigated whether the NLRP3 inflammasome affects osimertinib responsiveness. A total of 47 patients who received second-line osimertinib treatment and were pathologically diagnosed with NSCLC and genetically diagnosed with the EGFR T790M mutation were included. PFS ranged from 2 to 32 months, with a median PFS of 11 months (95% CI 7 to 17) (figure 7A). We analyzed the relationships between the expression of NLRP3 inflammasome-related proteins such as NLRP3 and IL-1β in macrophages of the TME and the response to osimertinib. The median progression-free survival (mPFS) was significantly longer in patients with a high proportion of CD68+NLRP3+ cells than in those with a low proportion of CD68+NLRP3+ cells (20 months vs 7 months; HR for disease progression or death (HR), 0.1331; 95% CI 0.5466 to 0.3241; p<0.001) (figure 7B and C). The mPFS was 17 months for patients with a high proportion of CD68+IL-1β+ cells versus 7 months (HR 0.2689; 95% CI 0.1158 to 0.6243) for patients with a low proportion of CD68+IL-1β+ cells (figure 7D). High proportions of CD68+NLRP3+ cells and CD68+IL-1β+ cells were significantly associated with better PFS, with areas under the curve (AUCs) of 0.8922 and 0.8245, respectively (figure 7E and F). Consistently, we observed that the proportion of CD68+NLRP3+ cells was strongly correlated with the proportion of CD68+IL-1β+ cells in the TME (figure 7G). Overall, NLPR3 inflammasome-related proteins in the TME exhibited a high predictive value in patients with EGFR T790M-mutant NSCLC treated with osimertinib.

Discussion

Recent reports have revealed that antitumor therapies such as cytotoxic agents and radiation induce NLRP3 inflammasome activation to enhance the immune microenvironment. However, since the EGFR-TKI gefitinib activates the NLRP3 inflammasome to induce organ toxicity, we attempted to investigate whether TKI-induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation reshapes the TME causing the major difference in outcomes among patients with the same EGFR mutation. Our results demonstrated that TKIs also induced an antitumor immune response and tumor suppression independent of the target kinase but dependent on NLRP3 inflammasome activation in myeloid cells. In our prior research, we determined that the NLRP3 inflammasome detects mitochondrial dysfunction.29 Research has shown that the inhibition of Ca2+ mobilization hinders the assembly and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome complex. Moreover, during ATP stimulation, Ca2+ signaling is essential for facilitating mitochondrial damage.56 The EGFR-TKI osimertinib induced lysosomal dysfunction and lysosomal TRPML1 channel activation, which resulted in intracellular Ca2+ accumulation and thus mitochondrial damage and ROS production. As DAMPs, ROS are sensed by NLRP3 and promote NLRP3–ASC binding and ASC–procaspase-1 binding, which are required for NLRP3 inflammasome assembly, pyroptosis, IL-1β production and T-cell activation in the TME. Lower NLRP3 inflammasome expression predicted poorer osimertinib response in patients with NSCLC with the EGFR T790M mutation. In addition, rIL-1β supplementation enhanced osimertinib sensitivity in a mouse xenograft tumor model. Our findings revealed an off-target role of EGFR-TKIs in inducing tumor immunity by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome.

IL-1β exhibits a paradoxical duality in cancer biology, acting as both a promoter of tumorigenesis and a mediator of therapy-induced antitumor immunity. This apparent contradiction stems from fundamental differences in cellular context, concentration kinetics, and immunological consequences between chronic inflammation in established tumors versus acute, therapy-induced inflammatory responses. Under chronic, tumor-promoting conditions, IL-1β drives malignancy by activating NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways, contributing to lung inflammation, fibrosis, and carcinogenesis.57 Its overexpression stimulates the TME, promoting cancer cell proliferation, angiogenesis, macrophage polarization towards protumor phenotypes, and accumulation of immunosuppressive MDSCs, collectively accelerating tumor growth and metastasis.58 59 This protumorigenic role underpins findings like the CANTOS trial, where blocking IL-1β with canakinumab reduced lung cancer incidence and mortality.60 Conversely, during cancer therapy (including chemotherapy and radiotherapy), induced IL-1β production often exerts antitumor effects. The key distinction lies in the immunological reprogramming triggered by therapy-associated damage: chemotherapy or radiation activates inflammasomes (eg, NLRP3) in immune or cancer cells, leading to controlled IL-1β maturation. This shifts the immune milieu towards activation. (1) Enhancing CD8+ T-cell responses9 and increasing macrophage production of IL-1β, particularly in patients with chemotherapy-responsive small cell lung cancer.61 (2) Specific therapeutic agents can harness IL-1β’s direct anti-proliferative potential: trans-retinoic acid exerts part of its anticancer effect by selectively inducing IL-1β, which directly inhibits lung cancer cell proliferation.62 (3) Activation of specific pathways (eg, NLRC4 and estrogen receptor β) in cancer cells leads to IL-1β release that inhibits metastasis, promotes tumor cell death (eg, pyroptosis), and blocks colonization.63 64 Thus, while chronic IL-1β signaling fuels tumor progression, acute, therapy-induced IL-1β acts as a danger signal that helps overcome immunosuppression and engages antitumor immunity. Supporting this paradigm, our study demonstrates that combining rIL-1β with the targeted agent osimertinib significantly enhances antitumor efficacy compared with osimertinib alone, primarily through modulation of the immune microenvironment. This highlights the potential to strategically leverage IL-1β’s immunostimulatory capacity within the context of cancer treatment.

Consistent with the findings of this study, previous methods of enhancing the antitumor effects of IL-1β predominantly involve the immunomodulatory functions of macrophages.65 In patients with small cell lung cancer who exhibited a positive response to systemic chemotherapy, there was a notable increase in the production of IL-1β by macrophages.61 The activation of NLRP3 inflammasomes by chemotherapy facilitates the maturation of IL-1β and enhances the activation of antitumor CD8+ T cells.9 Nevertheless, the precise role of IL-1β in the context of targeted therapy for malignant tumors remains to be elucidated. In this study, we observed that the combination of rIL-1β and osimertinib produces a more pronounced antitumor effect by modulating the immune microenvironment, as compared with the administration of osimertinib alone. IL-1β signaling elicits a prolonged response from both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, and the capacity of these T cells to produce IFN-γ is augmented with the additional administration of IL-1β.66 67 Van et al demonstrated that the systemic administration of IL-1β in WT mice significantly enhanced the proliferation and cytotoxic activity of CD8+ T cells, in contrast to mice that had been individually adapted to antigen-specific immunity.68 In alignment with the findings of this study, Sarkar et al demonstrated that IL-1 signaling can directly influence CD8+ T cells without necessitating additional helper pathways.69

Although previous studies have demonstrated that TKIs such as gefitinib and imatinib activate the NLRP3 inflammasome in macrophages and produce IL-1β, the specific mechanism and biological functions involved in tumor therapy are still not known. Here, we found that in an Nlrp3−/− xenograft tumor model, osimertinib did not inhibit tumor growth because osimertinib could not induce NLRP3 inflammasome-dependent antitumor immunity, such as CD4+ T and CD8+ T cells and IFN-γ production in tumors. It is noteworthy that in our study, the observed differences in outcomes between CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells were occasionally not statistically significant. We hypothesize that this may be attributed to variations in the distribution of IFN-γ+CD4+ T-cell locations, as a significant statistical difference was apparent when employing the IFN-γ+CD4+ T cells to tumor weight ratio in our calculations. Moreover, an NLRP3 inhibitor blocked osimertinib antitumor efficacy and antitumor immunity. When we investigated the tumor tissue of patients with NSCLC with the EGFR T790M mutation who received osimertinib treatment, we found that NLRP3 and pro-IL-1β expression in macrophages was correlated with osimertinib-induced benefits. Furthermore, rIL-1β supplementation enhanced osimertinib-induced tumor suppression and antitumor immunity, providing a strategy to improve outcomes. Another interesting phenomenon observed in our flow cytometry results is that PMN-MDSCs decreased following treatment with osimertinib and rIL-1β, but increased in response to NLRP3 inhibition. This finding is not entirely consistent with some previous studies. In renal cell carcinoma and pancreatic malignancy models,70 71 IL-1β has been reported as a key factor promoting PMN-MDSCs expansion, activation, and immunosuppressive function, while anti-IL-1β treatment decreases MDSCs populations and delays tumor growth. We postulate that this discrepancy may arise from the heterogeneity of TMEs across different cancer types.

A ubiquitous mechanism for NLRP3 inflammasome activation is mitochondrial damage and subsequent ROS production. An unresolved question is what is responsible for TKI-induced mitochondrial damage. When we observed mitochondrial damage via immunofluorescence, macrophages were filled with cytoplasmic vacuoles after osimertinib treatment. The cytoplasmic vacuoles were identified as lysosomal vacuoles, and lysosomal leakage was observed, effects connected to NLRP3 inflammasome activation through the release of lysosomal cathepsins. Numerous studies have demonstrated the involvement of cathepsin in the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, with the majority highlighting the role of cathepsin B released following lysosomal injury. Certain chemotherapeutic agents, such as 5-fluorouracil and gemcitabine, may induce lysosomal damage, leading to the release of cathepsin B through an as-yet-undefined signaling pathway, thereby further activating inflammasomes.25 Furthermore, cathepsin B released into the cytosol may translocate to the mitochondria, resulting in potential damage. We hypothesized that cathepsin B may be liberated into the cytosol following lysosomal injury, thereby contributing to the activation of NLRP3. However, this hypothesis requires further investigation. Lysosomal dysfunction can arise from various factors, among which alterations in lysosomal pH, such as increased acidity, are a significant cause.72 The cytoplasmic vacuoles were labeled with LysoTracker and identified as lysosomal acidification vesicles. Notably, as a lysosomal Ca2+ efflux channel, TRPML is important in regulating lysosomal pH and therefore the degradative function of lysosomes. These lysosomal acidification vesicles were accompanied by Ca2+ release, and both were blocked by BAF-A1. The calcium chelator BAPTA abolished osimertinib-induced mitochondrial damage and ROS production, indicating that these processes serve as the initial factor of inflammasome activation. The largest intracellular calcium store is the ER; however, through blocking the ER Ca2+ channel IP3R, we found that ER calcium played a very limited role in the above process. Further investigation revealed that lysosomal TRPML1 is essential for inflammasome activation. Future studies need to investigate the events upstream of lysosomal vesicles and TRPML1 activation triggered by osimertinib.

Nevertheless, certain limitations persist in our experiments. LLC cells in the established mouse model lacked EGFR mutations. Consequently, the observed effects are independent of EGFR mutation status and reflect a specific response to osimertinib treatment. Notably, this study was validated solely at the murine level by simulating the priming signal through LPS. Furthermore, previous research has indicated that the activation of NLRP3 results in organ toxicity and is associated with the pathogenesis of several inflammatory diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease, diabetes, gout, and atherosclerosis.73 74 To substantiate evidence-based medical conclusions, further clinical trials are essential.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings revealed that osimertinib-mediated lysosomal dysfunction and Ca2+ efflux are initial events in NLRP3 inflammasome activation and serve as effective predictors of osimertinib responsiveness. On the basis of evidence that IL-1β supplementation restores osimertinib sensitivity in NLRP3-deficient mice, therapeutic strategies to overcome osimertinib resistance in patients with NSCLC with low NLRP3 inflammasome activation are needed.
