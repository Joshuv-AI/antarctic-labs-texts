Beyond the hospital: family caregivers’ experiences in supporting stroke recovery in Ghana
Authors: Richard Adade, Emmanuella Akua Asamoah, Asantewaa Nana Yaa Theresa, Nancy Baisel, Appau Obed, Frank Twum, Douglas Fofie, Clement Osei Tutu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionStroke remains a leading cause of mortality and long-term disability globally, with profound consequences for both survivors and their families. In Ghana, where formal post-stroke rehabilitation and long-term care services are limited, family caregivers play a central role in supporting stroke survivors. However, there is limited qualitative evidence on how caregivers understand stroke and experience caregiving within their sociocultural context, creating a gap in knowledge needed to inform context-specific interventions.MethodsThis qualitative study explored the experiences of family caregivers of stroke survivors attending Suntreso Government Hospital in Ghana. Fifteen caregivers were purposively selected. Data were collected through in-depth, semi-structured interviews and analyzed using thematic analysis.ResultsCaregivers demonstrated partial awareness of stroke risk factors, particularly hypertension and diabetes; however, stroke onset was often perceived as sudden and unexpected, reflecting gaps between knowledge and functional preparedness. Caregiving involved intensive support with activities of daily living, including feeding, bathing, mobility assistance, and medication management, which substantially disrupted caregivers’ social, economic, and occupational lives. The findings highlight the need for integrated interventions that extend beyond patient-focused care to include caregiver training, psychosocial support, financial protection mechanisms, and community-based rehabilitation services. Introduction

Stroke is a life-altering neurological condition that profoundly affects both survivors and their families [7]. Beyond the immediate physical impairments such as paralysis and speech difficulties, stroke often leads to long-term cognitive, emotional, and functional limitations that reduce independence and quality of life [7, 10, 14]. These effects extend to family members, who frequently assume caregiving roles, often experiencing psychological distress, social disruption, and financial strain. Recent studies continue to highlight the dual burden of stroke on both patients and their informal caregivers [10, 14].

Globally, stroke remains one of the leading causes of death and disability. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study, stroke is the second leading cause of death and a major contributor to disability-adjusted life years worldwide [10]. The burden is particularly high in low- and middle-income regions, including sub-Saharan Africa, where incidence, prevalence, and mortality continue to rise [1]. In Ghana, stroke is consistently ranked among the top three causes of death and contributes substantially to adult disability [3, 8]. This high burden is driven by modifiable risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and lifestyle changes associated with urbanization.

The impact of stroke varies significantly depending on the location and extent of brain damage, as well as individual factors such as age, comorbidities, and access to timely medical care [7, 14]. For instance, individuals with severe neurological impairment often require long-term assistance with activities of daily living, while those with milder forms may recover partial independence but still require support [7, 10, 14]. Evidence from sub-Saharan Africa shows that many stroke survivors experience persistent cognitive and functional impairments, further increasing dependence on caregivers [4].

Advances in acute stroke care have improved survival rates, resulting in a growing number of stroke survivors who require long-term care. In many contexts including the current study, this responsibility falls on informal caregivers—defined broadly as family members, friends, or significant others who provide unpaid care and support to individuals with chronic illness or disability [2, 10, 18]. In Ghana, where formal rehabilitation and long-term care services are limited, family caregivers provide the majority of post-stroke care [16].

Caregiving for stroke survivors is associated with multiple challenges. Caregivers frequently assist with activities such as feeding, bathing, mobility, medication management, and emotional support [9, 12]. These responsibilities can lead to physical exhaustion, emotional stress, social isolation, and financial hardship. Studies in Ghana and similar contexts report that caregivers often reduce working hours or leave employment, while also experiencing stigma and limited access to formal support services [3, 9]. Despite these challenges, many caregivers rely on personal resilience, cultural values, and social or religious support systems to cope [11, 13].

Although a growing body of research in Ghana has examined stroke and its associated burden, much of this evidence has focused on quantitative approaches, including measures of disability, quality of life, and psychological distress among stroke survivors and caregivers [3, 8]. These studies provide important epidemiological and burden-related insights but offer limited in-depth exploration of caregivers lived experiences. In particular, there is still a paucity of qualitative research examining how family caregivers understand stroke, experience caregiving responsibilities, and adapt within their sociocultural context. This gap is critical, as caregiving in Ghana is largely informal and shaped by cultural expectations, yet these lived realities remain underexplored. Addressing this gap is essential for developing context-specific and culturally appropriate support interventions.

Therefore, this study explores the experiences of family caregivers of stroke survivors attending Suntreso Government Hospital in Ghana, with a focus on their knowledge of stroke, the multidimensional burdens they face, and the coping strategies they employ.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted at the Suntreso Government Hospital in the Kumasi Metropolis, Ashanti Region of Ghana. The hospital is a public District Hospital under the Ghana Health Service, established in 1963 and currently providing a range of inpatient and outpatient services through approximately twelve clinical and support departments. The study was specifically carried out in the Physiotherapy Unit, which provides rehabilitation services for patients with musculoskeletal and neurological conditions, including stroke. The unit operates from Monday to Friday, 8:00 a.m. to 2:00 p.m., and manages referrals from various departments within the hospital and other health facilities. Stroke is one of the most commonly managed neurological conditions in the unit, with an estimated 100 stroke patients referred annually. The Physiotherapy Unit therefore provided an appropriate setting for exploring the experiences of family caregivers of stroke survivors receiving rehabilitation care.

Research design

This study employed a qualitative descriptive design to explore the experiences of caregivers of stroke survivors. A qualitative approach was considered appropriate as it allows for an in-depth understanding of participants lived experiences, perceptions, and challenges within their natural context. Semi-structured interviews were used to generate rich, detailed data.

Participant recruitment and sampling

The study included 15 caregivers of stroke survivors (see detail profile of participants in Table 1 below). Participants were selected using purposive sampling to ensure the inclusion of individuals with direct caregiving experience relevant to the study objectives. Caregivers who accompanied stroke survivors to the Physiotherapy Unit during the data collection period were approached and screened for eligibility. Inclusion criteria were: (1) caregivers aged 18 years and above, (2) individuals who had provided care to a stroke survivor for at least six months, and (3) willingness to provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria included caregivers with less than six months of caregiving experience and those unable to participate due to time constraints or other personal reasons. Two individuals declined participation due to limited caregiving experience and time constraints. Data saturation guided the sample size. Saturation was reached when no new themes or insights emerged from the interviews. After the 13th interview, no new codes were identified, and two additional interviews were conducted to confirm saturation.

Data collection instrument

Data were collected using a semi-structured interview guide developed specifically for this study based on the research objectives and a review of relevant literature on caregiving experiences in stroke. The guide included open-ended questions and probes to facilitate detailed responses. The interview guide was reviewed by a qualitative research expert to enhance clarity and relevance. A pretest was conducted with three caregivers of stroke survivors in the same health facility. Minor revisions were made, including rephrasing ambiguous questions and simplifying wording for better understanding. Data from the pretest were not included in the final analysis to avoid bias. The interview guide was originally developed in English and translated into Twi by a bilingual expert. To ensure accuracy and consistency, back-translation into English was performed, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion among the research team.

Data collection procedure

Interviews were conducted by EAA and ANYT, who has training and experience in qualitative research and interview techniques. Prior to this Ethical approval (CHRPE/AP/286/22) for the study was obtained from the Committee on Human Research, Publications and Ethics (CHRPE) of Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology. Permission to conduct the study was also obtained from the management of Suntreso Government Hospital. Data collection took place over a two-week period. Eligible caregivers were approached at the Physiotherapy Unit and provided with information about the study. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the interviews. Interviews were conducted face-to-face in a private setting within the hospital to ensure confidentiality. Most interviews were conducted in Twi, the preferred language of participants, while a few were conducted in English. Each interview lasted approximately 25 to 30 min and was audio-recorded with participants’ permission. Interviews were conducted without the presence of stroke survivors to allow caregivers to freely express their experiences.

Data analysis

Audio-recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim. Interviews conducted in Twi were translated into English during transcription by a bilingual expert. The translated transcripts were reviewed by RA and OA to ensure accuracy and consistency. Thematic analysis was used to analyze the data. Initial coding was conducted by EAA AND ANYT, who systematically reviewed the transcripts and assigned codes to meaningful segments of the data. These codes were then reviewed and refined by other members of the research team. Codes with similar meanings were grouped into broader categories and subsequently developed into themes. For example, codes such as “financial burden” and “cost of medication” were merged into the theme “economic challenges.” Themes were reviewed, discussed, and refined by all authors to ensure they accurately reflected the data. Representative quotations were selected to support each theme, and unique identifiers were assigned to participants to maintain confidentiality.

Trustworthiness of the study

The rigor of the study was ensured using established qualitative criteria of credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability. Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement with participants and peer debriefing among the research team. Dependability was ensured by maintaining a clear audit trail of the research process. Confirmability was achieved through independent review of transcripts, codes, and themes by multiple researchers to minimize bias. Transferability was supported by providing detailed descriptions of the study setting, participants, and context to allow readers to assess the applicability of the findings to other settings.

Findings

Overview of findings

The analysis generated four interrelated themes: (1) caregivers’ understanding of stroke and its causes, (2) recognition and onset of stroke, (3) caregiving roles and evolving responsibilities, and (4) multidimensional burden, social perceptions, and coping strategies. These themes reflect how caregivers make sense of stroke and navigate its impact within their sociocultural and economic context. Biomedical knowledge, lived experience, and misconceptions

Caregivers’ understanding of stroke was shaped by a mix of biomedical knowledge, personal experience, and sociocultural interpretations. Most participants associated stroke with hypertension and diabetes, often describing it as a direct consequence of uncontrolled “pressure” or chronic illness. This suggests some level of exposure to medical explanations, likely through healthcare interactions. However, this knowledge was often fragmented and inconsistently integrated with preventive understanding.

For example, one caregiver noted:

“I know it’s hypertension that brings about stroke.” (Participant 7, female, aged 45).

Another explained:

“When someone’s pressure goes very high and has diabetes too… pressure is the major cause.” (Participant 9, female, aged 52).

Beyond biomedical explanations, some participants emphasized behavioral factors such as poor medication adherence, indicating an awareness of the role of disease management in stroke occurrence.

“…when you default your drugs… it can cause stroke.” (Participant 3, male, aged 50).

At the same time, alternative interpretations emerged, with a few caregivers attributing stroke to stress and excessive thinking, reflecting culturally embedded understandings of illness causation.

“…he overthinks too much… I think that’s what led to his stroke.” (Participant 12, female, aged 42).

Others reported having no prior knowledge of stroke until their relative’s diagnosis, highlighting significant gaps in public awareness.

“I didn’t know anything about stroke until it happened.” (Participant 5, male, aged 38).

Dietary explanations, such as consumption of fatty foods, were also mentioned but often lacked clear biomedical grounding, suggesting partial knowledge mixed with misconceptions.

“…stroke may occur when you mostly take in fatty foods.” (Participant 11, female, aged 47).

Overall, these findings indicate that caregivers’ understanding of stroke is pluralistic, combining medical, experiential, and cultural belief systems.

Theme 2: recognition and onset of stroke

Stroke was widely experienced as a sudden and unexpected event, often occurring without what caregivers perceived as clear warning signs. Many participants described initial symptoms such as dizziness or fatigue as minor conditions, which delayed recognition of the seriousness of the situation.

One caregiver recounted:

“He only felt dizzy… the next morning, he had one-sided weakness.” (Participant 1, male, aged 55).

Similarly:

“…he complained of dizziness… then he fell.” (Participant 2, female, aged 48).

These narratives suggest that limited awareness of early stroke symptoms contributes to delayed response and reinforces the perception of stroke as an abrupt and unpredictable condition.

However, a small group of caregivers reported anticipating the condition due to prior medical advice or family history of stroke.

“…there is a history of stroke… so we were not surprised.” (Participant 5, male, aged 38).

This contrast highlights how prior exposure to health information may influence recognition, though it does not necessarily reduce the overall burden of the condition.

Theme 3: caregiving roles and evolving responsibilities

Caregiving was initially described as highly demanding, with stroke survivors becoming almost entirely dependent on caregivers for basic activities of daily living. Participants reported providing assistance with bathing, feeding, dressing, and mobility, reflecting the severe functional limitations associated with stroke.

“I was bathing him, feeding him, dressing him… doing everything.” (Participant 1, male, aged 55).

This level of dependency placed significant physical and emotional demands on caregivers, particularly in the early stages of the condition.

Over time, however, some caregivers observed gradual improvements in the functional abilities of stroke survivors, leading to a reduction in caregiving intensity.

“…now he can bath, eat, and even iron his clothes.” (Participant 1, male, aged 55).

This indicates that caregiving is not static but evolves alongside the recovery trajectory of the patient. Nevertheless, even with improvement, caregivers often remained actively involved in coordinating care and supporting daily routines.

Theme 4: multidimensional burden, social perceptions, and coping strategies

Interconnected social, economic, and emotional burdens

Caregiving imposed substantial and interconnected burdens on participants’ social lives, economic activities, and overall well-being. Socially, caregivers reported restricted movement and limited participation in community and religious activities due to the need for constant presence.

“…I’m not able to attend social events.” (Participant 5, female, aged 44).

Economically, caregiving disrupted income-generating activities, with some participants reducing work hours or completely abandoning their livelihoods.

“I have quit my business to take care of my mother.” (Participant 4, female, aged 41).

This loss of income, combined with ongoing healthcare expenses, resulted in significant financial strain.

“…no money is coming… we are in difficult times.” (Participant 11, male, aged 46).

However, the extent of financial burden varied depending on access to external support, such as remittances from family members abroad. This highlights underlying socioeconomic inequalities in caregiving experiences.

Stigma, cultural interpretations, and social responses

Caregivers’ experiences were further shaped by societal perceptions of stroke. In some cases, stroke was interpreted as a spiritual condition or moral failing, leading to stigma and negative judgments from the community.

“…people think he is not spiritually strong.” (Participant 2, female, aged 48).

Others reported that stroke was viewed as a fatal condition, creating fear and emotional distress.

“…they say she will die soon.” (Participant 4, female, aged 41).

In response, some caregivers and patients concealed the condition to avoid stigma, while others reported no stigmatizing experiences, suggesting variability in community attitudes.

Coping through faith, reciprocity, and hope

Despite these challenges, caregivers adopted multiple coping strategies rooted in cultural, emotional, and spiritual resources. Reliance on religious faith was particularly prominent, with many participants expressing belief in divine healing and support.

“My encouragement comes from God.” (Participant 3, female, aged 45).

Caregivers were also motivated by a strong sense of obligation and reciprocity, often framed as a moral responsibility to care for relatives who had previously supported them.

“He was there for me… so I must be there for him.” (Participant 2, female, aged 48).

Additionally, observable improvements in patients’ conditions fostered hope and resilience.

“…seeing improvement gives me hope.” (Participant 5, male, aged 38).

These coping mechanisms highlight the role of cultural values, social relationships, and spirituality in sustaining caregivers through the demands of caregiving.

Discussion

This study explored caregivers’ experiences in supporting stroke survivors, focusing on their knowledge of stroke, caregiving burden, sociocultural influences, and coping strategies. The findings demonstrate a complex interplay between biomedical awareness, lived caregiving realities, and sociocultural interpretations of stroke.

Caregivers generally demonstrated awareness of hypertension, diabetes, and other lifestyle-related conditions as key risk factors for stroke. This aligns with global evidence identifying these conditions, along with obesity and lifestyle transitions, as major contributors to stroke incidence, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [1, 7, 10]. Similarly, Ghanaian evidence highlights the growing burden of stroke driven by these modifiable risk factors [3, 8]. However, despite this awareness, caregivers were often unprepared for the sudden onset of stroke and did not consistently demonstrate preventive or early response behaviors. This suggests that knowledge remained largely descriptive rather than functional, reflecting limited translation into practical health literacy. This gap is important because stroke outcomes are highly dependent on early recognition and timely intervention [7, 14]. The findings therefore extend existing evidence by showing that awareness of risk factors alone is insufficient to ensure preparedness, underscoring the need for more practical, action-oriented health education strategies.

Caregivers consistently described stroke as a sudden and unexpected condition, often preceded by vague symptoms such as dizziness or fatigue that were initially misinterpreted. This reflects clinical evidence that stroke presentation varies depending on the extent and location of brain damage and often results in abrupt functional decline [7, 14]. It also aligns with findings that stroke frequently leads to long-term disability and dependence [10]. The sudden onset contributed to shock and delayed recognition even among caregivers who were aware of risk factors or had prior exposure through family history. This reinforces earlier observations that stroke is not consistently perceived as a medical emergency in many low-resource settings, where limited stroke awareness and low perceived threat contribute significantly to delays in seeking timely care [6]. The implication is that community-level education must go beyond risk awareness to emphasize early warning signs and urgency of response, as timely treatment is critical for reducing disability.

The study further revealed that caregiving placed extensive physical, emotional, and financial demands on families. Stroke survivors often required complete assistance with activities of daily living, including feeding, bathing, mobility, and medication management, particularly in the early stages of recovery. This is consistent with global evidence indicating that stroke survivors frequently experience persistent physical and cognitive impairments requiring long-term care [4, 10, 14]. Caregiving responsibilities extended beyond physical care to include emotional support and constant supervision, reflecting the broader impact of stroke on family systems [7]. These demands significantly disrupted caregivers’ employment and social lives, leading to reduced working hours, loss of income, and social withdrawal. Such findings are consistent with evidence linking caregiving to psychological stress and economic hardship [3, 9, 17]. In the Ghanaian context, these burdens are intensified by the absence of formal long-term care and rehabilitation systems, which places almost total responsibility on families [16]. This structural gap suggests that caregiving is not only a private family responsibility but also a reflection of systemic limitations in health service provision. The findings therefore highlight the need for integrated care models that include caregiver support as part of stroke rehabilitation rather than relying solely on informal caregiving systems.

Sociocultural interpretations also shaped caregiving experiences, as stroke was sometimes perceived as having spiritual or moral origins. Such beliefs contributed to stigma in some communities, with stroke being associated with curses or divine punishment. Although this finding was not expected within an urban hospital setting, it reflects broader evidence that health and illness in many sub-Saharan African contexts are often interpreted through spiritual and moral frameworks rather than purely biomedical explanations [7, 14]. In Ghana and similar settings, studies have documented that chronic and disabling conditions, including stroke, are sometimes attributed to supernatural causes, which reinforces stigmatizing attitudes and social distancing [3, 8]. These beliefs influenced social interactions and, in some cases, led caregivers and stroke survivors to conceal the condition. Such concealment is consistent with evidence that stigma reduces help-seeking behavior and limits access to social and emotional support, thereby increasing caregiver burden and isolation [1, 10]. At the same time, the absence of stigma in some cases suggests variability in community perceptions, potentially influenced by education levels, exposure to biomedical knowledge, and prior contact with healthcare services [14]. These findings highlight the importance of culturally sensitive health education strategies that address misconceptions while respectfully engaging existing belief systems in order to reduce stigma and improve community support for stroke survivors and their families.

Despite these challenges, caregivers demonstrated resilience through multiple coping strategies. Religious faith emerged as the most prominent coping mechanism, providing emotional strength and hope in the face of caregiving demands. This aligns with previous evidence showing the importance of spirituality in coping with chronic illness caregiving in similar contexts [11, 13]. Caregiving was often framed as a moral obligation grounded in reciprocity and strong family responsibility, consistent with evidence that stroke caregiving is frequently perceived as an expression of love, duty, and repayment for prior support received within the family [5, 15]. This sense of obligation, combined with emotional reciprocity and the perception of patient improvement, has been shown to sustain caregivers and reduce perceived burden during prolonged caregiving trajectories [15]. These findings suggest that effective caregiver interventions should not only address burden and stress but also recognize and integrate culturally embedded coping resources such as faith and family solidarity.

Overall, the study provides important insight into the lived realities of caregivers of stroke survivors in Ghana, an area that remains underexplored despite the increasing burden of stroke in the region [3, 8]. The qualitative design allowed for a nuanced understanding of caregiving experiences beyond measurable burden indicators, capturing social, cultural, and emotional dimensions often missed in quantitative studies. However, the study is limited by its small sample size and single-site design, which may affect transferability of findings to other settings. Additionally, reliance on self-reported data may introduce recall or social desirability bias. Despite these limitations, the findings contribute valuable context-specific knowledge that can inform both policy and practice.

Conclusion

This study highlights the significant physical, emotional, and financial burdens faced by family caregivers of stroke survivors in Ghana. While caregivers generally possessed knowledge of stroke risk factors such as hypertension and diabetes, many were still unprepared for the sudden onset of the condition. The demands of caregiving, including assisting with daily living activities, led to considerable disruptions in their work, finances, and social lives. Additionally, caregivers and stroke survivors faced stigmatization, with stroke being viewed as a curse by some community members. Despite these challenges, caregivers relied on coping mechanisms such as religious faith and reciprocating past care from survivors.

The findings emphasize the critical role family caregivers play in the care and rehabilitation of stroke survivors in Ghana, where formal caregiving services are limited. The study also draws attention to the emotional and psychological toll on caregivers, who often face stress, depression, and financial strain due to their caregiving responsibilities. The results underscore the urgent need for increased support and resources for caregivers to ensure better care for stroke survivors and improve the well-being of both caregivers and survivors. The following recommendations aim to reduce the caregiving burden, improve the quality of care for stroke survivors, and promote the well-being of caregivers in Ghana.

Recommendation

The findings suggest the need for a multi-level response involving health systems, community structures, and policy institutions to address the burden of stroke caregiving in Ghana.

The Ghana Health Service and Ministry of Health should integrate structured caregiver support into stroke care pathways within hospitals and rehabilitation units. This may include mandatory caregiver education sessions prior to discharge, focusing on practical skills in patient mobility, feeding, medication management, and recognition of complications. Such structured preparation could reduce the current gap between awareness and preparedness identified in this study.

In addition, hospitals should establish formal caregiver support and respite care services, particularly in tertiary and secondary facilities where stroke cases are frequently managed. This would help reduce caregiver burnout and prevent economic displacement resulting from prolonged caregiving responsibilities.

To address financial hardship, policymakers should consider expanding the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) to include selected aspects of long-term stroke rehabilitation and caregiver-related services. This could also be complemented by targeted social protection schemes for families affected by long-term disability conditions such as stroke.

At the community level, the Ghana Health Service, in collaboration with local health directorates and community health officers, should intensify stroke awareness campaigns that emphasize early warning signs and the urgency of seeking care. These campaigns should specifically target misconceptions that delay treatment and contribute to late hospital presentation.

Psychological support systems should also be institutionalized within stroke care services. Hospitals should incorporate routine counseling services for caregivers and establish caregiver support groups, enabling peer-to-peer emotional sharing and coping support.

Furthermore, health workers should be trained to adopt culturally sensitive communication strategies that acknowledge religious and sociocultural beliefs while promoting biomedical understanding of stroke. This is essential to reduce stigma and encourage earlier health-seeking behavior.

Finally, collaboration with religious and community leaders should be strengthened, as they play a significant role in shaping health beliefs and caregiving norms. Their involvement in education and psychosocial support programs may enhance acceptance and sustainability of interventions.
