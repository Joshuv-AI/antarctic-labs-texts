Effects of clay grains on the shear properties of unsaturated loess and microscopic mechanism
Authors: Jianing Hao, Shuairun Zhu, Ping Cheng, Guanxiong Ren, Libing Du, Qing Li, Shili Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Different clay grains affect the structural strength of loess through the cementation of skeletal particles. This study investigates both clay-clay grains and quartz-clay grains. Clay-clay grains mixed loess (CM-L) and quartz-clay grains loess (Q-L) samples were prepared, and their unsaturated shear properties analyzed. X-ray diffractometry (XRD) analysis was conducted to determine the types and proportions of clay grains. Ball mill grinding and laser particle size analysis were employed to ensure comparable sizes of clay grains, while mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP) tests confirmed similar pore characteristics. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) and unsaturated triaxial consolidation and drainage tests explored the impact of different clay grains on loess shear characteristics, assessing both microscopic and macroscopic performance. The results indicate that CM-L loess exhibits higher cohesion and a lower internal friction angle at the same matric suction level. The cohesion and internal friction angle of both CM-L and Q-L loess exhibit a linear relationship within the range of 50–200 kPa matrix suction. The cohesive force ranges for CM-L and Q-L loess are 60.31–80.07 kPa and 43.01–69.60 kPa, respectively, while the ranges for internal friction angles are 19.95°-19.59° and 25.91°-25.06°, respectively. Compared to Q-L loess, CM-L loess exhibits an average difference in cohesion of 23.18 kPa and in internal friction angle of -5.72°. The microscopic variation in shear strength can be attributed to the “fish scale-like” interlocking state between clay-clay grains and the “tower-like” scattering arrangement of quartz-clay grains. In conclusion, the effect of different clay-grain types on the shear strength of loess varies significantly. The present study elucidates the relationship between the influence of different clay grains on the mechanical properties of loess and their microscopic structural characteristics, thereby providing crucial data for investigating the microscopic effects on the structural strength of loess.

Introduction

Loess is a typical structural soil, extensively found in arid and semi-arid regions, primarily composed of chalk, sand, and clay grains1–4. This sedimentary deposit is characterized by its aeolian origin and distinct mineralogical composition, which includes both primary and secondary mineral components. The primary mineral particles, including quartz, feldspar, mica, and calcite, do not exhibit cohesive properties and act as the soil skeleton in the structural characteristics of loess, being less affected by water content5,6. In contrast, the secondary cement category plays a role in bonding skeleton particles in the structural characteristics of loess7–9. This category includes inorganic salts (soluble and insoluble) as well as clay particles (generally kaolinite, ilmenite, montmorillonite, and chlorite). This secondary cement is greatly affected by water content5,10, which is the focus of the study on the structural destruction mechanism of loess and accounts for its unique mechanical properties. Water molecules can form bonded water films of different thicknesses around the particles11. Changes in the thickness of the water film can affect the contact friction and sliding friction between the particles, thus affecting the shear strength, internal friction angle, and cohesion of loess12,13. Therefore, both the content and type of clay grains substantially impact the strength of loess, with different types of clay grains leading to differences in the strength and deformation characteristics of loess.

Several scholars have conducted extensive research on the influence of clay-clay grains on soil properties. Through microstructural, chemical, and image analysis, it has been firmly established that the cementation of calcite and clay between grains plays a crucial role in determining the metastable structural characteristics of loess5. The introduction of clay-clay grains into soil has been proven to significantly enhance its matric suction, cohesion, and shear strength, with these parameters exhibiting a proportional increase in relation to the clay content14–17. Due to variations in clay particle content across different regions of China’s Loess Plateau, researchers have collected samples from multiple locations to investigate these differences18. Their findings indicate that an increased concentration of clay in loess leads to alterations in water content dynamics, pore water pressure generation, and damage processes associated with loess slopes. Specifically, higher levels of clay content shift the damage pattern from general degradation towards erosion and shallow sliding phenomena, ultimately resulting in more severe erosion damage. This research also highlights that a higher proportion of clay-clay grains improves effective cohesion, liquid-plastic limit values as well as cementation between soil particles19–21. This is related to the inherent properties of clay itself. Based on its volumetric change upon water addition, clay can be classified into expanded and non-expanded types22. Their natural coexistence results in complex shear behavior23–26, which is a significant contributor to the intricate mechanical properties of soils containing clays, such as loess. Some scholars have also investigated the influence of nonclay-clay grains on the properties of loess. Research conducted by Adami and Rinaldi on remoulded loess samples stabilized with amorphous silica observed that the inclusion of silica resulted in time-dependent increases in stiffness and strength. This enhancement is primarily attributed to cementation processes facilitated by silica in an alkaline environment, which bonds the particles together, thereby creating a much stiffer structure27. Similarly, Kong et al.21 modified the content of non-clay grains in loess by adding nano-SiO2 and found that higher nano-SiO2 content led to an increase in compressive strength of loess. These improvements were attributed to physical rather than chemical interactions, as confirmed by mineralogical species analyses. Considering that loess typically exists in an unsaturated state, matric suction plays a significant role28–34. Triaxial tests have been conducted to analyze the deformation, strength properties, and variations in moisture content both compacted loess and in-situ loess under unsaturated conditions. These tests demonstrate that both matric suction and confining pressure significantly influence the mechanical behavior of unsaturated loess35–37. This body of research further elucidates the intricate relationship between structural properties and moisture dynamics in loess. Contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of its geotechnical behavior and informing enhanced engineering practices, it has been observed that collapsible unsaturated gypsum soil exhibits similar wetting characteristics38,39 and changing patterns in parameters such as shear strength, cohesion, and angle of internal friction with varying water content as unsaturated loess40,41. These findings can serve as a valuable reference for further investigating the impact of different clay particles on the shear strength of unsaturated loess.

The mechanical behaviour of unsaturated soils is governed by their internal suction stresses, which are closely related to the moisture conditions of the soil. The effective stress principle proposed by Bishop42 laid the foundation for the mechanical analysis of unsaturated soils, while the relational equation proposed by Khalili and Khabbaz43 further deepened the understanding of the shear strength of unsaturated soils. With the increasing understanding of the properties of unsaturated soils, researchers have begun to explore the response of unsaturated soils under complex conditions such as temperature changes, combined loads, and seismic actions. Studies have shown that the mechanical behaviour of unsaturated soils is not only affected by the traditional effective stress, but also significantly affected by changes in soil water content status and suction44–47. Matrix suction occupies a central position in the mechanical modelling of unsaturated soils, and its influence can be reflected by the adjustment of suction stress characteristic curve (SWCC) and shear strength parameters43,48. With the change of environmental conditions, especially the transient flow during infiltration, the suction and corresponding physical and chemical properties of the soil will be changed, which in turn affects the soil’s bearing capacity and stability49–51. In addition, it has been shown that changes in temperature have a significant effect on lateral earth pressure in unsaturated soils, a finding that is important for understanding and predicting soil behaviour under changes in ambient temperature44. Meanwhile, the complex response of unsaturated soils when subjected to inclined and eccentric loads has also been widely studied, and these studies help to design safer and more reliable civil engineering structures47,52,53. And they provide the basis and reference for the study of mechanical properties of unsaturated loess.

Scholars have conducted extensive research on the influence of clay grains on the mechanical properties of loess, unveiling their significance as a crucial factor impacting these properties. However, limited studies have been conducted on the differential effects of various types of clay grains and the microscopic mechanisms underlying their influence on loess’s mechanical properties. In this study, we aim to quantify the disparities in how clay and non-clay clay grains affect the shear properties of unsaturated modified loess by creating comparable microphysical conditions, and analyse the influence mechanism from the microscopic level. The modified loess samples underwent GDS unsaturated triaxial consolidation and drainage shear tests to investigate the effects of clay grains with similar particle size characteristics and non-clay quartz grains on the shear characteristics of unsaturated loess. The microscopic mechanisms were elucidated by capturing SEM images at various magnifications.

The research findings have substantiated the imperative of investigating the mechanical properties of loess under diverse particle compositions. Moreover, they have furnished valuable insights and empirical evidence for comprehending the microscopic aspects, conducting finite element simulations, and employing machine learning techniques to study the mechanical characteristics of loess. The expanded outcomes derived from this research can be effectively applied in engineering practices encompassing foundation design, stability assessment of loess slopes, and loess improvement and other engineering practice.

Materials and methods

Study area and sample parameters

The loess samples were collected from a natural landslide area in Yan’an City, situated in the central part of the Loess Plateau (109°34′41″E, 36°35′24″N)(Fig. 1(a), Fig. 1(b1)). Excavation was conducted at a depth of 2.5 m from the soil surface to collect natural loess samples (Fig. 1(b2)). The sampling point is a typical natural loess landslide section, providing an easily accessible and representative sample. Adjacent to the sampling site, there is an ongoing excavation and filling project as well as a large-scale loess landslide control and observation site, facilitating subsequent engineering applications and comparative research of the findings. Material and specimen preparation

The majority of loess is typically homogeneous33–35. In this study, two modified loess samples, CM-L and Q-L, were prepared based on loess samples collected from Yan’an. The homogenized modified loess samples CM-L and Q-L exhibit consistent skeleton particles and physical states. Specifically, they incorporate clay-clay grains and quartz-clay grains into the original loess54 at a mass fraction of 5%. The preparation process of the specimens is illustrated in Fig. 4.

When preparing the loess substrate, firstly, the loess is crushed and subjected to air-drying. Secondly, it undergoes sieving through a 2.5 mm sieve. Subsequently, it is dried in a constant temperature oven at 105 °C for 8 h to facilitate moisture evaporation within the soil.

Flow chart of the specimen preparation: (a) Loess base material preparation. (b) clay-clay grains. (c) quartz-clay grains. (d) Triaxial specimen preparation process. (e) CM-L Triaxial specimen (f) Q-L Triaxial specimen.

In the preparation of CM-L and Q-L modified loess, the clay grains tend to agglomerate easily in humid environments due to their large specific surface area, light weight, and strong water absorption capacity55. The loess base material and clay grains must be thoroughly mixed together, which is achieved through a four-step process: drying, pre-mixing, sieving, and re-mixing. Drying, the various materials are dried at a low temperature to ensure their readiness for use. Pre-mixing, the different materials are placed in a mixing tray and initially blended with a spatula until they achieve visual homogeneity. Sieving, after the pre-mixing stage, the materials are passed through a 1 mm sieve at least 6 times. Re-mixing, the dry mix is vigorously shaken in a PE bag in all directions, ensuring no less than 20 shakes in each direction.

The samples were sprayed with deionized water containing 13.6% natural moisture content (refer to Table 1) and subsequently encapsulated in PE bags. The were stored under controlled room temperature conditions and shielded from light for a minimum of 48 h to ensure an even distribution of water content throughout the specimens. The water-preserved samples were subsequently sieved through a 1 mm mesh sieve, and a five-layer static pressure tool was employed to fabricate a cylindrical standard triaxial specimen measuring 50 mm in diameter and 100 mm in height for suction-controlled unsaturated triaxial compression experiments utilizing a GDS triaxial apparatus56. The study conducted a total of 10 CM-L and Q-L specimens, out of which 9 were utilized for the GDS unsaturated triaxial test, while the remaining specimen was employed for SEM imaging and MIP test.

Test methods

(1) Microstructural characterization by scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

To investigate the spatial positional relationship between skeleton particles and clay grains, as well as the distribution state of clay grains in the modified loess samples, microstructural analyses were conducted using a Thermoscientific PrismaE scanning electron microscope. Prior to SEM scanning, soil samples were prepared for microscopic examination: they underwent natural air-drying, were cut into strips measuring approximately 2 × 1.5 × 1.5 cm with a 2 mm-deep groove carved in the center, and folded along these grooves.A relatively flat side was selected and any loose dust on the surface was removed using a rubber bulb. Subsequently, the specimen was affixed to the base of an electron microscope observation chamber for imaging purposes utilizing a scanning electron microscope. Images at magnifications of 500×, 2000×, 5000×, and 20,000× that provided clear and suitable details for analysis were chosen.

(2) Unsaturated triaxial shear test

The unsaturated triaxial experiments are conducted in accordance with the “Standard for testing methods of unsaturated soils (T/CECS 1337–2023)”. The experimental setup for consolidation and drainage shear (CD) tests involved the utilization of a GDS fully automated unsaturated triaxial shear tester, as illustrated in Fig. 5. The utilization of axis translation techniques in a three-axis instrument facilitates the simultaneous manipulation and real-time recording of multiple experimental parameters, encompassing matric suction, pore water pressure, pore air pressure, confining pressure, and axial pressure in comparison to conventional triaxial apparatuses. The data recording frequency of 10 s per iteration provides a more precise reflection of the experimental process and parameter variations.

The confining pressure was set at 100 kPa, 200 kPa, and 300 kPa, while the matric suction was set at 50 kPa, 100 kPa, and 200 kPa respectively.

The laboratory temperature was maintained at a constant 22 °C, and the specimens were prepared using airless deionized water. To prevent excessive moisture content changes that could affect specimen density, each specimen preparation time did not exceed 3 min. A total of 9 specimens were prepared. Due to the lengthy testing time required for each specimen, repeating the experiment under identical conditions was unnecessary. Continuous monitoring of data changes during the experiment is essential, and if any sudden changes occur in the process, timely troubleshooting should be conducted before re-experimentation. The testing process is divided into three stages, enabling control over the mechanical behavior of the soil under different conditions. The first phase, soil water balance, involves achieving the desired matric suction by regulating pore air and applying pore water pressure. It requires a significant amount of time to attain the desired suction, and equilibrium can be considered achieved when the specimen’s volume change is less than 0.05% of the total volume within 24 h. The second phase, isosuction consolidation, entails maintaining a constant matric suction while adjusting the confining pressure to reach the target value. This process linearly increases perimeter pressure with a maximum rate not exceeding 50 kPa/hour. When there is a change in counterpressure volume within 0.03 ml within 2 h, it indicates that consolidation has reached its standard. The third phase, constant rate shearing, necessitates maintaining both matric suction and net confining pressure at a constant level. To ensure dissipation of pore water pressure effectively, it is crucial to keep shear rate as low as possible at a consistent rate of 0.012 mm/minute. The test will be stopped once axial strain reaches 15%.

Results and analysis

Analysis of similar physical conditions

In order to mitigate the impact of inconsistent grain size and pore distribution between CM-L and Q-L modified loess, it is crucial to establish comparable physical conditions. Firstly, we ensured that the particle size characteristics of the clay-clay grains mixture and quartz-clay grains added to the loess base material were similar, as confirmed by the results depicted in Fig. 3. Additionally, we analyzed the pore distribution characteristics of both modified loess samples using mercury intrusion porosimetry method. By measuring their respective pore distributions under varying external pressures, we determined the corresponding pore volume for each specific pore size. The MIP experiments presented in Fig. 6 demonstrate a high degree of consistency in pore size distribution patterns between these two types of modified loess. This finding indicates that differences in shear strength among modified loess are primarily attributed to inherent properties of distinct clay grains.

Microstructural characterization

The electron microscope scans of CM-L and Q-L loess at various magnifications are presented in Figs. 7 and 8, respectively. Specifically, the magnifications (a)-(d) correspond to 500x, 2000x, 5000x, and 20,000x.

At a magnification of 500× (Fig. 7(a) and Fig. 8(a)), the characteristics of large particles, known as skeleton particles, in both types of loess become clearly discernible. The skeleton particles of CM-L loess exhibit distinct boundaries, are well dispersed, and demonstrate a pronounced sense of granularity, whereas those of Q-L loess display indistinct boundaries and exhibit a certain degree of agglomeration. At a magnification of 2000× (Fig. 7(b) and Fig. 8(b)), the CM-L loess exhibits a high concentration of skeletal particles, with a low concentration of clay particle clusters and aggregates. In contrast, the Q-L loess displays a low concentration of skeletal particles, with a high concentration of clay particle clusters and aggregates. At a magnification of 5000× (Fig. 7(c) and Fig. 8(c)), the skeletal particles of both types of loess do not make direct contact with each other, but rather are bonded together by clay grains. At a magnification of 20,000× (Fig. 7(d) and Fig. 8(d)), the distinct morphological characteristics of individual clay grains become apparent. The flaky nature of clay grains in CM-L loess and the globular shape of those in Q-L loess are characteristic features, which align with the findings from SEM experiments on clay particles conducted by previous scholars57–60.

Macromechanical characterization

The deviatoric stress-axial strain relationship curves of CM-L and Q-L, subjected to the same matric suction but varying confining pressures, are depicted in Fig. 9. It is observed that an increase in confining pressure leads to a corresponding rise in deviatoric stress, thereby indicating prominent strain hardening characteristics.

The deviatoric stress curves of the two soil samples exhibit a consistent and evident regularity, with notably high initial curve slopes at small axial strain ε1 values. The turning point for the change in curve slope occurs at an axial strain ε1 of 1.5%. Matric suction has a limited influence on the strain characteristics of the two modified loess samples; however, there is still a tendency for them to transition from strong hardening to weak hardening as matric suction increases. When comparing equal axial strains, the CM-L loess exhibits lower deviatoric stress than the Q-L loess.

According to the theory of shear strength of unsaturated loess43,56 the deviatoric stress at the point of specimen failure is defined as the shear strength (qf). In accordance with the guidelines for determining shear strength, for strain-hardening type behavior, the shear strength is considered as 15% of the axial strain’s deviatoric stress; whereas for strain-softening type behavior, it is determined by taking into account the maximum deviatoric stress56.

Therefore, the impact of different types of clay grains on the structural properties of loess exhibits significant variations and should not be disregarded. In comparison to nonclay-clay grains, clay-clay grains exert a stronger bonding effect and a weaker frictional effect on the intergranularity of the loess skeleton particles.

Micromechanisms of clay grains affecting the strength of loess

After analyzing the aforementioned results of unsaturated triaxial shear tests, it is evident that there are significant differences in the structural properties, namely cohesion and angle of internal friction, between modified loess CM-L with added clay-clay grains and modified loess Q-L with added nonclay-clay grains. The similarity in the amount of added clay grains, pore distribution between the two samples, and consistency in the distribution pattern of different pore diameters indicate that both the properties of clay grains themselves and their interactions with skeleton particles have a substantial impact on loess’ ability to withstand external forces. After analyzing the SEM images of the modified loess at two different magnifications, it was found that the physical properties, morphological characteristics, fugitive states, and spatial distribution modes of the different clay grains are different.

The clay grains in CM-L loess predominantly exhibit a flaky morphology, which is characteristic of clay-clay grains. In contrast, the clay grains in Q-L loess are predominantly spherical, typical of quartz-clay grains. Furthermore, the spatial arrangement differs between these two types of clay grains: the fish scale-like structure of clay-clay grains allows them to bond to larger skeleton particles and form distinct lamellar laminations, thereby better reflecting the morphology of the skeleton particles. This accounts for the clearer particle boundaries observed at lower magnifications (500×). On the other hand, quartz-clay grains display a tower-like structure with weaker and more dispersed spherical features on an individual level. These quartz-clay grains extend further into surrounding spaces on the surface of skeleton particles, resulting in ambiguous particle boundaries at lower magnifications (500×).

The application of confining and axial pressures induces rotation and displacement of the skeleton particles. As the confining pressure increases, the interaction forces between the particles are enhanced, resulting in a greater resistance to rotation and displacement of the skeleton particles. This is macroscopically observed as an increase in shear strength.

Different types of clay grains have varying effects on skeletal particles, leading to a significant disparity in the increase of shear strength between the two types of loess under identical confining pressure, despite both exhibiting an enhancement in shear strength. Evidently, CM-L loess demonstrates lower shear strength compared to Q-L loess, indicating weaker cementation and friction effects of clay-clay grains as opposed to nonclay-clay grains at the natural water content (13.6%). This primarily arises from the fact that water present in unsaturated loess not only provides matric suction but also combines with clay grains to form a water film that exerts a robust lubricating effect. The morphology and distribution state of diverse clay grains vary significantly, resulting in a notable discrepancy in the role played by the water film surrounding soil particles.

The disparity in the shear strength parameters of the two modified loess samples, subjected to identical matric suction conditions, can be attributed to the influence of water film on distinct clay grain types11. The augmented presence of clay grains in this experiment intensified the impact of water film, leading to significant variations in the structural strength between clay and nonclay-clay grains.

These micro-mechanisms accurately reflect the laws governing the relationship between shear strength qf and mean stress pf for the two modified loesses depicted in Fig. 10; Table 4, which quantitatively express the correlation between the shear strength parameter and clay minerals from a macroscopic perspective, further highlighting significant differences in strength parameters among different clay species under similar unsaturated conditions.

Therefore, the micromechanism by which clay grains affect the shear properties of unsaturated loess is that the physical properties determine the bond strength and friction coefficient of the clay grains in the unsaturated state; the morphological characteristics affect the contact properties and contact area between the particles; and the distribution state and spatial distribution affect the interparticle stability. In summary, larger particles form a skeleton structure providing support, while clay grains contribute to bonding, filling, and wrapping actions playing a cohesive role. This elucidates why CM-L loess exhibits higher cohesion and lower internal friction angle under identical matric suction conditions.

Conclusions

In this study, it is revealed through both macroscopic mechanical experiments and microscopic characterization that the influence of different clay grain types on the shear properties of unsaturated loess under similar conditions cannot be ignored. Therefore, considering the impact of various clay grains is a crucial factor in studying the mechanical properties of unsaturated loess. It should be noted that conducting experiments on unsaturated soils requires advanced equipment, strict experimental conditions, and a lengthy experimental period, making it challenging to apply them widely in engineering practice. Therefore, in addition to studying different types of clay grain, it is also necessary to study the mechanical properties of loess with different clay grain content to provide more comprehensive data for finite element simulation prediction and artificial intelligence prediction. Additionally, The study on the improvement of wet subsidence properties of loess can also be an important extension of this study. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1) The influence of matric suction on the strength parameters of CM-L and Q-L loess exhibited significant variations. The cohesion of both modified loess demonstrates a linear increase with an increment in matric suction, albeit slightly less for CM-L loess compared to Q-L loess. The slopes of the c-s relationship curves are 0.1317 and 0.1773, respectively. Conversely, the internal friction angle experiences a slight decrease as matric suction increases. The slopes of the c-s relationship curves are 0.1317 and 0.1773, respectively. Conversely, the internal friction angle decreases slightly with increasing matric suction. The slopes of the φ-s relationship curves are − 0.0024 and − 0.0057, respectively. Under equivalent suction conditions, CM-L loess exhibits higher cohesion than Q-L loess while possessing a lower internal friction angle than that observed in Q-L loess. Within the range of 50 to 200 kPa suction levels, there exists an average difference in cohesion between these two types of loesses amounting to 23.18 kPa; similarly, there is an average difference in internal friction angle measuring at -5.72°.Under identical matric suction and confining pressure conditions, destructive stress by CM-L loess is comparatively lower than that by Q-L Loess.

(2) The microstructural characteristics of CM-L loess and Q-L loess exhibit significant differences. SEM images show that at lower magnification (500-5000x), the skeletal particle boundaries of CM-L loess are more clearly defined, with less clay lumps and focal clumps present. At higher magnifications (20,000×), the morphological features of clay grains become evident, displaying flaky shapes for clay-clay grains in CM-L loess and spherical shapes for nonclay-clay grains in Q-L loess. The bonding between clay grains and non-clay grains to the surface of skeleton particles takes on “fish scale-like” and “tower-like” forms respectively.

(3) The clay grains exhibit diverse physical properties, morphological characteristics, distribution states, and spatial distributions, which result in distinct bonding effects on the skeleton particles and subsequently influence the shear properties of unsaturated loess. Variations in the surface water film layer among different clay grains and their water binding shapes lead to varying magnitudes and directions of friction between the skeleton particles. These factors contribute to disparities in loess cohesion and internal friction angle, thereby impacting the shear strength of both unsaturated loess samples.
