Comparison of Clinical Characteristics of Pneumocystis Pneumonia with or without Human Immunodeficiency Virus: A Prognosis Contrary to Initial Clinical Features
Authors: Juyeong Lee, Joong Sik Eom, Hye Jin Shi, Shin Hee Hong, Yoonseon Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) is a life-threatening infection. The prevalence of PJP in patients without human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) (non-HIV-PJP) is increasing. Previous studies have separately evaluated the clinical characteristics of PJP patients with and without HIV infection. Therefore, this study aimed to comparatively analyze the clinical characteristics of PJP patients with and without HIV.Materials and MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed patients with PJP who were admitted to a single tertiary center for 15 years. PJP patients were classified according to the presence or absence of HIV, and differences in their clinical characteristics and prognoses were analyzed. Forty-four HIV-PJP patients and 175 non-HIV-PJP patients were included.ResultsCough, dyspnea, typical computed tomography findings of PJP, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) pneumonitis co-infection were more common in the HIV-PJP group. The durations from symptom onset to PJ polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing and to the start of treatment were longer in the HIV-PJP group; however, the time from the PJ PCR test to antibiotic administration was shorter in the HIV-PJP group. The 28-day survival rates were 68.6% and 88.6% in the non-HIV-PJP and HIV-PJP groups, respectively (p=0.011). The survival rate of patients with CMV pneumonitis co-infection in the non-HIV-PJP group was significantly lower.ConclusionThe clinical characteristics of patients with HIV-PJP and non-HIV-PJP were statistically different. The initial clinical features appeared to be worse in the HIV-PJP group; however, the 28-day mortality rate was higher in the non-HIV-PJP group.Keywords: Pneumonia, pneumocystis, immunocompromised, HIV, cytomegalovirus infection, mortality rate

INTRODUCTION

Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) is a life-threatening infection in immunocompromised patients, particularly those with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, organ transplants, malignancies, and autoimmune diseases receiving high-dose glucocorticoids or certain immunosuppressants.1,2 Before the 1980s, PJP was known as a type of pneumonia that mainly affected immunocompromised individuals. However, it was studied in earnest in the 1980s owing to a rapid increase in the number of cases related to HIV, and research indicates that the number of patients with PJP and HIV (HIV-PJP) has decreased owing to the use of antiretroviral agents and prophylaxis for PJP.3,4,5 Meanwhile, the prevalence of PJP in patients without HIV (non-HIV-PJP) has increased, and non-HIV-PJP has become more common than HIV-PJP in recent years6,7 as a result of increased use of immunosuppressive agents to treat conditions such as malignancies, autoimmune diseases, and solid organ transplantation.1,7

Previous studies have evaluated the clinical manifestations, underlying factors, and outcomes of PJP in patients with or without HIV separately; however, relatively few studies have compared the two groups. A study published in 1984 found a longer median duration of symptoms, lower mean respiratory rate, and higher median room-air arterial oxygen tension in HIV-PJP patients than that in non-HIV-PJP patients; however, the survival rates were similar between the two groups.8 Significant differences in P. jirovecii counts and lung inflammation between patients with and without HIV who had PJP were found in a study in 1989.9 Another retrospective study, conducted from 2005 to 2013, found that the mortality rate of the non-HIV-PJP group was higher than that of the HIV-PJP group.10 However, some studies evaluated the underlying factors of non-HIV-PJP without including HIV-PJP.11,12 A recent retrospective study comparing the clinical characteristics of patients with HIV-PJP and non-HIV-PJP with coinfection at two tertiary care hospitals in the United States found that cough and altered general condition were more prominent in the HIV-PJP group, but mortality was significantly higher in the non-HIV-PJP group, especially among those with cytomegalovirus (CMV) pneumonitis coinfection.13

The clinical characteristics of PJP may change owing to changes in various underlying issues, such as the prevalence and treatment of related diseases. A study in the United States found that the proportion of deaths due to PJP has been gradually decreasing in the HIV-PJP patient population, given that anti-retroviral therapy (ART) and PJP prophylaxis for HIV have been actively implemented for a long time, whereas PJP prophylaxis in the non-HIV population is relatively less widespread.14 Although a similar trend was observed in another study conducted in South Korea,15 recent studies comparing the clinical characteristics of HIV-PJP vs. non-HIV-PJP patients are lacking, particularly in South Korea. Therefore, this study aimed to comparatively analyze the clinical characteristics of patients with PJP with and without HIV who received PJP treatment over the last 15 years at a single tertiary teaching hospital.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

We retrospectively reviewed adult patients diagnosed with PJP who were admitted to Gil Medical Center, a large tertiary care teaching hospital with 1450 beds in South Korea, from September 2008 to September 2023. The diagnosis of PJP was defined as positive results on P. jirovecii polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or identification of P. jirovecii by periodic acid-Schiff and Gomori methenamine silver staining, performed on expectorated sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), in addition to clinical symptoms, signs, and radiologic findings.16,17 We collected data from 219 patients who presented with symptoms and signs such as fever, cough, dyspnea, and pulmonary infiltration on radiologic tests; underwent multiple tests; and were ultimately diagnosed with PJP. Thirty-six patients were diagnosed with HIV-PJP and 80 with non-HIV-PJP using BALF. Two patients with HIV-PJP were diagnosed using periodic acid-Schiff and Gomori methenamine silver staining of BALF instead of PCR.

We excluded patients with a positive PCR result but no constitutional symptoms and/or signs. Patients with concurrent HIV infection and immunosuppression for other causes were excluded. For example, patients with an HIV infection who received chemotherapy for diffuse large B-cell lymphoma were excluded from the study. Patients who received empirical treatment based on a clinical presumption alone without positive PCR results were also excluded. We further excluded patients who were transferred before their clinical course could be confirmed and could not be traced, who were discharged voluntarily, or who refused treatment.

Variables and definitions

Patients were divided into two groups: HIV-PJP and non-HIV-PJP. Patients with HIV-PJP were reviewed for their HIV diagnosis and history of antiviral therapy. Patients with non-HIV-PJP were reviewed for underlying diseases, history of immunosuppressant use, and steroid dosage as a cause of immunodeficiency. Patients with an unclear or unidentified cause of immunodeficiency were not excluded.

Constitutional symptoms included fever (≥38.0℃), cough, dyspnea, tachypnea (respiratory rate ≥30/min), and confusion, and signs included hypotension (systolic blood pressure <90 mm Hg) and any condition requiring an oxygen supply device (nasal prong, simple mask, reservoir mask, high-flow nasal cannula, or mechanical ventilation). Since confirming the oxygen demand was difficult owing to the research method, the initial and peak oxygen demands were indirectly estimated based on the type of oxygen supply device. The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was used to evaluate comorbidities at admission.

White blood cell (WBC), absolute neutrophil, absolute lymphocyte, and platelet counts; high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels; chest radiography and computed tomography (CT) scans; and the presence of CMV deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA)emia and CMV pneumonitis were reviewed. The cluster of differentiation (CD)4+T cell lymphocyte count was evaluated only in patients with HIV-PJP. All patients underwent chest radiography and CT. Diffuse bilateral ground-glass opacities on chest radiographs were considered typical findings. Diffuse ground-glass opacity involving the perihilar or mid-zones, reticular opacity or septal thickening, and a crazy-paving pattern on CT were considered typical findings. Other findings, such as pleural effusion, cavitation, focal consolidation, and pneumothorax, were considered atypical.18,19 Regardless of the quantitative value of whole blood CMV PCR, all positive cases were classified as CMV DNAemia. CMV pneumonitis was defined as a case in which whole blood CMV PCR or BALF CMV PCR was positive and the attending physician diagnosed CMV pneumonitis based on various clinical characteristics and disease course, including the patient’s symptoms, signs, and imaging findings. Considering the possibility of subjective physician judgment, the research team re-evaluated whether the clinical progress was actually consistent with CMV pneumonitis. None of the patients attempted lung biopsy for the diagnosis of CMV pneumonitis.

The times from symptom onset to antibiotic administration and from symptom onset to PJ PCR test prescription were measured. The time from PJ PCR prescription to antibiotic dosing was also measured. Specimens collected for PJ PCR were obtained and stored on the prescription date, sent to an external laboratory three times a week, and required 7 days for results to be reported. There was no time limit for PJ PCR prescription and specimen acquisition. Empirical administration was defined as the administration of trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) before the PJ PCR results were reported. TMP-SMX administration, dosage, and duration and alternative drugs administered were reviewed. For patients with CMV pneumonitis, the administration period of ganciclovir or valganciclovir was confirmed. The medications and indications used to treat PJP, which were used as standards, are listed in the Supplementary Material (only online). The appropriateness of TMP-SMX, its alternatives, and adjuvant glucocorticoid therapy in terms of indication and sufficient dose was evaluated (i.e., whether it was within 80%–120% of the regular dose for the specified period). The association of survival rate with oxygen demand, appropriate antibiotics and their timely administration, CMV pneumonitis complications, and glucocorticoid administration was assessed. The 28-day all-cause mortality rates were also assessed.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The variables are described using numbers and proportions. All tests were two-sided, and significance was set at p<0.05. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test, and odds ratios (ORs) were calculated. All ORs described in this study are unadjusted. Oxygen demand was indirectly assessed based on the type of oxygen supply device, using linear-by-linear association analysis, which evaluates whether there is a linear trend in the relationship between two ordinal variables. Fisher’s exact test was used to analyze confusion and to determine the association between confusion and appropriate antibiotic administration in HIV-PJP patients, as well as the association of CMV pneumonitis with adjuvant glucocorticoid administration and survival rate. For continuous variables, an independent sample T test was used, and the two groups were compared using a 95% confidence interval. The Mann–Whitney test was used to compare the treatment period for CMV pneumonitis. Kaplan–Meier curves were used to compare the 28-day survival rates between the two groups. The significance of the survival curves was tested using the log-rank test.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Gachon University Gil Medical Center Institutional Review Board (IRB No. GAIRB2023-464) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Since this was a retrospective study, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

RESULTS

Among the patients with HIV-PJP, 39 were newly diagnosed with HIV at the time of the visit, and five had been previously diagnosed but had voluntarily discontinued ART for over 1 year. A total of 175 patients were classified as having non-HIV-PJP based on their underlying diseases, and those with multiple underlying conditions were listed under each applicable disease category (Table 1).

Clinical manifestation

More patients with HIV-PJP exhibited respiratory symptoms than those with non-HIV-PJP. The OR was 2.58 for cough (p<0.01) and 3.87 for dyspnea (p<0.01). Tachypnea showed low statistical significance (OR, 2.10; p=0.07). Indicators of disease severity, evaluated by the type of oxygen supply device and admission to the intensive care unit, were not statistically significant.

No statistically significant differences in WBC, absolute neutrophil, and absolute lymphocyte counts were observed between the two groups. Platelet counts were lower in the non-HIV-PJP group than in the HIV-PJP group, although values remained within the normal range (271.18×103 cells/mm3 vs. 197.21×103 cells/mm3; p<0.01). Hs-CRP (7.79 mg/dL vs. 11.30 mg/dL; p<0.01) and BUN levels were higher in the non-HIV-PJP group than in the HIV-PJP group (17.03 mg/dL vs. 25.56 mg/dL; p<0.01).

Typical chest radiography findings were more frequently observed in the HIV-PJP group than in the non-HIV-PJP group (OR, 1.98; p=0.09). However, high-resolution CT findings showed that patients with HIV-PJP had statistically more typical findings than patients with non-HIV-PJP (OR, 3.57; p=0.02). The rate of PJP diagnosis using BALF was higher in the HIV-PJP group (OR, 5.34; p<0.01). Co-occurrence of CMV DNAemia (OR, 2.91; p=0.01) and CMV pneumonitis (OR, 4.32; p<0.01) at the time of PJP diagnosis was higher in the HIV-PJP group (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

This study focused on the clinical characteristics of patients with HIV-PJP and non-HIV-PJP. We explored the baseline characteristics, initial symptoms and signs, diagnostic test results, and treatment-related data between the two groups to investigate the causes of differences in mortality. Compared with previous studies, we compared HIV-PJP and non-HIV-PJP to identify differences in various clinical characteristics, including mortality, delays in diagnostic testing and empirical treatment, and other related factors.

On admission, the proportion of patients with HIV-PJP who reported respiratory symptoms was higher than that of patients with non-HIV-PJP. However, consistent with several international studies,10,21 we observed a higher mortality rate in the non-HIV-PJP group than in the HIV-PJP group, which contrasts with the higher proportion of respiratory symptoms observed in patients with HIV-PJP in our study. This finding may be attributed to a combination of several contributing factors, as discussed below.

The patients’ baseline characteristics differed between the groups. Patients with HIV-PJP were younger than those with non-HIV-PJP. Patients with HIV are first infected at a sexually active age; therefore, the possibility that patients with HIV who develop opportunistic infections due to untreated infections are elderly is relatively low. Although each comorbidity had different characteristics, many patients were older owing to the nature of cancer, accounting for the majority of non-HIV-PJP patients in this study. The CCI was used to evaluate mortality related to age and underlying diseases.22 We observed a significant difference in the CCI scores between the two groups, which requires cautious interpretation. In this study, contrary to the CCI scores, patients with non-HIV-PJP had more comorbidities than did those with HIV-PJP. In addition, the CCI score of 6 for AIDS was assigned before the development of ART and the rapid increase in the survival rate of patients with HIV; thus, AIDS may have been overscored in the CCI and needs to be reassessed.23 Therefore, the “true” score as the 10-year survival rate, which is the true meaning of the CCI, may not have made a valid difference in this study; in fact, the results may be the opposite.

Contrary to the respiratory symptoms at admission, the results of some laboratory tests (hs-CRP, BUN, and platelet count) associated with infection were outside the normal range in patients with non-HIV-PJP. Limper, et al.9 showed that patients with HIV-PJP have a higher proportion of P. jirovecii organisms but fewer neutrophils than patients with non-HIV-PJP, suggesting that a relatively weaker immune response may be the cause of differences in laboratory test results. However, in addition to this presumed cause, the underlying patient characteristics, such as comorbidities, may have influenced the results of the laboratory tests. Furthermore, whether the significant differences in the results of the laboratory tests had a clear association with mortality was unclear, and relevant literature in this regard is lacking. In other words, the usefulness of aforementioned results for differential diagnosis, outcomes, and patient management—beyond the clinical differences between the two groups—could not be confirmed in this study.

The difference in the diagnosis and treatment process between the two groups is notable. HIV-PJP showed a relatively subtle clinical course and typical imaging findings. As the HIV antigen/antibody immunoassay results were quickly confirmed during the initial evaluation, a presumptive diagnosis of PJP was made before receiving the PJ PCR results. In contrast, PJ PCR was often performed later in non-HIV-PJP patients than in HIV-PJP patients, as PJP was not initially suspected, owing to the diversity of underlying diseases and immunosuppressive treatments, the rapid clinical progression, and atypical imaging results. Furthermore, the interval between PJ PCR testing and the first antibiotic administration was longer in non-HIV-PJP patients than in HIV-PJP patients. The proportion of patients who received empirical antibiotics without PJ PCR confirmation was higher in the HIV-PJP group than in the non-HIV-PJP group, possibly because non-HIV-PJP is more difficult to diagnose than HIV-PJP, which may also explain the differences in the timing of testing and treatment.

The differences in the timing of examination, diagnosis, and treatment between the two groups might be considered a source of bias if this study aimed to compare the prognosis of PJP patients. However, these differences are experienced by patients suspected of having PJP in clinical practice owing to various factors, such as laboratory conditions and medical staff specialties. PJP can lead to fatal outcomes owing to delays in diagnosis and treatment. In this study, the non-HIV-PJP group had a significantly higher mortality rate. Therefore, the differences in timing can be interpreted as clinically important results.

The medications for treating PJP have been established, and the survival benefits of adjunctive glucocorticoids in severe cases have been studied for HIV-PJP and non-HIV-PJP patient populations.24,25 However, this study confirmed that the survival rate of patients who received appropriate antibiotics according to the recommended dose, regimen, and duration in the non-HIV-PJP group was lower, and that adjunctive glucocorticoid administration only slightly increased the survival rate in this group, without statistical significance. Given that, in the non-HIV-PJP group, the proportion of appropriate antibiotic administration was lower in relatively mild cases with low oxygen requirements, it can be assumed that patients with mild disease were administered lower doses for shorter durations, while patients with more severe disease tended to receive standard treatment. In a study comparing low and standard doses of TMP-SMX in non-HIV-PJP patients, no difference in the clinical outcomes was observed between the two groups.26 Therefore, the generally recommended dose and treatment period can be applied differently depending on the patient group and severity. However, in this study, the rate of switching to second-line drugs due to adverse reactions was lower than that reported in the aforementioned study, and treatment failure and related antibiotic replacement rates were not evaluated. Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting the relationship between TMP-SMX usage, dosing, and mortality. Meanwhile, adjunctive glucocorticoids are indicated only for severe disease, but the survival rate in our study did not reflect the differences in the severity of underlying diseases and comorbidities between patients who received the drug and those who did not.

CMV infection is a common and important acute problem in patients with PJP. Several studies have reported that CMV pneumonitis coinfection in patients with non-HIV-PJP is associated with increased mortality.13,27 In this study, the rate of CMV pneumonitis coinfection at the time of PJP diagnosis was significantly higher in the HIV-PJP group. However, the 28-day survival rate was higher in the HIV-PJP group, while the survival rate of patients with CMV pneumonitis coinfection in the non-HIV-PJP group was significantly lower. Therefore, active evaluation and treatment for CMV pneumonitis is necessary as a differential diagnosis. However, this result does not reflect the relatively greater diversity and severity of comorbidities, including underlying diseases, in non-HIV-PJP patients, making it difficult to specifically evaluate the contribution of CMV pneumonitis to mortality. Furthermore, the rate of BALF PJ and CMV PCR among patients with non-HIV-PJP in this study was low, which may have led to a delay in the diagnosis of PJP and CMV pneumonitis, eventually affecting the prognosis. Although it is difficult to determine the clinical significance of CMV DNAemia alone, this study included it for the following two purposes. First, in cases where the attending physician presumptively diagnosed and treated CMV pneumonitis without confirmation of BALF CMV PCR, whole blood CMV PCR results were used as part of the evaluation of adequacy. Second, given that combination steroid therapy is necessary for treating severe PJP in immunocompromised patients, CMV DNAemia was used for screening and monitoring of organ manifestations such as CMV pneumonitis, and as a prognostic indicator.

This study had several limitations. It was conducted at a single institution, and there was a large difference in the number of patients between the two groups. Since this study compared patients with a single disease (HIV) to those with various underlying conditions collectively referred to as non-HIV, we attempted to include non-HIV-PJP patients in the study. However, the number of HIV-PJP cases confirmed at our hospital was relatively small, contributing to the imbalance between the two groups. The risk of the underlying characteristics was confirmed using the CCI score, which is a relatively dated index that is overscored in the case of AIDS, as previously described. For other conditions, the classifications and scores used in this study did not clearly reflect disease stage or prognosis. The diagnosis of CMV pneumonia may have been influenced by the subjective clinical judgment of the treating physician, and this could not be fully controlled due to the retrospective nature of the study.

In this study, owing to its retrospective nature and reliance on diverse patient characteristics and medical records, there were limitations in performing various statistical analyses, including multivariable analysis. For odds ratios, unadjusted odds ratios were used only. It was difficult to control for various confounders or interactions that could be clinically presumed and classified in detail within the group, such as underlying diseases causing immunosuppression, the stage or life expectancy of each condition, and the medications being administered. In addition, the sample sizes for each subgroup were too small to apply these factors effectively. Some data were also missing due to the nature of the retrospective study. Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting the odds ratios presented in this study.

At our institution, an infectious disease specialist is involved in the diagnosis and treatment of HIV-PJP from the time of the patient’s first visit; however, this protocol is not consistently applied to patients with non-HIV-PJP. This point may be related to inappropriate diagnosis and treatment. As non-HIV patients are also immunocompromised, interdisciplinary collaboration is needed to differentiate various opportunistic infections and facilitate the rapid and appropriate diagnosis and treatment of PJP. PJP is difficult to suspect in patients without HIV-related immunosuppression due to complex underlying conditions, current clinical status, atypical imaging findings, and clinical course. In this study, data on collaboration with the infectious disease department and associated outcomes were not evaluated. However, the more atypical chest X-ray findings, delays in diagnostic testing, and later initiation of empirical treatment observed in non-HIV-PJP patients in this study are considered consequences of diagnostic difficulty. Based on these findings, early involvement of a specialist is presumed to offer meaningful clinical benefits in situations where PJP is difficult to suspect. A similar approach may also be applicable to other opportunistic infections, warranting further research.

This study was conducted using patient data collected over a long period of 15 years and 1 month. During that time, there was no significant difference in the diagnosis and treatment methods of PJP, and there was no change in the test date and reporting period of the PJ PCR of the research institution. However, it can be assumed that the underlying conditions of PJP patients changed over time due to advances in medical technology, such as the development of new diagnostic tools, treatments for underlying diseases, and resulting improvements in prognosis. These changes were not reflected in the study and represent a limitation related to the extended research period. Such changes might have been more accurately captured if the study had been conducted over a shorter period.

In conclusion, we found several statistically significant differences between patients with HIV-PJP and non-HIV-PJP in terms of symptoms, signs, and clinical characteristics. Patients with HIV-PJP had higher rates of respiratory symptoms at diagnosis, typical radiological findings, CMV pneumonitis co-infection, and adjunctive glucocorticoid therapy; they also had longer durations of illness, antibiotic administration, and CMV pneumonitis treatment in the presence of co-infection. However, in the non-HIV-PJP group, diagnosis and treatment after the initial presentation were relatively delayed; the 28-day mortality rate of patients with non-HIV-PJP was higher, and in cases of combined CMV pneumonitis, the mortality rate was very high, unlike that observed in the HIV-PJP group. These differences may be attributed not only to the characteristics of the underlying diseases but also to differences in the medical environment, development of related drugs, and the appropriateness of the diagnosis and treatment processes. Understanding the underlying diseases and considering the presence or absence of other opportunistic infections, such as CMV disease, and their differential diagnoses are necessary; therefore, collaboration among related departments is needed to improve the prognosis of patients with non-HIV-PJP.
