Lithocholic acid ameliorates ulcerative colitis via the PXR/TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling pathway and gut microbiota modulation
Authors: Yaoyao Liu, Jie Gao, Lu Chen, Yanfang Chen, Jingjing Jiang, Hong Chen, Li Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory condition of the colon, closely linked to dysbiosis of gut microbiota and imbalances in bile acids. Lithocholic acid (LCA), a secondary bile acid, plays a crucial role in maintaining gut health; however, its specific therapeutic potential in UC remains to be fully elucidated. This study investigates the efficacy of LCA in alleviating UC and explores the underlying mechanisms, particularly focusing on the PXR/TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling pathway and gut microbiota modulation. Using a dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis model, our findings demonstrate that LCA administration significantly alleviates colitis symptoms, evidenced by reduced disease activity index (DAI), increased colon length, improved intestinal barrier function, and decreased colonic inflammation. Mechanistically, LCA activates the pregnane X receptor (PXR), which inhibits TLR4-mediated NF-κB/NLRP3 inflammasome activation, leading to reduced colonic inflammation and lower levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Furthermore, LCA remodels gut microbiota by promoting beneficial bacterial growth, such as Akkermansiaceae, Lactobacillaceae and Muribaculaceae, while suppressing pathogenic and opportunistic pathogens, including Enterobacteriaceae and Bacteroidaceae. The gut microbiota-dependent effects of LCA were corroborated through antibiotic treatment and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) experiments. Notably, the absence of intestinal flora affected PXR expression and activity, modifying the aforementioned effects. Overall, our findings reveal that LCA ameliorates experimental colitis by regulating the PXR/TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling cascade and modulating gut microbiota composition. This study underscores LCA’s potential as a targeted therapeutic strategy and a promising microbiota-focused approach for managing UC, offering new insights into the role of bile acids in intestinal health and disease management. Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC), a prominent form of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), is characterized by extensive inflammation of the colorectal mucosa, typically presenting with abdominal pain, diarrhea, and bloody, mucus-laden stools [1]. Its incidence has risen markedly in recent years, adversely affecting patients’ quality of life and imposing considerable socioeconomic burdens on healthcare systems [2]. Current treatments primarily include 5-aminosalicylic acid, corticosteroids, thiopurines, biologics and small molecule drugs [3]. Despite these options, low remission rates and loss of therapeutic efficacy necessitate proctocolectomy in 10–20% of refractory cases [1, 4].

The etiology of UC is multifactorial, involving interactions between environmental factors, genetic predispositions, immune dysregulation, infections, and alterations in gut microbiota [5]. In UC patients, microbial imbalance disrupts host defenses, leading to inflammasome activation, excessive immune responses, and chronic inflammation [6, 7]. These changes compromise epithelial integrity, promote toxin production, and decrease beneficial metabolites, such as bile acids, short-chain fatty acids, and tryptophan derivatives, which are critical to IBD pathophysiology [8]. Interactions between gut microbiota, bile acids, and immune systems are implicated in various diseases, including IBD, obesity, diabetes, and cancers [9, 10].

Lithocholic acid (LCA), a key secondary bile acid, is produced by intestinal microbiota through the 7α-dehydroxylation of chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) [11, 12]. Studies show that IBD patients exhibit elevated levels of primary bile acids (e.g., cholic acid and CDCA) and reduced levels of secondary bile acids, such as deoxycholic acid and LCA [13, 14]. LCA is involved in modulating inflammation, immunity, and metabolism [15], and has been shown to alleviate early-stage dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis in mice via the vitamin D receptor [16].

The Pregnane X receptor (PXR), a nuclear receptor predominantly expressed in the gut and liver, is pivotal for maintaining intestinal immune homeostasis by regulating xenobiotic metabolism and inflammation [17]. Its flexible ligand-binding domain enables interactions with diverse ligands, including bile acids like LCA. While other bile acid receptors (e.g., FXR, TGR5, VDR) have been extensively studied in metabolic regulation and intestinal disorders [13, 18, 19], PXR’s role in bile acid-mediated cellular responses remains less understood, particularly concerning intestinal inflammation. Reduced PXR expression, or deletion of its gene (NR1I2), along with downregulation of target genes like ABCB1/MDR1 and CYP3A4, are associated with increased IBD risk [20–23]. Notably, PXR activation has been reported to mitigate DSS-induced colitis [24]. Thus, we hypothesize that LCA exerts therapeutic effects in colitis, at least partly through PXR activation.

Evidence suggests that PXR negatively regulates inflammatory pathways, including Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), and the NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, thereby modulating innate immunity and inflammation [25, 26]. NLRP3, a key inflammasome, activates caspase-1, facilitating the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18 [27–30]. This process is tightly linked to NF-κB signaling, which can be acitved by TLR4 and regulates the expression of numerous genes involved in inflammation and immune responses [31, 32]. Consequently, PXR may serve as a therapeutic target by inhibiting the TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 axis, thereby alleviating intestinal inflammation.

This study aims to investigate the role of LCA as a PXR ligand in mitigating colitis and to elucidate its specific mechanisms. Furthermore, we will explore the impact of LCA on gut microbiota in mediating these effects, given the strong association between microbial dysbiosis, UC, and bile acids.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Dextran sulfate sodium (DSS, MW: 36–50 kDa) was procured from MP Biochemicals (Santa Ana, CA, USA). Lithocholic acid (LCA, MW: 376.57 g/mol) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS; L2880) were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The antibiotics vancomycin, neomycin sulfate, metronidazole, and ampicillin were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Animals

Six- to eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were obtained from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China) and housed under controlled environmental conditions (temperature: 25 °C, relative humidity: 50%, light/dark cycle: 12 h). After a one-week acclimation, the mice were randomly assigned to four groups(n = 12): CTRL (control), LCA, DSS, and DSS + LCA. Acute colitis was induced by administering 3% DSS in water from days 1–7. LCA (3 mg/10 g, based on previous studies) dissolved in corn oil was given via oral gavage to the LCA and DSS + LCA groups on days 8–14 [16, 33–35], while the CTRL and DSS groups received corn oil alone. Body weight, disease activity index (DAI; incorporating weight loss, stool consistency, and rectal bleeding), as well as other clinical signs such as fur condition, food intake, and spontaneous activity were monitored daily. At the end of the experiment, mice were euthanized using pentobarbital sodium overdose. Colon length was measured, and tissue samples were frozen at − 80 °C for subsequent analysis. All experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Protection and Utilization Committee of Southeast University.

Antibiotic treatment and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)

To establish the pseudo-germ-free (PGF) mouse model, mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free environment and provided with irradiated, sterilized diets and autoclaved water and received a 3-week course of antibiotic cocktail (ABX) in their drinking water: 0.5 mg/mL vancomycin, 1 mg/mL neomycin sulfate, 1 mg/mL metronidazole, and 1 mg/mL ampicillin. Mice were then assigned to four groups (n = 8): CTRL, LCA, DSS, and DSS + LCA, with treatments as described above.

FMT procedures followed established protocols [36, 37]. Donor mice were allocated to CTRL, DSS, and DSS + LCA groups. Donors in the DSS and DSS + LCA groups received 3% DSS solution for 7 days, while the CTRL group received water. After this, the DSS + LCA group was treated with LCA for an additional 7 days, while the CTRL and DSS groups received vehicle. Three days after the final treatment, stool samples were collected from donor mice, combined, and processed under sterile conditions. Approximately 100 mg of stool was resuspended in 1 mL saline, mixed for 10 s, and centrifuged at 800 g for 3 min. The resulting supernatant was orally administered to recipient mice within 10 min to preserve bacterial viability. Recipient PGF mice underwent 21 days of ABX treatment, DSS induction for 7 days, and FMT for 1 week.

16 S rDNA gene high-throughput sequencing

DNA was extracted from stool samples using the hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method. Extracted DNA was eluted in 50 µL of elution buffer and stored at − 80 °C for further processing. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification products were purified with AMPure XT beads (Beckman Coulter Genomics, Danvers, MA, USA) and quantified using a Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen, Woburn, MA, USA). Amplicons were prepared for sequencing and analyzed on a NovaSeq PE250 platform (Illumina) at LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). Raw sequencing data were demultiplexed, trimmed, and merged using FLASH, followed by quality filtering with fqtrim (v0.94). Chimeras were removed via Vsearch (v2.3.4), and DADA2 generated amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Taxonomic classification was performed using the SILVA 138 database (90% confidence) via BLAST. α-diversity and β-diversity analyses were conducted in QIIME2 with rarefaction. Data visualization (including principal coordinates analysis (PCoA), taxonomic composition plots, and heatmaps) was performed using the OmicStudio tools at https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool.

Intestinal permeability assay

Intestinal barrier function was assessed by administering fluorescein isothiocyanate-conjugated dextran (FITC-dextran, 4 kDa; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) via gastric gavage at 60 mg/100 g body weight 4 h before sacrifice. FITC-dextran levels in plasma were measured fluorometrically at excitation/emission wavelengths of 490/530 nm.

Histopathology and Alcian blue staining

Distal colon tissues were fixed in 10% neutral formalin overnight, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned (4 μm thickness). Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or Alcian blue. Two independent pathologists, blinded to the study groups, assessed tissue damage using a standardized scoring system [38].

Biochemical analyses

Levels of liver enzymes (ALT and AST) in plasma and cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α) in colon tissue or cell culture supernatants were quantified using ELISA kits (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell culture and drug treatment

Caco-2 cells were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and cultured in DMEM containing 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 1% non-essential amino acids (NEAA) under 5% CO₂ at 37 °C. Cells were seeded at 1 × 10⁵ cells/well in 24-well plates. After 24 h, cells were stimulated with 10 µg/mL LPS for 24 h, followed by treatment with 25 µM LCA for an additional 24 h.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was determined using the CCK-8 assay (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Cells were seeded at 1 × 10⁴ cells/well in 96-well plates, treated with varying concentrations of LCA (0, 12.5, 25, 50, 100, or 200 µM) for 24 h, and incubated with CCK-8 reagent for 3 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Trans-Epithelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurements

To assess barrier integrity, Caco-2 cells (1 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were seeded onto Transwell inserts (0.4 μm pore size, 0.33 cm² area; Corning, NY, USA), with 100 µL added to the apical (AP) side and 600 µL culture medium to the basolateral (BL) side. Media were replaced every other day for the first 7 days and daily thereafter. Transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) was measured on days 3, 7, 14, and 21 using a Millicell-ERS-2 V-Ohm meter (Millipore, MA, USA), and values (Ω·cm²) were calculated by multiplying resistance by membrane area. By day 21, the Caco-2 cells had reached full differentiation and polarization, displaying both the structural and functional characteristics of mature enterocytes, including tight junction formation and robust barrier function [39].

Paracellular marker FITC-Dextran flux

Paracellular permeability was assessed by adding 100 µL of 1 mg/mL FITC-dextran (4 kDa) to the AP chamber of Transwell inserts, while 600 µL of D-Hanks buffer was added to the BL chamber. After 2 h of incubation, fluorescence in the BL medium was measured (λex = 490 nm, λem = 530 nm), and the permeability coefficient (Papp) was calculated based on the flux [39].

RNA interference

PXR knockdown in Caco-2 cells was achieved using siRNA transfection (Genepharma, Suzhou, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Western blotting

Proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Equalized samples were separated via SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membrane was then blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h, followed by incubation overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody: anti-PXR (1:5000, 67912-1-Ig), anti-E-cadherin (1:1000, 20874-1-AP), anti-occludin (1:2000, 13409-1-AP), anti-claudin 2 (1:500, 26912-1-AP), anti-β-actin (1:20000, 66009-1-Ig) from Proteintech Group, Inc(Wuhan, China), and anti-NLRP3 (1:1000, WL02635), anti-p-IκBα (1:1000, WL02495), anti-IκBα (1:1000, WL01936) from Wanlei Biotechnology Co. Ltd (Shenyang, China). Following incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h, protein signals were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence detection.

Real-Time quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated using RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara, Dalian, China) and reverse-transcribed to cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). qPCR was performed using SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Vazyme), and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.3.1 (San Diego, CA, USA) and presented as mean ± SEM. Two-group comparisons were conducted using Student’s t-test, while multiple group comparisons employed one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

LCA ameliorates DSS-Induced colitis in mice

This study utilized a DSS-induced colitis mouse model to evaluate the therapeutic potential of LCA in UC. Mice were treated with 3.0% DSS in drinking water for 7 days, followed by oral gavage of LCA for an additional 7 days (Fig. 1 a). DSS treatment significantly reduced body weight and induced a marked increase in DAI, reflecting colonic and systemic inflammation, while LCA administration mitigated these effects, preventing DSS-induced body weight loss and lowering DAI scores (Fig. 1. b, c). In addition, compared with the CTRL group, the colon length of mice in the DSS group was markedly shortened, while the colon length of mice in the DSS + LCA group was significantly increased, indicating that LCA could effectively alleviate the colon shortening of mice with colitis (Fig. 1. d).

LCA improved gut barrier integrity and reduced colonic inflammation in DSS-treated mice. We assessed intestinal permeability using FITC-dextran; the DSS + LCA group showed lower serum levels of FITC-dextran compared to the DSS group, indicating enhanced epithelial barrier function (Fig. 1 e). Histological analysis revealed that LCA ameliorated DSS-induced epithelial injury, crypt damage, and inflammatory cell infiltration, as demonstrated by H&E staining (Fig. 1 f). Similarly, Alcian blue staining indicated that LCA partially preserved acidic mucins and goblet cells in the colonic epithelium (Fig. 1 g). At the molecular level, DSS treatment disrupted the expression of tight junction proteins, increasing claudin-2 expression while reducing occludin and E-cadherin levels. These changes were reversed by LCA (Fig. 1 h). Furthermore, LCA significantly suppressed DSS-induced increases in pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α (Fig. 1 i). Importantly, LCA showed no hepatotoxic effects, as indicated by unchanged serum AST and ALT levels (Fig. 1J).

These findings demonstrate that LCA alleviates the colitis-related symptoms, gut barrier damage and colonic inflammation in DSS-treated mice.

LCA protects against LPS-Induced barrier dysfunction and inflammation in Caco-2 cells

The effects of LCA on epithelial barrier integrity were further examined in a Caco-2 monolayer model. Cell viability assays confirmed that LCA concentrations of up to 100 µM had no cytotoxic effects after 24 h of treatment (Fig. 2 a). Monolayer integrity was assessed by measuring TEER and Papp (Fig. 2 b, c). After 21 days of culture, the cells exhibited TEER values of approximately 477.447 Ω·cm² and Papp < 0.5 × 10⁻⁶ cm/s, confirming the integrity of the monolayer model [40]. Exposure to 10 µg/mL LPS for 24 h caused a progressive decline in TEER values, which was mitigated by 24-hour treatment with 25 µM LCA (Fig. 2 d). Similarly, LPS significantly increased Papp, while LCA effectively prevented this permeability change (Fig. 2 e), suggesting that LCA improves monolayer barrier function.

Consistent with in vivo observations, LCA reversed LPS-induced alterations in tight junction protein expression, decreasing claudin-2 levels and increasing occludin and E-cadherin expression (Fig. 2 f, g). Additionally, LCA treatment significantly suppressed LPS-induced secretion of inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in the supernatant of Caco-2 cells (Fig. 2 h). These results highlight LCA’s ability to preserve epithelial barrier function and attenuate inflammation in vitro.

LCA attenuates colitis via PXR activation and TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 suppression

PXR, a critical regulator of bile acid metabolism and immunity, was explored as a potential target of LCA [41]. DSS treatment significantly downregulated PXR expression at both mRNA and protein levels in colonic tissues, while LCA administration restored PXR expression (Fig. 3 a, b). Furthermore, LCA significantly upregulated the expression of PXR target genes Abcb1a and Cyp3a11 in colonic tissues of DSS-induced mice (Fig. 3 a), indicating a potential role of PXR signaling in the alleviation of colitis.

Given the crucial roles of TLR4, NF-κB, and NLRP3 in inflammatory responses [42], particularly NF-κB’s control of pro-inflammatory genes, the impact of LCA on these pathways was examined. DSS treatment markedly elevated TLR4 expression, phosphorylated IκBα (p- IκBα, indicative of NF-κB activation), and NLRP3 inflammasome levels in colon tissue, all of which were reduced by LCA (Fig. 3 b, c). In LPS-treated Caco-2 cells, LCA attenuated TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling and simultaneously enhanced PXR expression, recapitulating the in vivo observations in DSS-induced colitis mice (Fig. 3 d-f). These findings underscore LCA’s role in mitigating colitis by modulating the TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 pathway via PXR activation. These findings suggest that LCA-mediated colitis amelioration correlates with both PXR activation and TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 suppression across in vivo and in vitro models.

PXR Silencing abolishes lca’s protective effects

To confirm the role of PXR in mediating LCA’s effects, PXR expression was silenced in Caco-2 cells using siRNA. As anticipated, siRNA knockdown markedly decreased PXR mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 4 a). Post-knockdown, Caco2 cells were exposed to LPS and subsequently treated with LCA as previous described. In cells with siRNA-mediated PXR suppression, LCA failed to counteract the LPS-induced reduction in tight junction proteins E-cadherin and occludin, and the increase in claudin-2 (Fig. 4 b, c), indicating a loss of LCA’s protective impact on the intestinal barrier. Furthermore, LCA treatment did not mitigate the LPS-induced elevation of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α (Fig. 4 d), suggesting an absence of its anti-inflammatory effects.

Given our previous findings that LCA may alleviate colitis by modulating the TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 pathway via PXR, we assessed these signaling components. In Caco2 cells with silenced PXR, LCA did not affect the activation of TLR4, NF-κB, and NLRP3 (Fig. 4 e). These findings confirm that PXR activation is essential for LCA’s protective effects on epithelial barrier integrity and inflammation.

LCA modulates gut microbiota composition

16 S rDNA sequencing revealed that LCA significantly altered the gut microbiota composition in DSS-treated mice. Venn diagrams were employed to analyze the distribution of Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs), revealing both unique and shared ASVs across groups (Fig. 5 a). Alpha diversity, assessed via Chao1 and observed species indexes, showed a significant decrease in the DSS group compared to the CTRL group, whereas the DSS + LCA group exhibited higher alpha diversity than the DSS group (Fig. 5 b). Beta diversity was evaluated using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) with Jaccard and unweighted UniFrac distance matrices, which visually illustrated clustering patterns across groups (Fig. 5 c). Adonis analysis confirmed significant overall inter-group differences (Jaccard: R² = 0.220, p = 0.003; unweighted UniFrac: R² = 0.236, p = 0.01), with notable divergence in the CTRL vs. DSS comparisons (Jaccard: R² = 0.204, p = 0.003; unweighted UniFrac: R² = 0.230, p = 0.003). Conversely, comparisons between CTRL and DSS + LCA, as well as DSS and DSS + LCA, did not reach statistical significance. While these pairwise contrasts involving DSS + LCA lacked significance, the marked difference between CTRL and DSS, combined with the intermediate effect sizes in CTRL vs. DSS + LCA, suggests that LCA may partially mitigate DSS-induced dysbiosis. These trends, though limited by sample size, are consistent with LCA’s proposed role in modulating microbial composition.

We then analyzed the gut microbiota across all samples to identify compositional differences between the groups. At the phylum level, the predominant phyla identified in all groups were Verrucomicrobiota, Firmicutes, Proteobacteria and Bacteroidota (Fig. 5 d). Family-level analyse showed notable differences in taxonomic composition: Akkermansiaceae, Lactobacillaceae and Muribaculaceae were abundant in CTRL and LCA groups, while Enterobacteriaceae and Bacteroidaceae were more prevalent in the DSS group (Fig. 5 e). Genera such as Akkermansia and Lactobacillus, known for their protective roles in maintaining the intestinal barrier, were enriched in the CTRL and LCA groups. Conversely, inflammatory-associated genera, including Escherichia-Shigella and Bacteroides, were prominent in the DSS group but exhibited reduced abundance following LCA treatment (Fig. 5 f).

To identify bacteria alterations induced by LCA treatment, further analysis using linear discriminant analysis (LDA) of effect size (LEfSe) revealed distinct bacterial community differences among the four groups (Fig. 6 a, b). Notably, genera such as Akkermansia and Lactobacillus exhibited higher abundance in the CTRL group, whereas Escherichia-Shigella and Bacteroides were more prevalent in the DSS-induced colitis group, suggesting their potential role in the pathogenesis of colitis, a finding corroborated by the heat map results. While Akkermansia was elevated in the DSS + LCA group, as shown in the stacked bar plots, it did not achieve a statistical significance in the LEfSe analysis. Conversely, the genera Candidatus Soleaferrea, Romboutsia, Ruminococcaceae, and Erysipelatoclostridium, which belong to the phylum Firmicutes, were significantly more abundant in the DSS + LCA group, suggesting their potential contribution to the mitigation of colitis. These results suggest that LCA treatment may attenuate dysbiosis associated with DSS-induced colitis.

LCA relieve colitis in a gut microbiota-dependent manner

The role of gut microbiota in LCA’s efficacy was investigated using PGF mice (Fig. 7 a). In the absence of gut microbiota, LCA’s beneficial impacts were nullified, showing no significant differences between the DSS and DSS + LCA groups in body weight (Fig. 7 b), DAI scores (Fig. 7 c), and colon length (Fig. 7 d). Intestinal permeability (Fig. 7 e), tissue damage (Fig. 7 f, g), and epithelial tight junction protein expression (Fig. 7 h) were also unaffected by LCA treatment in PGF mice. Furthermore, LCA did not significantly alter the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in colonic tissues of PGF mice (Fig. 7 i). These findings indicate that the presence of gut microbiota is essential for LCA’s protective effects against colitis.

Fecal microbiota transplantation restores lca’s effects

To further confirm the microbiota-dependent effects of LCA, we conducted FMT experiments. Fecal microbiota from CTRL, DSS, and DSS + LCA groups were transplanted into DSS-induced PGF mice (Fig. 8 a). Compared to the FMT-CTRL group, FMT-DSS mice exhibited more severe weight loss (Fig. 8 b), higher DAI scores (Fig. 8 c), shorter colons (Fig. 8 d), increased intestinal permeability (Fig. 8 e), greater tissue damage (Fig. 8 f), fewer goblet cells (Fig. 8 g), decreased expression of tight junction proteins (Fig. 8 h), and elevated inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 8 i). These adverse effects were ameliorated in the FMT-DSS + LCA group. Collectively, these results strongly suggest that gut microbiota is crucial for the therapeutic effects of LCA in colitis.

Gut microbiota influences PXR expression and TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling

To investigate the interplay between intestinal microbiota and PXR, as well as the TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling pathway, we assessed the expression of relevant proteins in CTRL, LCA, DSS, and DSS + LCA groups in PGF mice. Results indicated that LCA could not effectively stimulate PXR expression or activate the TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 pathway when intestinal bacteria were depleted (Fig. 9 a). Conversely, in FMT mice, the DSS + LCA group showed significant activation of the PXR receptor, which in turn activated the TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling pathway (Fig. 9 b).

To elucidate these findings, we analyzed PXR expression in both PGF and untreated mice. Antibiotic treatment led to a marked reduction in PXR expression as well as its target genes, Abcb1a and Cyp3all (Fig. 9 c, d). This suggests that antibiotic depletion of gut microbiota impairs PXR expression and function, preventing LCA from activating PXR in PGF mice. In contrast, PXR expression and function partially recover in the gut of FMT-treated mice, thereby facilitating intestinal protection.

Discussion

UC remains a global health challenge, necessitating the development of novel and effective therapies. LCA, a critical secondary bile acid, exclusively produced through microbial biotransformation in the colon, acts as a signaling molecule for various nuclear and membrane receptors [43, 44]. As integral components of the circulating bile acid pool, LCA and its derivatives influence numerous biological processes, including host metabolism, immune regulation, and the inhibition of inhibiting Clostridium difficile growth [39, 45, 46]. LCA’s anti-colitis effects may involve multiple pathways, including TGR5-mediated epithelial regeneration and VDR-dependent barrier enhancement [47, 48]. However, the role of PXR as a bile acid receptor and its interaction with the intestinal microbiota remain insufficiently characterized. Consequently, this study investigates the relatively underexplored LCA-PXR pathway and its relationship with gut microbiota. Our findings demonstrate that LCA alleviates DSS-induced colitis primarily by regulating the PXR/TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling pathway and modulating gut microbiota composition.

The DSS-induced colitis model is widely utilized for studying UC in humans, as it exhibits hallmark features of UC such as weight loss, diarrhea, and bloody stool [49]. In this study, LCA treatment alleviated DSS-induced weight loss and reduced disease activity index (DAI) scores, consistent with improved clinical outcomes. It also improved colon shortening, mucosal destruction, and inflammatory cell infiltration, aligning with previous findings that LCA can diminish early disease activity and protect colonic mucosa [16].

UC pathophysiology is closely linked to intestinal barrier dysfunction, particularly alterations in tight junction proteins. Active UC is characterized by elevated claudin-2 and reduced occludin expression, which compromise barrier function and increase intestinal permeability [50]. Consistent with these findings, DSS treatment in this study decreased occludin and E-cadherin expression while increasing claudin-2 levels. LCA treatment efficiently reversed these changes restoring epithelial barrier integrity and reducing intestinal permeability. Furthermore, LCA significantly decreased the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, which are known to exacerbate inflammation and tissue damage in colitis [51]. Supporting in vivo results, in vitro experiments demonstrated that LCA reinforced epithelial barrier function and attenuated LPS-induced inflammatory responses in Caco-2 cells. Overall, our research indicates that LCA effectively mitigates colitis, strengthens intestinal barrier function, and lowers inflammatory cytokine levels, within a safe dosage range.

PXR is traditionally recognized for its role in xenobiotic detoxification but also participates in lipid and glucose metabolism, inflammation, and innate immunity regulation [52, 53]. PXR activation ameliorates DSS-induced colitis, with agonists like pregnenolone 16α-carbonitrile and rifaximin showing efficacy [54, 55]. Consistent with this, the expression and activity of PXR were significantly impaired in DSS-induced colitis mice, while LCA treatment significantly enhanced PXR expression. In vitro, LCA countered LPS-induced PXR downregulation, but its protective effects were lost when PXR was silenced with siRNA. These results underscore the critical role of PXR activation in LCA-mediated colitis protection.

PXR exerts immunomodulatory effects through interactions with TLR4, NF-κB, and NLRP3 inflammasome [41]. TLR4 initiates NF-κB signaling by phosphorylating and degrading IκB-α, thereby enabling NF-κB nuclear translocation and activation of pro-inflammatory genes [26, 56, 57]. NF-κB activation further regulates NLRP3 inflammasome activation, which has been implicated in the pathogenesis of IBD [58–60]. Elevated NLRP3 activity is observed in IBD, and its inhibition has been shown to ameliorate colitis [61]. In human umbilical vein endothelial cells, PXR activation by rifampicin or SR12813 reduces NLRP3, inhibiting IL-1β and IL-18 maturation, while PXR inhibition increases NLRP3 expression [62]. In DSS-induced colitis mice and LPS-stimulated Caco-2 cells, we observed decreased PXR and increased levels of TLR4, IκB-α phosphorylation and NLRP3, correlating with elevated TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β levels. PXR knockdown negated the protective effect of LCA and failed to inhibit the above indicators, suggesting that LCA alleviates colitis by modulating the PXR/TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 axis.

Intestinal dysbiosis, marked by a reduction in beneficial bacteria and an increase in pathogenic species, is a crucial factor in UC pathology [63]. This study employed 16 S rDNA sequencing to elucidate LCA’s effect on intestinal flora structure. The analysis revealed a partial recovery of microbial alpha diversity in the DSS + LCA group compared to the DSS group, while beta diversity analysis indicated potential differences in community structure between these groups. These trends, alongside compositional shifts observed in other metrics, may reflect subtle LCA-mediated biome restructuring, though larger studies are needed to confirm these effects. LCA showed a trend toward enriching beneficial bacterial families, including Akkermansiaceae, Lactobacillaceae and Muribaculaceae, while significantly reducing the abundance of pathogenic and opportunistic pathogens such as Enterobacteriaceae and Bacteroidaceae. The deficiency of Akkermansiaceae has been closely associated with IBD, as it enhances intestinal barrier function by modulating host immunity and increasing goblet cell mucin secretion [64]. Lactobacillaceae and Muribaculaceae demonstrate anti-inflammatory properties by elevating mucoprotein expression, producing short-chain fatty acids, and competing with pathogens for ecological niches within the mucus layer, thereby resisting pathogen colonization [65, 66]. Conversely, an increase in Enterobacteriaceae has been linked to the onset of IBD [67]. Though Bacteroidaceae contributes beneficial functions under physiological conditions, its abundance may rise in certain inflammatory state [68, 69].In our study, the reduction of Bacteroidaceae may be attributed to its ecological competition with the enriched Akkermansiaceae and Muribaculaceae, potentially inhibiting its proliferation. However, we emphasize that these observations are preliminary and require validation through larger-scale studies or complementary functional analyses to establish causal relationships.

Dysbiosis not only contributes to UC pathology but also presents a therapeutic opportunity, as evidenced by the efficacy of probiotics, prebiotics, and FMT in UC management [70, 71]. To evaluate the role of gut microbiota in LCA’s therapeutic effects, PGF mice were utilized. Antibiotic-mediated depletion of gut microbiota abolished LCA’s beneficial effects on colitis symptoms, including weight loss, DAI scores, and intestinal permeability, indicating that the presence of gut microbiota is essential for LCA efficacy. FMT experiments further validated this observation, as mice receiving feces from DSS + LCA-treated donors showed significant improvements in colitis symptoms compared to those receiving feces from DSS-treated donors. These results confirm that gut microbiota is crucial for LCA’s therapeutic effects in colitis. While antibiotic treatment does not achieve complete sterility, the degree of microbiota depletion is sufficient to disrupt bacterial metabolite-dependent host pathways. Future studies utilizing germ-free models will help further validate these findings.

Further investigation revealed that gut microbiota modulates PXR expression and activity, which in turn influences the TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 pathway. Antibiotic treatment and colitis severely impaired PXR expression and activity in the DSS group, preventing an effective response to LCA stimulation. Consequently, PXR expression did not significantly increase in the DSS + LCA group compared to the DSS group, nor was there effective inhibition of the TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 axis. In contrast, following FMT, PXR expression in the DSS + LCA group exceeded that of the DSS group, leading to further inhibition of the pathway. Some studies have shown that in germ-free mice, liver PXR and its target genes are significantly lower than those in SPF mice [72]. Claus et al. found that gut colonization in germ-free mice significantly increases liver PXR and its target gene Cyp3a11 expression [73]. Depletion of indole-producing microflora induces an Nr1i2 −/− like state, reducing PXR expression and its target genes while increasing intestinal inflammation [26]. Our results are consistent with the findings mentioned above. Comparing PXR and its target genes (Abcb1a and Cyp3a11) between untreated CTRL and ABX groups confirmed that PXR expression and activity significantly declined following antibiotic treatment. These findings imply that intestinal flora depletion after antibiotic treatment greatly affects PXR expression and activity, failing to inhibit the TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 axis to alleviate colitis. After FMT, the expression and activity of PXR were partially restored, which further played a role in alleviating colitis. While in vitro tests, untreated Caco-2 cells initially showed robust PXR expression, allowing LCA to exert its anti-inflammatory effects. These results highlighting the critical interplay between gut microbiota and PXR in modulating inflammation and intestinal barrier function.

Interestingly, while earlier studies focused primarily on how gut microbiota influence LCA production, our findings suggest a bidirectional relationship, where LCA not only depends on gut microbiota for its production but also actively modulates microbial composition. LCA modulates gut microbiota by enriching beneficial bacteria and suppressing harmful taxa, while activating the PXR/TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling pathway to mitigate inflammation. Furthermore, the gut microbiota modulates PXR expression and activity, which in turn influences the therapeutic efficacy of LCA. However, the specific microbial taxa responsible for modulating PXR activity require further investigation.

In conclusion, this study identifies a novel mechanism by which LCA mitigates UC by modulating the PXR/TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling axis and gut microbiota composition. These findings pave the way for the development of LCA-based therapeutics for UC, offering valuable insights into the interplay between host-microbiota interactions and bile acid.
