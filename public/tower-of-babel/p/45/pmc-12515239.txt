Nasal microbiota dysbiosis and functional alterations in children with adenoid hypertrophy: potential biomarkers and post-surgical microbial recovery
Authors: He Jiang, Ping Ye, Shuai Chen, Juan Zhao, Long Chen, Qian Sun, Wenming Jia, Heng Liu, Xin Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractNasal cavity and nasopharynx are habitats for both pathogenic and non-pathogenic bacteria. Adenoid hypertrophy (AH) is the main cause in children, with a high incidence, for the airflow reduction in the upper airways. However, limited research described the differences of nasal microbial compositions in AH patients and healthy children. To determine the association between nasal microbiota and AH, total DNAs were collected and extracted from the nasal cavity and the hypertrophic adenoid of the donors, and the 16S rRNA gene V3–V4 region was sequenced. Microbial diversity was compared between the healthy and AH groups, and the enriched functional pathways were predicted. Although the alpha-diversity of microbial communities did not show statistically significant differences between the groups, principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) revealed that the microbiota component in the nasal cavity as well as in the hypertrophic adenoid tissues of children with AH was significantly different from that of healthy children. Genera Alloiococcus, Moraxella, Streptococcus, and Bacteroides were specifically enriched in the AH group’s samples, among which Moraxella spp. was significantly enriched in both nasal cavity and adenoid tissues, indicating a potential association of it with AH. In addition, Tax4fun prediction revealed disordered microbial functions in AH children. Nutrient metabolism, signaling system, chemotactic reaction, genetic replication, and reproduction-related pathways significantly decreased in the AH group but increased after adenoidectomy treatment. This study revealed a high association between specific microorganisms and AH disease. Future work is needed to investigate the role of Moraxella spp. in AH development.Key points • Microbiota in the nasal cavity has high specificity in distinguishing patients with adenoidal hypertrophy from healthy individuals, indicating that the homeostasis of microbiota in the nasal cavity is highly associated with the healthy status of adenoid. • Several bacterial taxa, including Alloiococcus, Moraxella, Streptococcus, and Bacteroides, are bio-markers for adenoidal hypertrophy, which enriches specifically in both nasal cavities and hypertrophied adenoid tissues of patients. • In the nasal cavity of patients with adenoidal hypertrophy, microbial functions related to pathogenicity were enriched, indicating an association between adenoidal hypertrophy and disorder of microbiota in the nasal cavity. Introduction

Adenoidal hypertrophy (AH) is a high frequently occurred disease in children aged 6 to 10, primarily caused by recurrent upper respiratory tract infections or chronic inflammation (Aljurayyan et al. 2018; Kimbell et al. 2019). The enlargement of adenoids can lead to nasal obstruction, impaired drainage, and the accumulation of inflammatory secretions, significantly affecting children’s quality of life by causing growth disorders, behavioral issues, and learning deficits (Garetz et al. 2015; Katz et al. 2014; Kimbell et al. 2019; Marcus et al. 2012). Fortunately, adenoidectomy surgery is the primary treatment to alleviate these symptoms (Boss et al. 2017; Diercks et al. 2018; Fehrm et al. 2020, 2018; Inoshita et al. 2018). The nasal cavity and adenoids, as key components of the upper respiratory tract, host diverse microbial communities that play a critical role in immune defense and disease development (Proctor and Relman 2017). The unique structure of adenoids, with their folds and crypts, provides an ideal habitat for microorganisms, which interact with the mucosal immune system and influence adenoid health (Ren et al. 2013). However, the specific microbial dynamics associated with AH and the impact of adenoidectomy on these communities remain poorly understood.

Recent studies have highlighted the role of microbial communities in upper respiratory tract diseases, including AH, adenotonsillitis, and otitis media (Ren et al. 2013). The nasal and adenoid microbiota, dominated by Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, Fusobacteria, and Bacteroidetes, are essential for maintaining respiratory health and modulating immune responses (Bomar et al. 2018; Fazlollahi et al. 2018; Johnston and Douglas 2018; Liu et al. 2021; Ren et al. 2013). Pathogens such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis, Pseudomonas aeruginosa Fusobacterium sp., Neisseria sp., Porphyromonas sp., and Actinobacillus sp. are frequently enriched in children with AH or tonsillar hypertrophy, suggesting a potential link between microbial dysbiosis and disease severity (Ari et al. 2019; Fago-Olsen et al. 2019; Johnston et al. 2019; Prates et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2023). Despite these findings, there is a lack of systematic research on how microbial communities in the nasal cavity and adenoids change during AH development and how surgical intervention alters these dynamics.

In this study, we collected samples from the nasal cavity of children with AH before and after adenoidectomy surgery as well as healthy children and samples from hypertrophic adenoids for bacterial diversity detection and major functional pathway prediction. By comparing microbial profiles across these groups, we aimed to elucidate the relationship between nasal microbiota dynamics and AH progression, as well as the impact of adenoidectomy on microbial community structure. Our findings provide new insights into the role of nasal microbiota in AH and may offer novel strategies for disease prevention and treatment through microbial modulation.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study employed a prospective case–control design and was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Qilu Hospital of Shandong University (approval no. KYLL-2019–2-058). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants’ legal guardians prior to sample collection. All procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Children diagnosed with AH and scheduled for adenoidectomy at the Department of Otorhinolaryngology, Qilu Hospital of Shandong University, were recruited. The diagnosis of AH was confirmed based on endoscopic findings (Cassano et al. 2003), and the hypertrophic adenoid tissues (n = 40) were collected during endoscopic-assisted transoral adenoidectomy under general anesthesia. Exclusion criteria encompassed chronic conditions (such as asthma, primary ciliary dyskinesia, immunodeficiency, fungal sinusitis, benign/or malignant tumors, cystic fibrosis), acute illnesses, and ongoing or recent (within three months) use of drugs like steroids or antibiotics. The inclusion criteria of healthy controls were as follows: (1) normal routine physical examination and development; (2) the absence of respiratory diseases in the month preceding sample collection; (3) no history of adenoid hypertrophy; and (4) no use of antibiotics or microecological agents in the preceding three months. Nasal swabs were collected from 36 healthy children and 40 children with AH before surgery and from the latter group within an interval of 14 ± 3 days post-surgery (Fig. 1).

Research workflow: From experimental design to data analysis

Nasal sample collection and DNA preparation

Nasal swab samples were meticulously collected from both AH patients and healthy individuals. Sterile polyester-tipped swabs were used for sample collection. Operators wore sterile gloves and face masks during the procedure to minimize exogenous microbial contamination. The swab was gently extended into the middle portion of the nasal cavity and then rotated five times from the nasal septum to the inferior turbinate of the nasal mucosa, applying constant pressure to ensure effective sampling (Goggin et al. 2018). Subsequently, the swab was promptly placed in a sterile tube. After adenoidectomy, the adenoids were placed in a sterile container and promptly taken to the microbiology laboratory (Brook et al. 2000). To maintain sample integrity, all samples were immediately stored at − 80℃ until further processing. For the extraction of total genomic DNA, the Qiagen DNeasy Kit (Qiagen, Dusseldorf, Germany) was employed following the manufacturer’s protocol. The extracted DNA was assessed for concentration and purity using the Nano Drop™ One (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA USA). According to the concentration, the DNA was appropriately diluted to 1 ng/mL using sterile water for subsequent analysis.

Sequence synthesis and data analysis

Sequencing of 16S rRNA gene amplicons targeting the V3 and V4 regions was performed on samples corresponding to treatment groups: healthy (NOR, n = 36), patients with adenoid hypertrophy (AH, n = 40), patients after adenoidectomy surgery (AT, n = 31), and tissue samples collected from hypertrophic adenoid during adenoidectomy surgery (Tis, n = 40). The amplification of the variable 3 and 4 regions (V3–V4) of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene sequences in each sample was carried out using barcoded primers: forward 5′-CCTACGGGNBGCASCAG-3′ and reverse 5′-GGACTACNVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′ (Sun et al. 2013; Walters et al. 2016). The subsequent amplification and sequencing procedures were conducted on a MiSeq sequencer with MiSeq Reagent Kits v2 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) following previously established protocols (Van den Abbeele et al. 2010). Sequence data generated from the MiSeq platform were analyzed using the Quantitative Insights into Microbial Ecology 2 (QIIME2) (2020.2) pipeline (Bolyen et al. 2019). Initially, primer sequences were removed from demultiplexed reads using Cutadapt (v.4.1). Subsequently, the sequence number in each sample was rarefied to the same depth (62,917 sequences per sample). Paired-end reads were quality filtered and processed using DADA2 (v.1.16) with the following parameters: raw reads were truncated at 240 bp (forward) and 200 bp (reverse) to remove low-quality regions, and a maximum expected error threshold of 2 was applied to filter out low-quality reads. Chimeric sequences were identified and removed using the consensus method implemented in DADA2. This resulted in the generation of an amplicon sequence variant (ASV) table with 10,218–70,726 assembled sequences per sample. Amplicon sequencing yielded a total of 11,511,475 sequences, and 18,494 amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) (Table 1 and Table S1). Taxonomic classification of the ASVs was accomplished using the SILVA 138 database (https://www.arb-silva.de/). Tax4fun2 was employed to predict the potential functions of the bacterial community based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing data (Asshauer et al. 2015). Tax4Fun predictions were mapped to the annotated pathway and module catalogs of the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database.

Statistical analysis

Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was performed to compare the overall composition of the bacterial community, functional pathways, and genes across groups. Differences in the relative abundance of microbial features in the 16S rRNA sequencing data were determined using linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) analysis through the Galaxy web application (http://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/galaxy/) (Van den Abbeele et al. 2010). Bacterial abundance profiles were calculated at taxonomic levels from phylum to genus, and a logarithmic LDA score 2.0 was set as the threshold.

For the differential abundance analysis of predicted functional pathways and modules, the Games-Howell test was utilized, as demonstrated by principal component analysis (PCA) using the Statistical Analysis of Taxonomic and Functional Profiles (STAMP) software v2.1.3 (Parks et al. 2014). The Games-Howell test was chosen over ANOVA or similar parametric tests due to its robustness in handling datasets with unequal variances and sample sizes, which is a common characteristic of microbiome data. This non-parametric post-hoc test does not assume homogeneity of variance and is particularly suitable for pairwise comparisons when the assumption of equal variances is violated (Games and Howell 1976). Additionally, the Games-Howell test provides reliable results even with small sample sizes, making it an appropriate choice for our dataset.

Analysis of similarity was applied to assess the significance of dissimilarity in pathway and module abundance among the four groups. All statistical data analyses were conducted using JMP Pro 16 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Graphs, including box and bar charts, were generated using GraphPad Prism v8 unless indicated otherwise (Basham 1997). Pairwise comparison of means was performed using the Mann–Whitney U test (α = 0.05), and multi-group comparisons were executed using the Kruskal–Wallis test (α = 0.05). Normally distributed data were presented as mean and standard deviation and analyzed by ANOVA using Turkey’s t-test (α = 0.05). Non-parametric data were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank sum test.

To account for different components of diversity, metrics for α-diversity, including Chao 1, richness, Shannon, and Simpson indices were calculated, while Bray–Curtis was used for β-diversity. All results were derived from data with at least three replications.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 76 children (39 male and 37 female) were enrolled in the study, with 36 healthy individuals and 40 children diagnosed with adenoid hypertrophy (AH) (Table 2, Table S2). Their ages ranged from 3 to 12 years old with a mean age of 5.43 ± 1.48 and mean BMI at 16.9 ± 2.67 (Table S2). The chronic rhinosinusitis evaluation showed a significant difference between AH patients and healthy ones (P = 0.01). Redundancy analysis (RDA) was performed on the AH groups for finding the association between AH severity and other physical and chemical characteristics. However, non-significant characteristics were determined to be highly associated with disease severity (data not shown).

Microbial community structure in hypertrophic adenoid tissue was highly different from nasal cavity

To explore the alteration of microbial diversity and structure in the AH patients, we profiled the microbial composition in their nasal cavity and hypertrophic adenoid tissue and compared them with healthy controls. Overall, no significant difference was detected for Shannon and Simpson index among four groups of samples (P = 0.053 (Shannon) and P = 0.067 (Simpson)). However, we found a significantly higher microbial richness (Richness, Chao1, and ACE) in Tis samples than in others (P = 0.013, 0.013, and 0.026, respectively), which demonstrated that more microbial taxa were colonized in hypertrophic adenoid tissue than in the nasal cavity (Fig. 2a). The number of ASVs also confirmed this result (Table 2).

(a) Alpha diversity metrics of nasal microbiota in healthy children (NOR), children with adenoid hypertrophy (AH), children post-adenoidectomy (AT), and hypertrophic adenoid tissue (Tis); (b) Principal component analysis (PCA) of microbial communities based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarities among the four groups; (c) Relative abundance of dominant bacterial phyla (mean abundance ≥ 0.9%) in nasal and adenoid tissue samples; (d) Relative abundance of dominant bacterial genera (mean abundance ≥ 0.9%) in nasal and adenoid tissue samples

Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on the Bray–Curtis distance revealed conspicuous differences in microbial structure among all four groups, especially between adenoid tissue and nasal cavity samples (Fig. 2b). This finding showed that the adenoid and nasal cavity formed microbial communities with distinct characteristics, which were manifested at both phylum and genus levels. Overall, the majority ASVs were assigned to the phyla Proteobacteria (37.04 ± 15.08%), Firmicutes (25.99 ± 8.75%), Actinobacteria (10.80 ± 11.60%), and Bacteroidetes (10.33 ± 11.17%) in all samples. Additionally, phyla Nitrospirae, Gemmatimonadetes, Fusobacteria, Acidobacteria, and Planctomycetes ranged from 0.11% to 2.8% (Table S3, Fig. 2c). Specifically, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, Gemmatimonadetes, Nitrospirae, and Acidobacteria accumulated in the nasal cavity samples more than in Tis samples, while Bacteroidetes and Fusobacteria were more detected in Tis samples (Fig. 2c). At the genus level, the nasal cavity samples were dominated by Sphingobium, Pseudomonas, Streptococcus, Aggregatibacter, and Faecalibacterium, whereas Prevotella, Bacteroides, Streptococcus, and Moraxella were more abundant in Tis samples (Fig. 2d). These results demonstrated that the microbial components were quite different in hypertrophic adenoid compared with nasal cavity.

Microbial community structure varied between healthy and diseased subjects

The PCoA showed a visible alteration of the overall microbial structure between NOR and AH/AT samples, with a smaller diversity within the group for NOR samples (Fig. 2b). Both oval-shaped clusters exhibited visible within-group variations in AH and AT samples. Although AH and AT samples displayed overlapping clustering patterns, indicating similar microbial compositions, the abundance of bacterial taxa significantly changed after adenoidectomy surgery. The relative abundance of Nitrospirae was significantly lower in the AH group than that in the AT and NOR groups. In contrast, Firmicutes, Gemmatimonadetes, and Fusobacteria were redundant in the AH group compared to the other two (Fig. 2c). The linear discriminant analysis effect size (LefSe) method was performed to further identify the key microbiota members responsible for discriminating diseased subjects from healthy ones. Thirty-six taxa were identified in the AH group as the key microbiota compared with the NOR group, while only 25 taxa were identified specifically in the AT group (Fig. 3a, b). From a phylogenetic perspective, the microbes in the NOR group were derived into three clusters, while those in the AH samples clustered into one major group, indicating the microbial composition was dramatically altered in diseased children compared with healthy ones (Fig. 3c). A similar result was detected in random forest analysis. Four genera (Alloiococcus, Moraxella, Streptococcus, and Bacteroides) were confirmed as taxa highly associated with the patients’ samples but were reduced after surgery (Fig. 4a). In contrast, Sphingobium, Pseudomonas, Faecalibacterium, and Paenisporosarcina were significantly abundant in the NOR and AT groups, while detected significantly lower in the AH group (Fig. 4a). Additionally, Moraxella and Bacteroides were redundant not only in the nasal cavity of AH but also in hypertrophic adenoid, indicating their high correlation with the occurrence of AH (Fig. 4b).

Pathways related to pathogenesis were enriched in nasal microbiomes of adenoid hypertrophy children

Functional metagenomic predictions based on differences in taxonomic composition suggest potential distinctions in the functional and ecological properties of microbial communities within different groups. Notably, AH samples exhibited considerable within-group variation (red circle in Fig. 5a), while NOR samples displayed a more concentrated pattern (blue circle). Significantly, the intra-group variance among AT samples was less pronounced than that of AH, with AT samples forming an elliptical cluster that partially overlapped with the NOR sample cluster. This outcome suggests a discernible shift in the functional profile of nasal cavity microbes in diseased children after AT surgery. Consistent findings were observed in PCA of functional modules, where NOR and AT modules tended to cluster together, whereas AH samples displayed greater dispersion (Fig. 5b). These results indicate that the nasal microbial functions in diseased children tend to align more closely with a normal pattern after surgery, mirroring the observed changes in microbial diversity. To gain deeper insights into the alterations in microbial functions within the nasal cavity of diseased children, a STAMP analysis was conducted on all 354 predicted pathways and 294 modules, revealing 173 and 139 pathways and modules, respectively, exhibiting differential abundance between NOR and AH groups at P ≤ 0.05. Of these, 21 pathways and modules with abundance > 0.01 were selected for detailed presentation in Fig. 5c and d, respectively.

Broadly, the carbon and nitrogen metabolic pathways of the microbiota in the AH group exhibited a significant decrease compared to those in the NOR group. This trend extended to critical processes such as the bacterial two-component system, chemotactic reactions, amino sugar, and nucleotide sugar biosynthesis pathways (Fig. 5c). These findings underscore fundamental physiological disorders and diminished metabolic activities within the nasal flora of diseased children. Particularly noteworthy was the impact on chemotaxis reactions to external chemicals. To better understand the impact of adenoidectomy surgery on nasal microbial function, we compared the abundance of predicted modules among the NOR, AT, and AH samples to discern the trends in specific modules (Fig. 6). A notable shift was evident in the enrichment of degradation (M00044) and a decline of biosynthesis (M00025) for tyrosine, indicating a reduction in tyrosine levels within the nasal flora of the diseased group. Previous studies have highlighted the potential of bacterial-enhanced tyrosine metabolism in preventing allergic airway inflammation (Wypych et al. 2021). Abundance was also observed in instances such as phenylalanine biosynthesis (M00024) and methionine degradation (M00035) pathways in individuals with AH. In contrast, lysine and melatonin biosynthesis (M00030, M00037), as well as tryptophan metabolism (M00038) exhibited significant reductions. However, these distinctions between healthy and diseased groups diminished after surgery, reaffirming our hypothesis: surgery not only recovered the microbial components but also reconstructed the microbial function within the nasal flora of AH children, aligning it more closely with a normal physiological state.

Discussion

We investigated the correlation between microbial communities and hypertrophic adenoids through an analysis of metagenomic sequencing results from children’s nasal cavity and adenoid samples. Our findings identified four predominant phyla (Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, and Bacteroidetes) and five predominant genera (Sphingobium, Pseudomonas, Streptococcus, Aggregatibacter, and Lactobacillus) across all samples, aligning with previous studies (Johnston et al. 2018; Marazzato et al. 2020; Ren et al. 2013). Although no significant differences were observed in the Shannon and Simpson indices between healthy and diseased groups, there was a higher richness of ASVs in the Tis group compared to others. These findings indicate notable variations in microbial structure and diversity between adenoid tissue and nasal cavity samples. Indeed, AH did not affect the microbial diversity in the nasal cavity; rather, the sampling sites had a greater effect on microbial composition. This is consistent with Zhang et al.’s findings that the presence of obstructive sleep apnea caused by AH did not have a significant impact on the microbiota in the nasal cavity. Instead, it might affect flora in a specific part (Zhang et al. 2023). The nasal cavity, exposed to air with a relatively low abundance of anaerobic bacteria, may create a less favorable environment for certain bacterial species compared to adenoid tissue. Piters et al. (Bassis et al. 2015) observed in their research that the bacterial community in the lungs shares greater similarity with that of the oral cavity than the nasal cavity, indicating potential challenges for nasal cavity bacteria to traverse the upper respiratory tract to reach the lungs. This difficulty may extend to bacterial migration into the adenoids, contributing to substantial differences between bacterial communities in the nasal cavity and adenoids. Nevertheless, our analysis identified a shared group of bacterial taxa between AH nasal cavity and adenoid tissue, particularly the genus Moraxella. This result supports our hypothesis that specific microbial abundance may be potentially associated with the occurrence of AH.

Despite no observable differences in α diversity among nasal cavity microbial communities in healthy and diseased groups, the PCoA analysis revealed a significant distinction between the NOR and AH/AT groups. The microbial pattern in the nasal cavity of children with AH was significantly different compared with healthy ones, and adenoidectomy treatment seems to reduce this difference in bacterial taxa abundance. This result suggests a correlation between the nasal microbial components and AH phenotype. Notably, microbial diversity exhibited substantial differences between the NOR and AH groups at both the phylum and genus levels. Some predominant bacterial groups, including Streptococcus, Haemophilus, Staphylococcus, and Moraxella, have previously been identified as potential pathogens in upper airway diseases (Dirain et al. 2017; Prates et al. 2018; van Staaij et al. 2003). However, contradictory findings have also been reported (Marazzato et al. 2020), underscoring the limitations of culture-independent research. Factors such as sample methods, age, gender, overall health, and allergic conditions may contribute to disparate results. It is crucial to note that our use of 16S rRNA gene sequencing allowed only genus-level allocation of reads. Despite conducting analysis at the species level, we did not detect previously reported pathogenic species, such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Moraxella catarrhalis (Dirain et al. 2017; Ren et al. 2013). To address this limitation, further investigations employing culture-based bacterial isolation and in vivo experiments are warranted to detect bacteria at the species level and confirm the abundance of pathogenic species.

In addition to the difference of microbial structures, there were notable variations in the abundance of pathways and modules between the NOR and AH/AT groups. For instance, key biochemical activities, including starch, glucose, and nitrogen metabolism, signaling pathways, and chemotactic reaction pathways, exhibited a decrease in the AH group, indicating a fundamental impact on the basic functioning of the nasal flora with the occurrence of AH. Changes in signaling pathways and chemotaxis could influence bacterial interactions, especially critical functions for reproduction and colonization, potentially contributing to alterations in the nasal microbial community. Wypych et al. (Wypych et al. 2021) demonstrated that enhanced L-tyrosine metabolism in gut bacterial communities could prevent allergic airway inflammation. Then, the nasal microflora, located in the upper respiratory tract, may more directly influence airway immune responses and allergies through altered tyrosine metabolism. Experiments have confirmed that the pathway of tyrosine metabolites can act on distal airway epithelial cells to reduce allergic airway reactions. We did detect an accumulation in metabolism and a decline in the biosynthesis of tyrosine, respectively, in diseased nasal flora, indicating a potential correlation with the occurrence and progression of AH.

In this study, the observed reduction in metabolic pathways, particularly tyrosine and melatonin biosynthesis, presents intriguing possibilities for future therapeutic interventions. Given that the study found a reduction in tyrosine biosynthesis in the nasal flora of the diseased group and that previous research has indicated that bacterial-enhanced tyrosine metabolism can prevent allergic airway inflammation, this suggests that therapeutic strategies could be developed to promote tyrosine production in the nasal microflora to mitigate the progression of AH.

Melatonin, a natural hormone with antioxidant properties, has been linked to improvement of sleep conditions, inflammation-related health problems, and mental well-being (Bazyar et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2020; Reiter et al. 2021; Sanchez-Lopez et al. 2018; Xu et al. 2020). The decline in melatonin synthesis observed in the nasal flora of the AH group could contribute to the acceleration or exacerbation of AH severity and may offer a novel explanation for the poor sleep experienced by AH children. Moreover, this reduction may offer a novel explanation for the poor sleep experienced by AH children, a condition traditionally attributed to respiratory obstruction by AH. Future studies focusing on detecting melatonin levels in AH children could be conducted to validate and further explore this hypothesis. In addition, our conclusion on pathway and module analysis was not only speculated from comparison between healthy and AH group but was further supported by the observed trends in AH group before and after surgery, indicating a reduction in differences post-surgery, thus validating our initial hypothesis.

The study’s findings have several potential clinical implications. The identification of specific bacterial taxa, such as Alloiococcus, Moraxella, Streptococcus, and Bacteroides, particularly Moraxella, as biomarkers could lead to earlier diagnosis of AH. Additionally, the research suggests interventions could be developed to target altered microbial functions and pathways associated with AH, such as restoring metabolic balance or promoting tyrosine and melatonin production. Post-surgery monitoring of the microbial composition could be used to assess treatment effectiveness. The study also contributes to a better understanding of the pathogenesis of AH, highlighting the potential for the nasal microbiome to be a therapeutic target. These findings could lead to personalized treatment plans, and the potential use of Moraxella sp. as a biomarker is clinically significant for prompt intervention and prevention.

In the future research, we will explore the role of Moraxella spp. in the development of AH using culture-based or in vivo experiments. This is particularly important because the current study, while identifying Moraxella as a potential biomarker for AH, did not establish a causal relationship. The study did note that Moraxella spp. were significantly enriched in both the nasal cavity and adenoid tissues of the AH group, suggesting a potential association with the disease. In vivo experiments, such as using animal models, could help to determine whether Moraxella can directly cause or exacerbate AH. Such studies would be essential to validate the potential of Moraxella as a therapeutic target and to develop more effective treatments and preventative measures for AH.

In summary, our study highlights, for the first time, a potential correlation between nasal microbial communities and AH disease by analyzing microbial structural and functional diversity in healthy and AH-afflicted children. While our conclusions are drawn from culture-independent sequence analysis, further in vivo experiments with specific strains are essential to delve deeper into the underlying mechanisms. Although we lack sufficient evidence to designate Moraxella spp. as the causal agent for AH, it may serve as a valuable biomarker for early AH diagnosis, holding clinical significance for prompt intervention and prevention.
