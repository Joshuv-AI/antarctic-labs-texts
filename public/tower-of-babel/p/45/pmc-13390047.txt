Machine learning to classify left ventricular hypertrophy using electrocardiographic feature extraction by variational autoencoder
Authors: Amulya Gupta, Christopher J. Harvey, Ashley DeBauge, Sumaiya Shomaji, Zijun Yao, Yongkuk Lee, Amit Noheria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTraditional electrocardiographic (ECG) criteria for left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) have modest diagnostic yield.ObjectiveThis study aimed to develop and validate machine learning (ML) models for LVH diagnosis from ECG.MethodsECG calculations (rate, intervals, and axis); R-wave, S-wave, and overall-QRS amplitudes; and QRS voltage-time integrals were obtained from 12-lead, vectorcardiographic X-Y-Z–lead, and 3-dimensional (root-sum-square) ECGs. Deep learning–enabled latent embeddings (30 per ECG) were extracted using a variational autoencoder (pretrained on unselected 1.18 million ECGs) from representative-beat signals. Logistic regression, random forest, light gradient boosted machine (LGBM), residual neural network and multilayered perceptron network models using ECG features (calculations and embeddings) and sex, and a convolutional neural network (CNN) using ECG signals alone were trained to predict LVH (left ventricular mass index, women >95 g/m2; men >115 g/m2) on 482,734 ECG-echocardiogram pairs (±45 days). Area under the receiver operating characteristic curves were reported from a holdout testing set.ResultsIn the testing set (n = 54,984), the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve for LVH classification was higher for ML models using ECG features (LGBM 0.794; multilayered perceptron 0.793; residual neural network 0.795) than the best individual ECG variable (Z-axis QRS voltage-time integral 0.707), the best traditional criterion (Cornell voltage-duration product 0.716), and the CNN using ECG signals (0.788). Among patients without LVH who had a follow-up echocardiogram >1 year later, LGBM false positives, compared with true negatives, had a 3.07-fold higher odds of developing future LVH (95% confidence interval 2.44–3.86; P < .0001).ConclusionML models are superior to traditional ECG criteria for classifying LVH. Models trained on extracted ECG features, including deep-learning latent space representations, can outperform CNN models trained on ECG signals.

Key Findings ▪Vectorcardiographic ECG metrics and conventional voltage-based criteria demonstrated moderate discrimination for the diagnosis of left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH). In contrast, machine learning models trained on ECG signals achieved superior diagnostic performance.▪Latent representations derived from a pre-trained variational autoencoder enabled the use of computationally efficient tree-based models, such as Light Gradient Boosted Machine (LGBM), as an alternative to convolutional neural networks, while achieving comparable or improved classification performance.▪The predicted probabilities generated by the fully trained LGBM classifier exhibited a strong linear association with left ventricular mass index (LVMi), the structural substrate underlying LVH. Further, individuals classified as false positives had a 3-fold higher risk of subsequent LVH development, suggesting that apparent misclassifications may reflect subclinical or evolving myocardial substrate.

Introduction

Left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) refers to increased left ventricular (LV) mass, characterized by an increase in LV wall thickness and/or enlargement of the LV dimensions. This is often secondary to pathologic or physiological stressors such as chronic hypertension, valvular heart disease, athletic training, or genetic conditions. LVH is associated with more than a 2-fold increase in cardiovascular morbidity and all-cause mortality.1 Early detection and initiation of pharmacological treatment and lifestyle modifications can improve outcomes.2

Transthoracic echocardiography is the standard of care for the diagnosis of LVH. Although noninvasive and widely available, echocardiography-based universal screening—even among high-risk populations, such as those with hypertension—is not cost-effective.3,4

Electrocardiography (ECG) is an affordable, widely accessible, and frequently used diagnostic tool for cardiovascular screening. Often considered an extension of the cardiovascular physical examination, it is estimated that more than 100–300 million ECGs are performed annually in the United States.5 Traditionally, LVH diagnosis via 12-lead ECG relies on voltage-based criteria, yet these show poor sensitivity, limiting their utility as standalone screening tools.6, 7, 8

Machine learning (ML) can reduce reliance on human interpretation and yet increase the diagnostic accuracy of ECG.9,10 Several ECG-based ML models have been developed for detecting LVH, with varying sensitivities and specificities.11 Many of these studies use deep-learning convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures to train models using ECG signals often with fewer than 10,000 training ECGs. Given that each 12-lead 10-second ECG signal at 500 Hz consists of 60,000 data points, using such a high-dimensionality input for deep learning with a limited number of samples can result in overfitting and reduced generalizability.12, 13, 14 In contrast, non–neural network ML architectures, such as logistic regression, random forest, and gradient boosted machine, are not suited to use high-dimensional ECG signal data as input and are usually limited to using extracted ECG features, which may result in loss of predictive information.14

To mitigate these limitations, while preserving the advantages of deep learning, we developed a variational autoencoder (VAE) that can encode a 0.75-second representative cardiac beat from X-Y-Z–lead ECG signals into 30 variables in the learned latent space.14, 15, 16 These VAE latent space representations retain the ECG morphologic information and can reconstruct back the ECG signal with high fidelity. In this study, we aimed to train and test different ML models using extracted ECG features including the latent embeddings or the ECG signal to classify LVH from the representative-beat ECG.

Methods

Patient selection and data retrieval

An automated retrospective retrieval of records was performed from our clinical database at The University of Kansas Health System between May 2010 and January 2022 to search for ECGs and echocardiograms performed on the same patient within 45 days of each other. Echocardiograms-ECG pairs with echocardiographic LV mass index (LVMi) of >95 g/m2 for females and >115 g/m2 for males were labeled as “LVH,” whereas the rest of the pairs were assigned to the “no LVH” group.17 The study was approved by the institutional review board, which granted a waiver of an informed consent owing to the retrospective design. All procedures conformed to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data extraction

ECGs were acquired from the Philips 12-lead ECG system. The 12-lead 1200-ms representative-beat signals along with standard measurements such as heart rate and PR interval were extracted. Echocardiograms were standard clinical studies performed for clinical indications both as outpatient and inpatient evaluations. Individual echocardiogram numeric variables including diastolic measurements of LV internal diameter, interventricular septum, and posterior wall thickness from 2-dimensional parasternal long-axis view were extracted using a backend query in Healthcare Enterprise Repository for Ontological Narration, a search discovery tool that facilitates searches on various hospital electronic data sources.18,19 LV mass was calculated using the American Society of Echocardiography–recommended formula—0.8 × 1.04 × (LV internal diameter + interventricular septum + posterior wall thickness)3—and indexed to body surface area.20

ECG processing

The details of ECG processing performed using Python are provided in previous publications.17,21,22 In summary, vectorcardiographic X-Y-Z–lead ECG signals were reconstructed from 12-lead ECGs using Kors’s matrix.23 The root-sum-square (L2 norm) of these orthogonal leads signal was used to derive the 3-dimensional (3D) ECG lead. Voltage-time integrals of QRS (VTIQRS) were obtained by the integration of the instantaneous voltage over the duration of QRS. R- and S-wave amplitudes were calculated as the maximum (max) positive and negative deflections respectively of the QRS complex from the isoelectric line. Overall-QRS amplitudes were calculated as the max absolute difference between the R and S deflections.

Traditional criteria and univariable models

Based on review of literature, we selected the underlying metrics used for the diagnostic cutoffs of 5 widely used ECG-based LVH diagnostic criteria for comparison, that is, Peguero-Lo Presti criteria (max S + Sv4), Cornell voltage (RavL + Sv3), Cornell voltage-duration product (VDP), Sokolow-Lyon criteria (SV1 + max R [V5 or V6]), and Gubner-Ungerleider criteria (RI + SIII).24 For comparison, we also selected summary QRS measurements, namely QRS duration, amplitudeQRS-3D, and 3D VTIQRS (VTIQRS-3D).21,22,24 The latter 2 were calculated off the QRS from the 3D (L2 norm) ECG. In addition, amplitudeQRS and VTIQRS in the vectorcardiographic X, Y, and Z projections were included.

VAE

We previously trained a VAE on 1.18 million unlabeled ECG signals to encode a 0.75-second segment centered on the 1.2-second representative-beat X-Y-Z–lead ECG signals into 30 variables.14 The VAE has a dual neural network architecture with the encoder taking the ECG input and outputting 30 Gaussian distributions in the learned latent space and the decoder sampling from the 30 distributions to reconstruct the ECG signal. The networks were rewarded in training to learn a latent space to enable accurate reconstruction of the original signal from the latent space representations or embeddings. Our VAE is able to reconstruct the original signal back from the latent variables with high fidelity.14, 15, 16 The X-Y-Z–lead representative-beat ECGs included in this study were processed using this VAE to extract these 30 latent variables per ECG.

Model training and testing

Approximately 10% of the medical record numbers in the dataset, representing unique patients, were withheld as the testing set, and the remainder were used for model training. We trained the following ML architectures on the training set: logistic regression, random forests, light gradient boosted machine (LGBM), residual neural network (ResNet), multilayered perceptron (MLP), and CNN. All the models were trained on the abovementioned ECG features except for the CNN, which was trained directly on the representative-beat X-Y-Z–lead ECG signals. Sex was provided to the models as the definition of LVH is sex specific. The results are reported from the performance of the trained models in the holdout testing set. We also report the models’ performance in 4 subgroups based on intraventricular conduction, a QRS duration of ≤120 ms, right bundle branch block (RBBB) (QRS duration >120), left bundle branch block (LBBB) (QRS duration >120 ms), and nonspecific intraventricular conduction delay (IVCD) (QRS duration >120 ms but not meeting either RBBB or LBBB criteria). The American Heart Association–American College of Cardiology Foundation–Heart Rhythm Society criteria were used for classifying bundle branch blocks.26

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as percentages. Comparisons were made using Student t test for continuous variables and χ2 test for categorical variables. Model performance is reported as the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and sensitivity at fixed 75% specificity. AUC comparisons were made using DeLong’s variance. Model performance is also presented using area under the precision-recall curve, precision, and recall (at the same 75% specificity threshold) in the Supplemental Appendix. ECG processing and model training was performed in Python (version 3.12.7). Statistical analysis was conducted in R (version 4.4.1), and a 2-tailed P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 537,718 ECG-echocardiogram pairs from 89,145 patients were included, with 237,592 pairs (44.2%) belonging to females. The mean age of the overall population of ECG-echocardiogram samples was 65.0 ± 15.4 years. This dataset was split into approximately 90% of training (n = 482,734) and 10% of testing sets (n = 54,984). In the training set, 70,659 female samples (33.1%) and 77,777 male samples (28.9%) had echocardiographic LVH. In the testing set, 8051 female samples (33.6%) and 8793 male samples (28.4%) had LVH. Univariable models

Among the linear univariable models, Z-axis VTIQRS (VTIQRS-Z) was the best predictor of LVH in the overall population, with an AUC of 0.707 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.694–0.720), which was closely followed by VTIQRS-3D with an AUC of 0.704 (95% CI 0.691–0.717; P = .0472). Furthermore, VTIQRS-Z demonstrated the highest performance in the narrow QRS subgroup (AUC 0.704; 95% CI 0.686–0.721; all pairwise comparison P < .0001), whereas VTIQRS-3D performed best in the typical RBBB subgroup (AUC 0.683; 95% CI 0.651–0.714; all pairwise comparison P < .0001). The best performing criteria in LBBB were amplitudeQRS-3D (AUC 0.655; 95% CI 0.624–0.685) and amplitudeQRS-Z (AUC 0.652; 95% CI 0.622–0.682; P = .5051), and in IVCD were QRS duration (0.677; 95% CI 0.622–0.732), followed by VTIQRS-Z (0.646; 95% CI 0.591–0.701; P = .0308).

Traditional LVH criteria

Overall, the performance of the metrics underlying traditional ECG criteria for predicting LVH was not substantially better than univariable predictors, with AUCs ranging from 0.545 to 0.716. Cornell VDP was the best performing traditional criteria overall population (AUC 0.716; 95% CI 0.704–0.729; all pairwise comparison P < .0001), narrow QRS subgroup (AUC 0.713; 95% CI 0.696–0.730; all P < .0001), and IVCD subgroup (AUC 0.694; 95% CI 0.640–0.748; all P < .0001). In the RBBB subgroup, Cornell VDP (AUC 0.678; 95% CI 0.646–0.710) performed the best, followed by Peguero-Lo Presti criteria (AUC 0.668; 95% CI 0.636–0.700; P = .0449). In the LBBB subgroup, Peguero-Lo Presti criteria (AUC 0.651; 95% CI 0.621–0.682) performed the best, followed by Cornell VDP (AUC 0.635; 95% CI 0.604–0.665; P = .0004) and Sokolow-Lyon criteria (AUC 0.627; 95% CI 0.596–0.658; P = .0045). In general, these traditional criteria metrics seemed to perform better in females than males.

ML models

All ML models outperformed the univariable measurements and traditional LVH criteria metrics (all pairwise P < .0001). Among the ML models, LGBM (AUC 0.794; 95% CI 0.782–0.807), MLP (0.793; 95% CI 0.781–0.805; P = .2745 compared with LGBM), and ResNet (0.795; 95% CI 0.783–0.807; P = .4980 compared with LGBM), which were trained on ECG features—including VAE latent embeddings—and sex, were the best performing models in the overall population. The CNN model, which was trained on the raw ECG signal alone, demonstrated an AUC of 0.788 (P < .0001 compared with LGBM). Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the largest evaluation of ECG criteria and ML models for LVH prediction to date. We have applied the innovative framework of using deep learning–based latent space embeddings extracted from ECG for building ML models, which allows models that are computationally simpler than CNNs to make accurate predictions without overfitting.

Salient findings

First, the underlying metrics for traditional criteria using QRS-based cutoffs demonstrated modest utility in diagnosing LVH, with the Cornell VDP showing the highest discrimination among them (AUC 0.716). Second, univariable measurements including VTIQRS-Z and VTIQRS-3D were at par with traditional criteria metrics for the diagnosis of LVH, with VTIQRS-Z achieving the best AUC (0.707). Third, our ML models outperform both traditional criteria metrics and univariable measurements, with LGBM, ResNet, and MLP demonstrating the highest performance (AUC 0.793–0.795). Furthermore, the LGBM model trained on ECG features captured the underlying trend of cardiac mass, not just the LVH labels, showing strong correlation with LVMi and predicting future development of LVH. Finally, the performance of traditional criteria metrics, univariable measurements, and ML models varies across sex and QRS morphologies.

Univariable models

Previous studies have demonstrated the utility of linear univariable predictors of LVH, such as QRS duration and area.22,27 In our analysis, we evaluated QRS duration and vectorcardiographic amplitudes/voltage-time integrals for predicting LVH across various subgroups. Among them, VTIQRS-Z and VTIQRS-3D emerged as the best overall discriminators. Similar to Cornell VDP, both voltage and duration components of the QRS complex are incorporated in these measurements. Prolongation of the QRS duration in LVH is likely attributable to delays in ventricular activation associated with larger ventricular dimensions to depolarize, increased wall thickness, and associated intraventricular conduction abnormalities. Furthermore, given that VTIQRS-3D is calculated from the reconstructed 3D orthogonal leads, it ostensibly captures the sum of net surface potentials produced by the ventricular depolarization wavefront in a cardiac cycle.28 The absolute values of surface potentials, especially in Z-axis (anteroposterior or sagittal axis), are likely to be increased owing to LVH as the mean depolarization vector shifts posteriorly.29 Consequently, VTIQRS-Z and VTIQRS-3D are likely to be increased in LVH and could be explored as a simple univariable predictor of LVH.

Traditional ECG criteria

As demonstrated in previous studies, our analysis reaffirmed the modest-to-fair discrimination of LVH offered by standard ECG criteria using a large dataset.30,31 Unlike other voltage-based rules, Cornell VDP, which emerged as the best overall traditional criterion metric, accounts for both QRS voltage and duration in its calculation, both of which are affected in LVH.32 In the subset of ECGs with LBBB, Peguero-Lo Presti criteria performed better than Cornell VDP. Although the difference in performance was marginal and is not definitive, physiologically it could be explained by obfuscation of LVH-related changes in QRS duration owing to QRS prolongation inherent to conduction delay in LBBB. In addition, compared with the combined population, individual criteria generally performed better in females and males separately. This underscores the importance of using different cutoff values for females and males, recognizing the sex-based differences in ECGs and definition of LVH.30,31

ML models

We tested several ML architectures for LVH prediction, including simple models (LR), tree-based models (RF and LGBM), and neural networks (ResNet, MLP, and CNN). The best overall performance was shown by LGBM, ResNet, and MLP with AUCs of ∼0.79. Calibration for these models was generally acceptable. However, the LGBM model demonstrated systematic overprediction of risk although the calibration slope remained close to 1 (minimal over- or underfitting), suggesting preserved risk ranking. Such miscalibration can be readily corrected at deployment with simple recalibration (eg, intercept adjustment) and would not materially affect discrimination or threshold-based decision rules (given that these are invariant to monotonic shifts in predicted probabilities).

Performance declined across all models in the subgroups with conduction abnormalities. Interestingly, these models, using the summary ECG features including VAE embeddings, performed slightly better than the CNN model, which had access to the raw signal data. The use of the LGBM architecture for such classification tasks is pragmatic for a few reasons. As an implementation of gradient boosting decision trees, it enables efficient model training with relatively low computational resources and can be achieved without overfitting when the number of training samples are limited.

We further evaluated the physiological relevance of the trained LGBM model. First, we plotted the prediction probabilities from this model against LVMi, which showed a strong linear positive correlation for both females and males, suggesting that the model captures meaningful physiological patterns rather than artificial class boundaries. Second, we analyzed the false positives produced by this model for future development of LVH, finding that the false positives had 3 times the odds of developing LVH in the future compared with true negatives. This indicates that the model may capture underlying ECG abnormalities even before patients meet the criteria for overt LVH diagnosis on echocardiography.

Previous literature

In a recently published study from China, Zhu et al33 used a large dataset comprising of more than 90,000 ECGs to create deep-learning multilabel classifier algorithms. They achieved AUCs ranging from 0.78 to 0.92 using their 12-lead model and showed that a reduced 4-lead model using leads I, aVR, V1, and V5 had equivalent performance. In a Taiwanese study, Liu et al34 developed a deep-learning model for predicting LVH using approximately 23,000 training samples. They achieved high AUCs ranging from 0.83 to 0.89 across different validation sets. However, the definition of LVH used in this study was different, using an LV mass of >186 g for females and >258 g for males. In a South Korean study, Kwon et al35 developed an ensemble deep neural network + CNN model using approximately 36,000 training samples, combining information from ECG signal, ECG features, and patient demographics. While using higher cutoff values for LVMi (109 g/m2 females and 132 g/m2 males), their model achieved AUCs ranging from 0.87 to 0.88 in validation sets.

In a study from Massachusetts General Hospital, Haimovich et al36 create ML models for predicting LVH in specific disease populations such as cardiac amyloidosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and aortic stenosis using a total of 34,258 training samples. Similar to our approach, they used a pretrained deep-learning model to produce latent embeddings and trained a simpler classifier for LVH classification although they used a full 10-second ECG signal instead of representative-beat ECG. Their model achieved AUCs ranging from 0.69 to 0.96 in various subgroups. Haimovich et al36 used data from the UK Biobank to create a CNN model trained on 32,000 samples and achieved AUCs ranging from 0.62 to 0.65 in predicting LVH.

Owing to heterogeneity in study populations, data structures, and labels for LVH, it is difficult to evaluate the performance of models across studies. Nonetheless, the AUCs attained by ML models for LVH prediction in our study are comparable with previous work.

Limitations

Our work is best understood in the context of its limitations. Both training and testing sets for the models were from a single center, and generalizability to other datasets needs to be evaluated. The inclusion of in-hospital ECGs may have biased our dataset toward sicker patients, who often get multiple ECGs in each hospital stay. Furthermore, given that the representative-beat ECGs were derived from a proprietary system, additional signal segmentation and binning steps are required in processing ECGs from other systems. In addition, automated processing was used for LVMi calculation and ECG feature extraction and to measure or calculate the metrics for traditional criteria and univariate models. Without individual oversight of each extracted value, the dataset may be prone to inaccuracies in the extracted variables and labels.

Conclusion

Traditional QRS-based ECG criteria demonstrate only modest performance in detecting LVH. Simple univariable models, especially VTIQRS-Z, may perform at par with traditional criteria. Regardless, ML techniques can significantly enhance the accuracy of ECG-based diagnosis of LVH over both traditional criteria and univariable models. Our findings highlight the utility of dimensionality reduction via VAEs, which enables the application of a deep-learning framework to train non–deep-learning ML models to high-dimensional ECG signal data without compromising performance and without overfitting. Such an approach offers both transparency and scalability, suggesting potential for clinical integration, although external validation is necessary before broad clinical utilization of these ML models.
