Joint trajectories of burden and benefits of caregiving among informal caregivers of older adults with functional limitations: a longitudinal study
Authors: Yongjing Ping, Jeremy Lim-Soh, Truls Østbye, Rahul Malhotra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInformal caregiving for older adults, often long-term, can be concurrently burdensome and beneficial. This longitudinal study aims to delineate distinct joint trajectories of burden and benefits of caregiving among informal caregivers of older adults with functional limitations. We conducted group-based multi-trajectory modelling on longitudinal data – collected at four time-points – on burden (modified-Caregiver Reaction Assessment) and benefits (short Positive Aspects of Caregiving scale) from 274 informal caregivers of older adults in Singapore to delineate joint trajectories of burden and benefits. We used multinomial logistic regression to identify factors associated with the joint trajectories and linear regression to examine the overall well-being across caregivers with distinct joint trajectories. Four distinct joint trajectories were identified: persistently low burden and high benefits (44.5% of caregivers); persistently moderate burden and very high benefits (23.0%); persistently moderate burden and high benefits (19.0%); and persistently high burden and high benefits (13.5%). Having care-related support from an experienced/trained live-in home help worker at baseline (versus those without) was associated with persistently lower burden. Being more prepared in caregiving at baseline was associated with persistently higher benefits. The findings highlight the meaningful heterogeneity in joint trajectories of burden and benefits of caregiving. Introduction

Given rapid population aging and the rising prevalence of functional limitations (i.e., limitations in basic or instrumental activities of daily living [ADLs/IADLs]) with age1, the proportion of older adults with functional limitations will continue to increase. This suggests a growing need for long-term care. Informal caregivers, who are family members or friends of individuals with chronic diseases or disabilities2, are an important source of long-term care – it has been estimated that 70–95% of care needs are met by informal caregivers3.

Informal caregiving for older adults with functional limitations has been associated with both burdensome and beneficial experiences for caregivers4. Burden has been defined as perceived the physical, psychological, and social strain due to caregiving5, and benefits has been defined as the perceived psychological and social reward resulting from caregiving6. A meta-analysis of cross-sectional studies has reported that burden and benefits of caregiving are inversely correlated7, suggesting that informal caregivers having higher burden concurrently experience lower benefits, and vice versa. However, as informal caregiving is often long-term8, longitudinal studies are important to understand if such an inverse correlation extends over time, that is, if informal caregivers with persistently high or increasing burden simultaneously have persistently low or decreasing benefits. Only a few recent longitudinal studies have examined the concurrent change (i.e., joint trajectory) in burden and benefits of caregiving, showing that a large proportion (56–72%) of informal caregivers do experience persistently moderate burden and high benefits. Nevertheless, these studies also report that a proportion (12–25%) of informal caregivers experience either persistently high burden and high benefits9,10 or an increase in both burden and benefits over time11. This points to heterogeneity in the extent and progression of joint trajectories of burden and benefits of caregiving.

Conceptual framework

Folkman (1997)’s stress and coping model helps us understand the value of studying such heterogeneity12. Informal caregivers may gain benefits from caregiving, such as a sense of self-accomplishment, via successfully managing care needs of themselves and care recipients; those who fail to meet care needs of themselves and care recipients may not gain benefits from caregiving and perceive their caregiving role as burdensome. Nevertheless, caregivers may apply meaning-based coping strategies, such as resetting their caregiving goals, reappraising the meaning of care provision, or thinking positively, to justify their caregiving role and gain benefits from caregiving alongside burden. Based on this model, caregivers having persistently low or decreasing burden along with persistently high or increasing benefits may face few care needs, which are feasible to accomplish. Caregivers with persistently high or increasing burden as well as benefits over time may face more care needs, while they can still find their caregiving role meaningful. On the other hand, caregivers with persistently high or increasing burden along with persistently low or decreasing benefits may fail to meet care needs as well as find meaning from a demanding caregiving role. Therefore, untangling heterogeneity in joint trajectories of burden and benefits of caregiving can help identify the proportion and characteristics of informal caregivers who have a demanding caregiving duty and/or cannot gain benefits from caregiving over time.

Research gaps

Despite the few studies that have assessed the heterogeneity in joint trajectories of burden and benefits of caregiving9–11, three critical gaps remain. First, according to a recent systematic review on trajectories of burden or benefits of caregiving13, very few studies focus on the joint trajectories of burden and benefits of caregiving among informal caregivers of older adults with functional limitations. Existing studies either relate to caregivers of persons with moderate-to-mild dementia11 or older adults with terminal illness (i.e., advanced stages of cancer or dementia)9,10. While studies on caregivers of older adults with terminal illness are informative, they may overestimate the extent of burden over time, as these older adults often have more care needs, such as more ADL/IADL impairments or problem behaviors14. Given rapid population aging and the gap between life and health expectancy in later adulthood15, the care needs of older adults will continue to grow, requiring more research to understand the caregiving experiences over time of informal caregivers of older adults with functional limitations, irrespective of the health conditions contributing to the limitations.

Second, based on the modified stress appraisal model16, caregiver and care recipient socio-demographics, care recipient functional limitations, caregiving time, and care-related support are associated with burden and benefits of caregiving. Empirical research has shown that being a spousal caregiver, perceiving less financial adequacy, spending more time in caregiving, or supporting care recipients with more ADL/IADL impairments or problem behaviors are associated with joint trajectories of persistently high or increasing burden and/or persistently low or decreasing benefits9–11. However, the use of care-related support, such as home- or community-based services (HCBS) and assistance from additional caregivers, have not been comprehensively studied13, even though existing studies are based in high-income countries (i.e., United Kingdom and Singapore) where many care-related support options are available17,18. Two studies have evaluated the effect of hiring live-in home help workers on joint trajectories of burden and benefits, but did not find an association9,10. Nevertheless, such categorization (i.e., employing a live-in home help worker or not) omits the caregiving history of these workers as some may acquire better caregiving skills with previous experience or training in supporting older adults.

Finally, previous evidence, examining the well-being of informal caregivers across distinct joint trajectories of burden and benefits, has mainly focused on caregiver psychological well-being (such as grief and distress)9. However, it is unclear if caregiver physical or social well-being, beyond psychological well-being, varies across distinct joint trajectories of burden and benefits. Such evidence may suggest different interventions: for example, those following joint trajectories associated with poorer physical well-being may need support that helps care recipients perform their daily activities; those with joint trajectories linked with poorer psychological well-being may need support for managing psychological distress; and those with joint trajectories associated with poorer social well-being may need support to build and maintain social connections.

Aims and hypotheses

This study focuses on informal caregivers of older adults with functional limitations in Singapore, a country with a rapidly aging population19 and a strong role of informal caregivers in eldercare, given its cultural norms emphasizing filial piety20. Furthermore, a national survey in Singapore reported that around 80% of informal caregivers had provided care for more than one year21, highlighting the value in assessing the patterns of burden and benefits of caregiving over time among informal caregivers of older adults. The first aim is to delineate distinct joint trajectories of burden and benefits of caregiving among informal caregivers of older adults with functional limitations and determine the proportion of caregivers following each joint trajectory. Since previous studies show three or four distinct joint trajectories of burden and benefits and most caregivers follow a stable (i.e., no change over time) joint trajectory9–11, we hypothesize that there are at least two distinct joint trajectories (H1) and the largest proportion of informal caregivers have a stable joint trajectory (H2). The second aim is to identify if having care-related support predicts the delineated joint trajectories. Based on previous literature9, we hypothesize that informal caregivers with care-related support from an experienced/trained live-in home help worker or from HCBS at baseline (compared to those without) follow joint trajectories indicating persistently low or decreasing burden and persistently high or increasing benefits (H3). The third aim is to compare caregiver well-being (assessed in terms of depressive symptoms, and quality of life [QoL] - physical, psychological, social, and environmental) of informal caregivers, at the end of the trajectory period, across the distinct joint trajectories. We hypothesize that informal caregivers with joint trajectories indicating persistently high or increasing burden and persistently low or decreasing benefits have more depressive symptoms and poorer QoL, in all domains, than those with persistently low or decreasing burden and persistently high or increasing benefits (H4).

Methods

Study population

Longitudinal data from the Caregiving Transitions among Family Caregivers of Elderly Singaporeans (TraCE) study was analyzed. In the study, care recipients were defined as older adults who received assistance for any ADL/IADL, while informal caregivers were defined as care recipients’ family members, aged ≥ 21 years, involved in at least two of the following activities for care recipients: (a) providing direct care, (b) ensuring care provision, and (c) making decisions on treatment or care services. Its detailed methodology is presented elsewhere22. Briefly, 2,028 Singapore citizens or permanent residents aged ≥ 75 years, who had participated in two nationally representative surveys, were approached for administration of a screener assessing their functional limitation status. This age group was chosen as they have substantially higher prevalence of functional limitations than those aged 60–74 years23,24, and thus likely to be care recipients. A total of 278 older adults and their informal caregivers participated in the TraCE study after informed consent (Fig. 1). Baseline interviews were conducted between April 2019 and May 2020. The first, second, and third follow-up interviews were conducted between 6 and 10, 15–20, and 21–29 months after baseline, respectively.

Flowchart of study sample.

Ethics declaration

The TraCE study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the National University of Singapore (LS-18–387 C). All methods performed in this study were in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants to confirm their voluntary participation before the baseline interview. All participants provided informed consent for publishing their data. Data analyses were conducted based on de-identified data from all participants. Sensitive information on participants was anonymized.

Measures

Burden and benefits of caregiving

Burden of caregiving was assessed at each interview by three negative domains of the modified Caregiver Reaction Assessment (validated in Singapore) - disrupted schedule and health problems (8 items, e.g., “Since caring for the care recipient, it seems like I’m tired all of the time”), financial strain (2 items, e.g., “Caring for the care recipient puts a financial strain on me”), and lack of family support (5 items, e.g., “Since caring for the care recipient, I feel my family has abandoned me”)25. Each item had a five-point response scale, ranging from “Strongly disagree” (score = 1) to “Strongly agree” (score = 5). Items of the three domains were averaged to generate a total burden score (nCRA score, range: 1–5; higher score reflects higher burden; Cronbach’s α values: 0.90–0.92 across all interviews).

Benefits of caregiving was examined at each interview by the short-Positive Aspects of Caregiving scale, also validated in Singapore26. It has two domains – self-affirmation (5 items, e.g., “Providing care to the care recipient has made me feel more useful”) and outlook on life (2 items, e.g., “Providing care to the care recipient has strengthened my relationships with others”). Each item has a five-point response scale, ranging from “Strongly disagree” (score = 1) to “Strongly agree” (score = 5). Items of the two domains were averaged to generate a total benefits score (PAC score, range: 1–5; higher score reflects higher benefits; α values: 0.85–0.91 across all interviews).

As there are no pre-defined cutoff points for the extent of burden and benefits of caregiving based on the nCRA and PAC scores, we simply divided the score range of 1 to 5 into five categories of equal range: 1-1.8 (Very Low), 1.81–2.6 (Low), 2.61–3.4 (Moderate), 3.41–4.2 (High), and 4.21-5 (Very High).

Care-related support sources

The following care-related support sources, adapted from the modified stress appraisal model16, relevant in the Singapore context, were included: (1) HCBS. Use of any home-based (e.g., meal delivery services) or community-based (e.g., day care center) services for care recipients in the past six months was recorded (yes/ no). (2) Migrant domestic worker (MDW). Employment of live-in home help workers, mostly women from neighboring low-income countries for helping in housework and caregiving, termed as MDW, is common in Singapore18. Based on care-related support from an MDW in the past month and the MDW’s caregiving history, a three-category variable was created: no MDW/ inexperienced and untrained MDW/ experienced or trained MDW (i.e., those with previous experience or formal training in providing care for an older person) (3) Relatives. Any relatives (e.g., sibling, adult child) of informal caregivers, providing care-related support in the past month, was recorded (yes/ no). (4) Caregiver training and preparedness. Attendance of any caregiver training in the past (yes/ no), and caregiver preparedness in caregiving were included. The latter was measured by an eight-item scale examining the perceived preparedness to cater for separate needs of care recipients (e.g., “How well prepared do you think you are to take care of the care recipient’s physical needs?”)27. Each item had four responses, ranging from “Not at all prepared” (score = 0) to “Very well prepared” (score = 3). All items were added to generate a total score (range: 0–24; α value: 0.89 at baseline interview), a higher score indicating better caregiver preparedness. The baseline value of these care-related support sources was used.

Covariates

Two other domains – background characteristics and primary stressors – of the modified stress appraisal model were used to select baseline covariates since factors under these domains were associated with both burden and benefits of caregiving13,16. Background characteristics included various caregiver socio-demographic factors, such as age, gender, ethnicity, education, working status, housing type, cohabitation and relation with care recipient, and perceived financial adequacy. Caregiver previous experiences with caregiving (i.e., caregiving duration, previous history of caregiving, and concurrently caring for another person), and care recipient characteristics, including age and gender, were also considered. Primary stressors included care recipient ADL/IADL limitations, impairments in memory, behavior and mood, and chronic health conditions. Detailed information on all covariates is provided in Supplementary Text.

Caregiver well-being

Depressive symptoms were measured at each interview by the 11-item version of the Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression (CES-D) scale28, a higher score indicating greater depressive symptoms (range: 0–22; α values: 0.83–0.86 across all interviews). QoL was measured at each interview by the 26-item World Health Organization Quality of Life scale (WHOQOL-BREF)29, evaluating four domains of QoL: physical (7 items; α values: 0.74–0.79 across all interviews), psychological (6 items; α values: 0.76–0.82 across all interviews), social (3 items; α values: 0.63–0.78 across all interviews), and environmental (8 items; α values: 0.74–0.81 across all interviews). The score of each domain was on a 0–100 scale, with a higher score indicating a better QoL.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics – mean and standard deviation for continuous or discrete variables, and number with proportion (relative to overall sample) for categorical variables – of care-related support sources and covariates were presented.

To test H1 and H2, Group-Based Multi-Trajectory Modelling was applied to identify distinct joint trajectories of burden and benefits of caregiving over four time-points: baseline (T1) and three follow-up interviews (T2, T3 and T4). It is an extension of Group-Based Trajectory Modelling, allowing for the pattern of two or more measures to be assessed jointly over time; it uses maximum likelihood estimation to estimate joint trajectories and the posterior probability of individuals following each joint trajectory; individuals are categorized under the joint trajectory with the highest posterior probability30. Maximum likelihood estimation has been shown to perform better than multiple imputation for missing data in longitudinal studies employing latent growth models31. The size of our analytical sample was comparable to previous studies, with 200–300 participants, that have employed Group-Based Multi-Trajectory Modelling9,32.

A two-stage approach was applied to identify the best-fitting Group-Based Multi-Trajectory Model (GBMTM). First, starting with one joint trajectory, six GBMTMs, each adding an additional joint trajectory, were developed. The one joint trajectory model assumed that all participants followed the same joint trajectory. Additional joint trajectories, in the two- to six-joint-trajectory GBMTMs, assumed distinct participant sub-groups that followed two to six joint trajectories respectively. The analyses resulted in one to six distinct joint trajectories of burden and benefits of caregiving, with the optimal shape for each joint trajectory. The shape of each joint trajectory, within each GBMTM, was tested sequentially from cubic (two inflection points) to intercept (no change over time). The optimal shape was the one with statistically significant intercept and slope values. Second, the best-fitting GBMTM was chosen from the six models, using the following criteria: (1) minimum % of caregivers in each joint trajectory > 5%, (2) average posterior probability of caregivers in each joint trajectory > 0.7, (3) Odds of Correct Classification > 5; (4) Entropy closest to 1, and (5) Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) closest to 033. The application of multiple criteria in addition to average posterior probability was suggested in previous literature to avoid identifying spurious trajectories34.

After identifying the best-fitting GBMTM, each delineated joint trajectory was named given the extent of burden and benefits of caregiving over time, based on the cutoff points aforementioned. Cohen’s d (0.2: small; 0.5: moderate; 0.8: large) was used to assess the magnitude of the difference in nCRA and PAC scores, at baseline and all follow-ups, between caregivers with distinct joint trajectories of burden and benefits35. This helped further evaluate if each joint trajectory with a different extent of burden or benefits of caregiving was categorized and named meaningfully.

Three sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the best-fitting GBMTM among caregivers with different patterns of missing data on nCRA and PAC scores: (1) only caregivers with complete data on nCRA and PAC scores at all four time points; (2) only caregivers with data on nCRA and PAC scores at two or more time points; and (3) only including caregivers with missing data but not due to care recipient’s death. A fourth sensitivity analysis was conducted by including the covariates associated with missing data on nCRA and PAC scores to evaluate the improvement of model performance via the inclusion of these covariates.

To test H3, multivariable multinomial logistic regression was applied to examine the association of care-related support sources and covariates at baseline with joint trajectories of burden and benefits of caregiving identified in the best-fitting GBMTM. All care-related support sources and covariates were included in the regression model. Relative risk ratios (RRRs) and their 95% confidence interval (95%CI) were reported. Listwise deletion was applied by only including participants with complete data (thus, excluding six participants with missing values on caregiving duration). In sensitivity analysis, we used multiple imputation with predictive mean matching to impute these missing values and rerun the model. First, we generated 20 imputed datasets to reduce bias relative to a single imputation36. Then, the multivariable multinomial logistic regression model was run in each imputed dataset, and the resulting coefficients and standard errors were pooled across the imputed datasets according to Rubin’s combination rules37.

To test H4, multivariable linear regression was used to test the associations of the joint trajectories of burden and benefits with caregiver depressive symptoms and each QoL domain, at the last follow-up, among caregivers who completed all interviews (n = 145). All care-related support sources and covariates were included in the regression models to generate results that were comparable across the different caregiver well-being measures. Beta-coefficients and their 95% CI were reported. Listwise deletion was applied to handle missing data due to loss to follow-up and missing values in the well-being measures or covariates. To evaluate the robustness of the analysis for H4, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by using inverse probability weighting to account for the exclusion of participants with missing data. First, for each well-being outcome measure, a logistic regression model, including baseline values of the care-related support sources and covariates, was applied to estimate the predicted probability of having complete data. Then, the weight for each participant was calculated as the inverse of the estimated predicted probability. The inverse probability weight was calculated separately for each well-being outcome measure since the number of participants with complete data differed by measure (i.e., 137 for depressive symptoms, 141 for physical quality of life, 134 for psychological quality of life, 126 for social quality of life, and 140 for environmental quality of life). Finally, the multivariable linear regression models were run accounting for the inverse probability weights.

Furthermore, to determine if the difference in depressive symptoms and each QoL domain scores between the distinct joint trajectories of burden and benefits was meaningful, a threshold of half the standard deviation of these scores at the last follow-up was used as the minimal important difference for the corresponding score; the standard deviation of depressive symptoms and each QoL domain score at the last follow-up was calculated among 145 informal caregivers completing all four time points. The use of this threshold followed the recommendation of a systematic review determining the minimal important difference across different health-related quality of life measures38. A difference between the distinct joint trajectories in depressive symptoms and each QoL domain score above this threshold was considered as meaningful. Stata 17 was used for all statistical analyses, and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Joint trajectories of burden and benefits of caregiving

Supporting H1, the best-fitting GBMTM identified four distinct joint trajectories of burden and benefits of caregiving (Fig. 2). The four joint trajectories were: (1) persistently low burden and high benefits (joint trajectory with the lowest burden; 122 caregivers [44.5%]), (2) persistently moderate burden and very high benefits (joint trajectory with the highest benefits; 63 caregivers [23.0%]), (3) persistently moderate burden and high benefits (52 caregivers [19.0%]), and (4) persistently high burden and high benefits (37 caregivers [13.5%]). Through the model selection process, GBMTMs identifying four or five joint trajectories of burden and benefits of caregiving had a similar performance (Supplementary Table 1). The model with four distinct joint trajectories was selected for parsimony. All sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the best-fitting GBMTM (Supplementary Table 2). Cohen’s d values suggested that the magnitude of burden between trajectories indicating low, moderate, or high burden and the magnitude of benefits between trajectories indicating high or very high benefits was substantially distinct at baseline as well as all follow-ups (Supplementary Table 3).

Care-related support associated with the delineated joint trajectories

None of the delineated joint trajectories could be considered as the most favorable (i.e., with the lowest burden and the highest benefits concurrently). Thus, to identify the association of care-related support sources with the delineated joint trajectories, two sets of analyses were conducted: (1) one considered caregivers with persistently low burden and high benefits (i.e., those with lowest burden) as the reference group, and (2) the other considered caregivers with persistently moderate burden and very high benefits (i.e., those with highest benefits) as the reference group.

Supporting H3, informal caregivers with care-related support from an experienced/trained MDW at baseline (versus those without) were less likely to follow joint trajectories indicating persistently moderate burden and high benefits (Relative Risk Ratio [RRR] = 0.31, 95% Confidence Interval [95%CI] = 0.11 to 0.90), persistently moderate burden and very high benefits (RRR [95%CI] = 0.32 [0.11 to 0.89]), or persistently high burden and high benefits (RRR [95%CI] = 0.15 [0.03 to 0.64]; Table 2), compared to those with persistently low burden and high benefits. Furthermore, compared to caregivers with persistently moderate burden and very high benefits, those perceiving more preparedness in caregiving at baseline were less likely to follow a joint trajectory indicating persistently moderate burden and high benefits (RRR [95%CI] = 0.87 [0.79 to 0.96]; Table 3). Results of the regression models run in the multiple imputed data supported the robustness of the main analyses (using listwise deletion to handle missing data) for H3 (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5).

Discussion

This longitudinal study revealed three sets of key findings. First, the delineation of four distinct joint trajectories pointed to the heterogeneity in joint trajectories of burden and benefits of caregiving. Second, the investigation into the association of various care-related support sources at baseline with the delineated joint trajectories identified two potentially beneficial sources that were associated with persistently lower burden or persistently higher benefits – support from an experienced/trained MDW and better preparedness in caregiving. Third, caregivers with persistently higher burden, irrespective of the extent of benefits, had poorer well-being at the last follow-up.

Aligned with H1, heterogeneity in the number of joint trajectories of burden and benefits, was primarily driven by the extent of burden over time, as all delineated joint trajectories indicated persistently high or more benefits. For caregivers with persistently low burden, the reason why they experience concurrently high benefits may be due to their less demanding caregiving role. Our results show that caregivers with persistently low burden, versus those with moderate or high burden, were more likely to be caring for older adults with less impairments in ADL/IADL, memory, behavior, and mood at baseline (Supplementary Table 8). Less care needs of care recipients may be easier to manage, which may result in caregivers gaining a stronger sense of self-affirmation from caregiving. However, caregivers with higher burden (moderate or high burden vs. low burden) over time may gain less benefits because the care needs of their care recipients may be more challenging. The reason for these caregivers still having persistently high or more benefits may be due to the ability to reappraise caregiving role as meaningful. In Singapore, where cultural norms emphasize filial piety20, adult child caregivers (the most common caregivers [73.4%] in our study) may consider caregiving as a reciprocation to their parents20. Under the reciprocity of filial piety, adult child caregivers may consider caregiving role as important and meaningful even when their parents have more care needs. Thus, caregivers experiencing persistently moderate or high burden may reappraise a demanding caregiving role as meaningful and beneficial. Further research, especially qualitative studies, is needed to help better understand how caregivers gain benefits from a burdensome caregiving role.

Heterogeneity in joint trajectories of burden and benefits was also observed for the pattern of co-progression over time - while a large proportion (86.5%) of caregivers had stable joint trajectories (supporting H2), the remaining had a curvilinear joint trajectory. The pervasiveness of stability in joint trajectories may be due to the already long caregiving duration at baseline (8.5–11 years on average; Supplementary Table 8). After an extended duration of caregiving at baseline, caregivers’ ability to manage care needs from themselves and care recipients and find meaning in caregiving may not fluctuate during the follow-up period of the current study. While caregivers with the curvilinear persistently high burden and high benefits trajectory also had a long caregiving duration (12 years on average; Supplementary Table 8), they experienced increasing burden and declining benefits from T1 to T2. This may be attributed to restrictions during COVID-19 pandemic in Singapore. Caregiver interviews at T1 and T2 were mostly conducted before and after, respectively, a strict COVID-19 related lockdown39, when HCBS access was restricted40. Caregivers with persistently high burden and high benefits had the highest proportion utilizing HCBS for care recipients and faced the most care recipients’ care needs at baseline (Supplementary Table 8). Thus, an increase in their caregiving responsibilities during the COVID-19 pandemic, coupled with the most care recipient care needs at baseline across caregivers in the various joint trajectories, may explain the observed pattern.

Consistent with H3, compared to caregivers following the joint trajectory indicating persistently low burden and high benefits (i.e., the one with the lowest burden), caregivers with care-related support from an experienced/trained MDW at baseline (versus those without) were less likely to follow any joint trajectory indicating persistently greater burden (i.e., those indicating persistently moderate burden and high benefits, persistently moderate burden and very high benefits, and persistently high burden and high benefits). This indicates the utility of sharing of caregiving duties by informal caregivers with home help workers who have relevant experience or training in eldercare. Additionally, among caregivers with persistently moderate burden, those being more prepared in caregiving at baseline were more likely to experience persistently higher benefits. This may be because caregivers more prepared in caregiving are more optimistic and are able to develop positive attitudes towards caregiving6. Thus, more prepared caregivers may have stronger positive reappraisal skills to gain benefits from a demanding caregiving role than those less prepared.

Aligning with H4, informal caregivers perceiving persistently high benefits but higher burden had greater depressive symptoms and poorer QoL at the last follow-up, compared to those with persistently low burden and high benefits. These findings are consistent with previous evidence that poorer well-being is observed from caregivers experiencing a more burdensome caregiving role13. Furthermore, caregivers with persistently moderate burden and very high benefits had poorer physical QoL but similar extent of depressive symptoms and psychological QoL versus those with persistently low burden and high benefits. The measure for burden in our study captured physical, psychological, social, and financial domains25, whereas the assessed benefits were largely psychosocial in nature26,41. Thus, the detrimental effect of persistently moderate burden on caregiver psychological and social well-being may be buffered by the concurrent persistently very high benefits.

Implications

Our study findings have several practical implications. First, the stable co-progression of burden and benefits over time for most informal caregivers suggests that repeated assessments may not be required to evaluate the extent of burden and benefits among informal caregivers of older adults, at least among those who have been providing care for a relatively long duration. This may be useful information for health and social care agencies and workers caring for informal caregivers, saving financial and human resources. Second, the finding that caregivers with support from an experienced/trained MDW at baseline have lower extent of burden over time indicates the importance of a shared caregiving role between informal caregivers and home help workers experienced or trained in eldercare. Interventions facilitating the employment of experienced/trained live-in home help workers as well as training of such workers may reduce burden among informal caregivers of older adults with functional limitations. Provision of support to older adults by live-in home help workers is common in many Asian and non-Asian countries beyond Singapore, such as Indonesia, Malaysia, South Korea, Thailand, Germany, and Canada42–44. This finding, supporting the advantage of experienced/trained live-in home help workers, is thus relevant for settings beyond Singapore. Third, as caregivers being more prepared in caregiving have persistently higher benefits, there is value in enhancing and re-enforcing caregiver preparedness. This could be achieved through caregiver training that equip caregivers with knowledge or skills, such as stress management or nursing skills, which have been shown to enhance caregiver preparedness45. Finally, caregivers with persistently higher burden may still be at risk of poorer physical (i.e., less physical QoL), psychological (i.e., more depressive symptoms and poorer psychological QoL), and social (i.e., less social and environmental QoL) well-being, regardless of their persistently high benefits. Interventions that combine supporting care recipients’ ADLs/IADLs, managing caregiver psychological distress, and enhancing caregivers’ social participation may be needed to enhance the holistic well-being of informal caregivers with persistently higher burden.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study are the use of longitudinal data with repeated measures at four time-points, of scales of burden and benefits of caregiving validated in the Singapore context, of GBMTM to examine joint trajectories of burden and benefits of caregiving, and of multiple fit statistics for best-fitting GBMTM selection. However, this study has its limitations. First, there is no anchored starting point of joint trajectories of burden and benefits of caregiving as the TraCE study enrolled existing caregivers. An anchored starting point, say after initiation into caregiving, may facilitate better interpretation of the joint trajectories and capture the variation in burden and benefits in early stages of caregiving. Studying the joint trajectories in the early stages of caregiving will also help understand if stable trajectories are also pervasive then, or other patterns, showing change, are more common. Second, the temporal variation in care recipients’ conditions and care needs may affect the joint trajectories of burden and benefits of caregiving. However, we were unable to include them (or other time-varying variables) in the group-based multi-trajectory model due to issues with model convergence. We therefore included only their baseline values, as time-invariant covariates, in our analysis (Supplementary Tables 9 and 10). There is still value in considering baseline values of care recipient conditions and care needs in the analysis, since this provides information on how the ‘current’ status of the variables is associated with future joint trajectories of burden and benefits of caregiving. Third, HCBS use was considered as dichotomous variable due to sample size considerations, while the frequency and duration of HCBS use, and the quality of HCBS may also affect joint trajectories of burden and benefits of caregiving. Future research may consider including more detailed information on HCBS use by care recipients and caregivers. Fourth, the variable of care-related support from relatives was also dichotomized due to sample size considerations, precluding the understanding on how the number of relatives offering support, the relationship between relatives and informal caregivers, or time spent in caregiving by relatives, affect the joint trajectories of burden and benefits of caregiving. Future research should consider studying these aspects.

Conclusion

Overall, this study highlights the heterogeneity in the joint trajectories of burden and benefits of caregiving among informal caregivers of older adults with functional limitations and its practical implications. Support from an experienced/trained live-in home help worker was associated with persistently lower burden, indicating the importance of sharing caregiving responsibility among informal caregivers with experienced or trained formal caregivers. Furthermore, combining the evidence that caregivers with support from an experienced/trained live-in home help worker had persistently lower burden and those being more prepared in caregiving experienced persistently higher benefits, service providers may together consider training these workers and informal caregivers to enable informal caregivers to reduce burden and enhance benefits.
