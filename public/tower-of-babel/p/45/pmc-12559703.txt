Proximity-induced nodal metal in an extremely underdoped CuO2 plane in triple-layer cuprates
Authors: Shin-ichiro Ideta, Shintaro Adachi, Takashi Noji, Shunpei Yamaguchi, Nae Sasaki, Shigeyuki Ishida, Shin-ichi Uchida, Takenori Fujii, Takao Watanabe, Wen O. Wang, Brian Moritz, Thomas P. Devereaux, Masashi Arita, Chung-Yu Mou, Teppei Yoshida, Kiyohisa Tanaka, Ting-Kuo Lee, Atsushi Fujimori
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We performed angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy studies on the triple-layer Bi2Sr2Ca2Cu3O10+δ over a wide doping range. Although the doping level of the inner CuO2 plane is extremely low in underdoped samples, the d-wave SC gap is enhanced to the unprecedentedly large value of Δ0 ~ 80–100 meV at the antinode. This gap persists well above Tc without a Fermi arc, indicating a “nodal metal”. We attribute the nodal metallic behavior to the unique local environment of the inner clean CuO2 plane, sandwiched by nearly optimally-doped two outer planes and hence subject to strong proximity effect from both sides. In the nodal metal, quasiparticle peaks show electron-hole symmetry, suggesting d-wave pairing fluctuations. Thus the proximity effect on the innermost CuO2 plane is the strongest in the triple-layer cuprates, which explains why the Tc reaches the maximum at the layer number of three in every multi-layer cuprate family.

Introduction

How the superconductivity emerges in the cuprate superconductors through carrier doping into the Mott insulator and what the microscopic origin of the pseudogap have been outstanding issues in condensed matter physics. In single- and double-layer cuprates, antiferromagnetism disappears and superconductivity appears at the hole concentration of ~ 0.051. Above the superconducting (SC) critical temperature (Tc), the d-wave SC gap closes near the node while the gap remains open as a “pseudogap” in the antinodal region up to the pseudogap temperature T* (>Tc)[Fig. 1(a)]2,3. Thus an arc-like Fermi surface, called Fermi arc, is formed in the nodal region in the temperature range Tc < T < T*4 (Fig. 1(a)). For the single-layer cuprates La1−xSrxCuO4 (LSCO), Bi2Sr2CuO6+δ (Bi2201), and the double-layer cuprates Bi2Sr2CaCu2O8+δ (Bi2212), the magnitude of the SC gap Δ0 extrapolated from the node to the antinode, the magnitude of the pseudogap Δ* in the antinodal region, and the temperature T* below which the pseudogap opens vary as functions of the hole concentration, as shown in Fig. 1(b), 1(c). Δ* and T* are similar between the single- and double-layer cuprates while the SC gap Δ0 and Tc are very different between them3. The origin of the pseudogap has been extensively studied both experimentally and theoretically, but still under debate as to whether it is a precursor to the superconductivity, namely, pairing fluctuations5–11, an ordered phase that competes with superconductivity12–20, or phenomena arising from strong electron correlations such as electron fractionalization21–24.Fig. 1Electronic structure in the superconducting (SC) and pseudogap (PG) states for single- and double-layer cuprate superconductors.a Electronic states on the Fermi surface. In the triple-layer cuprate Bi2Sr2Ca2Cu3O10+δ (Bi2223), the carrier concentration difference between the outer CuO2 plane (OP) and inner CuO2 planes (IP) is large compared to the other multi-layer cuprates families25–28. Indeed, we observed that, in the IP of the underdoped (UD) Bi2223, the doped hole concentration was extremely small according to ARPES measurements combined with Luttinger sum-rule analysis and that the observed SC gap was unprecedentedly large Δ0 ~ 80–100 meV. This d-wave-like gap did not collapse up to Tpair ≳ 100 K (≫Tc ~ 65–80 K) and the system behaves as a “nodal metal" with a point node. The superconductivity with the large d-wave gap and the nodal-metallic pseudogap are likely due to the proximity effect from the adjacent OPs with higher carrier and superfluid densities. In the temperature range Tc < T < Tpair, electron-hole symmetry is observed in the ARPES spectra near the Fermi level (EF) close to the node, indicating that the nodal metal is a precursor of the d-wave SC state. Above Tpair, the node finally collapsed to a Fermi arc and became a conventional pseudogap state.

So far, there have been several reports on different kinds of “nodal metals" in the cuprate superconductors. Kanigel et al.29 have extrapolated the lengths of the Fermi arcs of various cuprates as a function of T/T* to T = 0, and concluded that the ground state of the pseudogap state has a Fermi arc of zero length, that is, the ground state is a nodal metal with a point node, but they have not observed the actual point node directly. Chaterjee et al.30 observed a d-wave-like pseudogap with a point node in a lightly-doped non-SC cuprate. The present finding of the “nodal metal" above the Tc of the SC samples is qualitatively similar to the persistent point node observed by high-resolution laser ARPES studies of the optimally-doped (OPT) and overdoped (OD) double-layer Bi2Sr2CaCu2O8+δ (Bi2212) by Kondo et al.31. However, they have concluded that the point node persists up to ~20–40 K above Tc from the analysis of life-time broadening, and the cobservation of the point node in their raw data has been limited only to ~5–10 K above Tc. The present observation of a robust d-wave SC-like “nodal metal" in the inner clean CuO2 plane is contrasted with the observation of hole pockets of the antiferromagnetic (AFM) origin in the inner CuO2 planes of 5- to 6-layer cuprates32,33, where superconductivity is weakened compared to the outer CuO2 planes and the CuO2 planes of the singe- and double-layer cuprates. The present results should lead us to elucidate the electronic states of the triple-layer high-Tc cuprates that bring the highest Tc in every multi-layer cuprate family. Particularly, the observed very large Δ0 ~ 80–100 meV may give us a key for room temperature superconductors.

In order to see the temperature range where the point node of IP persists above Tc, we measured the ARPES spectra of UD75 over a wide temperature range as shown in Fig. 4(a). The figure shows EDC’s on the Fermi surface (k = kF) of the UD75 sample from T = 12 K to 300 K. Below Tc, the gap closes only at ϕ = 0°, confirming the d-wave SC gap. The point node persists up to T ≳ 100 K, that is, Tpair is in the range of 100–150 K, that is Tpair ~ 1.5Tc. At T = 200 K, the point node finally collapsed and a short Fermi arc appeared (Supplementary Fig. S5). At T = 300 K, a well-defined QP peak at EF was no longer observed. However, the QP peak was recovered after cooling down to 12 K, indicating that the disappearance of the QP peak at 300 K is not due to the surface degradation or contamination but an intrinsic property of the underdoped IP (Supplementary Fig. S7). These results suggest that d-wave pairing fluctuations or incoherent d-wave pairs exist up to temperatures as high as ~1.5Tc, that is, Tpair ~ 1.5Tc despite the extremely low carrier concentration of the UD Bi2223. The large pseudogap with the point node in the underdoped Bi2223 may suppress the inter-layer tunneling of the QP between the planes40. This explains the extremely strong anisotropy of the QP mass between the a-b plane and the c axis in underdoped Bi222341.

In order to see whether the slightly-doped single CuO2 plane has a tendency to form a nodal metal or not, we have calculated the spectral function A(k, ω) of the Hubbard model using the Determinant Quantum Monte Carlo method44–47 (for details, see Supplementary Fig. S9). The magnitude of the pseudogap in the antinodal region is about 90 meV at the lowest hole doping of 5%, which is in good agreement with the ARPES experimental results. However, we could not reproduce the nodal metallic behavior, and invariably obtained the formation of Fermi arcs around the (π, π) direction. This is partly because the spectral width obtained in the calculations remains large in the nodal region, partly because of the high simulation temperatures, but also because a certain amount of hole doping is necessary to induce superconductivity in the Hubbard model48.

Discussion

Δ0, Tpair, and Δ* for the OP and IP bands are plotted as a function of hole concentrations in Fig. 5(a). The figure shows that Δ0, which is constant in the underdoped region in the single- and double-layer systems3,39, increases with decreasing doping in the triple-layer system. This may be related to the fact that the nodal metal in the triple-layer system is stable compared to the single- and double-layer systems. Besides, the unexpectedly large Δ0 ~ 80–100 meV is a unique feature of the underdoped clean IP in the triple-layer cuprates that leads to the high Tpair. As shown in Fig. 5(a), we found three segmented temperature regions for the IP of the triple-layer cuprates: i) In the SC state (T < Tc), the energy gap shows a typical d-wave-like gap with the point node; ii) Between Tc < T < Tpair, a pseudogap with a point node (nodal metal) opens due to incoherent pairing (strong SC fluctuations); iii) At T > Tpair, the energy gap shows a Fermi arc around the node and the pseudogap opens. As for the energy gap of OP, whose hole concentration was above 0.1, a d-wave-like gap was observed as IP below Tc, but the Fermi arc appeared slight above Tc. The greater stability of the nodal metal in the IP of the UD Bi2223 samples (represented by the much larger temperature range Tc < T < Tpair) than in the single- and double-layer cuprates of similar doping levels may also be due to the proximity of the two OPs with high superfluid densities sandwiching the IP. The proximity effect induces not only the superconductivity in the IP below Tc but also the nodal metallic state in the wide temperature range of Tc < T < Tpair ~ 1.5Tc. It should be noted that, in addition to the reduced disorder as in the case of the IP’s of 5- and 6-layer cuprates, the local environment of the IP in the triple-layer UD Bi2223 samples is unique in that it is sandwiched by the nearly optimally-doped OP on both sides. The Cooper pairs from both sides may enhance superconductivity in the IP due to the proximity effects, i.e., pair-tunneling effect. (Single-particle tunneling can also enhance Tc, but an unrealistically large value has to be assumed49,50.) On the other hand, the second-inner plane (IP1) of the 5- and 6-layer cuprates is sandwiched by optimally-doped OP and heavily-underdoped innermost plane (IP0), causing the weaker proximity effect than in Bi2223 as illustrated in Fig. 5(b)32,33. We consider that these different environments of the inner CuO2 planes leads to their strikingly different behaviors in spite of the similarly extremely underdoped situations, namely, the hole pockets resulting from the AFM order in the 5- and 6-layer cuprates against the nodal metallic behavior in the present triple-layer Bi2223, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 5(b). Once the SC state is stabilized in the IP of Bi2223, the clean environment of the IP relatively free from disorder will enhance SC fluctuations above Tc and lead to Tpair as high as ~1.5Tc. Note that a pseudogap has also been observed up to ~1.5Tc in another clean system, the Fermi gas of Li atoms51. Recently, the layer-number (n) dependence of the p-d charge-transfer gap Δ of the OP in the Bi-based multilayer cuprate series were measured by EELS and was correlated with the n dependence of Tc52. However, the magnitude of the d-wave gap Δ0 of OP is the same between n = 2 and n = 3 (Figs. 1 and 5), suggesting that the role of IP and/or that of interaction between IP and OP is necessary to explain the highest Tc at n = 3.

According to the calculation of the CuO2 plane using the extended t-t’-t"-J model53,54, the energies of two possible ground states in the heavily lightly-doped region, that is, the AFM and SC states are nearly degenerate, and are sensitively controlled by the parameter t’ and t" values. It has also been shown that the phase transition between the AFM and SC states is of the first order54. Therefore, the AFM and SC phases might be easily switched by an additional parameter. The present experimental results suggest that the SC proximity effect tends to suppress the hole pocket, which arises from AFM order, and to favor the SC state in the triple-layer cuprate, while in the 5- and 6-layer cuprates, the relatively weak proximity effect leaves the electronic states in the innermost CuO2 planes in the AFM ordered phase competing or coexisting with the SC state. This may explain why the triple-layer cuprates show the highest Tc in each multi-layer cuprate family.

Methods

High-quality single crystals of Bi2Sr2Ca2Cu3O10+δ were grown by the TSFZ method55. Underdoped Bi2223 samples were successfully obtained in the two-step annealing to control their doping levels56. Optimally doped and overdoped Bi2223 samples were annealed in oxygen flow and under high oxygen pressure, respectively55,57. Superconducting transition temperature (Tc) was determined by SQUID measurement and shows ~64, ~70, ~75, ~80, ~110, and ~110 K. They are denoted by UD64, UD70, UD75, UD80, OPT110, and OD110, respectively.

The electronic structure around EF was studied by bulk-sensitive ARPES using low-energy photons. ARPES measurements were carried out at BL7U of UVSOR-III Synchrotron using a MBS A1 electron analyzer and tunable linearly polarized light of hν = 7–18.5 eV photons, and at HiSOR BL-9A using a SPECS ASTRAIOS 190 electron analyzer and linearly polarized light of hν = 7.2 eV and 18 eV. Clean sample surfaces were obtained for the ARPES measurements by cleaving single crystals in − situ in an ultrahigh vacuum better than 6 × 10−9 Pa. The total energy resolution was at ~5–7 meV. The Fermi level (EF) of the sample is a reference to that of evaporated gold in electrical contact with the sample. The measurements were performed at well below Tc (~10 K) in the SC state and above Tc (the detailed temperature is shown in the text) in the normal state.
