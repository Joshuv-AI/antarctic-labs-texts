Cannabidiol enhances tamoxifen efficacy via CREB-mediated suppression of ERα signaling in ER-positive breast cancer cells
Authors: Bu Gyeom Kim, Okhyeon Kim, Do-Yeon Lee, Bo Ram Kim, Dae Yeong Kim, Yoon Namgung, Jun Woo Bong, Sanghee Kang, Sun Il Lee, Sang Cheul Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEstrogen receptor alpha (ERα) plays a crucial role in the proliferation and survival of ER-positive breast cancer cells, and tamoxifen (TAM) remains the mainstay of endocrine therapy. However, acquired resistance to TAM remains a major clinical challenge. Despite advances in molecular profiling and targeted therapies, the mechanisms underlying TAM resistance remain incompletely understood. Emerging evidence suggest that dysregulation of intracellular signaling pathways, including the cAMP/CREB axis, may contribute to endocrine therapy failure.MethodsWe investigated the effects of cannabidiol (CBD) on TAM sensitivity and elucidated its underlying mechanisms in ER-positive breast cancer models. Human breast cancer cell lines (MCF7 and T47D) were treated with CBD, TAM or their combination. Apoptosis, proliferation, and protein expression were evaluated by flow cytometry, western blotting, and immunofluorescence. In vivo efficacy was examined in xenograft models.ResultsCBD significantly enhanced TAM-induced cell death and apoptosis in ER-positive breast cancer cell. Mechanistically, CBD suppressed the cAMP/CREB signaling pathway, leading to downregulation of ERα and its target genes (TFF1, GREB1, CCND1). Co-immunoprecipitation revealed that CBD inhibited the interaction between phosphorylated CREB and CBP, resulting in transcriptional suppression of ERα. In vivo, combined treatment with CBD and TAM synergistically inhibited tumor growth and reduced ERα and p-CREB expression levels in tumor tissues.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that CBD restores TAM sensitivity through CREB-mediated downregulation of ERα signaling in ER-positive breast cancer. This study suggests the potential application of CBD as a novel adjuvant agent to overcome TAM resistance and improve therapeutic outcomes in patients with ER-positive breast cancer.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-026-01588-x.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) remains the most frequently diagnosed malignancy worldwide and constitutes the second leading cause of cancer mortality among women [1]. Based on the molecular expression profiles of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), breast cancers are categorized into four subtypes: Luminal A, Luminal B, HER2-enriched, and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) [2, 3]. Approximately 70% of all breast tumors display ER positivity, underscoring the pivotal contribution of estrogen signaling to tumorigenesis.

ER signaling functions as a central oncogenic driver in breast cancer, regulating the transcription of multiple downstream targets such as cyclin D1, MYC, Bcl-2. And vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which play significant roles in proliferation, cell cycle, cell survival, and angiogenesis [4]. For most of the ER-positive breast cancer patients, endocrine therapy (ET) is the first choice for treatment. ET for BC currently consists of (1) selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs); (2) selective estrogen receptor down-regulators (SERDs); and (3) aromatase inhibitors (AIs). These therapeutic modalities act either by antagonizing ER activity (SERMs) or by suppressing estrogen synthesis (AIs), and are administered singly or in combination [5].

Tamoxifen (TAM), a SERM, competitively binds to the ER ligand-binding site, thereby blocking estrogen-mediated transcriptional activity in tumor cells. Its clinical benefit in overall survival among ER-positive patients has been well established through large-scale randomized trials [6, 7]. Nevertheless, individual patient responses can vary substantially, with some experiencing durable remissions while others demonstrate primary or acquired resistance. Identifying the clinicopathologic and molecular factors underlying tamoxifen responsiveness remains an area of active investigation.

Despite advances in multimodal treatment strategies such as surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy, tamoxifen-based endocrine therapy continues to represent the gold standard for preventing recurrence in ER-positive breast cancer. However, resistance to tamoxifen remains a major clinical challenge limiting its long-term effectiveness [8]. Despite the widespread clinical use of tamoxifen in ER-positive breast cancer, therapeutic resistance remains a significant clinical problem. Clinical evidence indicates that approximately 30–40% of patients with early-stage ER-positive breast cancer eventually relapse while receiving tamoxifen therapy, reflecting intrinsic or early acquired resistance to endocrine treatment. In more advanced disease settings, de novo resistance to tamoxifen has been reported in up to ~ 50% of cases, and acquired resistance develops in approximately 40–50% of patients who initially respond to tamoxifen, ultimately limiting long-term treatment efficacy [9–11]. Collectively, these findings underscore the high prevalence of tamoxifen resistance across disease stages and highlight the urgent need for strategies that can restore or enhance endocrine sensitivity in ER-positive breast cancer.

Multiple molecular mechanisms have been implicated in tamoxifen resistance, including alterations in ERα expression or function, mutations in the ESR1 gene, and activation of alternative survival and proliferative signaling pathways. However, the precise regulatory networks that modulate ERα transcriptional activity and endocrine sensitivity remain incompletely understood, underscoring the need for novel therapeutic strategies that can restore or enhance tamoxifen responsiveness [12, 13].

Cannabidiol (CBD), a non-psychoactive phytocannabinoid derived from Cannabis sativa L., has recently attracted attention for its broad pharmacological benefits, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and cytoprotective properties [14]. Various studies have shown that CBD induces apoptosis in malignant cells through both receptor-dependent mechanisms via CB1, CB2, and TRPV1-and receptor-independent pathways [15, 16]. Furthermore, CBD modulates the interplay between apoptotic and autophagic signaling cascades in breast cancer models, resulting in non-receptor-mediated programmed cell death. Interestingly, CBD has also been found to enhance the perfusion of chemotherapeutic drugs across barriers through inhibiting the expression of breast cancer resistance protein in a concentration dependent manner [17].

In this study aims to investigate the potential synergistic effect of CBD with TAM in ER-positive BC. We evaluated the effects of CBD in combination with TAM on two human ER-positive breast cancer cell lines, MCF7 and T47D. following co-treatment, morphological changes were observed, accompanied by suppressed cell proliferation and enhanced apoptotic cell death. Furthermore, both the expression and nuclear localization of ERα as well as its downstream target genes, were markedly reduced. Collectively, these findings suggest that CBD may potentiate tamoxifen-induced apoptosis through the downregulation of the ERα signaling pathway in ER-positive breast cancer cells. While the individual anti-cancer effects of CBD and TAM have been extensively characterized in various tumor models, their combined effects on apoptosis and ERα regulation in ER-positive breast cancer has not yet been elucidated. This study suggests that co-administration of CBD and TAM exerts a synergistic effect by interfering the ERα-dependent signaling pathway in ER-positive breast cancer cells.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

The human breast cancer cell lines (MCF7 and T47D) and breast epithelial cell lines (MCF10A) were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). MCF7 and T47D cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin (P/S). MCF10A cells were cultured in mammary epithelial basal medium (MEBM; CC-3151, Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) containing supplements (CC-4176, Lonza) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Reagents and antibodies

CBD was obtained from Hammer Enterprises (Evergreen, CO, USA). CBD dissolved in absolute ethanol (EtOH) was stored at -20℃. Z-VAD-FMK was purchased from Promega Corporation (G723A, Madison, WI, USA). 17β-estradiol (E2) and Forskolin (FL) was purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Antibodies against cleaved PARP (#9541), cleaved caspase-3 (#9664), cleaved caspase-8 (#9496), cleaved caspase-9 (#9509), CREB (#9197), phospho-CREB (#9198), CBP (#7389) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). Antibodies against ERα (sc-7207), PLCγ1 (sc-7290), phospho-PLCγ1 (sc-136186), PKA (sc-390548), phospho-PKA (sc-293036), Lamin B1 (sc-20682), β-tubulin (sc-166729) were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, TX, USA). Antibody against β-actin (A5316) was purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Anti-rabbit IgG horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-linked antibody (#7074) was purchased from Cell Signaling Technology. Anti-mouse IgG HRP-linked antibody (#1706516) was purchased from Bio-Rad (Hercules, CA, USA).

Transient transfection

For transient transfection, cells were transfected with small interfering RNA (siRNA) against.

ERα (sc-29305, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) or CREB (s3497, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using Lipofectamine RNA iMAX (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was examined using the EZ-Cytox cell viability assay kit (DoGEN, Daeil Lab Service Co. Ltd., Seoul, South Korea). Cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 96-well plates and treated with CBD and tamoxifen at the indicated concentrations for the indicated time. After treatment, cells were incubated with the WST-1 reagent for 4 h at 37 °C, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (SPECTRA 190; Molecular Devices, Sunnydale, CA, USA). The combination index (CI) was also calculated to examine the synergistic effects between CBD and tamoxifen by Compusyn software (version 1.0, Inc., Paramus, NJ, USA). CI values 1, < 1, and > 1 indicated an additive effect, synergism, or antagonism, respectively.

Colony forming assay

Cells were seeded and treated with CBD and tamoxifen for 24 h, then detached by trypsinization and replaced in 6-well plates. After 2 weeks of incubation, colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.5% crystal violet solution. Colony images were captured using a light microscope. Then the number of colonies was scored using Image J software v1.54 (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Apoptosis analysis

Apoptotic cell death was estimated using the FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit I (556547, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were treated with chemical compounds at indicated concentrations for the indicated time. The cells were then harvested by trypsinization, and centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 5 min. The pellets were resuspended in 1x binding buffer containing Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) for 30 min at room temperature (RT) in the dark. Sample were analyzed using flow cytometry (BD LSR Fortessa-X20), and data were processed with FlowJo software v10.10 (BD Biosciences).

Quantitative real time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (TR-118, Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transcripts were amplified using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). qRT-PCR was performed on a QuantStudio 6 Flex System (Applied Biosystems). Amplification of transcripts were conducted using specific gene primer for TaqMan probes and mater mix reaction system (4369016, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The relative mRNA expression of target gene was calculated using 2−ΔΔCt method, which was normalized to the expression of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). TaqMan probes were as follows: GAPDH (Hs99999905_m1), ESR1 (Hs00174860_m1), CREB1 (Hs00231713_m1), TFF1 (Hs00907239_m1), GREB1 (Hs00231713_m1) and CCND1 (Hs00765553_m1).

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (R0278, Sigma Aldrich) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (P8340 and P5726, Sigma Aldrich). Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay (23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of whole-cell lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (GE HealthCare Life Sciences). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h at 4 °C, incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The membranes were probed with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody. Signals were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence protocol (ECL; DoGEN, Daeil Lab Service Co. Ltd.). Blots were normalized to β-actin as a loading control and quantified using Image J software.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

Cell lysate was incubated with lysis buffer containing 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) (36978, Sigma Aldrich), protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails on ice for 5 min. Cells were harvested and debris was removed by centrifugation. Protein concentration was determined by performing BCA assay. Supernatants were incubated with primary antibodies for overnight at 4 ℃ and Protein G PLUS-Agarose beads were added for 1 h at 4 °C. Immunoprecipitates were washed and separated by centrifugation and heated with 2x sample buffer. Supernatants were then assessed using Western blot analysis.

Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis

Cells were seeded above the cover slip and fixed with 3.7% formaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS and blocked with 3% BSA blocking solution. Cells were then incubated overnight with ERα and phospho-CREB primary antibodies at 4 °C. Cells were then incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (Invitrogen) or Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Invitrogen) for 30 min at 4 °C. Cells were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Invitrogen). Cells were mounted and images were visualized using a confocal microscope (LSM900, Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Phospho-Kinase Array Kit

Various kinase phosphorylation sites were analyzed using the Proteome Profiler Human Phospho-Kinase Array Kit (ARY003C, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, MCF7 cells were treated with 4 µM CBD for 24 h. Cells were harvested and incubated with lysis buffer 6 for 30 min on ice. Cell lysate was then centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 5 min. Proteins were quantified by the BCA assay. Proteins were incubated with each membrane for overnight at 4℃. Each membrane was incubated with diluted detection antibody cocktail A and B for 2 h. Next, each membrane was incubated with streptavidin-HRP for 30 min. The phosphorylation signal was developed with electrochemiluminescence solution and detected on X-ray films.

Bioinformatics analysis

The correlation coefficients of ERα and CREB in patients with breast cancer were evaluated using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis with The Breast Cancer Gene Expression Miner v5.2 (BC-GenExMiner v5.2) database (http://bcgenex.ico.unicancer.fr/). BC-GenExMiner v5.2 was used to annotate breast cancer RNA-seq data from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA).

Xenograft experiments

All procedures of animal experiment were governed on the basis of animal care guidelines approved by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Korea University College of Medicine (KOREA-2021-0217). Female BALB/c-nude mice aged 8 weeks (n = 4) were taken from Orient Bio. 17β-estradiol pellets (0.72 mg, 60-day release; Innovative Research of America) were implanted subcutaneously in BALB/c-nude mice 1 week before cell injection. MCF7 cells (1 × 107 cells) were implanted into right fourth mammary fat pads. Tumor volumes were monitored until their sizes reached approximately 100 mm3 in size. Animals were randomized into four treatment group. Mice were intraperitoneally (i.p.) injected with 10 mg/kg of CBD and/or 10 mg/kg of Tamoxifen every two days. Tumor size were measured every two days using a digital caliper. Tumor volume was calculated with the following formula: tumor volume (mm3) V = 1/2 x (Length x Width2).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) assay

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. After blocking with 3% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) for 35 min and heated at 100 ℃ for 20 min. The slides were then universally blocked with a blocking buffer (Dako, Glostrup, Denmark) at RT for 1 h. Following the incubation, tissue slides were incubated with primary antibodies against CREB, phospho-CREB, and ERα for overnight at 4℃. On the next day, slides were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and counterstained with DAB substrate. Stained slides were observed using a microscope.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed independently and confirmed at least three times. The significance of statistics was determined using the GraphPad Prism Software (La Jolla, CA, USA). All results were evaluated using unpaired Student’s t-test, and p-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant. Each *, **, *** stands for *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001, respectively.

Results

To evaluate the anti-proliferative effects of CBD and Tamoxifen (TAM) in ER-positive breast cancer, MCF7 and T47D cell lines were exposed to escalating doses of each compound for 24 h. Both CBD and TAM reduced cell viability in a concentration-dependent manner in both cell lines (Fig. 1A and B). To quantitatively evaluate the interaction between CBD and TAM, combination index (CI) values were calculated using the Chou-Talalay method. The analysis indicated a condition-dependent interaction, with selected combinations showing CI values below 1 (S1A). Based on this analysis, 4 µM CBD and 5 µM TAM were selected as the working concentrations for subsequent experiments. To further investigate the combinatorial effects, colony formation and apoptosis assay were performed under the selected conditions. The combined treatment significantly reduced colony forming ability compared to single treatment (Fig. 1C). In addition, Annexin V/PI staining revealed that apoptotic cell death was markedly increased in both MCF7 and T47D cells following combination treatment (Fig. 1D and S1B). To assess estrogen dependency, MCF7 cells were treated with CBD and/or tamoxifen in the presence or absence of 10 nM 17β-estradiol (E2). As the results, apoptosis was not significantly affected by E2 supplementation, indicating ligand-independent combination effects (S2A and S2B). Western blot analysis further revealed increased cleavage of PARP and activation of caspases-3, -8, and − 9 following co-treatment, indicating caspase-dependent apoptosis (Fig. 1E). Next, pretreatment with the pan-caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK abrogated these apoptotic responses, confirming the involvement of the caspase cascade (Fig. 1F). The expression levels of c-PARP, c-caspase 3,8, and 9 were suppressed under Z-VAD-FMK treatment condition. Collectively, these results indicate that combination treatment with CBD and TAM induces caspase-mediated apoptosis in ER-positive breast cancer cells.

ERα is a key transcriptional driver of growth and survival in ER-positive breast cancer [18], we next examined whether CBD modulates its expression and localization. Western blot and immunofluorescence (IF) analyses showed that CBD treatment markedly decreased ERα protein levels in both MCF7 and T47D cell lines (Fig. 2A and B). Furthermore, CBD substantially reduced the ERα in a time course-dependent manner (Fig. 2C). Once a ligand is bound to ERα in the cell membrane, it allows the receptor to change its conformation in an active form, dimerize, translocate to the nucleus, and interact with transcriptional coactivators. Indeed, activation of ERα allows the expression of factors such as MCY, Cyclin D1, FOXM1, GREB1, BCL2 or amphiregulin, IGF-1, and CXCL-12, which have oncogenic potential, increasing cancer cells proliferation and the risk of DNA damage in response to estrogen [19]. To verify this signaling pathway, we observed nuclear translocation of ER alpha using cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions and then western blot analysis. Fractionation experiments demonstrated that CBD inhibited nuclear translocation of ERα, retaining it within the cytoplasm (Fig. 2D). Quantitative RT-PCR confirmed that CBD downregulated ESR1 mRNA and its target genes, including CCND1, GREB1, and TFF1 (Fig. 2E and F). These findings indicate that CBD suppresses both ERα expression and transcriptional activity in ER-positive breast cancer cells. Notably, tamoxifen treatment alone did not significantly reduce ERα protein levels, whereas CBD treatment resulted in a marked decrease in ERα expression. These findings suggest that the enhanced efficacy of the combination treatment may arise from complementary mechanisms involving both functional inhibition and downregulation of ERα signaling. Consistent with these findings, co-treatment with CBD and tamoxifen resulted in a reduction of ERα protein levels comparable to that observed with CBD treatment alone (S3A).

To identify upstream regulators responsible for CBD-induced ERα downregulation, a phospho-kinase array was performed. CBD treatment reduced phosphorylation of several kinases, including p38a, PLCγ1, ERK1/2, and CREB (Fig. 3A and B). These kinase proteins are involved in the cAMP-PKA-CREB signaling pathway. The cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB) is a transcription factor that plays a pivotal role in various biological processes [20]. Activation of CREB by the cAMP signaling pathway occurs through phosphorylation, which strengthens the physical association between CREB and the CREB-binding protein (CBP) within the nucleus. CREB enhances apoptosis resistance by upregulating anti-apoptotic genes, while simultaneously activating cell cycle and metabolic regulatory genes, thereby regulating the transcriptional activity of multiple target genes [21]. To confirm this result, we examined its activation state. IF and western blot analysis revealed that total CREB levels were unaffected, but phosphorylated CREB was strongly diminished in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 3C–E). Moreover, CBD inhibited phosphorylation of upstream activators, including PKA and PLCγ1 (Fig. 3F). Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) demonstrated that CBD disrupted the CREB-CBP interaction without altering CBP expression, confirming interference with CREB-dependent transcription (Fig. 3G). Together, these findings suggest that CBD interferes with cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling pathway, thereby reducing CREB-mediated transcriptional activity.

To clarify the functional connection between CREB inhibition and ERα regulation, transient transfection using siRNA against ERα and CREB in MCF7 cell lines were performed. CREB silencing significantly decreased ERα expression, whereas ERα knockdown did not alter CREB protein levels (Fig. 4A–D). These results indicate that CREB acts upstream of ERα regulation by CBD treatment. Next, CREB depletion enhanced TAM-induced apoptosis and further suppressed cell proliferation, as shown by elevated cleaved PARP levels and Annexin V-positive populations (Fig. 4E-G and S3A). Moreover, mRNA levels of ERα target genes were reduced in CREB-silenced cells treated with TAM (Fig. 4H). Conversely, pharmacological activation of the CREB pathway using forskolin (FL) restored ERα expression even in the presence of CBD (Fig. 4I). We analyzed the correlation coefficients in patients with Luminal A and B subtypes to assess the interaction between ERα and CREB based on RNA-seq analysis using the BC-GenExMiner v5.2 database. We found that a positive correlation between ESR1 and CREB1 expression in patients with Luminal A and B breast cancer. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CBD enhances tamoxifen sensitivity by suppressing the CREB-ERα axis in ER-positive breast cancer cells.

Discussions

Estrogen receptor (ER) signaling plays a pivotal role in the initiation and progression of breast cancer, serving as both a prognostic marker and a predictor of therapeutic response to endocrine therapies [22]. Activation of ER by oestrogen triggers downstream signaling cascades, including MAPK and PI3K pathways, thereby promoting proliferation and survival of breast cancer cells [22, 23]. Consequently, endocrine therapy targeting ER signaling remains the main treatment for ER-positive breast cancer patients [24]. In recent years, therapeutic strategies have focused on attenuating estrogen-driven tumor growth either by reducing estrogen availability or by pharmacologically blocking ER function [25].

Tamoxifen (TAM), a selective estrogen receptor modulator (SERM), has been endocrine therapy for more than three decades due to its clinical efficacy, safety, and affordability. It acts as a competitive antagonist to estrogen by binding ERα, thereby regulating downstream target gene transcription in ER-positive breast cancer cells [26, 27]. However, despite its clinical benefit, acquired and de novo resistance to tamoxifen significantly compromise treatment efficacy, particularly in metastatic disease [28]. Consequently, combination therapies that leverage drug synergism have gained attention as potential approaches to overcome resistance and enhance treatment outcomes.

In our previous study, we demonstrated that cannabidiol (CBD) suppresses angiogenesis and stemness in breast cancer cells by interfering with the HIF-1α pathway [29], highlighting its potential as a therapeutic adjuvant. Consistent with these findings, the present study provides further evidence that CBD exerts potent anti-cancer activity in ER-positive breast cancer cells. We observed that CBD alone induced apoptosis and inhibited the ERα signaling cascade, while its combination with TAM produced a synergistic effect on both MCF7 cells and xenograft mouse models. Importantly, apoptosis induced by CBD and its combination with tamoxifen was not affected by estrogen, indicating that these effects occur independently of ligand-mediated ER activation. This synergy appears to be mediated through the inhibition of the CREB-ERα axis, leading to enhanced apoptotic cell death and reduced tumor proliferation in both in vitro and in vivo models. Notably, the enhanced sensitivity to tamoxifen observed upon CBD treatment cannot be explained solely by ERα expression, but rather by the suppression of ERα transcriptional activity and signaling output. Previous studies have demonstrated that ERα can remain functionally active through ligand-independent mechanisms and coactivator-mediated transcription, even in the presence of endocrine therapy [5, 19]. In this context, CREB and its coactivator CBP have been shown to play critical roles in sustaining transcriptional programs that promote cell survival and proliferation [21].

Our findings suggest that CBD disrupts the CREB-CBP transcriptional axis, thereby attenuating ERα-driven gene expression and reducing residual ERα signaling activity. This dual effect-combining receptor modulation by tamoxifen with transcriptional suppression by CBD-may explain the observed enhancement of tamoxifen responsiveness. Such a mechanism is consistent with previous reports indicating that targeting ER signaling activity, rather than receptor abundance alone, is critical for overcoming endocrine resistance [30]. This reduction in residual ERα signaling likely enhances the antagonistic effect of tamoxifen, thereby reinforcing its ability to inhibit ER-driven transcription and promote apoptotic cell death.

These findings suggest that the combined use of CBD and TAM may potentiate endocrine responsiveness in resistant ER-positive breast cancers by re-sensitizing tumor cells to TAM. Given that nearly half of ER-positive breast cancer patients ultimately develop resistance to tamoxifen monotherapy, our data propose a mechanistically grounded strategy that could mitigate therapeutic resistance while preserving efficacy. The suppression of the cAMP/CREB- ERα axis by CBD represents a novel mechanism that may explain the observed improvement in tamoxifen sensitivity.

From a translational and clinical standpoint, these results provide compelling rationale for exploring CBD as an adjuvant or sensitizer in endocrine therapy. As CBD is already under investigation for several clinical indications with a favorable safety profile, CBD in combination with tamoxifen could offer a low-toxicity strategy to enhance therapeutic efficacy in ER-positive breast cancer. Such combination approaches may also allow dose reduction of tamoxifen, thereby minimizing adverse effects such as endometrial hyperplasia or thromboembolic events.

Despite these promising findings, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although our data demonstrate that cannabidiol enhances tamoxifen sensitivity and suppresses ERα signaling, the experimental models used in this study were limited to tamoxifen-sensitive ER-positive breast cancer cell lines and xenograft model. While these models provide valuable mechanistic insights, they cannot fully recapitulate the complex tumor microenvironment, pharmacokinetics, and immunological interactions observed in human patients. Established tamoxifen-resistant models or cell lines harboring clinically relevant ESR1 mutations, which are frequently associated with endocrine resistance in patients, were not included. Second, the concentrations of CBD used in this study may not directly reflect clinically achievable plasma levels in patients. The pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic interactions between CBD and TAM remain poorly characterized, particularly concerning potential alterations in CYP450-mediated metabolism that could influence drug efficacy or toxicity profiles. Furthermore, validation of these findings in tamoxifen-resistant or ESR1-mutant models, as well as in more aggressive ER-positive subtypes, will be important to further establish the translational relevance of this combinatorial strategy.

Third, while our findings identify the CREB-ERα axis as a key regulatory pathway underlying the synergistic effects of cannabidiol and tamoxifen, further validation using large-scale in vivo validation, additional preclinical models, including patient-derived models or endocrine-resistant systems will be required to fully establish the translational relevance of this mechanism. Future studies incorporating tamoxifen-resistant and ESR1-mutant breast cancer models, as well as clinical correlative analyses, will be essential to determine the broader applicability of cannabidiol as an adjuvant strategy to overcome endocrine resistance. Lastly, the potential variability in CBD formulations, purity, and bioavailability across commercial sources poses a challenge for standardization in clinical settings. Future studies should focus on defining optimal dosing regimens, evaluating long-term safety, and identifying biomarkers predictive of response to CBD-TAM therapy.

Further in vivo studies and clinical trials are warranted to optimize the dosing regimen, assess pharmacokinetic interactions, and confirm long-term safety and efficacy. Nonetheless, our findings highlight the promise of CBD as a potential endocrine sensitizer, opening new avenues for the management of hormone receptor-positive breast cancer.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that CBD enhances the anti-cancer efficacy of tamoxifen in ER-positive breast cancer by promoting apoptosis and inhibiting the ERα signaling pathway. CBD alone suppressed tumor cell proliferation and downregulated the CREB-ERα axis, whereas its co-administration with tamoxifen produced a synergistic effect in both in vitro and in vivo models. Mechanistically, CBD potentiated the pro-apoptotic response of tamoxifen by attenuating ERα expression and its target genes, suggesting a complementary interaction between the two agents. Our findings highlight the potential of CBD as a safe and effective adjuvant or sensitizer in endocrine therapy. Incorporating CBD into clinical strategies may improve therapeutic outcomes, enhance tamoxifen sensitivity, and reduce the risk of recurrence in ER-positive breast cancer patients. These results support further translational and clinical studies to validate the therapeutic promise of this combination.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrates that cannabidiol (CBD) restored tamoxifen sensitivity in ER-positive breast cancer by inhibiting the cAMP/CREB signaling axis, leading to downregulation of ERα expression and activity. Importantly, the combined treatment of CBD and tamoxifen synergistically induced apoptosis and suppressed tumor growth in both in vitro and in vivo models. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of CBD as an adjuvant to endocrine therapy and suggest a viable strategy to overcome tamoxifen resistance in ER-positive breast cancer.
