Unraveling Optimal Dose and Responsive Markers for Human Cord Blood Derived Mononuclear Cells in Alleviating Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia in Neonatal Mice
Authors: Jia Chen, Yuhan Chen, Xue Du, Liu He, Yanhua Wang, Huiling Han, Qiuping Li, Huixia Zhou, Zhichun Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), characterized by impaired alveolarization and dysregulated vascularization, is a severe health burden for neonates worldwide. Hyperoxia induced acute lung injury is a major contributor to the progression and deterioration of BPD. An increasing number of animal studies have revealed that human umbilical cord blood derived mononuclear cells (hUCB-MNCs) infusion significantly attenuated the hyperoxia-induced acute lung injury through regeneration capacity. Currently, clinical application requires determination of the optimal dose and adjustment to good manufacturing practices. In this work, we comprehensively investigated the optimal dose of hUCB-MNCs in alleviating hyperoxia-induced lung injury in neonatal C57BL6/J mice. Mice with hyperoxia exposure were implanted with low (3×104 cells/kg, Dl), middle (3×105 cells/kg, Dm) and high (3×106 cells/kg, Dh) dose of hUCB-MNCs at postnatal day 7. Three weeks after graft, characteristics exhibited in lungs including morphology, function and cytokine expression were thoroughly analyzed. Implantation of hUCB-MNCs sharply reverted the impaired lung architecture induced by hyperoxia exposure dose dependently as evidenced by indicated parameters. Attenuated expression of IL-1β concomitant with enhanced expression of IL-10 and IL-2 were shown in Dh inoculated groups, where Dl and Dm failed to restore the level of IL-10, IL-1β and IL-2. Significantly re-escalated marker of angiogenic marker VEGFA, CD31 in lung tissue were uniquely observed in Dh group. Mechanistically, our study revealed the appropriate dose of intravenous infusion of hUCB-MNCs in alleviating hyperoxia-induced lung injury through modulating reactive oxygen species response in neonatal mice. Therefore, a tight control of hUCB-MNCs density or levels of CB-MNC related products is of great significance.

Introduction

Extreme preterm infants are at a high risk for developing bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), which is characterized by alveolar simplification and vascularization impairment, leading to chronic oxygen supplementation until late infancy and lifelong disturbances in pulmonary function and neurodevelopment (1). Conventional managements for BPD are symptom oriented whereas the mortality rate remains at high levels with survivors displaying systematic adverse effects. Therefore, developing novel and efficient therapies to reduce overall morbidity and improve lifelong quality in extreme preterm infants with BPD are of great significance.

Mononuclear cells (MNCs) are source of stem cell reservoir containing high level of primitive multipotent stem cells, progenitor cells and regulatory immune cells (2). Among various derivation including the bone marrow (BM-MNCs), the peripheral blood (PB-MNCs) and cord blood (CB-MNCs), UCB is considered a promising source for MNCs due to its easy availability, high proliferation capacity, and less immunogenicity (3). A wide range of preclinical studies and clinical trials have revealed that MNCs with adult-appendage derivations are capable of presenting superior biological activity and regenerative efficacy in adult pulmonary conditions (4, 5). However, few studies have reported the translational potential of hUCB-MNCs in neonatal circumstances (6, 7). Recently, autologous mononuclear cell infusion have attracted tremendous amounts of attention in preventing moderate or severe BPD of surviving very preterm neonates through strong immune-modulatory capacity (8, 9). While the exclusion criteria of autologous CB-MNC infusion have restricted the extensive application of CB-MNC in majority of the very preterm neonates. Typically, high-risk factors accounting for the unhealthy pregnant mothers including pre-eclampsia, prenatal steroids, infection and/or inflammation, prenatal and postpartum nutrition would affect the intrauterine environment and therefore destroy the integrity and quality of hUCB-MNC. It is therefore of urgent need to determine the regulatory mechanism of hUCB-MNCs in rodent model before assessing the safety and feasibility of allogeneic instead of autologous hUCB-MNC infusion on human subjects, which can benefit the very preterm survivors to the largest extent. We previously draw a clear conclusion on evaluating the alleviating effect and potential mechanisms in hUCB-MNCs implantation on rodent BPD model (10), which resolved the long lasting controversy about whether hUCB-MNCs infusion can restore the pulmonary morphology and functions (11).

Given that hUCB-MNCs intravenous infusion could be a novel therapy for BPD by the above evidence. Major concerns of the pre-clinical studies are whether outcomes such as release of paracrine immune factors, recovery of damaged structures and improved overall survival are associated with the number of CB-MNC cells infused into the recipient mice. For several studies have examined the dose-response protective effect of CD34＋ cells (12), mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) (13), or human embryonic stem cells (14), part of which are considered to be subsets of hUCB-MNCs in respiratory or nervous system of mouse models (15). Infusion significantly improves the survival rate or pathological status of the recipient mice. So, it is of vital importance to examine whether infusion of MNCs improve pulmonary inﬂammation and function in hyperoxia reared mice model in a dose-dependent manner.

Recent progress in normal or pre-conditioned stem cells have outlined the key molecular and cellular pathways that attributes to the preservation of lung morphology and maintenance of pulmonary functions. Studies have shown that a myriad of cytokines (i.e., TNFα, IL-6, IL-1β, IL-10) or neovasculatory factors (i.e., VEGFA, TGF-β) modulated by MSC or MSC derivate could serve as markers reflecting anti-inflammatory and pro-angiogenesis mechanism (16). Intriguingly, stimulated natriuretic peptide B is a solid endothelial indicator of peripheral pulmonary artery re-vascularization and down-regulated thyroid transcription factor-1 (17) represented the reversed parenchymal fibrosis. Returned to normoxia level of renin–angiotensin system implied the attenuating pulmonary hypertension and right-ventricular hypertrophy (18). Although these specific markers highlighted the beneficial effect of stem-cell based therapy towards critical features of BPD, none of which evaluated the threshold of functional stem cell infusion by unraveling novel responsive marker, especially in hUCB-MNC infusion circumstances. In this study, We preliminary determined the optimal dose of hUCB-MNC infusion through systematic analysis of lung morphology, pulmonary functions and immune-modulatory effects. Mechanistically, we identified well known T cell receptor dephosphorylating enzyme (19), protein tyrosine phosphatase non-receptor type 4 (PTPN4), as a featured marker by dictating oxidative stress, which provide guidance in manipulating hUCB-MNC infusion.

Materials and Methods

Ethics statement

All experimental protocols involving human derived cells were rigorously reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board at the institution of pediatrics in the seventh medical center of PLA general hospital (No. 2021-026). In conducting the research on the effects of human UCB cells towards hyperoxia-induced acute lung injury-BPD model, strict adherence to ethical guidelines and compliance with established standards was maintained to ensure the integrity and ethical acceptability of the work. The aims, methods, and potential implications are reviewed by the seventh medical center ensuring that all animal related experiments were conducted responsibly and ethically.

Cell isolation and identification

hUCB units were supplied by Shandong Qilu stem cell engineering Co. Ltd. For the extraction of hUCB-MNCs, the cryopreserved cord blood units were thawed immediately and gently shook in a 37℃ water. Cord blood was collected into 50 mL centrifuge tube thrice volume of premixed suspension buffer. This procedure was completed within 5 minutes and the collected hUCB was stored at room temperature. MNCs were isolated by centrifugation over a Ficoll-Hypaque density gradient at 700 rpm for 20 minutes at 4℃ in premixed suspension buffer. Cells at the interface were collected by adding premixed suspension buffer followed by centrifugation at 500 rpm for 5 minutes at 4℃. The collected cells were washed thrice with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and subsequently re-suspended in serum-free Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium. The morphology of re-suspended MNCs were determined by Wright’s-Giemsa staining.

Mouse experiment

All experimental mice maintained on a C57BL6/J background were purchased from Beijing SiPeiFu Laboratory Animal Technology and housed under pathogen-free conditions. Newborn pups were delivered spontaneously and reared with their dams. The experiment began within 24 hours after birth and continued through postnatal day 14. Newborn pups were randomly assigned to five groups: normoxia group (NG), hyperoxia group (HG), hyperoxia with 3×104 cells/kg (Dl), 3×105 (Dm), or 3×106 (Dh) hUCB-MNCs administration group. NG newborn pups were kept with a nursing mother in standard cage at room air throughout the experiment, while HG newborn pups were maintained with a nursing mother in standard carge within 100L Plexiglas chambers in which the hyperoxia (85% O2) condition was maintained. Humidity and environmental temperature were maintained at 50% and 24℃. Nursing mother mice were rotated daily between litters in the normoxia and hyperoxia groups to avoid oxygen toxicity. Survival and body weights of mice pups in each group were checked daily throughout the experiment. Invasive lung function testing was performed at P28 by the positive-pressure ventilation technique in anesthetized, nonparalyzed, tracheotomized animals with intact chest wall. Mice were deeply anesthetized with an i.p. injection of 140 mg/kg of ketamine and 14 mg/kg of xylazine to eliminate all spontaneous breathing under anesthesia. Body weights were recorded at the start of the procedure. After lung function testing and pulmonary blood flow, the animals were euthanized by sectioning of diaphragm and abdominal vessels. And the whole lung tissue was obtained for morphometric and biochemical analyses (Fig. 1A, 1B). Detailed numbers of animal were indicated in figure legend of corresponding sections. All methods are reported in accordance with Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments guidelines (20).

Infusion of hUCB-MNCs in mouse models

Hyperoxia exposed pups at P7 were further randomly assigned to receive hUCB-MNCs intravenous infusion. For cell administration, 3×104 (Dl), 3×105 (Dm), or 3×106 (Dh) cells/kg in 0.03 mL (PBS, Ph 7.4) were delivered intravenously to pups by injections through great saphenous vein (Fig. 1C). For NG and HG, equal volume of saline was given intravenously. For blinding of above experiments, mice were body-tagged with simple numbers. The person who performed the experiments did not know the identity of the specific samples until data were collected and analyzed. After the procedure, the animals were returned to their dams.

Hematoxylin & eosin staining

For morphometric analyses, lungs were fixed in situ by tracheal instillation of 4% buffered formalin at a constant inflation pressure of 20 cmH2O, and then fixed overnight at room temperature in the same fixative. The fixed lung tissue was embedded in paraffin wax after tissue processing. Sections (5 μm thick) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Images of each section were captured with a magnifier digital camera through representative images were displayed and lesion areas were quantified by ImageJ version 1.45 (NIH; http://imagej.nih.gov/ij) in 6 random microscopic fields (×200) per sample.

Lung morphology

Left-lobe lung sections were stained with hematoxylin & eosin. For each morphometric analysis, sections with large airways or blood vessels were excluded. Four to six areas per slide were quantitated and averaged per slide. Images were acquired with a Nikon Eclipse TE300 inverted microscope and Hammamatsu C4742 digital camera, using Metamorph software (Universal Imaging) and quantification was performed using ImagePro software (version 6.0). For radial alveolar counts (RAC) measurement, the well-established method to quantify alveolarization areas were randomly chosen and photographed at ×20 magnification. For each of six images, a perpendicular line was drawn from the center of a bronchial or bronchiolar airway to either the edge of the lung or the nearest connective tissue septum or airway. A minimum of 40 lines was drawn for each lung, and the number of septae intersected was counted for each line. Mean chord length (Lm) (MCL) of the airspace was estimated, as previously described. To prevent inadvertent observer bias, investigators were double blinded to the assigned groups to performed image acquisition and analyses. Values were pooled for each individual animal for statistical analysis.

Pulmonary function assessment

For detection of respiratory motion function, data were monitored and acquired by Acqknowledge (Biopac systems. Inc.), a pulmonary maneuver system. During data collection, basic stable HR (heart rate) was recorded by electrocardiogram before tracheotomy operation. Mice HR were maintained at proper level (<10% variation of basic HR) and breathing rate were maintained stable to ensure the reliability of the physiological data. Basic parameters including peak inspiratory flow, peak expiratory flow, tidal volume (TV), breathing per minute and minute volume (MV).

For detection of pulmonary blood flow, all mice subsequently transferred to evaluate pulmonary blood flow with laser doppler flowmetry (LDF) using Moor FLPI (Moor Instruments). Mice were ventilated (Alcott biotech) with an average breathing frequency of 150 breaths/minute, inspiratory/expiratory ratio 2.0 and TV 1.0 mL/kg. The blood flow of bilateral lungs and heart were synchronizing measured after they were fully exposed. The pulmonary blood flow signal intensity was normalized to heart surface blood.

All the maneuvers and perturbations were performed until at least three reproducible measurements were recorded. A coefficient of determination of 0.95 was the lower limit for acceptance of a measurement. For each parameter and each animal, the average of at least three measurements was calculated.

Reagents and antibodies

For immunohistochemistry (IHC), primary antibodies including anti-vascular endothelial growth factor VEGFA (Cat. #GB15165-100; 1：100 for IHC) and CD31 (Cat. #GB12063-100, 1：200 for IHC) were all purchased from Service Bio. Differentiation regulator PTPN4 (Cat. #TA351566; 1：100 for IHC) was purchased from ORIGene. Primary antibody including VEGFA (Cat. #19003-1-AP, Proteintech. 1：1,000 for Western blotting [WB]) and CD31 (Cat. #77699, CST. 1：2,000 for WB). Second antibody including goat anti-mouse IgG-HRP (Cat. # abs20001; 1：2,000 for WB) and goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (Cat. # abs20002; 1：2,000 for WB) were purchased from absin.

Western blot

Tissue samples was mixed with RIPA lysis buffer (Cat. #G2002, Service bio) followed by sonication and centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 minutes. Then, 15∼20 mg of equal amount of protein in different groups was loaded in 12% separating gel and 5% concentrated gel for electrophoresis (Cat. #D032207, Ygyr) and blotted onto a 0.2 μm NC membrane (Cat. #1060001, Cytiva). Membranes were blocked in 5% skimmed milk (Cat. #BS102, Biosharp) and incubated with primary antibodies and secondary antibodies. The membranes were finally subjected to ECL exposure (Cat. #WBKLS0500, Immobilon Western, Millipore) by Eblot Touch Image Pro.

Immuno-histochemistry

The lung tissues were paraffined and sectioned into 5 μm slides. Subsequently, the sections were stained with indicated primary antibodies followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and developed with DAB substrate (brown). Images were obtained under the Nikon Bx60 microscope connected to a Nikon DP70 camera with Cell-F imaging software (Soft Imaging System). All staining was assessed by a quantitative imaging method using image J software; the percentage of immunostaining and the staining intensity were recorded. An H score was calculated using the following formula: ＋Area%=IOD (sum)/Area (sum) % indicates the percentage of positive cells versus all cells.

RNA extraction and real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated using Trizol method and reverse transcribed to cDNA using the ReverTra AceⓇ kit (Toyobo, TRT-101). Quantitative real-time PCR reactions were conducted using SYBR Green PCR master mix (Toyobo, QPX-201) through ABI QSFlex6 system. Primers were listed below:

human Actin Reverse: 5’-GGCATAGAGGTCTTTACGGATG-3’

human PTPN4 Forward: 5’-TCTTCCGCCTGGATAGACCA-3’

human PTPN4 Reverse: 5’-TCACTGGGGGACATGTGAAC-3’

Cytokine assay

Resected lungs were snap-frozen and stored at −80℃ for inflammatory factor analysis. For IL-2, IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-10 measurements, Commercial Enzyme linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) kits were used (R&D systems) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The plate was read immediately after the assay at 450 nm (reference wavelength 630 nm) using a microplate reader (Biotek) and values were reported in pg/mL.

Analysis of the differentially expressed genes in indicated groups

High throughput sequencing (HTS) were introduced to uncovering the RNA samples were collected and quality verified before library preparation and Libraries were therefore established (21), from RNA (RIN>7) using the Trio RNA-Seq Library Preparation Kit (NuGen), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Libraries were sequenced using The NovaSeq 2000 System (Illumina) were utilized as the sequencing platform of the library to obtain 50 base pair (bp), paired-end reads. Reads were mapped to the mouse reference genome (Genome Reference Consortium Mouse Build 38 mm10) using STAR, and read counts were generated using HTS. The sequential analysis of differential gene expression and normalized read counts were generated using DESeq2 package for R as previously described. Shared genes implicated rom differentially expressed genes between Control and BPD groups as well as MNC (Dh) group injected BPD and BPD groups were further analyzed to discover the candidate markers defined as dose response.

Study approval and ethics statement

Animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Ethic Committee of seventh medical center of PLA General Hospital (No. 2021-026). All animals were housed, cared for, and used in compliance with the guidelines regarding the human use and care of laboratory animals for biomedical research published by the National Institutes of Health (No. 85-23, revised 1996).

Statistical analyses

All the statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, Inc.) software. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA, respectively. All quantitative data are expressed as the mean±standard deviation (SD). The p-values, odds ratios, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated. A two-sided p-value<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All p-values were adjusted by Bonferroni correction.

Results

Evaluation of dose dependent intravenous infusion of hUCB-MNCs

We initially verified the property of hUCB-MNCs by performing Wright’s-Giemsa staining and the purified cells meets the criteria (Fig. 2A), therefore we went on to analyze the phenotypic divergences in indicted groups. We initially determined whether the pulmonary resident of infused hUCB-MNCs is dose dependent so as to guarantee the prerequisite of our study. Taking into account of species divergence, human GAPDH expression were utilized to trace the presence of hUCB-MNCs derived RNA in the recipient mice lung tissue, which was observed only in the hUBC-MNCs infused groups, with 0.027±0.003, 0.071±0.008, and 0.189±0.035 of human GAPDH relative to mice actin expression in Dl, Dm (p<0.05 vs. Dl), and Dh (p<0.001 vs. Dl, p<0.01 vs. Dm), respectively, in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2B).

Body weight gain and survival rate

Birth weights were not significantly different between the five experimental groups at P1 (3.18±0.17g and 3.22±0.13 g in NG and HG, respectively). However, body weight at P28 in HG was significantly lower compared to NG (10.83±0.52 g vs. 15.77±0.36 g, p<0.001). The reduced body weight gain observed in HG was significantly improved in Dl, Dm and Dh (Dl vs. HG: 15.773±0.36 g vs. 10.83±0.52 g, p<0.05; Dm vs. HG: 16.49±0.34 g vs. 10.83±0.52 g, p<0.05; Dh vs. HG: 17.24±0.53 g vs. 10.83±0.52 g, p<0.05, respectively) (Fig. 2C). Systemic necropsy at postnatal day 28 (PN28) revealed no observable morphology abnormalities in Dl, Dm, and Dh (Supplementary Fig. S1).

HG significantly increased mortality by the end of experiment (P28) compared to the zero mortality of NG. The increased mortality observed in HG (p<0.05, vs. NG) was significantly decreased with hUCB-MNCs treatment in Dh (p<0.05, vs. HG) but not in Dl and Dm (p>0.05, vs. HG) (Fig. 2D).

Histopathology

Impaired alveolar growth, as evidenced by micro-vascular hyperemia and dilatation, adjacent alveolar fusion, alveolar structure destruction and emphysematous, inflammatory cell exudation, lung tissue structure disorder, was observed in HG compared to NG. After hUCB-MNCs treatment, hyperoxia-induced impairments in alveolar growth and morphological changes were improved, showing significant attenuation in Dl, Dm and Dh, with the best attenuation in Dh (Fig. 3A). In morphometric analyses, the MCL were significantly higher in HG (56.26±8.79μm) than in NG (36.46±1.94 μm, p<0.001) (Fig. 3B). The mean RAC, indicating the volume of the alveoli, was significantly reduced in HG than in NG (129±27.96/mm2 vs. 272.17±41.58/mm2, p<0.001) (Fig. 3C). The data demonstrate significant attenuation of decreased MCL in Dh (37.10±2.31 μm with 34% attenuation vs. HG, p<0.001), in Dm (46.49±4.74 μm with 18% attenuation vs. HG, p<0.001), and in Dl (51.45±3.6 μm with 9% attenuation vs. HG, p<0.001). Increased RAC in Dh (258±42.09 per mm2 with 200% improvement vs. HG, p<0.001), in Dm (194.33±24.65 per mm2 with 150% improvement vs. HG, p<0.05), and in Dl (159.83±21.98 per mm2 with 145% improvement vs. HG, p>0.05). These findings support the protection of hUCB-MNCs intravenous infusion with the best result in Dh.

Pulmonary motion and respiratory blood flow test

In line to the alterations in lung morphology, HG displayed shallow and slow breathing, and the lowest number of respiratory rate per minute upon hyperoxia exposure, evidencing by the longer expiratory time with small sawtooth wave compared to those in NG. Interestingly, hUCB-MNCs treatment counterbalanced the hyperoxia-induced impairments in respiratory motion changes, showing different degrees of improvement in Dl, Dm and Dh, with the most stable sine wave in Dh (Fig. 4A). In pulmonary motion analyses, the MV mean dynamic volume of lung, indicating the ventilation of lung, were significantly lower in HG (7.16±0.47 mL) than in NG (13.40±0.59 mL, p<0.001). Our data demonstrate significant improvement of increased MV in Dh (Dh vs. HG, 13.01±0.57 mL vs. 7.16±0.47 mL, p<0.001), but not in Dm (Dm vs. HG, 9.72±0.17 mL vs. 7.16±0.47 mL, p<0.001) and Dl (Dl vs. HG, 7.91±1.42 mL vs. 7.16±0.47 mL, p>0.05) (Fig. 4B).

In order to explore the potential impact of hUCB-MNCs towards peripheral pulmonary vascular remodeling under hyperoxia exposure, lung perfusion with LDF were employed (Fig. 4C). HG displayed lower left or right lung/heart blood flow ratio compared to that in NG (p<0.05). Our data showed after hUCB-MNCs treatment left or right lung/heart blood flow ratio were improved in P28. In the right lung/heart blood flow ratio was significantly increased in Dl (0.62±0.13), Dm (0.69±0.12) and Dh (0.58±0.07) than that in HG (0.36±0.1, p<0.05). But in the left lung/heart blood flow ratio, there was no statistical significance in Dl (0.70±0.07), Dm (0.69±0.03) and Dh (0.73±0.05) than in HG (0.44±0.09, p>0.05) (Fig. 4D).

IL-6: Dh vs. HG, 38.18±3.171 vs. 44.12±3.639, p<0.05; Dm vs. HG, 53.93±3.290 vs. 44.12±3.639, p>0.05; Dl vs. HG, 56.89±8.473 vs. 44.12±3.639, p>0.05 (Fig. 5B). Meanwhile, hyperoxia-induced increase of anti-inflammatory cytokines were robustly recovered in Dh while those in Dm or Dl were not albeit gradient up regulation were shown. Collectively, protein expression of cytokines displayed generally restoration at Dh and Dm while not at Dl.

Cytosolic expression of growth factors VEGF and CD31

Since vascular remodeling and growth reactivation are the main routine to alleviate hyperoxia induced lung injury. We systematically examined the protein expression levels of the classical vascular factor VEGFα. We observed significantly decreased level of positive staining method of VEGFA in HG vs. NG, 1.899±0.31 vs. 0.04322±0.013, p<0.0001 (Fig. 6A) and CD31 in HG vs.NG, 1.404±0.34 vs. 0.2850±0.16, p<0.0001 were observed (Fig. 6C). Hyperoxia-induced abrogation in VEGFA were significantly increased in Dh (Dh vs. HG, 1.739±1.30 vs. 0.04322±0.03, p<0.0001, but not in Dl and Dm (Dl vs. HG, 0.2472±0.14 vs. 0.04322±0.03, p=0.8275; Dm vs. HG, 0.6795±0.19 vs. 0.04322±0.03, p=0.0228) (Fig. 6B). To better assess the capacity of hUCB-MNC in restoring angiogenesis, Expression of vascular endothelial cell marker CD31 were also determined and we observed notable increase in Dh vs. HG, 1.530±0.36 vs. 0.2850±0.16, p<0.001 and Dm (Dm vs. HG, 0.8252±0.48 vs. 0.2850±0.16, p=0.0543), but not in Dl (Dl vs. HG, 0.3846±0.12 vs. 0.2850±0.16, p=0.9829) (Fig. 6D). We further verified the protein expression of VEGFA (Fig. 6E) and CD31 (Fig. 6F) in indicated groups by Western blot. Detailed statistical analysis of protein expression guided us to draw identical conclusion that hUCB-MNCs are capable of restoring angiogenesis through escalating VEGFA and CD31 expression in a dose dependent manner.

Expression of ROS responsive marker PTPN4

Although mechanical ventilation is essential and lifesaving in clinical practice of BPD, the detrimental effect of oxygen toxicity accompanied by oxidative stress produces large amount of free oxygen radicals secondary to hyperoxia exposure, which aggravates developmental lung injury. Further, oxygen sensors are highly correlated with the homeostasis of T cell energetic metabolism. We therefore focused on markers that act as responsive mediator of reactive oxygen species (ROS) as well as T cell effector signaling. Among which PTPN4 (a widely expressed non-receptor protein tyrosine phosphatase) has come into the center of stage. Interestingly, in consistency with the our previous HTS analysis (Supplementary Table S1 and Fig. 7A). Immuno-histochemistry analysis revealed that strikingly declined mRNA expression of PTPN4 (HG vs. NG, 0.2776±0.16 vs. 2.364±0.34, p=0.0066) induced by hyperoxia exposure was uniquely reversed in Dh (Fig. 7B, 7C). Dh vs. HG, 1.852±0.36 vs. 0.2776±0.16, p=0.015, but not in Dl and Dm (Dl vs. HG, 0.2555±0.12 vs. 0.2776±0.16, p=0.9999; Dm vs. HG, 0.5809±0.48 vs. 0.2776±0.16, p=0.5641). Further validation of qPCR also supported the notion above (Fig. 7D).

Discussion

Clinically, the hyperoxia is one of many contributors to BPD, although other stimulus (i.e. inter alia baro- and volu-trauma from mechanical ventilation, and single or multiple types of infection) might also be considered. While oxygen exposure is the optimal, if not best, method to readily and feasibly recapitulate the pathological hallmarks of BPD at preclinical rodent animal model (representative mice)：disturbance to the gas exchange structure, cumulatively reflected by a change in total number of alveoli, alveolar density and gas exchange surface area, as well as thickening of the alveolar septal walls. In our study, prolonged exposure of newborn C57BL6/J mice to hyperoxia (85% O2) for 14 days increased mortality, retarded growth, and developed lung injuries similar to those seen in the extreme preterm human infants with BPD, exhibiting decreased alveolarization as evidenced by significantly increased MCL and decreased RAC at P28.

Accumulating evidence has suggested that human MSCs can possess the potential to facilitate tissue repair and stimulate lung maturation and improve outcomes under hyperoxia-induced BPD. But translation of MSC at clinical level are not readily applied due to the higher level of quality control and release criteria for UC-MSCs in neonatal manipulation than that of the utility in adulthood as well as the inadequate establishment of general standards in assessing ways of delivery. Considering the side effects that different MSC products display varying levels of highly pro-coagulant tissue factor (TF) and may adversely trigger the instant blood-mediated inflammatory reaction (IBMIR) (22). Suitable strategies for assessing and controlling hemocompatibility and optimized cell delivery are crucial for the development of safer and more effective MSC therapies (23). MNCs are optimal alternatives in stem cell-based therapies, especially CB-MNCs, which is a plentiful source of stem cell reservoir containing a high level of primitive multipotent stem cells (HSC), endothelial progenitor cells, and lymphocytes including regulatory T cells and NK cells as well as canonical differentiated monocytes (24). Second, CB-MNCs can be used for autologous transplantation and can be collected at birth and there exist no ethical issues with the collection. Third, UCBCs, with low immunogenicity are less susceptible to immune responses after transplantation and have no tumorigenicity (25).

The optimal route for stem cell transplantation in a lung injury has not been determined yet. Intravenous (IV) stem cell delivery for regenerative tissue therapy has been increasingly used at both experimental and clinical level. Data have shown that intravenous infusion of stem cells does not yield a large number of cells reaching the target organ primarily due to the reason that majority of stem cells resided in lung (26). While strategies for local stem cell delivery increase risks and side effects such as bleeding and tissue injury (tracheal rupture). There are only two studies disclosed which routine can appropriately delivery MNCs to neonatal mice in alleviating experimental BPD at animal level while both the studies take Rats as model animal and the experimental procedure that mimic hyperoxia BPD varies (27, 28). While intratracheal transplantation of stem cells (especially MNC or MSC) in treating pulmonary disease is more often used in experimental adult mice or rat. In this study, we used mice as experimental model and local IT injection induced occlusion associated with mice airway diameter size and inappropriate cell dosage administration could not be omitted.

There lies the foundation that routinely IV transfusion proceed safer and faster in clinical trials as previous studies regarding placental and umbilical cord derived stem cell summarized (29). It is worthwhile to launch an independent study to determine the optimal routine in MNC administration using different animal model.

As displayed above, we demonstrate that intravenous administration of hUCB-MNCs to hyperoxia-exposed mice had significant effects on stimulating alveolarization, promoting tissue repair, and alleviating pro-inflammatory responses with substantial evidence (Fig. 3, 5). Also, hUCB-MNCs transplantation exhibited strong capacity of enhancing the overall lung motion function and maintaining pulmonary-vascular flow homeostasis. Strikingly, hUCB-MNCs treatment at a dosage of 3×106 cells/kg exhibited the largest capacity of enhancing the overall lung motion function and maintaining pulmonary-vascular flow homeostasis according to the lung morphology upon hyperoxia damage (Fig. 4). Notably, the maximum dosage were infused intravenously due to technical limitations, the supreme concentration of purified hUCB-MNCs is 1×108 cells/mL, and 3 μL is the tallest volume that could be given intravenously to newborn mice pups at P7 that weighed an average of 2.85±0.36 g in HG. The maximum number of cells feasible for intravenous infusion in a newborn mice pup was 3×106 cells. In our previous study, the intravenous infusion cells localized in the lung tissue at P28 was positively associated with the protective effects against hyperoxia-induced lung injuries (10). In the present study, the congruency of pulmonary phenotypes (Fig. 2, 3) and molecular evidence reflecting the dose-dependent residence of infused hUCB-MNCs (Fig. 5, 6) are a strong support of the notion that alleviating effects of hUCB-MNCs towards BPD are largely dependent on its low immunogenicity and high clonal forming capacity, which are consistent with the similar examination conducted in murine AD model (30). Therefore, these evidences indicate that improvement of hyperoxia-induced neonatal lung injuries relates to hUCB-MNCs administered, and that intravenous infusion of 3×106 cells/kg seems to be the possible dose to maximize protective effects in the hyperoxia induced model and setting, at least in our context.

Inflammation is generally considered to be detrimental in recovery from hyperoxia induced lung injuries, but single use of anti-inflammatory treatments targeting specific inflammatory mediators have yet been ineffective (31). We have detected the factors regarding inflammatory regulation (Fig. 5). Interestingly, significant increase in IL-1β, IL-6 along with decreased in IL-10 and IL-2 observed in HG support the assumption that inflammatory responses mediated by inflammatory cytokines play an important role in the development of BPD (32-34), the unexpected changes in TNF-α level are explainable since CB are impaired to produce IL-12 and IL-15,contributed to the decrease in TNF-α (35). Hyperoxia-induced increased pro-inflammatory factor and decreased anti-inflammatory factors were reshaped in Dl, Dm and Dh, while Dh exhibited the strongest counterbalancing effect after hUCB-MNCs infusion. The escalated VEGF and CD31 expression after hUCB-MNCs treatment implied the efficiency of hUCB-MNCs in rescuing the devastative developing lung in experimental BPD model through remodeling of the endothelial systems. We assumed that the combinatorial beneficial outcome of anti-inflammatory and vascular remodeling effect might be attributable to paracrine effects is similar to MSC, including trophic factor secretion and immunomodulation by cytokines (16). Collectively, these findings with regard to distinctive cytokines suggest that the intravenous infusion of 3×106 cells/kg might be the possible optimal dose to achieve effective inflammatory reshaping and vascular remodeling effects in the model.

As a classical member of non-receptor protein tyrosine phosphatase family member, PTPN4 have long been demonstrated as an independent prognostic marker of cancer progression and initiation, especially in digestive tract cancer. Intriguingly, the newly reported miR-34a-5p/PTPN4 axis have been demonstrated to be a ROS-mediated target in thyroid cancer model (36). miR-34a-5p a derivates of pre-miRNA miR-34a, which have been well documented as a key non-coding RNA in orchestrating angiogenesis targets (i.e. Notch and Ang1-Tie2) (37) and alveolarization targets (i.e. PDGFR) (38) of hyperoxia induced lung injury. The miRanda targets prediction also implied that PTPN4 is a target of miR-34a. In our research, we revealed that the BPD mice displayed the lowest level of PTPN4 upon hyperoxia exposure. After hUCB-MNCs infusion, significantly upregulated PTPN4 expression were detected in Dh but not in Dl and Dm group. Recently, interests have been shifted to the possible mechanisms of PTPN4 in hyperoxia/reoxygenation induced cell apoptosis (39) and nerve injury repair (40). Functional studies of PTPN4 in modulating BPD pathogenesis awaits further exploration in understanding the management of BPD and its most severe complications in nervous systems, possibly through the popular lung-brain axis (41). Once again, our study demonstrated the potential novel roles of PTPN4 in regulating hyperoxia induced lung injury through ROS induced vascular endothelial impairments.

The current knowledge that the primary drivers of BPD pathology are inﬂammation, oxidative stress, and parenchymal fibrosis are well accepted. BPD causes long-term issues like emphysema and pulmonary hypertension. Previous study indicates that MSC therapy can reduce inﬂammation, mitochondrial impairment, lung injury, and fibrosis in alleviating BPD. How other stem cell-based therapies (especially MNC) can recover the simplified alveolar and arrested saccular stage development from hyperoxia induced lung injury to normal alveolar structure remains obscure. More elaborate mechanistic investigations will better promoting the translation and wide application of hUCB-MNCs for human neonates with BPD, breaking the limitation of the current study.
