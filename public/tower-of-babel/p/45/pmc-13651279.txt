Cutaneous Larva Migrans on the Palm: An Unusual Site with Dermoscopic Correlation
Authors: Sejal Jain, Keshavamurthy Vinay
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

A 45-year-old female agricultural worker presented to the dermatology department for evaluation of a linear skin rash on her left palm of 3 months’ duration. The lesion progressed by a few millimeters every day and was associated with intense itching, disturbing her sleep. She had a history of harvesting flowers prior to the onset of lesions.

On cutaneous examination, there was a single serpiginous tract extending across the thenar eminence (Figure 1A). It was orangish-yellow distally and erythematous at the proximal end. The leading edge showed a small, crusted point corresponding to the larval entry site. Dermoscopy revealed the reddish-brown whiplash-like tunnel with surrounding erythema and scattered red dots and clods (Figure 1B). Focal yellowish-brown scaling and a terminal crusted point corresponding to the larval entry site was seen. A clinical and dermoscopic diagnosis of cutaneous larva migrans (CLM) was made.

(A) Single serpiginous tract extending across the thenar eminence. (B) Dermoscopy revealing a reddish-brown whiplash-like tunnel with peripheral erythema, scattered red dots (yellow arrow), and a crusted terminal entry point (black box). (Dermlite DL4, polarised mode, 10×). (C) Post treatment clinical image showing resolution of signs of inflammation (erythema, hemorrhagic crusting) following treatment.

Also known as creeping eruption, CLM is a neglected zoonotic tropical disease caused by the accidental percutaneous penetration of animal hookworm larvae, most commonly Ancylostoma braziliense and A. caninum.1 Although typical sites include the feet, buttocks, and thighs, palmar involvement is rare. Dermoscopy serves as a rapid, noninvasive adjunct for confirmation.2 Our patient was treated with a single oral dose of ivermectin (200 µg/kg), resulting in complete resolution of the lesion and pruritus within 5 days. Dermoscopically, there was disappearance of the red dots, clods and erythema; the remnant tunnel was visible (Figure 1C).

Recognition of CLM in atypical locations is crucial to avoid misdiagnosis as scabies or phytophotodermatitis.
