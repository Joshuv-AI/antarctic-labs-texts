DNA-dependent protein kinase catalytic subunit (DNA-PKcs) drives angiotensin II-induced vascular remodeling through regulating mitochondrial fragmentation
Authors: Litao Wang, Lin Wu, Yuxin Du, Xiang Wang, Bingsheng Yang, Shuai Guo, Yuan Zhou, Yiming Xu, Shuofei Yang, Yingmei Zhang, Jun Ren
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDNA-dependent protein kinase catalytic subunit (DNA-PKcs) is a novel instigator for mitochondrial dysfunction, and plays an important role in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular diseases. However, the role and mechanism of DNA-PKcs in angiotensin II (Ang II)-induced vascular remodeling remains obscure.MethodsRat aortic smooth muscle cells (SMC) and VSMC-specific DNA-PKcs knockout (DNA-PKcsΔVSMC) mice were employed to examine the role of DNA-PKcs in vascular remodeling and the underlying mechanisms. Blood pressure of mice was monitored using the tail-cuff and telemetry methods. The role of DNA-PKcs in vascular function was evaluated using vascular relaxation assessment.ResultsIn the tunica media of remodeled mouse thoracic aortas, and renal arteries from hypertensive patients, elevated DNA-PKcs expression was observed along with its cytoplasmic translocation from nucleus, suggesting a role for DNA-PKcs in vascular remodeling. We then infused wild-type (DNA-PKcsfl/fl) and DNA-PKcsΔVSMC mice with Ang II for 14 days to establish vascular remodeling, and demonstrated that DNA-PKcsΔVSMC mice displayed attenuated vascular remodeling through inhibition of dedifferentiation of VSMCs. Moreover, deletion of DNA-PKcs in VSMCs alleviated Ang II-induced vasodilation dysfunction and hypertension. Mechanistic investigations denoted that Ang II-evoked rises in cytoplasmic DNA-PKcs interacted with dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) at its TQ motif to phosphorylate Drp1S616, subsequently promoting mitochondrial fragmentation and dysfunction, as well as reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. Treatment of irbesartan, an Ang II type 1 receptor (AT1R) blocker, downregulated DNA-PKcs expression in VSMCs and aortic tissues following Ang II administration.ConclusionOur data revealed that cytoplasmic DNA-PKcs in VSMCs accelerated Ang II-induced vascular remodeling by interacting with Drp1 at its TQ motif and phosphorylating Drp1S616 to provoke mitochondrial fragmentation. Maneuvers targeting DNA-PKcs might be a valuable therapeutic option for the treatment of vascular remodeling and hypertension.Keywords: DNA-PKcs, Vascular remodeling, Drp1, Mitochondrial fragmentation, Hypertension

Introduction

Vascular remodeling is a prominent pathological hallmark associated with hypertension and other vascular anomalies en route to multiple cardiovascular complications, including stroke, myocardial infarction, atherosclerosis, aortic aneurysm and dissection, and restenosis following stent placement [1,2]. Vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), predominant constitutents of vascular wall, are mainly responsible for vascular remodeling and vascular dysfunction [3]. In response to pathological injury, VSMCs exhibit prominent phenotypic plasticity and dedifferentiate from a quiescent ‘contractile’ state to an activated ‘synthetic’ state, along with pronounced cell proliferation and migration capacity [3]. Phenotypically modified VSMCs are central to vascular wall thickening, stenosis, and other pathological alterations during vascular remodeling [4]. A better understanding of regulatory mechanisms underneath VSMCs-evoked vascular remodeling should hold great promises for timely diagnosis and treatment of vascular diseases.

DNA-dependent protein kinase (DNA-PK), composed of a large catalytic subunit (DNA-PKcs) and a regulatory factor Ku70/80 heterodimer, is activated upon DNA double-stranded breaks (DSBs) [5] or reactive oxygen species (ROS) [6]. DNA-PKcs usually resides within the nucleus although it is also scattered across cytoplasm, mitochondria, lysosomes, and Golgi apparatus [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. Despite its “trademark” as a DNA DSB sensor, recent evidence indicates that DNA-PKcs possesses unique properties beyond mediation of nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ) [12]. For example, DNA-PKcs phosphorylates transcription factor upstream stimulatory factor (USF)-1 to promote fatty acid synthesis evoked by insulin [13]. DNA-PKcs also contributes to type 2 diabetes and obesity by suppression of mitochondrial function and energy metabolism [10]. Findings from our group revealed that DNA-PKcs promoted cardiac ischemia reperfusion injury and metabolic liver damage through regulating mitochondrial homeostasis and mitophagy [14,15]. Nonetheless, the etiological nature of DNA-PKcs in angiotensin II (Ang II)-induced vascular remodeling remains elusive.

Emerging evidence has suggested a prominent role for mitochondria, the energy core of VSMCs, in vascular remodeling via various mechanisms [16]. Thereinto, inhibition of excessive mitochondrial fragmentation suppresses VSMCs proliferation and migration, subsequently attenuating excess intimal hyperplasia and pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) [17,18]. Mitochondrial fragmentation is usually triggered by aberrant mitochondrial fission mediated by cytoplasmic translocation of dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) to membrane surface of mitochondria to instigate its phosphorylation [19,20]. Inhibition of Drp1 through pharmacological or genetic manipulation has displayed promises for the management of Ang II-induced vascular remodeling [21,22], albeit with poorly clarified mechanisms of action. DNA-PKcs, as a serine/threonine protein kinase with more than 700 substrates of phosphorylation [23] and at least 26 protein phosphorylation targets in cytoplasm [24], is turned on by stressors such as aging and irradiation [10,25]. Accordingly, we hereby hypothesized that DNA-PKcs modulates Drp1-mediated mitochondrial fragmentation to participate in Ang II-induced vascular remodeling.

Evidence from our current work showed that Ang II exposure led to DNA-PKcs translocation into the cytoplasm, resulting in excessive mitochondrial fragmentation in VSMCs by directly interacting with Drp1 to promote S616-site phosphorylation both in vivo and in vitro. VSMC-specific deletion of DNA-PKcs attenuated pathological phenotypes of vascular remodeling in Ang II-infused mice. Moreover, Ang II receptor blockers (ARBs) were identified as DNA-PKcs inhibitor and thus DNA-PKcs inhibition could contribute to the anti-vascular remodeling and antihypertensive responses of irbesartan. In sum, these data unveiled an anti-vascular remodeling feature for irbesartan and identify DNA-PKcs as a potential therapeutic target against Ang II-induced vascular remodeling and hypertension.

Materials and methods

The sources of reagents and detailed methods can be found in the Supplemental Materials.

Animals

Mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) animal room free access to water and normal chow on a 12:12-h light: dark cycle with suitable room temperature and proper humidity (45–65%). All animal experiments and care were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhongshan Hospital Fudan University in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Human tissue samples

All experimental procedures using human tissues conformed to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and the research protocol was approved by Institutional Review Board of Renji Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (No. KY2021-105). All patients or their proxies provided written informed consent prior to specimen collection. Renal artery was obtained from brain-dead organ-donor patients with or without hypertension within 4–6 h immediately following death. Tissues collected were immediately stored at 4% paraformaldehyde or liquid nitrogen as required.

Statistical analyses

The GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA) was used for statistical analyses. All data were presented as mean ± SEM. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Statistical significance between two groups was analyzed using unpaired Student's t-test. When more than two groups were compared, one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's or Bonferroni's post-hoc test was used. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

DNA-PKcs expression is increased in Ang II-induced vascular remodeling and arteries of hypertensive patients

To determine whether DNA-PKcs is involved in the pathogenesis of vascular remodeling, mice were infused with Ang II as previously described [26]. Pathological feature of thoracic aortas was assessed on day 7 and 14. An increase in medial thickness, adventitial collagen content and external elastic laminar (EEL) perimeter was observed on day 14 (Fig. 1A and B), accompanied by an overtly elevated systolic blood pressure (SBP) (Fig. 1C). Immunohistochemical staining noted upregulated DNA-PKcs protein in thoracic aortas of Ang II-infused mice (Fig. 1D). Similar results were also found in aortic tissues using Western blot analysis (Fig. 1E). Notably, DNA-PKcs expression was increased in VSMCs of renal arteries from hypertensive patients (Fig. 1F and G). Anthropometric characteristics of hypertensive patients and normotensive subjects were presented in Table S1. These results indicate the possible involvement of DNA-PKcs in the pathogenesis of vascular remodeling and hypertension-related arterial pathology in human.

VSMC-specific deletion of DNA-PKcs protects against Ang II-induced vascular remodeling and hypertension in mice

To investigate whether deletion of DNA-PKcs affects the progression of vascular remodeling, VSMC-specific DNA-PKcs knockout mice (DNA-PKcsΔVSMC) were generated by crossing DNA-PKcs-Loxp (DNA-PKcsfl/fl) mice with Tagln-Cre/ERT2 mice (Fig. 2A). Western blots showed reduced DNA-PKcs protein level in aortas, but not hearts of DNA-PKcsΔVSMC mice following tamoxifen stimulation (Fig. S1A). In addition, we examined histological alterations in several organs (heart, liver and kidney) of DNA-PKcsΔVSMC mice and failed to identify any discernible abnormalities (Fig. S1B). Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay and Western blot analysis of phospho-H2AX (γ-H2AX) also indicated subtle DNA damage in aortas in the face of DNA-PKcs deficiency (Figs. S1C and S1D). Following the experimental protocol indicated in Fig. 2B, Ang II infusion was applied in DNA-PKcsfl/fl and DNA-PKcsΔVSMC mice for 14 days. In DNA-PKcsfl/fl mice, picrosirius red staining indicated increased adventitial collagen content in thoracic aortas following Ang II infusion. Similar effects were observed using hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining for detection of medial thickness and elastin van Gieson (EVG) staining to evaluate EEL perimeters. However, all these detrimental effects were mitigated in DNA-PKcsΔVSMC mice (Fig. 2C and D). Moreover, DNA-PKcsfl/fl mice treated with Ang II exhibited increased SBP, whereas this phenotype was attenuated in DNA-PKcsΔVSMC mice (Fig. 2E). Taking advantage of a 24-h telemetry monitoring device, DNA-PKcs deficiency was found to dramatically alleviate Ang II-induced hypertension (Fig. S2A). Meanwhile, immunofluorescence results demonstrated more pronounced vascular remodeling in Ang II-infused DNA-PKcsfl/fl group, and such pathological change was reversed in the DNA-PKcsΔVSMC mice receiving Ang II (Fig. 2F). These findings highlight that DNA-PKcs deficiency in VSMCs protects against Ang II-induced vascular remodeling and hypertension.

To further explore whether antihypertensive property of DNA-PKcs deletion is mediated through alterations in vascular function, the mesenteric arteries of DNA-PKcsfl/fl and DNA-PKcsΔVSMC mice were isolated, to evaluate vascular function ex vivo. Contraction-relaxation curves were constructed in response to phenylephrine (PE), acetylcholine (ACh), and sodium nitroprusside (SNP) to assess alterations in vasoconstriction and vasodilation. We observed that VSMC-dependent vasodilation in response to SNP was slightly increased in DNA-PKcsΔVSMC mice compared with DNA-PKcsfl/fl mice. However, little differences were noted for PE-envoked vasoconstriction or endothelium-dependent vasodilation in response to ACh between DNA-PKcsfl/fl and DNA-PKcsΔVSMC mice at baseline (Figs. S2B–S2D). Notably, DNA-PKcs deficiency dramatically alleviated Ang II-induced vasodilation defect, without affecting PE-induced vasoconstriction (Fig. 2G–I). These data reveal that DNA-PKcs deletion inhibits Ang II-evoked hypertension through mitigating vasodilation dysfunction.

Pharmacological inhibition of DNA-PKcs with NU7441 ameliorates progression of Ang II-induced vascular remodeling and hypertension

To discern whether inhibition of DNA-PKcs ameliorates Ang II-induced vascular remodeling, NU7441, a specific inhibitor of DNA-PKcs, was employed in Ang II-infused mice [8]. Animals were randomly divided into three groups namely saline, Ang II, Ang II + NU7441 (Fig. S3A). As expected, Ang II-elevated SBP levels were dropped by approximately 20 mmHg in Ang II + NU7441 group evaluated using a noninvasive tail-cuff method (Fig. S3B) and the 24-h telemetry monitoring (Fig. S3C), without changes in body weight and kidney weight/tibial length (TL) ratio (Fig. S3D). Meanwhile, NU7441 treatment significantly alleviated Ang II-induced vascular remodeling, manifested by decreased collagen content, medial/adventitial thickness, and EEL perimeters (Figs. S3E–S3G). Interestingly, staining of heart tissues with collagen dye picrosirius red and HE indicated that Ang II-infused mice developed structural alterations, most notably as cardiac hypertrophy and interstitial fibrosis, accompanied with increased heart weight/TL ratio. These pathological structural alterations were blunted by NU7441 (Figs. S3D and S3H), suggesting promises of targeting DNA-PKcs for Ang II-induced cardiac remodeling. These results strongly support a significant role for DNA-PKcs in the development of vascular remodeling and hypertension.

Ang II activates DNA-PKcs to promote the dedifferentiation of VSMCs

Given the critical role of Ang II in vascular remodeling and hypertension, whether Ang II regulated DNA-PKcs expression in VSMCs was examined. In cultured VSMCs, Ang II exposure markedly upregulated DNA-PKcs expression (Fig. 3A). To further determine the Ang II receptor subtype mediating Ang II-evoked effects on DNA-PKcs expression, RNH-6270 and PD123319 were employed to block Ang II type 1 and type 2 receptors (AT1R and AT2R) [27], respectively. As shown in Fig. 3B, RNH-6270 treatment abolished Ang II-induced alteration in DNA-PKcs expression, suggesting a vital role for AT1R in Ang II-provoked DNA-PKcs expression.

Since VSMC dedifferentiation is a critical event leading to the pathogenesis of vascular remodeling [28], we next investigated possible involvement of DNA-PKcs. Ang II treatment promoted VSMC proliferative phenotype manifested by cell counting and WST-1 proliferation assays, and unregulated proliferative markers including cyclin D1 and proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA). DNA-PKcs knockdown itself did not aggravate DNA damage and influence cell proliferation, although it significantly inhibited Ang II-induced rises in proliferation of VSMCs (Figs. S4A–S4D, Fig. 3C and D). Consistently, the number of Ki67-positive VSMCs in the cross-sections of thoracic aortas was significantly decreased in DNA-PKcsΔVSMC mice compared with DNA-PKcsfl/fl controls (Fig. 3E). In addition, DNA-PKcs deficiency markedly attenuated Ang II-induced VSMC migration manifested by the Boyden chamber assay (Fig. 3F).

Of note, lamellipodia is actin-mediated membrane protrusions at the leading edge of polarized cells, which is a characteristic feature of migrating cells [17]. We then evaluated lamellipodia in Ang II-challenged VSMCs with or without DNA-PKcs knockdown. Inhibition of DNA-PKcs significantly suppressed Ang II-induced lamellipodia formation as indicated by phalloidin staining (Fig. 3G). In parallel, protein levels of the VSMC contractile markers including alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), calponin and smooth muscle protein 22-alpha (SM22α), were significantly decreased in dedifferentiated VSMCs but they were rescued by DNA-PKcs deletion (Fig. 3H). Next, NU7441 was employed to pharmacologically inhibit DNA-PKcs, and our data noted that Ang II-induced proliferative and migrative phenotypes of VSMCs were blocked by NU7441 treatment (Figs. S4E–S4H). Collectively, these data insinuate a causal role for DNA-PKcs in the switching of VSMCs from a contractile to a proliferative phenotype in response to Ang II.

Ang II activates DNA-PKcs, inducing it to translocate from the nucleus into the cytoplasm in vitro and in vivo

Previous studies have found that DNA-PKcs translocates from nucleus into cytoplasm in response to pathological insults [9,29]. Western blot analysis showed a prominent nuclear localization of DNA-PKcs upon non-stressed condition and a time-dependent rise in cytosolic DNA-PKcs in VSMCs treated with Ang II (Fig. 4A), with an elevation in the total DNA-PKcs protein abundance (Fig. 3A). To verify whether DNA-PKcs cytoplasmic translocation occurred in VSMCs specifically, human aortic endothelial cells (HAECs) were subjected to Ang II exposure. However, Ang II failed to cause DNA-PKcs translocation into cytoplasm from nucleus (Fig. S5). Immunofluorescence assay of VSMCs also demonstrated the nuclear export of DNA-PKcs following Ang II stimulation (Fig. 4B). Consistent with these results, the effects were also observed in mouse aortas infused with Ang II (Fig. 4C).

Since abnormal DNA cytosolic release is reported to trigger DNA-PKcs redistribution from nucleus into the cytoplasm [25,30], we performed the immunofluorescence assays using a DNA antibody. As shown in Fig. 4D, cytosolic DNA was undetectable in normal VSMCs, however, Ang II evoked release of DNA fragments into cytoplasm. Moreover, the transcription of mRNAs encoding nuclear proteins, such as Ptbp1 and Nup35, was elevated in aortic tissues from mice subjected to vascular remodeling or in the cytosol of VSMCs exposed to Ang II (Fig. 4E). As expected, ELISA analysis revealed that DNA-PKcs kinase activity was also increased following Ang II challenge both in vivo and in vitro (Fig. 4F).

To assess the underlying clinical relevance of nucleus-to-cytoplasm translocation of DNA-PKcs in hypertensive vascular remodeling, protein levels in renal arteries from hypertensive patients were analyzed. Western blot analysis indicated evident DNA-PKcs nuclear export in hypertensive patients (Fig. 4G). Overall, Ang II exposure and hypertension activate DNA-PKcs, resulting in its translocation into cytoplasm in vitro and in vivo.

Deletion of DNA-PKcs retarded vascular remodeling through alleviating mitochondrial dysfunction

To explore the mechanisms of action underlying DNA-PKcs deficiency-mediated anti-vascular remodeling effects, we performed RNA sequencing for comparison of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using thoracic aortas from DNA-PKcsfl/fl and DNA-PKcsΔVSMC mice after saline or Ang II infusion (Fig. 5A). Compared with saline-infused DNA-PKcsfl/fl mice, Ang II infusion provoked upregulation of 2212 genes in aortas, including Prkdc encoding DNA-PKcs (Table S3). Moreover, thoracic aortas from Ang II-infused DNA-PKcsΔVSMC mice displayed 190 upregulated and 1176 downregulated DEGs compared with those from DNA-PKcsfl/fl mice. Biological process description of Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis illustrated that these DEGs were primarily enriched in mitochondrial function and membrane management, including mitochondrial fission, mitochondrial membrane organization, and ROS metabolic process (Fig. 5B, Table S4). Thus, genes regulating mitochondrial function and dynamics were significantly impacted by DNA-PKcs deletion in vascular remodeling. Notably, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) demonstrated that the ROS metabolic process was inhibited in remodeled aortas from DNA-PKcsΔVSMC mice (Fig. 5C). We next performed dihydroethidium (DHE) staining in thoracic aortas and found that Ang II infusion significantly facilitated ROS production, the response of which was diminished by DNA-PKcs deficiency (Fig. 5D). NADPH oxidase 2 (NOX2) and p47phox mRNA levels in aortas were markedly increased following Ang II infusion, the effects were attenuated in DNA-PKcsΔVSMC mice (Fig. 5E). In line with these findings, MitoSOX fluorescence showed that Ang II-induced boost in mitochondrial ROS generation in VSMCs was suppressed by DNA-PKcs deletion (Fig. 5F). Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was also stabilized by DNA-PKcs small interfering RNA (siDNA-PKcs) transfection in Ang II-treated VSMCs in vitro (Figs. S6A and S6B). Using Seahorse flux analysis, Ang II exposure was found to suppress basal oxygen consumption rate (OCR), adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis-linked OCR, maximal OCR and spare respiratory capacity, along with an obvious drop in ATP production, denoting compromised mitochondrial bioenergetic function. However, the Ang II-evoked anomalies were partially diminished by DNA-PKcs knockdown (Fig. 5G–I). Additionally, we further used the mitochondrially targeted antioxidant (MitoTEMPO) to eliminate mitochondrial ROS and found that VSMC phenotypic switching was partially reversed by MitoTEMPO (Fig. 5J), suggesting that DNA-PKcs-mediated mitochondrial ROS accumulation provoked VSMC dedifferentiation. Together, these data indicate that DNA-PKcs deletion suppresses Ang II-induced vascular remodeling through alleviation of mitochondrial dysfunction.

DNA-PKcs phosphorylates Drp1S616 and promotes mitochondrial fragmentation

Disarrangement in mitochondrial morphology is a determining factor of mitochondrial dysfunction [31,32]. As shown in Fig. S7, GSEA analysis revealed that mitochondrial fission was inhibited in aortic tissues from Ang II-infused DNA-PKcsΔVSMC mice. Further analysis of mitochondrial morphology showed that VSMCs exhibited normal mitochondria under non-stressed conditions, whereas Ang II-treated cells possessed pronounced fragmented mitochondria. Knockdown of DNA-PKcs using siRNA inhibited Ang II-induced mitochondrial fragmentation (Fig. 6A). In line with these findings, morphometric analysis of transmission electron microscopy images showed that Ang II infusion evoked overt mitochondrial fission, as manifested by reduced mitochondrial length and perimeter, in thoracic aortas from DNA-PKcsfl/fl mice, whereas these detrimental changes were reconciled in DNA-PKcsΔVSMC mice (Fig. 6B).

Mitochondrial fragmentation is often resulted from enhanced mitochondrial fission, which is modulated through the recruitment of Drp1 to mitochondrial outer membrane and phosphorylation of Drp1 [33,34]. Western blot analysis of aortic tissues depicted that Ang II increased mitochondrial localization of Drp1, the effects of which were greatly attenuated by DNA-PKcs deletion (Fig. 6C), indicating governance of Drp1 biological function by DNA-PKcs. To further determine whether DNA-PKcs affects Drp1 function, molecular docking was conducted through an online docking server HPEPDOCK [35]. The docking results suggested an intriguing interaction between the kinase domain of DNA-PKcs and the peptide residues of phosphorylation site in Drp1, and the 616-site serine of Drp1 (red) was one of the nearest sites likely binding to the catalytic loop of DNA-PKcs (purple), with a docking score of −171.4 (Fig. 6D). Sequence alignment indicated that the amino acids surrounding Drp1S616 were evolutionarily conserved (Fig. S8A). In vitro, the GST-Drp1WT protein, but not the mutant GST-Drp1S616A protein, was directly phosphorylated by purified DNA-PKcs protein (Fig. 6E). Consistently, Phos-tag gel analysis unequivocally confirmed an evident phosphorylation of Drp1WT by recombinant DNA-PKcs protein, while none phosphorylation of Drp1S616A was deciphered (Fig. S8B). Consequently, Western blot analysis and immunofluorescence staining indicated that pS616-Drp1 expression in thoracic aortas was significantly increased in Ang II-treated DNA-PKcsfl/fl mice, an effect that was reversed in DNA-PKcsΔVSMC mice (Figs. S8C and S8D). Similar findings were also observed in DNA-PKcs knockdown VSMCs following Ang II treatment in vitro (Figs. S8E and S8F). Consistent with these findings, Drp1 mainly diffused throughout the cytoplasm with little colocalization with mitochondria under non-stressed conditions. Nonetheless, more Drp1 puncta existed in the fragmented mitochondria in Ang II-treated VSMCs. Knockdown of DNA-PKcs by siRNA abrogated the Ang II-mediated localization of Drp1 to mitochondrial fractions (Fig. S8G).

To further explore whether DNA-PKcs-induced vascular dysfunction is mediated by Drp1, we assessed vascular function of Mdivi-1-treated DNA-PKcsΔVSMC mice implanted with an Ang II osmotic pump. Our results indicated that Drp1 inhibitor Mdivi-1 offered no additional benefits on PE-induced vasoconstriction and SNP-mediated vasodilation in DNA-PKcsΔVSMC mice, suggesting contribution of Drp1 inhibition to DNA-PKcs deficiency-conferred relief of vascular dysfunction. Interestingly, Mdivi-1 slightly although significantly improved ACh-mediated vasodilation, indicating participation of endothelial cells in Ang II-induced vascular dysfunction (Fig. 6F–H). These data suggest that Ang II-induced DNA-PKcs translocation promotes the localization of Drp1 to mitochondria and phosphorylates Drp1S616, leading to mitochondrial fragmentation and subsequently vascular dysfunction.

DNA-PKcs binds directly to Drp1 by identifying the TQ motif and facilitates mitochondrial fragmentation

To further investigate the mechanism that DNA-PKcs enhances Drp1 activity, we examined whether cytoplasmic DNA-PKcs directly binds to Drp1 and the impact of the interaction on mitochondrial fragmentation. Fluorescent microscopy investigation was used and revealed that DNA-PKcs was localized to mitochondria in VSMCs upon Ang II exposure (Fig. 7A). This finding was substantiated by Western blot analysis of aortic tissues from mice subjected to Ang II-induced vascular remodeling (Fig. 7B). Coimmunoprecipitation assays further indicated that endogenous DNA-PKcs interacted with Drp1 following Ang II exposure (Fig. 7C). Then, we examined the mechanism of DNA-PKcs interaction with Drp1. DNA-PKcs was reported to interact with protein(s) via identifying serine or threonine residues followed by glutamine (SQ/TQ) motif, resulting in protein phosphorylation [36,37]. Interestingly, we found an evolutionarily conserved SQ/TQ motif in Drp1 protein domain (Fig. 7D). To explore whether DNA-PKcs binds to Drp1 via SQ/TQ motif, we constructed various mutants, including Drp1 without Ser450 (HA-Drp1ΔS450), Drp1 without Thr451 (HA-Drp1ΔT451), Drp1 without Gln452 (HA-Drp1ΔQ452), or Drp1 without Thr451 and Gln452 (HA-Drp1ΔT451Q452). VSMCs with Drp1 knockdown were transfected with aforementioned mutants prior to Ang II challenge. As shown in Fig. 7E, loss of the TQ motif, but not Ser450, abolished the interaction of DNA-PKcs with Drp1. Consequently, phosphorylation of Drp1S616 upon Ang II challenge was ablated by TQ motif deficiency (Fig. 7F). Moreover, Ang II-induced mitochondrial fragmentation was abrogated by reconstitution of HA-Drp1ΔT451, HA-Drp1ΔQ452 or HA-Drp1ΔT451Q452, but not HA-Drp1 or HA-Drp1ΔS450 (Fig. 7G and H). To further determine whether the kinase activity of DNA-PKcs is indispensable for Drp1S616 phosphorylation and mitochondria fission, the specific DNA-PKcs inhibitor NU7441 was employed. In Ang II-stimulated VSMCs, NU7441 treatment inhibited DNA-PKcs-mediated Drp1S616 phosphorylation (Fig. S9A), as well as mitochondrial fission (Fig. S9B). Overall, TQ motif in Drp1 promotes its direct interaction with DNA-PKcs, thus facilitating Ang II-induced mitochondrial fragmentation.

Irbesartan suppresses DNA-PKcs expression and protects against vascular remodeling

To decipher whether the classical antihypertensive agents [Ang II receptor blockers (ARBs)] suppress DNA-PKcs levels, conventional anti-hypertensive drugs were examined to reveal the ability of ARBs in the downregulation of DNA-PKcs protein levels, especially irbesartan. Irbesartan suppressed DNA-PKcs expression by 70% in VSMCs upon Ang II stimulation (Fig. 8A). To further examine whether irbesartan affects DNA-PKcs level in the remodeled thoracic aortas in vivo, vascular remodeling mouse models were established followed by treatment with irbesartan via intragastric administration for 2 weeks (Fig. 8B). Morphologically, irbesartan markedly alleviated Ang II-induced adventitial collagen deposition, increased medial thickness and EEL perimeters (Fig. 8C and D), as well as SBP elevation (Fig. 8E). Moreover, treatment of irbesartan, an AT1R blocker [38], overtly decreased DNA-PKcs expression in aortic tissues in response to Ang II infusion (Fig. 8F). These data suggest that DNA-PKcs inhibition might be involved in the anti-vascular remodeling and anti-hypertensive effects of classical ARBs such as irbesartan.

Discussion

Emerging evidence has suggested an involvement of DNA-PKcs in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular diseases including cardiac ischemia perfusion injury [14] and hypoxic pulmonary hypertension [39]. However, little is known with regards to the role of DNA-PKcs in Ang II-induced vascular remodeling. Taking advantage of genetic deletion of DNA-PKcs in vivo and in vitro, our current results elaborated a critical role for DNA-PKcs in mediating VSMC dedifferentiation and consequently vascular remodeling, as summarized in Fig. 8G. This is supported by several salient findings as follows. First, total DNA-PKcs protein expression was increased and DNA-PKcs was translocated into cytoplasm from nucleus in Ang II-induced mouse model of vascular remodeling and hypertensive patients. Genetic deletion and pharmacological inhibition of DNA-PKcs attenuated vascular remodeling in vivo. Second, cytoplasmic DNA-PKcs appeared to interact with Drp1 by recognizing the TQ motif to evoke S616 phosphorylation of Drp1. Third, Drp1S616 phosphorylation contributed to mitochondrial fragmentation and ROS production, resulting in phenotypic switching of VSMCs and subsequently vascular remodeling. Fourth, the ARB irbesartan downregulated DNA-PKcs in remodeled thoracic aortas and alleviated vascular remodeling. Taken together, these findings strongly favor an essential role for DNA-PKcs nuclear export in Ang II-induced vascular remodeling involving Drp1S616 phosphorylation. Considering the availability of DNA-PKcs inhibitors in preclinical studies [40,41], our findings should offer promises for targeting DNA-PKcs in the management of vascular remodeling.

Global DNA-PKcs knockout mice exhibited lymphocyte deficiency [42]. To circumvent such issue, VSMC-specific DNA-PKcs knockout mice were used in our study. Our results found that DNA-PKcs deletion hampered Ang II-evoked vascular remodeling. Moreover, our data revealed that DNA-PKcs deficiency inhibited ROS production and partially rescued VSMC phenotypic switching in vitro. These results collectively denote a likely contribution for VSMC dedifferentiation in DNA-PKcs-elicited vascular remodeling. However, we failed to discern dampened cell proliferation following DNA-PKcs knockdown at baseline, which might result from incomplete knockout efficiency and the compensatory effects of Ataxia Telangiectasia Mutated (ATM) and ATM-and Rad3-related (ATR) [43]. Moreover, we examined anti-vascular remodeling effect of a specific DNA-PKcs partial inhibitor, NU7441, at a physiological dose in the absence of notable side-effect on B cell function [10]. As expected, our results showed that NU7441 markedly attenuated progression of vascular remodeling, and VSMC proliferative and migratory phenotypes. Of note, pharmacological inhibition of DNA-PKcs using NU7026 significantly attenuated PAH and neointima formation after wire-injury through suppressing neuron-derived orphan receptor (NOR1)-mediated VSMC proliferation [39,44]. These data indicated that targeting DNA-PKcs might be a new avenue for the treatment of vascular proliferative diseases, although DNA-PKcs played an important role in DNA quality control. Moreover, increased DNA damage and DNA-PKcs expression were observed in PAH and atherosclerotic plaque, implying the likely contribution of DNA repair to these pathological conditions [45,46]. Given that ROS triggers multiple DNA damage [47], it is tempting to speculate that excessive DNA injury in hypertension might trigger unchecked activation of DNA-PKcs, resulting in VSMC phenotypic switching and subsequently vascular remodeling. However, the possibility of DNA-PKcs blockade might affect other factors involved in vascular remodeling should not be excluded at this time. Interestingly, pathological cardiac remodeling induced by Ang II infusion was also partially removed following NU7441 administration. These findings indicated the capacity of DNA-PKcs in multi-organ remodeling, and suggested translational potential of NU7441 for the clinical tissue remodeling, although further research is needed.

Aside from its well-known role in two DNA damage response (DDR) pathways, DNA-PKcs acts as a serine/threonine kinase and controls a variety of biological processes in cells [48]. Except for nucleus, DNA-PKcs is known to localize within the cytoplasm where it regulates apoptosis [29], cytoskeletal reorganization [49], autophagy [50] and protein ubiquitination [51]. Moreover, DNA-PKcs is also localized in several organelles, such as mitochondria [10], lysosomes [11] and the Golgi complex [9]. In middle aged mice, DNA-PKcs was activated by aging to suppress AMP-dependent protein kinase (AMPK) phosphorylation, thereby inhibiting mitochondrial function and mitochondrial integrity [10]. In cancer cells, DNA-PKcs-mediated phosphorylation of nucleotide-sensing γ1 subunit of AMPK primed AMPK complex to lysosomal activation, thereby activating starvation-induced autophagy [11]. As for the Golgi apparatus, DNA-PKcs may be translocated from nucleus into cytoplasm upon doxorubicin exposure or oxidative stress. Of note, cytoplasmic DNA-PKcs induced Golgi dispersal throughout cytoplasm and apoptotic cell death by phosphorylating Golgi protein GOLPH3 [9]. However, precise molecular mechanism(s) underlying DNA-PKcs translocation into cytoplasm remains largely elusive. Previous studies have reported that DNA leakage from nucleus into cytoplasm in response to ultraviolet irradiation might trigger the nuclear export of DNA-PKcs [25,30]. In our study, we showed for the first time that increased cytoplasmic DNA-PKcs directly interacted with Drp1 to evoke its S616-site phosphorylation, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and VSMC dedifferentiation by way of excess mitochondrial fragmentation in the setting of vascular remodeling. Notably, a recent study uncovered that DNA-PKcs indirectly modulated mitochondrial damage including mitochondrial content and biogenesis declines through mitochondria-related AMPK pathways in aging [10]. Although both of the scenarios rely on DNA-PKcs kinase activity, mechanisms underlying the DNA-PKcs release into the cytoplasm remain to be further addressed.

Similar to ATM and ATR, two other important kinases involved in the cellular response to DNA damage, DNA-PKcs tends to target proteins including SQ/TQ motifs [36,37]. Nonetheless, DNA-PKcs may also phosphorylate substrates on serine or threonine followed by leucine, methionine or other aliphatic amino acids [52]. In this study, using the HA-Drp1 mutants, we identified that TQ motif of Drp1 was an evolutionarily conserved substrate for DNA-PKcs. Interestingly, the perceived interaction between DNA-PKcs and Drp1 likely promoted Drp1-evoked mitochondrial fragmentation and dysfunction (e.g., loss of mitochondrial membrane potential and ROS overproduction) following Ang II challenge. Consistent with our findings, previous studies suggested that platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) triggered cell migration by increasing mitochondrial fission and ROS production [17]. Furthermore, Drp1 silencing in glucose-infused hypothalamic tissue reduced mitochondrial ROS level along with ROS-mediated downstream metabolic signaling [53].

Data from our current study favored a role for DNA-PKcs as an inducer for Drp1 activation and mitochondrial fission during vascular remodeling. Drp1-mediated mitochondrial fission in VSMCs elicits a critical regulatory role in vascular diseases [17,18,54]. Wang and colleagues demonstrated both Drp1 inhibitor Mdivi-1 and siDrp1 prevented mitochondrial fission and reduced pulmonary arterial SMC proliferation, thus mitigating pulmonary hypertension [18]. Perturbation of mitochondrial fission by expressing the dominant-negative mutant Drp1-K38A or Drp1 silencing overtly suppressed VSMC migration, while transgenic mice expressing Drp1-K38A displayed markedly reduced neointima formation after femoral artery wire injury [17]. It is notable that deletion of macrophage Drp1 could also attenuate intimal thickening following vascular injury by nullifying macrophage-mediated inflammation [55]. These observations highlighted engagement of Drp1-dependent mitochondrial fission in the progression of pathological vascular remodeling. Furthermore, the contemporary mechanism behind Drp1 phosphorylation was ambiguous in vascular remodeling. Previous studies noted that PDGF stimulated Drp1 phosphorylation at serine 616 by extracellular signal-regulated protein kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2), while several kinases including Rho-associated protein kinase (ROCK), protein kinase C (PKC), cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1), and calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) may also phosphorylate Drp1S616, leading to mitochondrial fission [17,56]. Of note, previous report indicated that MEK/ERK signaling preserved glioblastoma radioresistance through sustained expression of DNA-PKcs [57]; while another study found that inhibition of ERK facilitated repair of DSBs through DNA-PKcs activation in etoposide-treated MCF7 cells [58]. Our results indicated that ERK phosphorylation was partially suppressed in Ang II-infused DNA-PKcsΔVSMC mice (data not shown). These data, nonetheless, favored an intricate interplay between DNA-PKcs and ERK signaling, although further study is needed to elaborate the precise mechanism of action. Collectively, our data revealed that inhibition of Drp1 activation via interrupting the DNA-PKcs/Drp1 protein interaction might be a potential therapeutic strategy to reconcile vascular remodeling.

ARBs, especially AT1R antagonists, have been shown to prevent vascular remodeling [59,60]. Here in our study, we noted the ability of ARBs, especially irbesartan, in suppressing DNA-PKcs expression in VSMCs in response to Ang II challenge, indicating the role of DNA-PKcs as a pharmacological target for conventional therapeutic interventions. Furthermore, through in vivo studies, we demonstrated that irbesartan reduced expression of DNA-PKcs in thoracic aortas, ultimately, attenuating Ang II-induced vascular remodeling and hypertension. These findings reveal that inhibition of DNA-PKcs might play an unreported key role in the anti-vascular remodeling pleiotropic effects of ARBs on VSMCs.

Our study suffers from several limitations. First and foremost, we have focused on VSMC phenotypic switching as the primary mechanisms of vascular remodeling. However, other mechanisms including VSMC inflammation, senescence and apoptosis should not be ignored in the setting of DNA-PKcs deficiency for modification of vascular remodeling. Moreover, pathological cardiac remodeling was attenuated to a moderate degree by NU7441. Further study is needed to consolidate treatment efficacy on cardiac geometry using cardiomyocyte-specific DNA-PKcs knockout mice. Finally, although our study provides a direct link between DNA-PKcs-triggered mitochondrial fragmentation and vascular remodeling in vivo and in vitro, whether this mechanism works in other vascular diseases warrants further clarification.

In conclusion, results presented here showed that increased DNA-PKcs expression and its translocation from nucleus into the cytoplasm evokes mitochondrial fragmentation and damage, and triggers VSMC phenotypic switching, contributing to Ang II-induced vascular remodeling and hypertension through phosphorylation of Drp1S616. More interestingly, the DNA-PKcs-inhibitory effect exerted by ARBs identifies DNA-PKcs as a promising target against Ang II-induced vascular remodeling and hypertension.
