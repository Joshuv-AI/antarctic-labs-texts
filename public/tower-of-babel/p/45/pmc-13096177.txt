Reliability of contactless vital sign measurement algorithms for use in drone-based mass casualty triage
Authors: İsmail Tayfur, Perihan Şimşek, Emine Cansu Akgül, Yusuf Şamil Ateş, Abdülkadir Gunduz, Mert Bal, Arda Üstübioğlu, Burcu Bayramoğlu, Selim Altinarik, Benjamin Ryan, Michael Jeff Donahoo, Aybüke Karakoç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn mass casualty incidents, the demand for healthcare services far exceeds existing capacity, underscoring the importance of triage. This study supports drone-based triage by developing and validating algorithms for non-contact vital sign measurement from drone footage. Heart rate was measured by extracting average frame colour values from RGB videos and applying signal processing. Respiratory rate was obtained by analyzing temperature changes in the nasal region. Body temperature was analyzed based on the maximum temperature values in the forehead region’ thermal images. For oxygen saturation measurement, a deep learning model trained with features extracted from thermal images. Thirty-seven participants (mean age: 29.7 ± 8.45 years; 64.9% male) were simultaneously recorded using a drone-mounted camera and monitored with a standard bedside reference monitor, with seven recordings obtained outdoors and 30 indoors. The videos were analyzed by creating 15-second segments for respiratory rate and 13-second segments for the other parameters, with 1-second shifts. The accuracy rates of the oxygen saturation, body temperature, heart rate, and respiratory rate measurement algorithms were 98.65%, 98.59%, 97.70%, and 85.22%, respectively, for indoor recordings, and 99.60%, 98.48%, 96.85%, and 82.83% for outdoor recordings. The mean differences between the image processing–based and reference measurements for indoor recordings were 1.26% for oxygen saturation, 0.19 °C for body temperature, -0.37 breaths/min for respiratory rate, and −0.31 beats/min for heart rate; for outdoor recordings, the corresponding mean differences were 0.34%, 0.27 °C, −0.52 breaths/min, and −0.38 beats/min, respectively. The vital sign measurement algorithms demonstrated strong performance and can be successfully integrated into drone-based triage and other remote monitoring systems.Keywords: Drone, Image processing, Photoplethysmography, Remote triage, Vital signsSubject terms: Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Disasters and other mass casualty incidents (MCIs) strain healthcare resources. They also pose safety risks to responders and require swift action to save lives. Accurate and rapid triage is crucial for maximising survival rates in MCIs by allocating limited medical resources efficiently to patients with the highest chance of survival1. Strong scientific evidence demonstrates that effective triage is vital for saving the lives of severely injured but salvageable patients in major incidents such as the Boston Marathon bombing (2013), the September 11 attacks (2001), and the Tokyo sarin gas attack (1995)2–4. However, there are barriers and challenges to the effective implementation of MCI triage, including ensuring scene safety, maintaining situational awareness, making rapid decisions, and providing emergency care under extreme conditions at the incident site5. Technological advances enable the overcoming of these challenges facing MCI triage by making remote triage (RT) possible and allowing it to become more efficient and effective6.

Remote triage is a medical assessment process often performed using tools such as telemedicine platforms, mobile applications and wearable devices. The emergence of remote triage has been driven by an increasing need for care among people with multiple morbidities and the elderly population, and by barriers such as working hours and geographical access to primary health care services (PHCs)6,7.

A recent literature review draws attention to the use of wearable healthcare devices, portable/wearable ultrasounds, and chemical sensors that measure vital signs in MCI prehospital settings where evacuation is limited and patients remain on the scene for a long time. The review reports that the integration of these technologies with remote triage and decision support systems (DSS) makes the triage process more objective and resource-oriented. However, it is stated that sensor-based remote triage applications are sensitive to noise and motion artifacts, have limitations in terms of energy and battery life, generally operate depending on multiple sensors, and that most of these technologies are still at the pilot or research level and lack field validation8.

Olivia et al. developed a wearable sensor- and artificial intelligence–supported remote triage system, and its accuracy rate was reported as 95%9. Similarly, Salman et al. developed an IoMT-supported sensor, text-based measurement, and artificial intelligence–based remote triage system for emergency care systems. The system was reported to have achieved a 93.5% success rate in terms of performance and accuracy metrics10. Today, drones or unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) are used for remote triage in disasters and emergencies. Jain et al. (2018) reported that drone and standard triage achieved 100% accuracy11. Other studies also found comparable accuracy rates12,13. However, in these studies, triage decisions were based only on watching drone camera images. However, with current technology, vital signs, triage’s key determinants, can now be extracted contactlessly from video images14.

Mösch et al. developed a drone-based triage system and reported an overall triage classification accuracy of 89%. In their system, heart rate (HR) was measured remotely using drone-captured images and remote photoplethysmography (rPPG), yielding a Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of 14.3. The authors noted that better results can be achieved when the subject’s face is continuously captured and well illuminated14. Huang et al. proposed a vision-based remote measurement method to estimate HR and respiration rate (RR) from facial videos captured by onboard cameras on unmanned vehicles and UAVs. In the study, the reported MAEs were 1.38 beats/min for HR and 1.62 breaths/min for RR15. Al-Naji et al. (2019) used drones and proposed a computer vision-based system to measure HR and respiratory rate (RR). As the study result, a good agreement and correlation with standard reference instruments was reported16.

Recent advances have demonstrated that vital signs can be measured remotely and contactlessly from drone-captured images using imaging-based technologies. The growing frequency and magnitude of MCIs around the globe have led to an urgent need for remote and contactless triage systems. However, a notable gap in the literature on integrated systems combining these capabilities to create a comprehensive remote triage system remains. Therefore, the aim of this study was to develop a system capable of measuring respiratory rate (RR), oxygen saturation (SpO₂), HR, and body temperature (BT) based on video image processing techniques to perform remote triage in disasters and other MCIs using drones.

Methods

Study population

In this prospective study, 37 healthy subjects were selected using a convenience sampling method. The eligibility criteria were willingness to participate, and being haemodynamically stable. The presence of a condition such as nausea or persistent cough, which may interfere with the video recording, was selected as an exclusion criterion.

Experimental design

Video recordings were obtained in indoor (n = 30) and outdoor (n = 7) environments (Fig. 1). To evaluate the accuracy of the non-contact vital sign measurement algorithms and video images were recorded while participants were continuously monitored using a bedside reference device. During the recordings, participants remained seated in a fixed position, and one-minute video segments were captured in both settings. Indoor recordings were performed under incandescent lighting, with the camera positioned at a 90° angle to the participants, 150–180 cm away. Outdoor recordings were made in natural sunlight. The drone was positioned approximately 5–6 m away from the participants.

Outdoor recordings.

Study devices

A drone camera was used to take video recordings for the vital signs’ detection. The drone camera was DJI Zenmuse H20T (with Thermal Camera), 23 × Hybrid Optical Zoom, 200 × Maximum Zoom, 20 MP 1 / 1.7 “CMOS Sensor, Video Resolution: 4K / 30fps. During the video images recordings, participants’ vital signs were measured with a bedside Pulse Oximetry Monitor. In the study, the extraction of vital signs from video images was performed using Lenovo 90RT0018TX/Legion T5 / INTEL Core I7-11700F/32GB RAM/512GB SSD/ WINDOWS 10 HOME Desktop Computer.

System design of proposed algorithms

The proposed framework’ system design was performed separately for HR, RR, oxygen saturation, and BT measurement algorithms. The first step was the selection and truncation of ROI in all algorithms (Fig. 2).

Design of proposed algorithms.

Heart rate detection algorithm

Video-based photoplethysmography (vPPG) was used for HR detection from video images. Heart rate detection with vPPG is based on the detecting changes in skin colour in response to changes during a cardiac cycle that are not visible to the naked eye in blood volume. ROI selection is performed to extract the areas where color changes are most evident and to filter out unwanted facial regions17. The Viola-Jones Algorithm was preferred for ROI detection, and the forehead region was selected as the ROI18.

RGB channels extraction from ROIs

In the development of the HR detection algorithm, it is necessary to examine the colour changes for all pixels within the ROIs in all video frames19. Therefore, as a preprocessing step, RGB values for all pixels of the ROIs were extracted from each frame of the video recordings, and these data were stored as a three-dimensional data matrix. Then, the average value of the RGB channels in each frame was calculated separately. As a result, for each frame, the colours in the ROI were represented by their corresponding average RGB values, and three time series based on the three channels were derived.

The green channel (G) was preferred for the acquisition of unprocessed PPG signals from the ROI defined in the study. This is because the wavelength of green light (~ 525 nm) produces higher signal changes at the skin surface in response to changes in blood volume. Therefore, it provides higher accuracy in rPPG compared to the red and blue channels20.

Noise removal

Signals generated by changes in blood volume are usually noisy and contain various artefacts such as motion, baseline drift, and interference from environmental light sources. Signal optimization techniques are applied to remove these artefacts and thereby isolate the PPG signal21. One of the most common of these techniques is temporal filtering22. Selecting the most suitable filtering algorithm improves the accuracy of vital sign detection and classification by enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)23. Heart rate measurement

For HR detection, a short-time Fourier transform (STFT) (Eq. 3) was applied using sliding windows on the single-channel signal. The STFT is an extension of the classical Fourier transform and allows the local spectral properties of the signal to be analysed as a function of time. In this method, only a certain part of the signal is selected with the help of a window function, and this window is shifted along the time axis on the signal. The spectral content of the signal in the time-frequency plane is obtained by applying the Fourier transform separately for each window25. In practice, the long-duration signal was divided into shorter segments of equal length, and the Fourier transform was applied to each segment independently. In this way, the frequency content of the signal was analysed along with its change over time. The spectrogram, which is the STFT output, was used to visualise the distribution of the dominant frequency components corresponding to the pulse signal over time and to perform HR estimation based on frequency analysis.

Oxygen saturation detection and signal processing

In SpO₂ measurements using image processing, infrared images are preferred because they allow the detection of tissue heterogeneity with very high sensitivity up to a depth of 1.5 cm. First, each video sequence was processed to detect the facial region, after which anatomically relevant regions were determined for signal extraction. Face detection was performed using the Viola–Jones algorithm. Facial movements were tracked between frames using OpenCV-based tracking functions to ensure spatial consistency throughout the recording.

Three Regions of Interest (ROIs) were selected: the forehead, left cheek, and right cheek. Regions of interest were defined based on geometric information obtained from the bounding box of the face and the facial landmarks. The ROI locations were defined with respect to the detected facial region and iteratively updated to preserve spatial consistency across consecutive frames. Before feature extraction, the ROI time series were processed using Eulerian Video Magnification. This allowed low-amplitude physiological variations associated with blood perfusion to become more distinguishable. The amplification factor was set to α = 120, with a frequency range of 0.4–4 Hz27. Standard OpenCV functions (VideoCapture, resize, cvtColor, and zoom) were used during video preprocessing.

After ROI stabilization and signal enhancement, the thermal recordings were segmented into fixed-length temporal windows, and features were computed within each window. Rather than feeding raw pixel values into the model, we summarized each ROI using a small set of descriptive statistics to reduce sensitivity to noise and motion. For every window and each ROI, we computed the mean and standard deviation of pixel intensities, as well as the variability of these quantities over time (i.e., the standard deviation of the window-wise means and the standard deviation of the window-wise standard deviations). As a result, a total of 12 input features (3 ROIs × 4 statistical features) were obtained for each segment. Prior to model training, all features were scaled using RobustScaler to reduce the impact of outliers, and reference SpO₂ values were normalized to a range of 0–1 to support stable regression learning.

Model architecture and training procedure

For SpO₂ estimation, a regression-based neural network approach was adopted, using extracted statistical features as direct input. For this purpose, instead of convolutional architectures that perform end-to-end learning from image data, a multilayer perceptron (MLP) structure was preferred, which can more explicitly model the relationships in the feature space. The model learns progressively more compressed representations of the physiological signal by processing the input vector consisting of 12 features across successive fully connected layers28.

The ReLU activation function was used in the hidden layers. Dropout optimization was implemented in the intermediate layers, particularly in the early layers of the network, to limit overfitting and improve generalization capability. For estimating continuous SpO₂ values, the output layer was defined as a single neuron with linear activation.

The Adam optimization algorithm was used in the training process. The learning rate, batch size, and training time were determined based on preliminary trials on training and validation data to ensure stable convergence. Mean Squared Error (MSE) was monitored as the loss function, and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) was used as an additional evaluation metric to monitor training performance. An early stop strategy was implemented to terminate training if the improvement in validation performance stalled.

Respiratory rate detection

Thermal images were used to measure RR. First, they were converted to grayscale using the cvtColor function in OpenCV and analyzed offline in Python. RR measurement had two stages: ROI detection and tracking temperature changes within the ROI. The nostrils region, where respiratory heat changes are visible, was identified using the facial landmark detection model proposed by Kuzdeuov et al. They developed a dataset for thermal-to-visual conversion and thermal-visual face recognition. To validate it, two models were trained: Histogram of Oriented Gradients and Support Vector Machines (HOG + SVM) and a U-Net-based deep learning model. In this study, the HOG + SVM model was used. The nose region was detected by selecting facial landmarks 30–3629. This allowed precise algorithmic identification of respiration-related temperature changes in thermal images.

Tracking temperature changes within the ROI

Following the detection process, the gray-level pixel values ​​of the nostril region were used as the primary signal source in the tracking process. Nostril tracking was performed using the cvMatchTemplate function in OpenCV version 2.4.530. Throughout the tracking process, the average temperature value of the nostril region was calculated in each frame, and the time-dependent variation of this value was analyzed to obtain a periodic breathing signal. The average temperature of the rectangular ROIs was determined by taking the arithmetic mean of the intensity values of all pixels within this region. The obtained average temperature values were plotted against the time axis to create a time series corresponding to breathing.

To determine the number of breaths from the breathing signal, the obtained time series was filtered with a 5th-order digital Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency of 1 Hz. This filter has a cutoff frequency low enough to eliminate high-frequency interference in the signal, but high enough to allow the passage of frequency components up to 60 breathing cycles per minute. The time intervals (T seconds) between the peaks of each respiratory cycle in the filtered signal were automatically determined by the software. A cycle duration was obtained by averaging these time intervals; the number of breaths was calculated in terms of breaths per minute by multiplying the inverse of this average duration by 6030.

Body temperature detection

The face region was defined as the ROI for BT detection. Face detection was performed using a pre-trained Haar Cascade Classifier (HCC) based on Haar-like features provided by the OpenCV library. In this process, the source code was adapted and implemented according to the study requirements based on the OpenCV reference implementation. The Haar Cascade Classifier identifies facial structures by exploiting local intensity contrasts between adjacent rectangular regions in the image. In practice, this feature allows the model to capture characteristic patterns created by brightness transitions, such as between the periorbital region and the cheek regions. The classifier is trained on labeled image sets to distinguish these patterns through the presence or absence of Haar-like features, following the principles of the Viola-Jones framework31.

In infrared images, each pixel value does not directly correspond to an absolute temperature value. However, regardless of the complexity of environmental factors, the pixels within the same image maintain their consistency and retain the same proportional factor relative to the original thermal radiation32. Based on this principle, the images obtained from the infrared camera were converted into grayscale format using the OpenCV library, and the analysis was conducted based on this normalized temperature distribution. Heatmap and thresholding

Grayscale images were converted to temperature maps, as temperature maps provide a clearer and more interpretable visualisation of the thermal distribution compared to the original infrared images32. In this transformation, the intensity value of each pixel was mapped to its visual equivalent colour scale, making temperature gradients more apparent. Thresholding was then applied to identify potential body regions. In this process, regions above 28 °C, which is considered the limit of severe hypothermia, were defined as ROIs33. Thus, only thermal regions potentially corresponding to BT were included in the analysis, reducing the influence of environmental interference.

Contour detection and temperature calculation

OpenCV was used to extract contours from the thresholded binary image in order to identify potential BT regions. Each contour’s average temperature was determined and contrasted with a predetermined BT threshold. Contours above this limit were marked as possible fever regions. To confirm if the detected temperature belonged to the face, the overlap between each contour and the face bounding box was checked. The Intersection over Union (IoU) was defined as the ratio of the overlap area to the union area. If IoU exceeded 0.5, the temperature value was accepted as valid for the face region34 (Fig. 3).

Data and statistical methods

. In one participant’s video, extreme values were detected from the reference device during the first seven seconds of measurement. Therefore, the first seven seconds of the relevant participant’ video were excluded from the analysis, and statistical analysis was performed on the remaining data. 15 s video sections were used for RR measurements, and 13 s for other vital parameters. These sections were created with 1-second shifts using the sliding window method. In this context, statistical analyses for indoor recordings were performed on 1,373 video segments for RR and 1,433 video segments for the other vital parameters, while for outdoor recordings the corresponding numbers were 322 and 336, respectively.

Descriptive statistics were reported as number, percentage, mean, standard deviation, minimum, median, and maximum. Bland-Altman analysis assessed the agreement between algorithm-based measurements and the bedside monitor. Agreement between algorithm-based and reference vital signs measurements was evaluated using linear mixed-effects models. This approach accounts for correlated error structures introduced by repeated measurements within individuals while preserving statistical efficiency35. Model assumptions for the linear mixed-effects models were assessed through visual inspection of Q–Q plots to evaluate normality and by examining residual-versus-fitted plots to identify potential bias. Correlation tests were used to analyse relationships between reference and test measurements. MSE, RMSE, and MAE values were calculated for the algorithms. All analyses were performed in SPSS v27. Error and accuracy rates were computed using the given formulae.

Ethical approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the Hamidiye Scientific Research Ethics Committee of the University of Health Sciences (Decision No: 2/27), and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the research. Participants provided written informed consent before data collection. Privacy was protected by the anonymisation, encryption and storage of all facial and thermal images on secure, access-controlled servers. No personally identifiable information was linked to the image data.

Given the potential use of drones in real-world emergency and disaster settings, operational safety and data protection are essential. Drone operation followed national aviation authority safety regulations. The design and deployment of the system for MCI triage scenarios will adhere to the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and other applicable data protection laws. In emergency situations where it is not possible to obtain prior informed consent, post-incident reporting and ethical oversight procedures are implemented in accordance with existing rules and regulations.

Discussion and conclusion

In this study, algorithms were developed for the contactless measurement of HR, BT, SpO₂, and RR using video-based image and signal processing techniques, for the purpose of drone-integrated remote MCI triage. Comparison of reference and algorithmic measurements obtained in indoor and outdoor environments revealed that the algorithms operate with high accuracy rates and low bias values. These results support the field applicability of the proposed methods. In this respect, the study provides important preliminary evidence regarding the usability of drone-based remote triage systems in real-life scenarios.

In the study, in addition to controlled indoor settings, the vital sign measurement algorithms were tested on images obtained in outdoor environments, and their performance was evaluated under natural environmental conditions. The inclusion of outdoor recordings, albeit limited in number, contributed to the evaluation of the robustness of image processing-based vital sign measurement algorithms under different environmental conditions. Despite light variability specific to outdoor settings and motion-related artefacts, the results obtained were comparable to those observed indoors. A slight decrease in accuracy was noted for RR measurements in outdoor environments, which may be attributed to the greater sensitivity of respiratory movements to environmental factors and motion-related errors. However, the similarity in mean differences between algorithmic and reference measurements in different environments suggests that environmental conditions did not cause significant systematic bias. These findings suggest that the developed algorithms can maintain their functional performance in natural outdoor settings, and that they can be integrated into drone-based triage and remote monitoring systems. Nevertheless, further studies are needed to confirm these findings, particularly those conducted across diverse environmental conditions and with larger sample sizes.

The UAVs are increasingly used for medical surveillance to improve public health monitoring and emergency response36. In addition to surveillance, drones are used for the delivery of medical supplies, such as blood products, vaccines, and laboratory specimens. This approach is particularly valuable in remote or resource-limited regions, where it improves healthcare accessibility and supports infectious disease control efforts37,38. By extending healthcare services to previously inaccessible areas, UAVs play an important role in telemedicine and global health. They enable remote monitoring, disease tracking, and rapid response interventions36. Recent advances in edge computing have improved the efficiency of telemedicine systems. This approach allows data to be processed locally at the source rather than relying solely on remote cloud servers. As a result, latency is reduced, data transmission is minimized, and faster decision making is enabled in time-critical situations39,40. The system conceptualised in this study integrates the principles of edge computing with UAV-based medical surveillance. This capability facilitates rapid triage in MCIs and provides several operational advantages. These include remote monitoring, rapid response, and lower risk for responders. Additionally, keeping critical data locally contained is essential for maintaining operational continuity and data security in disaster contexts.

Heart rate is a key parameter in diagnosing conditions such as arrhythmia, hypovolemia, hypoxia, and anxiety, and is widely used in triage to identify high-risk patients41. In this study, algorithm-based and reference HR measurements showed strong agreement. Additionally, measurements differing by ± 5 bpm (E5) accounted for 6.4% of cases. The reported E5 rate was higher than that observed by Addison et al. (3.59%) under ambient light and lower than Rasche et al. (17%) in ICU settings42,43. The study demonstrates that the developed HR measurement method complies with ANSI/AAMI/IEC standards (2016)44.

The amplitude of physiological signals derived from heartbeats is very low, making non-contact measurement highly sensitive to motion and illumination noise. Facial expressions, head movements, and lighting changes can distort the vPPG signal, as even millimeter-scale pixel shifts in the ROI can degrade signal quality. Therefore, motion tracking and noise reduction are essential22. In this study, an adaptive bandpass filter was used to suppress noise. Previous studies have employed alternative denoising techniques such as Flood-Fill42, Wavelet Transform combined with PCA/ICA45, and Spatio-Temporal Image conversion46, and they reported RMSE values between 1.1 and 7.2 bpm. Similar to our study, Martinez-Delgado et al. used OpenCV, the Viola-Jones face detection method, and the Eulerian Video Magnification algorithm to detect the face region, reporting an RMSE of 1.0971 bpm in their study47. Also, a recent review reported that DL-based algorithms achieved RMSE values ranging from 11.88 to 0.43 bpm48. Our result (RMSE: 2.48) falls within this range, suggesting that the proposed approach performs comparably to some deep learning–based and EVM-based methods while offering a low-cost, feasible alternative for drone-based applications. However, motion artefacts occurring in the same frequency band as the pulse signal highlight the need for higher robustness under real disaster conditions. Future studies should aim to improve motion tolerance and evaluate performance across varying illumination, spectrum, and skin tone conditions.

Respiratory rate is an independent prognostic marker for in-hospital mortality and vital risk detection49. In this study, Bland-Altman analysis showed high agreement between image-based RR measurements and reference (bias: -0.31 breaths/min for indoor recordings and − 0.38 breaths/min for outdoor recordings). Most measurements were within the limits of agreement, and no systematic deviation was observed. However, the accuracy of the RR algorithm was lower than that of the other vital parameter algorithms. A significant reason for this is thought to be the window length. Indeed, a window length of approximately 32 s is recommended for video-based RR measurements because it allows RRs detection as low as 3.75 breaths per minute50,51. However, in this study, the window length was kept at 15 s due to the critical importance of time in extraordinary disaster conditions and the high casualty numbers.

The accuracy of video-based respiratory rate measurement may vary depending on environmental conditions and the techniques used in algorithm development. In this study, RR was measured by calculating periodic mean temperature changes in the nostril region, and the MAE was 2.43 breaths/min for indoor recordings and 2.56 breaths/min for outdoor recordings. In recent years, there has been a growing interest in estimating respiratory rate (RR) from photoplethysmography (PPG) signals. Iqbal et al., combined multiple techniques including selective windowing, signal conditioning, and modified Welch filtering to achieve high accuracy and robustness to noise. They reported a MEA of 2.05 breaths per minute52. Others used various fusion techniques to extract respiratory-induced variation signals and combine instantaneous RR estimates and achived MAE ranging from 1.44 to 1.98 breaths per minute53,54. Another method used in contactless respiratory rate measurement is the optical flow method, which enables measurement with a mean absolute error (MAE) ranging from 0.03 to 0.69 breaths/min55. For remote measurement of respiratory rate, tracking temperature changes around the nostril area using thermal imaging, as employed in this study, is a promising non-contact method. Using this approach, similar to our study, Xinchi and Pereira reported RMSE values ranging from 0.36 to 3.98, depending on the respiratory pattern56.

Moreover, the performance of video-based RR estimation is heavily affected by motion noise, especially in the presence of large movements. Cheng et al. study showed that filtering out motion artefacts can improve the performance of non-contact monitoring of RR estimation57. Similarly, Jagadev et al., who measured RR from temperature variations in the nostril region of thermal images, reported that their “ensemble of regression trees” tracker maintained real-time and automatic tracking of the nostril region (ROI) even during head motion and occlusion, automatically discarding frames where the ROI was out of view or covered, thereby improving the reliability of respiration data58. In addition, illumination variation and respiratory pattern differences across subjects reduce the stability of RR measurement over short time windows. Based on these findings from the literature, future work will incorporate motion filtering and adaptive windowing techniques to minimize noise sensitivity and enhance the accuracy of RR estimation.

SpO₂ is critical for assessing cardiopulmonary health. Recent methods, including video-based PPG, deep learning, and multispectral imaging, have been used for SpO₂ estimation and reported RMSE values between 0.99 and 3.6227,59–61. The SpO₂ estimation algorithm presented in this study demonstrated an RMSE of 1.50% for indoor recordings and 0.68% for outdoor recordings. However, Bland–Altman analysis revealed a systematic bias between the two measurements, with a negative slope indicating overestimation at low SpO₂ levels and underestimation at high levels. This suggests limited predictive performance at extreme ranges. Similar issues have been reported in the literature62,63. The limitation likely arises from the insufficient number of low SpO₂ cases in the training dataset. To improve reliability, future studies should address class imbalance when predicting low SpO₂ levels.

The mean difference (0.19 °C for indoor recordings and 0.27 °C for outdoor recordings) and the RMSE values (0.60 °C indoors and 0.66 °C outdoors) reported in this study indicate that the BT measurement algorithm produces results close to the reference method and demonstrates high accuracy. Similar to our study, the RMSE value was found to be 0.439 °C in the study of Lin et al. who developed a BT measurement algorithm using thermal video images64. Venkatakrishnan et al. developed a BT measurement algorithm based on processing RGB images and reported that the RMSE value ranged between 0.54 and 1.1 °C65. While RGB images are susceptible to external factors such as environmental lighting, skin colour and camera quality, thermal imaging is less affected by such variables. Therefore, the developed algorithm is considered to be an algorithm with operational value in terms of rapid detection of individuals with suspected infection, especially in disaster environments or quarantine areas by integrating it into drone-based remote triage systems. However, further studies that incorporate environmental factors and account for individual differences are needed in order to achieve narrower confidence intervals and higher accuracy.

Radar-based sensing systems, an important non-contact measurement technology, can estimate vital signs by analyzing phase and amplitude changes in reflected electromagnetic signals caused by micrometer-level movements of the chest wall66. It has been reported that radar systems are capable of reliable measurements with error rates generally within ± 10% under controlled conditions and below ± 5% under ideal conditions67. In this study, the fact that 6.4% of measurements differed by ± 5 bpm (E5) indicates that the developed system demonstrates a considerably higher level of accuracy compared to radar-based sensing systems. However, under real-world conditions, the accuracy of radar-based systems decreases significantly due to factors such as motion artifacts, multipath effects, and the suppression of cardiac signals by respiration66. Therefore, radar-based sensing systems are not considered to provide a significant advantage over image processing–based methods for drone-assisted triage. More comprehensive comparative studies are needed in this regard. In recent years, artificial intelligence algorithms and machine learning models have attracted attention and yielded promising results, particularly in the fields of cancer diagnosis and medical imaging68,69. In this study, these technologies were used for contactless and remote extraction of vital signs for remote triage purposes, presenting an innovative approach in this field. By examining the feasibility of drone-based, contactless vital sign measurement for remote triage, this study demonstrated that the developed algorithms are capable of measuring vital signs with high accuracy within an approximately 13-second stable video segment. However, similar to other image-based vital sign measurement algorithms reported in the literature, the system remains affected by motion. In disaster or mass-casualty conditions, movement is inevitable and expected. Therefore, enhancing the system’s robustness against motion-induced artifacts constitutes one of the most important and primary objectives for future development to ensure reliable performance under real-world field conditions. Moreover, further validation is needed under diverse populations, lighting conditions, and outdoor environments with larger sample that reflect real mass-casualty scenarios. Future work will focus on expanding the validation process across these variables to enhance generalizability and robustness. In addition, this system is part of a larger project in which integration with a comprehensive decision support system (DSS) for drone-based triage. In future phases, the fully integrated platform will be tested as a unified system to evaluate its operational performance and decision-making capability in real-world mass-casualty settings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the algorithms were only tested on healthy volunteers, and their performance in individuals of different age groups, health conditions (e.g., individuals with respiratory or circulatory disorders), or skin tones was not evaluated. This limits the generalizability of the developed algorithms to clinically heterogeneous populations. Second, the majority of the data collection process was conducted in controlled indoor conditions, with participants in a fixed position and relatively constant lighting conditions. Although a limited number of outdoor recordings were also obtained and the algorithms were tested under natural conditions, the limited outdoor sample size restricted a comprehensive evaluation of the effects of varying light intensity, shadowing, wind, background complexity, and motion artifacts on algorithm performance. Furthermore, complex conditions that may be encountered in real disaster and mass casualty events, such as low lighting, intense movement, smoke, dust, precipitation, or the presence of multiple injured individuals-were not simulated in this study. Therefore, prior to field implementation of the developed algorithms, further validation studies are required in settings that better represent real-life scenarios, involving diverse populations and encompassing both indoor and outdoor environments.
