The study of vaginal wall thickness in adults based on histopathological measurements
Authors: Xinrong Wang, Juan Liu, Juan Zou, Can Luo, Dongmei Wei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
To accurately measure the vaginal mucosa thickness across different age groups using histopathologic techniques and investigate the factors that may influence the thickness changes. This study aims to provide clinicians with objective evidence of variations in vaginal mucosal thickness, facilitating personalized medical decisions for patients. A retrospective analysis was conducted on clinical data from 348 patients who underwent local vaginal wall resection at the West China Second University Hospital, Sichuan University, from January 2021 and May 2022. The thickness of vaginal mucosa, epithelium and lamina propria was measured precisely under the microscope. And the 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 90th percentile values of vaginal mucosa thickness across different age groups were counted and charted a dot-line plot. The percentile values for vaginal mucosa thickness exhibited a decreasing trend with increasing age; vaginal mucosa thickness showed significant correlations with times of delivery (P = 0.031) and age (P < 0.001), both of which were negatively associated. And vaginal mucosa thickness demonstrated no significant correlation with body mass index (BMI) (P = 0.325), times of abortions (P = 0.511), times of gestation (P = 0.101), menstrual cycle (P = 0.533), or types of delivery (P = 0.056); epithelial thickness showed significant associations with age (P < 0.001) and types of delivery (P = 0.017), both of which were negative correlations. Moreover, BMI (P = 0.429), times of abortions (P = 0.764), delivery (P = 0.079), gestation (P = 0.475), and menstrual cycle (P = 0.950) were nonassociated with epithelial thickness; lamina propria thickness displayed a significant correlation only with age (P = 0.002), and there were no obvious correlations observed between lamina propria thickness and BMI (P = 0.374), times of abortion (P = 0.417), delivery (P = 0.053), gestation (P = 0.101), types of delivery (P = 0.132) and menstrual cycle (P = 0.495). Moreover, when the age segmentation was thresholded at 35 and 50 years, both epithelial thickness and vaginal mucosa thickness were significantly correlated with age (P < 0.05). Lamina propria thickness was associated with age when the age threshold was set at 35 years (P = 0.007), whereas it showed no strong link with age when the age threshold was 50 years (P = 0.072). This study has innovatively established percentile reference values for vaginal mucosa thickness based on histopathology, furnishing clinicians with objective evidence of variations in vaginal mucosal thickness to facilitate personalized medical decisions for patients. The findings demonstrated a strong link between vaginal mucosa thickness and age, with epithelium likely playing a predominant role, while the association with lamina propria appeared to be less significant. Further research involving a larger sample size is warranted to elucidate the potential relationship with the lamina propria.

Introduction

The vagina, as a vital female reproductive organ, plays a pivotal role in various essential physiological functions, including childbirth and sexual activity. Throughout a woman's life, the vaginal wall undergoes continuous changes, with its thickness varing with age1. The vaginal wall histologically consists of four layers: epithelium, lamina propria, muscularis and adventice2,3. The epithelium primarily comprises stratified squamous epithelium, and the lamina propria is a connective tissue layer composed of elastin, collagen and fibroblasts4; the muscularis consists of smooth muscle cells, and the adventice is a loose layer of connective tissue that separates the vaginal muscularis from the paravaginal tissue5,6. Among these, the epithelial layer and lamina propria collectively form the mucosal layer. The thickness of the vaginal mucosa is closely related to women's reproductive health. Vaginal atrophy, characterized by changes in the vaginal mucosa, can lead to genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM).

Currently, the objective methods for assessing vaginal atrophy primarily include vaginal maturation index7 and vaginal pH value. However, the measurement of vaginal maturation index is costly and time-consuming, making it impractical for routine clinical use. The interpretation of vaginal pH values is susceptible to various factors such as smoking8, bacterial vaginitis, blood, semen, or vaginal medications9. Each of these methods has its limitations. The health of the vaginal mucosa is intricately linked to the thickness of its various layers10. Thinning of the vaginal mucosa thickness can indicate diminished glycogen content within the vaginal epithelium, signifying vaginal thinning and atrophy. Consequently, quantifying vaginal mucosal thickness may serve as a reliable objective indicator for assessing vaginal health. Previous measurements of vaginal mucosal thickness typically utilized non-invasive imaging techniques3,11; however, the results of these measurements via ultrasound have shown significant variability across different studies. Presently, there is a paucity of clinical research based on large sample sizes to establish normative data for vaginal mucosal thickness in Chinese women.

The vaginal mucosa is influenced by hormonal levels, which are significantly associated with age. The vaginal wall becomes progressively thinner with advancing age1. However, precise measurements of vaginal wall thickness across different age groups and the extent of this thinning remain undetermined in the current literature. With the increasing demand for improved quality of life and the global aging population, many women are experiencing issues such as vaginal dryness, reduced sexual sensitivity, dyspareunia, sexual dysfunction, and GSM. The health of the vaginal mucosa is pivotal for women's reproductive well-being. Vaginal energy-based treatments and injection therapies targeting these conditions have garnered significant attention and are progressively being incorporated into clinical practice12,13. Consequently, it is imperative to undertake clinical research on vaginal mucosal thickness across diverse age groups to furnish evidence-based data that underpins these emerging medical interventions. To the best of our knowledge, there have been no prior studies that have precisely measured vaginal mucosa thickness in paraffin-embedded specimens using histological techniques. Consequently, this study represents the first attempt to accurately examine and understand the thickness and variations of vaginal mucosa among different age groups by histopathological means. The study collected clinicopathological data from 348 patients who underwent local vaginal wall resection. Vaginal mucosa thickness was measured using microscopy, and percentiles of vaginal mucosa thickness in different age groups were analyzed. And, the study examined the correlation between age and vaginal mucosa thickness, investigated the influencing factors on vaginal mucosa thickness, epithelial thickness, and lamina propria thickness. Additionally, it explored the impact of changes in epithelial and lamina propria thickness on vaginal mucosa thickness.

Research subjects and method

Research subjects

The study included patients who underwent local vaginal wall resection at the West China Second University Hospital, Sichuan University, from January 2021 to May 2022. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients who underwent hysterectomy for benign uterine conditions with partial vaginal wall resection; (2) Resected vaginal wall specimens with intact vaginal mucosal histological structure; (3) Vaginal wall specimens assessed by two experienced pathologists as free from malignant components; (4) Comprehensive clinical data available; (5) Adult females. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Use of exogenous oral estrogen therapy in the past 6 months; (2) Use of vaginal local estrogen or energy therapy in the past 6 months; (3) History of vaginal surgery/mesh treatment; (4) Genital tract anomalies, VAIN, or vaginal cancer; (5) Acute genital tract inflammation or pregnancy.

Method of measurement

The HE (hematoxylin–eosin) staining sections of the vaginal wall from the selected study subjects were observed and evaluated by two experienced pathologists under an OLYMPUS microscope, as illustrated in Fig. 1, where measurements were taken for the mucosal layer, epithelium and lamina propria. The measurements from both pathologists were aggregated after averaging the results.Figure 1Structure of the vaginal wall (HE staining, × 20).

Research method

(1) collection of general information and clinical data from the enrolled study subjects: including age, menopausal status, BMI, times of gestation, times of delivery, times of abortion, types of delivery, and the menstrual cycle in which patients underwent surgical resection (proliferative phase/secretory phase); (2) measurement of vaginal mucosa thickness as well as epithelial and lamina propria thickness; (3) analysis of the correlation between age and mucosal thickness; (4) investigation of factors influencing mucosal thickness; (5) study of factors affecting epithelial and lamina propria thickness, and analysis of the impact of changes in epithelial and lamina propria thickness on vaginal mucosa thickness.

Statistical method

Statistical analysis of the data was performed using SPSS 25.0 software. Origin2021 was used to chart the percentile dot-line plot of vaginal mucosa thickness. Linear regression was employed to examine the correlation between age and vaginal mucosa thickness. Mann–Whitney U test was applied to analyze the association of age segments, types of delivery, and menstrual cycle with mucosal, epithelial, and lamina propria thickness. And box plot illustrating the thickness of vaginal mucosa, epithelium, and lamina propria based on different factors was generated using GraphPad Prism 8. Lastly, the correlation between continuous variables such as BMI, times of gestation, and times of abortion with mucosal, epithelial, and lamina propria thickness was assessed using Pearson Correlation Analysis. When P < 0.05, the differences were considered statistically significant.

Ethical approval statement

Ethical approval was granted by the the Ethics Committee of West China Second University Hospital, Sichuan University (No. 2023/179) on September 21, 2023. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. All procedures were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines.

Analysis of the dominant role of epithelial and lamina propria thickness changes on vaginal mucosa thickness variation

Histologically, the vaginal mucosa consists of both an epithelium and a lamina propria. Combining the results from Tables 2, 3, 4, it could be observed that age had a significant correlation with vaginal mucosa thickness and epithelial thickness when the age thresholds were 35 and 50 years. Remarkably, when age was segmented using 50 years as the threshold, it did not exhibit significant correlation with lamina propria thickness (P = 0.072), but maintained a significant association with epithelial thickness (P < 0.001). This suggests that changes in epithelial thickness primarily drive variations in vaginal mucosa thickness, while the relationship with lamina propria thickness is less pronounced.

Discussion

Vaginal mucosa thickness in females undergoes continuous changes with age, and it can serve as an objective indicator for evaluating vaginal mucosal health, as well as for diagnosing and managing female reproductive conditions. This study is the first to objectively measure the thickness of the vaginal mucosa, epithelium and lamina propria in women of different ages by microscopic histopathologic examination techniques. By aggregating the measurements of vaginal mucosa thickness in different age groups, the detailed values were provided for the 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 90th percentiles of vaginal mucosa thickness corresponding to different age segmentations. Previous researches have not reported percentile values of vaginal mucosa thickness among various age groups, and these values can serve as a reference for clinical measurements of vaginal mucosa thickness. Also, the study utilized a dot-line plot to clearly illustrate the changing trends in different percentile values and analyzed the correlation between vaginal mucosa thickness and age, substantiating that the vaginal mucosa thickness became progressively thinner with advancing age. When examining the influencing factors of vaginal mucosa thickness, it was observed that the significant factors associated with mucosal thickness were the times of delivery and age, both negatively correlated. BMI, times of abortion, times of gestation, menstrual cycle, and types of delivery showed no significant correlation with vaginal mucosa thickness; the influencing factors significantly associated with epithelial thickness were age and the types of delivery, with normal delivery resulting in thinner epithelial thickness compared to cesarean section; the only determinant significantly correlated with lamina propria thickness was age. Subsequently, by analyzing age in segments, it was understood that 35 years served as the threshold for older women, as ovarian reserve gradually diminishes in women after the age of 3514,15. And 50 years is the average age of menopause in women. Therefore, by using the age thresholds of 35 years and 50 years, it was found that vaginal mucosa thickness and epithelial thickness were both correlated with age. However, in the analysis of lamina propria thickness, there was no significant relevance between age and lamina propria thickness, when the age threshold was set at 50 years. This demonstrates that changes in vaginal mucosa thickness are primarily due to alterations in epithelial thickness and not significantly related to lamina propria thickness.

The study revealed that vaginal mucosa thickness was correlated with age, with older adult women having thinner vaginal mucosa compared to younger women. This is consistent with previous findings in the literature1. Farage et al.1 mentioned that during puberty, the vagina matured under the influence of adrenal and gonadal steroid hormones. As it matures, the development of follicles leads to an increase in estrogen production, resulting in thickening of the vaginal epithelial cells and the generation of glycogen within the cells. During the reproductive phase, the vagina is sensitive to ovarian steroid hormones, and estrogen can cause hyperplasia and keratinization of vaginal epithelial cells, leading to mucosal thickening. After menopause, ovarian atrophy and a decline in estrogen levels lead to progressive thinning of the vaginal mucosa, resulting in vaginal atrophy, such as GSM. The typical manifestations of vaginal atrophy associated with GSM include a reduction in vaginal length and diameter, the disappearance of vaginal fornix, thinning of the epithelium, and loss of elasticity, resulting in vaginal folds, erythema, pallor, and dryness. Vaginal dryness, irritation, itching, burning sensation, and pain are the most common symptoms16,17. More research pointed out that local vaginal estrogen could induce normal proliferation and lubrication of the atrophic vaginal epithelium, which could reverse atrophic changes and significantly improve vaginal symptoms such as vaginal burning and dryness, while avoiding systemic effects18–21.

The study further revealed that the changes in vaginal mucosa thickness at different ages were primarily attributed to alterations in epithelial thickness, with no significant correlation with lamina propria thickness. Stratified statistical analysis by age, whether using 35 or 50 years as the cutoff, demonstrated that changes in vaginal mucosal thickness were attributable solely to the relationship between epithelial layer thickness and age, with no significant correlation to lamina propria thickness. With age came changes in hormone levels in the body, which affected the epithelial thickness to some extent. Chen et al.22 found that estrogen receptor (ER) was distributed in the vaginal epithelium, and estrogen receptor was divided into two categories: Erα and Erβ, of which Erα existed in the vaginal epithelium of both premenopausal and postmenopausal women, while ERβ was only found in the vaginal epithelium of premenopausal women. In postmenopausal women, the number of ER in the vaginal epithelium decreased, leading to reduced estrogenic effects on the vagina, and the vaginal epithelium gradually shrank as a result. This perspective also suggests that it is primarily the squamous epithelium of the vaginal mucosa that is responsive to ovarian steroid hormones. Therefore, with increasing age, the decrease in estrogen levels affects the changes in the epithelial thickness, thereby influencing the alterations in vaginal mucosa thickness.

To our knowledge, previous literature has not analyzed the relationship between vaginal mucosa thickness and clinical indicators such as BMI, times of abortion, times of gestation, menstrual cycle, and types of delivery. Our study, for the first time, confirms that there is no significant correlation between vaginal mucosa thickness and BMI, times of abortion, times of gestation, menstrual cycle, and types of delivery. Previously, we assumed that a higher BMI index might affect vaginal mucosa thickness. However, this study discovered no significant correlation between BMI index and vaginal mucosa thickness. Analyzing the reasons behind this, it is possible that a higher BMI index, associated with increased body fat, primarily impacts the proliferation of adipose cells, which has no obvious relationship with the epithelium and lamina propria in histology. Hence, its effect on vaginal mucosa thickness appears to be minimal.

It's noteworthy that in studies to measure vaginal wall thickness by Hakan et al.11 using three-dimensional (3D) high-frequency transvaginal ultrasound, and by Adrian et al.3 and Ahkam et al.23 using transabdominal ultrasound to measure vaginal wall thickness, the average total thickness of the vaginal wall in postmenopausal women was lower than that of premenopausal women. However, vaginal mucosa thickness did not differ significantly between premenopausal and postmenopausal women in these studies. This is inconsistent with our results of using microscopy to measure the thickness of the vaginal mucosa in paraffin specimens and finding that the vaginal mucosa thickness was lower in postmenopausal women than in premenopausal women. Analyzing the inconsistency in findings, it could be attributed to a couple of factors. Firstly, it might be related to the way of measurement, as the measurement error of ultrasound for small distances may be larger than that of the microscope. Secondly, it could be due to the inclusion of the study subjects. This study primarily included cervical cancer patients who underwent surgical resection of the vaginal wall to exclude the influence of exogenous hormones. Additionally, sample size and other factors could also play a role in the observed differences.

Furthermore, when the lamina propria thickness was investigated with different age thresholds in this study, it was concluded that the lamina propria was not significantly related to age at an age threshold of 50 years. This result may be influenced by the site of the specimen sent for examination. Previous studies have indicated that the lamina propria in the anterior vaginal wall is thicker than that in the posterior vaginal wall24. Since the specimens were not specifically categorized as anterior or posterior vaginal wall, this might be one of the contributing factors to the conclusion that there was little correlation between lamina propria thickness and age. It is possible that changes in lamina propria thickness may also affect vaginal mucosa thickness to some extent. To gain a clearer understanding of this relationship, further research with a larger sample size and specific site categorization is necessary.

There are some limitations in this study. Firstly, in order to accurate specimens, it is required that the sections must be cut completely perpendicular to the epithelial tissue. Since its oblique section will be thicker than the vertical line, the more inclined it is when sliced, the thicker the epithelium on the section will be. In practical application, it's challenging to ensure that every specimen is perfectly perpendicular to the epithelial tissue during sampling, which could introduce some measurement error. Additionally, the paraffin-embedded specimens may have undergone tissue dehydration during preparation, potentially leading to a slight reduction in measured values. This dehydration effect may overall skew measurements lower, introducing a certain bias, although relative values remain consistent. Future studies utilizing fresh tissue for measurements would offer measurements closer to real human tissue thickness, thereby enhancing accuracy of measurements and reliability of research conclusions.

Conclusion

This study has constructed percentile reference values for vaginal mucosa thickness based on histopathology, which can enable clinicians to make personalized medical decisions for patients and provide objective evidence of changes in vaginal mucosal thickness. This study has shown that there is a correlation between the vaginal mucosa thickness and age, with the crucial influencing factor likely being the epithelium, while the relationship with the lamina propria appears to be less pronounced. Clinicians can improve the function of the dominant epithelial layer by utilizing laser and other physical energy treatments, and administering localized injections to the vaginal wall. These therapies, such as local platelet rich plasma (PRP) or collagen injections, can promote collagen remodeling, cell proliferation, and angiogenesis within the vaginal mucosa. These interventions can increase the epithelial layer's thickness, thereby improving vaginal mucosal health and alleviating a range of symptoms associated with vaginal atrophy, including dryness, dyspareunia, and reduced sexual sensitivity. Consequently, these measures can significantly enhance patients' clinical outcomes and overall quality of life. Further exploration of the potential relationship with the lamina propria awaits future research with a larger sample size.
