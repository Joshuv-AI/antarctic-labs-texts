Diagnostic accuracy of plain radiographs compared to CT scans in classifying talus injuries
Authors: Reza Fereidooni, Seyed Arman Moein, Seyyed Hamidreza Ayatizadeh, Amirhossein Kamalinia, Mohammad Amin Ahmadi, Seiied Hossein Heidari, Asal Seifaei, Amir Reza Vosoughi, Esmail Rayatdoost
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTalus injuries are clinically significant due to the talus bone’s essential function in foot biomechanics and the complexity of its healing process. Although plain radiographs are more accessible and involve lower radiation exposure than CT scans, they often lack sufficient detail for accurate diagnosis. Given their frequent use in initial assessments, this study aims to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of plain radiographs in classifying talus injuries within a patient group with confirmed talar fractures.MethodsSample size determination aimed to adequately represent rare fracture subtypes while resembling real-world prevalence, indicating a minimum of 140 cases. Radiographs of 159 patients with talus fractures were randomly selected from a database. Two authors interpreted CT scans to establish an accurate gold standard diagnosis. Three diagnosticians assessed the radiographs independently, searching for 10 talus injuries and fracture characteristics. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and their confidence intervals were calculated for each injury type and overall injuries. Individual and pooled diagnosticians’ responses were used to compute diagnostic metrics.ResultsDiagnosticians achieved only moderate interrater agreement, with a Kappa coefficient of 0.445 (p < 0.001). The overall sensitivity of plain radiographs for detecting talus injuries was 60% (95% CI: 59.10%-61.87%). Sensitivity for identifying fracture characteristics, such as comminution and displacement was lower, at around 53%. Fractures of the head, body, and neck exhibited variable sensitivities, with talus neck fractures demonstrating the highest sensitivity at 76.73% followed by head (52.78%) and body (51.92%). The lateral process, posterior process, and medial tubercle of talus displayed sensitivity rates ranging from 51.52 to 55.56%, while osteochondral fractures showed the lowest sensitivity at 33.33%. Sensitivities for tibiotalar (82.22%) and talonavicular (75%) dislocations were satisfactory compared to subtalar dislocations (57.36%). Hawkins type 2 yielded the lowest sensitivity at 29.76% (95% CI: 25.66%-33.86%).ConclusionWhile plain radiographs are accessible for detecting certain talus injuries, they fall short in precisely classifying injury locations and identifying details, such as displacement and comminution. CT scans are recommended when there is high clinical suspicion, as they offer greater accuracy for complex and subtle injuries.Level of evidenceLevel II.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09136-3.

Introduction

The talus, a small but pivotal bone within the ankle joint, plays a crucial role in the intricate biomechanics of the foot and ankle, acting as a keystone that transmits forces between the leg and foot [1, 2]. The talus can be injured in high-energy trauma scenarios such as motor vehicle collisions, falls from significant heights, and sports-related injuries [3–5]. Although these fractures are uncommon, accounting for only 0.1–2.5% of all fractures and less than 10% of foot and ankle fractures, they are fraught with complications owing to the talus’s extensive cartilaginous surfaces and unique vascular supply [3, 6–8]. The consequences of talus fractures, particularly those involving the neck and body, can be severe, leading to high rates of avascular necrosis (AVN), post-traumatic arthritis (PTA) and joint instability, which significantly impair long-term function and quality of life [9–11].

The diagnosis of suspected talus injuries generally begins with plain radiographs, a modality favored for its accessibility, rapid availability, and ability to provide an initial assessment of the bony anatomy [12]. However, the limitations of plain radiographs are well-documented [13, 14]. The complex anatomy of the talus, combined with the subtle nature of certain fracture types, significantly hampers the ability of plain radiographs to detect all but the most obvious injuries. Studies have shown that plain radiographs miss a significant percentage of talar fractures, particularly those involving the talar dome, lateral process, and posterior process, where the consequences of missed or delayed diagnosis may potentially be severe [15, 16].

Computed tomography (CT) is considered the gold standard for imaging of talus injuries and provides detailed cross-sectional images that reveal the full extent of the injury, including fracture comminution, displacement, and articular surface involvement [17]. These key details are essential for guiding treatment decisions and significantly influence patient outcomes [18, 19]. The advantages of CT in detecting subtle and complex fractures compared to plain radiographs underscore the need for a more nuanced approach to imaging in cases of suspected talar injury. However, despite its advantages, CT is not without its drawbacks, including higher costs, increased radiation exposure, and limited availability in some settings [20]. Thus, determining when plain radiographs are adequate and when CT is required is essential for optimizing the efficiency and effectiveness of talar fracture management.

Recent studies have highlighted the diagnostic limitations of plain radiography compared to CT in accurately assessing foot and ankle trauma, with significant implications for treatment planning. Kalantar et al. [21] found that plain X-rays missed a substantial number of fractures in high-energy trauma cases, including 11 out of 14 talus fractures that CT identified, which led to Changes in treatment plans for 7 patients. Similarly, Avci et al. [22] demonstrated low sensitivity of X-rays for detecting talus and calcaneus fractures, with CT showing higher detection rates (four fractures out of a total of 13 were missed on X-rays). Üstün et al. [23] reported that conventional radiography for talus fractures had a sensitivity of only 42%, albeit with high specificity (100%) and positive predictive value (PPV), indicating its limitations in initial diagnosis but reliability in confirmed cases. While these studies highlight CT’s advantages over plain radiography in detecting talus and ankle fractures, they are limited by small sample sizes and a lack of systematic case selection.

Given the complexity of talar fractures and the significance of an accurate diagnosis and assessment, there is a need to evaluate the diagnostic tools at our disposal. Accurately diagnosing talar fractures remains challenging, as clinicians may struggle not only to detect their presence but also to precisely determine their location. This study aims to systematically compare the diagnostic accuracy of plain radiographs to CT scans in detecting and classifying fractures in patients with talar injuries. By analyzing the sensitivity, specificity, and other diagnostic metrics, this research seeks to provide crucial insights into how effectively X-rays can be utilized in the initial assessment of talar fractures. Evaluating the diagnostic accuracy of commonly used imaging modalities and recognizing their limitations are essential to support informed clinical decision-making by practitioners.

Methods

Study settings and participants

This study was conducted at two level 1 trauma centers in Shiraz, Southern Iran: Chamran Hospital and Shahid Rajaei (Emtiaz) Hospital. The study used a database of patients who presented with talus fractures or dislocations with or without associated fractures from January 2014 to December 2019. Radiographs were selected randomly but systematically from this database. The study involved three diagnosticians: one senior orthopedic surgery resident and two attending orthopedic surgeons.

Sample size

To avoid underrepresenting rarer injury subtypes, we chose a sample size that is large enough to include the rare subtypes while still reflecting real-world prevalence. We calculated the sample size based on the least prevalent and the most challenging pathology to diagnose among the talus injuries. Ensuring the sample size is large enough for the rarest and most challenging cases guarantees that it will also be sufficient for more common conditions. Talus fracture subtypes with a prevalence below 5% were excluded from the sample size calculation due to their limited clinical significance and to avoid overinflating the sample size.

According to the study by Vosoughi et al. [24], the rarest fracture with a prevalence higher than 5% was the posterior process fracture, having a prevalence of 14.5%. On the other hand, it is suggested that most of talus process fractures can be missed on plain X-rays [25, 26] . Combined lateral and posterior talus process fractures have a prevalence of 29.2% [24]. Data collection

Radiographs were randomly selected from the aforementioned database of talus fractures. The CT scans of all these patients were interpreted by a foot and ankle surgeon (ARV) and double-checked by SAM, an orthopedic surgeon. We matched the real-world proportions of different subtypes of talus injuries, as documented in the study by Vosoughi et al. [24] in the final selected radiographs that were presented to the diagnosticians. The numbers of each included talus injury are provided in Table 1. Radiographs that did not include essential ankle views (anterior-posterior, lateral, mortise) and foot views (anterior-posterior, lateral, oblique), that were only taken post-operatively, or that were taken with casts or splints were discarded and replaced with randomly selected radiographs of other patients from the database. Only when no adequate radiographs of the injury of interest were available were they substituted with a different injury type.

Diagnostic forms were designed to include 10 different injuries relevant to talus fractures. These injuries included talus head, neck, body, lateral process, posterior process, medial tubercle, osteochondral lesions, subtalar dislocation or subluxation, tibiotalar dislocation or subluxation, and talonavicular dislocation or subluxation. Osteochondral lesions of the talus are injuries that involve the cartilage and underlying subchondral bone of the talar dome and were classified as distinct from talar body fractures in our analysis. A talar body fracture was also distinguished from a talar neck fracture by the orientation of the dorsal fracture line, which in body fractures extends posterior to the lateral process and exits through the posterior facet of the talus, rather than exiting anteriorly, as seen in neck fractures [28]. Comminution and displacement of the fractures were also queried. Each diagnostician independently evaluated the radiographs and their reports were recorded. The diagnosticians were blinded to the patients’ CT scans and the results of other diagnosticians.

Statistical analysis

Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for each type of injury and overall injuries. To mitigate bias and consolidate the interpretations of all three observers, we pooled their responses and computed diagnostic metrics based on the aggregated data. These metrics are calculated using a 2 × 2 contingency table. Stata Statistical Software: Release 17 (StataCorp LLC, 2021) was used to carry out the analysis.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol and data collection procedures were reviewed and approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of Jahrom University of Medical Sciences – Code: IR.JUMS.REC.1402.110.

Results

For fractures of the head of the talus, the sensitivity and PPV were relatively low (52.78% and 59.38% respectively). Diagnostic performance of plain X-rays in detecting fractures within the talus body showcased a sensitivity of 51.92% (95% CI: 47.44% − 56.41%) and a specificity of 77.26% (95% CI: 73.50% − 81.02%). The sensitivity of the X-ray for talus neck was better than other major talus fractures and was calculated at 76.73% (95% CI: 72.94% − 80.52%), indicating its ability to correctly identify approximately three-quarters of talus neck fractures. Meanwhile, the specificity stands at 78.93% (95% CI: 75.27% − 82.59%), suggesting its capability to accurately exclude cases without talus neck fractures.

Lateral process, posterior process, and medial tubercle fractures displayed sensitivity rates of 55.56%, 52.38%, and 51.52% respectively. Osteochondral fractures exhibited the lowest sensitivity (33.33%) among the fracture subtypes of talus.

In addition to fractures, dislocations of talus articulations, including subtalar, tibiotalar, and talonavicular dislocations were investigated (Fig. 2). Tibiotalar and talonavicular dislocations demonstrated better sensitivities (82.22% and 75.00% respectively), while subtalar dislocations were more often missed by the observers (sensitivity of 57.36%). In the study, there were just four instances of talonavicular dislocation or subluxation. Interestingly, out of these, only one subluxation was overlooked by all three authors.

Discussion

Our study aimed to assess the diagnostic accuracy of plain X-ray in classifying different types of talus injuries in a group of patients, all of whom had talus injuries. This study reveals varying sensitivities across different injuries, with relatively high specificities and NPVs. Overall, a sensitivity of 60% (95% CI: 59.10%, 61.87%) was observed for plain radiographs in detecting talus injuries. The reported sensitivities varied across injury types, with higher sensitivities for major fracture types and dislocations (up to 76.73% for neck fractures and 82.22% for tibiotalar dislocations) and lower sensitivities for process fractures and osteochondral fractures.

The moderate interrater agreement among diagnosticians, with a Kappa coefficient of 0.445, suggests that there is some variability in the interpretation of plain radiographs. This diversity may be due to differences in experience or training of radiographic findings. This variability in interpretation could lead to inconsistent diagnoses and consequently, inconsistent treatment approaches across different clinicians or institutions.

The variability in sensitivity across different types of talus injuries demonstrates the strengths and limitations of plain radiographs. Talus neck fractures demonstrated the highest sensitivity, possibly due to their more apparent displacement and the clear anatomical landmarks that aid in their identification (Fig. 5). In contrast, body and head fractures showed lower sensitivities (Fig. 6). Osteochondral lesions and fractures of the talar processes proved even more challenging to detect. This difficulty in the visualization of osteochondral fractures may be attributed to their often subtle nature and location within the joint surface [15]. The processes on the other hand, are small and typically silhouetted by a bony structure (talar body) behind or over them in radiographic views (Fig. 7). While tibiotalar and talonavicular dislocations or subluxations were more readily identified, a relatively low sensitivity was observed for subtalar dislocations or subluxations, echoing previously published findings [29]. This discrepancy might be explained by the more obvious misalignment in tibiotalar and talonavicular dislocations compared to the subtler changes in subtalar joint relationships. These joints are also directly visualized with no overshadowing structures in some foot and ankle views; the talonavicular joint on the dorsoplantar view of the foot and the tibiotalar joint in the AP ankle view. Nevertheless, subtalar dislocations usually produce deformity [30], so clinical examination can still aid decision-making.

The low sensitivities in detecting fracture comminution and displacement are also noteworthy. Accurate assessment of these characteristics is crucial for treatment planning and prognosis, as they often influence the need for surgical intervention and the risk of complications such as osteonecrosis and post-traumatic arthritis [31].

Despite these limitations, it’s crucial to recognize the value and utility of plain radiographs in the initial assessment of suspected talus injuries. Plain radiographs demonstrated high specificity for ruling out major talus injuries. This suggests that when plain radiographs are negative for major fractures or dislocations, clinicians can be reasonably confident in excluding these significant injuries. Compared to some of the low sensitivities, the generally high specificities and NPVs can be carefully interpreted as the potential of plain radiographs to rule out certain significant injuries, thereby potentially reducing unnecessary advanced imaging. The high specificity of X-rays contrasts with the findings of Haapamäki et al. [32], who reported 12 false positives out of 72 cases. This result is particularly noteworthy, as specificity is not influenced by disease prevalence; unlike negative predictive value (NPV), which tends to increase as prevalence decreases [33]. The high NPV in this study may be owed to the low prevalence of most of these injuries and must be interpreted with caution.

Comparing our results with previous literature, we find consistent patterns highlighting the limitations of plain radiographs in detecting talus fractures. Dale et al. reported a sensitivity of 74% for plain radiographs in detecting talus fractures, which is comparable to our overall sensitivity of 60% for all talus injuries. Both studies identified that subtle injuries, particularly talar dome compression fractures, were frequently missed on radiographs. Our study found a low sensitivity of 33.33% for osteochondral fractures, aligning with Dale et al.‘s observation that 31% of dome compression fractures were not seen on radiography [34]. Similarly, Haapamäki et al. [32] reported a 78% sensitivity for radiography in detecting talus fractures, which closely matches our findings, especially for talus neck fractures (76.73% sensitivity). However, our study revealed even lower sensitivities for some fracture types, such as talus body (51.92%) and osteochondral fractures. Haapamäki et al. [32] emphasized the superiority of multi-detector CT (MDCT) in detecting fractures, particularly in complex cases and high-energy trauma, which aligns with our conclusion about the limitations of plain radiographs in accurately diagnosing talus injuries. These consistent findings across multiple studies underscore a limitation in the current standard of care for initial talus injury assessment and highlight the importance of advanced imaging techniques in certain cases.

The evidence regarding the outcomes of neglected or misclassified talus fractures is limited [35, 36]. However, talus fractures are prone to serious complications, with osteonecrosis and post-traumatic arthritis being particularly common [10, 37, 38]. The average occurrence rates for these complications are notably high, with osteonecrosis reaching up to 33% and post-traumatic arthritis up to 68% [11]. Research indicates that factors related to the injury itself are more significant in predicting these complications than other variables [39]. The limitations of plain radiographs in detecting subtle lesions and accurately assessing fracture displacement [40], can be juxtaposed with the report by Dale et al. that CT provides additional critical information in 93% of cases [34].

The evolving perspective on the urgency of surgical management for talus fractures provides an interesting context for interpreting our findings on diagnostic accuracy. Recent studies suggest that the timing of surgical fixation does not significantly impact the risk of osteonecrosis or post-traumatic arthritis [31, 37, 41, 42]. With plain radiographs missing approximately 40% of talus injuries, the reduced urgency for immediate surgery provides a valuable opportunity for more comprehensive diagnostic evaluation. Clinicians may prioritize obtaining accurate imaging, such as CT scans, when plain radiographs are inconclusive or when the mechanism of injury suggests a talus fracture despite negative initial X-rays. While many missed injuries may not require surgery, early and accurate diagnosis remains crucial for treatment planning, patient counseling, and avoiding unnecessary follow-up visits.

While our findings underscore certain diagnostic limitations of plain radiographs, it is important to acknowledge their crucial role in the initial assessment of suspected talus injuries. Plain radiographs remain a valuable and cost-effective tool for screening and identifying obvious fractures and dislocations [43, 44]. In resource-limited settings, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where access to advanced imaging like CT scans may be restricted, plain radiographs play a vital role in triaging patients and guiding initial management decisions [45]. A stepwise approach, utilizing plain radiographs as the first-line imaging modality followed by selective CT imaging based on clinical suspicion and initial radiographic findings, can be a pragmatic and cost-effective strategy in LMICs. This approach can help prioritize resources and minimize unnecessary radiation. CT imaging should be considered when a talus fracture is suspected or visualized on plain radiographs, as supported by our findings regarding the limited sensitivity of X-rays in detecting such injuries. If patients are unable to bear weight and no injury explaining their symptoms is evident on X-ray, it is reasonable to consider a CT scan when the mechanism of injury justifies further investigation.

This study has several limitations. First, it focused on assessing how well various talus injuries were detected and classified in patients with known talus injuries, rather than evaluating the ability to identify these injuries in a general population. This approach could lead to observer bias, as diagnosticians knew to look for talus injuries, potentially inflating sensitivity. Additionally, selecting patients with confirmed diagnoses may have introduced selection bias, overrepresenting severe or obvious injuries and limiting the study’s applicability to real-world scenarios. Second, the small sample size for rare injuries like talonavicular dislocations reduces the precision and generalizability of the findings for these subtypes. The study used data from two orthopedic trauma centers in one region, which may not reflect other settings or populations. Furthermore, despite blinding efforts, the study’s controlled environment does not fully replicate real-world clinical decision-making. Moderate variability among diagnosticians also suggests individual interpretation differences may have affected results. Lastly, variations in image quality, which could impact accuracy in practice, were not addressed.

Despite these limitations, the study’s large overall sample size, the inclusion of a broad range of talus injuries, and the use of CT as the gold standard provide valuable insights into the diagnostic performance of plain radiographs for talus injuries.

This study highlights several avenues for future research in talus injury diagnosis and management. This study does not specifically analyze the impact that accurate or complete fracture characterization would have on treatment. Prospective studies comparing plain radiographs to CT scans in acute trauma settings could evaluate real-time diagnostic accuracy and the impact of missed diagnoses. Developing and validating decision support tools for talus injuries could improve diagnostic accuracy and inform treatment planning. Cost-benefit studies may be done to compare the economic impact of different diagnostic algorithms and tools. Long-term follow-up studies are needed to understand the clinical consequences of missed or misclassified talus injuries, informing evidence-based guidelines for advanced imaging use. These research directions aim to refine diagnostic algorithms and improve overall patient care in talus injuries.

Conclusions

Despite its limitations, plain X-rays showed high specificity in ruling out major talus injuries. However, its sensitivity is moderate-to-low for detecting fractures of the talus head and body, injuries to the medial tubercle, osteochondral lesions, and fracture features like comminution or displacement. Knowing the variability in sensitivity across different types of talus injuries provides valuable insights into the strengths and limitations of plain radiographs. For correct diagnosis and treatment planning, a low threshold for an ankle CT scan is advised when there are suspicions of these injuries.
