Intravascular Lithotripsy With Ultra–Low Contrast OCT Guidance for Calcified Coronary Lesions in Advanced CKD Patients
Authors: Yoshinobu Murasato, Takahiro Mori, Yugo Fujimoto, Kansuke Tomozoe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo demonstrate the feasibility and safety of optical coherence tomography (OCT)–guided, ultra–low contrast percutaneous coronary intervention with intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) for severely calcified coronary lesions in patients with advanced chronic kidney disease.Key StepsAs procedural steps: 1) use prior angiograms for procedural planning; 2) perform OCT with low–molecular weight dextran to assess calcium morphology; 3) apply IVL for targeted calcium modification; 4) reassess with OCT for calcium fracture, lumen gain, and stent optimization; and 5) limit total iodinated contrast volume to ≤10 mL.Potential PitfallsPotential issues include: 1) geographic stent miss when relying on fluoroscopic markers—use side-branch wiring for reference; 2) side-branch compromise—consider jailed balloon protection; 3) dextran-related allergic reaction—switch to intravascular ultrasound if needed; and 4) excessive dextran (>100 mL)—avoid to reduce renal risk.Take-Home MessageOCT-guided ultra–low contrast percutaneous coronary intervention with IVL enables safe, precise treatment of heavily calcified lesions in advanced chronic kidney disease while minimizing renal injury risk.Key words: chronic kidney disease, intravascular lithotripsy, optical coherence tomography, ultra-low contrast

Contrast-induced nephropathy is a serious concern in patients with pre-existing chronic kidney disease (CKD) undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Minimizing or eliminating contrast use is critical for these high-risk individuals. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) offers high-resolution intravascular imaging and, when used with dextran or other alternatives, enables PCI with minimal or no iodinated contrast exposure.1, 2, 3, 4 Patients with advanced CKD often present with severe coronary calcification, which poses procedural challenges such as stent underexpansion and increased complication risk. Compared with rotational or orbital atherectomy, intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) enables effective calcium modification with a lower risk of coronary dissection, perforation, or distal embolization. OCT-guided PCI with minimal contrast is particularly advantageous in calcified lesions, where detailed assessment of calcium morphology5,6—including post-IVL calcium fractures—optimizes lesion preparation and stent deployment.

We report the first series to our knowledge of ultra–low contrast, OCT-guided PCI with IVL for heavily calcified lesions in patients with advanced CKD.

Case Summary

Two patients with stage 4 CKD presented with exertional angina and severe coronary calcification. Both patients underwent PCI with total contrast volume ≤10 mL and without any contrast-induced nephropathy or procedural complications.

Patient 1

An 82-year-old man with effort angina had a serum creatinine of 2.20 mg/dL and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 23.1 mL/min/1.73 m2. A severely calcified lesion was observed from the proximal to the mid left anterior descending artery (LAD) (Figure 1, Video 1). Using a prior angiogram as reference, a 7-F guide catheter was introduced via the radial artery, and guidewires were advanced to the LAD and diagonal branches originating from the calcified lesion. After predilation with a 2.0-mm noncompliant balloon, OCT (Ilumien Optis, Ultereon 2.0, Abbott Medical) was performed by manually flushing 8 to 12 mL of low–molecular weight dextran. OCT revealed severe concentric calcification (1.3-mm thickness) in the mid-LAD and continuous eccentric calcification (>1.5-mm thickness, arc: 270°-300°) from the distal left main artery (LM) to the proximal LAD (Figure 2A, Video 2).

IVL was performed using a 3.0/12-mm Shockwave C2 balloon (Shockwave Medical) from the mid-LAD to the distal LM. OCT confirmed multiple calcium fractures (Figure 2B, Video 3). After treating the diagonal branch with a 2.5/15-mm drug-coated balloon, a 3.0/33-mm zotarolimus-eluting stent and 3.5/18-mm everolimus-eluting stent were implanted sequentially from the mid-LAD to the proximal LM, which was plaque free. The proximal optimization technique was applied using a 4.0-mm noncompliant balloon in the LM, and a 2.25-mm balloon was used to dilate the diagonal branch ostium. OCT showed adequate minimal stent areas (distal LM: 9.2 mm2, proximal LAD: 6.1 mm2, mid-LAD: 6.1 mm2) and no significant malapposition (Figures 2C and 2D, Video 4) or strut jailing at the left circumflex ostium. A total of 10 mL of contrast medium was used.

The complex procedure, which included treating 2 bifurcations and severely calcified lesions, was completed with a fluoroscopic time of 60 minutes and radiation dose of 1.3 Gy (Figure 3, Video 5). Renal function was preserved 24 hours after PCI (serum creatinine: 1.99 mg/dL, eGFR: 25.8 mL/min/1.73 m2).

Patient 2

A 71-year-old man with effort angina had a serum creatinine of 1.89 mg/dL and eGFR of 28.4 mL/min/1.73 m2. Heavily calcified lesions were observed in the proximal and distal right coronary artery (RCA) (Figure 4, Video 6). Referring to a previous coronary angiogram, a 6-F guide catheter was introduced via the radial artery, and guidewires were advanced to the posterior descending and posterolateral branches. Optical frequency domain imaging (OFDI) (Fastview, Terumo Corp) using low–molecular weight dextran revealed a concentric calcified lesion (>1.0-mm thickness, arc: 360°) in the proximal RCA, an eccentric calcified lesion (>1.3-mm thickness, arc: 270°) in the proximal site of the distal RCA, and a fibrous concentric plaque in the more distal RCA (Figure 5A, Video 7).

After predilation with a 2.5-mm noncompliant balloon, a 2.5/33-mm sirolimus-eluting stent was implanted to cover the distal fibrous lesion across the posterior descending branch. IVL was performed using a 3.5/12-mm Shockwave C2 balloon at the proximal site of the distal RCA and proximal RCA with 40 pulses, respectively. OFDI confirmed multiple calcium fractures (Figure 5B, Video 8). A 3.5/22 zotarolimus-eluting stent and 3.5/24 everolimus-eluting stent were implanted sequentially from the distal side. All stents in the main RCA were dilated with a 3.5-mm noncompliant balloon at 16 to 20 atm. OFDI confirmed sufficient minimal stent areas (proximal RCA: 7.0 mm2, distal RCA: 7.4 mm2) and no significant malapposition (Figure 5C, Video 9). A total of 8 mL of contrast medium was used.

The complex procedure, which included treating 2 severely calcified lesions and a bifurcation lesion, was completed with a fluoroscopic time of 34 minutes and radiation dose of 1.2 Gy (Figure 6, Video 10). Renal function was preserved 24 hours after PCI (serum creatinine: 1.88 mg/dL, eGFR: 28.6 mL/min/1.73 m2).

Procedural Steps

Baseline coronary angiography

After overnight hydration with saline (1.5 L/d starting the previous day), baseline coronary angiography is performed in 1 or 2 projections using a biplane angiography system, with minimal contrast media consumption (<20 mL). Coronary calcified lesions are identified on fluoroscopy.

Percutaneous coronary intervention

PCI is scheduled after confirming stable renal function within 2 days of the baseline angiography and after overnight hydration. The procedural steps are as follows:

1.Engage the guide catheter in the target coronary artery, confirming synchronized movement of proximal coronary calcification on fluoroscopy.

2.Advance the guidewire with reference to the baseline angiographic images.

3.Perform OCT imaging with manual injection of 8 to 12 mL low–molecular weight dextran.

4.Assess lesion characteristics (location, extent, calcium thickness, and arc) on OCT and determine the IVL target site, referencing synchronized fluoroscopic image.

Assess lesion characteristics (location, extent, calcium thickness, and arc) on OCT and determine the IVL target site, referencing synchronized fluoroscopic image.

5.Perform IVL with an appropriately sized balloon (matching vessel reference diameter for concentric lesions, or quarter-downsizing for eccentric lesions).

Perform IVL with an appropriately sized balloon (matching vessel reference diameter for concentric lesions, or quarter-downsizing for eccentric lesions).

6.Repeat OCT imaging (8-12 mL dextran) to evaluate calcium fracture, modification, and lumen gain after IVL.

7.If calcium modification or lumen expansion is inadequate, perform high-pressure dilation with a noncompliant balloon.

8.Deploy a drug-eluting stent to fully cover the atherosclerotic lesion, extending from the proximal to the distal reference segment with plaque-free margins, under OCT and synchronized fluoroscopic guidance.

10.Perform additional optimization (eg, higher pressure postdilation, larger balloon use, or additional stenting for dissection) as needed, followed by repeat OCT evaluation.

11.Complete with final coronary angiography in 2 projections using <10 mL contrast medium.

After PCI

Renal function, cardiac enzymes, and complete blood count are assessed the following day. If renal function worsens, further monitoring is performed over several days.

Potential Pitfalls

Geographic miss in stent positioning

When relying on fluoroscopic markers (calcified lesions, spine, ribs), geographic miss can occur. Wiring a side branch adjacent to the lesion can help estimate the landing zone and ensure plaque-free margins.

Slow/no-flow phenomenon

Slow/no-flow is more common after calcium modification with rotational or orbital atherectomy than with IVL, as the ultrasonic mechanism of IVL is associated with fewer such events. If chest pain and changes in electrocardiogram occur, angiography with diluted contrast medium can be performed.

Side branch compromise/occlusion

Guidewire protection of side branches originating from the lesion is recommended during IVL. In lesions at high risk for side branch compromise after main vessel stenting (owing to inadequate main vessel dilation, significant contralateral plaque/calcium burden, or ostial disease), consider aggressive protective techniques, such as the jailed balloon technique. If compromise is suspected, angiography with diluted contrast medium is useful.

Allergic reaction to low–molecular weight dextran (Kounis syndrome)

Allergic reactions, including multivessel spasm, have been reported during OCT with dextran.7 In such cases, immediate conversion to intravascular ultrasound-guided PCI is required.

Renal function deterioration from excessive dextran use

In a previous study, ultra–low contrast (<20 mL) OCT-guided PCI using a mean dextran volume of 76 mL was performed safely without contrast-induced nephropathy in 8 patients with advanced CKD.8 Limiting dextran use to ≤100 mL (8-10 pullbacks) is recommended to minimize renal risk.

Conclusions

OCT-guided, ultra–low contrast PCI combined with IVL is a feasible and safe strategy for treating severe coronary calcification in advanced CKD. This approach allows precise lesion preparation and optimal stent deployment while minimizing the risk of contrast-induced nephropathy.
