Current and emerging therapeutic strategies targeting adherent-invasive Escherichia coli in Crohn’s disease: a review
Authors: Layla Ahmed Mohammed Abdelhadi, Yan Liang, Yuexia Liao, Guoqiang Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Considerable evidence has accumulated over the past two decades demonstrating that Escherichia coli, specifically adherent-invasive E. coli, contributes to the pathogenesis of Crohn’s disease. Adherent-invasive E. coli can adhere to and invade intestinal epithelial cells, survive, and replicate within macrophages, thereby enabling a key mechanism that induces chronic inflammation. Despite extensive knowledge of the molecular interactions between adherent-invasive E. coli and the host from these studies, translating this knowledge into targeted therapy remains limited.In this review, we summarize current and proposed treatments to prevent or eliminate adherent-invasive E. coli colonization. We provide a theoretical framework that categorizes these strategies into four main pillars: direct pathogen targeting (e.g., antibiotics, phage therapy), blockade of bacterial virulence factors (e.g., anti-adhesive compounds, QseC inhibitors), host-mediated clearance (e.g., autophagy inducers), and ecological intervention/restoration (e.g., fecal microbiota transplantation, Probiotic, Prebiotic and Probiotics). Finally, we discuss new modalities, including predatory bacteria, siderophore immunization, and other approaches. This review identifies the latest weapons against adherent-invasive E. coli, synthesizes experimental and clinical evidence to provide a comprehensive view of this evolving therapeutic arsenal, and offers ideas for future treatment of Crohn’s disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13099-026-00852-2.

Background

Crohn’s disease (CD) is a chronic, transmural inflammatory bowel disorder thought to result from a complex interplay among genetic susceptibility, environmental factors, and altered gut microbiota, leading to dysregulation of the immune response in susceptible individuals [1]. A key feature of this disease is the proliferation of pro-inflammatory bacterial species, including adherent-invasive Escherichia coli (AIEC), which has been suggested as a pathobiont central to its cause [2, 3]. AIEC was first isolated in 1998 from an ileal biopsy of a patient with CD, designated LF82 (for Lille-France), and has since served as a reference strain for studies of Inflammatory bowel disease(IBD)-associated E. coli strains [4]. Researchers have reported the whole-genome sequence of this pathotype strain [5], along with three other highly active human AIEC isolates, NRG857c [6], HM605 [7], and UM14647 [8], all of which belong to the B2 phylogenetic group. A high prevalence of AIEC has been reported in the ileal mucosa of patients with CD. For example, a recent study reported that AIEC strains were detected in the ileum in 55.0% of CD patients, compared with approximately 21.4% of healthy controls [9]. The higher prevalence of AIEC in CD patients compared to controls suggests a potentially important ecological link, although this study’s data does not clarify if or how AIEC contributes to inflammation or disease development.

The pathogenic process of AIEC is characterized by its ability to adhere to and invade intestinal epithelial cells via bacterial adhesins, such as type 1 pili, which bind the host receptor CEACAM6, overexpressed in the ileal epithelium of CD patients, and by microtubule polymerization and actin recruitment [10, 11]. This disruption of the epithelial barrier is a central event that enables luminal bacteria to reach the lamina propria beneath the epithelium. In the submucosa, upon encountering macrophages, AIEC displays its characteristic “invasive” phenotype. Unlike commensal E. coli, AIEC strains can survive and replicate within macrophages without destroying host cells, thereby secreting high levels of TNF-α, a key cytokine implicated in CD development [12, 13]. Burder et al. [14] reported that the survival strategy in macrophages involves stress-induced persister formation, a genetically drug-susceptible, quiescent (non-growing or slow-growing) bacterium that survives in stressful environments such as antibiotic exposure, acidic conditions, and starvation [15], and subsequently replicates within intracellular biofilm-like communities (IBCs). Additionally, comparative genomic and functional analyses revealed that this common phenotypic outcome is achieved through strain-specific mechanisms, including variations in phagosome manipulation, biofilm architecture, and host-response modulation [14]. A recent study revealed that AIEC ileal colonization is associated with severe intestinal fibrosis, driven by AIEC inhibition of intestinal epithelial cell-derived exosomal miRNA let-7b, which leads to intestinal macrophages adopting a fibrotic phenotype rather than a direct effect on macrophage regulation [16]. Furthermore, it has been reported that the invasiveness of AIEC is increased in the presence of short-chain fatty acids (propionate and butyrate) by upregulating the virulence genes [17, 18], leading to an elevated TNF-α production by macrophages, Caco-2 epithelial monolayer, and in vivo, worsening the colitis in the mouse model (Fig. 1) [19, 20]. In contrast, propionate and butyrate have demonstrated anti-inflammatory effects in human intestinal enteroids [18].

Due to intestinal colonization, adherence, and invasiveness, as well as AIEC intra-macrophagic behavior, targeting AIEC in CD patients has become more challenging. Therefore, the development of targeted therapies to eliminate/neutralize AIEC is urgently required. Hence, this review aimed to provide a concise yet comprehensive overview of current and prospective therapeutic strategies specifically designed to target AIEC in CD patients, categorizing them into four groups according to their mechanistic specificity, moving beyond a simple enumeration of strategies and toward a rational framework that can guide future research and the design of combination therapies.

Methods

In this narrative review, a systematic literature search strategy was employed to ensure comprehensive coverage of the subject. Scientific publications were obtained from PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. The keywords and their combinations included: “Adherent-invasive E. coli,” " AIEC,” “Crohn’s disease,” “Crohn disease,” “Crohns disease,” “Inflammatory Bowel disease,” “Inflammatory Bowel diseases,” “IBD,” “therapy,” “treatment,” “antibiotic,” “phage,” “probiotic,” “fecal microbiota transplantation,” “autophagy,” “immunotherapy,” “virulence factor,” and “dietary intervention.” The search encompassed studies published up to 2025 and was restricted to English-language publications. Articles were selected based on their relevance to (i) Original research articles (in vitro, ex vivo, in vivo animal models, and human clinical trials), authoritative review articles, and meta-analyses. (ii) Studies that explicitly investigated the pathogenic role of AIEC in IBD or evaluated therapeutic strategies that directly or indirectly target AIEC. (iii) Therapeutic Focus: All intervention types were considered, including pharmacological (antibiotics, small molecules), biological (phages, antibodies, peptides), microbial (probiotics, prebiotics, Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)), dietary, and host-targeted (immunomodulators, autophagy inducers), and other approaches. (vi) Priority was given to studies that provided precise mechanistic data on how a given strategy interacts with AIEC virulence or the host response. This methodology aimed to minimize selection bias and construct a robust, evidence-based foundation for evaluating the current and emerging therapeutic arsenal against AIEC in CD.

Anti-AIEC therapeutic strategies

Recently, a growing understanding of AIEC pathogenesis in CD has catalyzed the exploration of various therapeutic options to target this pathogen. In this review, we categorize currently available and proposed interventions targeting AIEC based on their fundamental modes of action, shifting beyond simple cataloging toward a rational analysis (Fig. 2). This framework elucidates the divergent therapeutic philosophies—from directly targeting the bacterium to reconfiguring the host environment—and helps identify synergistic strategies for future combination therapy.

Summary of comparative analysis of Anti-AIEC therapeutic strategies

Category 1: direct pathogen targeting

As summarized in Table 1, these approaches range from broad-spectrum antibiotics to biological agents, each with distinct impacts on the microbiota and resistance. Their main aim is to kill or inhibit AIEC.

Antibiotics

The role of antibiotics in managing AIEC-associated IBD is fundamentally paradoxical. While in vitro studies demonstrate that certain antibiotics, such as ciprofloxacin, rifampin, and clarithromycin, can effectively kill intracellular AIEC within macrophages [36, 37], their low specificity and propensity to cause collateral damage to the commensal microbiota, and the increasing prevalence of multidrug resistance among AIEC clinical isolates to commonly used antibiotics, severely limit their clinical application [38]. Furthermore, antibiotic treatment induces gut dysbiosis, which paradoxically promotes the initial colonization and dramatic expansion of AIEC, thereby potentially exacerbating the disease [21, 39]. Additionally, high resistance to β-lactams (50% of tested AIEC strains) and expanded-spectrum cephalosporins has been reported [40]. Despite their drawbacks, several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have examined the impact of prolonged antibiotic regimens on the prevention of postoperative CD recurrence. For example, Ornidazole demonstrated efficacy in preventing CD recurrence at 1 year after ileocolonic resection [41]. In a systematic review of RCTs, nitroimidazoles have been shown to effectively decrease clinical and endoscopic recurrence rates; however, they are associated with severe side effects [42]. Another meta-analysis of RCTs reported that administering antibacterial agents to patients with CD increased the likelihood of remission by 1.35-fold compared with those who did not receive them [43]. The combination of ciprofloxacin and metronidazole is commonly used in clinical practice to promote healing of complicated fistulas in patients with CD; however, limited data support this strategy [44, 45]. Rifamycin derivatives have been reported to induce remission in patients with active CD, whether used alone or in combination with other antibiotics [46, 47] (Table 2). Recently, an RCT was conducted from May 2016 to June 2021, during which 155 patients were screened, and 24 were randomized. They reported that the AIEC of all patients were sensitive to ciprofloxacin and rifaximin in vitro, and there was no statistically significant difference in endoscopic response between the Cipro-Rifa (50%) and placebo groups (33%). Additionally, 7/10 patients developed AIEC, and 3/10 retained AIEC+ (and gained ciprofloxacin resistance) in the antibiotic-treated group [48]. This result highlights the need to move away from antibiotics targeting AIEC in CD patients toward alternatives, such as modulating the gut microbiome. Consequently, the trial limits the use of antibiotic combinations aimed at AIEC colonization in clinical settings.

Antibiotics used in CD treatment

Peptide Pac-525 with RGD motif

To eliminate intracellular AIEC reservoirs, a novel strategy to address this critical challenge is to develop antimicrobial agents that actively penetrate host cells. For example, RGD-Pac525 is an antimicrobial peptide linked to Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) for eukaryotic cell penetration. When tested in macrophages and intestinal organoids, it significantly reduced the intracellular burden of AIEC-like pathogens without compromising host-cell viability [49]. This method combines bacterial killing and host-targeted nanoparticles, offering a framework for treatments that can penetrate and eliminate the intracellular AIEC niche.

Phage therapy

Phage therapy, which uses phages to manage bacterial infections, has been used since 1919 to prevent or treat various infections [50]. Phages, unlike antibiotics, can specifically lyse the target organism without affecting other bacterial populations, making them a suitable choice for eliminating drug-resistant bacteria in the gut [51]. Recently, it has been successfully tested against various pathovars of E. coli, including enterotoxigenic (ETEC), enteropathogenic (EPEC), enteroaggregative (EAEC), and enterohemorrhagic (EHEC) [52–54], as well as AIEC [22]. A recent study reported that a single dose of an orally administered cocktail of three virulent bacteriophages led to a significant reduction in faecal AIEC counts for 2 weeks and reduced colitis symptoms in a mouse model [22]. Another study found that a seven-lytic phage cocktail [EcoActive™] was effective against 95% of AIEC strains tested and did not lyse any non-AIEC strains. Furthermore, they reported that dysbiosis was not induced by long-term administration of the EcoActive™ cocktail in healthy mice [55]. Jackson et al. found that HER259 phage is active against clinical AIEC NGR857c strains, attenuates the virulence of this strain by suppressing the FimH adhesin, and exhibits synergistic therapeutic effects with sub-therapeutic budesonide in gnotobiotic models of AIEC-driven colitis [56].

Predatory bacteria

Bdellovibrio and related organisms (BALOs) are small Gram-negative predatory bacteria in the Deltaproteobacteria that feed on other Gram-negative pathogens [57]. Its predatory effect on oral pathogens was evaluated, and it was found to reduce biofilm-associated bacteria [58, 59]. The predatory impact of Bdellovibrio bacteriovorus on AIEC was tested; it significantly reduced AIEC LF82 adhesion and invasion into B.bacteriovorus pre-treated Caco-2 cells and inhibited LF82 biofilm formation when co-cultured with AIEC [23].

Category 2: Virulence factor blockade (pathogen disarmament)

This group focused on neutralizing key virulence genes of the AIEC pathogen to disarm it, rather than killing it. As shown in Table 1, this specific approach minimizes damage to the gut microbiota and lowers the risk of antibiotic resistance.

Anti-adhesive strategies

Among the virulence factors of AIEC, FimH, a mannose-binding adhesin, has been shown to promote intestinal inflammation by interacting with CEACAM 6 and toll-like receptor 4 in intestinal epithelial cells from patients with CD [60]. Given the considerable challenges of targeting intracellular bacteria, the strategic approach has sensibly shifted to initially blocking adhesion. This has prompted investigation of FimH antagonists, with heptyl mannoside (HM) emerging as the most effective inhibitor of AIEC adhesion. According to a previous report, when HM was conjugated to an isopropylamide (1 A-HM) or β-cyclodextrin and administered orally to mice, it significantly lowered LF82 levels in feces and reduced bacteria associated with the gut mucosa [61]. This study has recently been validated by another research that reported HM is a potent FimH antagonist capable of blocking AIEC adhesion to the intestinal mucosa in CD patients [62]. In contrast, FimH inhibitors thiazolylaminomannosides (TazMans) were reported to efficiently prevent AIEC attachment to intestinal cells in an ex vivo model, at approximately 10,000-fold and 100-fold lower concentrations than mannose and the potent FimH inhibitor heptylmannoside, respectively [63]. A second-generation TazMans with improved chemical stability were reported to reduce adhesion of clinical isolates of AIEC LF82 in vitro [64].

Alongside synthetic compounds, probiotic and prebiotic strategies have been explored for their potential efficacy as anti-adhesion agents. For example, Saccharomyces cerevisiae CNCM I-3856 exhibits significant anti-adhesive properties [65]. Further investigation revealed that this activity is linked to its yeast wall components: mannan, β-glucan, and α-glucan, capable of inhibiting over 95% of AIEC adhesion in vitro [66]. A novel bioinspired technique has been developed based on the carbohydrate decoy principle, wherein mannan oligosaccharides (MOS) are encapsulated within porous microgels. This approach competes with FimH in preclinical settings, inhibiting mucosal colonization, reducing inflammation, and markedly restoring microbial diversity [67]. The clinical potential of the FimH blocker antagonist TAK-018 has been reported to inhibit AIEC adhesion and reduce inflammation. It was well tolerated in patients with CD in a phase 1 clinical trial [60]. Furthermore, Sibofimloc (EB8018/TAK-018) is a gut-restricted, orally administered, first-in-class FimH blocker that targets FimH, a TLR4 receptor expressed on E. coli and other Enterobacteriaceae in patients with CD [68]. It has been shown to reduce inflammatory biomarkers in patients with CD [24] In addition to the ecological benefits of microbiome restoration demonstrated in microgel experiments, these factors reduce inflammatory biomarkers in patients with CD, thereby facilitating the development of non-antibiotic strategies targeting virulence mechanisms to mitigate dysbiosis and inflammation in CD.

Targeting inter-kingdom signaling

Targeting the conserved membrane histidine sensor kinase, QseC, with the biochemical inhibitor LED209 could reduce AIEC virulence in vitro and in vivo [25]. Rook et al. reported that inactivation of QseC in the AIEC strain reduced flagellar gene expression, flagellar assembly, and motility in LF82 in vitro. It reduced AIEC colonization and persistence within a low-complexity murine microbiota in vivo. Furthermore, it reduced disease severity across multiple preclinical colitis models without directly affecting host immune responses or luminal catecholamine levels. This study establishes QseC-mediated bacterial signaling as a factor in IBD development and supports the therapeutic potential of targeting bacterial virulence pathways to treat colitogenic microbiota.

Disrupting nutrient scavenging

Siderophores synthesized by AIEC mediate iron acquisition, which is essential for AIEC growth, survival, and virulence. Germer et al. [26] reported that immunizing mice with enterobactin conjugated to the mucosal adjuvant cholera toxin subunit B (CTB) induced robust antibody production that neutralizes siderophore function. Thus, it decreases AIEC mucosal association, lowers pathogen burden, and reduces inflammation across multiple mouse models. This work proposes siderophore immunization as a targeted strategy to alleviate AIEC-driven colitis.

Targeting rfaP/ybaT virulence factor

Leccese et al. identified that two AIEC virulence determinants, specifically the inner membrane transport protein (rfaP) and the LPS-core heptose kinase (ybaT), directly induce IL-23 hypersecretion and the production of IFNγ-producing pathogenic Th17 (pTh17) cells in CD patients [27]. They reported that the mutant LF82 lacking these two genes exhibited compromised intracellular persistence in CD-DCs, along with significantly reduced IL-23 secretion and impaired trans differentiation into pTh17 cells. This result provides insights into using these factors as critical bacterial targets to develop more effective therapeutic strategies, potentially offering the significant benefit of selectively inhibiting IL-23/pTh17 axis activation while preserving protective immune cells.

Weakening pathogen structural integrity

The EnvC protein is identified as a key driver of bacterial cell division in AIEC. Mikki et al. [28] reported that mutation of envC in AIEC impaired cell division and compromised the outer membrane barrier, thereby making the cells susceptible to vancomycin. Furthermore, this mutant had reduced colonization ability in a dysbiotic mouse model. This study provides insight into an anti-virulence approach targeting the cell wall by inhibiting EnvC, which could be used as a potential therapeutic intervention for CD.

Sialidase inhibition

A strategy that targets the sialidase inhibitor poly-S-Neu5Ac2en100 of bacterial sialidases, the enzymes responsible for cleaving sialic acids and removing O-acetyl groups from acetylated sialic acids on protective mucins [29]. This inhibitor reduced AIEC LF82 growth in vitro and ex vivo by efficiently blocking sialidase-mediated sialic acid foraging from mucins. This result highlights a novel therapeutic approach for CD.

Propanediol dehydratase (pduC) inhibition

A rational approach to anti-virulence therapy that targets essential, pathogen-specific metabolic pathways has recently been explored [69]. The propanediol utilization (pdu) operon, which includes the pduC enzyme, is a key metabolic determinant of AIEC colonization in the gut. The authors used computational drug discovery to successfully screen for and identify novel small-molecule inhibitors of pduC with promising binding affinity and drug-like properties, offering a blueprint for a particular ‘metabolic disarmament’ strategy against AIEC.

Category 3: Host-mediated clearance & immunomodulation

Instead of targeting the bacterium, host-mediated strategies strengthen the patient’s defenses against AIEC, primarily to eliminate its intracellular reservoir and restore immune balance in CD.

Autophagy induction

Autophagy is an intracellular process that degrades excess, damaged, or aged proteins and organelles to preserve cellular stability [70]. Defective autophagy is strongly correlated with IBD development. Polymorphisms in the NOD2, IRGM, and ATG16L1 genes are associated with CD and can impair autophagy’s ability to eliminate intracellular microorganisms [71–74]. Dendritic cells from patients with CD who express the NOD2 and ATG16L1 risk variants have been reported to exhibit defective bacterial trafficking, antigen presentation, and autophagy induction [75]. Studies have shown that modulation of autophagy could be a therapeutic approach for IBD by controlling inflammation and eliminating intestinal bacteria [76]. Lapaquette et al. [30]. Showed that the pharmacological stimulation of autophagy with rapamycin resulted in a significant reduction in the number of intra-macrophagic AIEC and the level of secreted pro-inflammatory cytokines. These findings support the hypothesis that restoring bacterium-triggered autophagy in patients with polymorphisms in ATG16L1, IRGM, or NOD2 (or combinations of these) could be a practical therapeutic approach to both reduce the number of internal AIEC bacteria and ameliorate the inflammatory response [30].

Immunoregulatory therapy

Immunoregulatory therapy is an emerging approach to reduce or control excessive immune responses to AIEC in patients with CD. Bovine lactoferrin, present in milk, acts through two mechanisms: it blocks the activity of virulence factors. It also exerts immunomodulatory effects in a mouse model and has been reported to inhibit AIEC by binding to type 1 pili. Additionally, it significantly inhibits the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-8, and IL-6, in vitro [31]. When administered orally, it alleviated the symptoms in LF82/DSS-induced colitis in mice [77]. Another highly targeted immunomodulatory approach involves activating specific host receptors that regulate gut homeostasis. In AIEC-infected mice, a G protein-coupled receptor 43 (GPR43) agonist that mimics propionate’s anti-inflammatory signal provided dual therapeutic benefits. It primarily reduced colitis severity and pro-inflammatory cytokines through host-mediated anti-inflammatory effects. The agonist increased the expression of epithelial-derived antimicrobial peptides (Reg3β/γ), demonstrating that modifying a single host signaling pathway can reduce inflammation and enhance mucosal defense against pathogens [19]. This shows that immunomodulation can directly clear pathogens. Administration of interleukin-18 (IL-18) has been shown to correct the defective epithelial-immune axis in AIEC-infected mice. Acting downstream of IL-22/STAT3 signaling, IL-18 simultaneously promotes epithelial stem cell expansion for tissue repair, restores Paneth cell antimicrobial function, and reactivates protective IFNγ-producing T cells, thereby promoting adequate AIEC clearance [78]. This exemplifies a systems-level immunomodulatory approach that coordinates multiple host defenses. Furthermore, anti-TNF agents have been reported to inhibit AIEC replication in macrophages by modulating FLOT-1 and Chitinase 3-like 1 expression in CD patients [79].

The mechanism by which AIEC exacerbates intestinal fibrosis in CD has recently been identified [16]. A recent study reported that AIEC colonization of intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) promotes fibrosis, facilitated by low levels of microRNA let-7b expression and by polarization of let-7b toward a profibrotic (CD16+CD206+) phenotype when it is packaged into exosomes by macrophages. The process is mediated by let-7 b targeting TGFβR1, a key regulator of the fibrotic TGF-β/Smad pathway. These findings underscore that the AIEC-exosomal let-7b-macrophage axis could be a potential therapeutic target to reduce fibrosis in CD [14].

Category 4: ecological restoration & competitive exclusion

Ecological strategies target AIEC by promoting a healthy microbiota through competitive exclusion, creating an environment that deters AIEC. As shown in Table 1, these broad interventions can offer long-term management by addressing dysbiosis.

Fecal microbiota transplantation

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) is a fundamental therapeutic approach that was first reported in Western medical literature 50 years ago [80] and proposed as a treatment for IBD over 25 years ago [32, 81]. It has demonstrated effectiveness in managing recurrent C. difficile infection [82, 83]. Studies have shown that the fecal flora composition in patients with IBD differs from that in healthy individuals [84, 85]. In IBD patients, there is an imbalance between pathogenic and commensal gut flora, with reduced bacterial numbers in the inflamed area [86]. This method can restore key components of the gut microbiota and alleviate inappropriate immune activation in CD, thereby rendering the intestinal environment unfavorable for AIEC colonization [87]. However, due to the chronicity of dysbiosis in IBD patients, multiple FMTs may be needed [88]. Studies suggest that FMT is more effective initially when administered via the upper gastrointestinal tract, with rates ranging from 75% to 100%, compared with 30% to 58% when administered via the lower gastrointestinal tract [89].

In a systematic review of 18 trials of FMT for IBD treatment, 122 patients achieved remission at a 45% rate. Publication bias arising from case reports was minimized by conducting a subgroup analysis primarily on cohort studies; as a result, the efficacy rate in this group decreased to 36% [90]. Another RCT investigated the effectiveness of FMT in patients with UC. The study involved administering FMT via colonoscopic infusion, followed by multi-donor FMT enemas, five days per week for eight weeks. The results showed that patients who achieved remission after FMT had higher microbial diversity and higher levels of Eubacterium hallii and Roseburia inulivorans in their stool and colon samples than those who did not [32]. They reported that donor feces containing Bacteroides were associated with remission in patients who underwent FMT, whereas donor stool containing Streptococcus species was associated with no response to FMT [32]. In a systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 cohort studies and one randomized controlled trial involving 228 patients to evaluate the efficacy and safety of FMT in CD, the authors found that 57% of adult patients achieved remission 2–4 weeks after FMT. Furthermore, they reported that FMT has significantly reduced CD activity index scores 4 to 8 weeks after FMT [91]. Initial investigations indicate that FMT may be a promising treatment for IBD [92]. However, extensive controlled trials are required to confirm its efficacy and safety.

Dietary intervention

Diet is an essential factor shaping the structure and organization of the host’s gut flora. Dietary interventions are regarded as cost-effective and simple methods for modulating the intestinal microbiota and managing disorders associated with microbiome imbalances [93]. The Crohn’s disease exclusion diet (CDED) is based on the theory that specific components of the Western diet promote a microbiome that promotes inflammation and may damage the gut mucosa. The diet focuses on eliminating gluten, dairy, and all baked products, as well as animal fats, emulsifiers, and all canned or processed foods [94]. A recent RCT in children showed that the CDED, combined with partial enteral feeding, achieved remission rates comparable to those obtained with exclusive enteral feeding [33]. CD patients often exhibit deficiencies in methyl-donor molecules such as folate and vitamin B12, along with alterations in DNA methylation. A Gimier et al. (2020) reported that adding methyl-donor molecules to the diet of a mouse model of CD reduces CEACAM6 gene expression and, consequently, decreases AIEC gut colonization [95]. Vitamin D plays a crucial role in maintaining intestinal mucosal integrity by protecting tight junctions. Mounting evidence indicates that Vitamin D deficiency increases susceptibility to AIEC colonization in DSS-treated mice, accompanied by significant epithelial barrier dysfunction and severe colonic injury. While incubation of polarized epithelial Caco-2-BBe cells with 1,25(OH)2 vitamin D3 protects the intestinal barrier against AIEC-induced degradation [96]. Thus, maintaining normal blood vitamin D levels may reduce AIEC colonization in individuals at risk.

Maltodextrin (MDX), a polysaccharide derived from starch hydrolysis, has been shown to significantly increase type 1 pili expression in AIEC LF82, enhance adhesion to gut epithelial cells, and promote biofilm formation [97]. The Specific Carbohydrate Diet (SCD) was initially developed in the 1920s as a treatment for celiac disease [98]. However, it has recently received more attention as a potential therapeutic regimen for IBD [99–101]. The SCD restricts the intake of complex carbohydrates, starches, cereals, and dairy products while promoting the consumption of certain vegetables, meats, and homemade yogurt, and permitting limited amounts of beans and hard cheeses. Studies have shown that this diet successfully excludes MDX [97], leading to a decreased risk of developing IBD [102]. Lau et al. (2021) identified low dietary fiber combined with oral antibiotics as an environmental factor that enhances the expansion of intestinal AIEC in the CD mouse model [103]. Although a systematic review of RCTs by Peters et al. [104] found that not all fibers are equally beneficial in IBD. Notably, fructo-oligosaccharides (FOS) were associated with lower patient well-being, whereas inulin showed more promising effects. Plissonneau et al. (2022) reported that supplementation with extruded linseed in a physically active mouse model of CD challenged with AIEC LF82 led to increased numbers of beneficial bacteria and butyrate levels, which were correlated with the Firmicutes/Proteobacteria ratio [105]. Despite this evidence, further well-designed, controlled trials are required to evaluate the impact of diet on AIEC gut colonization and its therapeutic potential, alone or in combination with other methods.

Probiotics, prebiotics, and postbiotics

Probiotics are live microorganisms, such as yeast and bacteria, that may confer beneficial health effects [106]. Numerous probiotic strains have since been examined in clinical trials, particularly in IBD [106, 107]. Probiotics have the potential to act as effective agents in managing pathogen growth, thereby controlling or preventing disease. Probiotics can adhere to the gut mucosa and prevent pathogenic bacteria from adhering to these sites; E. coli Nissle 1917 has demonstrated robust, significant inhibitory effects on AIEC adhesion and invasion [108]. A combination of various probiotic groups typically exhibits synergistic effects among themselves and broadens the range of efficacy against diverse infections [109]. A Study has shown that co-infection of AIEC LF82 with Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium led to a significant reduction in AIEC LF82 adhesion and persistence within HT29 intestinal epithelial cells [34], thereby inhibiting IL-8 release. Additionally, it significantly impaired the release of polarizing cytokines associated with the IL-23/Th17 axis by decreasing LF82 survival within macrophages and dendritic cells [26]. Others demonstrated reduced survival and proliferation of AIEC when co-cultured with Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG and Lactobacillus reuteri 1063 [110]. Additionally, they reported that the prebiotic long-chain arabinoxylans reduced mucin adherence in AIEC. In contrast, the prebiotics inulin and galacto-oligosaccharides restricted AIEC proliferation and survival [110, 111]. Emerging research highlights the potential of combining probiotics with natural bioactive compounds. A combination of a hydroalcoholic extract of Arthrospira platensis (Spirulina) with the probiotic Lactobacillus helveticus demonstrated a potent synergistic effect against AIEC. This combination not only blocked AIEC adhesion and invasion with high efficacy but also modulated the host inflammatory response by downregulating the NF-κB and STAT3 pathways and increasing the anti-inflammatory IL-10T [112]. Despite the promising results, the use of probiotics as a therapeutic strategy is limited by many obstacles. These include the risk of translocation of E. coli Nissle 1917 across the intestinal epithelium, which may lead to adverse effects in immunocompromised patients with impaired gut microbiota [113], and probiotics’ ability to survive bile salts and gastric acid in the gastrointestinal tract [114].

Postbiotics are defined as a “preparation of inanimate microorganisms and/or their components that confers a health benefit on the host.“ [115] They include short-chain fatty acids such as butyric, propionic, and acetic acids [116]. Butyrate and its receptor agonist were reported to protect intestinal epithelial cells from AIEC-induced damage by activating the host FFAR3 receptor. This signaling maintains mitochondrial health and barrier integrity, thereby preventing AIEC invasion and transcytosis [35]. Additionally, it suppressed IL-6, TNF-α, CD40, and CD80 by > 50% and enhanced phagocytosis of AIEC by 62% in monocytes/macrophages [117]. This approach highlights the therapeutic potential of restoring a specific host-microbe metabolic interaction disrupted in CD, while acknowledging that SCFAs may have complex and sometimes contradictory effects on pathogen virulence.

Colicins are bacteriocins that exhibit species specificity and selectively target bacteria through distinct modes of action [118]. Colicins E1 and E9 have exhibited potent eradication of biofilm-associated, cell-adherent, and intracellular AIEC strains (LF82, HM95, 419, and 615); these colicins were more effective than metronidazole and ciprofloxacin [119]. In contrast to antibiotics, colicins exhibit selectivity, indicating their potential to efficiently eradicate a particular microorganism, such as AIEC, while conserving the bacterial community that constitutes the healthy gut microbiome. They do not show toxicity towards a macrophage cell line or stimulate the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [119]. A genetically modified E. coli Nissle 1917 that secretes a specific colisin has been tested for targeting the AIEC strain. It has been shown that this method targets a specific etiological agent and offers advantages over conventional antibiotics by potentially reducing the risk of resistance, thereby potentially leading to long-lasting remission [120].

A recent study evaluated the efficacy of various combinations of bacterial and plant extracts in reducing AIEC levels in the ileal mucosa of patients with CD, suggesting a promising approach to disease management. It reported that a formulation combining two Lacticaseibacillus strains (Lbs. casei and Lbs. rhamnosus) with walnut extract significantly reduces colitis in mice. Additionally, this combination has maintained intestinal mucosal integrity [121]. Overall, few studies have demonstrated the therapeutic effects of postbiotics against AIEC in vitro and in mouse models; validation of safety, specificity, and efficacy in clinical trials is required.

Challenge and limitation

Despite promising preclinical data, AIEC-targeted therapies face challenges, including strain heterogeneity, the lack of standardized detection assays, and limited clinical validation. Research should integrate genomic, metabolomic, and immunologic profiling to stratify patient subgroups by responsiveness.

Conclusion and future prospects

The interplay between AIEC and CD mechanisms highlights AIEC as a key therapeutic target. This review covers anti-pathobiont strategies, ranging from antibiotics and ecological restoration to targeted therapies such as phage therapy, virulence blockade, and immunomodulation. A trade-off exists: broad-spectrum agents work quickly but risk disrupting the microbiota and promoting resistance, while targeted approaches promise precision but are mostly in early development.
