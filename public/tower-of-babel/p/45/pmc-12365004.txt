Evaluation of Global Surgery Perspectives and Knowledge Amongst Past Surgical Trainees in East, Central, and Southern Africa: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Yvan Zolo, Wakisa Mulwafu, Salome Maswime
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsAfrica faces a shortage of academic Global Surgery programs, limiting trainee exposure despite pressing surgical care inequities. Global Surgery seeks to improve access through systems and policy strengthening.MethodsA cross‐sectional electronic survey was conducted among COSECSA‐affiliated registrars and specialists to assess their Global Surgery knowledge and perspectives.ResultsOut of 75 responses, the age range 36–40 years was the most represented (32.39%). Participants came from 15 countries, Ethiopia, Malawi, and Uganda being the most represented. Most respondents were General Surgery specialists and had completed training within the past 1–5 years. The majority practiced in urban settings (68%), followed by rural (20%) and peri‐urban (12%). Familiarity with Global Surgery principles varied: 38.7% reported low, 33.3% moderate, and 28% high familiarity. Exposure to formal training in Global Surgery was reported by 17.6%, with 75.7% having none. Exposure included webinars (50.7%) and conferences (34.7%), with none having attained a degree in Global Surgery. Knowledge of fundamental Global Surgery concepts varied, with the Surgical Checklist most recognized (86.7%) and Geospatial Mapping least familiar. Respondents perceived their Global Surgery skills as Basic (40.5%), Insufficient (31.1%), Advanced (12.2%), and Expert (1.3%). 82.4% indicated that the inclusion of Global Surgery into the formal curriculum would be beneficial.ConclusionThere is limited knowledge and understanding of key concepts in Global Surgery by past surgical trainees and specialists, despite the challenges they must address in providing care in their clinical setting such as barriers to access, infrastructure, equipment, technology, and information systems. There is strong support for Global Surgery training to be incorporated into surgical training programmes, and a high interest in furthering Global Surgery education, by past and present surgical trainees in East, Central, and Southern Africa.

Introduction

Global Surgery is an emerging discipline at the intersection of surgery, public health, and public policy and aims to improve surgical care delivery and systems, particularly in resource‐limited settings [1]. Its core aim is to ensure safe, timely, universal access to essential surgical services by strengthening systems, shaping policies, and implementing surgical programs [2]. The comprehensive approach integrates components of health systems strengthening, including leadership development, to improve surgical services and systems [2, 3].

Eastern, Central, and Southern Africa face a critical shortage of surgeons due to limited training, workforce migration, and underinvestment in health systems [4]. In response, the College of Surgeons of East, Central, and Southern Africa, COSECSA, was established in 1999 to increase surgical capacity across member countries [5]. COSECSA is the largest surgical training institution in the region, covering 14 member (these countries are: Botswana, Burundi, Ethiopia, Kenya, Malawi, Mozambique, Namibia, Rwanda, South Sudan, Sudan, Tanzania, Uganda, Zambia, and Zimbabwe) [6]. Through competency‐based programs, it has produced over 1000 surgeons, addressing workforce gaps and promoting high‐quality surgical education [6]. Beyond increasing the surgical workforce, Global Surgery also tackles barriers to surgical care—accessibility, affordability, and acceptability—by advocating for resources, policy reform, and leadership training [4, 7].

Previous studies have examined various aspects of surgical training programs globally, including their influence on workforce training, surgical outcomes, and health system strengthening [8, 9, 10, 11]. For instance, the Lancet Commission on Global Surgery have emphasized the need for surgical system development in low‐ and middle‐income countries [12, 13], and academic institutions have evaluated training models in Latin America, Southeast Asia, and parts of sub‐Saharan Africa [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. However, much of this study focuses on technical capacity, surgical output, or institutional partnerships, with limited insight into the lived experiences and professional development of African‐trained surgical providers themselves related to Global Surgery. There remains a gap in understanding the perceptions of trained surgeons on Global Surgery. This study attempted to address that gap by exploring the Global Surgery perspectives and knowledge of some COSECSA‐trained surgeons and registrars.

Methodology

Study Design and Participants

This was a cross‐sectional descriptive, exploratory study targeting surgeons trained by the College of Surgeons of East, Central, and Southern Africa (COSECSA). Eligible participants included all individuals who had completed COSECSA training by the time of the survey, which was estimated to be 900 individuals. Surgeons not affiliated with COSECSA were excluded. Data were collected through an anonymous electronic survey administered via the REDCap platform [20] and disseminated through the COSECSA membership database.

Survey Development and Validation

The survey was developed collaboratively by all co‐authors, each of whom has expertise in global surgery education, surgical training in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), and academic program evaluation. Survey items were informed by a targeted literature review on global surgery education frameworks, previous needs assessments in surgical education, and the authors' direct experience with COSECSA and other global surgery initiatives. Draft versions of the survey were refined over a series of working sessions among the author team.

To assess content clarity and relevance, the draft survey was pilot‐tested with five COSECSA‐affiliated individuals (e.g., trainees and recent graduates) who did not participate in the final data collection. Feedback from the pilot phase informed revisions in wording, sequencing, and overall flow. The revised survey was then reviewed by two independent experts in global surgery education from the University of Cape Town's Global Surgery Division for face validity.

No formal psychometric validation (e.g., construct validity or reliability testing) was conducted. This was deemed appropriate as the survey's primary aim was exploratory: to gather data on familiarity with, perceptions of, and self‐reported exposure to global surgery concepts among COSECSA‐trained surgeons. Given the lack of prior research in this population and context, the instrument was designed as a needs assessment tool rather than a standardized instrument intended for generalization or repeated use.

Survey Content and Language

The final survey consisted of six sections: (1) demographics, (2) familiarity with global surgery, (3) needs assessment, (4) perceptions and barriers, (5) future plans, and (6) knowledge assessment. The survey was developed and administered in English, which is the official working and instructional language of COSECSA. It was not translated into other languages, as all COSECSA training, assessments, and communication occur in English.

Survey Administration

The survey was open for 20 weeks. Personalized email invitations were distributed through the COSECSA Secretariat. Each invitation included a brief description of the study, a digital informed consent form (see Appendix 1), and a unique link to the online survey. To increase participation, reminder emails were sent, for a total of four reminder cycles.

Data Analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics to summarize participant characteristics and response patterns. Qualitative responses to open‐ended questions were analyzed using content analysis to identify common themes. Associations between participants' knowledge of global surgery and their exposure to formal training were assessed using the χ 2 test in R (Version 4.4.2) [21].

Ethics and Privacy

Informed consent (Appendix 1) was obtained from all participants before completion of the survey (Appendix 2). Confidentiality and anonymity were strictly maintained throughout data collection and analysis. No personally identifiable information was collected. All data were stored securely, reported in aggregate form, and accessed only by the research team. Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Cape Town's (UCT) Surgical Departmental Review Committee (Appendix 3), COSECSA's Institutional Review Board (Appendix 4) and the University of Cape Town's Human Research Ethics Committee with reference number HREC REF: 289/2024 (Appendix 5).

Participant privacy was prioritized. All data was kept confidential, with no personally identifiable information disclosed. Data was securely stored, reported in aggregate form, and accessible only to the research team. Participants could contact the privacy officer for any concerns.

Familiarity With Global Surgery Principles

Respondents assessed their familiarity with the core principles of Global Surgery on a scale from 1 (not familiar at all) to 5 (very familiar). The most common response, chosen by 25 participants (33.3%), indicated a moderate level of familiarity [3]. A further 19 respondents (25.3%) rated their familiarity as 4 (somewhat familiar), while 13 (17.3%) described themselves as quite familiar. Meanwhile, 10 respondents (13.3%) reported having little to no familiarity [1], and only 8 (10.6%) considered themselves very familiar [5] (Figure 3).

The responses regarding formal training or education on Global Surgery principles reveal a distinct pattern. Thirteen respondents (17.3%) confirmed they had received formal training, while 56 (74.7%) respondents reported they had not. Additionally, 5 (6.7%) respondents were unsure or did not remember their training status, and 1(1.3%) response was missing.

Needs Assessment

For resources, 19 (25.3%) considered their settings well‐resourced, 13 (17.3%) as Good, and 6 (8%) as Very Good. However, 52 (69.3%) rated them poorly, with 35 (46.7%) as Average, 17 (22.7%) as Poor, and 2 (2.7%) as Very Poor. One (1.3%) did not respond. On equipment, 63 (84%) were dissatisfied, and 12 (16%) were satisfied. As for infrastructure, 30 (40%) were satisfied, and 45 (60%) dissatisfied.

On providing adequate surgical services, 43 (57.3%) felt capable, 20 (26.7%) did not, and 11 (14.7%) were unsure. Most (71, 95.9%) cited health system constraints, while 4 (5.3%) were not constrained.

For the emergency referral system, 58 (77.3%) were dissatisfied, and 17 (22.7%) were satisfied. On drug availability, 53 (71.6%) were dissatisfied, and 21 (28.4%) satisfied.

In terms of information systems, 58 (77.3%) were dissatisfied, and 17 (22.7%) satisfied. Quality assurance systems included mortality meetings (40, 53.3%), data registries (15, 20%), and audits (19, 25.3%), with 1 (1.3%) reporting all three.

Perceptions and Exposure

Most respondents (60%, 45) held positive views on Global Surgery, while 26.7% (20) had mixed feelings. Eight (10.7%) had no perception, and 2 (2.7%) expressed a negative view.

On the importance of covering Global Surgery in surgical training, 88% (66) considered it important, 10.7% (8) were uncertain, and 1.3% (1) did not think it was important.

When asked if learning more about Global Surgery would benefit their training, 85.3% (64) affirmed it would, 9.3% (7) were unsure, and 5.3% (4) felt it would not.

Barriers to including Global Surgery in the curriculum included the lack of specialists (61.3%, 46), underdeveloped curriculum (45.3%, 34), and no consensus on its necessity (46.7%, 35). Some (24%, 18) viewed it as a concept from high‐income countries. A few (5.3%, 4) felt it was irrelevant to African surgeons, and another 5.3% (4) cited general unawareness. Additionally, 2.7% (2) mentioned the heavy workload of trainees as a limiting factor.

Future Plans in Global Surgery

Most respondents (81.3%, 61) felt Global Surgery is relevant for COSECSA trainees, while 16% (12) were unsure, and 1.3% (1) thought it was not relevant. One (1.3%) response was missing.

On interest in a short course in Global Surgery, 92% (69) expressed enthusiasm, 2.7% (2) were unsure, and 5.3% (4) were not interested.

Regarding a 5‐day short course, 88% (66) showed interest, with 54.7% (41) preferring in‐person and 33.3% (25) opting for online. 5.3% (4) were unsure, and 6.7% (5) were not interested.

For Global Surgery research involvement, 50.7% (38) were interested in co‐investigating, 24% (18) in leading as principal investigator, and 8% (6) in data collection. 12% (9) were unsure, and 4.2% (3) were not interested. One (1.3%) response was missing.

On further education, 20% (15) were interested in a PhD in Global Surgery, 24% (18) in a Master of Global Surgery, 12% (9) in a PhD in Surgery, and 8% (6) in a Master of Surgical Science. Other interests included a Master of Global Health (6.7%, 5) and a Master of Public Health (9.3%, 7). However, 18.7% (14) were not interested in any programs. One (1.3%) response was missing and excluded from this analysis.

Knowledge of Global Surgery

Respondents reported varying Global Surgery proficiency levels: 30 (40%) as Basic, 23 (30.7%) as Insufficient, 9 (12%) as Advanced, 1 (1.3%) as Expert, and 11 (14.7%) as Intermediate. One (1.3%) did not provide a rating.

Most respondents (52%, 39) correctly identified that 5 billion people globally lack access to timely, safe, and affordable surgical and anaesthesia care. Regarding Africa, 21.3% (16) correctly noted that 1 billion people lack access. Caesarean section was identified as the most common surgical operation by 77.3% (58).

On the likelihood of Africans dying from surgical complications compared to high‐income countries, 20% (15) correctly stated that Africans are twice as likely to die. Additionally, 29.3% (22) recognized that basic surgical services could avert 77.2 billion DALYs annually.

Regarding the focus of Global Surgery, 46.7% (35) cited strengthening Surgical Systems, and 37.3% (28) cited strengthening Surgical Services. Surgical Training (8%, 6 mentions), Surgical Leadership (2.7%, 2 mentions), and Surgical Innovation (2.7%, 2 mentions) were also noted.

Statistical Analysis of Knowledge Level and Formal Training in Global Surgery

The χ 2 test of independence was performed to determine whether there is an association between the level of knowledge in Global Surgery and having received formal training. This statistical test is appropriate for evaluating whether there is a significant association between these two categorical variables. By comparing the observed frequencies with expected frequencies under the assumption of independence, the test determines if the distribution of knowledge levels is significantly related to formal training in Global Surgery.

The results revealed a Chi‐square statistic of 26.39 with 8 degrees of freedom, yielding a p‐value of 0.0009 (p‐value < 0.05), this suggests a statistically significant relationship between knowledge of Global Surgery level and formal training in Global Surgery. To assess the strength of the statistically significant relationship, Cramér's V was calculated and yielded a value of 0.42, indicating a moderate association between formal training and level of knowledge in Global Surgery.

Discussion

Summary of Results

This study reveals critical insights into the training and practice environments of surgical professionals in East, Central, and Southern Africa. Despite most respondents having completed their training recently and working in urban settings, formal education in Global Surgery remains scarce. A significant portion of surgeons have limited familiarity with its principles, highlighting a gap in knowledge that could impact service delivery. Furthermore, the high proportion of professionals working in under‐resourced settings with inadequate equipment emphasizes the pressing need for improved infrastructure. The strong support for incorporating Global Surgery into training programs highlights a significant demand for curriculum reform and the development of advanced degrees, such as Master's and PhD programs in Global Surgery, to better prepare surgeons to tackle regional surgical challenges.

Comparison With Existing Literature

The study suggests significant gaps in both formal Global Surgery education and access to necessary surgical resources, which may have important implications for surgical care across the region.

The observed low to moderate level of familiarity with Global Surgery principles among respondents is consistent with trends documented in similar settings, reinforcing the notion that Global Surgery needs more inclusion in the formal surgical curricula across Africa [15, 22, 23, 24]. This pattern reflects a broader issue within surgical education in low‐ and middle‐income countries, where training continues to prioritize technical skill acquisition over the systems‐based, multidisciplinary approach championed by Global Surgery initiatives [23, 25]. This limited exposure to Global Surgery as a formal discipline not only hinders the development of a well‐rounded surgical workforce but also impedes efforts to address the larger systemic challenges that plague healthcare delivery in resource‐limited environments [26].

Despite limited formal training, awareness of key Global Surgery concepts like the Surgical Safety Checklist and Quality Improvement highlights the role of alternative educational platforms in disseminating essential knowledge. Informal learning opportunities such as webinars, conferences, and workshops are crucial for healthcare professionals in under‐resourced regions to access up‐to‐date information and best practices [26, 27, 28]. However, informal sources cannot replace comprehensive programs that embed Global Surgery into core training. This highlights the urgent need to institutionalize such education in LMICs for a holistic understanding of surgical care.

A significant proportion of respondents work in resource‐constrained environments, reflecting well‐established literature on healthcare inequalities in the region [29, 30, 31, 32]. Most respondents reported severe under‐resourcing, with challenges such as insufficient equipment, inadequate facilities, and limited technological support. These findings highlight systemic weaknesses in low‐ and middle‐income countries, where infrastructural deficiencies hinder the delivery of quality care [30].

Despite challenges, over half of respondents felt confident in providing adequate surgical services, reflecting resilience in resource‐limited settings [33, 34, 35]. However, resilience alone cannot replace the need for infrastructure improvements. Without addressing systemic issues, reliance on adaptability may worsen fragile healthcare systems. Investments in infrastructure are essential for sustainable, high‐quality care.

Implications for Surgical Training

Our results highlight the need to integrate Global Surgery principles into surgical training across East, Central, and Southern Africa, addressing a gap despite the growing Global Surgery movement. Surgeons and trainees in the region lack exposure to the systems‐oriented approaches of Global Surgery, missing an opportunity to strengthen the healthcare workforce [36, 37, 38, 39]. Francis et al. [2] similarly advocate for this integration, aligning with our findings. The lack of systems‐oriented training hampers efforts to strengthen the regional healthcare workforce. Structured curricula focusing on leadership, systems strengthening, and resource management are essential to equip surgeons with both technical and systemic competencies.

Our analysis found a potential link between Global Surgery knowledge and formal training, reinforcing the value of structured education. However, inconsistent familiarity across the region suggests fragmented efforts. Standardized curricula are needed to ensure equitable and comprehensive Global Surgery education, especially in resource‐limited contexts.

Our findings further underscore the pivotal role that professional bodies and international collaborations must play in shaping the future of surgical education [40]. As suggested by Francis et al. [2], professional bodies such as COSECSA play a critical role in incorporating Global Surgery competencies into their accreditation standards. Our study supports aligning regional training with global standards to equip surgeons for local and global health challenges. COSECSA is well‐positioned to integrate Global Surgery competencies into its training and accreditation. Partnerships with institutions offering Global Surgery education could harmonize standards, promote collaboration, and enhance care quality. Strategic alliances and resource‐sharing may also reduce disparities and strengthen the surgical workforce.

While COSECSA has developed a strong competency‐based framework focused on technical and clinical skills, there is a notable opportunity for health systems education. Respondents expressed interest in training on infrastructure, policy, and resource management—key Global Surgery components. Integrating these into the curriculum would align COSECSA with global trends and empower surgeons to lead sustainable, resilient surgical systems in the region.

Survey Response Rate, Challenges and Study Limitations

Out of an estimated 900 eligible participants, 75 individuals completed the post‐course evaluation survey, resulting in a response rate of approximately 8.3%. While this rate is relatively low, it is not uncommon for voluntary, email‐based surveys, particularly those conducted without incentives. Several factors may have contributed to the limited response, including survey fatigue, time constraints among participants, or competing priorities. The decision was made not to offer incentives for this survey to maintain the integrity and voluntary nature of the feedback process.

This study has several additional limitations. Its cross‐sectional design prevents assessment of causality or changes over time. The reliance on self‐reported data introduces potential bias, and the use of electronic surveys may have excluded participants with limited internet access, thereby skewing responses toward more urban respondents. Moreover, the relatively small sample size, combined with an overrepresentation of participants from Ethiopia, Uganda, and Malawi, limits the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, multivariable regression was not performed due to sample size constraints and study design.

While the low response rate and these limitations may restrict broad applicability, the responses collected still offer valuable insights into participants' experiences and highlight important areas for future improvement. Though we had a low response rate, our findings still provides the first attempt to understand the global surgery educational needs of the entire COSECSA region. Future research should aim for a larger and more regionally balanced sample to better understand global surgery education across diverse settings.

Future Directions and Recommendations

This study suggests a need for more focused efforts to enhance surgical training in East, Central, and Southern Africa. Policymakers and professional organizations may consider strengthening healthcare infrastructure and exploring ways to support training initiatives, particularly in underserved areas. Expanding access to Global Surgery education—through online platforms and fellowship opportunities—could help address potential knowledge gaps. Incorporating leadership and systems‐based training into surgical curricula may also contribute to improved health system performance. The priorities identified by the Lancet Commission on Global Surgery, especially in leadership development, may serve as a useful framework. Ongoing research into the long‐term effects of Global Surgery training is recommended to guide evidence‐based policymaking and future investments in surgical education.

Conclusion

The findings suggest a potential gap in knowledge of Global Surgery concepts among past surgical trainees and specialists in East, Central, and Southern Africa, indicating that greater emphasis on Global Surgery within surgical education may be beneficial. There also appears to be notable interest in receiving Global Surgery training to enhance understanding and address regional healthcare challenges. While further research is needed to confirm these trends and their implications, integrating Global Surgery into surgical curricula could help better prepare the surgical workforce to meet the needs of patients in resource‐limited settings.
