Biomarker-guided use of corticosteroids in pneumonia
Authors: Abel Soriano Puig, Veronica Monforte, Marta Camprubí-Rimblas, Antonio Artigas, Adrián Ceccato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCommunity-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is one of the leading causes of death worldwide. Although corticosteroids have been proposed as immunomodulatory, controversies surrounding the results of clinical trials have limited their widespread use. This review aims to determine which biomarker-guided corticosteroid treatment for CAP is generally agreed upon in the latest published studies and to discuss the main aspects to be taken into consideration based on lessons learnt from patients with conditions such as influenza, SARS-CoV-2 infection or the recently identified subphenotypes in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). Most studies have demonstrated that high C-reactive protein concentrations at the time of admission are associated with a hyperinflammatory state and that patients are more likely to benefit from corticosteroid treatment if they have high concentrations. High levels of C-reactive protein (CRP) were used as an inclusion criterion in one clinical trial, demonstrating that treatment failure was reduced in the corticosteroid group. A post-hoc analysis of the results of several studies also showed that CRP levels above 200 mg/L were associated with benefits in patients receiving corticosteroids. Recent guidelines have proposed the use of corticosteroids in patients with severe CAP or septic shock. Corticosteroids could be more beneficial for patients with a hyperinflammatory subphenotype; however, there are currently no prospective studies evaluating this approach. Further studies are needed to clarify the role of biomarkers in personalised medicine for patients with CAP. In the meantime, patients with severe CAP or high CRP levels should be treated with corticosteroids.Keywords: Community-acquired pneumonia, Corticosteroids, C-reactive protein, Personalised treatment

Introduction

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is one of the major causes of death worldwide [1]. Since the advent of antibiotics, no other specific treatment has been able to alter the mortality rates associated with CAP [2, 3]. The use of corticosteroids as anti-inflammatory treatment has been proposed for more than 40–50 years [4]. However, the lack of reproducibility of the results in randomised controlled trials (RCTs) raised concerns about their wide use [5]. CAP hospitalised patients’ treatment does not always benefit from corticosteroids, so we must look for a better way to identify those patients with a higher probability of benefits and a lower probability of harm [6].

CAP can be categorised according to severity as either non-severe or severe [7]. Severe CAP (SCAP) is associated with greater benefits from corticosteroid use, particularly in patients with septic shock. A common issue throughout the studies analysing the effect of corticosteroids is the lack of a systematic definition of severe CAP. The heterogeneity of CAP severity makes it difficult to infer a relationship between treatments and real benefits [8, 9]. Nevertheless, new stewardship strategies based on biomarkers are currently under development.

Using corticosteroids for their anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties is still debated, but some promising results are showing that they can somehow modify inflammatory responses. The latest studies evaluating the use of corticosteroids in patients with pneumonia have shown mixed results. On the one hand, an improvement in survival rates was observed in the SONIA [10], the CAPE-COD [11] and the pneumonia subgroups of APROCCHSS [12], while the non-pandemic arm of REMAP-CAP [13] and ESCAPE [14] did not.

Several scientific societies recommend the use of corticosteroids through their guidelines. Meanwhile, in European guidelines [15] corticosteroids are recommended for CAP with septic shock; American guidelines recommend them for severe CAP [7, 16].

The severity of pneumonia is strongly correlated with the systemic levels of inflammatory cytokines (Interleukin [IL]-6, IL-10, and Interferon [IFN]-y). However, a pulmonary compartmentalised inflammation may exist that is not reflected in systemic inflammation. Thus, analyses of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) do not always correlate with the results found in blood [17].

Other studies with CAP patients observed an increased cytokine profile in serum in contrast to the BALF low-rate profile, suggesting a reduced local response but an accentuated systemic inflammation [18]. Moreover, systemic inflammation, despite being a necessary physiological response, causes damage in extra-pulmonary tissues. This idea reinforces the importance of considering local and systemic inflammation as different entities. (Table 1).

While local inflammation contributes to controlling infections, systemic exacerbated inflammation may also be responsible for clinical severity. This fact justifies using corticosteroids in concrete subgroups with exaggerated systemic responses.

Methods

This is a narrative review. For writing, we performed an evidence-based review of all the meta-analyses, Individual Patient Data Meta-analyses (IPD meta-analyses), medical guidelines and randomised clinical trials available online for the use of corticosteroids in CAP. Over the period of 2000 to 2025 in PubMed, Cochrane and Web of Science, search terms used were “community-acquired pneumonia” and “corticosteroid use in CAP” alone or in combination with “hydrocortisone”, “prednisone” or “methylprednisolone”. Additionally, terms used included “systemic inflammation”, “biomarker”, “CRP”, “procalcitonin” or “cortisol”.

The search strategy also combined “Medical Subject Headings”, or MeSH terms, and the aforementioned free-text terms related basically to community-acquired pneumonia, concrete biomarkers and corticosteroid therapy. For all that, Boolean operators were used in PubMed and other databases in a quite similar manner. Some examples using MeSH terms and Title/Abstract (T/A) search included [“community-acquired pneumonia” (MeSH Terms) OR “community acquired pneumonia” (T/A) OR “CAP” (T/A)] AND [“corticosteroids” (MeSH Terms) OR “corticosteroid therapy” (T/A) OR “glucocorticoids” (MeSH Terms) OR “hydrocortisone” (T/A) OR “prednisolone” (T/A) OR “methylprednisolone” (T/A)] AND [“biomarker” (MeSH Terms) OR “biomarkers” (T/A) OR “C-reactive protein” (T/A) OR “CRP” (T/A) OR “procalcitonin” (T/A) OR “cortisol” (T/A)] NOT [“COVID-19” (Mesh Terms) OR “Influenza” (Mesh Terms)].

Similar Boolean and use of MeSH Terms were applied to Cochrane Library and Web of Science searches. Field tags were also adjusted in each database.

Biomarker-use candidates for a guided Understanding of corticosteroids in CAP

Biomarkers candidate for guided anti-inflammatory therapy are usually those who are elevated with infection and may predict the development of organ failure or mortality [23].

C-Reactive protein (CRP)

C-Reactive Protein (CRP) is a reliable serum marker in evaluating inflammatory response. CRP showed a good relation in predicting treatment failure and mortality in patients with CAP [24, 25]. Also, patients who presented a decrease in CRP levels had a good response to antibiotics [26]. The time-dependent analysis of CRP patterns showed a difference between those with a fast response compared with non-responders (4.8% vs. 36.4%) [27]. Elevated CRP was used as an inclusion criterion in an RCT [28] and evaluated in meta-analyses [29] and observational studies [30].

Procalcitonin (PCT)

PCT is useful for antibiotic guide use, but it does not seem to be a real biomarker that distinguishes between benefits of corticosteroids well. No clinical randomised trial has demonstrated whether serum PCT could guide CAP patients selectively for corticosteroid treatment. PCT may be a reliable biomarker for the presence of systemic bacterial infections, but not for inflammatory systemic response qualitatively [31].

Serum cortisol

Cortisol serum levels were demonstrated to have a prognostic value in CAP. Christ-Cain et al. observed that CAP patients with an increased level of baseline cortisol were more likely to have severe complications [32]. Moreover, Kolditz et al. in 2012 concluded that serum cortisol levels at the time of diagnosis could correlate with the Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI), which, by the way, could be used for stratification of the CAP risk [33]. Nevertheless, patients with high levels of cortisol do not necessarily have to be treated with corticosteroids. In fact, high levels of CRP in CAP patients may benefit from corticosteroid treatment, while patients with high levels of cortisol don’t.

More clinical trials studying cortisol-guided therapy are needed to clarify serum cortisol levels as a predictable biomarker for using corticosteroids in CAP.

Clinical evidence

While there are many biomarkers that can be used to determine host response in CAP, none of them have been truly consistent in guiding corticosteroid therapy currently. There is still a need for research to identify a specific biomarker capable of reliably capturing host-response heterogeneity in CAP. CRP is cost-effective, easily accessible, and frequently used in daily clinical practice in some regions such as Europe, which makes it both efficient and standardised for guiding therapeutic decisions [34].

The first study assessing the efficacy of corticosteroid therapy in CAP was conducted by Confalonieri et al. [35]. The study was pivotal because it demonstrated a marked early reduction in CRP and a lower rate of septic shock, establishing the mechanistic basis for host-response modulation in CAP patients.

Torres et al. [28] conducted a randomised, multicentre clinical trial involving 120 patients with severe community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) (meeting ATS/IDSA guideline criteria and/or having higher PSI levels) at three Spanish hospitals. Inclusion criteria included a CRP measurement of ≥ 150 mg/L at the time of enrolment in the study. The patients were divided into two groups: one treated with methylprednisolone (0.5 mg/kg every 12 h for five days) and one treated with a placebo. The main outcome was ‘treatment failure within 72 hours’, defined as radiological and clinical failure, mechanical intubation, or septic shock. Secondary outcomes included 30-day mortality, length of hospital stay, and other side effects (e.g., hyperglycaemia).

The main results regarding the treatment failure rate were 13% for the corticosteroid group and 31% for the placebo group. The absolute risk reduction was approximately 18% (p = 0.02). There were no significant differences in 30-day mortality between the groups (10% vs. 15%; p = 0.37). Hospital mortality stay remained similar within groups. Observed side effects included hyperglycaemia in the corticosteroid-treated group (19% vs. 11%), with no major treatment-associated complications. This randomised clinical trial demonstrates the importance of selecting severe CAP patients with CRP serum levels of ≥ 150 mg/L as a subgroup that can benefit from corticosteroid treatment. However, this study had limitations, including a relatively small sample size of 120 participants. Only severe CAP patients admitted to hospital were included in the study. This means the study is not comparable to moderate or mild CAP patterns. Additionally, five days of patient analysis was insufficient to encapsulate long-term benefits or risks. Although treatment failure was reduced, no reduction in the mortality rate was observed.

The individual patient data (IPD) meta-analysis [29] published by Smit et al. in 2025 included eight randomised clinical trials. The study assessed adjuvant therapy with corticosteroids in hospitalised CAP patients until July 2024, including 3,224 patients. The main objective was to quantify 30-day mortality. The meta-analysis evaluated the heterogeneity of the treatment effect (HTE) by analysing whether PSI or baseline CRP predisposes one to a greater overall reduction in mortality. The global 30-day mortality results showed 6.6% in the corticosteroid-treated group versus 8.7% in the placebo-controlled group (odds ratio [OR] 0.72; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.56–0.94; p = 0.017). CRP study stratification proposed the so-called ‘responder-type’ phenotype, for which the study analysis developed a predictive model based on the effects of corticosteroids in two recently published studies. On the one hand, a CRP level of less than or equal to 204 mg/L was considered to have no predictable benefit (odds ratio [OR]: 0.98; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.63–1.50). No mortality reduction was then observed. Conversely, CRP above 204 mg/L was beneficial. The mortality rate of patients taking a placebo with an elevated CRP level (> 204 mg/L) was 13%, compared to 6.1% for patients taking corticosteroids (OR 0.43; 95% CI 0.25–0.76; p = 0.026). However, adverse effects were also detected. Hyperglycaemia was observed in 24.8% of patients treated with corticosteroids, compared to 12.8% of placebo patients. (OR 2.50; 95% CI 1.63–3.83; p < 0.0001). Regarding readmission to hospital, 7% of patients taking corticosteroids were readmitted, compared to 3.7% of patients taking the placebo. This IPD meta-analysis revealed strong evidence that corticosteroids reduce the overall mortality rate in patients with CAP, but this benefit is selective, applying only to patients with an elevated CRP (> 204 mg/L). In patients with low CRP levels, they do not seem to confer any advantage.

Dose, timing and duration of elevated CRP

An important issue among the studies is heterogeneity in terms of the kind of corticosteroids and doses. There are no studies comparing corticosteroids or dose, and no biomarker has been validated for identifying benefits or harms among different corticosteroids or doses. Nevertheless, different studies have evaluated corticosteroids using biomarkers. So, we may infer that in these identified populations we may use different corticosteroids or doses: For those with severe CAP with an elevated CRP (> = 150–200 mg/L): Methylprednisolone for a short period is administered for up to five days in a dose of 0.5 mg/kg/12 h. No tapering is required according to Torres et al. [28].

Specific aetiologies

Mostly, studies were performed including all aetiologies causing pneumonia. Recently, the SONIA study [10] has shown benefits in terms of survival in low-resource settings without analysing the causative pathogens; however, we will describe two specific aetiologies where the management is different based on available evidence.

COVID-19

Early after the pandemic disease was declared, Wu et al. [36] reported an association with better outcomes. The first recommendations were against the use of corticosteroids, mainly based on the results observed in influenza, SARS and MERS [37]. The first study published showing the benefits of corticosteroids in COVID-19 was the RECOVERY, a platform-based study [38]. Patients requiring advanced respiratory support and with more than 7 days since the onset of symptoms have the highest benefits. A meta-analysis combining the results of this study with the preliminary results of other RCTs, which were halted by these results, was published, confirming the findings of the RECOVERY study [39]. Observational studies confirmed the benefits of corticosteroids in those patients with more than 7 days of symptoms but also in those with higher severity and inflammation. Torres et al. suggested that the benefits are only observed in those with CRP > 150 mg/L [40]. Sinha et al. reported the highest benefits in those patients with hyperinflammation, a subphenotype with similar characteristics to those described in other cases of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [41].

Influenza

Corticosteroids were associated with the worst outcomes in patients with influenza, so corticosteroids are not recommended in this population [42, 43]. Recently, the REMAP-CAP showed similar results whether patients had influenza or not [13]. New studies are warranted to clarify whether corticosteroids are safe in influenza pneumonia, and which biomarkers may allow identifying patients at high risk of side effects or benefits.

Proposing a clinical algorithm for biomarker guided use for CAP

After first confirming whether CAP is viral or not (e.g., influenza virus or COVID-19), it is proposed to measure basal CRP at initial screening. If basal CRP is above or equal to 200 mg/L, adjuvant corticosteroid therapy is indicated or desirable. When levels of CRP are below 150 mg/L, and no septic shock or respiratory failure is diagnosed, it is advisable to call off corticosteroids routinely since no profit has been demonstrated. If CRP levels are between 150 and 200 mg/L, it is desirable to use methylprednisolone at 1 mg/kg twice per day. Conversely, when CRP levels are above 200 mg/L, hydrocortisone is needed at a dosage of 200 mg/day via continuous infusion for 4-7 days, with optional tapering (Fig. 1).

Subphenotypes

Recently, subphenotypes based on the inflammatory response have been identified in conditions closely related to pneumonia, such as ARDS and sepsis [20, 44, 45]. These subphenotypes can be identified using a simple method involving a point-of-care test. The use of subphenotypes in personalised medicine is proposed based on the sound logic that anti-inflammatory treatment could benefit patients with a higher inflammatory response, such as those with a hyperinflammatory subphenotype [45, 46]. As previously mentioned, corticosteroids significantly benefited patients with hyperinflammation in cases of SARS-CoV-2 infection. However, a main flaw of this strategy is that inflammation is not static: cytokines are molecules with a short half-life, so classification into subgroups may change over time [47, 48]. Pensier et al. recently observed that subphenotypes transitioning or remaining within the low inflammation range increased the risk of harm from corticosteroid use. Conversely, patients with a high inflammatory profile or hyperinflammation experienced greater benefits from corticosteroid use. Those who changed from hyperinflammation to hypoinflammation experienced no benefits or risks [49].

A recent multicentre study by Salton et al. characterised patients who died within seven days of being diagnosed with pneumonia. This group of patients was defined by the authors as the ‘fulminant phenotype’ and was reported to account for approximately 5.9% of patients within a total study population of 1,517 patients with severe CAP, with a 7-day mortality rate. In this analysis, early mortality was not associated with CRP levels and was lower in patients who received corticosteroids early on. Mortality was mostly driven by age or comorbidities [50].

Conclusions

Inflammatory biomarkers play a key role in modelling corticosteroid therapy CAP. This approach is based on a new paradigm of personalised medicine. For example, C-reactive protein (CRP) has emerged as a practical and reproducible indicator of the host’s inflammatory status. As previously mentioned, elevated CRP levels (> 150–200 mg/L, especially > 200 mg/L) are associated with high inflammation. This profile has been associated with a higher mortality rate and extensive systemic cytokine activation. In these patients, corticosteroid treatment, mainly consisting of methylprednisolone and hydrocortisone, has been shown to significantly reduce the likelihood of shock and the need for mechanical ventilation, thus supporting the idea that CRP is a reliable treatment response biomarker.

Procalcitonin (PCT) is useful for guiding the length of antibiotic medication but has not been described as a prognostic marker for corticosteroid therapy in CAP. Conversely, PCT reflects the burden of bacterial infection and the host’s response to pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs). Consequently, CRP is more closely associated with IL-6-driven systemic inflammation. Consequently, a patient with a low PCT value but high CRP may benefit from corticosteroid therapy, as they are at risk of a post-infectious hyperinflammatory state.

There are still some gaps that restrict the studies. Firstly, the dose, duration and tapering strategies of corticosteroid therapy vary widely across the studies, which prevents the provision of standardised recommendations. Secondly, many randomised controlled trials lacked comprehensive virological testing for influenza or SARS-CoV-2. This limitation may confound the observed effects because viral CAP does not respond completely to corticosteroid therapy, making an increased risk of secondary infection more likely. Thirdly, CRP thresholds that are useful for defining inflammatory phenotypes differ between study cohorts, and external validation is often required. Finally, an updated protocol for corticosteroid withdrawal is still missing. This uncertainty makes it difficult to determine whether sudden cessation could cause inflammation to flare up again or whether extended tapering could lead to metabolic fatalities. CRP may act as an indicator of a more severe inflammatory process, mainly mediated by IL-6, in which corticosteroids may be more beneficial. Future research is likely to adopt a unified approach to multiple biomarker profiles, such as CRP alongside IL-6 or transcriptomic signatures, as well as subphenotype adjudication. To enhance outcomes while minimising side effects, it is advisable to develop a personalised algorithm for CAP management.
