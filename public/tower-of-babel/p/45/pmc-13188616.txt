Health-related quality of life pre-war and during-war in cardiac patients: evidence from a multicenter prospective study – West Bank
Authors: Haitham Abu Khadija, Abdalaziz Darwish, Alaa Dabash, Duha Najajra, Yahya Z Fraitekh, Mohammad Masu’d, Anwar Zahran, Mohammad Maraqah, Ihab K Hemieid, Saif Yaseen, Zeina Farhoud, Ghina Daraghmeh, Layan Elayan, Laith Allam Mohmmad Ahmad, Mohammad Khader, Ameer Khader, Ameen Abu Awad, Wafiq Saleh Abdalkreem Othman, Amid Barq, Ahmad K Darwazah, Moath Nairat, Hasan Alkhatib, Nizar Abu Hamdeh, Mohammad Alnees
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWar imposes systemic and psychosocial stressors that can disrupt postoperative recovery and diminish quality of life. Quantitative data examining these effects in cardiac surgery populations remain scarce.ObjectivesTo assess whether the wartime period following October 7, 2023, independently affected postoperative health-related quality of life (HRQoL) among patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) or valve surgery in Palestine.MethodsIn this prospective multicenter cohort, 1,686 patients who underwent cardiac surgery between September 2022 and May 2025 were screened. After exclusion of 486 ineligible or non-followed participants, 1,200 patients were included: 465 operated pre-war and 735 during-war. HRQoL was evaluated using the 12-Item Short-Form Health Survey (SF-12), generating Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS) Scores. Associations between wartime exposure and SF-12 outcomes were analyzed using generalized linear models (GLMs) with robust standard errors and validated through propensity score matching (PSM, 1:1 nearest neighbor) matching on age, gender, diabetes, surgery type, cardiopulmonary bypass time, and hospital fixed effects.ResultsOpen-heart procedures increased by 5% monthly during-war. Postoperative complications were generally more frequent in this period, with notable rises in acute kidney injury (33.8% vs. 22.4%), atrial fibrillation (31.9% vs. 24.8%), and surgical site infection (8.8% vs. 4.1%). Mean PCS and MCS values were significantly lower during-war (p = 0.037 and p = 0.013, respectively). In adjusted GLMs, wartime exposure remained independently associated with reduced HRQoL (β = − 1.31 [95% CI − 3.27 to − 0.65] for PCS; β = − 1.19 [95% CI − 3.12 to − 0.75] for MCS). PSM confirmed these findings (PCS = − 1.47 [95% CI − 2.89 to − 0.06], p = 0.041; MCS = − 1.82 [95% CI − 3.67 to 0.04], p = 0.056).ConclusionsWartime conditions were independently associated with clinically relevant reductions in both physical and mental postoperative quality of life. Sustained access to rehabilitation, psychosocial support, and follow-up care is crucial to preserving recovery among cardiac surgery patients in conflict-affected settings.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12955-026-02506-y.

Introduction

Cardiac surgery, including procedures such as coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) and valve surgery, represents a cornerstone of cardiovascular care [1]. CABG and aortic valve replacement (AVR) are among the most commonly performed cardiac surgeries worldwide, with an estimated 1–1.5 million procedures performed annually [2, 3]. Cardiac surgery remains a major global health challenge due to its inherent complexity and the considerable postoperative morbidity and mortality it entails. Despite a steady decline in operative mortality in contemporary practice [4], postoperative complications remain common and exert a profound influence on recovery and survival [5]. The most frequent complications include respiratory compromise requiring prolonged ventilation, atrial arrhythmias, renal dysfunction, bleeding, cerebrovascular events, and infections [6, 7]. These complications markedly increase hospital length of stay, healthcare costs, and long-term mortality [8].

Quality of life (QoL) following cardiac surgery is influenced by demographic, clinical, psychological, and social factors. These include age, comorbidities, surgical type, and perioperative complications. Participation in structured cardiac rehabilitation, digital health engagement, and strong social support enhances recovery [9, 10]. In contrast, depression, anxiety, and prolonged recovery diminish it [9, 10]. During-war, disrupted healthcare services, movement restrictions, and psychological distress further compromised these predictors. Interrupted rehabilitation, limited access to follow-up, and increased mental health burden have collectively reduced postoperative recovery and overall quality of life (QoL) in affected cardiac surgery patients [11].

In Palestine, these challenges are exacerbated by recurrent conflict, limited mobility, and a fragile healthcare infrastructure. Before 7 October 2023, the West Bank’s healthcare system was already under strain. Contributing factors included, economic instability, high mental health burdens, and dependence on external referrals for tertiary care [12, 13]. In this prospective, multicenter cohort study, we will evaluate quality of life (QoL) in patients who underwent CABG and valve surgery pre-war and during-war. We will then explore predictors of quality of life (QoL) in each cohort.

Methodology

Study design, setting, and population

This prospective multi-center cohort study was conducted across nine cardiac surgery centers throughout the West Bank and East Jerusalem, Palestine. Centers included Ibn Sina Specialist Hospital, Specialized Arab Hospital, Al-Razi Hospital, Al-Mezan Hospital, Al-Ahli Hospital, the Arab Society for Rehabilitation, Palestine Medical Complex, Nablus Specialty Hospital, and Al-Makassed Hospital. Exposure was defined based on the calendar date of the index cardiac surgery. The pre-war period included surgeries performed between September 1, 2022, and October 6, 2023. The during-war period included surgeries performed from October 7, 2023, through May 31, 2025. The date of October 7, 2023, was treated as an a priori, prespecified calendar cutoff corresponding to the onset of the war, and no transition or washout window was applied.

The study population comprised adult patients (≥ 18 years) admitted for elective or semi-elective major cardiac surgeries, including CABG or valve surgery. A total of 1200 patients were enrolled, of whom 465 underwent surgery between September 1, 2022, and October 6, 2023 (pre-war cohort) and 735 underwent surgery from October 7, 2023, through May 31, 2025 (during-war cohort). Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All adult patients (≥ 18 years) who underwent coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) or isolated valve surgery at participating centers were eligible for inclusion. Cohort A included surgeries performed pre-7 October 2023, and Cohort B included those performed during-war. Exclusion criteria included minor or non-cardiac procedures, palliative bedside operations, age < 18 years, severe cognitive impairment or language barriers preventing QoL assessment, lack of follow-up, or incomplete clinical data. These criteria align with established cardiac surgery studies to ensure reliable and comparable QoL outcomes across cohorts. Of the 1,686 patients initially assessed for eligibility, 486 were excluded prior to cohort formation. Exclusions included 30-day postoperative mortality (n = 15), documented psychotic disorders precluding valid HRQoL assessment (n = 87), lack of follow-up or refusal to participate (n = 282), combined CABG and valve surgery (n = 70), age < 18 years or significant cognitive, language, or neurological impairment (n = 15), and other cardiac procedures not meeting inclusion criteria (n = 17). The final study cohort consisted of 1,200 patients (Fig. 1).

Instruments and tools

Data were collected using standardized English and Arabic versions of the SF-12 during outpatient follow-up. In participating centers, SF-12 administration was typically incorporated into later postoperative surveillance follow-up and was most commonly obtained around the one-year postoperative visit, rather than during early recovery visits, to better reflect a more stable phase of recovery after completion of early rehabilitation and short-term postoperative fluctuations. Due to the study workflow across multiple centers, the exact interval between surgery and questionnaire completion was not systematically recorded for all participants. Scoring was performed via the OrthoToolKit SF-12 platform, which automatically converts responses into norm-based PCS and MCS scores (mean = 50, SD = 10). Higher scores indicate better health-related quality of life.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Palestine Polytechnic University, Hebron, Palestine. Before data collection, each participant was informed about the study’s aims, potential benefits, and any associated risks, and we obtained written informed consent from all the patients. Participation was voluntary, and individuals were assured they could withdraw at any time without consequences. All data were kept strictly confidential and stored securely to protect participants’ privacy. In addition, formal approvals were obtained from all collaborating academic and healthcare institutions involved in the research.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using Stata version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between groups using independent t-tests or analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages and compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p < 0.05. Baseline comparability between the pre-war and during-war cohorts was additionally assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with values < 0.10 indicating small between-group differences.

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) outcomes were assessed using the 12-Item Short-Form Health Survey (SF-12), generating Physical (PCS-12) and Mental (MCS-12) Component Scores. To evaluate the independent association between wartime exposure and HRQoL, generalized linear models (GLMs) with Gaussian family and identity link were employed, providing β-coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

For the crude model, wartime period (pre-war vs. during-war) was entered as the sole independent variable. In the adjusted models, covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior literature [2, 3, 11], and guided by the Wilson and Cleary conceptual model of health-related quality of life, which links biological and clinical characteristics, symptom burden, and functional status to patient-reported HRQoL outcomes [14]. The PCS model was adjusted for age, gender, body-mass index (BMI), diabetes, surgery type (CABG vs. valve), cardiopulmonary bypass time (CPB), and postoperative complications including acute kidney injury and myocardial infarction. The MCS model was adjusted for age, gender, diabetes, surgery type, cardiopulmonary bypass time (CPB), and postoperative complications including acute kidney injury, myocardial infarction, sepsis, pneumonia, and reintubation. The models follow the guidelines of the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis [15].

Normality of SF-12 Physical and Mental Component Score distributions was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test stratified by war period. Given the large sample size and observed deviations from normality, robust standard errors were used. In adjusted analyses, robust standard errors clustered at the center (hospital) level were applied to account for within-institution correlation and ensure valid inference.

Covariate balance before and after propensity score matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with values < 0.10 indicating adequate balance. A summary of balance diagnostics is provided in Supplement 1 (eTable 6). To further minimize potential confounding, a propensity score–matching (PSM) analysis was conducted as a sensitivity analysis using the teffects psmatch procedure in Stata. Matching was performed using a 1:1 nearest-neighbor algorithm without replacement. The propensity score was estimated using a logistic regression model that included baseline demographic variables (age and gender), baseline comorbidity (diabetes), procedural characteristics (surgery type and cardiopulmonary bypass time), and hospital fixed effects to account for inter-institutional differences. Postoperative complications were not included in the propensity score model, as they occur after exposure and may lie on the causal pathway. Balance diagnostics demonstrated substantial improvement in covariate balance after matching. The matched analyses estimated the average treatment effect on the treated (ATET), comparing patients assessed during the war with those assessed pre-war.

Results are presented as β coefficients with robust standard errors, 95% CIs, and p-values. Graphical summaries, including flow diagrams and SF-12 score distributions, were generated to illustrate the analytic process and outcome differences between pre-war and post-war groups.

Table 1 summarizes baseline characteristics and postoperative outcomes pre-war and during-war period. Surgical volume increased substantially during the war. Patient age and sex distribution were similar between cohorts. Baseline clinical differences were modest. Patients treated during-war had slightly lower body mass index, lower prevalence of diabetes and renal impairment, and a higher proportion classified as NYHA class I. Valve procedures were more frequent during-war period. Postoperative complications occurred more often during-war. Increases were most evident for acute kidney injury, atrial fibrillation, and surgical site infections. These findings indicate a clinically meaningful rise in perioperative morbidity despite broadly comparable baseline patient profiles.

Several baseline characteristics demonstrated meaningful between-group differences (e.g., NYHA functional class and EuroScore II), while many demographic and laboratory variables showed small differences. Postoperative complications are presented descriptively for the clinical context and were not interpreted as baseline comparability measures.

Figure 2 compares the SF-12 Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS) scores among participants assessed pre-war and during-war. Although the mean differences between the two groups were relatively small (PCS: 42.9 ± 8.5 vs. 41.8 ± 9.4; MCS: 49.0 ± 9.9 vs. 47.5 ± 10.7), both reached statistical significance (p = 0.037 and p = 0.013, respectively). Normality testing using the Shapiro–Wilk test indicated deviations from normality for both PCS and MCS in the pre-war and during-war periods (all p < 0.001). Visual inspection showed that the PCS distribution during the war period was wider with a pronounced lower tail, explaining the lower mean despite relatively similar median values between groups. In contrast, both the mean and median MCS were lower during the war period, suggesting a more uniform downward shift in mental health–related quality of life. Discussion

In this study, we assessed whether the October 7th wartime period influenced postoperative health-related quality of life (HRQoL) among patients undergoing major cardiac surgery. The present analysis uses October 7, 2023, as a clearly defined calendar breakpoint and compares postoperative HRQoL between two surgical cohorts: patients who underwent cardiac surgery in the pre-war period and those who underwent cardiac surgery during-war period. Because SF-12 was collected once per patient during routine follow-up, our results represent a between-cohort comparison and do not quantify within-patient HRQoL trajectories over time. Future studies incorporating repeated HRQoL assessments at standardized postoperative time points would be better positioned to evaluate temporal trends and distinguish acute from persistent effects of conflict-related stressors. Our findings show consistently lower SF-12 Physical and Mental Component Scores in the during-war cohort, both in crude and fully adjusted models (Table 2), and this pattern persisted after propensity score matching (Table 3), providing strong evidence of a measurable decline in physical and psychological postoperative well-being associated with wartime conditions.

The predominance of patients classified as NYHA functional class I likely reflects the largely elective and semi-elective nature of the study population and contemporary referral practices that favor surgical intervention before advanced symptomatic deterioration. However, the distribution of NYHA functional class differed between the pre-war and during-war cohorts, with a higher proportion of patients classified as NYHA class I in the during-war cohort. This baseline difference should be considered when interpreting between-cohort differences in postoperative HRQoL. Because NYHA classification primarily reflects cardiac symptom severity rather than psychosocial stress or post-discharge recovery, differences in postoperative HRQoL observed during wartime may also relate to factors beyond baseline functional class, including disruptions to rehabilitation, healthcare access, and psychological stressors.

During the study period from September 1, 2022 to May 31, 2025, a notable 5% monthly increase in cardiovascular surgeries was observed during-war. This rise may reflect a combination of higher clinical demand, improved surgical capacity, and changes in referral or scheduling patterns. The observed increase in cardiovascular surgeries during-war may be explained by patient-, system-, and referral-related factors. Psychological and environmental stressors inherent to conflict can accelerate the onset or exacerbation of cardiovascular disease. Acute stress and disrupted medication adherence are recognized triggers for ischemic events. Moreover, limited access to outpatient and preventive care may have shifted the disease spectrum toward more advanced or urgent stages. This shift may have favored surgical management over conservative treatment.

Increased procedural volume was accompanied by slightly higher rates of postoperative complications, including acute kidney injury, atrial fibrillation, surgical site infection, and pneumonia. These findings suggest that elevated workload and resource pressure may have influenced perioperative outcomes. Similar observations were reported by Keizman et al. [11], who found that an increased cardiac surgery caseload during stressful periods was associated with greater operative strain and complication risk. Previous studies in high-volume cardiac centers have also shown that rapid growth in surgical activity can challenge team coordination, intensive care capacity, and postoperative monitoring efficiency. In contrast, Ahmed et al. [2] and Vervoort et al. [3] emphasized that maintaining optimized perioperative pathways and standardized rehabilitation protocols can mitigate the negative effects of increased workload.

According to Fig. 2, the during-war period was independently associated with lower health-related quality of life following cardiac surgery. Both the PCS and MCS declined significantly in the during-war cohort. The observed between-period differences in SF-12 summary scores were statistically significant but modest in magnitude. Although MCID thresholds for norm-based HRQoL summary measures are often cited in the range of approximately 3–5 points (or 0.3–0.5 SD), smaller mean shifts may still be relevant at the population level when observed across large cohorts and may reflect persistent, longer-term impacts that remain detectable at later postoperative surveillance [16–19]. In our study, SF-12 was generally obtained during routine later follow-up (most commonly around one year after surgery), a timepoint at which early postoperative fluctuations may have partially resolved; therefore, these estimates likely reflect residual differences persisting into later recovery rather than peak early postoperative impairment. The parallel decline in both physical and mental domains suggest a multifactorial process. Interrupted cardiac rehabilitation, disruption of health services, and psychological distress may have jointly contributed to this deterioration [20, 21]. Prior evidence confirms that participation in cardiac rehabilitation significantly improves postoperative HRQoL [19, 22]. In contrast, stress-related psychopathology after cardiac surgery adversely affects long-term recovery [21]. These findings underscore the need for conflict-resilient cardiac-care pathways. Such pathways should integrate mental-health support, protect access to rehabilitation, and ensure continuity of follow-up even in crisis contexts [20].

Although the direction and consistency of the associations support an adverse impact of wartime exposure on postoperative HRQoL, the magnitude of the observed differences was modest. Several factors may explain this finding. The study population largely comprised elective or semi-elective surgical patients who were medically optimized prior to surgery and who survived to later postoperative follow-up, limiting the range of observable HRQoL deterioration. In addition, SF-12 assessments were generally obtained during routine later surveillance (most commonly around one year after surgery), a phase at which early postoperative impairments may have partially resolved, and patients may have adapted to ongoing stressors. Finally, the SF-12 is a generic, norm-based instrument that captures broad health domains and may be less sensitive to acute contextual stress than more granular or disease-specific measures. Together, these factors likely attenuate the magnitude of between-period differences while still allowing detection of persistent, population-level effects.

The current study demonstrates that wartime exposure has a measurable negative impact on both physical and mental health domains of cardiac surgery patients. This effect is reflected in the Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS) scores of the SF-12 health survey. The PCS represents patients’ perceived physical functioning, while the MCS reflects emotional well-being. In our adjusted models, both components showed negative β coefficients. This finding indicates an inverse relationship between wartime exposure and postoperative health-related quality of life. Matanov et al. [23] found that exposure to armed conflict is associated with persistent reductions in subjective quality of life (QoL). These reductions were attributed to trauma, disrupted services, and social dislocation. Moreover, Lim et al. [24] demonstrated that the COVID-19 pandemic, another form of large-scale societal stress, produced clinically meaningful declines in psychological HRQoL. These effects were observed even when patients’ cardiac conditions were stable. Taken together, our findings support the notion that the observed decreases in PCS and MCS are not driven by surgical or physiological factors. Instead, they appear to reflect the broader psychosocial strain associated with wartime conditions. The mental stress, uncertainty, and social instability surrounding conflict likely impair emotional resilience and physical recovery alike. Recognizing this link emphasizes the need for targeted postoperative interventions. These should include psychosocial screening, mental-health support, and rehabilitation strategies for cardiac patients living in conflict zones.

The observed distributional patterns of SF-12 scores provide important insight into the mechanisms underlying the association between wartime exposure and health-related quality of life. In particular, the discrepancy between mean and median PCS values during the war period suggests that the overall decline in physical health–related quality of life was driven primarily by a subset of patients with markedly impaired recovery rather than a uniform deterioration across the entire cohort. This finding aligns with conceptual models of HRQoL, such as the Wilson and Cleary framework, which emphasize that patient-reported outcomes reflect a complex interplay between biological status, symptom burden, functional capacity, and broader environmental and psychosocial factors. Wartime conditions may selectively exacerbate vulnerabilities among certain patients, such as those experiencing postoperative complications or barriers to follow-up care, leading to increased heterogeneity in physical recovery trajectories.

In contrast, the more consistent downward shift observed in both the mean and median MCS scores suggests a more generalized impact of wartime stressors on mental well-being. Psychological distress, uncertainty, and chronic exposure to conflict-related stress may affect patients more uniformly, independent of surgical or procedural characteristics. This divergence between physical and mental HRQoL patterns underscores the importance of interpreting summary scores within their distributional context and cautions against relying solely on mean differences without consideration of underlying variability.

Methodologically, these findings highlight the value of combining graphical assessment with robust statistical modeling when analyzing patient-reported outcomes in large, heterogeneous populations. The use of robust and cluster-adjusted standard errors allowed us to account for deviations from normality and within-center correlation while preserving interpretability of effect estimates at the population level. Together, these approaches strengthen confidence that the observed associations reflect meaningful differences in patient experience rather than artifacts of distributional assumptions or institutional variation.

To further address potential confounding, a propensity score–matched (PSM) analysis was conducted as a sensitivity analysis (Table 3). By balancing key baseline demographic, clinical, procedural, and institutional characteristics, PSM enhances internal validity and reduces bias arising from systematic differences between pre-war and during-war cohorts. After matching, wartime exposure remained significantly associated with lower physical health–related quality of life, with during-war patients exhibiting reduced PCS scores compared with pre-war patients.

For mental health–related quality of life, the estimated effect of wartime exposure remained in the same direction and of similar magnitude but was attenuated after more extensive adjustment and did not reach conventional statistical significance. This pattern suggests that while the physical impact of wartime conditions on postoperative recovery appears robust, the mental health effects may be more sensitive to baseline characteristics and institutional context. These findings are consistent with prior work applying propensity score methods to conflict-affected populations. For example, Betancourt et al. used PSM to examine trauma exposure and mental health outcomes among refugee, immigrant, and U.S.-origin youth and demonstrated that large-scale stressors such as violence and displacement exert persistent adverse effects even after extensive adjustment [25]. Similarly, in the present study, the direction and magnitude of the PSM estimates were consistent with those observed in the multivariable regression models (Table 2), supporting the overall robustness of the association between wartime exposure and impaired postoperative quality of life. Taken together, the convergence of findings across analytical approaches suggests that wartime conditions impose a sustained burden on recovery and patient-reported well-being, with particularly consistent effects on physical health–related quality of life and more heterogeneous effects on mental health outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including its large, multicenter design with 1,200 cardiac surgery patients across nine centers, which enhances generalizability. Prospective data collection minimized recall bias, and the validated Arabic SF-12 ensured reliable HRQoL measurement. Comparing pre- and during-war assessments offers insight into conflict-related recovery.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Postoperative health-related quality of life (HRQoL) is inherently time-dependent, and the exact interval between surgery and SF-12 assessment was not consistently documented across all participants. Although assessments were generally conducted during routine follow-up, most commonly around one year after surgery, variability in timing may have influenced absolute HRQoL scores. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as associations between wartime exposure and HRQoL measured during routine later postoperative follow-up rather than at a fixed postoperative day. Preoperative SF-12 measurements were not routinely collected across participating centers and were therefore unavailable for inclusion in the present analyses. As a result, postoperative HRQoL could not be adjusted for baseline (preoperative) HRQoL, nor could within-patient change over time be evaluated. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as between-cohort associations rather than as estimates of pre-to-post change attributable to wartime exposure.

Educational attainment and other detailed socioeconomic indicators were not systematically collected across participating centers and therefore could not be included in the analysis. Because education level may influence health literacy, access to care, and patient-reported outcomes, residual confounding related to unmeasured socioeconomic factors cannot be excluded. In addition, several key psychosocial and mental-health confounders were not measured, including depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder symptoms, perceived social support, access to cardiac rehabilitation, and medication adherence. The omission of these variables may have resulted in residual confounding, and part of the observed decline in HRQoL may not be attributable solely to wartime exposure. Finally, because HRQoL outcomes were self-reported, wartime psychological stress may have influenced symptom perception and reporting independent of objective postoperative recovery. Consequently, lower SF-12 scores observed during the wartime period may reflect not only differences in postoperative recovery but also reporting bias related to altered symptom appraisal and emotional perception under chronic stress, a limitation inherent to HRQoL assessment in conflict settings. Selection bias related to loss to follow-up also cannot be fully excluded, as patients who survived and completed HRQoL assessment may represent a healthier subset of the surgical population. However, exclusion criteria and follow-up procedures were applied uniformly across the pre-war and during-war periods, reducing the likelihood of differential bias between cohorts.

Clinical and future implications

These findings highlight the need for resilient postoperative care models that extend beyond surgical success to sustain recovery under crisis conditions. Prior evidence demonstrates that participation in structured cardiac rehabilitation is associated with improvements in postoperative health-related quality of life and functional recovery [19, 22], while stress-related psychopathology after cardiac surgery adversely affects longer-term recovery [21]. In conflict settings, disruptions in healthcare access and continuity may further compound postoperative recovery challenges [20]. Future research should evaluate longitudinal HRQoL trajectories and assess the effectiveness of adaptive rehabilitation and follow-up strategies that preserve continuity of care in crisis contexts.

Conclusion

In this multicenter cohort, wartime exposure was associated with modest reductions in both physical and mental health-related quality of life following cardiac surgery. These findings underscore the need for conflict-resilient cardiac care pathways that safeguard continuity of postoperative follow-up, rehabilitation, and psychosocial support when healthcare delivery is disrupted. Beyond the local context, our results highlight a broader global surgery and humanitarian health priority: strengthening health system preparedness to sustain complex surgical care and recovery in settings affected by conflict and instability.
