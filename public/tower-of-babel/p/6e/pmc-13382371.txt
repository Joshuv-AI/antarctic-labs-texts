Comparison of AGA and SGA Infants Based on Prooxidant‐to‐Antioxidant Balance in Mashhad, Iran During 2019–2025: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Maryam Zakerihamidi, Fatemeh Bagheri, Nooshin Nesaee, Hassan Boskabadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsSmall for Gestational Age (SGA) infants face numerous challenges that may stem from a prooxidant–antioxidant imbalance. This study aims to compare AGA and SGA infants regarding their PAB (Prooxidant‐Antioxidant Balance) levels.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study was conducted on 898 neonates, including 798 (88.9%) appropriate for gestational age (AGA) infants and 100 (11.1%) small for gestational age (SGA) infants. The research utilized convenience sampling at Ghaem Hospital in Mashhad, spanning from 2019 to 2025. Data were collected using a questionnaire developed by the researchers, which consisted of three sections: neonatal information (including gestational age, first‐minute Apgar score, fifth‐minute Apgar score, and birth weight) and laboratory information (including umbilical cord pH and neonatal PAB levels). The SGA infants were classified as the case group, while the AGA infants served as the control group. The study compared PAB levels and neonatal outcomes—including asphyxia, retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), and sepsis—between the two groups.ResultsThe mean gestational age for the AGA group was 34.29 ± 4.31 weeks, while the mean gestational age for the SGA group was 34.75 ± 2.63 weeks. The 1‐min Apgar score showed significant differences, with a p‐value of 0.000, and the 5‐min Apgar score also differed significantly between the groups (p = 0.001), being higher in the SGA group. Additionally, the birth weight was significantly lower in the SGA group (p = 0.001). The mean neonatal PAB in the SGA group was 1.5 times greater than that of the AGA group (p = 0.000). When comparing the two groups, the incidence of acute asphyxia (p = 0.002), retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) (p = 0.028), intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) (p = 0.047), NEC (p = 0.000), and PDA (p = 0.049) showed statistically significant differences.ConclusionThe current study's results show that PAB levels are higher in SGA infants compared to AGA infants. Additionally, instances of ROP, and NEC were more prevalent in the SGA group.

Introduction

Infants classified as small for gestational age (SGA) are those whose birth weight and/or length standard deviation score (SDS) is less than −2 when compared to an appropriate reference population. Several underlying factors contribute to SGA, including nutritional, hormonal, vascular, and genetic influences [1]. The incidence of SGA infants ranges from 3% to 10% and is associated with poor neonatal outcomes, including stillbirth, seizures, infections, intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), hypoxic‐ischemic encephalopathy, and increased mortality [2, 3, 4]. Infants who are SGA may face a higher risk of various health issues later in life, including complications during and after pregnancy, ongoing short stature, and metabolic disorders [5]. These risks are particularly low in healthy, term SGA infants who quickly catch up in growth after birth and reach similar growth levels as non‐SGA infants [6].

Term SGA newborns exhibit higher oxidative stress (OS) and reduced antioxidant defenses at birth compared to term AGA newborns [7]. During pregnancy, the increased energy demand for the growing fetus leads to an excess of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the placenta. In the third trimester, both the placental and maternal antioxidant capacities (AOC) help neutralize OS [8]. During pregnancy, the mother's AOC is crucial for understanding the possible relationship between the pathophysiology of SGA and OS [9]. Pregnancy brings about changes in the mother's antioxidant defense systems [10]. Placental insufficiency, a cause of IUGR, is linked to ROS that arise from trophoblast invasion into the placenta due to the high energy demands of cell growth and proliferation [11]. During pregnancy, the body's increased energy needs result in a higher production of ROS [7]. The placenta is the primary source of OS. Maternal antioxidants are expected to counteract increased levels of ROS and thus maintain balance. Elevated OS results in an unfavorable prognosis for fetuses and infants [12], the fetus and newborn are prone to OS due to their immature antioxidant systems [13].

In addition to being suggested as an indicator for future metabolic and cardiovascular disorders, OS may also significantly contribute to the development of SGA [14, 15]. Considering the potential link between PAB and SGA, this study aimed to compare infants classified as Appropriate for Gestational Age (AGA) with those classified as SGA based on PAB.

Methods

This cross‐sectional study involved 898 neonates, including 798 (88.9%) AGA and 100 (11.1%) SGA. The research was conducted at Ghaem Hospital in Mashhad using a convenience sampling method from 2019 to 2025. To determine the sample size for a study comparing the Prooxidant‐Antioxidant Balance (PAB) between SGA and AGA neonates, no direct references were available in the literature. However, based on the study by Gupta et al., which compared the oxidant factor catalase (CAT) between the two groups (1.48 ± 0.24 vs. 2.31 ± 0.20 U/g Hb), it was estimated that approximately 85 individuals per group would be required using the formula for comparing two means of a quantitative variable in two populations, with an alpha of 0.01 and a beta of 0.01. Given the prevalence of SGA, estimated at 7% to 10%, approximately nine hundred neonates would need to be screened to obtain around 85 SGA individuals. The inclusion criteria for the neonates were based on being born prematurely at Ghaem Hospital. Data were collected using a researcher‐designed questionnaire that included three sections: neonatal information (gestational age, first‐minute Apgar score, fifth‐minute Apgar score, and birth weight) and laboratory information (umbilical cord pH and neonatal PAB). SGA neonates were assigned to the case group, while AGA neonates formed the control group. PAB levels and neonatal outcomes (such as asphyxia, retinopathy of prematurity [ROP], IVH, neonatal NEC, patent ductus arteriosus [PDA], and sepsis) were compared between the two groups. In this study, neonates with a birth weight below the 10th percentile for gestational age were classified as SGA, whereas those with a birth weight between the 10th and 90th percentiles for gestational age were classified as AGA. Neonates were classified as asphyxiated and included in the study if they exhibited at least two of the following signs at birth:

Signs of fetal distress include a heart rate below 100 beats per minute (bpm), a lack of heart rate variability, or late decelerations. Other indicators are the presence of thick meconium in the amniotic fluid, along with symptoms such as hypotonia, bradycardia, or respiratory depression. An Apgar score of less than 4 at 1 min and less than 7 at 5 min is concerning. Additionally, a need for resuscitation lasting over 1 min that involved positive pressure ventilation (PPV) and oxygen in the delivery room is significant. Finally, a pH level below 7.2 or a base excess greater than 12 within the first hour after birth should be closely monitored [16]. The retina specialist conducted retinal examinations on neonates after dilating their pupils, using indirect ophthalmoscopy. The incidence of ROP in the infants' eyes was assessed according to the international classification of ROP [17].

Between Days 3 and 7 after birth, a cranial ultrasound was performed to assess for IVH. The diagnosis and classification of IVH were based on the Papile criteria, which outline grading as follows: ‐ **Grade 1**: Hemorrhage confined to the germinal matrix. ‐ **Grade 2**: IVH without ventricular dilation. ‐ **Grade 3**: IVH accompanied by ventricular dilation. ‐ **Grade 4**: IVH with associated parenchymal hemorrhage. Grades 1 and 2 are classified as mild IVH, while grades 3 and 4 are considered severe IVH [18]. NEC in SGA neonates was diagnosed at Bell stage II or higher [19].

PDA was diagnosed through an echocardiogram conducted by a cardiologist [20]. Neonates with a positive blood culture, at least one clinical sign of infection, and one specified laboratory sign are diagnosed with definite sepsis [21]. Neonates who were enrolled in the study were monitored until they were either discharged or passed away.

This study was conducted after obtaining ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences, and informed consent forms were completed by the mothers. Neonates with congenital anomalies, those who died in the delivery room, and those classified as large for gestational age (LGA) were excluded from the study.

The statistical analysis, reporting, and interpretation of our findings were conducted in strict accordance with the statistical reporting guidelines for clinical research outlined by Assel et al. [22].

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for cross‐sectional studies.

A total of 0.8 cc of blood was collected from the infants' umbilical cords for testing the PAB using duplicate enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) assays. The PAB was determined through two reactions involving TMB: one enzymatic reaction in which TMB is oxidized by peroxides (specifically hydrogen peroxide, H2O2) to form cationic TMB, and another reaction in which this cationic form is reduced by antioxidants, such as uric acid.

To prepare the solutions, 16 mg of TMB powder was dissolved in 10 mL of DMSO to create a DMSO/TMB solution. For the cationic TMB solution (TMB+), 400 µl of the DMSO/TMB solution was mixed with 20 mL of sodium acetate buffer (pH 4.5, 0.05 M). A freshly prepared chloramine T solution (70 µL of 100 mM) was then added, mixed thoroughly, and incubated for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. Next, 25 μL of horseradish peroxidase was added to the cationic TMB solution, and 1 cc was aliquoted into microtubes for storage at −20°C. To prepare 200 μL of the TMB solution, the DMSO/TMB solution was mixed with 10 mL of sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.8, 0.05 M), and then 1 mL of the enzyme‐containing cationic TMB solution was added. This mixture was incubated for 2 min at room temperature in the dark. For the ELISA procedure, 10 μL of each serum sample, standard, or blank (distilled water) was combined with 200 μL of the TMB solution and dispensed into ELISA wells, where it was incubated for 12 min at 37°C. The standard solution contained 250 mM hydrogen peroxide and 3 mM uric acid in 10 mM NaOH. After incubation, 100 μL of 2 N HCl was added to each well, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an ELISA reader. A standard curve was generated from the absorbances of standard samples (H2O2 and uric acid at various concentrations), which allowed for the determination of serum sample concentrations and expression of the PAB value in HK units.

Data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 20). Descriptive statistics, including the mean and standard deviation or median and IQR for quantitative variables, and frequency with percentage for qualitative data, were employed. In this analysis, the T‐test or Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Chi‐Square tests were used to compare quantitative and qualitative variables between the AGA and SGA infant groups. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 898 infants were included in the comparison, comprising 798 infants (88.9%) who were AGA and 100 infants (11.1%) who were SGA. Among them, there were 442 male infants (49.2%) and 456 female infants (50.8%). Of the total, 675 infants (75.2%) were delivered vaginally, while 223 infants (24.8%) were born via cesarean section. The mean gestational age was 34.35 ± 4.13 weeks, with a first‐minute Apgar score of 6.72 ± 2.45 and a fifth‐minute Apgar score of 8.11 ± 1.77. The average birth weight was 2277.89 ± 940.48 g, maternal age was 28.46 ± 6.32 years, parity was 2.11 ± 1.34, and the mean PAB was 18.22 ± 22.89 HK. In the AGA group, the median gestational age was 35.00 ± 7.00 weeks, while in the SGA group, it was 34.00 ± 2.75 weeks. The Umbilical cord pH (p < 0.001), first‐minute Apgar score (p < 0.001), fifth‐minute Apgar score (p < 0.001), and PAB (p < 0.001) were significantly higher in the SGA group, whereas the birth weight (p = 0.001) was significantly lower in the SGA group (see Table 1).

Discussion

In the present study, the median neonatal PAB in the SGA infant group was nearly 2 times greater than that in the AGA infant group. According to Saker M's research, SGA infants had a distinct lipid profile when compared to AGA infants. Additionally, the SGA group exhibited altered markers of OS compared to the AGA group [2].

In the review of the literature, no study was found that compared PAB in newborns between two groups; most studies have instead focused on comparing oxidants or antioxidants. Therefore, greater attention is given to these studies. In Gupta P's study, infants with a SGA designation had twice the concentration of MDA compared to infants of AGA. This finding indicates significant oxidative damage, as MDA is a product generated in tissues due to free radical damage. The measurement of MDA serves as a sensitive indicator of lipid peroxidation and OS. In the study group, the activities of superoxide dismutase and reduced glutathione (GSH) were found to be 60%–65% of normal levels, while the control group showed only 44%. Similar results were noted for CAT. These enzymes are crucial as the first line of defense against ROS and are widely recognized as primary antioxidants [23]. In one study, plasma MDA concentrations in low‐birth‐weight neonates at birth were found to be higher than in AGA infants. Additionally, a decrease in total antioxidant status (TAS) and levels of vitamins A, E, and C (dietary antioxidants) was observed [24]. A study found that the balance of extracellular thiols and disulfides in SGA infants is disrupted. This is evident through lower levels of native thiols and higher levels of disulfides. Maintaining thiol/disulfide homeostasis is important for various cellular activities, including providing antioxidant protection [9]. SGA infants demonstrated decreased antioxidant activities, including reduced CAT activity, GSH levels, and lower vitamins E and A levels [25]. The activities of CAT and GSH peroxidase, along with levels of vitamins E, C, and A, were significantly lower in the cord blood of SGA infants compared to AGA infants [26]. Infants who are SGA have a higher level of reactive oxidant molecules compared to infants in the control group [27]. In Tayman C's study, the antioxidant protection in utero and after birth for SGA infants was found to be insufficient, leading to increased OS [28]. In Gupa P's study, elevated MDA levels in the umbilical artery and vein of SGA neonates suggested that these infants experience more severe lipid peroxidation and OS compared to AGA infants [23].

Lipid peroxidation in the placenta may contribute to the pathophysiology of SGA infants. This process is a significant consequence of OS and occurs when polyunsaturated fatty acids are oxidized. This oxidation produces various molecules and oxidation‐derived aldehydes, including malondialdehyde (MDA), acrolein, and 4‐hydroxynonenal [7]. Lipid peroxidation produces compounds like 4‐hydroxynonenal [20], can influence cell proliferation and differentiation at low physiological concentrations, whereas at higher concentrations they induce apoptosis and necrosis [29].

Higher levels of peroxide (POX) and lower serum AOC in SGA newborns suggest that OS may be linked to SGA pregnancies and can cause stress in newborns. OS may represent a significant connection between an unfavorable intrauterine environment and perinatal complications, increasing the risk of issues later in life. The blood found in the umbilical vein reflects the complex relationship between the mother and placenta. OS is more pronounced in mothers of SGA infants, potentially leading to fetal and neonatal problems. This situation may stem from a failure in the protective function of the placenta, although the placental antioxidant defenses have not been thoroughly studied. Evidence supporting the hypothesis that maternal stress plays a critical role in the development of SGA infants is found in the observation that mothers of SGA infants have higher levels of MDA and POX, while their AOC is lower and does not improve in the 3 days following delivery [7]. POXs serve as mediators in the process of lipid peroxidation. Their activity can stimulate additional peroxidation and lead to the formation of byproducts, including reactive aldehydes such as MDA and 4‐hydroxy‐2‐nonenal (HNE). These byproducts can have various toxic effects and may impact growth regulation [30].

Elevated levels of MDA have been observed in newborns who are SGA. Additionally, immunohistochemistry findings show a significant presence of HNE‐protein in the stromal cells of placental tissue from SGA infants, which supports this hypothesis. However, research has not yet determined whether prenatal OS and lipid peroxidation—stemming from increased ROS and/or reactive aldehydes in placental circulation—affect these infants. Since HNE is acknowledged as a toxic secondary messenger of free radicals, it may also serve as a growth‐regulating agent, potentially interfering with the action of humoral growth factors in serum. Such adverse conditions are likely to obstruct optimal intrauterine growth [30, 31].

MDA is a specific end‐product of lipid peroxidation (specifically the degradation of polyunsaturated fatty acids) caused by ROS. It serves as a widely accepted single marker of oxidative damage to lipids [32]. In contrast to individual markers like MDA—which only reflects downstream lipid peroxidation—the PAB is a comprehensive, photometric assay that simultaneously measures both the total pro‐oxidant burden and total AOC in a single solution. While discussing MDA provided mechanistic context on how OS damages fetal tissues, evaluating MDA or Total Antioxidant Capacity (TAC) alone offers a one‐sided view. Furthermore, traditional MDA assays (e.g., TBARS) are labor‐intensive, require heating steps, and suffer from high cross‐reactivity that can overestimate OS. Conversely, the PAB assay (modified TMB method) is rapid, automated, highly reproducible, and easily integrated into clinical laboratories. Crucially, since cord blood volume from newborns—especially SGA infants—is strictly limited, the PAB assay allows for a holistic assessment of the neonatal redox state using a single, minimal‐volume sample rather than requiring multiple independent tests [33].

ROP Was More Common in SGA Infants Than in AGA Infants

The inclusion of these specific neonatal morbidities is deeply interconnected with the PAB. SGA infants are highly susceptible to chronic hypoxia and ischemia‐reperfusion injuries, which trigger severe OS that is directly reflected by an altered PAB. Consequently, this redox imbalance drives several classic oxygen‐radical diseases of infancy. For instance, in ROP, hyperoxia and subsequent OS disrupt retinal vascularization through the dysregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [34]. Multiple risk factors for ROP have been identified. Currently, there is debate regarding the role of being SGA in the development of ROP [35]. The study by Al‐Qahtani found a link between ROP incidence and SGA infants [36]. A study conducted by Razak revealed that SGA infants have a higher likelihood of developing any stage of ROP, as well as severe and treated ROP. Neonatologists should recognize SGA as a significant risk factor for ROP when assessing and counseling families with SGA preterm infants. It is essential to follow ROP screening guidelines and conduct follow‐up examinations for SGA infants to ensure early diagnosis and treatment [37]. Fortes Filho and colleagues found that the incidence of severe ROP was approximately 65% in SGA infants compared to 12% in AGA infants [38]. Gortner and colleagues emphasized a higher prevalence of ROP in SGA infants compared with AGA infants, although there was no significant difference in the prevalence of stage 3 disease between the groups [39]. In contrast, the study by Arora revealed that AGA and SGA preterm infants exhibited similar rates of ROP, indicating that SGA is not an independent risk factor for ROP [35]. In the study by Akkoyun, the incidence of ROP at any stage was similar between the SGA and AGA groups in infants over 30 weeks [40].

Sen P identified the increased vulnerability of SGA infants to ROP as being influenced by factors such as intrauterine growth restriction, physiological vulnerability, the need for specialized hospital care, and lower levels of growth factors [41]. SGA infants are at a higher risk of developing ROP due to incomplete retinal vascular development and increased physiological vulnerability. While research is still ongoing to establish a direct causal relationship, one proposed mechanism suggests that SGA infants may have a greater need for angiogenic factors, such as VEGF, in response to higher levels of tissue hypoxia. This can lead to abnormal and fragile growth of retinal blood vessels, increasing the risk of ROP [37]. A major source of discrepancy among these studies is the differing definitions of SGA, which significantly hinders our ability to draw accurate conclusions about the outcomes for this group. In various studies, SGA and growth restriction have been used interchangeably, often based on different weight percentile cutoffs. These observations underline the necessity for personalized care and targeted interventions to address the specific needs of SGA infants who are at risk for ROP.

IVH Was Significantly Lower in SGA Infants

The findings from various studies are contradictory. A large retrospective cohort study conducted by Piscopo found that among very preterm infants with a mean gestational age at birth of 29 weeks who survived, being SGA was associated with a lower rate of IVH compared to infants who were not SGA [42]. The study by Farghaly showed that SGA status was significantly less associated with IVH [43]. This finding aligns with the study by Alda, which demonstrated a significantly lower incidence of IVH in SGA [44]. In the study by Dykes, it was found that SGA infants face a higher risk of developing IVH compared to those who are AGA. The causes of IVH in preterm SGA infants may differ from those in AGA infants, potentially being more closely linked to prenatal nutritional status rather than to iatrogenic events that occur after birth. Events occurring before or during labor that lead to a reduction in supporting tissue within the germinal matrix of SGA infants could significantly contribute to the development of IVH in these preterm infants [45]. In the study conducted by Cacciabaudo, the incidence of IVH was found to be higher in the SGA group compared to the control group. This suggests that even though SGA infants were more mature than the control infants, they still experienced a greater occurrence of IVH [46]. In the study by Alghadam, a higher birth weight in non‐SGA infants was linked to an increased risk of IVH, whereas in SGA infants, a higher birth weight was associated with a decreased risk of IVH [47]. In Jensen EA's study, no differences in the risk of severe IVH were observed between SGA and non‐SGA infants [48]. The development of IVH is linked to the high vulnerability of the immature germinal matrix to ROS, which induces endothelial damage and subsequent hemorrhage [49]. Although the reason for this observation remains unclear, the higher incidence of maternal hypertension and preeclampsia in SGA infants may actually provide a protective effect against IVH and reduce the negative impacts of other factors contributing to fetal growth restriction [50]. There may be fetal compensatory mechanisms that support brain growth and ensure nutrient delivery to other body systems [51].

The Incidence of NEC Was Higher in SGA Infants

In the study by Gidi, neonatal complications such as NEC were prevalent among in SGA infants [52]. In the study by Ree, the risk of NEC in SGA infants was more than twice that of AGA infants [53]. In NEC, elevated OS compromises the immature intestinal mucosal barrier, thereby exacerbating inflammatory responses and culminating in tissue necrosis [54]. Risk factors linked to the development of NEC in SGA infants are not yet clearly defined [55]. In infants who are SGA, the incidence of NEC has been higher. In the Gidi study, neonatal complications, including NEC were more common in SGA infants [52]. In the study conducted by Ree, the risk of NEC in SGA infants was more than double that of AGA infants [53]. The risk factors related to NEC development in SGA infants have not yet been clearly established [55].

The increased risk of NEC may be linked to incomplete maturation of the intestines, which can occur due to chronic fetal hypoxia in the uterus. This condition causes blood flow to be redirected away from the gastrointestinal tract to vital organs because of cardiovascular complications [56]. The higher occurrence of NEC in SGA infants may be linked to the severe malnutrition these infants often face, which makes them more vulnerable to infections. While the exact mechanisms that connect malnutrition to immune suppression are not fully understood, strong epidemiological evidence supports this relationship [57].

One limitation of the current study was the failure to examine maternal illnesses and the absence of maternal PAB assessment. Future research is recommended to investigate the long‐term outcomes of PAB in SGA and AGA infants.

Conclusion

The results of this study indicate that PAB levels are higher in SGA infants compared to AGA infants. This suggests that increased OS in SGA infants can disrupt neonatal oxidative homeostasis during a crucial period of growth, potentially leading to adverse outcomes. Additionally, complications such as NEC and ROP were found to be more common in the SGA group. Therefore, prospective studies focusing on SGA infants may provide valuable insights into the pathophysiology of OS in this population.
