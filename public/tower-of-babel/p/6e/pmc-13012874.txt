The blanking interval after atrial fibrillation ablation: time for reassessment with pulsed field energy
Authors: Juan F Rodriguez-Riascos, Hema S Vemulapalli, James Y Kim, Luis R Scott, Arturo M Valverde, Mayank Sardana, Abhishek J Deshmukh, Suraj Kapa, Chris Mcleod, Komandoor Srivathsan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThe clinical significance of early recurrences during the blanking period following pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) remains uncertain, particularly after pulsed field ablation (PFA). This study aimed to evaluate the prognostic impact of early recurrences at 30-, 60-, and 90-day thresholds on outcomes following PFA for atrial fibrillation (AF).Methods and resultsConsecutive patients who underwent PFA for AF across multiple centres within the Mayo Clinic health system were included. The primary endpoint was AF-free survival. Kaplan–Meier and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to compare outcomes between patients with and without early recurrences using 30-, 60-, and 90-day thresholds. A total of 1162 patients were included. The estimated AF-free survival at 1 year was 70%. Early recurrences within 30-, 60-, and 90-days post-ablation were significantly associated with late recurrences beyond 90 days (hazard ratio [HR] 5.9, 95% confidence interval [CI] 4.4–7.9, P < 0.01; HR 6.6, 95% CI: 5.0–8.6, P < 0.01; and HR 9.1, 95% CI: 7.0–11.7, P < 0.01, respectively). These findings remained significant among patients undergoing continuous rhythm monitoring. Early recurrences within 30 days in this subgroup were significantly associated with a 1-year AF burden >1% or the need for repeat AF ablation (odds ratio 25.0, 95% CI: 6.6–126.8, P < 0.01).ConclusionEarly recurrences following PFA are strongly predictive of long-term arrhythmia recurrence and are unlikely to represent transient inflammatory phenomena. Accordingly, the applicability of the conventional blanking interval after PFA should be reconsidered.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Catheter ablation, Pulsed field ablation

Introduction

Pulsed field ablation (PFA) has emerged as a safe and effective alternative to thermal ablation for pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) in the treatment of atrial fibrillation (AF).1,2 As a non-thermal energy modality, PFA enables myocardial-selective injury due to the lower irreversible electroporation threshold of cardiomyocytes compared to most extracardiac tissues.3

Multiple PFA platforms and catheter systems have demonstrated efficacy comparable to standard thermal ablation techniques in reducing atrial arrhythmia recurrence.4–6 Notably, PFA induces a distinct pattern of tissue injury. Histopathological studies indicate that within 14 days post-ablation, inflammatory infiltration begins to resolve and is progressively replaced by fibrosis, suggesting that durable and irreversible myocardial injury may be established within the first 30 days following the procedure.7

A post-ablation blanking period is commonly employed in both clinical trials and routine practice after AF ablation. Traditionally defined as a 90-day window, the blanking period is based on the premise that early post-procedural arrhythmias are often transient and related to acute inflammation rather than procedural failure.8 However, this paradigm stems largely from experience with thermal ablation and may not be applicable to PFA due to its unique biophysical properties and healing kinetics.9

Recent data suggest that early arrhythmia recurrences following PFA may be more predictive of long-term failure than previously assumed.10 Therefore, re-evaluating the utility and duration of the blanking period in the context of PFA is warranted. The present study aims to characterize the incidence of early arrhythmia recurrences and assess their association with long-term arrhythmia-free survival.

Methods

The data underlying this article will be shared on reasonable request to the corresponding author.

This retrospective study included consecutive patients with a history of AF who underwent PFA with the FaraWave pentaspline catheter, including PVI, between February 2024 and March 2025 at the three major Mayo Clinic centres in the United States (Phoenix, AZ; Jacksonville, FL; and Rochester, MN). Patients younger than 18 years of age and those who underwent PFA targeting only non-pulmonary vein atrial triggers were excluded. Both first-time and redo PVI procedures were included. The index procedure was defined as the first PFA performed at any of the participating Mayo Clinic sites. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our institution.

Procedure characteristics

All patients were brought to the electrophysiology lab after providing informed consent. Antiarrhythmic drugs (AADs) were discontinued at least five half-lives prior to the procedure. PFA was performed under general anaesthesia and uninterrupted anticoagulation. Intraprocedurally, heparin was administered to maintain an activated clotting time between 300 and 350 s. Intracardiac echocardiography and fluoroscopy were used for guidance. A standard decapolar catheter was positioned in the coronary sinus, and transeptal access was obtained as previously described.11

For all redo ablations, and in selected first-time procedures at the operator’s discretion, a baseline three-dimensional electroanatomical map was created using EnsiteX (Abbott), CARTO (Biosense Webster), or Rhythmia (Boston Scientific) systems. Mapping was performed with either multipolar diagnostic catheters or the ablation catheter itself.

In first-time ablations, the catheter was positioned sequentially in each pulmonary vein, with the number of energy applications varying according to the operator preference. PVI was confirmed by demonstrating a bidirectional block. Post-ablation remapping was performed at the operator’s discretion. In redo procedures, targeted PVs were identified based on electroanatomical mapping data and the presence or absence of baseline isolation.

Additional ablation beyond the PVs was guided by clinical factors such as prior atrial ablation history, persistent AF, coexisting atrial arrhythmias, inducible atrial flutter/tachycardia, and electroanatomical findings. AADs were either resumed or initiated upon discharge at the discretion of the treating electrophysiologist.

Clinical outcome and follow-up

The standard follow-up strategy consisted of clinic visits at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months, with a 12-lead ECG performed at each visit, 3-month 48-h Holter monitoring, and, when applicable, device interrogations. Additional monitoring was performed at the discretion of the treating physician based on the occurrence of symptoms. Data from external monitoring devices (smartwatches and home-based ECG monitoring devices) were also reviewed during follow-up appointments.

AF recurrence was defined as any electrocardiographically documented episode of AF lasting more than 30 s. All post-ablation recurrences were recorded and analysed. To assess the significance of early events, episodes were categorized as early recurrences (within 30, 60, or 90 days) and late recurrences (beyond these thresholds).

The primary effectiveness outcome was freedom from AF recurrence during follow-up (regardless of AADs use), excluding events occurring within the predefined blanking periods of 30, 60, and 90 days. The primary safety outcome included procedure-related complications such as cardiac tamponade, perforation, peri-procedural stroke, vascular access issues, coronary artery spasm, and conduction disturbances following PFA.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as means (standard deviation [SD]). Categorical variables are presented as absolute and relative frequencies. Survival analyses were conducted using Kaplan–Meier curves. The time-to-event endpoints between patients presenting with early recurrences at 30, 60, or 90 days were compared with log-rank tests. A bivariate Cox regression model was conducted for AF-free survival, and variables with a P-value <0.1 were included in the multivariate model. Statistical significance was established with a P-value of <0.05, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Statistical analyses were performed with R statistical software (version 4.2.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 1162 patients were included in the analysis. The mean age was 66.5 years (SD ±10), and 368 patients (31.7%) were female. Paroxysmal AF was present in 715 patients (61.5%). Rhythm monitoring strategy

A total of 137 patients had implanted cardiac devices providing continuous rhythm monitoring, and 441 patients used external monitoring devices. Twenty-six patients used both strategies. Overall, 552 patients had either in situ or external rhythm monitoring data available, while 610 patients were followed using clinical evaluation and intermittent monitoring (see Supplementary material online, Table S2).

Long-term outcomes

The median follow-up was 280 (±138) days. Survival analysis without a blanking period demonstrated an estimated AF-free survival of 79% (95% CI: 76–81%) at 6 months and 70% (95% CI: 67%–73%) at 1 year. (Figure 1A). When stratified by AF type, patients with paroxysmal AF demonstrated a higher 1-year AF-free survival rate compared to those with non-paroxysmal AF (73% vs. 65%; hazard ratio [HR] 0.69, 95% CI: 0.55–0.87; P < 0.01) (Figure 1B). Additionally, patients who underwent redo ablation had lower AF-free survival rates than those undergoing a first-time procedure (64% vs. 71%; HR 1.36; 95% CI: 1.05–1.77; P = 0.02) (Figure 1C).

Impact of early recurrences on long-term outcome

Early recurrences were observed in 94 patients (8.1%) at 30 days, in 129 patients (11.1%) at 60 days, and in 172 patients (14.8%) at 90 days. Among patients with early recurrences at 30, 60, and 90 days, 46.8%, 48.8%, and 47.1% were on AADs, respectively. Recurrence rates were higher among patients with external monitoring devices (9.4% at 30 days, 12.0% at 60 days, and 17.1 at 90 days) and those with in situ cardiac devices (18.2% at 30 days, 27.7% at 60 days, and 34.3% at 90 days), compared with patients without continuous monitoring (4.9% at 30 days, 6.7% at 60 days, and 8.9 at 90 days). The impact of early recurrences using the proposed blanking period thresholds of 30 and 60 days is presented in Figure 2, whereas the standard 90-day blanking period is shown in Figure 3. Recurrence within 30 days was associated with recurrences beyond 90 days (HR 5.9, 95% CI: 4.4 –7.9; P < 0.01), 60 days (HR 5.6, 95% CI: 4.2–7.5; P < 0.01), and 30 days (HR 5.5, 95% CI: 4.1–7.3; P < 0.01), while early recurrence within 60 days was associated with recurrences beyond 60 days (HR 6.4, 95% CI: 4.9–8.3; P < 0.01) and beyond 90 days (HR 6.6, 95% CI: 5.0–8.6; P < 0.01). Finally, recurrences within 90 days showed the strongest association with lower AF-free survival during follow-up (HR 9.1, 95% CI: 7.0–11.7; P < 0.01).

Notably, among the 552 patients with external monitoring devices, implanted cardiac devices, or both, early recurrences within 30 days were consistently associated with long-term arrhythmia recurrence (HR 5.4, 95% CI: 3.9–7.5, P < 0.01). These patients exhibited significantly higher rates of AF recurrence, with estimated 1-year AF-free survival of 52%, 70%, and 77% among patients with implanted devices, external monitoring devices, and no continuous monitoring, respectively (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1).

Among the 77 patients with implanted cardiac devices and available 1-year follow-up, early recurrences were not only associated with an increased risk of AF recurrence during follow-up but also with a higher likelihood of having an AF burden ≥1% or requiring repeat AF ablation at 1 year (OR 25.0, 95% CI: 6.6–126.8; P < 0.01).

In subgroup analyses stratified by AF type and history of prior ablation, the association remained significant across all groups. Early recurrence was associated with an increased risk of late recurrence in both paroxysmal and non-paroxysmal AF (HR 5.1, 95% CI: 3.4–7.7; P < 0.01 and HR 5.4, 95% CI: 3.7–8.2; P < 0.01, respectively), as well as in patients undergoing first-time and redo ablation (HR 5.2, 95% CI: 3.7–7.3; P < 0.01 and HR 6.5, 95% CI: 3.8–11.3; P < 0.01, respectively) (see Supplementary material online, Figures S2 and S3).

A total of 500 patients (43%) were discharged on AADs following ablation. Among these patients, 278 continued the same AAD regimen used before ablation, whereas 222 were prescribed a new regimen. There was no independent association between post-ablation prescription of AADs and recurrences during follow-up after a blanking period of 30 days (adjusted HR 1.2, 95% CI: 0.9–1.6; P = 0.28). Likewise, AAD use was not associated with early recurrences during the 30-day blanking period (odds ratio [OR] 1.2, 95% CI: 0.8–1.8; P = 0.44).

After multivariate analysis, the risk of late AF recurrence was lower in patients with paroxysmal AF (HR 0.64, 95% CI: 0.48–0.85; P < 0.01). Conversely, in addition to early recurrences, history of previous PVI (HR 1.56, 95% CI: 1.13–2.16; P = 0.01), a higher left atrial volume index (HR 1.01, 95% CI: 1.00–1.02; P = 0.03), and the presence of cardiac implantable devices or external monitoring devices (HR 1.67, 95% CI: 1.25–2.24; P < 0.01) were associated with a higher risk of late recurrences.

In our cohort, PFA achieved a high acute success rate, with bidirectional PVI accomplished in 99.9% of patients. The 1-year AF-free survival rate of 70% aligns with previously published studies.4–6 As expected, patients with non-paroxysmal AF exhibited lower AF-free survival rates, consistent with prior reports.12,13 Interestingly, patients with a history of prior PVI demonstrated higher long-term recurrence rates. This observation may reflect more advanced atrial disease, characterized by complex arrhythmogenic substrates extending beyond the pulmonary veins and progressive left atrial remodelling not fully captured by the echocardiographic variables included in our Cox model.

The concept of a post-ablation blanking period is grounded in the understanding that early arrhythmic episodes may be driven by transient inflammation and do not necessarily signify procedural failure. Although the 90-day blanking period is commonly applied after thermal ablation, the optimal duration remains uncertain.14

Prior studies of thermal ablation (e.g. radiofrequency and cryoballoon) have shown that early recurrences at 30, 60, or 90 days are predictive of late AF recurrence, with risk increasing over time.15,16 Our findings are consistent with this pattern: early recurrences were significantly associated with late recurrences.

Data specific to the blanking period following PFA are limited. As PFA induces myocardial injury via non-thermal electroporation, the kinetics of inflammation and fibrotic remodelling may differ. Histopathological studies suggest that fibrosis may begin as early as 14 days post-ablation.7 Reported incidences of early recurrence after PFA range from 7.1% to 31.6%,10,17,18 figures consistent with our observations and reinforcing the need to better characterize their clinical implications.

Our findings validate earlier reports linking early and late recurrence after PFA, while extending prior work by evaluating a shorter blanking period threshold of 30 days and providing device-specific data for the pentaspline FaraWave catheter.10,17–19 In contrast with a previous report, we demonstrated a significant association between late recurrence and very early recurrence (within 30 days).20

A secondary trial analysis reported by Boersma et al. documented early recurrence rates comparable to those observed in patients undergoing continuous monitoring in our study.10 Notably, in this study, a higher burden of early recurrence episodes was associated with an increased risk of late recurrences. Similarly, this study demonstrated a significant association in both paroxysmal and persistent AF. Moreover, the reported rate of freedom from late atrial tachyarrhythmias was consistent with our observed AF-free survival at 1 year, with slightly higher rates likely attributable to the exclusion of patients with prior failed AF ablations.

Di Biase et al. also reported a secondary trial analysis evaluating the impact of early recurrences on long-term outcomes. In this cohort, 18.3% of patients experienced early recurrences, with a 1-year freedom from atrial tachyarrhythmias of 29%, which was consistent with our findings. Notably, data specifically addressing recurrences within the first 30 days were not reported in that study.

In our cohort, analyses limited to patients with continuous rhythm monitoring yielded findings consistent with those of the overall population. These results support the robustness of our conclusions in a population with stringent rhythm surveillance, in which both symptomatic and asymptomatic recurrences are reliably captured.

These findings suggest that the concept of a blanking period should be reassessed. A key and challenging issue is defining an optimal threshold that distinguishes benign early recurrences related to post-PFA inflammatory changes from true procedural failure. According to our data, early recurrences occurring even within the shortest evaluated threshold of 30 days were clinically and statistically associated with late recurrences. Although further analyses using more granular, day-by-day data are warranted, our findings support a maximum tolerated blanking period of 30 days until more precise temporal data become available.

More recent guidelines propose shortening the blanking period to 8 weeks, an approach supported by accumulating evidence and the rationale of avoiding unnecessary exposure to repeat procedures, such as redo ablations.21 However, it is important to highlight that patients experiencing recurrences, including those occurring within the first 30 days, have an exceptionally high risk of long-term procedural failure. Therefore, even when a conservative management strategy is initially adopted, the likelihood of requiring further therapeutic intervention remains substantial in this population.

As proposed by Pürerfellner et al., the main implications of modifying the blanking period relate to changes in clinical practice regarding the management of early recurrences, trial design, and patient counseling.22 Maintaining a concept solely for the sake of data consistency, regardless of emerging scientific evidence, is not an appropriate approach. As new data become available, previously standardized parameters should evolve. However, we agree that a transitional strategy, presenting both the traditional 90-day blanking period and a non-blanking period analysis, represents a feasible and pragmatic alternative.

We suggest that early recurrences should be approached similarly to standard late recurrences, particularly in the setting of paroxysmal AF. Early recurrence should not automatically be equated with the need for redo ablation. In both early and late recurrences, once sinus rhythm is restored (with or without intervention) the timing of subsequent episodes and the associated symptomatic burden remain unpredictable. Therefore, a shared decision-making approach is essential, providing patients with realistic expectations rather than reassurance unsupported by robust data. When symptomatic burden, asymptomatic atrial remodelling, thromboembolic risk, or other clinical considerations outweigh procedural risk, redo ablation should be considered as a therapeutic option.

Limitations

This was a large, multi-centre, real-world registry conducted within a major US health system. However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the observational design introduces potential selection bias despite the inclusion of consecutive patients. Second, adjunctive lesion sets outside the PVs were performed using either PFA or thermal energy, introducing procedural heterogeneity. Third, variations in AAD adherence and rhythm monitoring may have influenced outcomes. Although multivariate adjustments were performed, residual confounding by unmeasured variables cannot be excluded. Additionally, our cohort includes both first-time and redo ablations, with variability in post-ablation management strategies. This limitation was addressed through subgroup analyses demonstrating consistent findings across these analyses. Finally, despite the large sample size, the number of documented early recurrences and the available monitoring strategies did not allow for analyses of weekly or daily blanking period thresholds.

Conclusions

Early arrhythmia recurrence within 30 days following PFA is a strong predictor of long-term AF recurrence. These early episodes may reflect procedural failure rather than transient inflammatory responses. These findings suggest that patients with early recurrence represent a high-risk subgroup and warrant closer follow-up.
