Maternal and Placental Inflammation Influence Formation of Neural Tube Defects in Quaternary Ammonium Compound Exposed Mice
Authors: Justin Kula, Ryan P. Seguin, Libin Xu, Dylan Davis, Sarah E. Payne, Chad E. Byrd, Terry C. Hrubec
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundQuaternary ammonium compounds (QACs) are common chemicals used for their antimicrobial, antistatic, and surfactant properties. Two common QACs, alkyldimethylbenzyl ammonium chloride (ADBAC or BAC) and didecyldimethyl ammonium chloride (DDAC), cause neural tube defects (NTDs) in mice and are proinflammatory in both mice and humans. The maternal immune system plays an integral role in normal fetal development with cytokine mediators in the placenta directing both placental and fetal development. This study was conducted to determine whether maternal systemic or placental inflammation was involved in QAC‐induced NTDs.MethodsPregnant CD‐1 mice received either a single gavage dose on gestational day (GD) 8.5; two gavage doses, one on GD 7.5 and the other on GD 8.5; or were dosed in the feed prior to mating and throughout breeding and gestation. Maternal whole blood, maternal bone marrow‐derived macrophages, placentas, GD 10.5 embryos, and GD 17.5 fetuses were collected for NTD, cytokine and QAC analysis. Cytokines IL‐6, IL‐10, IL‐12, PlGF, and TNF‐α were determined by ELISA. ADBAC and DDAC concentrations were determined by UPLC–MS/MS.ResultsADBAC and DDAC concentrated in placental tissue. Placentas from exposed pregnancies demonstrated increased production of inflammatory IL‐10, IL‐6, and TNF‐α in embryo–placental units with NTDs. Inflammatory IL‐10, anti‐inflammatory IL‐10, IL‐6, and TNF‐α were increased in macrophages isolated from QAC‐exposed mothers, and plasma IL‐6, and TNF‐α were correlated with NTDs.ConclusionThe placenta is the target tissue for ADBAC and DDAC‐induced teratogenicity. Both placental inflammation and maternal systemic inflammation are likely involved in QAC‐induced NTD formation in mice.

Introduction

Quaternary ammonium compounds (QACs) are a broad class of chemicals used in many products for their antimicrobial, antistatic, and surfactant properties. They are commonly found in disinfectants and cleaners, laundry products, personal care products, and used as preservatives in ophthalmic solutions and in food. QACs have long been considered safe but recent studies have begun to question this paradigm. We have shown that two common QACs, alkyldimethylbenzyl ammonium chloride (ADBAC, benzalkonium chloride or BAC) and didecyldimethyl ammonium chloride (DDAC) are reproductive and developmental toxicants in rodents. Exposure causes both male and female infertility in the parents and neural tube birth defects (NTD) in the offspring (Melin et al. 2014, 2015; Hrubec et al. 2017). ADBAC and DDAC are proinflammatory in mice and humans. In mice, bone marrow‐derived neutrophils exposed to ADBAC + DDAC exhibit a proinflammatory phenotype with increased production of interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF‐α) (Abdelhamid et al. 2020). In humans, we observed dose–response curves with increased inflammatory markers in people with higher QAC residues in their blood (Hrubec et al. 2021). QACs also alter sterol and lipid homeostasis and inhibit mitochondrial function in cell culture, rodents, and humans (Herron et al. 2016, 2019; Datta et al. 2017; Hrubec et al. 2021). Human exposure is ubiquitous. In a study conducted prior to the COVID‐19 pandemic, 80% of individuals had ADBAC and DDAC residues in their blood (Hrubec et al. 2021). A second study evaluating 15 different QAC species in human blood samples before and during the pandemic found a significant increase of 77% in the median concentrations of total QACs, and an increase of 174% in the median concentration of total ADBAC species (Zheng et al. 2021).

NTDs are common birth defects, with a prevalence of 1.86 per 1000 live births worldwide (Blencowe et al. 2018). The defect occurs from impaired closure of the neural tube during brain and spinal cord development resulting in spina bifida and anencephaly. The maternal immune system plays an integral role in the development of the fetus and can cause or prevent birth defects (Mukhopadhyay et al. 2016; Ornoy and Miller 2023). Cytokines act as mediators to activate signaling cascades, change cell behavior, and remodel tissue. A delicate balance of cytokine concentrations needs to be maintained throughout pregnancy to ensure proper embryogenesis and fetal development. As the mediator between the mother and fetus, the immune status of the placenta plays a critical role in directing fetal development (Ornoy and Miller 2023). Chronic inflammatory conditions such as diabetes and infections can disrupt this balance, causing structural damage to the placenta and changes in the developing fetus (Lian et al. 2004). Increases in proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF‐α and IL‐6 are associated with increased NTDs (Nivrenjeet et al. 2022) and fetal resorptions (Sousa et al. 2021). Placental inflammation can also cause abnormal embryonic heart development (Ward et al. 2023).

We found previously that ADBAC + DDAC exposure causes a fetal to placenta ratio mismatch which is seen along with increased embryonic loss and NTD formation (Hrubec et al. 2017). This indicates the placenta may be a target for QAC teratogenicity and possibly plays a role in NTD formation. Toxicant exposure can affect placental growth and remodeling by decreasing placental mass, altering cytotrophoblast fusion and syncytialization, and altering extravillous trophoblast invasion and spiral artery remodeling (Bloom et al. 2022). This study was conducted to investigate if QACs induce inflammation either in the mother or placenta as a mechanism for NTD formation. We hypothesize that exposure to ADBAC + DDAC during pregnancy alters cytokine profiles, inducing a proinflammatory state both systemically and in the placental microenvironment, leading to an increased rate of NTDs.

Materials and Methods

ADBAC and DDAC Reagents

All experiments used a mixture of ADBAC and DDAC in a ratio of 6.76% ADBAC (60% C14, 25% C12, 15% C16) (Sigma Chemical, St. Louis, MO) and 10.1% DDAC (AK Scientific Inc., Union City, CA).

Animal Husbandry and Dosing

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Animal care and generation of the ADBAC + DDAC free mice followed the procedure of Melin et al. (2015). Briefly, CD‐1 mice were housed in an ADBAC + DDAC free facility with a 12‐h light/dark cycle, 23°C, 30%–60% relative humidity, and provided Harlan 7913 chow (Harlan Teklad, Madison, WI) and distilled water ad libitum. Three separate dosing conditions were tested. For all exposures, ADBAC + DDAC free mice were transferred to a separate mouse facility utilizing ADBAC + DDAC disinfectants; other husbandry conditions, including husbandry staff, remained the same. Transfer of exposed groups to a separate facility is necessary to prevent contamination of ADBAC + DDAC free controls (Melin et al. 2015; Hrubec et al. 2017). Equivalent cohorts remained in the ADBAC + DDAC free facility as controls.

Single Oral Gavage Dose

At 7 weeks of age, a group of four males and 10 females was transferred and acclimated to the QAC use facility for 3 days before pairing for breeding. Females were checked for sperm plugs each morning and the presence of a plug was considered gestational day (GD) 0.5. Mice were orally gavaged with corn oil vehicle (controls) or with 5 mg/kg ADBAC + DDAC dissolved in corn oil (dosed) on the evening of GD 8.5 which is immediately prior to the critical period for NT closure. Tissues collected included GD 10.5 embryos, placentas, and bone marrow.

Two Oral Gavage Doses

Conditions for this experiment were identical to the single oral gavage dose except that mice received an additional gavage with either corn oil (controls) or with 5 mg/kg ADBAC + DDAC (dosed) on GD 7.5. Tissues collected included GD 10.5 embryos, placentas, and maternal blood.

Diet Dosed

At 7 weeks of age, 20 males and 20 females were transferred to the QAC facility. Mice were acclimated for 1 week to Nutra‐gel diet (purified dry mix formula, Bio‐Serv, Frenchtown, NJ) prepared following the manufacturer's instructions. Dosed mice were then fed the gel diet with ADBAC + DDAC incorporated into the feed to give a consumed dose of 60 mg/kg/day beginning 5 days before breeding and throughout gestation. Food intake was monitored daily to ensure accurate dosing. Pregnant dams were euthanized on GD 10.5 or on GD 17.5. Tissues collected on GD 10.5 included embryos and maternal blood. Tissues collected on GD 17.5 included fetal brains and placenta.

Tissue Collection

For the single and two gavage dose experiments, pregnant females were euthanized on GD 10.5 by CO2 inhalation. Females in the two gavage dose group were bled immediately following euthanasia and blood was transferred into a heparinized blood tube. The femur and tibia were removed under sterile conditions from euthanized dams in the single gavage dose group. Epiphyses were cut on either end and bone marrow was flushed with sterile phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS). Cells were suspended in DMEM/F‐12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% pen/strep, and M‐CSF and allowed to differentiate for 7 days. Following euthanasia in both the single and two gavage dose groups, uteri were removed under sterile conditions, and the placenta and embryo still within the yolk sac were dissected out and placed into sterile saline. The yolk sac was opened, and the umbilical cord followed to its insertion into the placenta which was then separated from the decidua and umbilical cord. Placentas were cut in half and placed into DMEM/F‐12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% pen/strep. In control mice, one placenta per litter was collected. In mice exposed to ADBAC + DDAC, two placentas were collected per litter, one from an embryo without a NTD and one from an embryo with an NTD.

For the GD 10.5 diet‐dosed experimental group, pregnant females were euthanized on GD 10.5 by CO2 inhalation and then bled. Blood was transferred into a heparinized blood tube. The uteri were removed and fixed in 10% buffered formalin for later dissection and assessment of embryos. For the GD 17.5 diet‐dosed group, dams were euthanized by CO2 inhalation and the fetuses and placentas were dissected out. Fetal weight and placental weight were obtained. Half the placentas and fetal heads from each GD 17.5 litter were frozen on dry ice for cytokine analysis while the remainder were fixed in formalin for QAC quantification.

Immune System Activation and Measurement

For each dam, blood collected from dams receiving two gavage doses and those exposed through the diet was split into two tubes: one unstimulated and one stimulated with 100 ng/mL LPS. Following the addition of LPS, both tubes of whole blood were cultured for 18 h in a standard CO2 cell culture incubator (Hrubec et al. 2021). The blood was then centrifuged and the plasma was removed for analysis of cytokine concentrations. For GD 10.5 placentas collected from the single and two gavage‐dosed dams, one half of each placenta was stimulated with 20 ng/mL LPS while the other half remained unstimulated. Following the addition of LPS, both samples were cultured for 18 h in a standard CO2 cell culture incubator. Following culture, the culture media were removed and assayed for cytokine concentrations. Bone marrow‐derived macrophages from dams receiving a single gavage dose were plated at 125,000 cells/mL on a 12‐well plate. Cells were divided into three wells: one unstimulated, one stimulated with 20 ng/mL LPS for 18 h for inflammatory interleukin‐10 (IL‐10) production, and one stimulated with LPS for 18 h after priming cells with 50 ng/mL PGE2 for 24 h for anti‐inflammatory IL‐10 production. For blood, maternal bone marrow‐derived macrophages (BMDM), and GD 10.5 placentas, TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐10 production was determined by ELISA (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) according to the manufacturer's instructions. For GD 17.5 fetal heads and placentas, frozen tissues were thawed and homogenized on ice in PBS with protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). The homogenate was assayed for TNF‐α, IL‐6, IL‐10, placental growth factor (PlGF), and interleukin‐12 (IL‐12) concentration by ELISA (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Protein concentrations in GD 17.5 placental and fetal head homogenates, and in BMDM cell pellets were determined using the Bradford method with BSA as a standard (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO).

Extraction of QACs From Whole Blood

Whole blood (75 μL) was mixed with sterile water (150 μL) and sonicated for 10 min at room temperature in polypropylene microtubes. The resulting mixture was spiked with deuterium‐labeled internal standards (3 pmol each of d7‐BACs C12, C14, and C16) (Herron et al. 2016) delivered in acetonitrile (75 μL). Spiked mixtures were vortexed for 2 min and rested for 10 min at room temperature. Protein was precipitated by adding 750 μL of organic solvent (acetonitrile:methanol, 4:1), vortexing for 2 min, and resting on ice for 15 min. After centrifugation for 15 min at 16,000g and 4°C, the supernatant (850 μL) was transferred to a glass LC vial and concentrated to approximately 600 μL under a gentle stream of argon gas. LC vials were capped, vortexed briefly, and loaded to an autosampler for LC–MS/MS analysis.

Extraction of QACs From Tissues

Tissue (20 mg) was digested in aqueous 1 M NaOH (80 μL) by heating the mixtures to 65°C for 1 h in screw‐top polypropylene tubes with O‐ring caps. The resulting tissue digest was spiked with deuterium‐labeled internal standards (0.8 pmol each of d7‐BACs C12, C14, and C16) delivered in acetonitrile (20 μL), vortexed for 2 min, and rested for 10 min at room temperature. Liquid–liquid extraction was performed three times with 300 μL of organic solvent (chloroform:methanol, 9:1), and the combined extracts were evaporated to dryness under argon gas. Sample residues were reconstituted in 250 μL of acetonitrile/water (4:1) mixture and transferred to LC vials before being loaded to an autosampler for LC–MS/MS analysis.

LC–MS/MS Analysis of QACs in Blood and Tissue Extracts

Levels of DDAC (C10:C10) and BACs (C12, C14, and C16) were measured by liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) in a Waters SYNAPT XS quadrupole time‐of‐flight (QToF) mass spectrometer coupled to a Waters ACQUITY UPLC system (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA). Aliquots (15 μL) of sample extracts (or standard curve) were injected onto a Thermo Hypersil GOLD C18 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm particle) run at ambient temperature and 0.400 mL/min flow rate. Mobile phase solvent A consisted of water with 0.1% formic acid and 2 mM ammonium formate and mobile phase solvent B was acetonitrile. The LC mobile phase gradient began at 30% B and was linearly increased to 80% B over 10 min, then raised and held at 100% B for 3 min, and then returned to 30% B to re‐equilibrate the LC column for 2 min. LC retention times were 7.74, 9.50, 10.34, and 11.16 min, respectively, for C12‐BAC, C14‐BAC, DDAC, and C16‐BAC. Analytes were detected in electrospray positive mode (ESI+) with the following source conditions: capillary voltage 2.5 kV, sampling cone 30 V, source temperature 150°C, desolvation temperature 500°C, cone gas 50 L/h, desolvation gas 1000 L/h, and nebulizer gas 6.5 bar. Mass accuracy was calibrated with sodium formate cluster ions prior to the run, and leucine‐enkephalin lock mass ([MH+] = 556.2771 m/z) was infused during the run to acquire a mass correction reference. Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) was performed by isolating precursor ions in the quadrupole, inducing fragmentation in the transfer cell, and detecting fragment ions by accurate mass in the time‐of‐flight mass analyzer. Mass transitions (precursor ion → fragment ion; collision energy) were monitored for DDAC (326.4 → 186.222 m/z; 18 eV), C12‐BAC (304.3 → 212.238 m/z; 16 eV), C14‐BAC (332.3 → 240.269 m/z; 17 eV), and C16‐BAC (360.4 → 268.300 m/z; 18 eV). Internal standard d7(benzyl)‐BACs were monitored by the same fragment ions and collision energies as their non‐deuterated equivalents, but the precursor ions were heavier by +7 Da. Peak areas were integrated within TargetLynx using MassLynx software (Waters Corporation) after accurate mass filtering (±0.010 m/z) of the LC–MS/MS chromatograms. BAC peak areas were normalized to their respective d7‐BAC internal standards and d7‐C14‐BAC served as the internal standard for DDAC.

Statistical Analysis

Differences in cytokine production between two sample comparisons were determined by a two sample T‐test for normally distributed data or its nonparametric counterpart, a Wilcoxon rank sum test for non‐normal distributions. Differences in cytokine concentrations between three or greater sample comparisons were determined by a one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or its nonparametric counterpart the Kruskal–Wallis test. In all cases, significance was set at p < 0.05. Correlations between cytokine content and embryo/fetal status were determined by Pearson's correlation. Results with r > 0.8 and p < 0.05 were considered strong and significant; r > 0.8 and p < 0.10 were identified as weaker correlations. Differences in NTDs, placental weights, and resorptions were determined by ANOVA with significance set at p < 0.05. Data are expressed as the mean ± SEM.

Results

QAC Concentrations in Tissues

During the production of QACs, different ratios of chain lengths are produced depending on chemical reaction conditions. Metabolism and toxicity of QACs vary by chain length and it is imperative to identify concentrations of chain lengths as well as total QAC. The dosed mixture contained C14‐BAC at the highest concentration (60%) followed by C12‐BAC (25%). In mice receiving two gavage doses, the proportions of chain length found in tissues were the same as those in the dosed mixture (Table 1). Placentas of gavage‐dosed mice contained C14 at 136.1 nM and C12 at 85 nM. In mice dosed in the feed, the tissue concentrations for each chain length were not in the same relative relationship as in the dosed mixture. Diet‐dosed placentas contained 37.7 nM C14 and 42 nM C12, thus indicating differential metabolism or absorption of chain length based on dosing regimen. Concentrations detected in maternal blood were higher in gavage‐dosed mice compared to diet‐dosed mice despite being dosed at a lower concentration. This again indicates absorption/metabolism differences between dosing regimens. It is likely the bolus gavage dose spiked the blood concentration and overwhelmed the capability for metabolic conversion, compared to the consistent dosing over time with a diet dose.

Interestingly, the GD 10.5 embryos of gavage‐dosed mice did not contain QACs in their tissues, while diet‐dosed mice did have QAC tissue residues at GD 10.5 but not at GD 17.5. The placentas of gavage‐dosed mice had significantly higher concentrations of total QACs than that found in maternal blood, 913 versus 130 nM, respectively. While maternal blood QAC concentrations were not determined with the GD 17.5 placental samples, GD 17.5 placental concentrations of total QACs were higher at 290 nM versus 12.8 for maternal blood collected on GD 10.5. In embryo–placental units with NTDs, QAC concentrations across all chain lengths were lower than that seen in embryo–placental units without NTDs (Table 1).

NTD Formation, Placental Weight, and Resorptions

Embryos of pregnant females in all three dosing regimens developed NTDs to similar extents with 6.3%–11.9% NTDs per litter (Figure 1). No NTDs were observed in control embryos. Placental weights on GD 10.5 for mice receiving either a single or two gavage doses of ADBAC + DDAC, were significantly lower in embryo–placental units with NTDs compared to controls (Figure 2). There was no significant difference in placental weights between litters receiving a single gavage dose and those receiving two gavage doses (Figure 2A). There was also no difference in placental weight at GD 17.5 between control and dosed litters (Figure 2B). Embryo loss (resorption) was higher in all dosed groups compared to controls but was not statistically significant in any treatment regimen (Figure 3).

GD 10.5 Stimulated Cytokine Production in Placentas

Placental IL‐6 and TNF‐α

No significant differences in IL‐6 or TNF‐α production were observed between dosed and undosed placentas for either baseline (unstimulated) or LPS stimulated production in litters receiving a single ADBAC + DDAC gavage dose (Figure 4). In mice that received two doses, there was a significant increase in LPS stimulated IL‐6 production in exposed placentas from embryo–placental units with NTDs. TNF‐α followed a similar pattern with a significant increase in stimulated TNF‐α production in embryo–placental units with NTDs (Figure 4). It is interesting to note that the increased inflammation was observed in embryo–placental units with NTDs, yet across all QACs quantified, QAC concentration was lower in embryo–placental units with NTDs. We also noted that the GD 10.5 placentas from exposed mice were more friable and delicate than placentas from control mice. Although not tested, it is possible that the placentas from embryos with NTDs have less integrity and ability to concentrate QACs resulting in greater exchange of QACs with maternal blood.

Gestational day 10.5 placental IL‐6 and TNF‐α production following stimulation with LSP by dosing regimen. Both IL‐6 and TNF‐α production increased significantly in placentas receiving two gavage doses of ADBAC + DDAC but not in placentas receiving only a single gavage dose. Bars indicate mean ± SEM; those identified by different letters indicate a statistically significant difference (p ≤ 0.05) between groups; two sample T‐test; N = 4 litters per treatment.

Placental IL‐10

Placental production of inflammatory IL‐10 was increased to a greater extent than either IL‐6 or TNF‐α by ADBAC + DDAC exposure (Figure 5). In mice receiving a single dose of ADBAC + DDAC, baseline inflammatory IL‐10 was significantly increased in exposed placentas from embryos both with and without NTDs. Additionally, stimulated inflammatory IL‐10 was increased in embryo–placental units with NTDs. In mice receiving two gavage doses of ADBAC + DDAC, baseline concentrations were not increased above those of controls; however, stimulated inflammatory IL‐10 was significantly higher in embryo–placental units with NTDs (Figure 5).

Gestational day 10.5 placental IL‐10 production following stimulation with LSP by dosing regimen. Baseline inflammatory IL‐10 production increased significantly in placentas receiving a single gavage dose of ADBAC + DDAC. Stimulated inflammatory IL‐10 production was also significantly increased in embryo–placental units with NTDs that received two gavage doses of ADBAC + DDAC. Bars indicate the mean ± SEM; those identified by different letters indicate a statistically significant difference (p ≤ 0.05) between groups; two sample T‐test; N = 4 litters per treatment.

GD 17.5 Baseline Placental and Fetal Brain Cytokines

At GD 17.5, ADBAC + DDAC exposure significantly increased the baseline concentrations of IL‐10, IL‐12, and PlGF in the placentas of diet‐exposed mice (Figure 6). Additionally, baseline concentrations of IL‐6, IL‐12, and TNF‐α were significantly increased over controls in GD‐17.5 fetal brains (Figure 6). QACs were detected in GD 17.5 placentas but not fetal brain tissue (Table 1) indicating a direct placental influence of fetal inflammation.

In the placentas of ADBAC + DDAC diet exposed mice, inflammatory IL‐10, IL‐12, and PlGF concentrations were significantly increased above controls. In fetal brains, baseline IL‐12, IL‐6, and TNF‐α were significantly increased over controls in diet exposed mice. Bars indicate the mean ± SEM; those identified by different letters indicate a statistically significant difference (p ≤ 0.05) between groups; two sample T‐test; N = 4–5 litters per treatment.

BMDM

To investigate the systemic inflammatory response in the mother, we evaluated cytokine concentrations in maternal BMDMs. BMDM samples were collected from mice receiving a single dose of ADBAC + DDAC. BMDMs were differentiated into macrophages prior to evaluating cytokine production. Baseline IL‐10 concentration was not affected in BMDMs exposed to a single oral gavage of ADBAC + DDAC compared to controls (Figure 7). Production of both inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory IL‐10 was significantly increased in ADBAC + DDAC exposed BMDMs. Production of anti‐inflammatory IL‐10 was greater than that of inflammatory IL‐10 (38.6 pg/pg protein and 25.8 pg/pg protein, respectively), indicating possible stimulation of a regulatory effect to control inflammation (Figure 7). Both baseline and stimulated TNF‐α production by BMDMs exposed to a single maternal gavage dose was increased with ADBAC + DDAC exposure above controls (Figure 7). There was no change in IL‐6 production by BMDMs with ADBAC + DDAC exposure.

Stimulated cytokine production in bone marrow derived macrophages (BMDM) isolated from mothers exposed to a single oral gavage dose of ADBAC + DDAC. Exposure significantly increased inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory IL‐10 concentrations as well as baseline and stimulated TNF‐α. Bars indicate the mean ± SEM; those identified by different letters indicate a statistically significant difference (p ≤ 0.05) between groups; two sample T‐test; N = 4–5 litters per treatment.

Maternal Plasma

Baseline concentrations of TNF‐α and IL‐6 in maternal plasma were not altered by ADBAC + DDAC exposure when dosed by gavage on 2 consecutive days, or when dosed in the diet throughout breeding and gestation (Figure 8). Production of TNF‐α and IL‐6 following stimulation of whole blood with LPS decreased significantly in ADBAC + DDAC dosed dams compared to controls. The decline was observed in both dosing methods (Figure 8). Decreased maternal TNF‐α and IL‐6 production may be in response to ADBAC + DDAC stimulated anti‐inflammatory IL‐10 production observed in BMDMs (Figure 7) dampening the inflammatory response.

Maternal plasma TNF‐α and IL‐6 production following stimulation with LSP by ADBAC + DDAC dosing regimen. Baseline TNF‐α and IL‐6 were not significantly altered in dams receiving either two gavage doses or dams dosed in the diet. Stimulated TNF‐α and stimulated IL‐6 production were significantly decreased compared to controls. Bars indicate the mean ± SEM; those identified by different letters indicate a statistically significant difference (p ≤ 0.05) between groups; two sample T‐test; N = 5–9 litters per treatment.

Cytokine Correlation Analysis GD 17.5

During fetal development, correlations exist between cytokines, growth factors, fetal weight, placental weight, and live/dead status. These factors are tightly regulated in normal pregnancy and can lose their associations with teratogenic exposure. Correlations were determined by dosing regimen for the different tissues to help identify the effect of QAC exposure on fetal outcome. In GD 17.5 placentas of control mice, strong and significant correlations were observed for associations between placental weight and live/dead (resorbed) status (live r = −0.9764, p = 0.0043; dead r = 0.9764, p = 0.0043; Table 2). Associations with less certainty were observed in control placentas for fetal weight and placenta weight with fetal placenta ratio (r = 0.8433, p = 0.0755 and r = −0.8392, p = 0.0755, respectively; Table 2). These associations are expected in a typical pregnancy and reflect normal coordination of fetal and placental growth. In GD 17.5 placentas of ADBAC + DDAC exposed mice, there were no associations between fetal weight, placenta weight, fetal placental ratio, and live/dead status. Loss of these associations indicates other factors are likely influencing fetal survival and placental growth and development (Table 2).

PlGF is produced by the trophoblast and belongs to the vascular endothelial growth factor family. PlGF is necessary for normal placenta development and low PlGF is associated with placental pathology, intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), preeclampsia (PE), and small for gestational age (SGA) (Benton et al. 2016). However, overexpression of PlGF is also detrimental with reduced placental vasculature, increased rate of placental detachment, and increased gestational loss (Kang et al. 2014). PlGF is proinflammatory and works in concert with NF‐κB to regulate uterine immune status throughout pregnancy (Albonici et al. 2020). Strong and significant associations were seen in control placentas between PlGF and IL‐12 (r = 0.9085, p = 0.0328) and with IL‐10 (r = 0.8875, p = 0.0445; Table 2). The significance of this association is not clear, and these associations were not observed in ADBAC + DDAC exposed placentas. However, there was a strong and significant association between PlGF and fetal placenta ratio (r = 0.9999, p = 0.007) with higher PlGF concentrations associated with large fetuses with small placentas indicating a resource partitioning mismatch between fetus and placenta.

PlGF is expressed in fetal mouse brain and is necessary for normal neurovascular development (Luna et al. 2016). In GD 17.5 fetal brains of control fetuses, PlGF was associated with less certainty with live/dead status (live r = 0.9162, p = 0.0838 and dead r = −0.9162, p = 0.0838; Table 2). This association was lost in ADBAC + DDAC exposed fetal brains. PlGF had a strong and significant negative correlation with fetal weight (r = −0.9712, p = 0.0288; Table 2) again demonstrating altered resource partitioning between the fetus and placenta resulting in mismatched fetal placental growth (Table 2).

Discussion

Consistent with our previous findings (Hrubec et al. 2017), exposure to ADBAC + DDAC caused NTDs in all exposure groups. In our current study, we observed that QACs concentrated in placental tissues at both GD 10.5 and 17.5. QACs were present in GD 10.5 embryos of diet‐dosed mothers but not gavage‐dosed mothers. Both one and two gavage doses of QAC increased inflammation (IL‐10, IL‐6, and TNF‐α) in the placentas of embryo–placental units with NTDs but not in embryo–placental units without NTDs. Placental inflammation was correlated with NTD formation in both dosing regimens. ADBAC + DDAC exposure increased maternal inflammation with inflammatory IL‐10, anti‐inflammatory IL‐10, IL‐6, and TNF‐α production increased in BMDMs of QAC‐exposed mothers. Maternal inflammation was correlated with NTD formation.

Chronic inflammatory states, as seen in diabetes, increase the risk of NTDs (Lian et al. 2004). Previous studies have linked direct dose–response relationships between increased inflammatory cytokines and blood concentrations of ADBAC + DDAC in humans (Hrubec et al. 2021). In rodents, ambient exposure from the use of an ADBAC + DDAC‐containing disinfectant in the mouse room shifted mouse neutrophils to a proinflammatory neutrophilic phenotype with increased transcript levels of IL‐6 and TNF‐α (Abdelhamid et al. 2020). These studies and our current findings clearly demonstrate that ADBAC + DDAC exposure is proinflammatory.

ADBAC + DDAC was detected in maternal blood, placental tissue, and embryos of diet‐dosed mice. Pregnant mice that were gavaged had higher levels of ADBAC + DDAC in their blood and placental tissue compared to mice who were dosed in the diet. This was seen despite the total dose administered being far lower (5 mg/kg/day) in the gavage‐dosed groups compared to the diet‐dosed group (60 mg/kg/day). The likely explanation for this is the way in which the dose is administered. A gavage dose administers the full concentration at one timepoint and results in a higher absorbed concentration in a short timeframe. Dosing in the feed spreads the dose over a longer time period and leads to a prolonged exposure time with less absorbed at any one point. This is particularly true for rodents who nibble at their food constantly throughout the day.

ADBAC and DDAC metabolism likely plays a role as well. Metabolism of ADBAC and DDAC primarily occurs by hepatic cytochromes P450 (Seguin et al. 2019; Nguyen et al. 2024). It is possible that the bolus gavage dose overwhelmed metabolism pathways, allowing blood concentrations to remain high for longer periods of time. It may also be possible that some of the gavaged ADBAC + DDAC are rapidly absorbed across the oral and esophageal mucous membranes directly into the bloodstream which would bypass the first pass metabolism in the liver. Incorporation of the ADBAC + DDAC into the feed may slow absorption such that the majority of the dose is absorbed into the hepatic portal system for transport to the liver and first pass metabolism. Further investigation is needed to understand the effect of dosing regimen on the metabolism of ADBAC + DDAC and the contribution of their metabolites to their inflammatory and teratogenic properties.

QACs were undetected in the GD 10.5 embryos of gavage‐dosed mice and in GD 17.5 fetuses of diet‐dosed mice but were detected in the GD 10.5 embryos of diet‐dosed mice. A possible explanation for this is the dosing duration and the placenta type at the time of dosing. In rodents, the yolk sac is the sole functional placenta for more than half of pregnancy as the definitive placenta does not begin to develop until GD 10 (Ornoy and Miller 2023). The yolk sac placenta develops as the blastocyst hatches from the zona pellucida and begins the implantation process (Panja and Paria 2021). The yolk sac placenta is well established at GD 7.5, the time of the first gavage administration and the definitive placenta is well established at GD 17.5 the time fetuses were evaluated. It appears that ADBAC + DDAC cannot directly cross the placenta into fetal tissues at these time points. Diet‐dosed mice began exposure to ADBAC + DDAC prior to breeding and exposure continued until GD 10.5 or GD 17.5. This creates opportunities for ADBAC + DDAC to enter the developing embryo prior to yolk sac placenta maturation. ADBAC + DDAC could also be transferred through the oocyte into embryonic tissues or be absorbed from luminal fluid of the oviduct and uterus into the blastocyst prior to implantation. Any ADBAC + DDAC present at GD 10.5 would likely be eliminated from the fetus over time resulting in no detectable QACs at GD 17.5.

Interestingly, we observed increased inflammation in embryo–placental units with NTDs, yet QAC concentrations were lower in embryo–placental units with NTDs. These placentas were also more friable and delicate than placentas from control mice. Placental consistency may reduce the ability of the placenta to concentrate QACs resulting in greater discharge of QACs to maternal blood. Additionally, as QAC exposure causes developmental delay (Hrubec et al. 2017), it is possible that the placentas from embryo–placental units with NTDs are physiologically younger than placentas from embryo–placental units without NTDs. The definitive placenta only begins to form on GD 10 and a delay in development could result in a “younger” placenta with less structural integrity and less ability to concentrate QACs than a slightly more mature placenta.

Placental damage and/or dysfunction can be teratogenic, resulting in birth defects in embryos and fetuses even when the inciting agent does not cross the placenta (Ornoy and Miller 2023). As the mediator between the mother and fetus, the immune status of the placenta plays a critical role in directing fetal development. Placental inflammation can cause pathologic signaling resulting in developmental changes in placental growth (Woods et al. 2023). As observed with the loss of placental cytokine correlations with ADBAC + DDAC exposure, there can be an overall loss of normal physiologic relationships between placental cytokines, growth factors, and various metrics of pregnancy, leading to adverse developmental outcomes. ADBAC + DDAC exposure causes a fetal‐placental ratio mismatch resulting in both large fetuses with small placentas and small fetuses with large placentas (Hrubec et al. 2017). Disruption in placental development can lead to decreased placenta weight resulting in fetal–placental weight ratio mismatch. Fetal–placental mismatch is a common cause of pregnancy complications such as IUGR, PE, and SGA fetuses. Human fetuses with spina bifida and anencephaly have lower placenta weight than placentas from fetuses without these malformations (White et al. 2021). This corresponds with the smaller placentas in embryo–placental units with NTDs observed in the present study.

ADBAC + DDAC increased concentrations of PlGF in GD 17.5 placentas. Overexpression of PlGF is detrimental with reduced placental vasculature, increased rate of placental detachment, and increased gestational loss (Kang et al. 2014). In ADBAC + DDAC exposed GD 17.5 placentas, a strong positive correlation between PlGF and increased fetal placenta ratio (large fetuses with small placentae) was observed. Disruption in angiogenesis could explain the smaller, more friable placental tissue we observed in GD 10.5 embryos exposed to ADBAC + DDAC particularly those with NTDs.

Increased TNF‐α causes increased fetal loss, IUGR, skeletal development retardation and NTDs (Taubeneck et al. 1995; Xu et al. 2006). We found increased production of both TNF‐α and IL‐6 in GD 10.5 placenta samples of mothers receiving two gavage doses of ADBAC + DDAC. We also found increases in baseline IL‐12 and baseline inflammatory IL‐10 in GD 17.5 placentas of mothers exposed to ADBAC + DDAC in the diet. Implantation and placentation are tightly regulated processes in which IL‐6 is a pleiotropic molecule that plays a role in the normal establishment of pregnancy (Robertson et al. 2010). IL‐6 is produced by decidual cells and trophoblasts and plays a role in trophoblast proliferation, invasion, and differentiation (Vilotić et al. 2022; Jauniaux et al. 1996). Either decreased or increased IL‐6 concentrations cause IUGR resulting in lower birth weight, preterm birth, NTDs, and fetal loss (Prins et al. 2012; Krajewski et al. 2014; Mukhopadhyay et al. 2016). Our results support this finding as both NTDs and decreased placental weight were seen with increased concentrations of both placental TNF‐α and IL‐6.

While it is not known if QAC exposure causes structural malformations such as NTDs in humans, many chemical and pharmaceutical exposures that induce NTDs are also linked to neurobehavioral disorders (Meador et al. 2007; Perera et al. 2012; Wang et al. 2015). IL‐6 has been implicated in various neurobehavioral disorders such as autism spectrum disease (Pandey et al. 2017; Rudolph et al. 2018). Increased concentrations of IL‐6 prenatally affect cognitive development postnatally (Yockey and Iwasaki 2018). We found ADBAC + DDAC exposure increased the concentrations of IL‐6 in the fetal brain. This indicates that prenatal ADBAC + DDAC exposure may increase the risk of neurobehavioral disorders. Further research is clearly needed to elucidate the role that ADBAC + DDAC exposure may play in both structural and functional birth defects such as postnatal cognitive‐behavioral disorders.

Inflammation observed in cultured maternal BMDMs shows that a single dose of ADBAC + DDAC can affect monocyte/macrophage precursors in the bone marrow and cause maternal inflammation even after BMDM cell differentiation. ADBAC + DDAC exposure increased the production of inflammatory IL‐10, anti‐inflammatory IL‐10, and TNF‐α in BMDMs. However, plasma markers of inflammation were not increased in exposed mothers. The increased production of anti‐inflammatory IL‐10 from BMDMs could be blunting the concentrations of IL‐6 and TNF‐α in the blood. Anti‐inflammatory IL‐10 possesses protective properties against inflammation‐induced damage (Iyer and Cheng 2012). The elevated production of anti‐inflammatory IL‐10 in BMDMs may represent a response to increased inflammation aimed at counteracting potential damage.

Increased inflammatory markers were observed in GD 10.5 placentas for both the one and two gavage dose groups. Baseline IL‐10 and stimulated inflammatory IL‐10 were increased in the single gavage group; while stimulated inflammatory IL‐10, and simulated IL‐6 were elevated in placentas receiving two gavage doses. Increased cytokine concentrations were highest in embryo–placental units with NTDs. This supports the idea that placental inflammation is involved in ADBAC + DDAC induced NTD formation. However, IL‐6 and TNF‐α were not consistently elevated in all tissue/dosing regimen combinations. There are two possible explanations for this observation. First, it is possible that QAC‐induced anti‐inflammatory IL‐10 production in the placenta to counteract the increased inflammation. This could dampen IL‐6 and TNF‐α production resulting in the minimal response seen with these cytokines in the GD 10.5 placentas. Secondly, ADBAC + DDAC exposure results in a nonmonotonic dose response for inflammatory cytokines in humans (Hrubec et al. 2021). Others have shown that QACs bind to both the estrogen and acetylcholine receptors to exert nonmonotonic dose responses (Takeuchi et al. 1993; Calabrese 2005; Datta et al. 2017). It is possible to speculate that the increased production, lack of response and even declines in IL‐6 and TNF‐α seen in some of the tissue/dosing regimen combinations simply represent different locations along the nonmonotonic dose–response curve.

Conclusion

ADBAC and DDAC are frequently encountered chemicals in everyday products and individuals are likely exposed to these common chemicals on a daily basis. We found that ADBAC + DDAC can circulate in the blood and concentrate in placental tissue. The chemicals induce an inflammatory response in the placenta, leading to alterations in placental size, structure, and fetal placenta mismatch. Placental inflammation is increased in embryo–placental units with NTDs. Maternal inflammation was increased, and inflammatory cytokines present in maternal plasma were strongly correlated with NTD formation. Taken together, the results of this study strongly suggest the placenta is a target tissue of ADBAC + DDAC exposure, resulting in placental inflammation and NTDs; however, maternal inflammation appears to have a role in NTD formation as well.
