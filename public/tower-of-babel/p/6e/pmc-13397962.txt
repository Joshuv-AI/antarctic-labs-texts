Coral reef aorta in a child: a case report
Authors: Ma Yanling, Zhu Hongtao, Qu Ning
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension in children is predominantly secondary in nature. When conventional antihypertensive therapy proves ineffective, rare vascular conditions such as Coral Reef Aorta (CRA) should be considered. These disorders are extremely uncommon in pediatric practice and are often overlooked.Case presentationA 12-year-old Uyghur patient presented with intermittent headaches and severe hypertension. Computed tomography (CT) angiography of the aorta revealed multiple calcified plaques in the descending thoracic aorta, which demonstrated a coral reef-like appearance, as well as renal artery stenosis. At the 18-month follow-up, there was no recurrence of hypertension.ConclusionThis case confirms that rare vascular diseases such as CRA must be included in the differential diagnosis of refractory hypertension of unknown cause in children. Early completion of aortic CT angiography is crucial for establishing a definitive diagnosis, guiding treatment, and preventing long-term complications.Keywords: Coral reef aorta, Children, Refractory hypertension, Case report

Introduction

Hypertension in children is predominantly secondary, with common causes including renal, endocrine, and cardiovascular diseases. Beyond these, clinicians must remain vigilant for a range of rare etiologies, particularly when conventional antihypertensive therapy proves ineffective. CRA represents an exceptionally rare vascular disorder characterized by the formation of numerous hard, coral-like calcified plaques within the aortic lumen, frequently causing severe narrowing or occlusion of the aorta and its branches. This condition predominantly affects middle-aged and elderly individuals with atherosclerotic risk factors. Its occurrence in childhood is extremely rare globally, leading to low awareness in pediatric practice. It is frequently misdiagnosed as other vasculitic diseases such as polyarteritis nodosa, resulting in delayed diagnosis and inappropriate treatment. Whole-aortic CTA serves as the key modality for definitive diagnosis and assessment of lesion extent. This paper analyzes the clinical presentation, imaging features, and treatment strategy of a 12-year-old pediatric CRA patient initially misdiagnosed with "polyarteritis nodosa," aiming to enhance pediatricians' ability to recognize and manage such rare conditions.

Case description

A 12-year-old Uyghur female presented to a local hospital on Day 0, with complaints of "intermittent headaches for 5 months and a new diagnosis of hypertension made 6 days prior". Prior to this visit, the patient had experienced intermittent headaches for over 5 months. The pain was described as a persistent dull ache, lasting 3–4 hours per episode, accompanied by dizziness, fatigue, and vomiting. Physical examination revealed blood pressure of 205/140 mmHg. Renal CT scans demonstrated multiple low-density lesions in both kidneys and the spleen, consistent with splenic and renal infarction. Atherosclerotic plaque formation was observed in the thoracic and abdominal aortas. The patient was diagnosed with "polyarteritis nodosa, bilateral renal infarction, splenic infarction, and aortic atherosclerosis." Treatment included "Continuous nitroprusside infusion for vasodilation, along with benazepril hydrochloride and spironolactone for blood pressure management". However, blood pressure control remained inadequate. The patient was transferred to our hospital on Day 2, presenting with blood pressure of 225/158 mmHg. Laboratory investigations at our institution ruled out disorders of calcium and phosphorus metabolism and connective tissue-related diseases. Subsequent comprehensive CTA of the entire aorta revealed abdominal aortic atherosclerosis (Fig. 1a, d); occlusion of the left renal artery and superior mesenteric artery (Fig. 1b); and multiple calcified pulmonary nodules (Fig. 1c). Following a comprehensive assessment of the patient's imaging features by the imaging center, interventional radiology department, and remote consultation, a diagnosis of "coral reef aorta" was established. Therapeutic recommendations included renal arteriography combined with renal balloon angioplasty to address vascular stenosis.

Surgical procedure

Following the Seldinger technique, a right femoral artery puncture was performed on the patient. After successful puncture, a sheath catheter was inserted. An arterial catheter was then advanced through the sheath. Following catheter placement, angiography was performed at the level of the celiac trunk and bilateral renal artery origins, which demonstrated a patent right renal artery trunk, with moderate stenosis at its origin. Right renal parenchymal perfusion was normal. Intraoperative angiographic findings correlated with renal scintigraphy results. An ultra-slick stiff guidewire, an 8F guiding catheter, and a multifunctional catheter were deployed. Selective catheterization to the right renal artery revealed moderate stenosis at the origin of the right renal artery trunk. A 4 × 30 mm balloon catheter was placed transcatheter for renal artery balloon angioplasty. During balloon expansion, no significant constriction was observed. After repeated balloon dilation, considering the patient's young age and financial constraints, renal artery stenting was not performed following thorough discussion with the family. Postoperatively, blood pressure decreased compared to baseline, and anticoagulant therapy was initiated immediately. Discussion

CRA is a rare aortic disease named for the appearance of its calcified plaques, which resemble coral reefs in seawater [1]. In 1984, Qvarfordt et al. first described CRA, characterized by severe and extensive hard plaques protruding into the aortic lumen. Most commonly affecting the para-aortic regions, these plaques cause significant stenosis and obstruction of the aorta and its visceral branches [2]. This leads to severe consequences such as hypoperfusion and embolism in distal visceral and lower extremity vessels, manifesting as intermittent claudication and refractory hypertension [3]. The disease incidence ranges from 0.6% to 1.8%, with a female-to-male ratio of 1.6:1 [4].

This condition may occur in both thoracic and abdominal aortas, with the most common sites being the suprarenal or perirenal regions near the renal artery origins. It typically affects the dorsal portion of the vascular lumen and frequently involves the celiac trunk, superior mesenteric artery, and bilateral renal arteries [5]. This differs from typical atherosclerotic occlusive lesions of the aorta, which commonly affect segments distal to the renal arteries, including the bifurcation and associated bilateral iliac artery systems. Atherosclerotic occlusive disease in the aorta tends to occur in the segment distal to the renal arteries. Compared to the infrarenal segment, atherosclerosis in the suprarenal aorta is less common, partly due to favorable hemodynamics. However, a small proportion of lesions may progress to large exophytic intra-aortic plaques, presenting unique risks compared to infrarenal atherosclerosis. Because of such plaques may restrict blood flow to visceral organs, the kidneys, and the iliac-femoral arterial system [6].

The etiology of CRA remains unclear. Adult cases often coexist with traditional cardiovascular risk factors such as hyperlipidemia, diabetes, hypertension, and smoking. Some studies suggest an association with infectious agents like syphilis and rubella, which may trigger vascular wall inflammation, altering its structure and function and thereby promoting calcific plaque formation. Additionally, hereditary conditions such as neurofibromatosis may increase CRA risk. Traumatic factors, such as minor aortic dissections caused by deceleration injuries in car accidents, may also play a role. Over time, extensive repair processes following closure of the dissection may trigger excessive calcification. However, these hypotheses remain unconfirmed [7, 8].

The symptoms of CRA depend on the severity of the disease and the involvement of aortic branches; coronary artery embolism is typically asymptomatic. When symptoms do occur, they are usually related to organ ischemia caused by obstruction or distal embolism, primarily leading to intermittent claudication and severe ischemia in the lower extremities and organs. The literature also reports symptoms such as heart failure, renovascular hypertension, abdominal angina, acute kidney injury, and congestive heart failure [9, 10]. Approximately 44.3% of patients present with hypertension-related symptoms due to renal artery stenosis or suprarenal aortic stenosis, requiring combination antihypertensive therapy. These may include headaches, dizziness, and visual disturbances. Intermittent claudication occurs in about 38.6% of patients, manifesting as lower limb pain, numbness, or weakness after walking, which resolves with rest. Approximately 21.4% of patients experience symptoms such as abdominal pain, weight loss, and diarrhea due to chronic visceral ischemia [11–13].

In open surgery, thromboendarterectomy is the standard procedure for treating atherosclerotic occlusive lesions of the aorta and is also commonly used for CRA. For CRA, this surgery involves excising the diseased segment of the aorta along with affected branch vessels, such as the celiac trunk, superior mesenteric artery, and bilateral renal arteries. Endovascular treatment is suitable for patients with limited lesions that do not involve visceral arteries or renal arteries. Typically, a femoral artery puncture approach is used to introduce a balloon for pre-dilation of the stenotic segment, followed by stent implantation. Alternatively, balloon post-dilation may be performed after stent placement to optimize stent apposition [14]. The optimal treatment strategy for CRA remains unresolved. Baldaia et al. reviewed the current treatment landscape through a literature analysis of 41 studies involving 124 patients. Open surgical repair was performed in 88.7% of cases, yielding an in-hospital mortality rate of 10.9% and a complication rate of 28.2%. The high mortality and complication rates in this cohort may be attributed to severe cardiovascular burden, multiple comorbidities, and the invasive nature of open surgical procedures. Additionally, 8.9% of patients underwent endovascular repair, including stent placement and balloon dilatation, with no reported postoperative complications [15]. The choice between open surgery and endovascular intervention requires individualized decision-making. Depending on the extent of calcific lesions, open surgery may be the most appropriate and sometimes the only feasible option. While open surgery allows for complete removal of calcified plaques, it carries significant trauma and high complication risks. Potential complications include iatrogenic injury to other abdominal organs, cerebral or myocardial infarction, hemorrhage, or iatrogenic embolism. Endovascular intervention offers minimally invasive advantages, with potential complications including aortic rupture or iatrogenic embolism [16–18].

This 12-year-old patient presented without traditional cardiovascular risk factors such as hyperlipidemia, diabetes, or smoking history, nor any documented hereditary diseases or trauma history. However, the patient had concurrent active pulmonary tuberculosis. Studies indicate that infectious agents (e.g., syphilis, rubella) may induce chronic inflammatory responses in vascular walls, leading to endothelial injury, matrix remodeling, and abnormal calcification. Although Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection is not listed among the known triggers of CRA, as a chronic granulomatous infection, it can induce systemic inflammatory states and local vascular involvement. However, its association with this disease requires further research confirmation. The core manifestations in this patient were refractory hypertension and imaging-characteristic calcified stenosis, initially misdiagnosed as "polyarteritis nodosa." CTA findings in polyarteritis nodosa primarily include vascular wall thickening and edema (double-ring sign), concentric smooth stenosis or occlusion, and calcification (often late-stage), potentially involving the entire aorta and all its branches. During active disease, diffuse, uniform FDG uptake in the vascular wall is observable. In contrast, coral reef aortic CTA is characterized by massive, irregular, hard "coral reef"-like or "popcorn"-like calcifications at the infrarenal abdominal aorta or main iliac bifurcation. The vessel wall is typically not thickened, shows no signs of edema, and the lesions are relatively localized, usually without FDG uptake. The child's comprehensive CTA findings highly correlated with the diagnosis of CRA. Treatment adopted a dual-pronged approach: 1. For the underlying infection, standard oral triple anti-tuberculosis therapy was administered with an adequate treatment duration. 2. Given the patient's young age, high risks of open surgery, and the lesion primarily involving visceral branch arteries, minimally invasive renal artery balloon angioplasty was chosen to address the stenosis in the functional right renal artery. This aimed to improve renal blood flow perfusion. The primary reason for not implanting a stent was the patient's young age. As the child grows and develops, the vessels will continue to enlarge and thicken. Implanting a fixed-size stent could potentially restrict future vascular growth, leading to relative stenosis and necessitating reintervention. Following balloon angioplasty alone, the anatomical structure of the vessel remains largely unchanged, preserving more options and operational flexibility for any future treatments that may be required. Postoperatively, a combination of antihypertensive medications and methotrexate was administered to reduce vascular inflammation. Regular follow-up over one year demonstrated well-controlled blood pressure with no complications. Long-term management and follow-up are critical: First, regular imaging follow-up (CTA at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months, then annually) to assess the patency of the treated vascular segments and to detect disease progression in other areas. Second, medication management with antiplatelet agents and statins to stabilize arterial plaques elsewhere in the body, reduce inflammation, and slow disease progression. Finally, lifestyle modifications are essential, including absolute smoking cessation, a healthy diet, and moderate exercise.

Conclusion

For children with refractory hypertension unresponsive to conventional antihypertensive therapy, clinicians should broaden their diagnostic approach to include rare vascular conditions such as coral reef aorta in the differential diagnosis. Whole-aortic CTA is crucial for definitive diagnosis, with characteristic intraluminal calcified plaques aiding differentiation from conditions like polyarteritis nodosa. Early recognition and targeted intervention are vital for improving patient outcomes and preventing severe complications.
