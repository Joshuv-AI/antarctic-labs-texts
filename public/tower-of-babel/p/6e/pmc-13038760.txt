Computational analysis of multi-omics data reveals CXCL10+ DC-Treg interaction drives immunosuppressive microenvironment in AFP-positive hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Zhi-Hui Luo, Wei-Ming Chen, Xin-Meng Yang, Jingwei Zhao, Ying-Lian Zhang, Qing-Han Huang, Ming-Yang Zhang, Na Wang, Fubing Wang, Jingjiao Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) is one of the most widely used clinical diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). However, its potential role in guiding treatment strategies remains limited, largely because the tumor microenvironment of AFP-positive HCC has not been well characterized. We integrated multiple types of public transcriptomic data to systematically delineate the features of AFP-positive HCC and their relevance to immunotherapy. Specifically, we used single-cell RNA-seq datasets, bulk RNA-seq data, and spatial transcriptomic data from several independent public cohorts. We found that STMN1+ and AFP+ malignant cell subsets were enriched in AFP-positive tumor tissues, while CYP3A4+ malignant cells were enriched in AFP-negative HCC. Regarding the immune microenvironment, we focused on two key immune cell types: regulatory CD4+ T cells (Tregs) and dendritic cells (DCs). We found that both Tregs and CXCL10+ DCs (DCs with high CXCL10 expression) were elevated in AFP-positive HCC. Moreover, these two cell types showed a highly significant positive correlation across multiple datasets. Spatial transcriptomic analysis revealed their spatial proximity, suggesting that the interaction between CXCL10+ DCs and Tregs shaped the immunosuppressive environment in AFP-positive HCC. Analysis of single-cell and spatial transcriptome data from patients receiving immunotherapy showed that the increased composition and spatial proximity of these two cell types were associated with non-response to immunotherapy. Our study, based on the computation and analysis of public data, revealed that the interaction between CXCL10+ DCs and Tregs may serve as a crucial factor contributing to the formation of the immunosuppressive microenvironment of AFP-positive HCC. This not only enhances researchers' understanding of the AFP-positive HCC microenvironment but also provides a potential immunotherapy target for AFP-positive HCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06167-4.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) makes up about 90% of primary liver cancers. Although early diagnosis and treatments such as liver transplantation, surgery, and immune checkpoint inhibitors have improved, the 5-year survival rate remains low at around 20% [1–3]. Key clinical factors influencing therapeutic efficacy in HCC include the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging system, patient age, and treatment modality, all of which are closely associated with disease progression and overall prognosis [4]. Within this context, serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), a classical clinical biomarker widely used in the diagnosis and surveillance of HCC, has emerged not only as a diagnostic aid but also as an independent prognostic indicator. Fluctuations in AFP levels reflect underlying tumor biology, the extent of disease progression, and responsiveness to treatment, independent of other clinical variables [5]. Understanding the role of AFP may help refine prognosis and guide personalized therapy in HCC patients.

AFP-positive (> = 20 ng/ml, AFP+) are significantly associated with aggressive HCC features, including larger tumor size, higher risk of postoperative recurrence, impaired hepatic functional reserve, and reduced tumor capsule integrity [6, 7]. However, approximately 30–40% of HCC patients exhibit AFP-negative status (< 20 ng/ml, AFP−), providing important insights for molecular subclassification of HCC [8]. Current studies have demonstrated that integrating EPCAM expression profiles with serum AFP levels enables classification of HCC into four molecular subtypes with distinct transcriptomic programs [9]. Notably, the AFP-high phenotype is markedly enriched in GPC3+ HCC, which is associated with poorer prognosis and more aggressive tumor progression [10]. These findings not only enhance our understanding of HCC heterogeneity but also provide a critical foundation for developing molecularly guided precision classification systems and personalized therapeutic approaches.

AFP not only directly modulates the malignant phenotype of HCC cells but also profoundly reshapes the tumor immune microenvironment. At the tumor cell level, AFP promotes proliferation and survival through activation of key signaling cascades, including caspase-3, cAMP/PKA, and PTEN/PI3K/AKT pathways [11, 12]. In terms of immune regulation, AFP induces the differentiation of CD4+ T lymphocytes toward regulatory Tregs, thereby fostering an immunosuppressive milieu [13, 14]. Notably, AFP+ HCC tumors exhibit marked enrichment of SPP1+ tumor-associated macrophages, which further potentiate the immune suppressive state [15]. However, current single-cell studies are constrained by limited sample sizes, potentially impeding accurate delineation of the immune landscape. Moreover, a systematic multi-lineage comparison between AFP+ and AFP- HCC remains lacking. These unresolved challenges underscore the urgent need for a more comprehensive and in-depth characterization of the tumor microenvironment.

To investigate the tumor ecosystem of AFP+ and AFP− HCC, we performed an integrated analysis combining single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq, n = 73) and bulk transcriptomic data (Fig. 1a). The comparison revealed clear differences in both malignant and immune compartments. AFP-positive HCC (AFP+ HCC) were enriched for proliferative and AFP-associated cancer cell populations, while AFP-negative HCC (AFP− HCC) contained more metabolically active subsets. In the immune landscape, AFP+ cases showed more Treg cells and impaired DC function, whereas effector T cells were more abundant in AFP− tumors. We also identified a CXCL10+ DC subset that increased with serum AFP levels and was spatially co-localized with Tregs, as confirmed by spatial transcriptomics. This DC–Treg interaction was associated with stronger CXCL10–CXCR3 signaling in AFP-positive HCC (AFP+ HCC) and was more pronounced in patients who did not respond to immunotherapy. Results

Cohort characterization of AFP-positive and AFP-negative HCC

To systematically delineate malignant and microenvironmental features of HCC in the context of serum AFP levels, we analyzed scRNA-seq data from 79 tumor specimens [16]. After applying stringent quality control filters (see Methods), samples with insufficient malignant cell content were excluded. This resulted in a final dataset of 73 high-quality samples, comprising 38 AFP+ and 35 AFP⁻ cases and totaling 557,035 cells for downstream analyses.

To evaluate the clinical relevance of AFP stratification, we first examined associations between AFP status and clinicopathological variables. Clinical metadata analysis revealed that AFP+ tumors were significantly associated with more advanced TNM staging (T: p = 0.029; M: p = 0.013), a higher prevalence of liver cirrhosis (p = 0.004), poorer histological differentiation (p = 0.002), and increased portal vein invasion (p = 0.013) (Fig. 1b). To confirm the robustness of these associations, we analyzed the independent TCGA-LIHC cohort (130 AFP+ vs. 147 AFP⁻) and observed consistent trends (Fig. S1a). Importantly, survival analyses in both datasets revealed a significantly more favorable prognosis in AFP⁻ patients (Fig. 1c), consistent with previous clinical observations [15]. Together, these results reinforce the prognostic and biological significance of AFP status and support the utility of this stratification for mechanistic interrogation of HCC pathogenesis.

We next performed unsupervised clustering to define cellular composition across the tumor microenvironment. Based on canonical lineage markers, all cells were assigned to one of 12 major cell types, including CD4+ T cells (IL7R, CD3E, CD3D), CD8+ T cells (CD8A, CD3E, CD3D), natural killer (NK) cells (NKG7, GNLY), B cells (CD79A, MS4A1), plasma cells (IGHG1, MZB1), macrophages (CD68, CD163), monocytes (CD14, FCN1), dendritic cells (CD1C, FCER1A), mesenchymal cells (COL1A1, COL1A2), mast cells (TPSAB1), endothelial cells (VWF), and malignant epithelial cells (KRT8, GPC3) (Fig. 1d, e).

While global cell-type frequencies—including tumor cells, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, and dendritic cells—did not differ significantly between AFP+ and AFP⁻ tumors (p > 0.05, Fig. S1b, S1c), we hypothesized that functionally relevant heterogeneity may exist at the subcluster level. Therefore, we next conducted finer-grained subclustering to resolve phenotypic variations within major cell populations and identify AFP-associated cellular programs that may contribute to differential clinical behavior.

STMN1+ and AFP+ malignant subpopulations predominate in AFP+ HCC

To investigate transcriptional heterogeneity within malignant epithelial cells stratified by AFP status, we extracted malignant cells and performed unsupervised clustering, which revealed 10 distinct malignant subpopulations (C0-C9; Fig. 2a). Each cluster exhibited unique transcriptomic signatures and was represented across both AFP+ and AFP⁻ tumors (Fig. 2b, c). However, comparative analysis of cellular composition using ratio of observed cell number to expected cell number (Ro/e) and proportion-based assessments revealed substantial shifts in cluster representation between subgroups. Specifically, Tumor_C1_CYP3A4 cells were enriched in AFP− HCCs, whereas Tumor_C3_STMN1 and Tumor_C6_AFP malignant cells were markedly expanded in AFP+ tumors (Fig. 2d, e). To validate these malignant phenotypes in an independent dataset, we constructed gene signatures from the top differentially expressed genes in the Tumor_C1_CYP3A4, Tumor_C3_STMN1, and Tumor_C6_AFP clusters (Supplementary Table 1), and computed enrichment scores of AFP+ and AFP− samples in bulk RNA-seq profiles from the TCGA-LIHC cohort based on these gene signatures. Consistent with the single-cell findings, gene sets corresponding to Tumor_C3_STMN1 and Tumor_C6_AFP were preferentially enriched in AFP+ tumors, whereas the Tumor_C1_CYP3A4 signature was predominantly expressed in AFP⁻ tumors (Fig. 2f). Prognostically, high expression of Tumor_C3_STMN1 and Tumor_C6_AFP signatures was associated with significantly poorer overall survival, while the Tumor_C1_CYP3A4 signature predicted a more favorable clinical outcome (Fig. 2g). These results suggest that the expansion of Tumor_C3_STMN1 and Tumor_C6_AFP tumor subpopulations may underpin the adverse prognosis observed in AFP+ HCC.

To gain insight into the functional roles of these malignant subsets, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) pathway enrichment analysis on their respective gene signatures (Supplementary Table 2). Genes within the Tumor_C1_CYP3A4 cluster were enriched for metabolic processes, including xenobiotic, acylglycerol, and fatty acid metabolism. In contrast, the Tumor_C3_STMN1 cluster was characterized by enrichment of cell cycle–related programs, such as mitotic division, chromosomal segregation, and organelle fission. Meanwhile, the Tumor_C6_AFP cluster exhibited enrichment in regulatory pathways including negative regulation of hydrolase and peptidase activity, as well as steroid metabolism (Fig. 2h). To further support these findings, we analyzed differential gene expression and pathway activity between AFP+ and AFP⁻ tumors in the TCGA cohort. AFP+ tumors exhibited significant enrichment in proliferation-associated pathways, while AFP⁻ tumors were predominantly enriched for metabolic pathways (Fig. 2i), reinforcing a functional dichotomy between these groups. Consistently, scMetabolism-based activity scoring of 11 categories encompassing all KEGG metabolic pathways revealed that Tumor_C1_CYP3A4 exhibited broadly higher metabolic activity than Tumor_C3_ STMN1/Tumor_C6_AFP, including in key pathways such as oxidative phosphorylation(OXPHOS), glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and fatty acid degradation (Fig. S2c–e, Supplementary Table 3) [17].

To explore the transcriptional regulatory mechanisms governing malignant heterogeneity, we performed SCENIC analysis on malignant cell subsets. Notably, within the Tumor_C6_AFP subpopulation, transcriptional regulators GTF2IRD1, HOXB8, and GRHL2 displayed significantly increased activity in AFP+ HCCs (p < 0.01, Wilcoxon test), whereas PDLIM5, ZNF25, and ZNF263 were preferentially active in AFP⁻ tumors (Fig. S2a, S2b). These data highlight divergent regulatory landscapes underpinning malignant subtypes across AFP-defined groups.

Collectively, these findings delineate a distinct enrichment of proliferative STMN1+ and AFP+ malignant cell subpopulations in AFP+ HCC. Their transcriptional and regulatory programs not only reflect key oncogenic processes but also likely contribute to the poor prognosis associated with AFP+ disease.

Treg-mediated immunosuppressive environment in AFP-positive HCC

To comprehensively delineate the T cell landscape in HCC, we identified 14 distinct T cell subsets across AFP+ and AFP− tumors based on canonical markers (Fig. 3a-c; Supplementary Table 4). Comparative analyses between AFP+ and AFP− tumors revealed significant compositional differences in three subsets. Specifically, AFP+ tumors exhibited depletion of cytotoxic CD8T_C1_GZMK cells and enrichment of both Tregs and CD8T_C5_IFIT3 cells (Fig. 3b and 3 d, 3e). Prognostic evaluation in the TCGA-LIHC cohort demonstrated that elevated CD8T_C1_GZMK signatures correlated with improved survival, whereas high Treg signatures were predictive of poor outcomes (Fig. 3g), highlighting their functional relevance in shaping tumor immunity. Functional annotation using 19 curated gene signatures (Supplementary Table 5) from Chu et al. [18] revealed marked functional heterogeneity within both CD8+ and CD4+ T cell compartments. Among CD8+ subsets, MAIT, CD8T_C5_IFIT3, and CD8T_C3_APOA2 cells exhibited enriched naive-like profiles, whereas CD8T_C1_GZMK cells displayed elevated cytotoxicity and CD8T_C2_PDCD1 cells manifested features of exhaustion (Fig. 3g; Fig. S3a). Next, we further compared the functional differences, including pathway enrichment, cell trajection and Transcription factors regulation, in CD8+ T cells between AFP+ and AFP− HCCs. GO enrichment revealed down regulation of immune effector pathways—including lymphocyte-mediated cytotoxicity and cell killing—in AFP+ tumors (Fig. S3b; Supplementary Table 6–7). This was accompanied by reduced cytotoxicity scores and elevated exhaustion scores relative to AFP− tumors (Fig. S3c). Pseudotime reconstruction traced differentiation from MAIT to CD8T_C2_PDCD1 (Fig. S3d-f), revealing persistently impaired cytotoxicity across the trajectory in AFP+ tumors (Fig. S3g-h). These functional changes were paralleled by dysregulation of exhaustion-associated transcription factors, including TOX, PRDM1, and EOMES and their targets genes (Fig. S3i, S3j). suggesting stable suppression of effector functions throughout CD8+ T cell differentiation.

The widespread dysfunction of CD8+ T cells in AFP+ tumors prompted investigation of the immunosuppressive role of Tregs [19]. Notably, immune checkpoint receptors CTLA4 and TIGIT—both highly expressed in Tregs—may mediate suppression of cytotoxic T cells and antigen-presenting cells (Fig. 3h). CTLA4 inhibits co-stimulation by competing with CD28 for CD80/86 binding, while TIGIT engages CD155 to dampen effector T cell activation [20, 21]. Treg abundance negatively correlated with the proportion of CD8T_C1_GZMK cells (Fig. 3i), implicating Treg-mediated suppression in the functional exhaustion of cytotoxic T cells. However, transcriptomic comparisons revealed similar gene expression profiles in Tregs from AFP+ and AFP− tumors (Fig. 3j), indicating that the immunosuppressive phenotype of AFP+ HCC is primarily driven by increased Treg infiltration rather than altered transcriptional programs.

Together, these results define a Treg-dominated immunosuppressive microenvironment in AFP+ HCC, characterized by depletion of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and sustained dysfunction across differentiation trajectories.

Attenuated antigen presentation capacity of CXCL10+ and CD1C+ dendritic cells in AFP+ HCC

To investigate DC heterogeneity and functional alterations in AFP+ HCC, we performed unsupervised clustering of DCs, identifying six transcriptionally distinct subpopulations: CD1C+ DCs, STMN1+ DCs, CLEC9A+ DCs, LAMP3+ DCs, CXCL10+ DCs, and APOC3+ DCs (Fig. 4a; Supplementary Table 8). Among them, CD1C+, CXCL10+, and APOC3+ subsets displayed high expression of canonical cDC2 markers, including CD1C, FCER1A, and CLEC10A (Fig. 4b). Comparative analysis of cell composition and Ro/e revealed a marked enrichment of DC_C1_CD1C and DC_C5_CXCL10 subsets in AFP+ patients compared to their AFP⁻ counterparts (Fig. 4c–e). To further delineate the transcriptional features associated with these shifts, we examined differentially expressed genes within the DC populations stratified by AFP status. In the CD1C+ subset, AFP+ tumors exhibited 90 upregulated and 121 downregulated genes, whereas in the CXCL10+ subset, 242 genes were upregulated and 117 were downregulated (Fig. 4f; Supplementary Table 9). These findings suggest distinct immunological states of DC subsets associated with AFP expression levels in HCC. Pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes further highlighted significant suppression of the “Antigen processing and presentation” pathway in both CD1C+ and CXCL10+ subsets in AFP+ tumors (Fig. 4g; Supplementary Table 10). This diminished functional capacity was largely attributed to reduced expression of major MHC molecules and chaperones, including HLA-DQA1, HLA-DRB1, HLA-B, and HSPA1A (Fig. 4h), suggesting a loss of immunostimulatory potential.

Notably, these findings reveal a functional dissociation between DC abundance and immunogenic activity in AFP+ HCC. Although CD1C+ and CXCL10+ DCs are numerically enriched, their capacity to process and present antigens appears markedly diminished.

CXCL10+ DCs promote Treg recruitment to establish an immunosuppressive niche in AFP+ HCC

To elucidate the cellular interactions contributing to the immunosuppressive milieu in AFP+ HCC, we performed correlation analyses of cell-type abundances in single-cell data. Populations elevated in AFP+ tumors exhibited strong positive correlations, clustering into distinct modules. Notably, GZMK+ CD8+ T cells, STMN1+ malignant cells, CD4+ Treg cells, and CXCL10+ DCs co-segregated within module 1 (Fig. 5a). Another AFP+-enriched cluster, module 4, comprised AFP+ malignant cells and IFIT3+ CD8+ T cells. Among these, only CXCL10+ DCs and Tregs (from module 1) were positively associated with the abundance of both two representative AFP+ tumor clusters (Tumor_C6_AFP and Tumor_C3_STMN1) and showed strong correlations with serum AFP levels (Fig. 5b). Given their positive correlation at the discovery cohort, we next validated the association between Tregs and CXCL10+ DCs in multiple independent datasets. Using gene signatures for these two cell populations, we computed enrichment scores across 25 bulk RNA-seq datasets and observed strong positive correlations (Fig. 5c). To further substantiate these findings at single-cell resolution, we analyzed the GSE151530 scRNA-seq dataset, comprising 33 HCC tumor samples. After isolating T cells and Myeloid Cells from each sample, we again observed a robust correlation between the enrichment scores of Tregs and CXCL10+ DCs (r = 0.77, p < 2.2 × 10⁻1⁶; Fig. 5d-f), reinforcing the notion of coordinated accumulation of these two populations in AFP+ tumors, suggesting potential crosstalk between these two immune populations. Additionally, we explored the relationship between the elevation of Tregs and CXCL10+ DCs and HCC molecular subtypes. Specifically, we performed correlation analyses between the gene set scores of Tregs and CXCL10+ DCs and three molecular subtypes identified by Hoshida et al., namely S1 (WNT/β-catenin and TGF-β–activated tumors), S2 (MYC/AKT-driven tumors), and S3 (hepatocyte-like differentiated tumors) [23]. The results demonstrated that Tregs and CXCL10+ DCs were positively correlated with the S1 subtype (P-values = 0.078 and 0.011, respectively), and also showed a positive correlation with the S2 subtype, albeit without statistical significance (Fig. S4). In contrast, both cell populations exhibited a significantly negative correlation with the S3 subtype (Fig. S4). To probe their spatial organization within the tumor microenvironment, we employed spatial transcriptomic analysis. In a representative HCC sample (HCC-4; from Wu et al.), moderate AFP expression was observed in malignant cell regions (Fig. 5gi-gii). Spatial enrichment scoring revealed that CXCL10+ DCs frequently localized to regions in close proximity to Treg-enriched areas (Fig. 5giii). Using CellTrek-based spatial deconvolution, we confirmed that among all DC subsets, CXCL10+ DCs were the nearest neighbors to Tregs (Figs. 5giv-gv), providing further evidence of physical interaction within the tumor niche. We next investigated the molecular basis of this association via ligand–receptor interaction analysis. A pronounced enrichment of CXCL chemokine signaling between CXCL10+ DCs and Tregs was observed, with AFP+ patients showing stronger interactions than AFP⁻ patients (Fig. S5a-b). In particular, CXCL10+ DCs expressed CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11, which were predicted to interact with CXCR3 on Tregs, supporting a model in which CXCL10+ DCs actively recruit Tregs through the CXCL9/10/11–CXCR3 signaling axis.

Together, these findings suggest that CXCL10+ DCs contribute to the immunosuppressive microenvironment in AFP+ HCC by facilitating the recruitment and localization of Tregs within the tumor niche.

Enhanced CXCL10+ DC–Treg interactions are associated with resistance to immunotherapy in AFP+ HCC

We next investigated the influence of immunotherapy on key AFP+-associated immune and malignant cell populations. Analysis of bulk RNA-seq data from 41 HCC patients receiving immune checkpoint blockade revealed that elevated expression of Tumor_C3_STMN1 and Tumor_C6_AFP malignant cell signatures, as well as Treg signatures, were significantly associated with poorer survival outcomes (Fig. 6a). To further examine the role of CXCL10+ DCs and Tregs in modulating treatment response, we leveraged both single-cell and spatial transcriptomic datasets from pre- and post-treatment HCC samples. Within a single-cell cohort (GSE235863), we extracted CXCL10+ DCs and Tregs before and after therapy (Figs. 6b, c), observing a strong positive correlation between their abundance scores (Fig. 6d), consistent with our earlier findings. Notably, in the non-responder group, both CXCL10+ DCs and Tregs significantly increased following treatment, whereas in responders, CXCL10+ DCs were markedly reduced after therapy (Figs. 6e and S6a). These data implicate both cell populations in resistance to PD-1 blockade and suggest that their depletion may be integral to therapeutic efficacy. To assess their spatial interactions, we next analyzed spatial transcriptomic datasets using CellTrek-based deconvolution and spatial proximity analysis. In the responder group, Tregs and CXCL10+ DCs were significantly farther apart than in the non-responder group (Figs. 6f-h, S6b-c), indicating that reducing the physical connection between these two cell types may enhance the effectiveness of immunotherapy.

Together, these analyses indicate that elevated abundance and spatial co-localization of CXCL10+ DCs and Tregs are associated with poor immunotherapy response in AFP+ HCC, suggesting that their interaction may foster a treatment-resistant, immunosuppressive microenvironment.

Discussion

AFP remains a pivotal biomarker in the clinical landscape of HCC, with elevated serum concentrations exhibiting a strong association with tumorigenesis [24]. Despite its widespread use in diagnosis and prognostic evaluation, the translational potential of AFP as a therapeutic indicator has been minimally explored. This limitation largely stems from an incomplete understanding of the cellular and molecular architecture of AFP+ HCC tissues. However, recent advances in single-cell and spatial transcriptomic technologies have transformed our capacity to interrogate the tumor microenvironment at unprecedented resolution [16, 25], offering new avenues to delineate the distinct molecular and immunological landscape of AFP+ HCC.

Integrated single-cell and bulk RNA-sequencing analyses revealed that AFP+ HCCs are defined by distinct malignant subpopulations marked by AFP and STMN1 expression, whereas AFP⁻ tumors are preferentially enriched for malignant cells expressing CYP3A4. This bifurcation in malignant cell states reflects a fundamental divergence in tumor biology: Tumor_C3_STMN1 cells in AFP+ HCCs are enriched for transcriptional programs associated with cell cycle progression and proliferative activity, while Tumor_C1_CYP3A4 cells, dominant in AFP⁻ tumors, display pronounced metabolic signatures, including xenobiotic metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation. Previous studies have demonstrated a positive association between AFP expression and more aggressive HCC phenotypes, characterized by poor differentiation and accelerated proliferation [26]. Notably, STMN1 is upregulated in highly invasive HCC and has emerged as a robust marker for tracing tumor differentiation trajectories [27, 28], further substantiating the findings of the present study. Moreover, recent studies have demonstrated that metabolic-type HCC subtypes exhibit high metabolic activity, low AFP expression levels, and are associated with favorable prognosis [29, 30]. In our study, we observed that the Tumor_C1_CYP3A4 malignant cell subpopulation is predominantly enriched within AFP− HCC, exhibiting a distinctive metabolic profile likely driven by CYP3A4-mediated enhancement of oxidative metabolism in HCC. In addition to these proliferative and metabolic signatures, we identified a unique malignant subpopulation in AFP+ tumors, termed Tumor_C6_AFP, which is enriched for genes involved in the negative regulation of peptidase activity. This population appears to be selectively governed by a distinct transcriptional network involving GTF2IRD1, HOXB8, and GRHL2—transcription factors not previously linked to AFP-associated oncogenesis—warranting further mechanistic exploration. Together, these findings delineate molecularly and functionally distinct oncogenic states in AFP+ and AFP⁻ HCCs, suggesting that divergent lineage commitments and metabolic reprogramming underlie their disparate clinical trajectories.

Treg-mediated immunosuppression in AFP+ HCC

AFP-mediated immunosuppression is thought to occur primarily through the skewing of CD4+ T cells toward a regulatory T cell (Treg) phenotype, thereby dampening anti-tumor immune responses [13]. In the present study, we observed a marked expansion of CD4+ Tregs in AFP+ tumors, accompanied by a concomitant reduction in CD8_C1_GZMK effector T cells. These findings are consistent with previous reports identifying Treg accumulation as a key driver of immune evasion in AFP+ HCC [15]. This immunosuppressive axis may be cytokine-mediated, as Tregs have been shown to secrete IL-10 and IL-35—immunosuppressive cytokines that contribute to CD8+ T cell exhaustion and functional suppression [31]. An alternative mechanism may involve the tumor-intrinsic upregulation of PRKAA2, which is frequently elevated in aggressive HCCs and has been implicated in promoting both CD8+ T cell exhaustion and the expansion of CD4+ Tregs [32]. Together, these findings underscore the pivotal role of Treg-mediated immunosuppression in the progression of AFP+ HCC and highlight potential molecular pathways underlying this phenotype.

CXCL10+ DC-treg crosstalk in immune evasion

Although previous studies have emphasized impaired DC function as one of the causes of weakened immune responses in AFP+ HCC patients [33], our analysis revealed an unexpected expansion of CXCL10+ DCs in AFP+ HCC despite their diminished antigen-presenting capacity. Prior research demonstrating AFP's ability to suppress DC secretion of cytokines like IL-12 may partially explain this finding, with the underlying mechanism involving inhibitory effects of AFP's D2 and D3 domains on DC function [13, 34].Our current study further elucidates that Treg-mediated immunosuppression in AFP+ HCC is orchestrated through CXCL9/10/11-CXCR3 ligand-receptor interactions between CXCL10+ DCs and Treg cells. This similar interaction pattern has also been reported in colon carcinoma, characterized by a significant increase in activated CXCR3+ Treg cells within tumors that preferentially interact with CXCL9-producing DCs (cDC1s, marked by CLEC9A, XCR1) [35]. The underlying mechanism involves CXCR3 expression in Treg cells impairing DCs' capacity for tumor antigen cross-presentation, consequently attenuating both primary activation and reactivation of CD8+ T cells in the tumor microenvironment, ultimately promoting tumor progression [35]. Distinct from these previous findings, our study is the first to specifically identify the enrichment of CXCL10+ DC (cDC2s, marked by CD1C, FCER1A) subsets and their interaction with Tregs in AFP+ HCC. Our data suggests that in AFP+ HCC, CXCL10+ DCs may facilitate immune evasion through a dual mechanism: intrinsic impairment of antigen presentation and active recruitment of immunosuppressive Treg cells. The CXCL10+ DC-Treg axis may not be restricted to AFP+ HCC; we found that these two cell components are positively correlated with the WNT/β-catenin and TGF-β–activated HCC subtype. In the study by Hoshida et al., patients with this HCC subtype had an average serum AFP level of 50 ng/ml, which also falls into the category of AFP+ patients [23]. These observations indicate that future exploration of the mechanisms underlying the CXCL10+ DC-Treg axis will also help deepen our understanding of HCC molecular classification.

Therapeutic implications of CXCL10+ DC-Treg axis

Finally, we extended our findings to the clinical setting by evaluating the impact of CXCL10+ DCs and Tregs on anti-PD-1 immunotherapeutic outcomes. In patients who exhibited poor responses to immune checkpoint blockade, both CXCL10+ DCs and Tregs showed a trend toward increased abundance post-treatment, accompanied by reduced spatial distance between the two populations. This finding suggests that enhanced interactions between CXCL10+ DCs and Tregs may contribute to immunotherapy resistance in HCC. Notably, some studies have demonstrated the importance of DCs in the application of immunotherapy [36]. These evidences support a model in which CXCL10+ DCs promote immunotherapeutic resistance through functional crosstalk with Tregs. Collectively, our results highlight the potential of targeting CXCL10+ DCs as a therapeutic strategy to enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy in AFP+ HCC.

Nevertheless, several limitations in this study should be acknowledged. The differential abundance of Treg and CXCL10+ DC cells between AFP+ and AFP− HCC was mainly identified in the Asian HCC cohort, and whether similar differences exist in other ethnicities warrants further investigation. Likewise, whether these changes are independent of age, gender, and other clinical parameters remains to be validated using larger-scale single-cell datasets. Although we demonstrated the potential interaction between Treg and CXCL10+ DC cells in AFP+ HCC using multiple single-cell datasets, bulk RNA-seq datasets, and spatial transcriptomic data, this conclusion still lacks direct experimental validation at the cellular level. Future studies using fresh tumor-derived Tregs and CXCL10+ DCs will be essential to mechanistically validate the CXCL10+ DC–Treg axis and assess its potential as an immunotherapeutic target. In particular, it is necessary to clarify the functional causal relationship between CXCL10+ DCs and Treg cells through experiments, rather than limiting the findings to correlational results. Furthermore, the underlying mechanism by which AFP induces the accumulation of Treg and CXCL10+ DC cells in AFP+ HCC remains to be fully elucidated. Additionally, due to the limited availability of single-cell and spatial transcriptomic data for HCC under immunotherapy, the conclusion that the crosstalk between Treg and CXCL10+ DC cells contributes to immunotherapy non-response requires further validation in larger cohorts.

Clinical implications

This study demonstrates that there are significant differences in the immune microenvironment between AFP+ HCC and AFP⁻ HCC. In particular, AFP+ HCC exhibits a distinct increase in the cellular components of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and CXCL10+ dendritic cells (DCs). Meanwhile, this study also reveals that Tregs and CXCL10+ DCs have spatial interactions, and such crosstalk can further reduce the efficacy of immunotherapy. Therefore, for patients with AFP+ HCC, inhibiting the interaction between CXCL10+ DCs and Tregs may serve as an effective therapeutic strategy. We found that this interaction may be mediated by the CXCL10/CXCR3 signaling pathway. It has been reported that inhibition of CXCR3 can slow tumor growth [35]. Previous studies have shown that CXCL10 exerts a chemotactic effect on Tregs [37]; additionally, other studies have indicated that upregulation of CXCL10/CXCR3 signaling can directly induce the recruitment of Treg cells, thereby promoting the recurrence of hepatocellular carcinoma after transplantation [38]. Furthermore, the present study clarifies the core identity of CXCL10+ DCs in the immunosuppressive microenvironment, suggesting that reducing the CXCL10/CXCR3 signaling pathway dominated by CXCL10+ DCs may be a key factor in improving the efficiency of immunotherapy for AFP+ HCC.

Conclusion

In this study, we delineate the immune landscape of AFP+ HCC and uncover a paradoxical enrichment of CXCL10+ DCs despite their functional suppression. Notably, the enrichment of CXCL10+ DCs is accompanied by an increased infiltration of Tregs, collectively shaping a profoundly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. This immunological crosstalk—marked by the spatial co-occurrence and interaction between CXCL10+ DCs and Tregs—may underlie the diminished responsiveness of AFP+ HCC to PD-1 blockade. These findings offer mechanistic insights into immune evasion in AFP+ HCC tumors and highlight potential therapeutic vulnerabilities in this molecular subtype.

Materials and Methods

Data collection

All datasets analyzed in this study were retrieved from publicly available repositories, and no new experimental data were generated in the present work. The single-cell transcriptomic data and corresponding clinical information used in this study were sourced from the Genome Sequence Archive (Accession: HRA001748) [16]. The original dataset includes clinical samples from patients with various subtypes of primary liver cancer, including HCC. During quality control screening of 79 HCC samples, those with fewer than 100 tumor cells and those lacking clinical alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) data were excluded. Ultimately, 73 HCC samples (38 AFP+ and 35 AFP−) met the criteria for analysis, ensuring the reliability of the data. The gene expression matrix and clinical information from the TCGA-LIHC dataset were downloaded from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Validation of the HCC scRNA-seq dataset was performed using data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (Accession: GSE151530), comprising 46 samples in total, of which 32 tumor samples were selected for analysis. An additional HCC scRNA-seq dataset related to immunotherapy (Accession: GSE235863) was obtained from the study by Guo et al. [39]. This dataset comprises 30 samples of CD45+ immune cells isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) prior to sequencing, collected from nine patients (four responders and five non-responders) and accompanied by annotations indicating pre- and post-immunotherapy status, tissue source, and treatment response classification. Bulk RNA-seq datasets were collected from HCCDBv2 [40], including GSE89377, GSE76427, GSE121248, GSE112790, GSE134568, GSE9843, GSE25097, GSE148355, OEP000321, GSE43619, ICGC-LIRI-JP, GSE109211, GSE64041, GSE22058, GSE87630, GSE36376, GSE124751, GSE14520, GSE63898, GSE54236, GSE164760, GSE19977, GSE46444, and GSE10143, yielding a total of 3,468 HCC tumor samples for validation analyses. Validation of HCC spatial transcriptomic data was conducted using the Genome Sequence Archive dataset HRA000437. Immune therapy microarray data for liver cancer were obtained from the GEO database, with accession number GSE202069. Spatial transcriptomic data of HCC tumor sections from patients receiving anti-PD-1 treatment were obtained from the study by Liu et al. [41] (dataset available at: https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/skrx2fz79n/1).

scRNA-seq data preprocessing

We performed rigorous filtering of the raw single-cell gene expression matrix using the Seurat R package (v.4.1.3) [42], based on the following criteria to remove low-quality cells: cells with a total UMI count below 500 or above 30,000 were discarded; genes detected in fewer than 500 or more than 6,000 cells were excluded; cells with a mitochondrial gene percentage greater than 25% were removed. Additionally, to further reduce background noise, mitochondrial and ribosomal genes were excluded from the analysis. Ultimately, a total of 557,035 cells from 73 HCC samples were retained for downstream analysis.

Cell clustering and annotation

To identify major cell types, we performed clustering and annotation of single-cell data using the scanpy Python package (v1.10.3) [43]. The raw expression matrix was normalized and scaled using the normalize_total, log1p, and scale functions. Subsequently, 2,000 highly variable genes were selected via the highly_variable_genes function and subjected to principal component analysis (PCA). To mitigate batch effects, the Harmony algorithm [44] was applied using the harmony_integrate function prior to downstream clustering. Dimensionality reduction and clustering were conducted using the first 50 principal components (PCs) through the neighbors, louvain (resolution = 1), and umap functions, yielding 12 distinct cellular subtypes. These subtypes were annotated based on canonical single-cell markers: CD4+ T cells (IL7R, CD3E, CD3D), CD8+ T cells (CD8A, CD3E, CD3D), NK cells (NKG7, GNLY), B cells (CD79A, MS4A1), Plasma cells (IGHG1, MZB1), Macrophages (CD68, CD163), Monocytes (CD14, FCN1), Dendritic cells (CD1C, FCER1A), Mesenchymal cells (COL1A1, COL1A2), Mast cells (TPSAB1), Endothelial cells (VWF), and Tumor cells (KRT8, GPC3). During cell type annotation, no discrete cell clusters were excluded, and all cells were included in the 12 defined cell populations. The annotated cell populations cover the major cell types reported in previous single‑cell studies of HCC [45, 46], including malignant tumor cells, lymphocytes (CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, NK cells, B cells, and plasma cells), myeloid cells (macrophages, monocytes, dendritic cells, and mast cells), and stromal cells (mesenchymal cells and endothelial cells).

Next, we performed a second round of clustering to refine sulations within tumor cells, DCs and T cells. The analysis was conducted using Seurat (v4.1.3) following data conversion from scanpy objects to Seurat objects through the Anndata Python package (v0.10.9). For tumor cell clustering, 2,000 highly variable genes were selected and analyzed based on the first 30 PCs. We systematically tested resolution values ranging from 0 to 1 with a step size of 0.1. Ultimately, we selected a resolution of 0.2, which not only ensures clear separation between distinct clusters but also minimizes redundancy among clusters. For DC analysis, 1,000 highly variable genes were selected and analyzed using the first 10 PCs with a clustering resolution of 0.2. In T cell analysis, dimensionality reduction and clustering were similarly performed using 2,000 highly variable genes but restricted to the first 20 PCs, employing a resolution parameter of 0.6.

We named the tumor cell subsets by combining the expression levels of marker genes and the results of GO enrichment analysis for the top highly expressed genes in each subset. CYP3A4 is indicative of metabolic function [47], SOX4 is associated with tumor progression-related functions including epithelial–mesenchymal transition, migration, and angiogenesis [48], STMN1 is related to cell cycle and cell proliferation [49], MLXIPL represents glycolysis/lipogenesis reprogramming [50], HLA-DRA corresponds to aberrant MHC II expression [51], and AFP represents the typical AFP high tumor state. These genes were used to name the clusters: Tumor_C1_CYP3A4, Tumor_C2_SOX4, Tumor_C3_STMN1, Tumor_C4_MLXIPL, Tumor_C5_HLA-DRA, and Tumor_C6_AFP. FABP1, PCSK1N, PAGE1, and CD164 are highly expressed genes in the remaining subsets (among the top marker genes ranked by log2FC), and were therefore used to name Tumor_C0_FABP1, Tumor_C7_PCSK1N, Tumor_C8_PAGE1, and Tumor_C9_CD164. The log2FC values for all genes can be found in Supplementary Table 1.

To assess and label the cellular states of T cell subpopulations, we used the AddModuleScore function to calculate gene signature scores based on a curated gene set from Chu et al. [18] (Supplementary Table 5) associated with T cell functional states in CD4+ and CD8+ T cell clusters. Based on these T cell functional gene sets, combined with the highly expressed genes (LogFC > 0.25, p < 0.05) in each cell subset identified in this study, we selected the marker genes presented in Dotplot. For subset of CD8+ T cells, GZMK marks an effector-like cytotoxic state (CD8T_C1_GZMK), whereas PDCD1 denotes an exhausted phenotype (CD8T_C2_PDCD1) [52]. APOA2, the top differentially expressed lipid-metabolism–related gene, defines a metabolically distinct subset (CD8T_C3_APOA2), consistent with previous reports that tissue-resident and tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells depend on elevated fatty acid uptake and oxidation [53, 54]. For the CD8T_C4 subset, we selected CST3 as the representative marker (CD8T_C4_CST3), given its detectable expression in T cells and documented immune-regulatory functions, rendering it a more suitable and biologically coherent label for this subset [55, 56]. IFIT3 defines an interferon-responsive, ISG-high state (CD8T_C5_IFIT3), consistent with observations from pan-cancer T cell atlas study. Mucosal-Associated Invariant T (MAIT) cells, also assigned to the CD8+ lineage, were identified by their semi-invariant TCR and moderate cytotoxic potential.

For CD4+ T cells, GPR183 is enriched in naïve-like or early memory CD4+ T cells (CD4T_C1_GPR183). The CD4T_C2_PLCG2 cluster was annotated based on the original study [16], as PLCG2 functions as a key mediator of TCR signaling and CD4+ T cell activation [57, 58]. CXCL13 serves as a canonical marker for Tfh-like and exhausted CD4+ T cells (CD4T_C3_CXCL13) [18]. Other clusters, including T stress (marked by a high stress-response signature), Treg (enriched for suppressive molecules and anti-apoptotic signatures), NKT (displaying innate-like lymphocyte features), and Cycling_T_cells (characterized by high glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation), were annotated using conventional functional nomenclature widely adopted in the field.

Validation cohort scRNA-seq data analysis

To validate the dependency between DC_C5_CXCL10 and Treg cells and their association with immunotherapy, we analyzed two scRNA-seq datasets: GSE151530 [59] and GSE235863 [39]. For GSE151530, which contains 46 samples, we selected 32 tumor samples from patients with HCC. As this dataset lacked cell type annotation, raw read count matrices were merged using Seurat (v4.1.3), followed by normalization, identification of 3,000 highly variable genes, dimensionality reduction using the first 20 principal components (PCs), and clustering at a resolution of 0.4. Cell types were annotated according to the procedure described in the section “Cell clustering and annotation”. Myeloid cells and T cells were extracted and subjected to a second round of unsupervised clustering and visualization, using 1,000 highly variable genes and 10 PCs for myeloid cells, and 2,000 highly variable genes and 20 PCs for T cells. The GSE235863 dataset comprises CD45+ immune cells, isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) prior to sequencing, from 30 samples collected from nine HBV+ HCC patients receiving anti-PD-1 plus lenvatinib combination therapy (four responders and five non-responders). It includes both pre- and post-treatment samples from blood and tumor tissue, with corresponding treatment response annotations. For this dataset, 2,000 highly variable genes and the first 20 PCs were used for analysis, and the original cell type annotations provided by the study authors were applied for visualization. Myeloid cells and T cells were extracted and visualized using the original UMAP coordinates reported in the source study. Because both datasets contained relatively limited cell numbers, detailed subpopulation annotation was challenging. Therefore, the AddModuleScore function was used to calculate signature scores for marker genes of DC_C5_CXCL10 and Treg populations within myeloid cells and T cells, respectively, to estimate their relative abundance.

Identification of differentially expressed genes

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for major cell populations and their subclusters were identified using the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat (parameter: min.pct = 0.25, thresh.use = 0.25). Additionally, DEGs were calculated based on AFP state grouping using the FindMarker function (parameters: min.pct = 0.1, logfc.threshold = 0, only.pos = FALSE).

Bulk RNA-seq and microarray data analysis

To characterize the features of different cellular subpopulations from scRNA data as applied to bulk RNA-seq and microarray data, we used the top 50 differentially expressed genes detected by the FindAllMarker function to compute cell-feature gene set scores for each sample using the GSVA method (v.1.48.3) [61]. For the TCGA-LIHC dataset, all tumor samples were divided into two groups based on patients’ blood AFP levels (cutoff: 20), and differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package (v.1.44.0) [62].

Transcription factor activity analysis

The transcription factor regulatory activity of tumor cells and T cell subpopulations was assessed using SCENIC (v0.12.1) [63]. SCENIC constructs gene co-expression networks based on single-cell RNA sequencing data and integrates motif enrichment analysis to identify target gene sets with significant regulatory potential. Subsequently, the AUCell algorithm was used to quantify the regulatory activity of transcription factors in individual cells, thereby revealing differences in functional states and regulatory patterns between tumor cells and T cell subpopulations.

Cell trajectory analysis of scRNA-seq data using Monocle2

To further investigate the relationship between the differentiation states of different T cell subtypes, we employed the Monocle2 algorithm (v.2.22.0) [64]. First, we processed the single-cell counts expression matrix and created a Monocle2 object. Then, we selected the top 2000 highly variable genes as defining genes for cell ordering using the FindVariableFeatures function from the Seurat R package. Dimensionality reduction and visualization were performed using Monocle2's reduceDimension function (method = 'DDRTree') and plot_cell_trajectory function.

Survival analysis

Survival analysis was performed using the R package survival (v.3.5.5), and Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated with the R package survminer (v.0.4.9). For single-cell samples, patients were grouped based on their corresponding clinical AFP state. For TCGA-LIHC samples, patients were classified into two groups using a serum AFP threshold of 20 (AFP+: ≥ 20, AFP−: < 20). To assess the relationship between the identified cell populations/subpopulations and the survival of HCC patients, the GSVA method was applied to calculate cell population signatures based on the top 50 DEGs. Patients were then stratified into high and low groups based on either the median or quartile expression levels.

Spatial transcriptomics data analysis

The ST data from immunotherapy-treated patients were processed using the Seurat (v.4.1.3) R package to generate a gene-by-spot matrix. Spots with fewer than 200 genes were excluded, and genes were retained if they had a read count greater than 10 or were expressed in more than three spots. Normalization was performed using the LogVMR function. Dimensionality reduction and clustering were conducted on the first 30 principal components with a resolution of 1.1. Feature scores for different cellular subpopulations were computed by selecting the top 50 DEGs for the corresponding subpopulations from scRNA data, using the AddModuleScore function with default parameters, and visualized via the SpatialPlot function.

CellTrek deconvolution analysis

ScRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics data were co-embedded using the traint function from the CellTrek R package (v.0.0.94) [65] with default settings. Spatial coordinates were then inferred by applying the celltrek function to the shared embedding (nPCs = 30, top_spot = 2, spot_n = 10; all other parameters at default), which trains a multivariate random forest on ST features and transfers spot-derived positions to individual cells via a sparse mutual nearest neighbor graph. To quantify spatial relationships between regulatory T cell and dendritic cell subpopulations, we employed the run_kdist function (ref_type = "all", k = 10, keep_nn = FALSE) to compute inter-cluster k-distance metrics.

Pathway enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of DEGs from both single-cell subpopulations and bulk-seq was performed using the 'clusterProfiler' R package [66]. The enrichGO function was employed to identify enriched GO terms for the gene sets, where pathways with p.adjust < 0.05 represent the functional pathways enriched in different cell subpopulations/samples.

Cell–cell communication analysis using CellChat

To investigate cell–cell interactions between T cell subpopulations and HCC malignant cells, we employed the R package CellChat (v.1.6.1) [67]. Specifically, we explored ligand-receptor pairs between DC subpopulations and Treg cells. Given the large number of cells, we performed downsampling before analysis by randomly selecting 20% of cells from each subpopulation. Additionally, ligands and receptors expressed in more than 10% of cells within each cluster were included in the subsequent analysis. The CellChatDB.human database was used, with the analysis specifically focusing on secreted signaling.

CellChat models intercellular communication as a weighted and directed network based on inferred ligand–receptor interactions. In this framework, “sender” and “receiver” represent cell groups that predominantly transmit or receive signals (quantified by outgoing and incoming communication strength, respectively), while “mediator” and “influencer” describe cell groups that regulate signal flow or exert broader control over information transmission within the network, as determined by network centrality measures.

Statistical analysis

Group comparisons of two continuous variables (e.g., cell proportions and gene set scores) were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The correlation of cell subpopulation proportions was estimated using Spearman correlation. Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method, and statistical significance was assessed using the two-sided log-rank test.

Clinical variables and definitions

Clinical and pathological variables used for cohort characterization were obtained from the original metadata of the HRA001748 and TCGA-LIHC datasets and harmonized across cohorts when applicable.

Serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) was included as the principal serum biomarker for HCC. Carbohydrate antigen 19–9 (CA199) and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) were analyzed as supplementary tumor-associated serum markers when available. Elevated AFP levels or positive AFP status are clinically associated with the presence of HCC [68]. In the HRA001748 dataset, AFP, CEA, and CA199 status were determined at the time of clinical diagnosis and directly extracted from the original clinical annotations. In the TCGA-LIHC cohort, AFP status was not uniformly provided as a categorical variable and was therefore defined using a cutoff value of 20 ng/mL, classifying samples into AFP-positive (AFP+) and AFP-negative (AFP⁻) groups. For continuous analyses, serum concentrations of AFP, CEA, and CA199 were log₁₀-transformed after adding a pseudo-count of 1 to reduce skewness and improve comparability across samples.

Tumor stage variables included the T, N, and M components of the TNM staging system and the composite TNM stage. The Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage was also included. TNM and BCLC are established clinical staging systems for HCC and reflect tumor progression status and disease severity. Histological differentiation was defined according to tumor grading and reflects the degree of malignant cell differentiation. Vascular invasion and venous invasion indicate tumor cell infiltration into blood vessels or the portal venous system and are recognized pathological features associated with tumor dissemination and metastatic progression [69].

Etiological variables included viral infection status, specifically hepatitis B virus (HBV), hepatitis C virus (HCV), or non-viral etiology, as well as the presence of liver cirrhosis. Chronic infection with HBV or HCV represents a major etiological driver of HCC, and liver cirrhosis is a well-established risk factor for hepatocarcinogenesis [69].

Survival-related variables included overall survival (OS) status and duration, progression-free survival (PFS) time, tumor recurrence status, and new tumor event status. OS was defined as the time from diagnosis or treatment initiation to death from any cause. PFS was defined as the time to documented disease progression. Tumor recurrence indicates relapses after initial treatment, and new tumor event status denotes the occurrence of a newly diagnosed primary or recurrent tumor during follow-up.

All variables were directly derived from the original dataset annotations. Only samples with available corresponding clinical metadata were included in downstream analyses.
