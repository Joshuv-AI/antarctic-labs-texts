Near‐gapless genome assemblies of Williams 82 and Lee cultivars for accelerating global soybean research
Authors: Vanika Garg, Aamir W. Khan, Kevin Fengler, Victor Llaca, Yuxuan Yuan, Tri D. Vuong, Charlotte Harris, Ting‐Fung Chan, Hon Ming Lam, Rajeev K. Varshney, Henry T. Nguyen
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractComplete, gapless telomere‐to‐telomere chromosome assemblies are a prerequisite for comprehensively investigating the architecture of complex regions, like centromeres or telomeres and removing uncertainties in the order, spacing, and orientation of genes. Using complementary genomics technologies and assembly algorithms, we developed highly contiguous, nearly gapless, genome assemblies for two economically important soybean [Glycine max (L.) Merr] cultivars (Williams 82 and Lee). The centromeres were distinctly annotated on all the chromosomes of both assemblies. We further found that the canonical telomeric repeats were present at the telomeres of all chromosomes of both Williams 82 and Lee genomes. A total of 10 chromosomes in Williams 82 and eight in Lee were entirely reconstructed in single contigs without any gap. Using the combination of ab initio prediction, protein homology, and transcriptome evidence, we identified 58,287 and 56,725 protein‐coding genes in Williams 82 and Lee, respectively. The genome assemblies and annotations will serve as a valuable resource for studying soybean genomics and genetics and accelerating soybean improvement.

BACKGROUND AND SUMMARY

Reference genomes are the crux of genomics and provide unprecedented opportunities to develop genomic resources for understanding genome structure and evolution as well as identifying variants for use in breeding programs (Varshney et al., 2020). During the last two decades, reference genomes of a multitude of plant species have been reported using different sequencing technologies. The quality of these reference genomes varies significantly from draft genomes with hundreds of unplaced scaffolds to nearly finished haplotype‐phased chromosome‐length assemblies. Recent advances in sequencing technologies and assembly algorithms have facilitated the construction of complete, gapless telomere‐to‐telomere (T2T) assemblies (Deng et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2023). By integrating long‐read sequences with complementary long‐range scaffolding technologies, such as optical mapping and Hi‐C, efforts have been made to develop T2T assemblies in many plant species such as Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana (L.); Wang et al., 2022), banana (Musa acuminata; Belser et al., 2021), and watermelon (Citrullus lanatus; Deng et al., 2022). T2T assemblies are the ultimate aim of genome assemblies. The gapless assemblies provide a complete and accurate picture of a plant genome, including the most complex, highly repetitive regions (telomeres, centromeres, and nuclear‐organizing regions) and have important implications in downstream genome‐based studies such as evolution and identification of genes/variations associated with specific traits.

In this data article, we report a successful attempt to construct chromosome‐length near T2T assemblies of different soybean cultivars. Soybean [Glycine max (L.) Merr] is one of the most important vegetable oil and protein feed crops, widely used as human food and animal fodder. Several genome assemblies for different soybean cultivars have been released and are being used intensively in soybean functional research and breeding programs (Chu et al., 2021; Garg et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2020; Schmutz et al., 2010; Valliyodan et al., 2019). In an endeavor to amplify genomic resources available for soybean research, we assembled and annotated two soybean cultivars, namely, northern US accession Williams 82 (Wm82) and southern US accession Lee (PI 548656), by employing long‐read sequencing, long‐range scaffolding, and advanced bioinformatics algorithms.

MATERIALS AND RESULTS

Plant material

For sample preparation, pure seeds of Wm82 developed from multi‐generation single seeding were provided by Dr. Graham from Corn Insects and Crop Genetics Research Laboratory, USDA and ISU, IA (https://www.ars.usda.gov). For Lee, seeds acquired from the USDA Soybean Germplasm Collection (https://www.ars‐grin.gov) were further purified by multi‐generation single seeding in the Soybean Genetics and Genomics Laboratory, the University of Missouri, MO (https://cafnrfaculty.missouri.edu/nguyenlab). Twenty‐five seeds of each cultivar were geminated in a greenhouse of the University of Missouri, MO. Young leaves of seedlings at the V2‐V3 growth stage (Fehr et al., 1971) were harvested for DNA extraction. For genome sequencing, high molecular weight (HMW) genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from 1 g of fresh young leaf tissues using the NucleoBond HMW DNA kit (Macherey‐Nagel) as per the manufacturer's instructions. A standard DNA phenol‐chloroform cleanup was performed on the extracted DNA using phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol, pH 7.8–8.2. The aqueous phase was extracted using chloroform/isoamyl alcohol. The purified DNA underwent ethanol precipitation and was eluted in an elution buffer for library construction.

PacBio library construction and sequencing

For PacBio sequencing, 15 μg of HMW gDNA of Wm82 and Lee was used to make unamplified libraries using the SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 2.0 (Pacific Biosciences). First, the gDNA was sheared using Megaruptor 3 (Diagenode) and sized on the Femto Pulse Systems (Agilent). Subsequent library construction was consistent with the protocol, with a deviation for size selection. Fragments less than 15 kb were removed using the PippinHT (SageScience). Final libraries were sized at 17.6 kb and 16.6 kb for Wm82 and Lee, respectively. The libraries were sequenced on Pacific Biosciences Sequel II platform using Chemistry 2.0 and a 30‐h movie. For each library, two SMRT cells were sequenced (Table S1). The subreads thus obtained were processed to yield HiFi reads using the circular consensus sequence algorithm of SMRT link v9. The HiFi reads were filtered to retain reads with ≥5 and ≤25 kb length. The filtered data comprised 8,271,987 HiFi reads (45.9X) with N50 of 17.0 kb in Wm82 and 9,021,517 HiFi reads (39.1X), with N50 of 18.6 kb in Lee.

Bionano optical maps construction

The isolated HMW gDNA was labeled and stained according to the Bionano Prep Direct Label and Stain protocol (Bionano Genomics). Briefly, labeling was done by incubating 750 ng gDNA with 1× DLE‐1 Enzyme for 2 h in the presence of 1× DL‐Green and 1× DLE‐1 Buffer followed by proteinase K digestion and DL‐Green cleanup (Bionano Genomics). DNA was pre‐stained by mixing the labeled DNA with 1× Flow Buffer in the presence of 1× DTT and incubating overnight at 4°C. Staining was carried out by adding 3.2 μL of a DNA Stain solution for every 300 ng of pre‐stained DNA and incubating at room temperature for at least 2 h before loading onto the Bionano Chip. Labeled and stained DNA was loaded and run on Saphyr Chips according to the Saphyr System User Guide. Data processing was performed using the Bionano Genomics Access software. In the case of Wm82, a 303 Gbp dataset was generated with a minimum molecule length of 500 kb, molecule N50 length of 653 kb, and an estimated effective assembly coverage of 200X. Similarly, for Lee, 265 Gbp were generated with a minimum molecule length of 300 kb, molecule N50 length of 435 kb, and an effective coverage of 192X. Both datasets were assembled via the Bionano Genomics Access software platform (v1.6.1) with the configuration file Arguments_nonhaplotype_noES_noCut_DLE1_saphyr.xml. The final genome map assembly for Wm82 consisted of 47 maps with a genome map N50 of 27.7 Mb and a total map length of 1030 Mb. For Lee, the genome map assembly included 52 maps with a genome map N50 of 27.9 Mb and a total map length of 1008 Mb.

De novo genome assembly, hybrid scaffolding, and pseudomolecule construction

The HiFi reads were assembled using Hifiasm version 0.16.1 (Cheng et al., 2021) with default parameters to generate draft contig genomes for Wm82 and Lee. The primary contigs were filtered for a minimum coverage of 10X (as determined by Hifiasm) and a minimum length of 70 kb. In total, 114 and 87 HiFi filtered contigs with N50 lengths of 17.9 and 20.5 Mb in Wm82 and Lee were generated, respectively. Initial hybrid scaffolds were generated from HiFi contigs and the Bionano genome maps using the Bionano Genomics Access software (v1.6.1) and the DLE‐1 configuration file hybridScaffold_DLE1_config.xml using auto‐conflict resolution. This resulted in 25 hybrid scaffolds for Wm82 (scaffold N50 = 51.0 Mb) and 30 hybrid scaffolds for Lee (scaffold N50 = 44.7 Mb). Extensive manual curation was performed within Bionano Access to precisely trim overlapping contigs at shared labels, remove spurious contigs, and make additional cuts in collapsed repeats not addressed by auto‐conflict resolution. Corrected Hifiasm HiFi reads were aligned to the curated hybrid scaffolds using minimap2 version 2.20 (H. Li, 2018). From this alignment, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and small InDel errors in the assembly, as well as small scaffold gaps, were identified using Samtools mpileup (v1.13) (Danecek et al., 2021) and corrected to generate a polished assembly. In addition, the corrected HiFi reads were used to fill additional gaps in the assembly and resolve some tandem repeats through manual curation in conjunction with the Bionano maps. After gap filling, the final assembly size of Wm82 was 1,013,741,020 bp with 99.84% of the assembly size in 20 pseudomolecules. The final assembly of Wm82 (Wm82.a5) retained just 14 kb gap regions. Similarly, the final assembly size of Lee (Lee.a3) was 1,016,382,015 bp, with 99.78% of the genome sequence anchored in 20 pseudomolecules (Figure 1a).

Gene prediction and annotation

Repetitive elements were identified and annotated in both genomes using de novo and homology‐based repeat approaches. RepeatModeler version open‐1.0.10 (default parameters) was used to create a de novo repeat library for each genome. The de novo repeat library thus obtained was combined with the known repeats from the RepBase to generate custom repeat libraries. These libraries were then used to screen the assembled genomes for repeats using RepeatMasker version open‐4.0.7 (“‐u ‐gff ‐e ncbi ‐xsmall”). Approximately 52% of each genome was annotated with repetitive elements, similar to the previous soybean genomes (Figure 1a; Table S2; Wm82.gnm4.4PTR (Valliyodan et al., 2019); Lee.gnm2.K7BV (Garg et al., 2022)). After repetitive sequences were masked, the protein‐coding genes were predicted by integrating homology‐based prediction, ab initio prediction, and transcriptome‐based evidence. For ab initio prediction, publicly available RNA‐Seq datasets for soybean were aligned to both assemblies using HISAT2 v2.1.0 with default parameters (Kim et al., 2019). The aligned files were then provided as input evidence for the BRAKER pipeline v2.1.5 (Hoff et al., 2019). Further, for transcript evidence‐based prediction, the alignment data from HISAT2 were assembled into reference‐guided transcripts using StringTie v2.0 (Kovaka et al., 2019). Additionally, the RNA‐seq datasets were assembled into de novo transcripts using Trinity v2.5.1 (Haas et al., 2013). The de novo assembled transcripts were further aligned to the respective genome using PASA pipeline v2.4.1 with GMAP v2018‐02‐12 (Wu & Watanabe, 2005). For homology‐based prediction, protein sequences from Arabidopsis, rice (Oryza sativa) and soybean were searched against the genome assemblies using Exonerate v2.2.0 (Slater & Birney, 2005). The gene model evidence from the above three approaches was integrated using EVidenceModeler v1.1.1 (Haas et al., 2008) to obtain 58,287 and 56,725 protein‐coding genes in Wm82 and Lee, respectively (Table 1, Figure 1a). The predicted gene models were then assigned functional annotations using DIAMOND BLASTP (E‐value cut off of 1E‐05) according to the best hits to the NCBI non‐redundant, Swiss‐Prot, and TrEMBL databases (UniProt Consortium, 2019). Further, InterProScan v5.39‐77.0 with default databases was used to identify conserved domains and motifs in the proteins encoded by the predicted gene models and assign Gene Ontology IDs to each gene model. Using these public databases, 99.45% (57,968) of the predicted genes in Wm82 and 99.52% (56,451) in Lee were assigned functional annotations.

Non‐coding gene and resistance gene analogs prediction

The ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes in both genomes were identified using a similarity search against rRNA sequences from Arabidopsis and rice (BLASTN; 1E‐05). The transfer RNA (tRNA) genes were predicted using tRNAscan‐SE v2.0 (Chan et al., 2021). Further, microRNA (miRNA) and small nucleolar RNA (snoRNA) genes were predicted by employing a similarity search against the Rfam database (release 14.2) using Infernal (v1.1.3) (Nawrocki & Eddy, 2013). We annotated 3839 non‐coding RNAs (including 284 miRNAs, 676 rRNAs, 1144 tRNAs, and 1735 snoRNAs) in Wm82 and 4065 non‐coding RNAs (including 285 miRNAs, 891 rRNAs, 1123 tRNAs, and 1766 snoRNAs) in Lee. Further, using the RGAugury pipeline (Li et al., 2016), we identified 1694 and 1763 resistance gene analogs (RGAs) in Wm82 and Lee, respectively (Table S3). Among the identified RGAs, receptor‐like kinases (1013 in Wm82 and 1026 in Lee) were the most abundant class, followed by NBS‐encoding genes (223 in Wm82 and 301 in Lee) in both genomes.

Identification of centromeres and telomeres

Centromeres and telomeres are challenging to assemble from short‐read sequencing data due to their highly repetitive nature and short repeat lengths. Taking advantage of the long read‐based assemblies, we identified telomeric and centromeric repeats in both the soybean assemblies. Using seven‐base telomeric repeats (“CCCTAAA”) as a sequence query, we identified all 40 telomeres in Lee and 39 in Wm82 (Figure 1a). Plant centromeres are usually composed of tandemly repeated monomers (or satellite DNA). Soybean centromeres are known to consist of two distinct satellite repeats (GmCent‐1 and GmCent‐2) (Gill et al., 2009). Using Tandem Repeats Finder, we identified CentGm‐1 (92 bp) and CentGm‐2 (91 bp)‐like long tandem repeats in all the chromosomes of both assemblies. Approximate locations of 20 centromeric regions were estimated, with lengths reaching up to 1.78 Mb for both the genomes (average 416.65 kb for Wm82 and 484.28 kb for Lee) (Figure 1a).

Improvements as compared to previous genomes of Wm82 and Lee

In order to assess the quality of the assemblies developed in this study, we compared them with the previously reported assemblies. We used minimap2 with “‐cx asm5” to compare the genomes and visualized the resulting paf file for synteny analysis. The analysis showed that these newly developed assemblies were greatly improved in terms of completeness, gap filling, and correction of several misjoins when compared to the previous assemblies (Figures S1 and S2). Notably, one of the key improvements was filling gaps, as the previous assemblies contained 56,691 gaps (42.29 Mb) and 8920 gaps (25.91 Mb) in Wm82 and Lee, respectively. In contrast, the newly developed assemblies had significantly fewer gaps, with a total of only 14.25 kb and 32.77 kb in Wm82 and Lee, respectively. Furthermore, these assemblies corrected 1512 and 878 misjoins in Wm82 and Lee, respectively, compared to the previous assemblies (Table S4). Some of the larger corrections are highlighted in Figure 1b,c. Importantly, filling these gaps also resulted in the completion of several previously incomplete and uncharacterized genes. The complete genes now contain the full sequences, including introns, exons, and regulatory regions, and are therefore more likely to be biologically functional. For instance, in the case of Wm82, the Glyma.18G061000 (Gm_Wm82_50036 in Wm82.a5) gene had a gap of 421 bp in the intronic region, which was filled in the Wm82.a5 assembly (Figure 1d). Similarly, in the case of Lee, the Gm.Lee_v2.0_08530 (Gm_Lee_09409 in Lee.a3) had a gap of 656 nucleotides in the coding sequence (CDS) region, which was filled in the Lee.a3 assembly. Due to the completion of the CDS sequence of this gene, it was annotated as GLYCINE‐RICH CELL WALL STRUCTURAL PROTEIN‐LIKE, whereas in the previous assembly, it was annotated as unknown (Figure 1d).

TECHNICAL VALIDATION

We evaluated the quality of the newly developed soybean assemblies using various approaches. In the first approach, the improved assemblies and predicted gene models were evaluated for completeness using Benchmarking Universal Single‐Copy Orthologs (Manni et al., 2021). Nearly 99.5% of the 5366 core fabales genes were identified as complete in both assemblies indicating the high quality of genome assemblies (Table 1).

Furthermore, we used a publicly available orthogonal Hi‐C dataset (BioProject ID: PRJNA679437) for Lee to validate the structural integrity of the pseudomolecules in Lee.a3. The Hi‐C dataset was aligned against Lee.a3 genome using the Juicer pipeline with default parameters (Durand et al., 2016). The resulting contact matrix confirmed the high contiguity of the genome assembly (Figure S3).

In the third approach, the Illumina paired‐end short reads from previously sequenced 200 cultivated soybean accessions with coverage ≥10× (Bayer et al., 2022) were mapped on the assembled Wm82.a5 and Lee.a3 genomes. The raw data were obtained from NCBI (BioProject PRJNA257011, PRJNA289660, and PRJNA639876) and filtered to obtain clean reads using Trimmomatic v0.39 (Bolger et al., 2014). The clean reads were then aligned against the genomes of Wm82 and Lee using BWA‐mem2 v2.2.1 (H. Li & Durbin, 2009). On average, >99% of the paired‐end reads were mapped to the genomes of Wm82 and Lee independently, suggesting the very high coverage of both genome assemblies.

Finally, we compared the presence of telomeric repeats in the new gapless assemblies with the previously reported Wm82 and Lee assemblies (Garg et al., 2022; Valliyodan et al., 2019), we identified 39 in Wm82.a5 and 40 in Lee.a3 compared to 26 and 22 in the previous Wm82 (Wm82.gnm4.4PTR) and Lee (Lee.gnm2.K7BV) genomes, respectively, indicating that these assemblies are complete.

VARIANT DETECTION BETWEEN WM82 AND LEE GENOMES

To identify the genomic differences between Wm82 and Lee genomes, we performed whole genome alignment using MUMmer4 (Marçais et al., 2018) with parameters “‐mum ‐l 40 ‐c 90”. The alignment was used to detect genome‐wide variations (Figure S4). The whole genome alignment was filtered using delta‐filter with parameters “‐m ‐i 90 ‐l 100”. The SNPs were identified using the “show‐snps” utility of MUMmer with parameters “‐ClTr”. The structural variations and short InDels were identified using Syri v1.6.3 (Goel et al., 2019) using the output of “show‐coords” with parameters “‐THrd”. A total of 1.48 million SNPs, 0.16 million short insertions, 0.16 million short deletions, 3205 large InDels (> 100 bp), 1621 translocation events, and 56 inversion events were identified between Wm82 and Lee genomes (Table S5). These inversion and translocation events amounted to 10.82 Mb and 11.81 Mb sequences in the reference genome (Wm82.a5). The structural variants identified comprised several high‐effect insertions in the coding region resulting in stop gained, stop lost and in‐frame insertions (Table S6). For instance, an insertion of 148 bp and 108 bp in Lee genome was identified in genes coding for BURP domain‐containing protein 9 (Gm_Wm82_10793), and kinesin‐like protein KIN‐12F (Gm_Wm82_50255), respectively. A 132 bp insertion in the Lee genome was identified in a gene (Gm_Wm82_52324) coding for MDIS1‐interacting receptor‐like kinase 2‐like isoform X2. This gene is an important component of the early immune response to fungal‐derived elicitor such as Fusarium (Coleman et al., 2020). Furthermore, a 309 bp insertion in the Lee genome was identified in the CDS region of a gene (Gm_Wm82_18340) coding for leaf rust 10 disease resistance locus kinases. Another 205 bp insertion was found in the Lee genome affecting a gene (Gm_Wm82_22102) encoding for zinc finger protein KNUCKLES‐like. This gene functions as a transcriptional repressor of cellular proliferation that regulates floral determinacy and relative size of basal pattern elements and is known to have a cadastral regulatory function in the Arabidopsis flower development (Payne et al., 2004).

CONCLUSION

We have generated high‐quality reference genome sequences for two elite soybean cultivars, Wm82 and Lee, using the combination of PacBio HiFi sequencing and Bionano optical mapping. The improved genome assemblies have significantly reduced the number of gaps and scaffold breaks compared to the previous assemblies. Our comprehensive analysis, including gene prediction, annotation, functional assignments, and identification of non‐coding genes and RGAs, provides valuable resources for future genomic studies and molecular breeding in soybean. The comparative genomic analysis and identification of structural variations between Wm82 and Lee genomes offer insights into the genetic diversity and potential targets for soybean improvement.
