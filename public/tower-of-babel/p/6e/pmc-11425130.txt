Investigation of the hydrostatic and plastic zones in hardness measurement using finite element simulation
Authors: Ferenc Oláh, Richárd Horváth, Mihály Réger
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The aim of this research was to evaluate the effect of the strain hardening of a homogeneous metallic material on the size and shape of hydrostatic and plastic zones forming in the indentation process with a spherical indenter. Precise measurements and finite element calculations (FEM) were performed for indentations and the results were compared with those of the expanding cavity model (ECM). We concluded that in the range of d/D (impression diameter/indenter diameter) = 0.42–0.5 there is a good agreement between the measured and FEM-calculated hardness results if the strain hardening effect is properly taken into account. The results show that the ECM model underestimates the size of the hydrostatic zone and overestimates that of the plastic deformation region. We have also proved that the hydrostatic and plastic zones have a more complex shape than a hemisphere. In the investigated cases, the ratio of the plastic and hydrostatic volumes beneath the indenter is nearly independent of the d/D ratio in the range of 0.42–0.5, and its value is around 6. In these cases, the calculated geometry and size of the hydrostatic and plastic zones can be assumed to be close to their actual values. These results demonstrate that the reliability of hardness measurement performed by spherical indenter is highest when the d/D ratio of 0.42–0.5 is fulfilled, within the d/D range of 0.24–0.6 allowed by the standard. In this paper, we provided a well-defined and reproducible way to define the boundary between the hydrostatic core and the plastic zone (for a homogeneous structure with a spherical indenter) taking into account strain hardening.

Introduction

During hardness testing, the conditions as a result of the penetration of the indenter can be described with the mechanical model of the expansion of a cavity, which is generally assumed to be spherical. In indentation tests, the shape and size of the hydrostatic and plastic zones forming beneath the indenter are largely influenced by the structure and properties of the investigated material near the indentation area. The expanding cavity model (ECM) approach assumes that the hydrostatic and plastic deformation zones forming under the indenter have a hemispherical shape. Strain hardening, which depends on the local plastic strain level around the indenter, affects the formation of both the hydrostatic and the plastic zone, especially in the case of metallic materials. First Bishop et al. [1] analysed the indentation process by analytical methods in 1945. This pioneering work defined the basic principles and general theoretical framework of the expanding cavity model. A milestone in the interpretation and measurement of hardness was Tabor's study, published in 1970 [2], which integrated theoretical foundations and practical experience in a comprehensive and detailed manner. Johnson, in his paper published 15 years later [3], offered a more sophisticated approach to modelling the indentation process and interpreting the results of hardness tests. In his expanding cavity model (hereafter: ECM), he assumed that a hemispherical hydrostatic stress state zone (“hydrostatic core”) and a plastic zone are formed under the indenter during loading (Fig. 1). The size and shape of the hydrostatic and plastic zones are controlled by stress and strain (elastic, plastic) fields under the indenter. Johnson [3] provided a relationship between hardness, the size of the hydrostatic and plastic zone, and the elastic–plastic mechanical properties of the material. The ECM model and its applicability was further developed by several researchers focusing on calculating residual stresses around the indentation [4], interpreting hardness measurement results in case of non-metallic substances [5,6,7], and analysing the typical damage mechanisms of the investigated materials [8], among others.Fig. 1Representation of the hydrostatic, plastic and elastic zones under the indenter (based on [3,5,2]).Fig. 1

The adaptation of the analytical model (ECM) of the cavity growing due to internal pressure to the hardness testing of practical alloys is limited. The original Johnson model [3] gives the expected hardness of materials with elastic–perfectly plastic behaviour, for testing with a conical indenter. The applicability of the model to practical alloys is linked, among other things, to Gao's work, namely the extension of the ECM theory to elastic–hardening material types [9,10]. The ECM model, complemented by Gao's deformation gradient theory, can determine the plastic zone and pressure and hardness during the indentation of strain-hardening materials. Due to the difference between the measured and calculated values, it became necessary to apply certain correction factors during the calculation [10]. Taking this into account, relative hardness for a linearly hardening material model and a spherical indenter can be estimated with the following equation:(4)Hσys=23[74+74ETE(14EσysaR−1)+(1−ETE)ln(14EσysaR)]

Mata et al. [11] and later Ginder et al. [12] attempted to derive closed-form solutions for the extension of the plastic zone for perfectly plastic and strain-hardening materials by introducing the so-called parametrical analogy approach (based on the concept of an equivalent spherical indenter). They found that strain hardening has a profound effect on the size of the cavity and the plastic zone. Deriving closed-form solutions for the size of the plastic zone required several simplifications, so the parametrical analogy method is not precise enough for the prediction of the shape of the plastic zone.

Xiao and Yu [13] established a relationship between hardness and uniaxial creep test results. Eumelen et al. [14] developed an analytical model to determine elastic and plastic properties based on indentation measurements. Johnson's approach was used by Zhao et al. [15] in interpreting of their shotblasting experiment results.

The shape of the plastic zone is affected by the anisotropy properties of the investigated material as well. Yoshioka [16] identified a more complex geometry than a hemisphere in the case of the nanoindentation of an anisotropic silicon single crystal. Puech et al. [17] investigated the deformation field around a micro-Vickers hardness impression created on a silicon crystal by Raman spectroscopy. They and Woodcock [18] also proved the approximate validity of Johnson's model in microhardness testing.

The indentation of inhomogeneous structures is also widely investigated. First Burnett and Rickerby [19] developed a calculation method for a soft substrate which had a hard surface layer. They assumed [20] that static microhardness and scratch testing can be described with common characteristics, since plastic deformation is probably similar in both. Korsunsky et al. [21] described a novel approach to interpret the composite hardness of coated systems. Gonda et al. [22] investigated different hard coatings on a soft substrate, Réger et al. [23] examined metal samples with inhomogeneous hardness distribution in the surface area. The authors mentioned above agree that the shape and size of the hydrostatic and plastic zones are affected by structural inhomogeneities of material structure, but there is currently no reliable model to describe the relationship between them.

The finite element modelling of indentation has been possible since the late 1980s. The elastic and plastic deformation of different combinations of film/substrate structures were investigated in detail by Bhattacharya and Nix [24,25]. Ma et al. [26] developed a method to determine the elastic–plastic properties of metal thin films from small-scale sharp indentation tests, using the finite element method. For a spherical indenter, Lee and Komvopoulos [27] and later Bahri et al. [28] performed three-dimensional finite element calculations in order to determine the elastic and plastic properties of the investigated material. Patel and Kalidindi [29] analysed the relationship between the indentation stress–strain function and the simple compressive stress–strain function using the finite element modelling of indentation. In these studies, the extent and shape of the hydrostatic and plastic zones have not been evaluated in detail.

According to the results of researchers discussed in this chapter, the shape and size of the hydrostatic and plastic zones are largely influenced by the structure and properties of the investigated material near the indentation area. If the properties (ie. hardness, elastic modulus) in this area can be assumed homogenous, strain hardening will strongly affect the formation of hydrostatic and plastic zones, especially in metallic materials. Strain hardening depends on the local plastic strain level, thus the developing inhomogeneous strain distribution below the indenter results in an inhomogeneous strain hardening field. The effect of strain hardening on the extent of characteristic indentation zones is not described in detail by the authors mentioned above. The main aim of this research is to evaluate the effect of strain hardening of a metallic material on the size and shape of the hydrostatic and plastic zones during indentation. Because of the complexity of this issue, we performed the analysis for relatively simple cases, assuming that the material investigated is homogenuous and the indenter is spherical. The response of a steel showing strain hardening was evaluated by finite element and analytical calculations, and calculated hardness was compared to measured hardness. The size and shape of the hydrostatic and plastic zone under the indenter, determined with the finite element method were also compared to the results of the ECM analytical model. The finite element tests were performed and compared for two types of material models (elastic–perfectly plastic I. and bilinear strain hardening II. material models).

Materials and methods

Material models used

In finite element modelling, material properties can be determined with the approximate description of the stress–strain diagram (elastic–perfectly plastic bilinear material model, or bilinear or multilinear strain-hardening material model). The use of the bilinear model is advisable because the material model can be easily determined and, at the same time, the required computing capacity is also reduced. We compared the results of a series of calculations performed using two types of bilinear models. The parameters of the material models were determined based on their tensile characteristics. Young's modulus, Poisson's ratio and yield strength were the same in both material models, and they determined behaviour in the elastic deformation range. Plastic deformation was characterized by the hardening (tangent) modulus, which gives the stress change associated with the given permanent deformation. Fig. 2 shows the characteristic behaviour of the two material models. In the perfectly plastic material model, there is no strain hardening after the yield point has been reached [30], i.e. in the case of permanent deformation, we assumed that the stress was corresponding to the yield point. In the case of the model including strain hardening, the hardening modulus was characterized by the slope of the line drawn between yield strength and tensile strength [31]. The calculations presented analyse how the two types of material models affect the characteristics of the hydrostatic and plastic zone interpreted according to the Johnson model. The finite element simulation and the validation test were performed for the S235JR (1.0045) structural steel. We previously performed three tensile tests to create an accurate material model, based on which the yield strength of the tested material is σ ys = 332 ± 2.3 MPa) and its tensile strength is σ m = 590.4 ± 6.9 MPa.Fig. 2Material models (I. elastic–perfectly plastic model, II. bilinear strain hardening model).Fig. 2

The calculation model

The software used for finite element modelling was the Ansys Workbench 2022 R2 version. It uses the Newton-Raphson method, presented by Crisfield already in 1978 [34], for large-deformation elastoplastic analysis. The stiffness matrices of the elements and the out-of-equilibrium force vectors were calculated by the reduced two-point Gaussian integration, therefore the degree of self-constraint in the adopted shape functions was minimized. The development of the Newton-Raphson method was presented by Ypma [35]. As it is one of the fastest calculation methods, it is widely used even today [36].

In the simulation software, the finite element mesh was created in a 3D model so the procedure can also be used for non-axisymmetric hardness testing (e.g. Vickers test). In this approach, it is sufficient to model a quarter of the specimen. It is advisable to define the indenter as a rigid body, since its deformation can only affect the results for very small indentation depths [37]. In the present calculation, the penetration depth of the spherical indenter with a diameter of 2.5 mm is 0.162 mm, which is consistent with the results of actual hardness testing performed in advance with a load of 187.5 kg (≈1840 N). In the simulation, the indentation of the indenter continued up to this value, at which point the vertical counterforce arising on the indenter reached the force used in hardness testing [38]. The results of the finite element calculation series were analysed based on data for 10 different indentation depths from 0.015 to 0.162 mm (d/D ratio between 0.15 and 0.5). When the planned depth had been reached, the diameter of the impression was 1.23 mm, the same as that of the impression in the hardness testing experiment. The diameter of the impression at the maximum indentation (1.23 mm) corresponds to the allowable ball diameter to impression ratio required by the standard [39]:(10)0.24‧D < d < 0.6‧D

Therefore in our case, this value was in the required range (0.49‧D).

The modelled specimen was an 8 mm × 8 mm × 8 mm cube-shaped section, which is large enough to prevent the penetration of the indenter causing permanent deformation at the outer boundaries of the section.

The optimization of meshing strategy in finite element modelling has been investigated in several studies. In their studies of plastic deformations, Benzley et al. [40] stated that hexahedron-shaped elements provided far more accurate results with the same number of elements. In this study, they also proved that tetra-geometry elements, with incorrect settings, could give incorrect results for static bending and torsional stress. They proved this by modelling real tests.

In the finite element modelling of crystal structures, Feather et al. [41] also had similar results. They found that with automatically generated meshing, the tetra-element mesh often gives better results, but at the same time, calculations can be faster with the better adjustability of hexahedral elements. Based on our own previous experience and results [38], the settings of the finite element mesh were chosen as follows: around the penetrating sphere on the test specimen in a radius of 2 mm, there is a point distance of 0.08 mm and then 0.5 mm further away. In both cases, the mesh is hexahedral (Fig. 3). For an indentation of 10 %, the mesh was no longer adequate, therefore in that case, the impression was recalculated with a modified mesh. In this case, the mesh was compressed within a radius of 0.8 mm to an element distance of 0.05 mm.Fig. 3Meshing of the specimen.Fig. 3

It follows from the principle of Brinell hardness testing that measured hardness depends on the applied loading force, the d/D ratio and the hardening ability of the tested material. Hardness reaches a maximum with increasing d/D ratio, then it decreases. The hardness function determined with the FEM model exhibits this maximum as shown in Fig. 4a and b. The ECM model, based on equations (2), (4), provided a monotonically increasing hardness. In the case of the perfectly plastic material model (Fig. 4a), the maximum of the Brinell hardness function provided by finite element modelling was 120 HB. The FEM and ECM hardness functions match in the range of 0.15–0.4 d/D but, due to the reasons mentioned above, at higher d/D ratios there is a significant difference between them. In the case of the strain-hardening model (Fig. 4 b), both FEM and ECM provided greater hardness compared to the perfectly plastic material model. In the case of the FEM results, the difference increased with the increase of the d/D ratio, i.e. with the increase of the plastic deformation rate (see last column of Table 1). Maximum hardness was 150 HB and the measured and the FEM hardness function match in the range of 0.35–0.5 d/D. Based on this, it is likely that the complex, plastic deformation and hardening conditions near the indentation are realistically described by the finite element approach of the elastic-hardening material model. Therefore, the applied FEM model represents the size and growth of the hydrostatic and plastic deformation zones near the indentation accurately. The dimensions and shape of the hydrostatic and plastic zone are analysed in the next chapter.

Size and shape of the hydrostatic core

As the load increases, the size of both the hydrostatic core and the plastic zone under the indenter increases. In the ECM approach, due to the conditions used in the model, both zones become hemispherical, and their size is clearly related to the radius of the indentation. According to the model geometry, the radius of the hydrostatic core must be the same as the radius of the impression, and the size of the plastic zone can be calculated with equation (1). Zone sizes calculated with the ECM model can only be interpreted in the case of the elastic–perfectly plastic material model.

In the case of both material models, the finite element approach provides an opportunity to identify and compare the boundaries of the hydrostatic and plastic regions. Fig. 5a shows the behaviour of the elastic-perfectly plastic material, Fig. 5b shows the behaviour of the elastic-strain hardening material. To determine the boundary lines of hydrostatic and plastic regions, the definitions described in Chapter 2.2 were considered to be valid. In order to compare the individual cases, let us have the radius of the hemisphere whose volume is the same as the volume characterized by the contour line. Fig. 5 shows the equivalent hemisphere radii determined in this way. The figure also shows the value of the theoretical hemispherical radii defined on the basis of the ECM model. Above Fig. 5, the contour of the hydrostatic zone determined from FEM can be seen for the two material models for different indentation depths. The hydrostatic zone always starts from the edge of the impression, that is, the radius of the zone on the surface of the sample is actually equal to the radius of the impression. However, in the depth direction, the shape of the zone differs from hemispherical, even in the case of the perfectly plastic material model. Fig. 5 shows that a linear proportion can be assumed with good approximation between the radius of the hydrostatic core determined by FEM and ECM in the range between 0.15 and 0.5 d/D. If hardening also occurs, the difference continues to increase, reaching the equivalent radius 1.9 at a ratio of 0.5 d/D. During indentation, a higher load is required for a given d/D ratio due to hardening, therefore hardening results in an increase in the volume of the hydrostatic core at a fixed d/D ratio.

Size and shape of the plastic zone

In the present series of calculations, the two approaches led to the same result, that is, both procedures can be used to determine the plastic deformation range. Discussion

The most important results of this research on the extent of the hydrostatic and plastic zone formed during hardness measurement are summarised in Table 1 and Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6. The relationship between the hardness calculated with the finite element model and measured hardness (see Table 1) can be followed in Fig. 4a and 4b. The fit between the measured and calculated hardness is significantly better when the bilinear material model is used than when the perfectly plastic material model is used. This is true for this specific case, but detailed statistical analysis is necessary for generalization. Agreement is particularly good between the d/D characteristic limits of 0.42 and 0.5. Based on the above, calculations with the bilinear material model in the range of d/D = 0.42–0.5 provide realistic results for the geometry and extent of the hydrostatic and plastic zones. The shape of the hydrostatic core calculated by FEM is shown in Fig. 5. In all cases, the boundary of the hydrostatic zone begins at the edge of the imprint, but its size (equivalent radius) is significantly larger than the value d calculated with the analytical ECM model, even for the perfectly plastic material model. In the range of d/D = 0.42–0.5 it is approximately true that the realistic size of the hydrostatic zone (i.e. for the bilinear material model) is nearly twice the size of the hydrostatic zone calculated with the ECM model (1.77–1.84 times, see Fig. 5 for d/D = 0.42–0.5 data series and Table 3). The ECM model, therefore, underestimates the size of the hydrostatic zone. In contrast, the ECM model overestimates the extension of the plastic deformation region. Based on the data series d/D = 0.42–0.5 in Fig. 6 and Table 3, the realistic equivalent diameter of the plastic zone is only about 75 % of the value provided by the ECM model. The results and conclusions can only be assessed in a partial, qualitative way in the light of the technical literature. Analytical models that include the effect of strain hardening ([[1], [3], [4], [5], [2], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]]) provide a relationship between tensile characteristics (i.e. yield strength, strain hardening exponent) and expected hardness, but do not provide an estimate of the size of the hydrostatic and the plastic zone. The theoretical considerations [1,3,2,9,10]and measurement experiences [6,11,28] presented in the literature are consistent with our computational results exhibited in the first part of this chapter. Similar results were obtained by Mata on the basis of his analytical and finite element calculations [6,11,28], although he mainly discussed the plastic deformation processes for a sharp indenter in his articles. Mata evaluated the shape of the plastic zone based on the location of the equivalent plastic stress isocontour lines, not by the equivalent plastic deformation rate (see Eq. (8)). The contour of the plastic zone and the equivalent stress isocontour lines are similarly located at the outer edge of the deformation zone (see diagrams on the right in Fig. 9a and b). He found a very similar tendency of the change of the shape of the plastic zone when he considered the strain hardening effect in his model).

The calculated geometry and sizet of the hydrostatic and plastic zones can be assumed to be close to their actual values. The presented finite element method makes it possible to explore the behaviour of more complex surface layers with the use of an axisymmetric or non-axisymmetric indenter. A nitrided surface is often used under a PVD layer because nitriding ensures a better connection and load distribution between the core material and the more rigid PVD coating. Also, the nitriding of tool steels prior to PVD coating improves their wear resistance [[42], [43], [44]]. In this respect, the characterization of the cooperation between the layer and the base matrix is of outstanding importance in the case of diffusion and compound layers (e.g. nitriding, carburizing hardening) and thin and duplex (PVD, CVD) coatings [19,21,22]. Our method can serve as the basis of further calculations and finite element modelling and can help understand the load transfer of different layers (e.g. base matrix + nitrided layer + PVD coating).

Conclusion

This study focuses on the analytical and finite element modelling of the characteristics of the hydrostatic and plastic zones under the indenter in Brinell hardness testing. Our goal was to calculate the shape and size of the hydrostatic and plastic zones more accurately, and to compare the results of the two approaches. Based on our results, the following conclusions can be made:-The volume of the hydrostatic core largely depends on the type of material model used in this study.-The volume of the hydrostatic core is about two times bigger in case of model II. (bilinear strain hardening model) than in case of model I. (elastic-perfectly plastic model).-The size of the plastic zone also depends on the material model used. The difference between the two material models at full load is ~ 55 % for the volume of both the hydrostatic and the plastic zones.-The volume ratio of the hydrostatic and plastic zones during the penetration of the indenter is nearly constant, but slightly decreases with indentation load.-In the finite element modelling of hardness testing, the bilinear strain-hardening material model (model II.) gives more accuracy. The elastic–perfectly plastic material model (model I.) is not accurate enough in such cases.-In the present calculation series, two approaches were used to define the limits of the plastic zone, which yielded the same result. It has been demonstrated that both Tresca's criterion and equivalent plastic deformation are suitable for determining the plastic deformation range.-The results demonstrate that the reliability of hardness measurement performed by spherical indenter is highest when the d/D ratio of 0.42–0.5 is fulfilled, within the d/D range of 0.24–0.6 – equation (10) – allowed by the standard.

In summary, the finite element modelling with the use of the bilinear strain-hardening material model (model II.) realistically describes the complex, inhomogeneous plastic deformation and hardening conditions and the size and growth of the hydrostatic and plastic deformation zones near the indentation mark. In the future, similar indentation analysis of materials with a more complex, inhomogeneous structure on the surface (e.g.: different layers, PVD coated or nitrided surface etc.) can also be attempted using the calculation method discussed in this paper.
