Mental disorders and osteoporosis treatment in patients with hip fracture—a Danish population-based cohort study
Authors: Rikke Sommer Haaber, Bente Langdahl, Rob Nelissen, Simon Storgaard Jensen, Kjeld Andersen, Alma B. Pedersen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryUsing Danish registries, we identified 57,887 patients with hip fracture aged ≥ 65 without recent anti-osteoporosis medication (AOM) and examined whether mental disorders affect AOM initiation after hip fracture. One-year initiation ranged from 6.5 to 17.0% among patients with mental disorders compared to 21% in patients without mental disorder. Initiation remains suboptimal, highlighting required targeted interventions.PurposeTo evaluate the impact of mental disorders on the initiation of anti-osteoporosis medication (AOM) following hip fracture.MethodsUsing Danish medical registries, we identified patients aged ≥ 65, with no recent AOM use and a first-time hip fracture between 2010 and 2020 (n = 57,887). Mental disorders were categorized using a 10-year history of hospital contacts into no mental disorder, organic mental disorders, substance use, schizophrenic, mood, and anxiety disorders. The outcome was initiation of AOM defined by pharmacy dispensations and hospital treatment codes. Among patients without AOM use in the year prior to hip fracture, 1-year cumulative incidence functions (CIF) with 95% confidence intervals of initiation of AOM with death as a competing risk were calculated. Analyses were conducted overall and stratified by sex, age, previous fractures, somatic comorbidity, mental comorbidity, living arrangement, and calendar year.ResultsThe 1 year CIF of initiation of AOM after hip fracture was 20.8% (20.4–21.1) for patients with no mental disorder, 6.5% (6.0–7.1) for organic mental disorders, 17.0% (15.6–18.5) for substance use, 11.5% (9.5–13.8) for schizophrenic disorders, 14.8% (13.8–15.8) for mood, and 16.1% (14.4–17.9) for anxiety disorders. Higher initiation of AOM was seen in patients < 80 years, females, with previous fractures, in patients with less somatic comorbidity and no other mental comorbidities, cohabiting in their own home, and in more recent calendar years.ConclusionAOM initiation following hip fracture remains suboptimal. Patients with mental disorders were less likely to initiate AOM after hip fracture, highlighting a persistent gap in secondary fracture prevention among vulnerable populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00198-026-07975-x.

Introduction

The global burden of osteoporosis is high and has been increasing over recent decades and is expected to continue to do so [1]. Osteoporosis increases the risk of hip and other fractures after no or minimal trauma [2]. At the age of 50 years, the remaining lifetime risk of osteoporotic hip fracture is 15% in women and 6% in men [3]. Patients with osteoporotic hip fracture have elevated risks of subsequent fractures of the hip and other sites [4, 5]. In addition, the mortality increases up to 5 years after a subsequent fracture [6]. Hence, post-fracture treatment with anti-osteoporosis medication (AOM) is critical. Despite progress in AOM treatment and guidelines recommending the use of AOM for secondary prevention for all patients with hip fracture [7, 8], a treatment gap has been documented globally [9]. Patients with mental disorders contribute to this treatment gap as they are at increased risk of osteoporotic fractures [10–12], tend to demonstrate reduced compliance with medical care, and are often subject to undertreatment stemming from various systemic and individual-level barriers [13, 14]. Mental disorders have been associated with poor outcomes after hip fracture, outcomes that may be improved by AOM treatment [15]. However, guidelines for the treatment of hip fractures do not account for mental disorders; consequently, a “one-size-fits-all” approach is currently applied [16]. As a result, patients most vulnerable to poor outcomes after hip fracture may also be the least likely to receive appropriate AOM therapy. In 2021, the prevalence of mental disorders in the older Danish population (≥ 65 years) was 18–24% for women and 13–18% for men [17]. Both patient groups are expected to increase substantially due to the aging population, shared risk factors, and the gap between patients needing and receiving treatment [9, 18–20]. Thus, identifying vulnerable groups that could benefit from stratified care is essential to closing the documented treatment gap in secondary prevention of osteoporotic fractures. The impact of mental disorders on AOM initiation after hip fracture is unknown.

Purpose

To evaluate the impact of mental disorders on the initiation of AOM after hip fracture.

Methods

Setting

This population-based cohort study was conducted in a Danish setting with free access to healthcare but with a limited patient out-of-pocket payment for prescribed medications. All data used in this study come from national registries, presented below, and were linked by a unique person identifier (CPR number) provided by the Civil Registration System (CRS) which is given to all citizens by birth or migration and incorporated in all national registries [21]. A brief description of the registries can be found in supplementary materials.

Study population

We included all patients with a hip fracture (diagnosis codes according to the International Classification of Disease 10th Revision (ICD-10) S72.0-S72.2) aged ≥ 65 who underwent surgery (NOMESCO Classification of Surgical Procedures codes NFB.0–99 or NFJ.4–9) as registered in the Danish Multidisciplinary Hip Fracture Registry (DMHFR) [22]. The inclusion period was restricted to January 1 st, 2010–December 31 st, 2020, to include patients with the possibility of 1 year’s follow-up and only include more recent years to make the findings more relevant for current management of patients with hip fracture. Since we were interested in initiating AOM after hip fracture, patients with previous use of any AOM in the year prior to hip fracture were excluded leading to a final study population of 57,887 patients (Supplementary table S1).

Exposure—mental disorders

Specific mental disorders, organic, substance use, schizophrenic, mood, and anxiety disorders, were identified through ICD-10 diagnosis codes in the Danish Psychiatric Central Research Register and the Danish National Patient Registry [23, 24]. Definition of mental disorder by ICD-10 was guided by several publications within psychiatry [25–29]. For each individual, the history of each specific mental disorder was evaluated based on the date of specific diagnosis/hospital contact. Since mental disorders often co-occur [25] and it is not clear which one occurred as the first one, patients were located into more than one exposure group. The ICD-10 codes for specific diagnoses were collected in a 10-year lookback period prior to the hip fracture to ensure both recent contacts at the same time as attempting to account for the comorbid burden of mental disorder. Patients without any of the specific mental disorders were classified as no mental disorder.

Outcome—initiation of AOM

Initiation of AOM was defined as the proportion of patients receiving at least one treatment code for any AOM or receiving at least one dispensation of any AOM in the first year after hip fracture surgery. Thus, the date of either the hospital code or the prescription, whichever came first, was used as the date of initiation. AOM treatment codes in-hospital were collected using the Danish National Patient Registry (supplementary, Table S2). AOM treatment with dispensation was collected from the Danish National Prescription Registry [26] using Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification (ATC) system codes (supplementary, Table S2). The Danish National Prescription Registry captures only dispensed medication, thus those collected by the patients at pharmacies, rather than issued.

Covariates

The relevant covariates were predefined based on existing knowledge and a priori drawings of directive acyclic graphs. Age, sex, and cohabitation were collected from the CRS, along with information on emigration and death [21].

The Danish National Patient Registry [24] was used to collect information on a 10-year preoperative history of major osteoporotic fractures (MOF) and chronic somatic disease groups defined as the number of eight major categories (cardiovascular, hepatic/gastrointestinal, malignancy, metabolic, musculoskeletal, neurological, pulmonary, and renal/hematological) based on the 27 most prevalent diseases in the population cf. previous literature [4, 27].

Patients’ living arrangement was combined from DMHFR (data from 2016 to 2020) and the CRS (data from 2010 to 2020) and categorized as cohabitating in their own home, living alone in their own home, or living in a nursing home/temporary facility.

The highest obtained educational level was retrieved from the Danish education register [28] at Statistics Denmark. Due to the age of patients and structural changes in the twentieth century with different meanings of an educational level, it is dichotomized as primary or more than primary education.

Pre-fracture mobility measured with the Cumulated Ambulation Score (CAS) and surgery-related variables such as surgery year, fracture type, and length of hospital stay were retrieved from the DMHFR.

Statistics

Patient characteristics by mental disorder are presented with numbers (n) and percentages (%) for categorical variables and with median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous values after controlling for distributions. Cumulative incidence functions (CIF) with death as a competing risk were calculated by the Aalen–Johansen method for initiation of AOM within 3 months and 1 year after the hip fracture. All estimates are presented in percent with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

For the main analyses, the specific mental disorders were analyzed separately, thus the same patients can appear in several groups depending on the number of groups they were allocated to from registered ICD-10 codes in the 10-year lookback period.

Analyses were stratified by age (≥ 80 or < 80 years), sex, previous MOF, hospital contact due to mental disorder within 1 year prior to hip fracture, mental- and somatic comorbidity burden, living arrangement, and calendar year (supplementary, Table S3). Cox proportional models were used to calculate cause-specific hazard ratios (HR) adjusted for sex, age, and calendar year (supplementary, Table S4) and assumptions were controlled by log-minus-log plots for each compared exposure group. All analyses were performed with the statistical software R version 4.4.1 using the Survival and Crmpsk packages for CIF results. Cause-specific HRs and model control were calculated by the Survival and survfit packages.

Ethics

In Denmark, registry-based studies do not require ethical committee approval, but the study was reported to the Danish Data Protection Agency through registration at Aarhus University (record number: AU-2016-051−00000, sequential number 880). The manuscript follows the guidelines for cohort studies described in Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE). Large language models, Microsoft Copilot and ChatGPT were employed to assist with language refinement and optimization of sentence structure for selected parts of the manuscript in the initial draft of the paper, as well as assisting in adapting and generating relevant R code for parts of the statistical analysis.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

We identified 14,042 (24%) patients with at least one mental disorder. The study population included those with no mental disorder (N = 43,845), organic mental disorders (N = 8260), substance use (N = 2704), schizophrenic (N = 824), mood (N = 4547), and anxiety disorders (N = 1660) (Table 1). Compared with no mental disorder, patients with a history of substance use were younger (median age 73 vs 83 years) whereas patients with organic mental disorders were older (median age 86 years). The sex distribution was similar in all groups except substance use, with more male patients. All groups had high levels of somatic comorbidity, with spikes in patients with substance use, mood, and anxiety disorders. Schizophrenic, mood, and anxiety disorders also tended to have more mental comorbidity (Table 1).

Initiation of AOM

The cumulative incidence of initiation of AOM was 20.8% for patients with no mental disorder (Fig. 1, Table 2). About 80% of initiation of AOM was defined from the pharmacy dispensation codes while 20% was defined from hospital codes, similarly across groups of patients with and without mental disorders (supplementary, Table S5). Discussion

Key findings

The 1-year AOM initiation varied widely, ranging from 6.5 to 17.0% for patients with different mental disorders, compared to 20.8% for patients without mental disorders. The initiation was lower for organic mental disorders and schizophrenic disorders across all stratified analyses.

Comparison with other studies and clinical implications

Organic disorders have been associated with non-initiation of AOM after hip fracture in several countries [29–31]. Part of the low initiation can be explained by an expected high cumulative mortality also indicated by a high proportion of patients with a low preoperative cumulated ambulation score, higher age, and living in nursing homes. This expected higher mortality may reduce the need and interest in reducing fracture risk in the years to come.

Patients with schizophrenic disorders might represent a socioeconomic vulnerable group which is also reflected by more patients in nursing homes and living alone. Their educational level also tends to be less compared to the other groups. The same tendency with low treatment initiation after hip fracture in this group is also seen in a Canadian study [32]. The age stratification showed largest difference in AOM initiation in no mental disorder and substance use, which could be expected as these patients were younger, had less comorbidity, live longer, and could have more benefit of treatment than older patients with more progressive disease such as dementia. Compared with other studies based on Danish patients with hip fracture, we find similar low treatment initiation rates regardless of inclusion of hospital data [33–35].

The tendency to higher initiation incidence with more recent calendar years is also found in other Danish studies. However, the tendency to lower initiation in patients with mental disorder found in this study remained similar over the years. There is no evidence of structural or practice changes that would specifically address the underlying causes of the treatment gap. Although patients with mental disorders have increased risk of osteoporosis, the results from this study indicate that they were less likely to receive treatment, underscoring a significant treatment gap in secondary prevention in this vulnerable population.

The ultimate goal of initiating AOM therapy is to prevent subsequent fractures in the population as the fractures would lead to markedly increased mortality and lower quality of life for the patients and additional societal costs [36, 37]. As initiation of AOM within the first year has shown the potential to decrease the risk of subsequent fractures up to 10 years after the index fracture [38] and most subsequent fractures happen within the first 2 years after the incident hip fracture [39], the treatment gap is considered very high despite the mortality rates for this population.

The pattern of lower initiation seen in patients coming from nursing homes might be a proxy for the patient’s overall physical status and age, including that many patients live there at the end of life. This can also explain the lower initiation in patients with more somatic comorbidity, indicating both frailty but also potential contra-indications for treatment. However, living alone in own residence also lowered the initiation compared to being cohabiting. This could indicate the importance of a surrounding network for the elderly population to initiate post fracture treatment.

The general 1-year survival (supplementary Figure1) across all groups is high enough to consider initiating treatment within the first year after hip fracture at a higher rate than observed in this study. Thus, this study highlights a persistent gap in secondary fracture prevention practice, especially in vulnerable populations which might be the patients that need the treatment the most. The results contribute with insights that may inform strategies for post-hip fracture care with the potential to support the development of stratified, patient-centered preventive interventions aimed at reducing future fracture risk. A new study with a published abstract from the 21 st Congress of the European Geriatric Medicine Society 2025 on the utilization of zoledronate at Danish hospitals with data from 2015 to 2023 shows a slight increase from 2020 to 2023. However, the large treatment gap in patients with hip fracture remains in 2023, as only ~10% of patients received IV zoledronate within 90 days of surgery.

This study highlights the potential value of initiating AOM during the hospital stay prior to discharge after hip fracture to improve treatment rates in this population, which is in line with recent international consensus [40]. However, the findings also emphasize the need for a greater focus on treatment initiation after discharge outside the hospital.

Strengths and limitations

In Denmark, all patients with hip fractures are treated surgically regardless of age and medical conditions, minimizing selection bias. The use of national registry-based data enables a detailed description of the study population and minimal information bias as data were recorded in the national registers continuously as events occurred, independent of the present research question. We had complete follow-up of all patients. The validity of the hip fracture codes from DMHFR is above 90% [41].

Of limitations, first, the Danish National Prescription Registry [26] captures only dispensed medication, thus those collected by the patients at pharmacies, rather than issued prescriptions. It has been shown however that about 85% of issued prescriptions are dispensed in the metabolic diseases area [42]. The completeness of hospital treatment codes in the Danish National Patient Registry is unknown. However, the treatment codes are a part of the Diagnosis Related Grouping system, which is employed in Denmark in the context of healthcare financing. Thus, the incitement for registering treatment is considered high. In addition, a high agreement between self-reported and prescription-captured data on AOM was shown previously [43].

The validity for the ICD-10 codes for mental disorders is generally high, in particular for disorders with clear diagnostic criteria. Since codes for mental disorders are recorded when events occur and independently of the hip fracture and AOM codes, the risk of information bias is minimal [23, 24, 26].

Second, the consequence of restricting the definition of mental disorder to ICD-10 codes and not including pharmacy prescriptions of medicines can misclassify some mild cases treated at their general practitioner as no mental disorder and less mental comorbidity in the other groups. However, relatively prevalent off-label use of the medications would introduce new misclassification problems. The focus of this study was to investigate moderate-severe cases and thus the ICD-10 codes were chosen. Lastly, the consequence of patients being included in several strata means that a direct comparison between groups in absolute numbers is violating the underlying statistical assumption of independent observations. Therefore, in the supplementary table S4, cause-specific HRs with comparison of each group of mental disorder compared to no mental disorder are provided.

Conclusion

AOM initiation following hip fracture remains suboptimal. Patients with mental disorders were less likely to receive post-hip fracture AOM treatment, highlighting a persistent gap in secondary fracture prevention among vulnerable populations.
