A general framework for invasion cycles in ecology
Authors: Violeta Calleja‐Solanas, Rafael O. Moura, José A. Langa, José R. Portillo, Fernando Soler‐Toscano, Oscar Godoy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTheory predicts that indirect interactions in ecological networks sustain species diversity through oscillatory dynamics. However, a framework linking interaction structure to the presence, type, and complexity of these cycles is lacking. Here, we develop an analytical toolbox combining invasion graphs with a mathematical decomposition of interaction matrices into symmetric and antisymmetric components. We find that invasion cycles—closed loops of species invasions—are suppressed when symmetric interactions dominate, reflecting strong self‐limitation. Conversely, antisymmetric dominance, indicating competitive asymmetries, leads to the well‐known cycles of single‐species invasion such as rock‐paper‐scissors as well as novel multispecies invasion patterns, in which several species simultaneously invade each transition of the cycle. As asymmetries increase, more complex cycles involving both sequential and simultaneous invasions emerge. Yet this potential for cycles is suppressed as variability in intrinsic growth rates increases. Our work clarifies when interactions drive cycles and introduces a simple ratio that assesses symmetric versus antisymmetric contributions in the interaction matrix, constraining cycle emergence and the number of species they can sustain.

INTRODUCTION

Ecologists continue to debate whether the vast biodiversity we observe in nature is maintained through mechanisms that operate between pairs of species, such as differences in phenology, natural enemies, and environmental preferences, or whether additional contributions from indirect effects within ecological networks are necessary (Bimler et al., 2024; Gallien et al., 2017; Godoy et al., 2017; Levine et al., 2017; Pajares‐Murgó et al., 2024; Ranjan et al., 2024; Soliveres et al., 2015). Understanding these different mechanisms is critical for predicting the assembly and dynamics of species within communities (Wootton, 1994). At one extreme, pairwise mechanisms promote a hierarchical structure of community assembly where species can coexist (Chesson, 2000; Godoy et al., 2024; Hofbauer & Schreiber, 2022) (Figure 1a). At the other extreme, chains of indirect interactions break such hierarchy and can lead to the emergence of cycles (May & Leonard, 1975; Serván & Allesina, 2021) (Figure 1b).

Although this debate arises primarily from differences in environmental settings, taxa, and methodologies, there is a broad consensus on the importance of the structure of species interactions (i.e., the interaction matrix) for modulating contrasted ecological dynamics (Godoy et al., 2017; McCann et al., 1998; Soliveres & Allan, 2018; Tylianakis et al., 2010). However, we still do not fully understand how to map the strength and signs of species interactions onto the cycles that can occur in nature. This lack of knowledge mostly arises because the oscillatory changes in species abundances can have different origins, such as limit cycles (Gilpin, 1975; Zeeman, 1993), invasion cycles (cycles arising via invasions or migration and local extinction of species that dynamically are heteroclinic cycles, as in Hofbauer & Schreiber, 2022; Krupa, 1997; Krupa & Melbourne, 1995; May & Leonard, 1975; Song, 2025; Vandermeer, 2025), or stochastic processes and noisy measurements; and these kind of cycles have been studied separately. In addition, the study of cycles is usually restricted to the most basic configuration where no universal superior competitor exists in the interaction matrix and just one species invades at a time (but see Kirlinger, 1989; Schreiber & Rittenhouse, 2004). This configuration can lead, among other dynamics (see May & Leonard, 1975), to the well‐studied intransitive case of the rock‐paper‐scissors, where one species becomes nearly dominant, then declines as another invades, creating oscillations in abundances (Alcántara et al., 2017; Allesina & Levine, 2011; Benincà et al., 2015; Calleja‐Solanas et al., 2022; Laird & Schamp, 2006; Matías et al., 2018; Rojas‐Echenique & Allesina, 2011; Vandermeer, 2013). Besides intransitivity, we do not systematically know which structure of species interactions can lead to other more complex cycles, such as multispecies invasions (Hofbauer, 1994; Schreiber, 1998). Moreover, beyond these theoretical underexplored possibilities, empirical observations indicate that cycles appear to be rare in nature (Friedman et al., 2017; Pajares‐Murgó et al., 2024). Coexistence can arise without cyclic invasions; for instance, microbial consortia can display emergent coexistence driven by strong, context‐dependent interactions rather than cyclic dominance (Chang et al., 2023). This pattern is consistent with common biological constraints—strong self‐regulation, asymmetric interaction strengths, trait or environmental filtering that lowers effective intrinsic growth rates—that collectively bias communities toward acyclic assembly. Thanks to the continuous effort from the 1990s of conceptualization of assembly graphs (Hofbauer, 1994; Law & Morton, 1993, 1996; Morton et al., 1996) to the present day drive to integrate community assembly and coexistence (Godoy et al., 2024; Song, 2025; Spaak & Schreiber, 2023), the time is ripe to combine mathematical tools with computational modeling to investigate how invasion cycles generally emerge.

To address these limitations, we use an approach borrowed from mathematics, yet to be exploited in ecology. That is, focusing on Volterra‐Lyapunov stable interaction matrices and decomposing them into their symmetric and antisymmetric components (Bortolan et al., 2025; Meyer, 2023). We focus on these matrices because their invasion graphs are complete: invasion cycles show a direct correspondence between a cycle in the interaction matrix, a cycle in the invasion graph, and cyclic dynamics in the temporal behavior of the system. Under these matrices, any cycle in the invasion graph represents a genuine sequence of equilibrium‐to‐equilibrium invasions. Briefly, the symmetric component captures the baseline strength of interactions within the system. It captures whether community assembly is modulated by self‐limiting effects or by a competitive hierarchy excluding inferior competitors. On the other hand, the antisymmetric component captures the asymmetries in interaction strength among species, which may lead to the absence of hierarchy and then of a universal best competitor. Because the symmetric and antisymmetric components of the matrix align well with existing ecological knowledge, they allow making clear predictions about how they contribute to the emergence of cycles. In particular, in ecological communities with strong self‐limiting effects (Gallien et al., 2024; Matías et al., 2018; Pajares‐Murgó et al., 2024; Ulrich et al., 2018), we predict that the likelihood of cycles is low. This is because the symmetric component of the interaction matrix (that contains the intraspecific interactions) dominates, while the antisymmetric component remains negligible. Meanwhile, in ecological communities with weak self‐limiting effects, and therefore, lesser importance of the symmetric component of the interaction matrix, we predict that the emergence of cycles depends on whether species exhibit asymmetries in their interaction strength (i.e., no competitive hierarchy). If species present asymmetries in interaction strengths (weak versus strong) and signs (positive versus negative), the presence of cycles would be widespread. Some examples include marine food webs, insect communities, or plant communities in stressful and productive environments (Benincà et al., 2015; Bimler et al., 2024; Neutel et al., 2002; Soliveres et al., 2015; Vandermeer & Perfecto, 2023).

When studying cycles, it is crucial to recognize that, alongside the interaction matrix, differences in intrinsic growth rates between species are also key to defining the dynamics of ecological systems. The inherent ability of species to grow significantly influences their ability to recover from low density, withstand environmental variability, and ultimately, the coexistence of entire communities (Chesson, 2000; Gallien et al., 2017). For analytical tractability, several classic theoretical studies initially examined cycles under simplifying assumptions such as equal intrinsic growth rates across species (e.g., Laird & Schamp, 2006; May & Leonard, 1975). More recent theoretical work and empirical evidence suggest that cycles may be more likely when variability in intrinsic growth rates is low, that is, when species' innate demographic potential is relatively similar, (Gallien et al., 2018; Godoy et al., 2017; Matías et al., 2018; Ranjan et al., 2024; Soliveres et al., 2015). These findings motivate the prediction that the likelihood of cycles decreases as differences in intrinsic growth rates increase. However, a systematic assessment of this prediction in combination with the interaction matrix is currently lacking.

Here, we introduce a framework capable of predicting the presence of invasion cycles of any length and number of species between transitions. We believe this framework is paramount to enriching our understanding of the prevalence of cycles in nature and predicting assembly structures that have not yet been explored. Additionally, focusing on the assembly of communities through invasion graphs has the benefit of incorporating the combined effects of species' intrinsic growth rates and interactions matrix on community dynamics (Almaraz et al., 2024; Hofbauer & Schreiber, 2022; Serván & Allesina, 2021). Briefly, an invasion graph illustrates all possible transitions among subcommunities (i.e., subsets of species that coexist, a stationary solution of our model) due to single or multispecies invasions until the community reaches a final noninvasible community. These subcommunities, represented by nodes, are depicted at different levels according to their number of species (Figure 1).

By examining the conditions under which cycles arise in the invasion graph—considering the ratio of symmetry to asymmetry in interactions and the variation in intrinsic growth rates—we discover three main groups of cyclic structures: (1) Level‐1 cycles or cycles of subcommunities of a single species, that is, generalizations of the rock‐paper‐scissors dynamics for any number of species (Figure 1a). (2) Level‐ ℓ cycles, which are composed of subcommunities of ℓ species and allow for simultaneous invasions of more than one species. (3) Multi‐level cycles in which the subcommunities are composed of a varying number of species. Beyond these three categories, we found that cycles are more likely to occur when the antisymmetric component dominates the interaction matrix and species show similar intrinsic growth rates, regardless of community richness. Our results open the discussion of the ecological constraints, both internal (i.e., species interactions) and external (i.e., environmental conditions), on cycles in natural systems.

METHODS

The core of our analysis uses invasion graphs to study cycles in different structures of community assembly (Hofbauer & Schreiber, 2022; Serván & Allesina, 2021). The nodes of an invasion graph are subsets of coexisting species, technically known as subcommunities or admissible communities (e.g., 2,3 is the subcommunity composed of species 2 and 3 in Figure 1a). Nodes are connected by directed links when there is a potential transition due to invasions by missing species (e.g., the arrow from 2,3 to 1,2,3 in Figure 1a means that species 1 can successfully invade the subcommunity 2,3). Note that invasion graphs, as defined in Hofbauer and Schreiber (2022), allow for simultaneous invasions of multiple species (such as the link from 1 to 1,2,3 in Figure 1a). They are not equivalent to assembly graphs (e.g., in Serván & Allesina, 2021; Song, 2025), which traditionally only consider single‐species invasions.

Within the framework of invasion graphs, a cycle is defined as a closed loop that represents sequences of species invasions that eventually return to the initial subcommunity, generating oscillations in species abundances. This sequence of invasions is encoded directly in the topology of the invasion graph (red lines in Figure 1b) and reflects temporal turnover driven by species arrivals. Specifying this definition is important as the term “cycle” refers to different concepts in the ecological literature, particularly in studies of intransitivity and community assembly. Periodic orbits in the phase space, such as limit cycles, are also plainly referred to as cycles. They also generate oscillatory population dynamics, but they are not driven by sequential invasions. They instead emerge from intrinsic population dynamics within a subcommunity. Invasion cycles are the only ones that show a direct correspondence between a cycle in the interaction matrix, a cycle in the invasion graph, and cyclic dynamics in the temporal behavior of the system.

We model the dynamics of n species through the generalized Lotka–Volterra equations, where each species can interact positively or negatively. This set of nonlinear differential equations describes the changes in the density xi of species i over time as: (1) dxidt=xiri+∑j=1nAijxj

The vector r=ri of intrinsic growth rates denotes how the species would grow isolated under given abiotic conditions. In turn, interactions among species are phenomenologically captured by the n×n interaction matrix A. Its diagonal elements Aii represent intraspecific interactions, that is, how a species limits itself. Interspecific interactions are captured in Aij, the effect of species j on the per capita growth rate of species i. The interaction matrix can be expressed as A=AS+AJ, the sum of a symmetric AS and antisymmetric AJ components, where AS=A+AT2 and AS=AS⊤, AJ=A−AT2 and AJ=−AJ⊤. To analyze how an interaction matrix A determines the presence of cycles, we compute the asymmetry ratio: (2) AsymA=AJAS, defined as the ratio between the magnitude (strength) of the entries of the antisymmetric AJ (3) AJ=∑i,j∣AJij∣, and symmetric (4) AS=∑i,j∣ASij∣, components of the interaction matrix. Such decomposition covers a wide range of ecological phenomena: when intraspecific competition dominates, that is, large AS as in diagonally dominant matrices, Equation (6), communities stabilize in the absence of cycles. On the other hand, systems with greater asymmetry of interactions, that is, larger AJ, create directional imbalances that can promote complex cyclic behavior.

The framework is flexible enough to explicitly describe the structure of all connections between subcommunities, but we restrict our study to Volterra‐Lyapunov stable (VL‐stable) matrices A (Figure 1b). A matrix is VL‐stable if there exists a matrix H=diag(h1,…,hn) with hi>0 such that (5) A=HS+J, with S a symmetric and negative definite (i.e., stable) matrix and J an antisymmetric matrix (Bortolan et al., 2025). There are three reasons for this choice. The practical reason is that the links in the invasion graph are indeed connections in the phase space of the Lotka–Volterra system (Almaraz et al., 2024) when matrices meet the condition of Volterra‐Lyapunov stability, Equation (5). Secondly, the spectrum of interaction matrices that are Volterra‐Lyapunov stable is broad. From an ecological perspective, there is no loss of generality in focusing on these matrices, just as there is typically none in assuming matrices are invertible. Thirdly and critically, VL‐stable matrices contain an ecologically relevant subset of matrices, Diagonally Dominant (DD) matrices, which satisfy (Figure 1a): (6) |Aii|−∑j≠i|Aij|≥0,∀i

In a DD matrix, self‐regulation plays a stronger role than interspecific competition, which in ecology is related to stabilizing coexistence (Adler et al., 2018; Daniel et al., 2024; Saavedra et al., 2017). The DD matrices always have an asymmetry ratio of less than one by definition. Mathematically, diagonal dominance can also be defined by columns: (7) |Aii|−∑j≠i|Aji|≥0,∀i

Both definitions are closely tied to competition asymmetry, as species with stronger self‐limitation are less likely to exclude others, promoting coexistence and reducing the dominance of a single fast‐growing competitor (Daniel et al., 2024; Levine & HilleRisLambers, 2009).

To systematically explore the conditions under which invasion cycles emerge before testing empirical matrices, we coupled our theoretical framework with an algorithm recently developed (Bortolan et al., 2025). This method serves two main goals. First, it generates random VL‐stable matrices that allow exploring the invasion graph for any number of species (Appendix S1: Section S1). Second, it also generates interaction matrices tailored to include simultaneous invasions of one or more species (Appendix S1: Section S2), which expands beyond the traditional approach of considering cycles between single‐species equilibria (Gallien et al., 2017; May & Leonard, 1975; Song, 2025; Vandermeer, 2025). To create VL‐stable matrices, we first generated symmetric and antisymmetric synthetic matrices by drawing their elements independently from U−1,1 (allowing for positive and negative interactions) and then tuned their asymmetry ratio from 0 to 3 (Appendix S1: Section S1), obtaining a range of values depicted in Appendix S1: Figure S1. The variability of intrinsic growth rates was explored by sampling them as ri~U1−b,1+b with b∈0,1. Within that range, r is always positive, r¯=1, but allowing for negative r and a normal distribution does not change our conclusions (Appendix S1: Section S3).

The connection between the dynamical model and the invasion graph is made via the invasion growth rate. In general, the invasion growth rate of the species i on the subcommunity I is the average species per capita growth rate when rare. In particular, for the family of models in which the per capita growth can be expressed as a sum of an intrinsic growth and a linear density‐dependent term of interactions (like the generalized Lotka–Volterra or Ricker models), the invasion growth rate is defined as: (8) RiI=ri+∑j∈IAijxj*

If the subcommunity is empty, I=∅, we use the convention Ri∅=ri (Hofbauer & Schreiber, 2022). Since the connections between the nodes of an invasion graph are established via successful invasions, the graph contains a link from a subcommunity I to a subcommunity I′ if, and only if, I≠I′, and RjI>0 for every species j that is in I′ but not in I, and RiI′<0 for every species i that is in I but not in I′. This indicates that the conditions in I allow the invading species j to establish and persist. Conversely, for the transition to occur, any species that was in the initial subcommunity I but not in the new one I′ cannot invade it. Because the invasion graph is a directed graph, cycles emerge when a path of directed links starts and ends at the same node. The main benefit of using invasion graphs is that they define invasions considering both the interaction matrix A and intrinsic growth rates r. Therefore, invasion graphs are a powerful tool for studying the synergies between species interactions and performance in cycle formation.

Links in invasion graphs are defined by the signs of the invasion growth rates, that is, Equation (8), which are model‐dependent. In the Lotka–Volterra model, the sign of the invasion growth rate, and thus the invasion graph structure, does not change under common parameterizations: for example, dxidt=xiri+∑j=1nAijxj vs. dxidt=rixi+∑j=1nAijrixj. Comparing results across such formulations, however, requires the appropriate change of variables.

RESULTS

We show that the presence of cycles in ecological systems depends on the asymmetry ratio of the interaction matrix A. Following our predictions, the dominance of the antisymmetric component of interactions promotes many different types of cycles, while the preponderance of the symmetric component inhibits them (Figure 2). Therefore, the probability of cycles increases with the magnitude of the antisymmetric component AJ and decreases with the symmetric component AS. At one extreme, a community has a high probability of containing cycles when the symmetric component is weak compared with the antisymmetric component, especially for systems with high richness (curves in Figure 2 are steeper when n is larger). At the other extreme, such probability goes to zero when the antisymmetric component tends to zero, that is, when A becomes symmetric. There is a special case in which this probability decreases sharply at a ratio that we have computationally found to be AsymA=1. This particular case includes diagonally dominant matrices. This limitation of cycles is explained because the self‐limitation rates Aii are in the symmetric component of A, that is, the diagonal of AS is the diagonal of A, and the diagonal of AJ is zero (Figure 1a). In other words, when A is diagonally dominant, its symmetric component is always larger than its antisymmetric component, so DD matrices are located before the region where the asymmetry ratio equals one (proof in Appendix S1: Section S4). The mathematical proof describing why diagonally dominant matrices do not contain any type of cycle is currently unknown except in particular cases, for instance, when cycles are among subcommunities of one single species (Bortolan et al., 2025). However, numerical evidence suggests that diagonally dominant matrices contain no cycles (we observed no cycles in millions of simulations using DD matrices in Lotka–Volterra models —with both positive and negative intrinsic growth rates). This suggests that diagonally dominant matrices are fundamentally incompatible with the existence of cycles and that our results are robust.

Influence of the asymmetry ratio on the probability of cycles. For 20 values of the asymmetry ratio, defined in Equation (2), with a range in species richness, we randomly sampled 200 matrices A and calculated the associated invasion graphs for a total of 16.000 matrices. The probability of an invasion graph containing any cycle increases with the asymmetry ratio and with the species richness. That is, the greater the asymmetry ratio, the more likely it is to find a cycle; and for a fixed asymmetry ratio, the larger the potential number of species n, the more likely to find a cycle. Intrinsic growth rates are equal to 1.

Our results also show that when the antisymmetric component of the matrix dominates, we obtain three distinct classes of cycles (Figure 3). The first category (Figure 3a) encompasses a generalization of the rock‐paper‐scissors to any number of species. This generalization means that cyclic dynamics following the May‐Leonard case of sequential transitions (May & Leonard, 1975) of single species between subcommunities can be described for assemblages of any odd number, as previous work suggested (Allesina & Levine, 2011; Vandermeer, 2011). Nevertheless, this generalization also encompasses cycles with an even number of species. Overall, the first category Level‐1 cycles includes any assemblage of species that contains an invasion cycle of sequential single invasions of an arbitrary length. Such cycles can contain all species within the assemblage or only a subset of them (Figure 4). The other two categories correspond to cycles with more complex transitions beyond the traditional single‐species subcommunities. In more detail, the second category (Level‐ ℓ cycles) corresponds to cycles of single‐species invasions between states with ℓ>1 species. Figure 3b shows the rock‐paper‐scissors‐lizard‐Spock system, a generalization of the rock‐paper‐scissors for a richness of five species, where each subcommunity has three species sequentially invaded by one species at a time. Two examples of the literature include the level‐2 cycles of (Kirlinger, 1989; Schreiber & Rittenhouse, 2004), which study systems of two predators feeding on two prey. Finally, the cycles in the third category (Multi‐level cycles) can involve not only sequential but also simultaneous multispecies invasions, transitioning between subcommunities of a varying number of species. Figure 3c shows an example of a cycle transitioning between subcommunities composed of either one or two species. Finally, we also found that cycles composed solely of one‐species equilibria (the first category) are more common than cycles containing at least a subcommunity of ℓ<n species (the latter two categories in Figure 3), and that probability increases with the asymmetry ratio (Figure 5).

To study the effect of the antisymmetric component of the matrix in isolation, the values of the intrinsic growth rates (r) in Figures 2 and 5 were all taken equal to one. Nevertheless, as theoretical and experimental work has shown before, the probability of finding a cycle in the invasion graph also depends on the variation of the intrinsic growth rates. For our study, and in agreement with previous findings, we found that the probability of cycles is inversely proportional to the variability of r (Figure 6a). This means that cycles are more frequent when variation in intrinsic growth rates is minimal. Conversely, there are fewer cycles in an invasion graph when intrinsic growth rates are more variable, including both positive and negative values. When combining the effect of both the asymmetry ratio and the variation in intrinsic growth rates, we discovered that the highest probability of finding a cycle in an invasion graph corresponds to a low variation in intrinsic growth rates and a high asymmetry ratio (right bottom corner in Figure 6a). If we focus now on the complexity of the invasion graph—understood as the frondosity (Godoy et al., 2017), that is, how many nodes an invasion graph has compared with the potential number of n species combinations—we find an opposite pattern for the asymmetry ratio. This means that more symmetric interactions produce a larger number of subcommunities at equilibrium in the invasion graph, and therefore, enlarge the number of possible invasion paths to coexistence (Figure 6b). A lower variation of intrinsic growth rates also promotes more complexity of the invasion graph. Therefore, invasion graphs with more frondosity (i.e., with a larger number of different subcommunities) emerge under the combination of low asymmetry ratio and low variation in intrinsic growth rates.

DISCUSSION

Despite decades of research, ecologists still strongly debate the prevalence and mechanisms of cycles in nature (Adler et al., 2018; Bimler et al., 2024; Gallien et al., 2017; Godoy et al., 2017; Laird & Schamp, 2006; Soliveres & Allan, 2018). This debate is more broadly linked to the importance of mechanisms of biodiversity maintenance that operate between pairs of species versus indirect chains of interspecific interactions (Chesson, 2000; Gallien et al., 2017; Levine et al., 2017; May & Leonard, 1975). Here, we present a general framework that captures the essence of this discussion and show a metric that predicts the presence or absence of invasion cycles for any given ecological community composed of an arbitrary number of species. This metric, called asymmetry ratio, summarizes the balance between the symmetric and the antisymmetric components of a matrix containing the interaction strengths and signs within and between species. In more detail, our framework expects the presence of cycles when the antisymmetric component in the interaction matrix dominates, AsymA=AJ/AS>1, but restricts them when the symmetric component dominates, AsymA<1. In ecological terms, this means that cycles are absent when the assembly of a community is governed by stronger intraspecific than interspecific interactions. Alternatively, cycles are present when intraspecific interactions are overall weak, and there are strong asymmetries in interspecific interactions. This result is a novelty that completes the study of cycles and symmetric interactions. MacArthur (1969) found a Lyapunov function that excludes cycles in symmetric systems, but it had not been proven that asymmetrical interactions promoted cycles. Overall, our framework shows that the complexity of biotic interactions we observe in ecological communities can be summarized in a simple asymmetry ratio that predicts the presence of cycles (Figures 2 and 6).

The study of cycles in ecology has dealt with sequential changes in species dominance and hence in their abundance. Our study enlarges this current view by showing that these sequential dynamics are only a small part of the possible invasion structures. With larger species pools, invasion cycles can theoretically be much more complex (Schreiber, 1998; Schreiber & Rittenhouse, 2004), including cycles with multispecies invasions or varying species invasions (Figures 3, 4, 5). Whether this occurs in nature remains to be empirically described. A natural next step is therefore to revisit already available empirical datasets, such as plant communities interaction matrices derived from neighborhood experiments or observational models (Adler et al., 2018; Daniel et al., 2024; García‐Callejas et al., 2021). However, this requires translating the values of interaction coefficients into a common dynamical framework. Current empirical studies infer interaction coefficients using a heterogeneous array of population models (e.g., Lotka–Volterra, Ricker, Beverton–Holt, or hyperbolic equations) that are not dynamically equivalent, making the inferred interactions not directly comparable across studies. Moreover, because invasion graphs also depend on intrinsic growth rates, further progress requires empirical studies to explicitly estimate intrinsic growth rates alongside interaction matrices. Other systems with strong potential for testing these predictions include microbial communities, where recent work has suggested that complex cyclic assembly dynamics may occur (Song, 2025). If our predictions hold, they could also advance our understanding of the relationship between multispecies invasion cycles and community coalescence, a process in which entire microbial communities are replaced by others (Diaz‐Colunga et al., 2022; Rillig et al., 2015).

Our analysis goes beyond testing the canonical rock‐paper‐scissors motif by surveying the wider space of mathematically admissible cycles and asking which biological constraints suppress them. Several recurrent features systematically suppress the probability of cycles (Figure 6). This perspective aligns with findings from very different systems (Chang et al., 2023; Gallien et al., 2024; Godoy et al., 2017). In particular, our findings explain the rarity of cycles, based on two features commonly observed in ecological communities that are often coupled: intraspecific competition, and more generally, self‐limitation, stronger than interspecific competition (Adler et al., 2018; Levine & HilleRisLambers, 2009), and differences in species' intrinsic growth rates (Godoy et al., 2017; Ranjan et al., 2024). Seeing it from the opposite perspective, this coupling indicates that the presence of cycles and their frondosity (i.e., the number of nodes contained in the invasion graph) are constrained to ecological configurations governed by high interaction asymmetries and low variability in intrinsic growth rates. We also find computational evidence that species richness promotes cycles (Figure 2), generalizing previous results with only competitive matrices (Figure III.3. in Yodzis (1978)).

By evaluating both the structure of species interactions and the variability in intrinsic growth rates, we have identified which components are key to promoting cycles and to explaining their rarity, at least in plant communities (Friedman et al., 2017; Pajares‐Murgó et al., 2024). A few clarifications help situate these results. First, while our analyses consider a single trophic level, the asymmetry‐ratio framework readily extends to multitrophic systems and can be applied to empirical data to reveal how trophic structure reshapes the balance between symmetric and antisymmetric components. Furthermore, Spaak and Schreiber (2023) argued that cyclic invasion graphs pose challenges for the application of modern coexistence theory (MCT). In general, when internal dynamics of subcommunities are not at equilibrium, MCT permanence criteria become hard to apply. Our work, however, is complementary to MCT rather than conflicting with it. Instead of testing permanence, our goal is to map the combinations of interaction structures and intrinsic‐growth‐rate variation that make invasion cycles of all kinds possible. In this sense, our framework advances the study of community assembly by extending beyond the scope of MCT. Finally, restricting to Volterra–Lyapunov stable matrices guarantees that the invasion graph is complete, thereby easing the application of MCT. Here, “complete” means the graph includes every invasion transition: it has no false‐negatives (Schreiber (2025), Lemma 4.8) and no false‐positives (ruled out by VL‐stability). As a result, our graphs actually provide an estimate of cycle incidence relative to non‐VL‐stable matrices by avoiding missed transitions.

In sum, the emergence of cycles is more than the classic dichotomy regarding the presence or absence of a universal best competitor. Invasion cycles are related to a more nuanced perspective of the complementarity of processes governing species population dynamics. Rather than positing that our findings imply that cycles are irrelevant, here we refine where cycles should be most plausible: communities with comparatively symmetric cross‐limitation, weak diagonal dominance, and stable background conditions that reduce variability in growth rates. By mapping how common empirical constraints the emergence of invasion cycles, we propose an explanation for past null findings and delineate the concrete characteristics of communities where cyclic invasion dynamics could still be found but have not been searched. Therefore, this framework provides clear general expectations of when cycles should be rare in nature and the associated consequences for the assembly and dynamics of ecological communities.
