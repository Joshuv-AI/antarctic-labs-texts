A novel prognostic signature integrating disulfidptosis- and ferroptosis-related genes in acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Huihui Jiang, Li Liang, Tingting Liu, Zhe Zhao, Nana Wang, Jingtao Wang, Yuanyuan Hu, Dongmei Wang, Jingjing Ye, Fei Lu, Chunyan Ji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Acute myeloid leukemia is a highly heterogeneous hematopoietic malignancy, and we constructed a prognostic signature combining disulfidptosis-related genes and ferroptosis-related genes to predict the prognosis, immunotherapy response, and drug sensitivity of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) patients. The TCGA-LAML datasets underwent random partitioning into training and validation sets. Subsequently, a prognostic risk signature was formulated using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator algorithm. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and receiver operating characteristic curve analysis were employed to assess the clinical significance of the signature. The results of the immune infiltration difference analyses are displayed, and the drug sensitivity analyses were used to identify potentially effective drugs for AML patients. qPCR was employed to validate the expression levels of the signature genes and compared the signature with existing signatures and mutated genes. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses have underscored the signature as an autonomous prognostic risk determinant. Scrutiny into immune infiltration has unveiled significant associations: the risk score exhibits a favorable correlation with monocyte and M2 macrophage counts but an adverse correlation with resting mast cell counts. The expression patterns of immune checkpoint genes diverge between the distinct risk cohorts. Patients categorized as high-risk demonstrate enhanced benefits from cyclopamine, 443654, and 770041, whereas those classified as low-risk exhibit more pronounced advantages from cytarabine and AZD6244. The risk signature demonstrates superior prognostic accuracy compared to established signatures and mutated genes. In summary, our study may provide potential prognostic biomarkers and individualized precision therapy for AML patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10238-025-01670-7.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML), accounting for approximately 80% of adult leukemia cases, arises from malignant clonal hematopoietic stem cells driven by diverse genomic alterations [1, 2]. Despite recent advances in targeted therapies (e.g., antibody–drug conjugates and immune checkpoint inhibitors), relapse and refractory disease remain significant challenges due to AML’s biological heterogeneity [3–5]. Identifying novel prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets is therefore critical for advancing personalized precision medicine in AML.

Disulfidptosis, a novel regulated cell death mechanism recently identified by Liu et al. [6], is mechanistically distinct from classical apoptosis and ferroptosis [7]. This process is triggered under glucose-deprived conditions, where SLC7 A11-overexpressing cells exhibit excessive intracellular disulfide accumulation. Elevated SLC7 A11 levels promote pathological disulfide bond formation within the actin cytoskeleton, resulting in cytoskeletal contraction, disulfide stress, and ultimately rapid cell death [8]. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that SLC7 A11 overexpression drives tumorigenesis and progression in multiple malignancies [9–11], whereas its genetic depletion or pharmacological inhibition significantly suppresses tumor growth and metastatic potential. Furthermore, oxidative stress in cancer cells exacerbates disulfide metabolism dysregulation, critically impacting cellular survival and proliferation [12, 13]. Notably, disulfidptosis-related genes associated with lung adenocarcinoma, Alzheimer’s disease, and renal cell carcinoma have been identified as potential prognostic biomarkers [14–16]. However, the clinical significance of disulfidptosis in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) remains poorly characterized, necessitating further mechanistic and translational investigations.

Ferroptosis is characterized by iron-dependent accumulation of lipid peroxides, with its execution being tightly regulated by metabolic pathways governing phospholipid peroxidation and related cellular processes [17]. Mounting evidence underscores the pivotal role of ferroptosis in diverse pathological conditions, including cancer and neurodegenerative diseases. Notably, ferroptosis-related genes have been increasingly utilized to establish prognostic risk signatures across malignancies—for instance, in colorectal cancer [18, 19] and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [20]. Building upon these findings, we systematically integrated ferroptosis- and disulfidptosis-related genes to build a prognostic model to comprehensively evaluate their combined prognostic accuracy in AML.

Disulfidptosis and ferroptosis represent distinct yet interconnected modes of regulated cell death. Emerging evidence suggests that these pathways may synergistically influence tumor progression through shared metabolic vulnerabilities (e.g., redox imbalance, glutathione depletion) while targeting different cellular compartments. Integration of these death pathways identifies synthetic-lethal target pairs that transcend conventional prognostic models, offering a molecular scaffold for developing sequential therapeutic strategies tailored to AML’s dynamic clonal architecture.

In this study, we constructed a reliable and sensitive prognostic risk signature based on six disulfidptosis- and ferroptosis-related genes. A total of 130 samples of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) were randomly allocated into two groups: a training group and a validation group. Survival analysis using the K-M method showed that patients categorized as high-risk had a shorter overall survival. Univariate and multivariate analyses demonstrated that the six genes, identified through signature-based methods, represent an independent prognostic factor for patients with AML. The differences in drug sensitivity and the immune microenvironment between the high- and low-risk groups may provide new treatment methods for AML. The importance of combining ferroptosis with disulfidptosis for the treatment of AML was emphasized in this study and provides a new perspective for understanding the two modes of cell death.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

Transcriptomic profiles and corresponding clinical data for 151 acute myeloid leukemia (AML) samples, complemented by 444 normal tissue datasets from GTEx, were acquired from the UCSC Xena database (https://xenabrowser.net). After quality control, 130 AML cases with complete clinical annotations were retained for downstream analyses. The disulfidptosis-related gene set (n = 16) was curated from the seminal study by Liu et al., while ferroptosis-related genes (n = 259) were systematically extracted from the FerrDb repository (http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb/). As all datasets were derived from de-identified public resources, this study was exempt from institutional ethics committee review per applicable guidelines.

Identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

First, we matched the original mRNA expression data with the FRGs and DRGs and then performed a correlation analysis between FRGs and DRGs (| r |> 0.3 and p < 0.05), obtaining particular FRGs (pFRGs) highly correlated with DRGs. We employed the truncation criteria of |logFC|> 1 and FDR < 0.05 to identify the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between normal samples and AML samples using the “limma” R package.

Protein‒protein interaction (PPI) network

We submitted highly related pFRGs and DRGs to the STRING database (http://www.string-db.org/) to obtain PPI network information and modified it through Cytoscape software (3.9.0). We utilized the DEGs to generate the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network by submitting them to the STRING database.

Construction and validation of the prognostic signature

AML samples were randomly divided into a training dataset and a validation dataset using the “caret” R package with a ratio of “1:1”. Candidate genes for the prognostic signature in AML patients were screened using LASSO regression analysis. The risk score was calculated based on the expression level and correlation coefficient of the signature genes as follows: Risk score = (expression of a gene A*corresponding coefficient of gene A) + (expression of a gene B*corresponding coefficient of gene B) + (expression of a gene N*corresponding coefficient of gene n). Patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups based on their median risk score. K‒M survival analysis was performed to assess survival disparities between the high- and low-risk groups. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and time-dependent ROC curves were constructed to validate the prognostic precision of the risk signature.

Functional enrichment analysis

To deepen our understanding of the biological functions and signaling pathways associated with the candidate genes, we utilized the “ClusterProfiler” R package to conduct Gene Ontology (GO) analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis. The GO analysis covered molecular function (MF), biological process (BP), and cellular component (CC) terms. FDR and p < 0.05 were considered to indicate significant enrichment, and the results were visualized through the “ggplot2” R package.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

GSEA was utilized to investigate whether there were potential variances in biological processes or functional pathways among high- and low-risk patients. P values < 0.05 and FDR < 25% were considered to indicate statistical significance.

Development of a nomogram based on the risk score and clinical variables

We examined the association between the signature risk score and clinical factors and then developed a nomogram to predict the overall survival of 1-, 3-, and 5-year in AML patients. A calibration curve was employed to evaluate the predictive performance of the nomogram.

Immune cell infiltration and immune microenvironment analysis

Increasingly, studies have revealed a link between cell immune infiltration in tumor cells and the progression and prognosis of tumors. Therefore, we compared the differences in immune cell infiltration levels between the high-risk group and low-risk group by using the CIBERSORT, CIBERSORT-ABS, QUANTISEQ, MCP-counter, XCELL, and EPIC algorithms and further explored the correlation between immune cells and signature genes and risk scores. The integration of six complementary computational algorithms (CIBERSORT, CIBERSORT-ABS, XCELL, QUANTISEQ, EPIC, and MCP-counter) to evaluate immune infiltration was driven by the need to overcome limitations inherent to single-method analyses and to comprehensively characterize the tumor microenvironment (TME). The ESTIMATE algorithm was utilized to estimate the immune score and stromal score of AML patients. To assess the potential response to immune checkpoint blockade therapy, we investigated variations in the expression levels of key immune checkpoint molecules, including CD160, CD86, PDCD1, and LGALS9, between the high and low-risk groups.

Drug sensitivity analysis

We employed the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) (http://www.cancerrxgene.org) database to forecast drug sensitivity. This involved analyzing the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of drugs and investigating variances in drug sensitivity between high- and low-risk groups with the “pRRophic” R package.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA extraction from cells was performed using TRIzol reagent following specified protocols. Subsequently, Gene expression levels were determined via RT-PCR assays utilizing a Prime Script RT reagent kit (Tsingke, Beijing, China) and gene-specific primers listed in Supplementary Table S1. The relative gene expressions were adjusted based on GAPDH normalization, and relative expression analysis was conducted using the 2−ΔΔCTmethod.

Prognostic significance of the signature based on DRGs and FRGs in AML

The primary purpose of univariate and multivariate regression analysis is to evaluate whether the signature serve as an independent risk factor for predicting the prognosis of AML patients. Univariate Cox regression analysis revealed that risk score and age were risk factors for predicting survival in AML patients (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5A). Multivariate analysis similarly indicated a significant correlation among risk score, age, and the prognosis of AML patients (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5B). Construction of a nomogram to predict AML patient prognosis

Because the risk score is associated with AML patient prognosis, the nomogram assigns points based on the values of each variable (Sex, Age, Risk score), and summing these individual points yields a total score, which is then converted into the probability of clinical outcomes for individual patients. As shown in Fig. 7A, a representative patient’s total score corresponds to predicted probabilities of developing AML within 1 year (0.572), 3 years (0.253), and 5 years (0.134). The calibration curve indicated that the actual survival rate of the patients was consistent with the predicted results, confirming the excellent predictive performance of the nomogram (Fig. 7B).Fig. 7Construction the nomogram based on risk score, sex and age. To further explore additional prognostic value of the signature beyond existing markers, subgroup validation across key AML mutations revealed differential performance: TP53 wild-type (C-index 0.710, 95%CI 0.628–0.792; p < 0.05) versus mutant (0.667, 0.205–1.129; p = 0.479); FLT3 wild-type (0.684, 0.603–0.765; p < 0.05) versus mutant (0.857, 0.592–1.122; p < 0.05); NPM1 wild-type (0.687, 0.605–0.768; p < 0.05) versus mutant (0.917, 0.765–1.068; p < 0.05). This pattern confirms robust prognostic capacity across molecular subgroups except TP53-mutated cases, supporting its utility for precision risk stratification in AML management.

Difference analysis of the tumor microenvironment

The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a significant role in the development of AML. TME scores can provide valuable insights, which assess the abundance of immune cells and stromal cells. Utilizing the ESTIMATE algorithm, we obtained TME scores, including the ImmuneScore, StromalScore, and ESTIMATES scores, for the high- and low-risk groups. We observed a higher immune score in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk cohort, indicating a lower tumor purity in the high-risk group (Fig. 10F).

Discussion

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) represents one of the most prevalent hematological malignancies in adults, yet continues to pose significant therapeutic challenges. Despite recent advancements in targeted therapies and novel pharmaceutical agents, the 5-year survival rate remains alarmingly low at approximately 24% [24]. This clinical impasse has spurred investigations into alternative cell death mechanisms, particularly ferroptosis, which demonstrates remarkable potential in eradicating chemotherapy-resistant leukemic cells [25]. Intriguingly, emerging evidence highlights the therapeutic relevance of disulfidptosis in oncological contexts, as evidenced by recent breakthroughs in bladder cancer management [26]. Our pioneering study bridges two critical biological paradigms by integrating disulfidptosis-related genes (DRGs) with ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs), aiming to elucidate the molecular interplay driving AML pathogenesis. This innovative approach not only deepens our understanding of leukemogenesis but also unveils potential therapeutic vulnerabilities that could inform the development of next-generation treatment strategies.

Our study identified 29 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in AML compared to normal samples from the TCGA database, six genes (AIFM2, FTMT, MYB, SLC7 A11, PSAT1, and PRKAA2) were selected via LASSO Cox regression to construct a prognostic signature. This signature emerged as an independent predictor of AML outcomes in multivariate analysis and demonstrated robust predictive accuracy through ROC curves and nomogram calibration. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were significantly enriched in metabolism- and apoptosis-related pathways. Notably, monocytes have been shown to drive chronic inflammation and tumor progression, while M2 macrophages suppress antitumor immunity through the secretion of immunosuppressive factors, a mechanism that may underlie tumor immune evasion and therapeutic resistance. Consistent with our findings, mast cells may exert antitumor effects by releasing pro-inflammatory mediators to recruit CD4 +/CD8 + T cells and natural killer (NK) cells.

Immune infiltration analysis demonstrated that elevated risk scores were positively correlated with protumoral M2 macrophage and monocyte infiltration levels, but inversely associated with resting mast cell abundance, which may harbor antitumor potential. Strikingly, high-risk patients exhibited upregulated expression of immune checkpoint molecules (CD86, LGALS9, PDCD1) within the tumor microenvironment, suggesting that their poor prognosis is closely linked to an immunosuppressive milieu. These findings imply that immune checkpoint inhibitors may offer a more targeted therapeutic strategy for high-risk AML patients. Furthermore, the high-risk group displayed significantly higher immune scores and lower tumor purity, providing structural and biological validation for these conclusions. The genes expression of signature supported by experimental validation via qPCR. When compared with established prognostic signatures and existing mutated genes, our signatures exhibited superior performance, underscoring its clinical utility in risk stratification and personalized therapeutic planning for AML patients.

Emerging evidence highlights that iron overload exacerbates cellular dysfunction in AML through the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby compromising cellular homeostasis [27]. Notably, AML cells demonstrate a critical reliance on GPX4 and AIFM2 to counteract ferroptosis, with elevated expression of these proteins conferring a survival advantage. Clinical correlation analyses further revealed that patients with heightened GPX4 or AIFM2 expression exhibited significantly poorer overall survival outcomes compared to those with lower levels, underscoring the prognostic relevance of these molecular determinants in AML pathogenesis [28]. FTMT protected against erastin-induced ferroptosis in SH-SY5Y cells by sequestering mitochondrial iron, thereby preventing erastin-triggered labile iron pool (LIP) elevation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. This protective effect was further evidenced by the suppression of elastin-induced VDAC2/3 expression and NOX2 activation [29]. There is evidence to suggest that acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cell lines are more sensitive to the iron death inducer erastin compared to other tumor cell types [30]. Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) exhibits genetic heterogeneity, frequently driven by recurrent driver mutations or chromosomal rearrangements such as MLL gene fusions, which are linked to highly aggressive subtypes [31, 32]. MYB has been identified as a critical downstream mediator of MLL fusion oncoproteins, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic vulnerability in AML [33, 34]. PRKAA2 affects the metabolic reprogramming of malignant cells by coupling aerobic respiration with hypoxia-associated pathways. In addition, PRKAA2 facilitates immune escape of tumor cells by promoting CD8 + T-cell exhaustion and the formation of CD4 + Treg cells by reshaping the interaction between malignant cells and T cells and driving dynamic changes in T cells in the TME. Patients with high PRKAA2 expression may benefit from inhibitors of AMPK/AMPKα2 signaling [35]. The patterns of PRKAA2 expression can be used for developing personalized treatment approaches, contributing to the establishment of precision medicine for AML.PSAT1 impairs serine biosynthesis, TXNRD1 disrupts cysteine metabolism, and HRAS inhibits the xc-transporter-mediated cysteine/glutamate synthesis pathway, collectively reducing glutathione (GSH) production [36]. This leads to redox imbalance, enhanced lipid peroxidation, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, ultimately triggering ferroptosis [37]. The SLC7 A11-GSH-GPX4 axis is recognized as a key anti-ferroptosis mechanism in cancer cells [38]. Studies indicate that elevated SLC7 A11 expression correlates with adverse outcomes in AML, while its genetic or pharmacological suppression induces ferroptosis and impairs cell survival [39], highlighting cystine uptake blockade as a potential therapeutic strategy for AML. Furthermore, combining the anthracycline daunorubicin with sulfasalazine, an SLC7 A11 inhibitor, synergistically enhances ferroptosis [40].

Targeting specific ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs) or drug-response genes (DRGs) holds therapeutic potential for AML. For instance, lycorine suppresses B-ALL by downregulating PSAT1 to disrupt serine/glycine metabolism, suggesting its potential applicability in AML through metabolic interference [41]. MYB-targeting peptide mimetics, which disrupt MYB-cofactor interactions, may offer a novel strategy against MYB-dependent AML [42]. Additionally, erastin and sulfasalazine induce ferroptosis in AML via the SLC7 A11/xCT-GSH-GPX4 axis, highlighting this pathway as a actionable target [28]. While FTMT, AIFM2, and PRKAA2 remain understudied in leukemia, our findings provide foundational evidence for further exploration of these genes in targeted therapy development.

Nevertheless, several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, our conclusions are derived primarily from retrospective analysis of publicly available datasets, which inherently lacks integration with real-world clinical parameters and experimental validation of the identified prognostic mechanisms. To strengthen clinical translatability, future efforts should focus on validating these findings through prospective multicenter studies incorporating diverse AML cohorts, coupled with mechanistic investigations to elucidate the underlying biological drivers. Such rigorous validation in clinical practice settings will be crucial for establishing the signature's robustness and optimizing its utility for personalized patient management.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study identified signature-based FRGs and DRGs that might be related to the formation and progression of AML, and identifying signature genes might provide an effective strategy for treating AML patients.
