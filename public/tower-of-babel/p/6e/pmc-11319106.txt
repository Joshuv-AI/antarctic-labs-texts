Systemic Inflammatory Proteomic Biomarkers in Atopic Dermatitis: Exploring Potential Indicators for Disease Severity
Authors: Yu Ri Woo, Ji Hwan Moon, Ha Yeon Shin, Yoon Ji Bang, Seowon Song, Subin Lee, Dong Hun Lee, Hyun Je Kim, Jung Eun Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic inflammatory cutaneous disorder, that emerges from intricate interplays among genetic predisposition, immune dysregulation, environmental factors, and compromised skin barrier. Understanding the inflammatory pathway in AD is important due to its fundamental role in the pathogenesis of AD. This study aimed to explore the diverse spectrum of proteins linked to the inflammation of AD and the relationship between systemic biomarkers and clinical severity in AD.MethodsWe examined the blood samples from 48 patients with AD and 48 healthy controls (HCs) using the Proximity Extension Assay (Olink). Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified and Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to determine systemic proteomic biomarkers associated with severity of AD.ResultsA total of 29 DEPs were significantly up-regulated and 2 DEPs were significantly down-regulated in AD compared with the HC. The MCP-4, IL-18, MCP-3, TNFRSF9, and IL-17C were the top 5 highest DEPs associated with the severity of AD.ConclusionOur study sheds light on the intricate network of inflammatory proteins in AD and their potential implications for disease severity. Our results indicate that these systemic inflammatory proteins could be valuable for assessing AD severity and enhancing our understanding of the disease's complexity and its potential management strategies.Keywords: Atopic Dermatitis, Biomarker, Inflammation, Proteomics, Severity

INTRODUCTION

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic inflammatory skin condition influenced by genetic factors, immunological pathways, skin barrier dysfunction, microbes, and environmental triggers.1 Understanding the inflammatory processes is crucial for uncovering AD’s mechanisms and identifying potential biomarkers for disease severity. Aberrant immune response activation, particularly through the Th2 pathway and cytokines like IL-4 and IL-13, is a key factor in AD pathogenesis.2,3 Among the biomarkers, chemokine C-C motif ligand 17/thymus and activation-regulated chemokine CCL17/TARC has robust evidence correlating with AD clinical severity at both baseline and during therapy, acting as a chemoattractant for Th2 cells and reflecting disease activity and treatment response.4

Various studies have identified phenotypic differences in AD patients beyond the Th2 pathway, including age-related variations and distinct immune phenotypes among different ethnic groups.5,6,7 Different forms of AD, such as intrinsic versus extrinsic and pediatric versus adult, exhibit varied cytokine profiles.7,8 Despite exploration of individual markers in blood and skin,4 a comprehensive study encompassing both areas, particularly in a large cohort of East Asian patients, is still lacking.

Proteomics offers a robust method for identifying novel biomarkers and understanding complex diseases like AD.9,10 By analyzing systemic inflammatory biomarkers, this study aims to uncover insights into the systemic inflammatory response in AD. It seeks to establish correlations between systemic biomarkers and disease severity, potentially leading to the development of diagnostic and prognostic tools, and improving patient management and therapeutics in AD.

METHODS

Subject recruitment

A cohort of 48 adult individuals diagnosed with AD and an equal number of healthy controls (HCs) were enrolled in this investigation at Seoul National University Hospital and Eunpyeong St. Mary’s Hospital at the Catholic University of Korea. The AD was diagnosed based on the diagnostic criteria for Korean AD.11 The study's inclusion criteria allowed for participants of any race aged 18 to 90 years, excluding those who were pregnant or breastfeeding. They should not have a history or current diagnosis of other inflammatory skin conditions, autoimmune disorders, malignancy or acquired immunodeficiency syndrome. For the healthy control group, subjects with atopic comorbidities such as AD, allergic rhinitis, asthma, and allergic conjunctivitis were excluded. AD patients were assessed for disease severity using the Korean diagnostic guidelines, which utilize the eczema area and severity index (EASI) score.12 According to these guidelines, an EASI score of less than 16 indicates mild AD, a score between 16 and 23 indicates moderate AD, and a score of 23 or greater indicates severe AD.12

Sample collection and targeted proteomic analysis

Blood proteomics data was generated through the Olink (Uppsala, Sweden) platform, and the inflammation panel was utilized (Supplementary Table 1). Fresh blood samples were collected in heparin CPT tube (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and were centrifuged with 1,800 g for 20 minutes at room temperature. Plasma samples were stored at −80°C and thawed simultaneously at the time of experiment. Olink employs a metric known as Normalized Protein eXpression for protein normalization and quantification, which is a logarithmic value.

Data analyses

Statistical analyses and data visualization were carried out using Python version 3.11.3 (Python.org) and R Statistical Software (v4.2.3; R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).13 Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the PCA package in Python. The Seaborn package was used to perform hierarchical clustering and visualize the result with a heatmap. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified by comparing AD and HC samples for each protein using t-test and Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was used to control the false discovery rate of multiple hypothesis testing. A protein with an absolute log2 fold change bigger than 0.5 and adjusted P value smaller than 0.05 was considered a DEP. The DEPs were visualized by the ComplexHeatmap package in R. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was then performed using Enrichr to elucidate the biological significance of the DEPs.14

For correlation studies, we used Pearson correlation in two different ways. To identify the proteins that are possibly associated with the disease severity, we calculate the correlation between protein expression level and EASI score for each protein. The protein expression levels and EASI scores were separately normalized to a range between 0 and 1 to match the scale of two different data. To investigate the correlation between DEPs and the clinical parameters of AD, we calculated the all pairwise correlation of the set consisting of the DEPs and clinical parameters and carried out hierarchical clustering.

Ethics statement

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB number: 1908-031-1052 and 2108-123-1246) and Eunpyeong St. Mary’s Hospital at the Catholic University of Korea (IRB number: PC22SISI0076), and all subjects provided written informed consent.

DEPs of inflammation-related proteins in AD

The Olink proteomics platform was employed to assess 92 inflammatory proteins, utilizing the inflammation panel to distinguish expression variances between AD and HC. Distinct molecular variations in the serum of AD patients in comparison to HCs were demonstrated through heatmap and PCA (Fig. 1).

We identified 31 DEPs by using a log2 fold change of ≥ 0.5 and a P value of < 0.05 as criteria (Supplementary Table 2). In the AD cohort, 29 proteins, namely MCP-4, IL-13, EN-RAGE, ADA, 4E-BP1, STAMBP, MCP-3, IL-6, SIRT2, IL-10, TGF-alpha, TNFRSF9, CASP-8, TNFSF14, MMP-1, IL-18, CCL20, CXCL10, OSM, IL-17C, CCL4, VEGFA, CD5, AXIN1, HGF, CXCL11, IFN-gamma, CCL3, and IL-18, were found to be significantly upregulated compared to HCs (Fig. 2A and B). Conversely, IL-20RA and FGF5 were noted to be downregulated in the AD cohort compared to HCs. The heatmap of the identified DEPs across all samples was shown in Fig. 2C, providing a visual representation of the protein expression variations between the AD and HC groups.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis

To elucidate the biological significance of proteins linked to AD, a GSEA was conducted, utilizing GO terms. This analysis encompassed top 20 proteins identified as DEPs in this study. The 2023 GO analysis offers pivotal insights, particularly relevant to understanding AD, through its focus on molecular functions and biological processes. In terms of biological processes, monocyte chemotaxis (GO: 0002548) emerges as a significant focus, illustrating the movement of monocytes in response to diverse signals, which is essential for the inflammation and immune surveillance in AD (Fig. 3A). The inflammatory response (GO: 0006954) also features prominently, mirroring its pivotal role in the skin’s reaction to irritants and allergens typical in AD. Additionally, eosinophil chemotaxis (GO: 0048245) and eosinophil migration (GO: 0072677) are highlighted, underlining their importance in the skin’s inflammatory response associated with AD.

GO enrichment analysis of DEPs. The GO enrichment analysis highlights the most relevant (A) biological process and (B) molecular function terms associated with the DEPs.

GO = Gene Ontology, DEP = differentially expressed protein.

Prominent in the molecular function category of 2023 GO analysis is cytokine activity (GO: 0005125), highlighting its vital role in immune responses and cell signaling, key aspects in AD pathophysiology (Fig. 3B). The analysis also puts a spotlight on CCR chemokine receptor binding (GO: 0048020), chemokine activity (GO: 0008009) and chemokine receptor binding (GO: 0042379), crucial for deciphering the intricate interactions within the immune system and the cellular signaling pathways implicated in AD.

Correlation analysis between DEPs and the severity of AD

In our investigation into the connection between serum DEPs and AD severity, we conducted a detailed correlation analysis. This analysis revealed that MCP-3, IL-18, MCP-4, TNFRSF9, IL-17C, EN-RAGE, CD5, and IL-13 all showed a positive correlation with the severity of AD, indicating their potential as biomarkers for disease progression. Specifically, MCP-3 (R = 0.687, P < 0.001), IL-18 (R = 0.571, P < 0.001), MCP-4 (R = 0.567, P < 0.001), TNFRSF9 (R = 0.558, P < 0.001), and IL-17C (R = 0.436, P = 0.002) were among the biomarkers most strongly correlated with AD severity, as shown in Fig. 4.

In terms of age-related correlations, we found that CXCL10 (R = 0.592, P < 0.001), IL-10 (R = 0.561, P = 0.001), and IFN-gamma (R = 0.463, P = 0.009) positively correlated with the age of AD patients (Fig. 5).

Furthermore, our study highlighted a positive correlation between serum eosinophil count and proteins like MCP-3 (R = 0.726, P < 0.001), MCP-4 (R = 0.654, P < 0.001), IL-13 (R = 0.573, P < 0.001), IL-18 (R = 0.554, P = 0.001), EN-RAGE (R = 0.513, P = 0.003), and TNFRSF9 (R = 0.462, P = 0.009) (Fig. 5). Additionally, total IgE levels were positively correlated with CD5 (R = 0.513, P = 0.003), IL-18 (R = 0.482, P = 0.006), IL-13 (R = 0.473, P = 0.008), and TNFRSF9 (R = 0.458, P = 0.01) (Fig. 5).

DISCUSSION

Our study provides an in-depth exploration of the inflammatory biomarkers in AD, particularly serum DEPs and their correlation with disease severity and clinical parameters. By integrating demographic characteristics, proteomic profiling, GO enrichment, and correlation analyses, we offer a comprehensive understanding of the biological processes and molecular functions involved in AD.

Through proteomic analysis, we identified a set of proteins, specifically 29 upregulated and 2 downregulated, that distinctly separate AD patients from the HC group.

The upregulated proteins were Th2 cytokines (IL-13, IL-10, and TGF-alpha), Th1 cytokine (IFN-gamma, IL-18, and IL-8), Th17 cytokines (IL-6, and IL-17C), chemokines (MCP-4, CCL20, CXCL10, CCL4, CXCL11, MCP3, and CCL3), proteins involved in inflammatory response (EN-RAGE, TNFSF14, oncostatin M), cellular signaling and regulation (STAMBP, AXIN1, and 4E-BP1), in metabolism (ADA), in cell death (SIRT2, and CASP-8), and in tissue remodeling and angiogenesis (MMP-1, VEGFA, and HGF).

The correlation analysis in this study highlights MCP-4, IL-18, MCP-3, TNFRSF9, and IL-17C as the top 5 DEPs associated with AD severity. Their positive correlation with disease severity suggests their potential as biomarkers for monitoring disease progression.

MCP-3, a chemokine, recruits immune cells to sites of inflammation, contributing to skin inflammation in AD. Previous research supports the positive correlation between MCP-3 and AD severity.15 We found that Th2 chemokine MCP-4 levels are significantly increased in AD patients and correlate with disease severity. This aligns with studies on protein indicators in the blood of European-American AD patients.15,16,17 MCP-4 or CCL13 recruits eosinophils, monocytes, and lymphocytes, contributing to inflammation by attracting immune cells to the site.18 Our study also found a positive correlation between MCP-4 levels and eosinophils, suggesting that MCP-4 overexpression in AD may lead to eosinophil recruitment and persistence in the skin.

IL-18 is a cytokine that with diverse roles in AD, including promoting inflammation, modulating the Th1/Th2 cytokine balance,19,20 disrupting skin barrier,21 and exacerbating itchiness.20 It also induces IL-13 and/or IL-4 production by natural killer cells, mast cells, and basophils.15 A recent study found a positive correlation between lesional IL-18 levels, severity of AD, and transepidermal water loss.22 Additionally, IL-18 can stimulate other cytokines such as IFN-gamma and IL-13, perpetuating the inflammatory cycle in AD.23,24

TNFRSF9 (CD137 or 4-1BB) is crucial for T cell proliferation, survival, and differentiation, acting as a potent co-stimulatory molecule within the TNFRSF.25 It regulates immune balance through bidirectional signaling with its ligand CD137L, is involved in autoimmune diseases, activates cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, and aids in memory T cell formation.26 While TNFRSF9 is not explicitly linked to AD, its role in T cell activation and regulation suggests it could impact on AD pathogenesis or modulation, potentially influencing T helper cells involved in the disease.27

IL-17C, produced by keratinocytes, plays a role in amplifying inflammation in AD and psoriasis, potentially leading to epidermal hyperplasia.8,28,29,30 The IL-17C neutralizing antibody MOR 106 has shown efficacy in inhibiting Th2 and Th 17/22-driven inflammatory loops in both AD and psoriasis.31 Our findings also show that increased IL-17C levels in the serum correlated with the severity of AD, suggesting that IL-17C targeting therapies could be beneficial for AD.

Our research found a positive correlation between serum eosinophil count and proteins such as MCP-3, MCP-4, IL-13, IL-18, EN-RAGE, and TNFRSF9, which are key in AD-related immune processes. Eosinophils, essential in AD-associated allergic inflammation, show increased production, recruitment, and delayed apoptosis.32 MCP-3 and MCP-4 attract eosinophils to inflammation sites, while IL-18 may influence their activity and survival.33,34,35 EN-RAGE, part of the S100 protein family, is involved in inflammatory responses and likely supports eosinophil recruitment and activation.36,37 Though not fully understood, TNFRSF9-mediated T cell activation may also produce cytokines that attract or activate eosinophils.

The correlation between CD5, IL-18, IL-13, TNFRSF9, and total IgE levels in AD suggests a complex interplay of immune responses. CD5, expressed on T cells and a subset of B cells, may regulate B cells and IgE production.38 IL-13 is crucial for inducing IgE synthesis by B cells, and IL-18 can enhance IgE production.39,40 The link between TNFRSF9 and IgE levels suggests that T cell-mediated immune responses, influenced by TNFRSF9, promote B cell differentiation and IgE synthesis through cytokines like IL-4 and IL-13, which are key in stimulating IgE production.

The decreased levels of IL-20RA and FGF5 in AD patients compared to HCs offer new insights into their roles in AD pathogenesis. IL-20RA (IL20R1 or IL20RA), a receptor for IL-10 family, affects keratinocyte growth, differentiation, and the production of various chemokines, proteases, and growth factors.41,42,43 While IL-20 expression is elevated in AD skin,44 our study reveals reduced IL-20RA levels in serum, suggesting a potential feedback mechanism in keratinocyte function and immune response modulation in AD. FGF5 is a member of the FGF family, regulates cell growth, differentiation, and function.45 Studies on FGF-5 expression in AD skin are scarce, but disruptions in FGF pathways can impact skin barrier function and inflammation, which are crucial in AD. Mice lacking FGF receptors 1 and 2 in keratinocytes exhibited keratinocyte proliferation, dermal fibrosis, and inflammation, underscoring the importance of intact FGF signaling for skin health.46 Given AD's complexity, involving skin barrier dysfunction and immune response changes, FGFs may play a role in AD, but more research is needed to understand FGF-5’s specific involvement.

The GO enrichment analysis reveals insights into the biological processes and molecular functions involved in AD. Processes such as monocyte and eosinophil chemotaxis, and eosinophil migration, illustrate the dynamic cellular interactions in AD. Molecular functions like cytokine activity and chemokine receptor binding underscore the importance of these molecules in immune responses and cell signaling.

Our study focused on blood proteomics, but analyzing skin proteomics could offer a more localized view of inflammation and interactions in the skin. Previous research indicates that lesional AD skin shows higher levels of inflammatory proteins than blood, suggesting that skin proteomics reveals more inflammatory markers.10 Despite this, blood proteomics provides insights into systemic factors and is more convenient for large-scale and long-term studies. Future research should combine both approaches for a thorough understanding of AD.

The study’s main limitations include a small sample size from a homogeneous population, potentially limiting its representativeness for the broader AD population. The cross-sectional design hinders establishing causality between DEPs and AD. Lack of functional validation experiments and unaccounted confounding factors like environmental influences, patient lifestyle, and treatment history further constrain the study’s findings.

The lack of a significant difference in IL-4 levels aligns with other blood proteomics studies using Olink for AD.10,47 This might be due to IL-4’s transient expression, differences between mRNA and protein levels in blood, or assay limitations. Additionally, nine blood samples from severe AD patients were not washed out with immunosuppressants, which may have contributed to the decreased IL-4 serum levels. Future research should incorporate both blood and tissue samples, along with longitudinal analysis, to better capture IL-4’s dynamic expression in AD.

This study provides insights into the complex inflammatory profile of AD but acknowledges its limitations. Future research should address these limitations to enhance understanding and translate findings into clinical applications. Our analysis identified 31 DEPs in AD patients compared to HCs, revealing a complex interplay of cytokines and chemokines. These DEPs correlate with clinical severity, suggesting their potential as biomarkers for disease progression. This paves the way for targeted therapies and personalized medicine in AD, improving diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.
