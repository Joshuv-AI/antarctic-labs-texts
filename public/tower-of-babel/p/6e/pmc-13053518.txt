A Unique NCF2 Mutation in Chronic Granulomatous Disease: Clinical and Computational Insights into NADPH Oxidase Dysfunction
Authors: Hanyang Yu, Rurui Hu, Jianxin He, Xinlun Tian, Ting Zhang
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Chronic granulomatous disease (CGD) is a rare inherited immunodeficiency disorder resulting from defects in nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase complex. This enzymatic deficiency impairs the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) by phagocytes, compromising their ability to clear pathogens and cellular debris, thereby predisposing affected individuals to recurrent infections and granuloma formation [1–4]. Although more than 95% of cases are diagnosed by the age of 5 years, there are still certain patients diagnosed late in adulthood [5].

The NADPH oxidase complex consists of five subunits, gp91phox, p22phox, p47phox, p67phox and p40phox, encoded by CYBB, CYBA, NCF1, NCF2 and NCF4, respectively [6]. Mutations in any critical residues of these genes can be pathogenic, leading to CGD. While mutations in the X-linked CYBB gene account for the majority of CGD cases, NCF2 mutations are relatively rare (~ 7%), with most pathogenic variants resulting in absent or diminished p67phox expression [7]. Here, we present a case of an adult CGD patient carrying a homozygous p.Lys196del mutation in NCF2, which completely abolishes ROS production yet has previously been reported to retain normal p67phox expression. More importantly, we seek to investigate the molecular implications of this mutation by employing an advanced AI-driven protein structure predictor, AlphaFold, to gain deeper insights into its impact on NADPH oxidase function and disease pathogenesis.

A 20-year-old male initially presented to the emergency room (ER) with high fever, cough, and dyspnea following exposure to rain. Laboratory tests revealed leukocytosis with neutrophil predominance, along with elevated C-reactive protein (CRP, 293 mg/L) and procalcitonin levels. Chest CT imaging revealed extensive patchy infiltrates, bronchiectasis, nodules, and cavities lesions, predominantly affecting the right lung (Fig. 1A). The sputum culture at the ER identified Nocardia cyriacigeorgii, a gram-positive aerobic actinomycete commonly found in soil. However, despite targeted antibiotic treatment, no clinical improvement was observed.

To screen for additional infections, bronchoscopy was performed upon admission. Galactomannan (GM) tests were positive in both blood and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), indicating potential aspergillus infection. Meanwhile, interferon gamma release assay and acid-fast staining were negative in both blood and BALF, most likely ruling out tuberculosis. Therefore, the treatment regimen was adjusted to sulfamethoxazole, carbapenems, linezolid according to drug sensitivity results and empirical voriconazole was administrated for suspected fungal infection.

After a week, CRP level gradually declined and chest CT showed partial improvements in the absorption of the infiltrates (Fig. 1B). Fungal infection was also confirmed by successful isolation of Aspergillus fumigatus from patient’s sputum culture. However, despite continuing the same regimen for another week, the patient showed no further improvement, persisting with high fever and severe productive cough. CRP levels remained elevated at approximately 160 mg/L, and a repeat chest CT revealed newly developed consolidation over previous lesions, suggesting probable organizing pneumonia (Fig. 1C). In response, intravenous hydrocortisone (100 mg every 12 h) was administrated, leading to a rapid normalization of body temperature and a marked decrease in CRP level to 9 mg/L on the third day.

The patient had a medical history of recurrent pulmonary infections since infancy (starting at 3 months old) and a prior diagnosis of suspected inflammatory bowel disease via colonoscopy-guided biopsy 4 years ago, prompting us to consider the possibility of a primary immunodeficiency disorder. This time, whole exome sequencing identified a homozygous deletion in NCF2 gene (c.586_588delAAG: p.Lys196del), with both parents confirmed as heterozygous carriers. However, the parents denied consanguinity, and no similar symptoms were reported in other family members or relatives (Fig. 1F). To further assess the functional impact of this mutation, we performed a dihydrorhodamine (DHR) assay on neutrophils from the patient and his mother [8], revealing complete absence of ROS production in the patient’s neutrophils, whereas the mother’s neutrophils exhibited normal activity (Fig. 1G). The father’s DHR assay was not performed because he was not present when blood samples were collected; however, his heterozygous carrier status was confirmed genetically. These results support an autosomal recessive inheritance and confirm the diagnosis of CGD. Due to financial constraints, the family declined hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) and interferon-gamma (IFNγ) therapy. The treatment was subsequently adjusted to oral antibiotics and glucocorticoids after discharge. A follow-up chest CT at two months (Fig. 1D) and eight months (Fig. 1E) post-discharge demonstrated significant improvements in pulmonary lesions.

The p.Lys196del mutation was initially reported in NEJM [9] and was later included in mutation database for CGD [7], though it is absent in the ClinVar database. Interestingly, among over 100 distinct p67phox mutations, p.Lys196del is one of the few pathogenic variants previously shown in the literature to retain normal p67phox expression through immunoblot analysis [7, 9]. Given this rare characteristic, we sought to investigate the mechanistic implications of this variant.

Notably, the recently resolved activated-state structure of NADPH oxidase complex highlights the importance of residues 194–202 of p67phox, with K195, Y198, and L199 identified as key contributors, but not K196 [10]. Specifically, K195 interacts with E497 of NOX2, Y198 forms hydrophobic interactions with F491 and I502, and L199 makes van der Waals contacts with F451, E452, and A455. While K195 was reported to be functionally dispensable, Y198 and L199 play pivotal roles in stabilizing the complex [10]. Based on these findings, we propose that K196 deletion may perturb the spatial arrangement of the complex, potentially altering NOX2 activation.

With recent advancements in AI-driven protein structure prediction, we aimed to gain unprecedented insights into the functions of specific residues in protein-protein interactions. Using AlphaFold 3 (AF3) [11], we modeled both wild-type and K196-deleted complexes. AF3 effectively recapitulates the NOX2-p67phox interaction, with an exceptionally low root-mean-square deviation (RMSD = 0·248 within 5Å around K196) (Fig. 1H). However, K196 deletion not only disrupts the polar interaction between K195 and E497, but also introduces a novel polar interaction between the hydroxyl group of Y198 and either Q192 or K195 of p67phox, or S486 of NOX2, as observed across multiple prediction models. Importantly, this structural rearrangement further disrupts the original hydrophobic interaction of Y198 with F491 and I502 (Fig. 1I and J). These findings align with published experimental evidence that the Y198A mutation significantly reduces NADPH oxidase activity, as demonstrated in earlier biochemical studies [12] and confirmed by the recent structural analysis [10]. Together, these data highlight the functional importance of Y198 and offer new mechanistic insights into the impact of K196 deletion.

Although the predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) values for the relevant residues remain consistently above 90, indicating high confidence, discrepancies exist between the AF3-predicted complex and the experimentally resolved structure. For instance, AF3 predicts a polar interaction between K196 and Q192 that is absent in the resolved structure. Additionally, AF3 has inherent limitations, including its inability to account for post-translational modifications. This limitation is particularly relevant because K196 is a lysine residue—a canonical target for ubiquitination and SUMOylation—which could influence p67phox stability or interactions. Moreover, the static nature of AF3 predictions fails to capture dynamic conformational changes occurring under physiological conditions. These limitations point to additional approaches that could further clarify the mechanism, such as mass-spectrometry-based mapping of lysine modifications or cellular validation in neutrophil model systems. Nevertheless, despite these limitations, our integrated biochemical and computational analyses provide a coherent mechanistic interpretation of how K196 deletion leads to NADPH oxidase dysfunction, consistent with existing literature.

In summary, we report a homozygous NCF2 mutation (p.Lys196del) in an adult CGD patient, which represents a rare case where p67phox expression is preserved but functionally impaired. By integrating AI-driven structural modeling with biochemical assays, we provide mechanistic insights into NADPH oxidase dysfunction, suggesting that K196 deletion potentially perturbs NOX2 activation. Our study illustrates a novel framework for analyzing rare disease mutations and advancing precision medicine. As AI-based tools continue to evolve, they hold great potential for elucidating rare disease mechanisms and guiding personalized therapeutic strategies in the future.

Disclosure of AI-tool

In this study, apart from conventional biochemical methods, we utilized AlphaFold 3 (AF3) to assist in molecular structure prediction. AF3 was applied to simulate and predict structural changes associated with NCF2 mutation in our patient. With the predicted complex derived from AF3, we further analyzed the possible spatial interference of the NADPH oxidase complex with mutated NCF2.
