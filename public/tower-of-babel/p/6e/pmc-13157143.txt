Novel adult learning-based curriculum for pathology residents: components and critique at five years
Authors: Hafsa Nebbache, Aaron Daniel Shmookler, Justin Rueckert, Daniel Crump, Sahar Nozad, Nathan Shelman, Derek B. Allison, Robert J. McDonald, C. Darrell Jennings, Leanna Brignola, Therese J. Bocklage, Janna H. Neltner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Active learning forms the foundation of adult education methodology as it promotes durable retention of knowledge in accessible long-term memory. The most successful active learning strategy uses periodic testing, a memory recall, application, and consolidation activity termed “retrieval practice.” After reviewing adult learning science evidence, we created a two-year anatomic and clinical pathology residency core curriculum (Pathology Core Curriculum) in which we deployed six principles of adult learning, prioritizing retrieval practice as our key learning strategy. We hypothesized that the Pathology Core Curriculum would improve long-term knowledge retention which would correlate with progressively higher retrieval practice and resident in-service exam scores. Residents showed a significant upward trend in retrieval practice scores after initiation of the Pathology Core Curriculum. Retrieval practice scores most strongly positively correlated with surgical pathology resident in-service exam scores, as expected, given the preponderance of surgical pathology questions in the retrieval practices. For UK residents, the annual improvement in resident in-service exam scores post Pathology Core Curriculum significantly outpaced the pre–Pathology Core Curriculum resident in-service exam scores in eight of 10 disciplines and outpaced national gains/year in five of 10 disciplines. We also conducted an anonymized survey of residents and graduates to gauge satisfaction with and perceptions of the curriculum. Survey results indicated most residents agreed or strongly agreed the Pathology Core Curriculum promoted effective learning. Most graduates strongly agreed or agreed the Pathology Core Curriculum facilitated practice competency. In summary, the innovative and adaptable adult learning–based Pathology Core Curriculum is effective and well received by trainees. Long-term retention of accessible knowledge may endure for years through using spaced retrieval practices and other adult-learning principles in a pathology residency curriculum.

Introduction

Pathology residents in the combined anatomic/clinical pathology training track undertake a daunting task: they must become competent in the diverse, expanding practice of pathology in the constrained time of four years. To attain competency, foundational medical knowledge must be durably learned. Given that pathology residents are adults, the teaching approach for these trainees should ideally incorporate methods proven to be particularly effective in adults.1 Therefore, we specifically created a curriculum predicated on proven principles of adult learning, the Pathology Core Curriculum (PCC). We hypothesized that our residents would maximally benefit from a program that incorporated six adult learning principles, particularly focusing on one principle comprising periodic exams (memory testing) which is termed “retrieval practice” (RP). We anticipated that RP scores for senior residents experiencing the planned repetition of the PCC would generally exceed scores of junior residents new to the curriculum. We also hypothesized that RP scores would correlate with performance on a national quantitative exam, the pathology resident in-service exam (RISE).

The six adult learning principles we incorporated comprise 1) Spacing, which entails spreading specific subject material and study activities over time; 2) use of Concrete Examples, as adult learners focus particularly well on material that will be applied in practice, and case-based examples help retain information in the long term; 3) Dual Coding, by combining two different ways of receiving information, such as pairing verbal instruction with visual images; 4) Interleaving, by switching between related but distinct topics, which helps build neural connections across subject areas—such as combining histopathology and clinical chemistry (CC) in hepatobiliary topics; 5) Elaboration, by asking questions and delving deeper into the subject such as exploring in depth the underlying pathophysiology of an entity; and 6) Retrieval Practice, by reinforcing retention of learned information through periodic testing.2,3

In this paper, we describe our PCC components, correlate RP scores with national exam results, and provide a critique of the program at five years (after 2.5 cycles).

Materials and methods

As per the University of Kentucky College of Medicine Institutional Review Board regulations, this report was deemed “non-human subjects research.”

Pathology core curriculum description

The University of Kentucky (UK) Department of Pathology and Laboratory Medicine PCC comprises a two-year cycle of traditional (70%) and interactive (30%) educational sessions organized by body organ/system. The 1-h sessions occur approximately four times weekly, excluding a mid-summer hiatus. Each resident experiences the curriculum twice. Using six principles of adult learning, teaching sessions in an organ/system block are spaced, include dual coded learning materials, are interleaved with related topics, regularly include specific concrete examples of pathologic conditions, and provide opportunities for elaboration of topics. Critically, periodic retrieval practices simultaneously test and cement knowledge acquisition.2

Subspecialty faculty responsibilities include preparation and delivery of didactic sessions. As per each didactic session, the faculty member submits three to five RP questions. The faculty wrote 95% of the questions and pulled 5% from pre-made question-bank sources. The Residency Program Director or Vice Chair for Education reviewed and edited questions. Questions were derived from didactics content only. A few faculty reused questions in the second half of the curriculum cycle (accounting for about 5% of questions). Reused multiple-choice questions (MCQs) were re-formatted to change the answer selection order. Faculty members are encouraged to record and upload lectures to a shared online platform for optional later review. Faculty members are not responsible for keeping attendance, but residents are expected to meet a minimum attendance rate of 80%.

Didactic formats vary. Approximately 70% of didactic sessions are delivered in a traditional lecture format with senior residents encouraged to actively participate in the teaching session in a “one-room schoolhouse” model in which experienced learners informally assist the faculty in teaching trainees first encountering the subject matter. Approximately 30% of didactics comprise a diversity of active learning experiences such as team-learning Jeopardy™-like events, group solution of case scenarios, rapid-fire case reviews, and unknown slide conferences. Retrieval practices are delivered a minimum of one test per organ/system block with two cumulative semi-annual RPs. The latter consist exclusively of MCQs to simulate a Boards exam experience. By contrast, the organ/system block RPs encompass multiple question formats including true/false, fill-in-the-blank, short-answer, and identification of specific features in photomicrographs as well as MCQs. The RPs are created and administered in a commercial software platform (ExamSoft, Farmers Branch, Texas) which facilitates detailed analysis of resident performance to the individual question level. Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2 are examples of question types, and Supplementary Fig. 3 is an example of metrics generated per RP.

The PCC RP question bank currently stores 946 questions of which 625 were used in the RPs during the five-year period. Each question is cataloged and searchable by subspecialty, general subject, body site, and question source, among other tags (see Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2). A single question may cover more than one subspecialty such as SP and MB or SP and molecular genetics. For example, molecular genetics and/or genetic syndrome questions are embedded in 82 questions paired with categories of SP, HE, or CP. Each question is assigned a Bloom’s Taxonomy learning level by one faculty member or the residency program coordinator. Proportionately, Bloom’s level 1 comprises 37%, Bloom’s level 2 comprises 60%, and Bloom’s level 3 comprises 3% of the questions.

Performance requirements for the RPs varied over the course of the five years. Currently, a minimum threshold score is required to “pass” each RP. Residents must retake the RP if the initial score falls below the threshold. Setting a passing threshold evolved over the five years from initially no threshold to tiered thresholds ascending per training year to the current minimum threshold of 60% for all trainees. After each RP, a faculty content expert reviews the exam with the residents in an hour-long feedback session intentionally scheduled two to three days following test administration. Formal recognition awards are presented to the overall top scorer and the top first-year resident scorer semi-annually.

Retrieval practice performance data are integrated into the resident’s semi-annual meetings with the Program Director. Additionally, the Clinical Competency Committee includes RP scores, RISE scores, individual rotation evaluations, and 360° peer and staff reviews in a formal global competency assessment to determine specific Pathology Residency Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) Milestone achievements for each resident; thus, RPs add a quantitative component to resident assessment.

To maximize the beneficial effects of the PCC, new residents are introduced to an optimal self-learning strategy. Specifically, the “Study Cycle,” developed by Saundra Yauncy McGuire at Louisiana State University, is presented, which if adopted by the trainee, enhances long-term retention of the presented subject matter.4 Residents are also encouraged to augment the PCC sessions by creating their own personalized, mini-RPs in the form of flashcards (typically using an online format). Initial orientation to the PCC also includes an introduction to the “growth mindset” formulated by Carol S. Dweck which stresses the deep potential for successful learning in individuals prepared to exert the requisite effort.5

Correlation with pathology RISE

Retrieval practice scores were compared with RISE scores in residents experiencing the PCC to assess for correlations. Resident year of training, RISE subject category, and RISE score per trainee were compared with the trainee’s RP scores in the subjects of SP, CP, HE, MB, CC, transfusion medicine, and molecular pathology.

Additionally, the annual change in RISE score/category/year increase in training was evaluated in UK residents for four years before initiation of the PCC and four years post initiation of the PCC. This annual change was also compared with national average RISE score change/category/year increase in training.

Notably, at the University of Kentucky, the pathology RISE is administered over two half-days as a closed-book, proctored exam.

Resident/graduate survey

At year five of the PCC, all residents and graduates who had participated in the PCC were invited to complete a 12-question anonymous survey regarding the curriculum. The survey was administered online. A 5-point Likert scale was used to assess resident perceptions and opinions. Free-text comments were optional. Results were compiled and then distributed to the residents, graduates, and PCC faculty and Chair.

Discussion

In this report, we review our PCC that is founded on adult learning principles with a predominant role of spaced RP. Consistent with the reported positive effect of repeated spaced RPs, residents showed an increase in scores over time for the same topics/didactic sessions with second teaching-cycle scores exceeding first-cycle RP scores. Regarding external validation, our data support the positive effect of the curriculum, based on national exam (RISE) data comparisons. Remarkably, after initiation of the PCC, the average annual increase in RISE scores per year of additional training increased in eight of 10 subspecialty areas, consistent with a strong positive effect on long-term knowledge retention. Additionally, our residents showed a higher average score gain than the national average for five RISE categories.

We intentionally repeated the curriculum and semi-annual cumulative RPs (with minor modifications) over four years predicated on evidence that spaced RPs revisiting previously delivered content may mitigate the “forgetting curve” in which semantic recall dwindles through time.6 Despite a paucity of evidence of effectiveness in promoting years-long knowledge retention, RP remains the most effective way to durably learn subject matter, at least in the intermediate time frame of weeks, but exactly how it does so and the optimum number and timing of RPs per teaching session have not yet been determined.7,8 This ability of RPs to enhance long-term learning is known as the “direct testing effect” in which RPs physically change the test-taker’s brain by establishing new neural networks; the brain is not just used passively.2,9, 10, 11 Spaced RPs that recover prior presented material show a dose effect in increasing retention, although returns may diminish with over-testing.9 Fig. 1 confirms that repeated exposure to content (experiencing PCC cycle 2) results in an overall higher average RP score. However, PCC-naïve learners can achieve high scores, perhaps because of application of more effort including higher attendance rates for formal teaching sessions (Fig. 3).

Recognizing the value of giving detailed feedback in further strengthening long-term memory, we provided a feedback session after each RP and intentionally delayed the review to facilitate self-awareness of knowledge gaps (stimulated metacognition).1,2 Moreover, we scheduled the RP review sessions to include at least one night’s sleep as sleep consolidates memory, maximizing RP efficacy.11 Moreover, this deliberate time interval fostered elaboration by the residents before, during, and after the formal review (personally witnessed by the authors). Regularly awarding high-scoring residents promotes healthy competition and encourages amygdala-based reward-related motivation.10 In preparation for the feedback session, the faculty member reviewed the test results, including percent correct answer per individual question. This information guided more focused discussion as topics with a low correct-answer percentage would be addressed in greater depth.

Mechanistically, in an RP, two stages are required for optimal long-term memory activation: the immediate act of accessing a memory posed by the question (stage 1) then substantive feedback (stage 2); in other words, accessing the answer.11 Whether the initial answers are correct (which can be considered self-provided instantaneous feedback) is surprisingly less critical, presuming the learner has invested substantive initial effort before taking the RP.11 A blasé or indifferent study effort by the trainee will result in ineffective learning, even with the faculty delivering optimally spaced and written RPs and integrating other adult learning principles into a curriculum. As noted by an adult-learning expert, “the one who does the work does the learning.12

Residents were involved in the PCC beyond their role as learners. While the curriculum was crafted almost exclusively by the education committee of the Department, it was modified in response to resident critiques. In discussion with the residents, the number of RPs and threshold passing rates were lowered. Higher thresholds were considered too stressful by the trainees, and excess stress adversely affects performance.13 However, when a higher minimum pass rate was mandated, residents met the threshold, suggesting that the threshold was not excessively burdensome. The residents also requested that the RPs comprise only MCQs to simulate the Pathology Boards exams. Because MCQs are suboptimal for learning, a compromise ensued in which simulated Boards practice occurred with the semi-annual cumulative RPs, but more open-ended-type questions continued to be heavily used in the non-cumulative RPs. Both open-ended questions, such as short-answer ones, and MCQs that require multiple reasoning steps are more effective for enhancing long-term knowledge retention.9

Despite introducing performance anxiety with the use of RPs, most trainees agreed the PCC was a valuable component of their formal education. Whether RPs in residency produce long-enduring knowledge retention exceeding four years is unknown, but it is encouraging the graduates agreed “the RPs and follow-up review sessions helped me retain the material (subject matter) presented in the didactic sessions” and that “the PCC helped me effectively learn pathology topics that are important for my current practice.” Relatively few peer-reviewed publications in medical education assess such extreme, long-term subject matter retention using RPs as most studies evaluate retention for a period of weeks to a few months rather than years after the initial teaching.6,14, 15, 16 Notably, our PCC data suggest but do not prove that RPs may contribute to durable long-term learning that extends over years.

An adult learning–based curriculum requires buy-in not only from residents but also from faculty due to additional workload requirements to write and then review RPs with the residents. In a prior publication, we reported results of a faculty survey that indicated overall strong support for the PCC.17 Faculty also noted that RPs could identify areas of curriculum weakness requiring further teaching and provide a desirable quantitative metric of resident performance useful in semi-annual competency assessments.17

Tempering our conclusion that the PCC significantly enhanced long-term knowledge is the low number of learners experiencing the PCC (29 individuals) which magnified skewing effects of outlier performers. The disparate use of other teaching materials by residents could also unpredictably influence RISE scores. Some residents cram for the RISEs by reviewing the prior RISE question descriptors (per discussion with chief residents) which may inflate scores. Furthermore, each day on a SP rotation proffers a multitude of mini-RP opportunities, and therefore, the cumulative effect of additional SP rotations may outweigh the PCC RP effect on progressive, higher, annual SP RISE scores.

The high proportion of SP topics in our didactics and RPs compared with the other disciplines of CP, MB, HE, and MD inadvertently offered an informal experimental “control” for evaluating the effects of RPs on the RISE scores. If RPs enhance long-term learning, the positive correlation of SP RP scores with RISE scores, contrasted with inconsistent correlations for microbiology and CC, suggests that RPs consistently exerted a positive effect on the national test but primarily in the area that was most emphasized in the RPs. Too few questions in specific subspecialties in our curriculum may not demonstrably improve durable learning and thus would not consistently correlate with RISE scores.18 We thus believe that we are under-retrieving in several subspecialties. Attaining an optimal median: just enough, rather than too many or too few RPs (the former possibly causing burnout, cognitive overload or counter-productive boredom), awaits broadening of the PCC coverage of the more lightly addressed subspecialties.10,19

Although meeting a minimal RISE score is predictive of passing the Pathology Boards exams, it does not guarantee the ability to competently practice Pathology.20 However, in addition to achieving improved RISE performances after the PCC introduction, residents and graduates agreed that the PCC helped cement durable knowledge required in real-time practice scenarios (whether at the resident, fellow, or practitioner level). Thus, quantitative RP results as well as qualitative trainee views support the effectiveness of the adult-learning PCC. The personal impression of current residents and graduates that RPs improve pathology practice accords with research reporting improved clinical reasoning skills following incorporating RPs in training.9, 10, 11,13,16,20

In summary, internal departmental and external national comparisons of RISE results align with the hypothesis that an adult learning–based pathology residency curriculum improves durable long-term knowledge retention with effects lasting for years. Moreover, trainees and graduates agree the adult-learning curriculum is effective, based on anonymized survey data. Given the highly encouraging quantitative data and positive trainee reviews, we intend to continue to expand the PCC in depth and breadth, with the essential ongoing support and input of both faculty and residents.
