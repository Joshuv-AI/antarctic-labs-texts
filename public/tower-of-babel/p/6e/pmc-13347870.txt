Diagnostic delays and sexual health integration in Chlamydia trachomatis conjunctivitis: an 11-year retrospective study at a UK tertiary ophthalmic centre
Authors: Alice L Milligan, Dalia Abdulhussein, Yan Ning Neo, Chuiki Jasmine La, Nicole Quah, Shara Palanivel, Soonita Oomeer, Siyin Liu, Jeremy Hoffman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo describe the epidemiology, clinical features, diagnostic pathways and sexual health integration in patients with Chlamydia trachomatis conjunctivitis (CTC) presenting to a UK tertiary eye hospital over an 11-year period.DesignRetrospective case study.SettingTertiary ophthalmic emergency department and corneal and external eye disease service in London, UK.ParticipantsAll patients with laboratory-confirmed CTC from conjunctival nucleic acid amplification tests between 1 January 2013 and 31 December 2023. Of 312 identified cases, 267 with available medical records were included.Primary and secondary outcome measuresPrimary outcomes were time to diagnosis, initial misdiagnosis rates and documentation of sexual history. Secondary outcomes were clinical signs, management (including systemic treatment and referral to sexual health services), complications and temporal trends in testing and positivity.ResultsMedian age was 26 years (range 14–78 years) and 67.8% (181/267) were male. Presentation was unilateral in 88.0% (235/267). Common signs included conjunctival hyperaemia (95.1%) and tarsal follicles (85.4%). Diagnostic samples were obtained at the first visit in 13.1%, with a median time to diagnosis of 14 days (range 1–194 days). These findings highlight opportunities for genital chlamydia detection and support routine sexual health assessment, clear referral pathways and increased clinician awareness. Introduction

Chlamydia trachomatis conjunctivitis (CTC), also known as adult inclusion conjunctivitis, is a sexually transmitted infection caused by serovars D–K of the Gram-negative obligate intracellular bacterium C. trachomatis.1 Genital C. trachomatis infection remains the most common bacterial sexually transmitted infection in many high-income settings, including the UK, and predominantly affects adolescents and young adults.1,4 A substantial proportion of patients with CTC have concurrent genital infection, and conjunctivitis may be their only presenting feature.

Clinically, CTC often presents as a unilateral or asymmetrically bilateral follicular conjunctivitis with mucopurulent discharge, sometimes accompanied by punctate keratopathy.3 5 Early immunofluorescence studies, such as the work by Taylor-Robinson and colleagues using the MicroTrak direct smear test, found C. trachomatis in around 7% of adult conjunctival swabs, illustrating that ocular chlamydia accounted for a substantial minority of adult conjunctivitis cases even before nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) were available.6 However, these features overlap with viral, bacterial and allergic conjunctivitis, so clinical examination alone is an imperfect discriminator. Laboratory confirmation is therefore important. NAATs on conjunctival specimens offer high sensitivity and specificity, but ocular swabs are not always among the specimen types approved by manufacturers, which can make it difficult for approving bodies to endorse their routine use in local protocols and may discourage clinicians from requesting them.4

Over the past four decades, multiple studies have highlighted that CTC is frequently under-recognised in ophthalmic practice and that diagnosis depends on clinicians actively considering an underlying sexually transmitted infection. More recently, UK centres have shown that systematic approaches can improve detection and management. Lockington and colleagues in Scotland reported that introducing multiplex ocular PCR, including C. trachomatis, required a robust multidisciplinary strategy to ensure that identified cases were linked into sexual healthcare.7 Shah et al described a 10-year shared-care model in the National Health Service (NHS) Greater Glasgow and Clyde, in which a protocolised pathway between ophthalmology and sexual health clinics improved the management of ocular C. trachomatis infections.8 These studies demonstrate that when diagnostic pathways and shared-care arrangements are clearly defined, CTC can be identified and managed more consistently, but they also underline the complexity of embedding sexual health within ophthalmic services.

In contrast, during the period covered by the present audit there was no formal written protocol for the investigation of conjunctivitis or suspected CTC at our tertiary eye hospital. Our emergency department serves a large and diverse urban and regional population, with a mix of self-referrals and referrals from other units.9 Conjunctival swabs for culture and NAAT are recommended on individual cases where gonococcal or chlamydia conjunctivitis are suspected by the clinician and not routinely. Against this background, we undertook a retrospective review of all laboratory-confirmed CTC cases over an 11-year period at a UK tertiary ophthalmic centre. Our objectives were to describe the epidemiology and clinical features of these patients, to examine diagnostic pathways and delays and to evaluate sexual history documentation and linkage to sexual health services to identify specific gaps that could be addressed by future protocolised care.

Methods

Moorfields Eye Hospital NHS Foundation Trust is the largest specialist eye centre in the UK and provides care across a network of more than 30 sites in Greater London and beyond, delivering several hundred thousand outpatient appointments and tens of thousands of emergency attendances each year.9 The trust serves a highly diverse population in terms of age, ethnicity and socioeconomic background, reflecting both its central London location and its regional referral role. Referral pathways include self-referrals, referrals from community optometry and general practice and referrals from other hospital eye services, meaning that patients with chlamydial conjunctivitis may present through multiple routes and from a wide geographic catchment.

We identified all cases of laboratory-confirmed C. trachomatis infection from conjunctival swabs in the period from 1 January 2013 to 31 December 2023 at Moorfields Eye Hospital (including outreach sites). The paper and electronic patient records for these cases were retrieved, where available, and data extracted for clinical presentation, treatment and complications. Unavailable or incomplete records (ie, prior to transition to electronic records) were excluded. Data were extracted in an anonymised format into an excel sheet for analysis.

Conjunctival NAAT samples were collected using Aptima Combo 2 assay (Hologic, San Diego, California, USA), which simultaneously detects C. trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae by transcription-mediated amplification. There was no change in the testing method over the study period. The Aptima Combo 2 assay is locally validated for conjunctival specimens but performance has not been evaluated by the manufacturer. Clinical and demographic data were presented using descriptive statistics (Excel, Microsoft, Washington, USA).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this retrospective audit study. As a service evaluation of routine clinical practice using anonymised data, patient involvement in study design was not feasible. Results will be disseminated through open-access publication to ensure public availability and will be shared with local sexual health services and public health agencies to inform service improvement.

Results

Demographics

In the 11-year period, there were 312 cases of CTC, of which 267 were included. The 45 exclusions were due to the absence of documentation in electronic and/or paper notes. The majority, 67.8% (181/267), were male. The median age was 26 years (range 14–78 years). Over the study period, the median age decreased from 25 years to 22 years (table 1).

The median duration of symptoms was 14 days (IQR 7–21 days). Diagnosis

Routine conjunctival swabs are not mandated in our local unit. If there are signs of chlamydial or gonococcal conjunctivitis (such as large follicles, sexual exposure, chronic history, palpable pre-auricular lymph nodes), then a conjunctival swab for culture and NAAT is recommended. Conjunctival NAAT samples were obtained on first visit in 13.1% (35/267), second visit in 49.1% (131/267), third visit in 22.8% (61/267) and after >three visits in 15.0% (40/267). The median time from presentation to diagnosis was 14 days (range 1–194 days). An incorrect initial diagnosis was given in 63.6% (170/267) of cases, with 84.7% (144/170) presumed to be adenoviral conjunctivitis (table 3).

Sexual history

Sexual orientation is not routinely collected in ophthalmic hospital records. A sexual history was recorded in 24.0% (64/267) at first visit. Of those with a documented sexual history, genital symptoms were documented in 4.5% (12/64). Only 7.1% (19/64) had prior awareness of a genital chlamydia diagnosis.

Management

In the case of a positive result, our guidelines are to refer to the local genito-urinary clinic where systemic treatment would be commenced. Our guidelines also recommend a 3-day course of g. Azithromycin 1.5% two times per day for 3 days alongside systemic treatment. Appropriate treatment was presumed to have been received by patients referred by sexual health services or those referred on to sexual health services. This accounted for 50.9% (136/267) of cases. A minority, 1.5% (4/267), were given treatment in the ED. Appropriate systemic antibiotic therapy was therefore assumed in 52.4% (140/267). In 2.6% (7/267), topical treatment alone was provided, and in 44.9% (120/267) no treatment was documented.

Complications

Follow-up was available in 33.3% (89/267). Of those, the majority 94.5% (84/89) had no long-term complications. Ptosis was present in 2.2% (2/89), corneal epitheliopathy in 2.2% (2/89) and there was a single case of superior corneal scarring. Best corrected visual acuity was 6/7.5 Snellen at baseline, with 77.3% (69/89) recovered to baseline or better after treatment.

Discussion

Our findings sit within more than four decades of work highlighting the challenges of recognising and managing chlamydial conjunctivitis in ophthalmic practice. Early epidemiological studies, such as those by Ronnerstam and colleagues in 1985, showed that C. trachomatis accounted for a notable proportion of adult conjunctivitis cases and emphasised the difficulty of distinguishing CTC from other causes on clinical grounds alone.10 Subsequent work using direct immunofluorescence, including that of Taylor-Robinson and others, reinforced that laboratory testing was essential for accurate diagnosis in patients with chronic follicular conjunctivitis.11 12 Postema et al later demonstrated that more than half of men and three-quarters of women with chlamydial conjunctivitis had concomitant genital infection, most of them asymptomatic, and recommended routine referral of patients and partners to sexually transmitted disease clinics.13 Despite this longstanding evidence, our data show that, in a large UK tertiary eye hospital, CTC remains frequently misdiagnosed and incompletely integrated into sexual health pathways.

In this setting, nearly two-thirds of patients were initially given an alternative diagnosis, most commonly presumed adenoviral conjunctivitis, and the median time to microbiological confirmation was 2 weeks. The clinical features we observed—unilateral or asymmetrically bilateral follicular conjunctivitis with conjunctival hyperaemia, tarsal follicles and mucopurulent discharge—overlap substantially with viral and bacterial conjunctivitis and explain why clinicians readily anchor on more common diagnoses. The high misdiagnosis rate and diagnostic delay therefore reflect both the non-specific clinical picture and the absence of a formal local protocol to prompt early NAAT testing in at-risk patients, rather than simple oversight by individual clinicians.

A central new contribution of this study is to quantify, at scale, the gap between ocular diagnosis and sexual health integration in routine practice. Sexual history was documented in only one quarter of confirmed cases, and just over half had systemic treatment documented or reasonably presumed via sexual health services, leaving a substantial minority with no recorded management beyond ophthalmic care. In the context of previous work showing that most genital co-infection in CTC is asymptomatic, this low level of documented sexual-health assessment represents an important shortfall in holistic care and a missed opportunity for STI detection and partner management, even if some referrals occurred without being captured in the ophthalmology record.

Our temporal data also provide insight into how service changes may influence testing behaviour. Over the study period, the total number of conjunctival swabs taken at our institution fell markedly, while the proportion positive for CTC rose. This pattern suggests a higher threshold for testing and changes in service configuration, including the expansion of remote triage and advice and guidance during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, rather than a genuine decline in ocular chlamydia incidence. In addition, although the Aptima Combo 2 assay is locally validated for conjunctival specimens, ocular swabs are not a manufacturer-approved sample type for C. trachomatis NAAT. This regulatory grey area may have contributed to clinician hesitation and limited incorporation of ocular NAAT into local protocols, even within a tertiary centre.

Recent UK work has shown that more structured approaches can mitigate some of these challenges. Lockington and colleagues reported that introducing multiplex ocular PCR testing, including C. trachomatis, required a robust multidisciplinary strategy to ensure that patients with positive results were informed and linked into sexual healthcare.7 Shah et al described a 10-year shared-care model in NHS Greater Glasgow and Clyde, where a protocolised pathway between ophthalmology and sexual health clinics improved identification and management of ocular chlamydia infections.8 Our audit complements these studies by providing a baseline description of CTC care in a large tertiary eye hospital operating without a formal CTC protocol and highlights the specific areas—particularly sexual history-taking, systematic NAAT testing in at-risk patients and closed-loop referral to sexual health services—that require attention if similar shared-care models are to be implemented more widely.

Management recommendations also need to reflect evolving national guidance. Current British Association for Sexual Health and HIV guidance recommends doxycycline as the preferred first-line treatment for uncomplicated chlamydial infection, with multi-day azithromycin reserved for specific indications, in part because of concerns about antimicrobial resistance and lower efficacy at some anogenital sites.1 Ophthalmology services could therefore ensure that their local pathways are aligned with up-to-date sexual health guidelines and that patients with CTC are referred promptly to services that can provide appropriate systemic therapy, testing for other STIs and partner notification.

Although this study focused on CTC, the underlying issues of awareness, sexual history-taking and fragmented referral pathways are relevant to other STI-related ophthalmic presentations. Gonococcal conjunctivitis, ocular syphilis and HIV-associated ocular disease can all present initially to eye services, and delayed recognition or incomplete linkage to sexual healthcare may have serious systemic consequences. Improving sexual-health integration within ophthalmology—through education, simple structured questions and agreed referral protocols—is therefore likely to benefit a broader group of patients than those with CTC alone.

Finally, our findings must be interpreted in light of several limitations. The retrospective design and incomplete documentation, particularly regarding sexual history and treatment, mean that some aspects of management may be under-recorded. We did not have access to genital screening results or partner notification outcomes, so the true prevalence of concurrent genital infection and the impact on onward transmission cannot be determined from these data. Nevertheless, the large number of laboratory-confirmed CTC cases, drawn from a highly diverse population attending a major UK tertiary eye hospital, provides a robust service-level picture. Taken together with the historical and contemporary literature, our results reinforce the message that chlamydial conjunctivitis should be considered an STI-related eye disease requiring coordinated ophthalmic and sexual healthcare, and they identify concrete targets for quality-improvement work in similar settings.

Shared-care models from other UK centres suggest practical ways to achieve this. For example, Shah et al reported that appointing a named sexual health adviser as a point of contact for ophthalmology, who is automatically copied into all positive ocular Chlamydia trachomatis NAAT results and proactively arranges follow-up, treatment and partner notification, improved engagement and outcomes in their service.8

Conclusion

This 11-year audit reveals delays in the diagnosis and management of CTC in ophthalmic practice, with two-thirds of cases initially misdiagnosed, median diagnostic delay of 14 days, and sexual history documented in fewer than one-quarter of confirmed cases. Given that 80% of CTC patients have concurrent genital infection, these deficiencies represent a significant missed opportunity for STI detection, treatment and prevention of reproductive complications. Ophthalmology services could improve care by implementing routine documentation of a brief sexual history and establishing clear referral pathways to sexual health clinics and increase awareness of CTC as a differential diagnosis for acute follicular conjunctivitis. Systematic integration of sexual health assessment into ophthalmic practice is essential to deliver holistic patient care and contribute to public health efforts to control chlamydia transmission.
