An AI-based approach to thermal bridge analysis
Authors: Marta Pomada, Krzysztof Cpałka, Piotr Lacki, Janina Adamus
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Buildings significantly contribute to climate change, accounting for approximately one-third of global energy consumption and a quarter of CO2 emissions. Therefore, all actions aimed at increasing building energy efficiency are of great importance. This article proposes an original approach to analyzing thermal bridges in window-to-wall connections using artificial intelligence (AI). A fuzzy system (FS), employed as a universal approximator, proves particularly effective in this context. The FS, trained using data generated through conventional thermal bridge analysis conducted in the TRISCO program, offers interpretability that distinguishes it from other AI models. The FS was utilized to estimate linear heat transmittance coefficients, which quantify heat loss through thermal bridges. The proposed AI approach demonstrates excellent performance, generating precise linear heat transmittance coefficient values. Importantly, due to its ability to generalize knowledge, the trained system accurately determines the value of the Ψ coefficient for cases not included in the training data – those for which traditional analysis using the TRISCO program had not been previously performed. By leveraging this approach for thermal bridge analysis, it becomes possible to reduce the need for classical analyses, which often involve time-consuming calculations, expensive experiments, and extensive designer expertise in selecting optimal solution.

Introduction

Buildings are a major source of energy use and greenhouse gas emissions, posing economic and ecological problems. In 2022, residential buildings consumed 21% of energy, the highest in the real estate sector. Non-residential buildings used 8.8% and infrastructure development over 3%. Heating, ventilation and air conditioning systems alone account for 38% of energy use in buildings1–3.

To reduce energy consumption, the European Commission adopted “Fit for 55” package in 20212. It aligns climate and energy legislation with the EU’s goal of cutting greenhouse gas emissions by 55% by 2030. Key points include: zero emissions for new buildings by 2030, public buildings must be emission-free by 2028 and 20% of existing buildings must be ready to achieve zero emissions by 2030. The construction sector must achieve net zero emissions by 2050. The authors of3,4 and emphasize that choosing energy-saving materials and solutions is crucial. AI tools can help by improving decision-making, optimizing designs, and streamlining construction processes.

Accurately predicting heating and cooling loads is crucial for designing energy-efficient buildings, especially smart homes using Internet of Things technologies5. Machine learning and deep learning models, such as artificial neural networks (ANNs) or FSs, provide flexible and reliable solutions6,7. These models have been used to estimate heating loads and improve the design of solar and water heaters by predicting performance without complex physical knowledge8,9. AI tools also enhance safety and efficiency in building design, particularly in heat transfer8. For example, a machine learning framework using finite element simulations and models like multivariate linear regression, XGBoost, and deep neural networks can optimize energy management in buildings with thermally anisotropic envelopes10.

Recent achievements in using AI systems are gaining attention for solving complex thermal problems. Traditionally, thermal engineering relies on laborious equations and correlations. AI systems simplify this process by offering fast and reliable predictions once trained. They provide precise approximations, often outperforming traditional models, and – unlike conventional approaches – require only input/output samples from a pretrained system, making them effective even with incomplete or noisy data. The article11 proposed an ensemble machine learning model to predict cooling/heating loads, using data like glazing area, orientation, height, relative compactness, roof area, and wall area. The model achieved an R2 value of 0.999 for heating load and 0.997 for cooling load, outperforming existing models.

In a review of heat transfer studies using ANNs, Kamble et al.12 highlighted the back propagation (BP) neural network algorithm as a powerful tool, that offers better predictions with reduced deviations. Traditional methods often fail to solve complex thermal problems, while ANNs align well with experimental observations. For energy efficiency and comfortable indoor conditions, accurate room temperature prediction is crucial. Romazanov13 used a recurrent ANN with gated recurrent units to predict room temperatures in public buildings, determining the most effective configuration through training on varying measurement sequences. Ipek et al.14 explored the relationship between rubberized concrete mixture proportions and thermal conductivity using ANNs. Goetzke-Pala et al.15 applied ANNs with (BP) error and the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno algorithm for non-destructive moisture content evaluation in saline brick walls.

Standard methods to assess the thermal properties of building envelopes include theoretical methods, heat meters, simple hot boxes, infrared thermography, and thermometric methods. A review by Nardi16 focused on the heat transfer coefficient (U-value) and thermal resistance (R-value). The analysis shows a sharp increase in studies on this topic, categorized into measurement-based and non- measurement-based methods. The earliest document on this topic dates to 1981 with a significant rise in publications since 2009, resulting in 483 in 2023. In17the evaluation of the U-value for windows was explored, focusing on: type of tests (laboratory or in situ), examined window part (center pane or entire window), measurement techniques, data analysis method (steady-state or dynamic), required time and accuracy, advantages and disadvantages.

Nardi et al.16 highlighted AI’s potential in innovative applications but noted that successful implementation requires good datasets and trials on model architectures, including input layers, hidden layers, neurons, and data for training and testing. Both AI and ANNs need a deep understanding of AI capabilities and in situ measurement datasets. The lack of literature on AI in U-value assessment indicates a promising research area. Luo et al.18 identified lighting control and building-integrated photovoltaics (BIPV) as effective strategies to reduce primary energy consumption and CO2 emissions in building operations. They proposed three machine learning techniques to predict energy loads: ANN, support vector machine (SVM), and long-short-term-memory (LSTM) neural network. These models were tested with different heat transfer coefficients of windows and walls, and window-to-wall ratios. To fully utilize BIPV technology, it’s essential to ensure proper construction and materials for external walls, including the load-bearing layer and thermal insulation, and to install windows in a way that eliminates thermal bridges at the window-to-wall interface. Studies19–21 have examined the impact of window installation methods, material and geometric parameters, and window types on the thermal and humidity properties of the window-to-wall connection. These studies highlight that each design variant requires numerical simulations and analytical calculations, which can lengthen the design process and discourage the use of innovative and ecological solutions.

To reduce heat losses in buildings, which mainly come from heat transfer and ventilation (i.e. ventilation – 30 ÷ 40%, external walls – 15 ÷ 25%, roof – 20 ÷ 25%, floor on ground – 5 ÷ 10%, windows and doors – 15 ÷ 25%, thermal bridges – 5 ÷ 10%)22–24, it’s important to select insulating materials with low thermal conductivity for external walls and eliminate thermal bridges, especially from window installation. Large windows can extend linear bridges, so it’s essential to consider both insulation and installation methods when designing window-to-wall connections.

The values of linear and point heat transmittance coefficients are crucial for determining the impact of thermal bridges on heat losses in buildings. The linear heat transmittance coefficient (Ψ [W/(m²·K)]) helps assess how these bridges affect a building’s energy performance. This coefficient can be derived from technical literature25,26the EN ISO 14,683 standard27or calculated analytically according to the EN ISO 10,211 standard28. Authors29,30 note that catalog or analytically calculated thermal bridges may differ by about 20% from actual values. They recommend numerical calculations, which are 95% consistent. Viot et al.31 also advocate for numerical calculations for each new solution.

To assess thermal bridges, the two-dimensional (2D) analysis is typically used, Ψ coefficient under steady-state conditions. However, since real conditions are unsteady, a three-dimensional (3D) analysis is more appropriate. Choi et al.32 used 3D analysis for window-to-external wall connections. Nagy and Stocker33 investigated masonry walls and corner connections. Mao et al.34 studied insulating vacuum panels. 3D models are constructed using Finite Element Method (FEM) software, analyzing heat flow between nodes at various temperatures and boundary conditions. Ascione et al.35 confirmed the usefulness of numerical simulations for assessing heat losses due to thermal bridges in external walls, suggesting their use in energy audits.

Adopting the most accurate classical method of determining the linear bridge value at the window-to-wall connection allows for a realistic assessment of heat losses and building energy efficiency, but it extends the design time. To enhance this process, AI tools, including FSs, are investigated in this work. Their results are compared with numerical calculations for window installation in the thermal insulation layer. The main goal is to show how AI tools can enhance external wall design by reducing thermal bridges at the window-to-wall connection, thereby empowering designers to quickly create energy-efficient exterior walls that minimize heat loss.

Motivation

Minimizing heat loss at window-to-wall connections requires precise estimation of the linear thermal transmittance coefficient (Ψ), typically performed through 3D simulations (e.g., TRISCO, compliant with EN ISO 1021128). However, such analyses are time-consuming, require design experience, and must be repeated for each geometric or material change. Furthermore, existing tools offer limited capacity for rapid comparison of multiple configurations. This creates a need for decision-support methods that combine the precision of classical approaches with the speed and flexibility of AI-based tools. One such a solution is the use of fuzzy systems as approximators of Ψ, trained on reference data from numerical calculations.

Novelty elements

The proposed approach to estimating the linear heat transmittance coefficient (Ψ) at window-to-wall connections using FSs has not been described in the literature so far. The innovation consists in transferring knowledge from classical numerical analyses (e.g. TRISCO) to an intelligent system that – after learning – enables fast, precise and interpretable prediction of Ψ for new design variants. Results and discussion

The first part of the work focuses on determining the linear heat transmittance coefficient using classical numerical and analytical design approach. Calculations for cases of window installation within the thermal insulation layer, considering various material and geometric parameters of the external wall and different window positions were carried out.

Conventionally, analyzing the linear heat transmittance coefficient at window-to-wall connections involves time-consuming numerical analyses (e.g. TRISCO). These analyses consider parameters such as the load-bearing layer of the wall, the thermal insulation layer, window position relative to the wall face, and expensive experimental verification. In the article, we propose an alternative approach using a fuzzy system. While based on classical analysis results, this approach allows for result aggregation and generalization. It aims to facilitate the design of external walls with windows by using a selected intelligent system that learns the results of classical analysis during supervised machine learning. We refer to this approach as the ‘intelligent approach’ in our work.

The analysis of numerical and analytical calculation results clearly indicates that the material type and the thickness of both the load-bearing and insulating layers significantly impacts the linear heat transmittance coefficient Ψ. The requirement of the building without thermal bridges with a coefficient Ψ ≤ 0.01 W/(m∙K) was met by as many as 83% of the analyzed variants, including all variants of the external wall with resole boards as an insulating material.

Based on the values of the linear heat transmittance coefficient listed in Table 1, it is possible to assess the impact of shifting the window into the thermal insulation layer. In each variant, optimal window location leads to a decrease in the Ψ coefficient. This reduction ranges from 3% to as much as 40%. The thicker insulating layer and the greater the shift of the window into the insulation layer, the effect is more pronounced. Among those cases where Ψ coefficients exceeded 0.01 W/(m∙K) (gray cells in Table 1), relocating the window into the insulating layer resulted in meeting the requirement for a building free from thermal bridges in as many as 91% of cases.

Analyzing the influence of the load-bearing wall material, assuming the same insulating material, it can be noticed that the higher the material’s thermal conductivity coefficient λ, the lower the linear heat transmittance coefficient Ψ. It does not depend on the type and thickness of the thermal insulation layer material. It was also noticed that the greater the thickness of the wall’s load-bearing layer, the smaller the differences between the Ψ coefficient values calculated for the 150 mm and 300 mm thick thermal insulation layer.

The material type and thickness of the insulating layer have a greater impact on the values of the linear heat transmittance coefficient for the adopted window installation method. Assuming the same material and thickness of the load-bearing layer, it turns out that the lower the thermal conductivity coefficient λ of the insulating material, the lower the values of the linear heat transmittance coefficient Ψ. But the effect of the thickness of the insulating layer is inversely proportional. Comparing the variants in which the window flush was aligned with the wall face, the lowest values of Ψ coefficient were obtained for the smallest thickness of the insulation layer, and as the thickness of the insulation layer increases, the Ψ coefficient increases (deteriorates), even though the insulating properties of the entire wall increase (they are getting better).

Analysis of fuzzy system results

The classic analysis of thermal bridges occurring at the window-to-wall connection gave very good results, and what’s more some of the results of numerical calculations, as discussed in works19,20were positively verified in experimental tests. Repeated analyzes for various materials and thicknesses of the building wall’s structural layers as well as different position of the window in relation to the wall allowed for the collection of a large set of numerical data. This allowed for an attempt to implement the knowledge hidden in these data into the AI system as a result of machine learning. The goal of the intelligent approach is to create an AI system for determining the output value of the Ψ coefficient, i.e. parameter 6, for parameters 1 ÷ 5 given in Table 2.

The aim of the simulations was to prepare a dataset for a fuzzy system which, for the given parameters of the model shown in Fig. 8, determines the value of the Ψ coefficient related to the energy lost as a result of the thermal bridge. Discussion summary

Based on the analysis conducted, the following conclusions can be drawn:

The method of window installation and the type of wall construction materials are interrelated and should be considered simultaneously.The type of material of the insulating layer and its thickness have a dominant influence on meeting the adopted criteria for assessing the quality of window installation and applicable standard requirements.Each structural and material solution of the window-to-wall connection requires numerical and analytical calculations and other, faster tools should be sought.Classical approaches to thermal bridge analysis can complement approaches arising from the use of artificial intelligence methods (referred to as intelligent approaches). In particular, classical approaches can generate training sets. These sets can be used in machine learning to implement knowledge into an intelligent system.Using an intelligent approach to thermal bridge analysis (e.g., estimating the linear heat transmittance coefficient, which is a measure of heat loss through a thermal bridge) reduces the need for classical analysis, which often requires a specific approach and extensive knowledge and experience from the designer.The intelligent approach to thermal bridge analysis proposed in this study works very well, and the achieved accuracy is satisfactory. It was assumed that this approach is based on the use of a fuzzy system, which generates linear heat transmittance coefficient values with high precision.In future works, we plan to expand the system’s knowledge to include the possibility of taking into account other wall materials and insulation; the use of other types of artificial intelligence systems; building systems dedicated to the considered application (in particular, appropriately preparing training data), etc.

Classical approaches to thermal Bridge analysis in window installation systems

Thermal bridges are localized disruptions in the homogeneity of a building envelope (in terms of geometry or thermal conductivity), which lead to increased heat transfer and localized drops in internal surface temperatures. In design practice, they are particularly relevant at structural joints, such as window-to-wall connections, as they affect the building’s energy efficiency, thermal comfort, and durability. Accurate analysis of thermal bridges helps assess their impact on heat loss and prevents design errors that could result in condensation or material freezing.

In both literature and engineering practice, several recognized methods exist for analyzing thermal bridges. These methods can generally be divided into three main groups:

Numerical methods are the most accurate and versatile. They rely on numerical simulations according to EN ISO 10,21128, which allows the use of the Finite Element Method. This approach involves discretizing the studied object (e.g., a wall segment with a window opening) into small elements for which heat transfer equations are solved locally. Simulations account for actual material properties (thermal conductivity coefficients λ), boundary conditions (e.g., fixed temperatures on internal and external surfaces), and possible nonlinearities. The outcome includes detailed maps of temperature distribution and heat flux density, from which the Ψ-value can be calculated. Tools such as TRISCO, COMSOL Multiphysics, THERM, and HEAT2 — based on FEM or similar discretization methods — are commonly used in building analysis. The main drawbacks of this group are their labor- and time-intensiveness, and low flexibility, i.e. any change in geometry, material, or layer thickness requires a new model and a separate simulation41,42.Catalog-based methods are grounded in EN ISO 14,68327, which introduces so-called empirical methods. These rely on catalogs of standard Ψ-values assigned to specific construction details (e.g., walls with windows, balconies, lintels, etc.). Instead of running simulations, the designer selects the Ψ-value from a table that most closely matches the examined configuration. While fast and practical, the accuracy of this approach depends on how closely the specific case aligns with the configurations listed in the catalog, and this may represent a limitation of the approach, particularly for atypical configurations43.Measurement-based methods assume the possibility of estimating the linear thermal transmittance coefficient Ψ through empirical measurements in real or laboratory conditions. These approaches are especially useful for assessing existing buildings, but their accuracy can be limited by the difficulty of controlling environmental conditions, such as wind, solar radiation, or humidity. Therefore, measurement methods are usually used for validation purposes and are not applied during the design phase.

Concept of the classical approach

The classical method involves reproducing the geometry of a construction joint (e.g., window installation layout) in a simulation environment and solving a steady-state heat conduction problem. This is typically done using FEM-based software, such as TRISCO, which enables 2D or 3D heat flow analysis. The simulation outputs temperature distributions and heat flux densities within the analyzed building envelope fragment. Intelligent models offer a promising solution by transferring engineering knowledge from classical analyses into simpler, interpretable predictive models.

Workflow of the classical approach

The classical thermal bridge analysis procedure can be divided into stages, aiming to determine the linear thermal transmittance coefficient Ψ for a specific structural connection – most commonly a window-to-wall interface. The process begins with clearly defining the analysis objective and identifying key geometric and material parameters: wall type, layer thicknesses, and material properties. A 2D or 3D geometric model is then created to represent the construction node accurately, following the requirements of EN ISO 10,21128 – particularly concerning reference dimensions and minimizing boundary effects from adjacent components. Each layer is assigned its thermal conductivity λ, accounting for possible anisotropy.

Next, boundary conditions are defined – typically fixed temperatures at internal and external surfaces, with no heat loss due to radiation or convection, in line with the standards. Once the model is fully parameterized, FEM calculations are carried out, yielding temperature fields and heat flux distributions. From this data, the total heat flow through the junction is determined and compared with reference flows from components without the thermal bridge, enabling calculation of Ψ. An optional final step involves verifying surface hygiene criteria by determining the local surface temperature factor and assessing condensation risk according to EN ISO 10,21128.

While the method offers high precision, its complexity and time demands limit its practicality in early-stage design and comparative analyses.

Limitations of the classical approach

The classical method relies on several simplifying physical assumptions that enable efficient and repeatable calculations compliant with technical standards. Most notably, it assumes steady-state conditions with no temporal variation in thermal loads and excludes transient phenomena. The analysis ignores the presence of moisture, vapor condensation, and moisture migration within materials. Each material is treated as isotropic, meaning its thermal conductivity is uniform in all spatial directions. Moreover, heat transfer via radiation or convection is neglected, assuming conduction is the sole mechanism.

Despite these simplifications, the method delivers high accuracy and complies with normative requirements, making it the standard approach for designing building envelopes. Nevertheless, it’s important to recognize that these assumptions limit the ability to model some physical phenomena observed in real-life operating conditions.

Scope of the classical approach

The classical method is widely used in the design and energy performance evaluation of construction details, especially in low-energy and passive buildings. It is primarily applied to detailed thermal bridge analyses at critical junctions where precise Ψ-values are essential to meet energy requirements. It plays a key role in preparing technical documentation for passive buildings, including certification aligned with Passive House Institute (PHI) guidelines41.

Additionally, it supports the optimization of material and geometric solutions to minimize heat loss and improve thermal-hygienic conditions. It also enables verification of alternative installation technologies – such as shifting the window frame into the insulation layer – by precisely analyzing how such changes affect the thermal behavior of the connection.

Relevance of the classical approach for the development of intelligent methods

The high accuracy and regulatory compliance of results obtained using the classical method make it a reliable reference for building and training predictive AI models, such as FSs or ANNs. Since this approach captures complex relationships between geometric/material parameters and Ψ-values, the simulation data can be treated as expert knowledge, suitable for training simplified models.

Once trained, these models can significantly accelerate the analysis process, eliminating the need for repeated, time-consuming simulations. This is particularly valuable in early design phases, variant assessments, and optimization tasks, where fast and repeated exploration of material–geometry configurations is essential. Such an approach is proposed in this article.

AI-based approach to thermal Bridge analysis in window systems

This section presents the concept behind the proposed approach to thermal bridge analysis in window systems, considerations for choosing an AI system, a description of the applied AI system, the learning procedure, and the key features of the proposed method.

Concept of the AI approach

The proposed approach focuses on estimating the linear thermal transmittance coefficient (Ψ) for thermal bridges in window systems. It combines the classical computational methodology discussed in the section Numerical methods (which employs FEM) with the capabilities of AI-based systems. The central idea is to transfer data from earlier numerical simulations – performed using specialized TRISCO software based on FEM and compliant with EN ISO 10,21128 – into an AI model.

TRISCO, as a recognized engineering tool, enables precise modeling of temperature distribution and heat flow in complex construction systems. However, each simulation requires significant time, expert knowledge, and effort. Our proposed approach repurposes archived simulation results as a training dataset for FS, which learns to replicate the relationship between construction parameters and the resulting Ψ value.

Unlike typical “black box” models such as neural networks, the FS operates as a “gray box” model – its fuzzy rule structure allows for partial interpretability of the decision-making process. As a result, this approach offers considerable acceleration and simplification of design analyses, enabling fast, automated, and interpretable estimation of the Ψ coefficient based on defined construction parameters.

This method is particularly useful in prototyping and early-stage optimization of architectural solutions in energy-efficient building design.

Features of the proposed method

The constructed FS, trained on specific cases defining window geometry and materials, demonstrates generalization capability. In practice, this means it can accurately estimate the linear thermal transmittance coefficient (Ψ) for data combinations not encountered during training, provided they fall within the defined range of input parameters (e.g., similar layer thicknesses or materials). The system’s performance on previously unseen data (i.e., cases not analyzed in TRISCO) is clearly illustrated in Figs. 5, 6 and 7 in Results and discussion, which show the potential for rapid parameter exploration and visualization of Ψ values. Naturally, the problem addressed in this article is open to further development: the parameter ranges can be adapted to specific applications and data availability.

The finite element method (FEM) used in TRISCO and the AI system (e.g., FS) represent fundamentally different methodologies. FEM is a general method based on physical laws and differential equations, offering high computational and memory complexity but yielding precise results. In contrast, FS is an approximate approach, often used as a surrogate model in optimization processes where direct manipulation of the optimized object is not feasible. AI-based systems, including FSs, do not model physical relationships directly but approximate input–output dependencies. Their complexity depends primarily on internal parameters such as the number of fuzzy rules, network layers, or neurons. However, this complexity is typically much lower than that of FEM.TRISCO serves as a benchmark method and a source of accurate Ψ values. The AI-based method is an approximation that aims to replicate TRISCO’s behavior for cases not included in the training phase (i.e., without TRISCO simulations). Nevertheless, the approximation accuracy is high in terms of RMSE (7), a standard metric for AI systems.
