Introduction

Nephrolithiasis is a common and recurrent disease with substantial clinical and socioeconomic burden. Lifetime prevalence approaches 10–15% in industrialized countries and continues to rise [1]. The majority of stones are composed of calcium oxalate and are strongly influenced by dietary and environmental factors. However, a subset of patients harbor underlying metabolic or genetic disorders that require specific evaluation and management [1–4]. Failure to recognize these conditions may lead to recurrent stone events, cumulative renal damage, and, in severe cases, kidney failure.

Stone episodes are associated with intense pain, emergency department visits, repeated surgical interventions, and loss of productivity. Recurrent lithogenesis promotes nephrocalcinosis, chronic inflammation, and progressive decline in renal function. Early identification of high-risk phenotypes is therefore essential to prevent long-term complications.

Urinary stone analysis and crystalluria assessment provide valuable diagnostic information [5]. Quantitative morphoconstitutional analysis of calculi allows identification of lithogenic mechanisms. Urine sediment microscopy is an inexpensive and underutilized tool [5, 6].

A systematic diagnostic approach integrating clinical history, urinary biochemistry, stone analysis, and microscopic examination is essential to distinguish dietary stone disease from inherited metabolic disorders. We report a case of genetically confirmed primary hyperoxaluria type 3 (PH3) in which marked calcium oxalate monohydrate crystalluria served as the key diagnostic clue.

Case report

A 31‑year‑old woman presented with intermittent flank discomfort and a history of recurrent nephrolithiasis beginning at age 9. She had previously undergone left percutaneous nephrolithotomy at 18 and flexible ureteroscopy at 28. Serum creatinine was 0.8 mg/dL, with an estimated glomerular filtration rate > 90 mL/min/1.73 m².

During the initial consultation, an early‑morning urine sample was obtained and examined within minutes. Urine microscopy revealed marked calcium oxalate monohydrate (COM) crystalluria, quantified at 280/mm³ (Fig. 1). COM crystalluria exceeding 200 crystals/mm³ is considered highly suggestive of primary hyperoxaluria when observed in an appropriate clinical context. This finding, together with the patient’s early‑onset recurrent stone disease, prompted immediate suspicion of primary hyperoxaluria and led directly to targeted genetic testing, initiated before the results of 24‑hour urine studies were available.

Subsequent Fourier Transform Infrared spectroscopy of passed stones demonstrated calcium phosphate (40%), calcium oxalate dihydrate (30%), and COM (30%). Two consecutive 24‑hour urine collections later confirmed isolated hyperoxaluria (45 and 72 mg/24 h; normal < 45 mg/24 h), with normal urinary calcium, citrate, and pH (Table 1). Genetic analysis identified a homozygous HOGA1 splice‑site variant (c.700 + 5G > T), establishing the diagnosis of primary hyperoxaluria type 3 (PH3).

Initial conservative management included increased hydration (> 2 L/day), dietary calcium (1 g/day), and potassium citrate supplementation, which normalized urinary citrate, but crystalluria persisted after 14 months. Adjunctive phytate therapy (Lit-Control® pH Balance, 255 mg every 12 h) was introduced. Within several weeks, urine microscopy showed progressive reduction and eventual disappearance of COM crystals (Table 2). During 24 months of follow-up, renal function remained stable, with no new stone formation or growth of existing stones, and no adverse effects were observed.

Discussion

Primary hyperoxaluria (PH) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder of glyoxylate metabolism, characterized by excessive endogenous oxalate production, recurrent nephrolithiasis, and progressive risk of renal impairment [1]. Three genetically distinct forms are recognized: PH1, PH2, and PH3. PH1 is the most common and carries the poorest prognosis, whereas PH2 and PH3 are less frequent and typically follow a milder course. PH3 results from biallelic mutations in HOGA1, which encodes 4-hydroxy-2-oxoglutarate aldolase, a mitochondrial enzyme expressed in the liver and kidney [1]. Loss-of-function mutations in HOGA1 lead to glyoxylate accumulation and subsequent oxalate overproduction. Our patient presented with childhood-onset, recurrent nephrolithiasis and was diagnosed with PH3 after genetic confirmation of the homozygous splice-site mutation c.700 + 5G > T, one of the most frequently reported HOGA1 variants in Europe [1].

Although stone analysis revealed a mixed calcium oxalate and calcium phosphate composition, such findings are nonspecific and cannot differentiate PH3 from idiopathic stone disease, as mixed stones are common in PH2 and may occur in other conditions, including medullary sponge kidney, infection, or hyperparathyroidism [7].

Given the patient’s childhood onset, long-standing recurrence, and documented hyperoxaluria, a metabolic disorder was already strongly suspected. In this context, the diagnostic process relied on a comprehensive evaluation integrating clinical history, biochemical testing, stone analysis, and urine microscopy. Urine microscopy played a critical role in diagnosis. Detection of > 200 calcium oxalate monohydrate (COM) crystals/mm³ is highly suggestive of primary hyperoxaluria and was particularly important when only one of two 24-hour urine oxalate measurements was elevated [1, 8]. The markedly elevated COM crystalluria obtained immediately at presentation did not act as a standalone discriminator between dietary and inherited disease, but it provided a timely and compelling clue that accelerated our suspicion of PH and prompted same‑day initiation of genetic testing.

Urine sediment microscopy not only aids diagnosis but also provides real-time feedback on lithogenic activity, allowing clinicians to assess the impact of therapeutic interventions before new stones are detectable by imaging. Persistent crystalluria indicates ongoing stone formation, whereas its resolution signals successful suppression of lithogenesis, as observed in this patient. Calcium oxalate crystallization is the most frequent form of crystalluria in human urine due to the very low solubility of calcium oxalate. The type of crystal depends mainly on the urinary calcium-to-oxalate molar ratio [9]. In hyperoxaluric conditions with normal or low calcium, the monohydrate form predominates, serving as a specific marker of hyperoxaluria, while the dihydrate form is less diagnostic.

Urine oxalate measurements are prone to pre-analytical variability, and plasma oxalate is informative only in kidney failure, rarely available, and not useful in this patient [1]. While genetic testing provides a definitive diagnosis, it is costly, has long turnaround times, and is not universally accessible; by contrast, urine microscopy is rapid, cost-effective, and widely available [8, 9].

Management emphasizes urine dilution, citrate supplementation, and dietary calcium to reduce lithogenic risk [1, 10–12]. In this patient, adjunctive phytate therapy coincided with disappearance of COM crystals, suggesting a potential role for crystallization inhibitors, though evidence remains limited, and phytate should not be considered primary therapy. Phytate (inositol hexakisphosphate, InsP6) is a naturally occurring polyphosphate abundant in whole grains, legumes, nuts, and seeds [13]. It inhibits nucleation, growth, and aggregation of calcium oxalate and phosphate crystals, a mechanism supported by experimental and clinical data [14, 15]. In our patient, phytate supplementation was associated with resolution of crystalluria, stabilization of stone burden, and preservation of renal function over two years, suggesting a potential adjunctive role in selected patients—although controlled studies are needed. Unlike PH1, for which RNA‑interference therapies are available, disease‑specific treatments for PH3 remain limited [1].

Conclusion

Urine microscopy contributed an important early clue within a broader diagnostic framework, complementing urinary oxalate measurements and accelerating suspicion of a rare metabolic disorder. High-density COM crystalluria offers a rapid, inexpensive diagnostic tool, particularly in patients with normal kidney function. Conservative therapy with hydration, citrate, and dietary calcium remains the mainstay of management, and adjunctive measures such as phytate may further reduce lithogenic risk. This case highlights the importance of integrating urine microscopy into routine evaluation of patients with recurrent nephrolithiasis.
