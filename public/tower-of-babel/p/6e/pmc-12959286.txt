Treatment Paradigm Shifts in Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension
Authors: Wen Wen, Yunshan Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a life-limiting pulmonary vascular disease characterized by a progressive increase in pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), leading to escalating right ventricular afterload and, ultimately, right-sided heart failure. Current therapeutic strategies for PAH have largely been extrapolated from the management of systemic hypertension, with a predominant focus on correcting vasotonic imbalance through vasodilation.1 However, such an approach fails to account for the fundamental physiological and pathologic differences between pulmonary and systemic hypertension. Systemic hypertension is primarily driven by neurohormonal activation—most notably the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system—resulting in sustained vasoconstriction, sodium retention, and volume expansion. These processes promote endothelial dysfunction and end-organ damage, and modulation with vasodilators effectively lowers blood pressure and alters disease progression.2,3 By contrast, PAH is not defined solely by elevated pulmonary arterial pressure but by a progressive, self-perpetuating remodeling of the distal pulmonary vasculature. Endothelial dysfunction, excessive proliferation of pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells, adventitial thickening, and resistance to apoptosis collectively narrow the arteriolar lumen, generating a largely fixed increase in PVR and ultimately precipitating right ventricular failure.4,5

These pathobiological differences carry important therapeutic implications. Current guideline-recommended therapies for PAH—including endothelin receptor antagonists, phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors, soluble guanylate cyclase stimulators, and agents targeting the prostacyclin pathway—remain predominantly vasodilatory in their mechanism of action and continue to form the cornerstone of contemporary management, despite their limited effects on the underlying vascular remodeling process.6 These agents improve symptoms, exercise capacity, and short-term hemodynamics, and they have undoubtedly transformed the natural history of PAH. Nevertheless, even with early and aggressive upfront triple-combination therapy, long-term survival remains suboptimal, and disease progression frequently persists.7 This raises a fundamental question: does the clinical efficacy attributed to so-called vasodilators in PAH truly arise from their vasodilatory effects? Notably, although these drugs are traditionally classified as vasodilators, direct evidence demonstrating that their clinical benefits are primarily mediated by pulmonary arterial vasodilation remains limited. If vasodilation were the dominant mechanism, one would expect immediate and consistent reductions in pulmonary arterial pressure; however, such responses are rarely observed in practice.8 In contrast, accumulating preclinical evidence suggests that many of these agents exert modest yet biologically meaningful antiproliferative effects on the pulmonary vasculature, indicating that their therapeutic benefit may be driven, at least in part, by suppression of vascular remodeling rather than by pure vasodilation.9, 10, 11 Collectively, these observations underscore that, although vasodilatory strategies may transiently alleviate the hemodynamic burden of PAH, they fail to adequately address the disease’s underlying structural pathology, reinforcing the need for therapies that directly target pulmonary vascular remodeling.

Against this backdrop, the therapeutic focus in PAH has begun to shift from vasodilation toward disease modification. Central to this evolving paradigm is the bone morphogenetic protein–transforming growth factor–β signaling axis, which plays a pivotal role in regulating vascular cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis.12 Disruption of this tightly regulated balance—most notably through reduced BMPR2 signaling—creates a proproliferative pulmonary vascular milieu that drives pathologic remodeling.13 Targeting this pathway, therefore, offers an opportunity to intervene upstream of irreversible structural change.

Sotatercept, a first-in-class activin receptor type ⅡA-Fc fusion protein, exemplifies this conceptual shift. By acting as a ligand trap for selected activins and growth differentiation factors, sotatercept rebalances transforming growth factor–β superfamily signaling, favoring antiproliferative over proproliferative cues within the pulmonary vasculature.14 Importantly, this mechanism is fundamentally distinct from that of existing PAH therapies. The clinical relevance of this approach has been supported by a growing body of evidence. Phase 2 and global Phase 3 trials have consistently demonstrated that sotatercept, when added to optimized background therapy, yields clinically meaningful reductions in PVR, improvements in exercise capacity, favorable shifts in World Health Organization (WHO) functional class, and reductions in biomarkers of right ventricular strain.15,16 More recent outcome-focused studies have further suggested benefits in high-risk populations and in patients treated early after diagnosis, reinforcing the concept that modulation of activin signaling may confer genuine disease-modifying effects.16 Although adverse events—including epistaxis, telangiectasia, thrombocytopenia, and hemoglobin elevation—are characteristic of this mechanism, they have generally been predictable and manageable, resulting in an overall favorable benefit-risk profile.17

However, until now, the evidence base for sotatercept has been derived almost exclusively from Western populations. This limitation is not trivial. PAH in Asia differs from that in Europe and North America with respect to epidemiology, genetic background, treatment practices, and baseline disease severity. Moreover, regional variations in background therapy—particularly the widespread use of parenteral prostacyclin in Japan—raises important questions regarding both efficacy and safety. Several considerations help place these findings into appropriate clinical context. As a regional Phase 3, open-label study conducted exclusively in Japanese patients with PAH, the primary objective was not to re-establish efficacy against placebo, but rather to confirm the consistency, robustness, and clinical relevance of sotatercept’s effects within an Asian population. This distinction is particularly important given the advanced disease profile of the enrolled cohort. Participants had a median disease duration exceeding 10 years; all were receiving background combination therapy, and more than 90% were treated with triple therapy, including nearly half receiving parenteral prostacyclin. At baseline, all patients were classified as WHO functional class Ⅱ (63%) or Ⅲ (37%), reflecting a population that was clinically stable yet persistently symptomatic despite intensive standard-of-care treatment.

Within this clinical context, sotatercept was associated with clinically meaningful improvements that were internally consistent across multiple disease domains. At 24 weeks, PVR decreased by a median of 99.2 dynes·s/cm5 (95% CI: −129.6 to −68.4), accompanied by a 41.8-m improvement in 6-minute walk distance (95% CI: 27.8-55.5 m), a magnitude generally regarded as clinically relevant in PAH. These hemodynamic and functional gains were paralleled by a reduction in N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide of 48.5 pg/mL, suggesting concordant alleviation of right ventricular wall stress. Notably, 19.6% of participants experienced improvement in WHO functional class by Week 24, including transitions from class Ⅲ to Ⅱ and from class Ⅱ to Ⅰ, despite the long-standing nature of their disease. Subgroup analyses further demonstrated consistent benefits across sex, PAH subtype, baseline hemodynamic severity, and use of parenteral prostacyclin, supporting the robustness of the observed treatment effect.

From a safety perspective, sotatercept demonstrated a profile consistent with prior global experience. Serious adverse events occurred in 13% of participants during the primary treatment period; none were considered treatment related, and no patients discontinued therapy because of adverse events. Commonly observed events—including epistaxis, hemoglobin elevation, thrombocytopenia, and telangiectasia—were clinically manageable with protocol-guided dose modification, and no new safety signals emerged in this intensively treated population. Collectively, these findings support the feasibility of integrating sotatercept into contemporary PAH management in Asia and provide reassuring evidence of benefit in a real-world–representative Japanese cohort.

In summary, this study provides important and timely evidence supporting the use of sotatercept in Asian patients with PAH, addressing a critical regional evidence gap in the evolving therapeutic landscape. The consistent improvements observed across hemodynamic, functional, and biomarker endpoints reinforce the concept that targeting activin signaling engages a central pathogenic mechanism of PAH rather than merely alleviating downstream hemodynamic consequences. More broadly, these findings underscore an ongoing shift in PAH management toward strategies that directly address the biological drivers of pulmonary vascular remodeling. In this context, sotatercept represents a mechanistically distinct addition to existing treatment paradigms, complementing vasodilator-based therapies with an approach aimed at restoring pulmonary vascular homeostasis.

Looking ahead, several questions remain. The optimal timing of sotatercept initiation, its positioning relative to existing combination strategies, and its long-term safety in broader real-world populations warrant continued investigation. Nevertheless, the data presented here mark an important milestone. They suggest that the benefits of targeting activin signaling extend across geographic and ethnic boundaries and that sotatercept may meaningfully alter the disease trajectory of PAH in Asia and beyond. Taken together, this work highlights a pivotal transition in PAH therapeutics—from treating elevated pressure to correcting vascular pathology—illustrating how insights into disease biology can be translated into tangible clinical benefit.
