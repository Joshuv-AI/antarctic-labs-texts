ABSTRACT
The high antigenic diversity of HIV has been a major obstacle to development of a broadly protective vaccine. Nevertheless, protective HIV broadly neutralizing antibodies (bnAbs) exist and have been proposed as templates for vaccine development1–6. Germline targeting is a conceptually radical vaccine design approach to elicit bnAbs, aiming to prime rare bnAb-precursor B cells possessing predetermined human genetic and structural features shared with template bnAbs, and then guide B cell affinity maturation to potent bnAb evolution with heterologous boosters7–11. Although the approach has shown promise in clinical12–17 and preclinical18–34 studies, it faces many immunological challenges and, to date, has not succeeded in generating bnAbs in humans or non-transgenic animals. Here we report testing of an adjuvanted protein germline-targeting vaccine in outbred non-human primates. The vaccine generated bnAb-class memory B cells and sera capable of neutralizing diverse HIV clinical isolates. bnAb lineages were generated in at least 50% of animals, achieving up to 67% neutralization breadth compared with the reference bnAb. Vaccine-induced bnAbs exhibited precise structural mimicry of human bnAb interactions with HIV envelope (Env), matching the germline-targeting predictions. Furthermore, serum bnAb activity developed in 44% of animals and in one instance reached titres expected to confer protection against diverse HIV isolates. These results demonstrate proof of principle that germline-targeting vaccines can reproducibly elicit prespecified classes of bnAbs to prespecified epitopes under endogenous conditions, supporting further optimization of this approach for HIV vaccine development.

Main

The HIV envelope (Env) glycoprotein trimer displays high antigenic variability from one isolate to another, posing a major challenge for prophylactic vaccine design. Critically, for the feasibility of an antibody-based HIV vaccine, multiple classes of bnAbs binding to different epitopes on Env have been identified6,35. Passively administered bnAbs can protect against infection with diverse viruses in both non-human primates (NHPs) and humans3–5. Vaccine generation of bnAbs is complicated by the inability of classical Env immunization strategies to activate bnAb precursors from the naive antibody repertoire7–10. In the germline-targeting concept, rare bnAb-precursor naive B cells sharing key genetic features of a particular bnAb must be primed by an engineered germline-targeting immunogen (priming immunogen). A series of engineered heterologous boost immunogens must then sequentially boost those bnAb-class B cells to evolve increasingly bnAb-like affinities through affinity maturation in germinal centres (GCs)6,11,15. Results from phase 1 clinical trials have demonstrated that germline-targeting priming immunogens can elicit bnAb precursors of the VRC01 class consistently in humans12–17. One trial further demonstrated that a single heterologous boost after a germline-targeting prime increased bnAb-precursor maturation15. To date, germline-targeting sequential vaccination has elicited bnAbs only in highly permissive mouse models with bnAb-precursor naive B cells at artificially high frequencies and limited competition from non-bnAb precursor B cells20,21,27. Many immunological and vaccine design challenges remain, and no germline-targeting vaccine has successfully generated mature bnAbs in humans or NHPs. The immunological challenges have wide implications, and the vaccine protein design challenges are relevant for many difficult pathogens. Here we successfully designed and tested a full germline-targeting sequential vaccination regimen.

A complete germline-targeting vaccine

The HIV Env V3-glycan supersite (including the prominent Asn332 glycan) is targeted by multiple bnAbs including BG18, the most potent V3-glycan bnAb isolated to date6,35,36 (Fig. 1a). BG18 lacks insertions or deletions, which is favourable for vaccine design6,25,35,37,38. BG18-class bnAb precursor B cells have been identified in the normal human B cell repertoire25 and rhesus macaques39, and these rare BG18-class precursors have been successfully primed in rhesus macaques with a germline-targeting HIV Env trimer immunogen, N332-GT539,40. Two types of BG18-class B cell receptors (BCRs) isolated from rhesus macaques have been defined: types I and III39. BG18-class precursor B cells occur at substantially (around eightfold) lower frequencies in rhesus macaques than in humans (type I at 1 in 50 million human naive B cells)39, making rhesus macaques a stringent model for vaccine-generated BG18-class bnAbs. It remained to be determined whether primed bnAb-class B cells are true bnAb precursors capable of developing into authentic bnAbs, or would terminate affinity maturation after one immunization due to antibody structural constraints or immunological cell biological constraints41–43. Thus, heterologous booster immunizations represent critical tests for BG18-class bnAb vaccine development and multiple germline-targeting and immunological concepts. Boost immunogens (Fig. 1b and Extended Data Fig. 1a) were designed based on mature BG18 structural interactions with Env, characteristics of BG18-class antibodies identified after N332-GT5 priming39, immunological considerations and boost-stage-specific data. The V3-glycan epitope in N332-GT5 contains 20 engineered mutations (germline-targeting mutations) to enable binding to diverse BG18-class precursors. Thus, the N332-GT5 V3-glycan epitope differs from clinical isolate wild-type (WT) Env trimers25,39. BG18-class monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) from N332-GT5-immunized rhesus macaques39 had no detectable affinity for WT Env trimers or for a booster candidate trimer with only three germline-targeting mutations (SF162P3-B20) (Extended Data Fig. 1b). We hypothesized that Env trimers with germline-targeting mutations enabling measurable affinity for post-prime B cells would be needed for the first heterologous boost (boost 1).

To devise a germline-targeting vaccination regimen capable of guiding bnAb affinity maturation, we followed five principles: (1) each booster should contain fewer germline-targeting mutations than the previous immunogen20 (shepherding; Fig. 1c); (2) boosters should possess an affinity gradient to help guide to affinity maturation toward bnAb development9,22 (Extended Data Fig. 1c); (3) WT Env trimer boosters should be incorporated as early as possible20 (Fig. 1c); (4) boosters should present V3-glycan epitope variants representative of HIV Env diversity20 (Fig. 1d,e and Extended Data Fig. 2); and (5) all boosters should exhibit native-like prefusion structures, glycoforms and antigenic profiles, and good thermostabilities (Extended Data Figs. 3 and 4). Immunizations with WT Env variants aimed to increase affinity for diverse Envs representing authentic clinical isolates (polishing). To implement these concepts, we designed three boost 1 immunogens (B46, B54 and B50) and one boost 2 immunogen (B20) to gradually reintroduce the WT V3-glycan epitope (Fig. 1b,c). WT trimers of increasing diversity were then used to further mature the response: a single WT trimer (DU156) for boost 3, a cocktail of five WT trimers (ABCG cocktail) for boosts 4 and 5, a cocktail of four trimers from recent clinical isolates (AMP cocktail) for boost 6 and, lastly, a cocktail of four trimer nanoparticles (NPs), two from the ABCG cocktail and two from the AMP cocktail, for boost 7 (Fig 1b–e and Extended Data Figs. 1–4). This sequence of immunogens resulted in the exposure of 12 fully glycosylated BG18 epitopes to the rhesus macaque immune system (Fig. 1d), including diverse glycosylated V1 loops within HIV diversity hotspots (Fig. 1e).

Four rhesus macaque study groups received different vaccine regimens (Fig. 1b and Extended Data Fig. 1a), all beginning with N332-GT5 priming39,40. Group 1 (control) received homologous boosts with N332-GT5 until boost 5. Groups 2, 3 and 4 each received a different heterologous boost 1 (B46, B54 or B50, respectively). After boost 1, groups 2–4 received identical regimens starting with B20 trimer as boost 2. N332-GT5 soluble protein trimer25 was adjuvanted with SMNP44 as the priming immunization using a slow-delivery escalating dose protocol39,40. All boosts were bolus immunizations adjuvanted with SMNP.

Sequential boosting of bnAb precursors

All immunogens induced antigen-specific serum IgG responses (Extended Data Fig. 5a). Deeper analysis initially focused on memory B (Bmem) cells. Antigen-specific Bmem cells were identified as double-positive for N332-GT5 labelled with two distinct fluorophores (N332-GT5++), and epitope-specific Bmem cells were defined as double-positive for N332-GT5 and negative for an epitope-knockout probe (N332-GT5++KO–) by flow cytometry analysis of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) (Extended Data Fig. 5b). After boost 1, all three heterologous boosters induced similar frequencies of epitope-specific Bmem cells compared with a homologous boost with N332-GT5 (Fig. 2a). Notably, all of the animals in groups 2–4 possessed high frequencies of epitope-specific boost-immunogen-binding (hereafter, boost binding) Bmem cells at week 12, in contrast to group 1 (group 1 versus groups 2–4 combined, P < 0.0001; Fig. 2b). Before boosting, all 24 animals had low levels of boost-binding B cells (Extended Data Fig. 6a). N332-GT5 epitope-specific B cells were next sorted from PBMCs for BCR sequencing and bioinformatic-based determination of BG18-class type I B cell responses (Methods and Extended Data Fig. 6b). After boost 1, all 22 animals tested had detectable BG18 type I BCRs with heavy-chain complementarity determining region 3 (HCDR3) lengths of at least 20 amino acids (Fig. 2c and Extended Data Fig. 6c,d). Groups 2–4 had higher BG18 type I BCR frequencies than group 1 (P = 0.01; Fig. 2c). BG18 type I B cells from all animals of groups 2–4 bound to the heterologous boost Env (Fig. 2d). Over 70% boost binding was obtained in each heterologous boost 1 group, compared with a median of 0% for group 1 (P < 0.001; Fig. 2d). By week 18, boost-binding BG18 type I B cells increased to a median of 100% in groups 2–4 (Extended Data Fig. 6e). Heterologous boost 1 was therefore highly successful at recalling and shepherding BG18-class B cells.

Antigen- and epitope-specific GC B (BGC) cells were next examined from lymph node (LN) fine-needle aspirates (FNAs) 3 weeks after boost 1. The heterologous boost groups had significantly more boost-binding and BG18-class BGC cells compared with group 1 (P < 0.001 and 0.039; Extended Data Fig. 6f). A median value of over 70% boost-binding BG18 type I BGC cells was obtained in each of the three heterologous boost-1 groups (Extended Data Fig. 6f), similar to the circulating Bmem cells. BGC and Bmem cell responses together demonstrate that each heterologous boost 1 robustly boosted the primed BG18-class responses, even with reintroduction of both Asn133 and Asn137 V1 glycans.

After boost 2 (B20), 10–20% of epitope-specific Bmem cells across groups 2–4 bound to boost 2 compared with around 5% in group 1 (P = 0.015; Fig. 2a,b). Almost all (22 out of 24) animals had detectable BG18 type I BCRs (Fig. 2c). In total, 30–100% of BG18-class cells from groups 2–4 bound to the boost 2 Env trimer, in contrast to none from group 1 (P < 0.01; Fig. 2d), indicating successful germline targeting and sequential affinity maturation.

WT Env trimer recognition

After boost 3 with a WT HIV Env trimer (DU156), all 18 of the heterologous boosted animals had epitope-specific B cells that bound to the boost 3 DU156 WT trimer (group 1 versus groups 2–4 combined; P = 0.1365; Fig. 2b). WT Env trimer-binding BGC cells were also elevated in LNs (group 1 versus groups 2–4 combined; P = 0.045; Extended Data Fig. 6f). BG18-class Bmem cell frequencies among groups 2–4 trended higher than group 1 (P = 0.087; Fig. 2c). Notably, groups 2–4 had significantly higher WT Env trimer-binding bnAb-class cells compared with group 1 (P = 0.003; Fig. 2d), and the majority of animals in groups 2–4 had WT Env trimer-binding BG18-class cells (14 out of 18; Fig. 2d). These findings demonstrate that vaccination can generate BG18-class B cells capable of WT HIV Env trimer binding.

Broad HIV Env trimer binding

After the ABCG Env cocktail immunization (polishing) as boost 4, group 2–4 B cells had higher Env cocktail binding compared with group 1 (P < 0.0001; Fig. 2b). All four groups received the ABCG Env cocktail at boost 5 (Fig. 1b and Extended Data Fig. 1a). After boost 5, Env cocktail binding by groups 2–4 had numerical but statistically insignificant increases over group 1 (P = 0.056; Fig. 2b). A total of 12 out of 18 animals from groups 2–4 had detectable BG18-class type I BCRs after boost 5 (Fig. 2c). Notably, over 50% of the BG18-class type I BCRs from groups 2–4 bound to at least 3 ABCG cocktail Env trimer proteins (Extended Data Fig. 6g). Overall, immunization with multiclade Env trimers for boosts 4 and 5 shepherded many BG18-class B cells to bind to diverse native HIV Env trimers with varied V1 loops and V3-glycan epitopes.

To further broaden the response, a cocktail of AMP trimers was used as boost 6. After boost 6, 11 animals from groups 2–4 maintained detectable BG18-class type I BCRs, over 50% of which bound to the AMP trimers (Fig. 2c,d). Group 1 was discontinued after boost 5. For boost 7, a cocktail of trimer NPs was used; 11 animals from groups 2–4 again maintained detectable BG18 type I BCRs, almost all binding to the NP trimers (Fig. 2c,d). Thus, BG18-class type I BCRs were further shepherded by boosts 6 and 7 and detected for over 2 years in these animals.

Maturation of bnAb-class lineages

To understand the processes underlying vaccine shepherding and formally determine relationships among BG18-class Bmem cells over time, clonal lineage assignments were made for all BG18-class cells. The highest number of clonal families were present after boost 1 (Extended Data Fig. 7). After boost 5, 1–3 BG18-class type I lineages were present in each of the 12 positive animals from groups 2–4 (Fig. 2e and Extended Data Fig. 7). For group 4, a median of 29% of the post-boost 1 BG18-class type I clones was still present after boost 5 (Fig. 2e). All post-boost 5 BG18-class lineages in groups 2–4 were also detected at at least two earlier timepoints, most at all previous timepoints, and most were maintained after boosts 6 and 7 (Fig. 2e).

Phylogenetic trees were built for each BG18-class type I clonal family with a Bmem cell sequence present after boost 5 and a previous timepoint (Fig. 2f,g and Extended Data Fig. 7). BG18-class type I clonal lineages spanned all timepoints and included both memory and BGC cells (Fig. 2f,g and Extended Data Fig. 7). Extensive somatic hypermutation (SHM) was observed for most bnAb-class lineages, with clear progression through boosts 1–5 and more modest accumulation after boosts 6 and 7. Furthermore, broad intraclonal diversity was observed for most lineages. Diversification and maturation patterns varied. The dominant BG18-class type I lineage from animal RQd20 had around 3,300 clonal members with broad early diversification observed after boost 1, and numerous clades accumulating extensive parallel SHM until boost 5 (Fig. 2f). The dominant BG18-class type I lineage from RYo20 instead had more uniform early diversification, with one branch point then diverging at boost 2 and subsequently exhibiting substantial further maturation and diversification until boost 7 (Fig. 2g). Overall, BG18-class type I clonal lineages developed marked clonal expansions and extensive SHM through six sequential immunizations while acquiring WT Env-binding ability.

Broad assessment of amino acid SHM confirmed the lineage tree observations. Groups 2–4 had a higher percentage of SHM in the variable region of the heavy chain (VH) after boosts 1, 2 and 3 compared with group 1 (P values after boosts 1–5, respectively: 0.031, 0.012, 0.004, 0.163 and 0.160; Fig. 3a). The median VH SHM percentage was over 14% in all three heterologous boosting groups after boost 5 (Fig. 3a), and the most mutated BCRs were nearly 30% mutated (Extended Data Fig. 7). For the variable region of the light chain (Vκ/λ; Fig. 3b and Extended Data Fig. 7), groups 2–4 had higher SHM percentage than group 1 at all timepoints except post-boost 1 (P values after boosts 1–5, respectively: 0.417, 0.003, 0.042, 0.010 and 0.026; Fig. 3b). VH and Vκ/λ SHM continued increasing after boosts 6 and 7 in groups 2–4 (Fig. 3a,b). After boost 5, BG18-class B cells using VH4 and Vλ3 genes similar to those of human BG18 were enriched, indicative of successful mimicry induced by vaccination (Fig. 3c,d). Other BG18-class clones with diverse VH and Vκ/λ genes were maintained, indicating numerous paths for BG18-class bnAb development after vaccination (Fig. 3c,d). BG18-class type I HCDR3s exhibited high diversity in the V–D and D–J junctions outside the shared D gene, yet 100% (296 out of 296) of post-boost-5 HCDR3s included key non-templated contact residues—Glu two residues after the D gene and Leu/Phe at the last D gene position (Supplementary Data 1)—as previously reported for BG18-class type I responses after N332-GT5 priming in rhesus macaques39. Thus, sequential boosting maintained these key residues.

To directly quantify affinity maturation, at least 1 BG18-class type I BCR per animal per timepoint, if available, was selected for antibody synthesis. After the cocktail immunizations, preference was given to BCRs binding the most Env probes. Affinities were determined for 238 BG18-class mAbs, for all immunogens plus WT Env trimers for HIV isolates BG50520,45,46 and V703-053747 (Supplementary Data 2). Post-boost-1 mAbs from groups 3 and 4 had higher median affinities for their boost-1 trimers (B54 and B50) compared with group 1 mAbs (Extended Data Fig. 7e), reflecting favourable directional affinity maturation. By contrast, mAbs from groups 2 and 1 had similar affinities for the group 2 boost 1 trimer (B46), probably because B54 and B50 have substantially fewer germline-targeting mutations (Fig. 1c). Most post-boost 2 mAbs from groups 2–4 had affinity for the boost 2 trimer B20 (3 germline-targeting mutations), with median dissociation constant (KD) values of 110–188 nM, whereas none of five post-boost-2 mAbs from group 1 showed affinity for B20 (Extended Data Fig. 7f), demonstrating the importance of the heterologous immunogens for affinity maturation toward binding of authentic HIV Env.

To assess affinity for diverse WT HIV Env isolates, we computed median KD values to 11 WT trimers for each mAb (Fig. 3e). Binding to diverse WT trimers was detectable after boost 2 and increased significantly in groups 2–4 after boost 3 (Fig. 3e). Group median KD values were similar between boosts 4 and 5, with no further gains for boosts 6 and 7 (Fig. 3e). Group 1 mAbs had no affinity for WT Envs (Fig. 3e). Thus, germline-targeting sequential immunization through boost 4 produced bnAb-class Bmem BCRs with substantial affinity for diverse WT trimers.

Among 84 BG18-class type I antibodies with detectable affinity for N332-containing BG505-MD3920,48,49, none (0 of 84) had detectable affinity for the N332T variant of BG505-MD39 lacking the N332 glycan (Fig. 3f). Thus, BG18-class type I antibodies elicited by vaccination bind to native Env in an N332 glycan-dependent manner, as does the human bnAb BG18.

Elicited mAbs have bnAb-like structures

To compare recognition modes of the vaccine-elicited BG18-class antibodies to mature BG18, cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures were determined for two BG18-class type I antibodies in a complex with WT Env trimers (Fig. 4a–d and Extended Data Fig. 8). Both antibodies used VH4 and Vλ3 genes similar to those of BG18, and RRr20_wk72_07 had an HCDR3 length of 23 amino acids, matching BG18, whereas RQd20_wk56_28 had an HCDR3 of 21 amino acids. Both antibodies showed binding modes matching that of BG18, with similar orientations of the heavy chain and light chain (Fig. 4a), nearly identical HCDR3 interactions with the GDIR motif (Fig. 4b) and substantial overlap in Env contact residues (Extended Data Fig. 8b and Supplementary Table 1). RQd20_wk56_28 made additional interactions to variable region V4 and β19 using HCDRs 1–3, probably due to having a shorter HCDR3 (Extended Data Fig. 8b,c). Both vaccine-elicited BG18-class type I antibodies made substantial interactions to the Asn332 glycan analogous to interactions made by mature BG18. Such interactions were not made by inferred-germline BG18 or BG18-class B cells elicited by N332-GT5 priming alone25,39 (Fig. 4c). Furthermore, RM_RRr20_07 probably exhibited a similar HCDR1 conformational change to human BG18 as it evolved from its germline configuration, resulting in several acquired contacts to the Asn332 glycan (Extended Data Fig. 8d). BG18-class type III responses

Three types of BG18-class antibodies have been described, all of which use long (at least 20 amino acids) HCDR3s and mimic the BG18 binding footprint and angle of approach25,39. Type I BCRs can be identified bioinformatically, based on their HCDR3 sequence similarities to the prototype BG18 bnAb25. Types II and III use more diverse sequence characteristics than type I, increasing the BG18-class B cells that can be primed and the potential paths to bnAb induction. Type II identification requires evidence of specificity for the V3-glycan epitope in addition to HCDR3 and light-chain sequence features25,39. Type III identity requires direct structural analysis to confirm BG18 binding mode39.

The full set of epitope-specific B cells with long HCDR3s (at least 20 amino acids) generated by immunization were screened for expanded lineages present at multiple timepoints, including after the final boost. All but three such lineages were BG18-class type I. Among the three non-type I lineages, one highly expanded lineage from the animal RTq20 contained around 6,500 clonal members and was present at all of the timepoints (Extended Data Fig. 9a). Expanded lineages from two additional animals were identified (animals RVj20 and RVz20), with lineage RVz20 containing around 1,000 clonal members (Extended Data Fig. 9a). The three lineages were also non-type-II. All three used the same VH gene and had similar HCDR3 features, and two used the same Vκ/λ gene (Extended Data Fig. 9b). As the BCRs had been characterized as V3-glycan epitope-specific by B cell sorting, we hypothesized that these related lineages should each be designated BG18 type III clonal lineages.

Proof of a BG18-like binding mode required structural analysis. We therefore determined a cryo-EM structure of a post-boost 4, putative BG18 type III Fab, RVz20_wk72_08 (Extended Data Fig. 9b), in a complex with the BG505 MD39 trimer (Fig. 4e–i). Notably, despite possessing immunogenetics unrelated to BG18, RVz20_wk72_08 showed substantial structural mimicry of the BG18–trimer interaction, including angle of approach and orientations of the heavy chain and light chain distinct from other V3-glycan bnAbs (Fig. 4e,i), HCDR3 interactions with GDIR (Fig. 4f), extensive interactions to the Asn332 glycan (Fig. 4g), and HCDR2 and LCDR3 interactions to the Asn392 glycan (Fig. 4h and Extended Data Fig. 9c). The HCDR3 of RVz20_wk72_08 and the other putative type III antibodies used the rhesus macaque D2-12 or D2-10 genes (Extended Data Fig. 9b,c). Rhesus macaque D2-12 showed partial sequence similarity to rhesus macaque D3-41/Hu D3-3 in the paratope39 (Extended Data Fig. 9c,d). On the basis of these structural similarities, we concluded that the RVz20_wk72_08 lineage and the two related lineages constituted BG18 type III responses.

The number of animals that produced any BG18-class response (type I, type III or both) after boost 5 increased to 78% once type III responders were included (Extended Data Fig. 9e). All type III antibodies tested had high affinity for diverse WT trimers and were specific for the V3-glycan epitope (Extended Data Fig. 9f). Thus, germline-targeting and sequential boosting in rhesus macaques generated highly expanded BG18 type III responses, in addition to type I responses, that could be affinity matured to bind diverse clinical isolates.

Vaccine-elicited serum bnAbs

The primary goal of HIV vaccination is to induce bnAbs in serum. Serum neutralization was evaluated after boosts 4–7 on a sentinel heterologous tier 2 pseudovirus (6811.v7.c18) that was neutralized by the best 12 BG18-class mAbs described above (Supplementary Table 4). After the boost 7 trimer–NP immunization, 7 out of 18 animals in groups 2–4 exhibited serum neutralization of the sentinel pseudovirus (Supplementary Table 5), consistent with the mAb data.

For the eight animals producing the best BG18 bnAbs in the memory compartment after boost 4 or 5 (Fig. 5b), we tested serum neutralization on the BG18-sensitive 63-pseudovirus panel after boosts 4–7 (Fig. 5c–e and Supplementary Tables 6–11). The highest-quality serum neutralization activity was detected after boost 7, with substantial increases in breadth (Fig. 5c) and geometric mean half-maximum inhibitory dilution (ID50) potency (Fig. 5e). At 2 weeks after boost 7, all 8 animals exhibited bnAb activity, with breadth relative to BG18 ranging from 16% to a marked 84% (Fig. 5c,e and Supplementary Tables 9 and 11). Mean neutralization breadth of the 8 animals was 41% relative to BG18. The geometric mean ID50 titres ranged from 52 to 481, with an overall geometric mean ID50 of 107 (Fig. 5c,d and Supplementary Table 9). Notably, serum neutralization breadth and potency were largely stable from 2 to 6 weeks after boost 7 (Fig. 5c–e and Supplementary Tables 9 and 10).

The epitope specificity of the post-boost-7 serum neutralizing response was assessed using two approaches. Asn332-glycan-variant (with or without Asn332 glycan) pseudoviruses were produced for four viral isolates neutralized by the majority of the eight animals. Serum neutralization from 6 of 8 animals was substantially reduced in the absence of the Asn332 glycan (Supplementary Table 12), confirming that the serum neutralization was predominantly directed to the V3-glycan epitope, per the vaccine design concept. Moreover, for the 8 animals with both mAbs and serum tested on the 63-pseudovirus panel (Fig. 5b–f and Supplementary Table 11), all mAb IC50 values were plotted against serum ID50 values for each individual animal (Fig. 5g). BG18-class mAb and serum neutralization values correlated strongly across all datapoints (P < 0.0001, r = 0.7131), and on an individual-animal level (Fig. 5g). When both post-boost-4/5 and post-boost-6/7 mAbs were tested for an individual animal, the post-boost-6/7 mAbs had higher r values but both had significant correlations (Fig. 5g). These correlations indicated that the serum neutralization was mediated by the predominant BG18-class lineages characterized in each animal. In conclusion, the AMP trimer cocktail and trimer–NP cocktail immunizations induced substantial serum bnAb activity in multiple animals receiving a germline-targeting sequential vaccination regimen.

Discussion

The germline-targeting vaccine design strategy aims to develop precision vaccines for HIV and other highly variable pathogens by inducing prespecified classes of bnAbs to predetermined epitopes through a series of priming, shepherding and polishing vaccinations. It has not yet been shown that germline-targeting vaccines can induce bnAbs in humans or NHPs. Here, we have demonstrated in NHPs that germline-targeting vaccine regimens can guide affinity maturation to produce prespecified bnAbs in Bmem cells and serum with excellent neutralization breadth. Structural studies confirmed precision induction of the targeted class of bnAb, and the bnAbs were generated in many animals. This proof of principle for serum bnAb induction by germline-targeting vaccines represents a major advance towards the development of a bnAb-inducing HIV vaccine. Achieving these results in NHPs, which have substantially rarer BG18-class bnAb precursors than humans, suggests that similar or better results may be attainable in humans. Notably, the N332-GT5 priming immunogen is currently being tested in a human clinical trial50.

The immune responses in the best performing animal provide a new benchmark. The serum of this animal had 84% neutralization breadth relative to mature BG18, which corresponds to approximately 52% breadth overall on standard HIV global neutralization panels. The potency of those serum bnAbs, a geometric mean ID50 neutralization titre of 481, is expected to confer 75 to 90% protection against HIV (or SHIV) for diverse global isolates5,51,52. This serum neutralization breadth and potency elicited by vaccination is comparable to that observed in elite neutralizers from natural infection53, raising the prospect that further optimized vaccines may elicit bnAbs superior to the best bnAbs occurring after HIV infection. Four other animals had geometric mean neutralization titres expected to confer around 50% protection against clinical isolates5,51,52, supporting further vaccine optimization.

These experiments demonstrate multiple immunological findings of note. Extremely rare precursor B cells can be reproducibly expanded and sustained through multiple rounds of immunizations, with iterative rounds of GC affinity maturation and Bmem cell development. In multiple animals, BG18-class lineages containing hundreds of memory cells developed over time. More generally, subdominant B cells can continue to participate successfully in sequential rounds of immunization, as evidenced by the fact that BG18-class B cells comprised only around 4% of the V3-glycan epitope-specific Bmem cells after boost 1 or 2 (1% of the N332-GT5-specific B cells) and yet were sustained in the majority of animals with progressive affinity maturation at each step of sequential immunization. Moreover, sequential boosting with heterologous antigens was able to support the persistence of many bnAb clones per animal, and could generate high bnAb levels of affinity maturation. Lastly, immunization with multivalent nanoparticles demonstrated that extensively affinity-matured vaccine-induced bnAb Bmem cells were capable of differentiating into plasma cells producing substantial levels of serum neutralizing antibodies.

Complementing the findings reported here, cross-neutralizing Abs to the HIV Apex epitope have recently been elicited in rhesus macaques, also supporting the concept of germline-targeting vaccines54,55. However, the specific Apex responses are strongly dependent on a unique D-gene motif (EDDY) that is germline encoded in rhesus macaques but lacks a human homologue, making direct human translation of current rhesus macaque Apex vaccine responses uncertain32,55,56.

Our study has limitations. We note opportunities to improve on the regimens tested. The boosting regimens had 7 boosters; future work should focus on developing improved sequential regimens that reproducibly elicit the broadest responses in most vaccine recipients with the fewest number of immunizations. Our data point to potential opportunities for regimen simplification. For example, the relatively minor gains in SHM, affinity and neutralization capacity at boost 5 (the second immunization with the ABCG cocktail) suggest that boost 5 might be dispensable. The favourable impact of trimer–NPs on serum neutralization at boost 7 suggests that such NPs should be tested at earlier stages, and higher-valency NPs should be developed and tested57–60, potentially allowing for fewer vaccinations. The 34-week interval between boosts 1 and 2 was due to logistical reasons; regimens should therefore be tested with a shorter interval. Cocktail compositions should be optimized, potentially reducing the number of components. Other technologies could assist translation of improved regimens: pulsatile release technologies might help to reduce the number of vaccinations while still allowing for multiple sequential boosters61, and mRNA vaccine platforms could be tested to facilitate scalable clinical manufacturing and explore potential advantages of membrane-anchored trimer delivery48,49. We also note that the durability of these bnAb responses has not been explored—this will need to be understood and maximized for an effective vaccine.

Overall, these findings demonstrate a series of key successes for germline-targeting and sequential vaccination concepts, in addition to new immunological findings, all of which have implications for future vaccine development.

Methods

Study design

The objective of the study was to evaluate germline-targeting sequential vaccine regimens for their ability to elicit BG18-class bnAbs in rhesus macaques. Twenty-four rhesus macaques were weight-, age- and sex-matched and assigned to one of four experimental groups—each group comprised six animals. The researchers were not blinded at any point. Immunizations were administered subcutaneously in the left and right mid-thighs. All of the groups were administered N332-GT5 and SMNP through escalating dose (ED). N332-GT5 was dosed 50 μg per injection site (100 μg total) and SMNP was dosed at 375 μg per injection site (750 μg total). This dose is consistent with the previous N332-GT5 immunizations done in rhesus macaques33,39,40. For the ED immunization, the total dose of N332-GT5 and SMNP was split over seven administrations, given every other day, in an exponentially increasing dose (0.1, 0.215, 0.58, 1.575, 4.28, 11.65 and 31.6 μg) as previously described39,62. All individual immunogen boosts were 100 µg per immunogen per dose with the same amount of SMNP used at every boost. Group 1 was given four boosts of N332-GT5 and SMNP and the fifth was the same cocktail as all other groups. The remaining groups had the first boost as follows: group 2 was B46, group 3 was B54 and group 4 was B50. Subsequent boosts for groups 2–4 were the same: boost 2 was B20, boost 3 was DU156, boosts 4 and 5 were a combination of trimers (ABCG cocktail) at 20 μg each per dose: AD8, Q23.17, P1981, TRO_11 and DU4221. Boost 6 was another combination of immunogens (AMP cocktail): 0636, 0860, 0712 and 1026. Finally, groups 2–4 were boosted with a combination of nanoparticle immunogens (trimer–NP cocktail): 1026-NP at 18 µg, 0860-NP at 18 µg, TRO_11-NP at 36 µg and AD8-NP at 18 µg. Group 1 was discontinued after boost 5.

Animals

Adult Indian rhesus macaques (Macaca mulatta) were housed at the Emory National Primate Research Center and maintained in accordance with NIH guidelines. All procedures were approved by the Emory University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) under protocol 202200105. Animal care facilities are accredited by the US Department of Agriculture (USDA) and the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AALAC) International. Rhesus macaques for this study were of mixed sex, an average age of 3 years and a weight range of 4–6.5 kg at the time of the priming immunization. Animals were pair-housed for the duration of the study. Veterinarians performed FNAs to bilaterally sample LNs in the rhesus macaques. Palpation was used to identify draining LNs. A 22-gauge needle with an attached 3 ml syringe was passed into the LN a maximum of five times. The samples were then placed into RPMI medium with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. If red blood cell contamination was observed, an ammonium chloride-potassium lysing buffer was used. The samples were counted, cryopreserved and stored in liquid nitrogen until the time of analysis. Blood was collected throughout the study in NaCitrate CPT tubes (BD biosciences) for PBMCs and plasma isolation and frozen. Serum was collected through serum clot tubes and frozen. Animals were treated with anaesthesia (ketamine (5–10 mg per kg) or telazol (3–6 mg per kg)) and analgesics for subcutaneous immunizations, LN FNAs and blood draws according to veterinarian recommendations and IACUC approved protocols. After completion of the proposed study and approval of the veterinarian staff, the animals were released to the centre for reuse by other researchers.

Design of boost immunogens

To guide the design of the boost-1 candidates, we used mAbs derived from BGC and Bmem cells induced by the priming immunogen in a previous NHP experiment39; the mAbs included both BG18-class type I bnAb precursors and non-BG18-class V3-glycan epitope-specific competitors. Post-prime BG18-class mAbs had no detectable affinity for WT trimers from representative BG18-sensitive isolates DU156 and BG505, or for a near-native trimer with only three germline-targeting mutations (SF162P3-B20) (Extended Data Fig. 1b). We hypothesized that such trimers would probably fail to boost the rhesus macaque responses primed by N332-GT5 consistently, and that trimers with additional germline-targeting mutations and higher affinity for the post-prime bnAb precursors would be needed for boost 1. We noted that in the previous rhesus macaque study of N332-GT5 priming, the binding of the post-prime V3-glycan-specific non-BG18-class competitors to candidate boost-1 trimers required the absence of glycosylation sites in the V1 loop, whereas the binding of most post-prime BG18-class responses could accommodate the presence of V1 glycans39. Here, to increase the probability that the first heterologous boost would activate previously primed BG18-class responses rather than non-BG18 competitors specific for the V3-glycan epitope, we designed the three boost-1 trimers with fully glycosylated V1 loops and verified glycan occupancy (Extended Data Fig. 3). These boost-1 trimers were (1) BG505_B46, a trimer derived from N332-GT5 with medium affinity (median KD of around 1 µM) for post-prime BG18-class mAbs; (2) BG505_B54, also derived from N332-GT5 but with fewer germline-targeting mutations and lower affinity for post-prime BG18-class mAbs (median KD of around 6.5 µM); and (3) 001428_B50, a trimer with binding properties similar to BG505_B54 (median KD of 5 µM) but derived from a different HIV isolate from clade C, 001428 (ref. 63). These three boost-1 trimers are referred to as B46, B54 and B50, respectively.

For the boosters after boost 1, we were not able to use mAbs induced by the previous immunization to guide booster design, so we used other lines of evidence to support our booster choices. For boost 2, we wanted to use a trimer from a different isolate with a different V1 loop compared with the prime and boost 1, and with fewer germline-targeting mutations than in boost 1, for reasons explained above. We elected to use a stabilized trimer based on the isolate SF162P3 with three germline-targeting mutations from N332-GT5 designed to interact with the tip of the HCDR3 for BG18 precursors. We hypothesized that these three mutations, which were also present in the prime and boost-1 candidates, would contribute to the boost-2 affinity for post-boost-1 BG18-class BCRs. The boost-2 trimer, termed SF162P3-B20 but referred to as B20, had substantial affinity (KD = 11 nM) for a minimally mutated variant of BG18 representing a partially mature bnAb (minBG18.6, with 6% amino acid SHM across VH and Vλ25) (Extended Data Fig. 1c), supporting the idea that B20 might boost partially mature BG18-class B cells. B20 had higher affinity (KD = 1 nM) for BG18 itself (Extended Data Fig. 1c), reflecting an affinity gradient that might help to select for SHM toward bnAb development9,22.

After the B20 boost 2 with only 3 germline-targeting mutations, for boost 3 we used a WT trimer. To increase the probability of boosting BG18-class responses, we selected a WT trimer from the DU156 isolate that is highly sensitive to BG18 neutralization. Like B20, the DU156 trimer had an appropriate affinity gradient, with substantial affinity for minBG18.6 and higher affinity for BG18 (Extended Data Fig. 1c).

At the time of boost-4 immunization, we had evidence that BG18-class Bmem cells were still present in PBMCs from all groups and were accumulating SHM after boost 2. However, we did not yet have results from after boost 3. We therefore wanted to diversify the BG18-class response to ensure there was broad recognition of Env. For boost 4, we delivered a multiclade cocktail of native trimers derived from clades A, B, C and G. At the time of selecting the composition of boost 4, we did not know the affinities of post-DU156 BG18-class Abs for different trimers. We therefore selected five isolates from a collection of stabilized trimers that were sensitive to BG18 (p1981, Q23.17, AD8, TRO.11 and DU422-1). These trimers were stabilized by MD39 mutations (Q23.17, TRO.11 and DU422-1), MD64 mutations (AD8) or a new set of mutations called MD39_BET1 (p1981) identified in a mammalian display library screen that adds BG505 amino acids to gp41. These five isolates had diverse V3-glycan epitopes and, in particular, a range of V1 loop lengths and numbers of glycosylation sites that generally fall within the hotspots of the HIV Env distribution (Fig. 1e and Extended Data Fig. 2).

At the time of the boost-5 immunizations, we speculated, but did not know, that post-boost 4 BG18-class BCRs might have sufficient affinity and breadth to neutralize diverse isolates of HIV. We also expected, but did not know, that the serum responses induced by soluble trimers might not be strong. Thus, hoping to drive strong serum bnAb responses, we gave a second immunization of the same ABCG cocktail with the hypothesis that the post-boost 4 BG18-class Bmem BCRs would have substantial affinity for the ABCG trimers and a second immunization would cause a strong BG18-class plasma cell response.

For boost 6, we elected to switch to a different trimer cocktail. At the time, we did not yet have affinity or neutralization data from post-boost 4 or 5. However, as the ABCG cocktail used for boosts 4 and 5 was primarily derived from older isolates that are highly sensitive to BG18 neutralization, for boost 6 we selected trimers that would be more challenging for BG18-class BCR recognition and were derived from more recent isolates of HIV. The aim was to increase the breadth of neutralization by the developing BG18-class BCRs. We selected four trimers based on clade C isolates from the AMP trials4,47 that had detectable but lower affinity for minBG18.6 and BG18 compared with the ABCG cocktail (Extended Data Fig. 1c). These trimers were stabilized using a modified version of the repair and stabilize method and included the following mutations: 204I, 535N, 538F, 548F, 556P, 569G, 573F, 588E, 589V, 613T, 636G, 647F, 651F, 655I and 658V64,65. As with the ABCG cocktail, the AMP trimers also had diverse V3-glycan epitopes (Fig. 1d,e and Extended Data Fig. 2).

After boost 6, we assessed serum neutralization but detected no substantial breadth or potency after boosts 4, 5 or 6. At the time, we knew that BG18-class Bmem BCRs with affinity for native trimers were present at least after boost 3. We reasoned that vaccinating with nanoparticles would probably induce a stronger plasma response than soluble trimers due to improved avidity, B cell activation capacity and trafficking66–68. Thus, for boost 7, we used self-assembling protein nanoparticles that each display 4 trimers genetically fused to a 12-mer scaffold protein. We used a cocktail of four such trimer nanoparticles (trimer–NPs), based on 2 trimers from the ABCG cocktail (AD8 and TRO.11) and 2 trimers from the AMP cocktail (0860 and 1026). Among the two cocktails, these trimers have intermediate affinity for minBG18.6 (Extended Data Fig. 1c). The nanoparticle scaffold protein used here has been used previously to form nanoparticles for an epitope-scaffold antigen30. The amino acid sequences of all immunogens are provided in Supplementary Data 3.

Protein purification

For expression of the N332-GT5 priming immunogen, a stable CHO cell line clone was developed using the Leap-In transposon expression system (ATUM, US10041077). The DNA sequence encoding the N332-GT5 trimer and its corresponding signal peptide was synthesized, expression constructs were designed and engineered based on the proprietary Leap-In transposon platform. The production of the trimer protein involved comprehensive upstream cell culture and downstream purification process development, followed by scale-up to large-scale cGMP manufacturing. With the exception of the signal peptide, the amino acid sequence of this trimer matches the N332-GT5 protein sequence reported previously39.

All remaining trimer immunogens were expressed in HEK293F or Expi293 cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as described previously32 and purified by either 2G12 affinity chromatography (B46, B54, B20, DU156) or lectin affinity chromatography (B50, AD8, Q23.17, p1981, TRO.11, DU422-1, 0636, 0860, 0712, 1026). Mycoplasma testing was performed on cell lines occasionally, but no cell line authentication was performed. For lectin purification, trimers were incubated with 7.5 ml of lectin beads from Vector Laboratories (AL-1243) per litre of protein supernatant, essentially as described previously32. A disposable plastic column from Bio-Rad (732-1010) was used. The trimer was further purified on the Superdex 200 PG size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) column (Cytiva, 28-9893-35) on the AKTA pure 25 l HPLC system. Immunogen preps were confirmed to have low endotoxin using the Endosafe instrument (Charles River). Trimer–4-mer NPs (1026–4-mer, 0860–4-mer, TRO.11–4-mer, AD8–4-mer) were purified with lectin followed by purification using the Superose 6 16/600 PG column (Cytiva). The homogeneity of purified trimers evaluated by SEC multi-angle light scattering (SECMALS) with the DAWN HELEOS II and Optilab T-rEX detectors (Wyatt Technology). Proteins were stored in 1× tris-buffered saline (TBS) at −80 °C.

Biotinylated sorting probes were expressed with a His-tag and an Avi-tag, purified by Ni++ affinity chromatography followed by SEC, and biotinylated using the BirA biotin-protein ligase reaction kit (Avidity) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Antibody production

Antibodies were produced either by Genscript using the TurboCHO-HT2.0 expression system or using a high-throughput antibody production method carried out as described previously using the ExpiCHO expression system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in baffled 96-deep-well blocks according to the manufacturer’s instructions with minor adjustments69.

Flow cytometry

Fluorescent antigen probes were made, at room temperature, by combining fluorophore-conjugated streptavidin and biotinylated probes N332-GT5 (N332-GT5 WT), N332-GT5 epitope knockout (N332-GT5 KO), booster WT and booster KO) with an appropriate volume of PBS in small increments across 45 min. Frozen PBMC and LN FNA samples were thawed in RPMI medium with 10% FBS, 1% GlutaMAX and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (R10). Recovered cells were then stained with the appropriate antibody panel for the sample type.

Probes for each boost immunogen were included in flow cytometry staining for each respective group, enabling determination of the proportion of N332-GT5 epitope-specific cells cross-binding the boost immunogen (N332-GT5++KO–boost++).

Thawed cells were incubated with both N332-GT5-KO and boost-KO probes for 20 min at 4 °C. Cells were then sequentially incubated with the combined booster WT probes for 15 min at 4 °C and then with the combined N332-GT5 WT probes for 30 min at 4 °C. A master mix of surface staining antibodies was added next, 2 μg per 5 million cells of anti-human Total-Seq-C hashtag antibodies (BioLegend) was added after the surface staining and the samples were then incubated for 30 min at 4 °C. Cells were then washed twice and prepared for sorting on BD FACSymphony S6 (BD Biosciences). Epitope-specific B cells were sorted at all timepoints according to the gating strategy shown in Extended Data Fig. 5b,c.

The following reagents were used in the PBMC staining panel: Alexa Fluor 647 Streptavidin, BV421 Streptavidin, PE Streptavidin, PE-Cy7 Streptavidin, BV711 Streptavidin, BUV615 Streptavidin, eFluor780 Viability dye, CD3 BV510, CD14 BV510, CD8 BV510, CD16 BV510, CD20 BUV395, IgG BV605 and IgD AF488. The FNA staining panel included the following reagents: Alexa Fluor 647 Streptavidin, BV421 Streptavidin, PE Streptavidin, PE-Cy7 Streptavidin, BV711 Streptavidin, BUV615 Streptavidin, eFluor780 Viability dye, CD3 BV510, CD14 BV510, CD8 BV510, CD16 BV510, CD20 BUV395, IgG BV605, CD38 AF488, IgM PerCP-Cy5.5 and CD71 PE-CF594 (Supplementary Table 14).

Anti-CD38-AF488 was conjugated using purified anti-CD38 (OKT10; Nonhuman Primate Reagent Resource (NHPRR) PR-0056) and the AF488 antibody labelling kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A10235). Anti-CD71–PE-CF594 was a custom order from BD Biosciences, with anti-CD71 (L01.1; 347510) conjugated to PE-CF594. Some of the oligo-labelled streptavidins were custom orders from BioLegend using their standard oligo tags.

Single-B-cell sequencing

Sample preparation

Once sorted, epitope-specific PBMC and LN FNA B cells were washed with PBS and loaded onto the Chromium chip and controller according to the manufacturer’s protocol (10x Genomics). Indexed V(D)J, feature barcode and gene expression libraries were prepared according to the 10x Genomics protocol using the Feature Barcoding Kit (10x Genomics) and sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) system.

BCR sequencing analysis

Demultiplexing followed by FASTQ generation were done using Cell Ranger v.7.2 and v.8.0 (10x Genomics). VDJ reads were assembled de novo using Cell Ranger and contigs were aligned to a custom M. mulatta germline VDJ reference using IgBLAST and the Change-O v.1.3.0 package from the Immcantation framework70. Sequences were assigned to animals using the MULTIseqDemux function in Seurat (v.5)71. Inferred germline sequences (CreateGermline.py) and clones (DefineClones.py) were determined with Change-O. SHM was quantified using the observedMutations command in SHazaM package v.1.2.0 comparing heavy chain sequences to the inferred germline sequences (masking the D gene sequences). Amino acid mutation frequencies were calculated using a custom Python script from in-frame translated BCR heavy and light chain sequences aligned to their corresponding translated germline references (germline_alignment_d_mask), excluding masked positions.

BCR probe binding analysis

To characterize the boost-binding Bmem cell responses in greater detail, cells were stained with the respective boost and boost-epitope-KO probes independently labelled with DNA oligos, allowing heterologous boost-probe binding by each B cell to be quantified bioinformatically.

Unnormalized probe hashtag unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts were extracted from Seurat v.5 output. UMI counts were separated into individual GEMs. Thresholds were then set for each oligo labelled probe by first concatenating the respective sorted cell from the sorting FCS files and matching the positive cell percentage from the probe thresholds to match the flow cytometry data. These thresholds were set individually for each GEM and sort. Only those BG18 BCRs that met the threshold were classified as boost+.

For the sorting experiments where cocktails of boosting probes were used, first the UMI counts were summed across the fluorophores and the threshold was set as above to match the flow cytometry percentages for the combined cocktail probes. Subsequently, individual thresholds were set for each cocktail probe by dividing the combined threshold by the number of probes included. BG18 BCRs were called as boost+ after the cocktails if they bound to at least 1 of the cocktail probes, the total number of cocktail probes each cell bound to was also quantified.

B cell lineage tree construction

Maximum-likelihood lineage trees were constructed for B cell clonal families using the R Package Dowser72. Specifically, the IgPhyML model was used in the GetTrees function73. Branch lengths were scaled to represent the estimated number of total mutations that occurred in each heavy chain sequence since its diversion with the most recent common ancestor. Tips are shaped by cell type and coloured by week of isolation.

Clones that contained more than 500 sequences were downsampled in order to be compatible with the IgPhyML model. All Bmem sequences were kept in the analysis as they represented more diversity than the BGC sequences. To do this, these clones were first split into Bmem and BGC sequences and the number of Bmem sequences was calculated and then subtracted from 500. The proportion of BGC sequences isolated from each individual sorting timepoint was then calculated based on the total number of BGC for that clonal lineage. The remaining sequences needed to achieve 500 total sequences were then randomly selected from each BGC timepoint according to the proportion calculated above.

Statistical analysis

Statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 or R v.4.5.1 (2025-06-13). Group comparisons in Figs. 2 and 3a,b and Extended Data Figs. 6 and 7c,d were performed using unpaired two-tail Mann–Whitney U-tests. In each case, the following comparisons were made (1) G1 versus 2, G1 versus 3 and G1 versus 4; (2) G1 versus 2–4 pooled. Across time comparisons were made using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test in Figs. 3e and 5 and Extended Data Fig. 7f. Between group analysis used an unpaired Wilcoxon rank-sum test in Fig. 3e and Extended Data Fig. 7e,f. Each test was considered independent and therefore no adjustments were made for multiple comparisons. For analyses involving multiple outcomes per animal, we used a single summary measure per animal. Specifically, we calculated the geometric mean of KD values for Fig. 3 and the geometric mean of neutralization titres for Fig. 5. For Fig. 5e, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used to compare timepoints 2 weeks post-boost 7 and 6 weeks post-boost 7 and to test whether the fold change at each timepoint differed from the reference timepoint 5 weeks post-boost 6. The geometric mean was computed across all mAb or mAb–antigen pairs for each animal at each timepoint. For the above figures, the P value from the pooled comparison (2) is indicated on the graphs using asterisks to indicate statistical significance (not shown, P > 0.05). All P values from these comparisons are listed in Supplementary Table 13.

SPR

Antibody–trimer binding kinetics were measured on a Carterra LSA using HC30M or CMDP Sensor Chip (Carterra) with 1× HBS-EP+ pH 7.4 running buffer (Teknova) supplemented with BSA at 1 mg ml−1 essentially as described previously74. The chip surface was prepared for ligand capture according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Typically, about 2,500 to 2,700 resonance units of capture antibody (SouthernBiotech, 2047-01) in 10 mM sodium acetate pH 4.5 was amine coupled. Phosphoric acid 1.7% was used as a regeneration solution with 60 s contact time and injected 3 times per cycle. A low-capture IgG method was used to assess binding to trimeric analytes with a ligand concentration of 0.1 to 0.2 µg ml−1 and a contact time of 3 to 5 min. Raw sensograms were analysed using Kinetics Software (Carterra), interspot and blank double referencing, Langmuir model. Analyte concentrations were determined by measuring the absorption signal at 280 nm on a NanoDrop 2000c Spectrophotometer. Analytes were dialysed into running buffer. Our referencing practices were dependent on the off-rate for a particular ligand. For fast off-rates (>1 × 10−2 s−1), we used automated batch referencing that included overlay y-align and higher analyte concentrations. For slow off-rates (≤9 × 10−3 s−1) we used manual process referencing that included serial y-align and lower analyte concentrations.

Negative-stain EM

Stabilized trimers were diluted to about 0.03 mg ml−1 in TBS, adsorbed onto glow-discharged copper mesh grids and stained with 2% (w/v) uranyl formate for 60 s. Automated data collection was set up using EPU on a Thermo Fisher Scientific Talos F200C TEM with a Ceta 16 M camera (200 kV, 2.0 Å pixel size, ×73,000 magnification). Particle picking and 2D classifications were performed using cryoSPARC75. The 2D class averages were analysed and particles belonging to classes that did not unambiguously resemble NL trimers (compact, trimeric phenotypes) were marked as non-native.

Cryo-EM

All Fabs and stabilized trimers used for structural analysis were prepared as described above. Three immune complexes were incubated overnight: (1) 0.2 mg of BG505 MD39 SOSIP with 0.2 mg RVz20_wk72_08 Fab and 0.2 mg RM20A3 Fab (gp41-base antibody to aid with angular sampling); (2) 0.2 mg of BG505 MD39 SOSIP with 0.2 mg RRr20_wk72_07 Fab and 0.2 mg RM20A3 Fab; and (3) 0.2 mg of V703-0537_L14 SOSIP with 0.2 mg RQd20_wk56_28 Fab and 0.2 mg 3BNC117 Fab (CD4bs antibody to aid with angular sampling). Complexes were purified the following morning using a HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 200 pg (Cytiva) gel-filtration column. Fractions corresponding to each immune complex were pooled and concentrated using an Amicon 100 kDa MWCO centrifugal device to between 3 and 15 mg ml−1 as determined using NanoDrop measurement of the absorbance at 280 nm (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The samples were vitrified using the Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with chamber conditions of 4 °C and 100% humidity, and blotting times between 3 and 6 s. Lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG; Anatrace) was added to the sample to a final concentration of 0.005 mM shortly before freezing. UltrAuFoil 1.2/1.3 (Au, 300-mesh; Quantifoil Micro Tools) grids were used and glow discharged (PELCO easiGlow, Ted Pella) for 40 s before sample application. After blotting, the grids were plunge-frozen into liquid-nitrogen-cooled liquid ethane.

RQd20_wk56_28 and RVz20_wk72_08 complexes were loaded onto the Thermo Fisher Scientific Glacios 2 TEM operating at 200 kV equipped with a Thermo Fisher Scientific Falcon 4i direct electron detector (×190,000 magnification and 0.718 Å pixel size). The RRr20_wk72_07 complex was collected using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Glacios TEM operated at 200 kV equipped with a Thermo Fisher Scientific Falcon 4 direct electron detector (×190,000 magnification and 0.725 Å pixel size). EPU software (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for automated data collection for all samples. Motion correction, dose weighting and CTF correction was performed using cryoSPARC Live75. cryoSPARC was used for the remainder of data processing. Particle picking was performed using blob picker initially followed by template picker. Particles were initially downsampled by a factor of 4 and multiple rounds of 2D classification were performed followed by three-dimensional (3D) ab initio reconstruction. The remaining particles were then re-extracted and Fourier cropped to reduce the memory requirements of large box sizes, resulting in image pixel sizes of 1.034 Å or 1.044 Å. 3D non-uniform refinement was performed76. Focused 3D classification was performed on the V3-glycan targeting Fabs to remove any particles with weak Fab density. For all cryo-EM maps, the global resolution was estimated using half maps and a Fourier shell correlation cut-off of 0.143, and the local resolution was estimated using the cryoSPARC Local Resolution tool with 0.5 FSC cut-off. Data collection and processing statistics are summarized in Extended Data Fig. 8a and Extended Data Table 1.

Initial models were generated using AlphaFold377 and docked into the cryo-EM maps using UCSF ChimeraX78. Manual building was performed in Coot (v.0.9.8)79 and real space refinement in Phenix80. Final models were validated using MolProbity and EMRinger in the Phenix suite. Buried surface area calculations and interface analyses were performed using UCSF ChimeraX. Antibody–antigen contacts are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Model statistics are summarized in Extended Data Table 1.

Angle of approach measurements

The latitude, longitude and heavy-chain–light-chain twist angles were measured as described previously39.

TZM-bl pseudovirus neutralization assay

Pseudoviruses were produced in HEK293T cells (RRID: CVCL_0063) co-transfected using FuGENE 6 (Promega, E2691) or PEI Max (Polisciences, 24765-1) with pseudovirus Env-expressing plasmid and Env-deficient backbone plasmid (PSG3DEnv). Pseudoviruses were collected at 72 h after transfection, concentrated (EMD Millipore, UFC905024) if needed for low productive viruses and kept frozen at −80 °C until use. On the day of the neutralization assay, viruses were prepared by diluting with D10 medium (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (Gibco, 10313-021), 10% FBS (Omega Scientific, FB-02), 1× penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, 15070-063) and 1x GlutaMAX (Gibco, 35050-061)) according to previously defined titres. DEAE-dextran (5 μg ml−1 final concentration) was added to all pseudoviruses except for the MLV control due to its potency. The serum samples were thawed and heat inactivated at 56 °C in a water bath for 30 min before the assay. Equal volumes of serially diluted serum samples (starting at 1:20 dilution), test mAbs and Den 3 negative control (starting at 50 μg ml−1) and BG18 positive control (starting at 5 μg ml−1) were incubated with HIV pseudoviruses in a round-bottom 96-well plate (Costar, 3788) at 37 °C for 1 h. TZM-bl cells, obtained from the NIH AIDS Reagent Program and tested for mycoplasma routinely (no cell line authentication was performed), were seeded 24 h before at 100,000 cells per ml in 50 μl per well of half-area 96-well plates (Costar, 3688). The plates were then removed from the 37 °C incubator, the culture medium was carefully aspirated and 25 μl of a mix of diluted serum or diluted mAbs + pseudoviruses was added to each well before further incubation at 37 °C for 24 h in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. After incubation for 24 h, 75 μl of culture medium was added and the plates were incubated in same conditions for additional 48 h. After 48 h (total 72 h), the culture medium was removed, and cells were lysed with 45 μl per well 1× luciferase culture lysis buffer (Promega, E1531) for 20 min at room temperature. Neutralization was measured by adding 30 μl luciferase reagent (substrate) per well (Promega, E1501) and measuring the luminescence. ID50 or IC50 values were calculated using a one-site fit (log[IC50]) nonlinear regression curve fit constrained from 100–0% (ID50) or 0–100% (IC50) in GraphPad Prism v.10.6.1. Neutralization experiments were measured in duplicate.

Epitope diversity

We obtained the ‘super filtered web’ HIV-1/SIVcpz Env amino acid sequence alignment containing 8,053 sequences (dated 2022) from the Los Alamos National Laboratory HIV Database81. The alignment was manually curated and saved as the file ‘HIV1_SFL_2022_env_PRO.txt’. We generated a mapping file (mapping.HXB2_resnums_to_absolute_positions_in_2022_alignment) using a bash script (see ‘bash command.txt’) to assign HXB2 residue numbers to their corresponding positions in the full sequence alignment. We executed bash scripts (see ‘bash command.txt’) to calculate amino acid frequencies (see ‘new-see-nmers.sh’, ‘new-see-single.sh’ and ‘V3_2025_resnums’) and glycosylation site frequencies (see ‘new-test-conservation-of-glyco-site-all.sh’) at residue positions of interest across all strains. We analysed the V1 loop using ‘new_analyze_v1.sh’ to determine loop length, number of glycosylation sites and site-specific counts for each strain. The V1 loop spans residues 132 to 156 in HXB2 numbering. To include the N156 glycan, we ran the analysis on residues 132 to 158, then recalculated the V1 loop length by subtracting 2 and saved as ‘V1_loop_all_2022_clean.xlsx’. Figure 1e was plotted in Python using the script saved as ‘V1_loop_heatmap_plot.ipynb’. All scripts can be accessed at GitHub (https://github.com/schieflab/Steichen2025).

MS-based N-glycan analysis

DeGlyPHER82 was used to ascertain site-specific glycan occupancy and processivity on the examined glycoproteins.

Proteinase K treatment and deglycosylation

HIV Env glycoprotein was exchanged to water using Microcon Ultracel PL-10 centrifugal filter. Glycoprotein was reduced with 5 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP-HCl) and alkylated with 10 mM 2-chloroacetamide in 100 mM ammonium acetate for 20 min at room temperature (24 °C). Initial protein-level deglycosylation was performed using 250 U of Endo H for 5 µg trimer, for 1 h at 37 °C. Glycorotein was digested with 1:25 proteinase K for 30 min at 37 °C. Proteinase K was denatured by incubating at 90 °C for 15 min, then cooled to room temperature. Peptides were deglycosylated again with 250 U Endo H for 1 h at 37 °C, then frozen at –80 °C and lyophilized. 100 U PNGase F was lyophilized, resuspended in 20 µl 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate prepared in H218O, and added to the lyophilized peptides. Reactions were then incubated for 1 h at 37 °C, subsequently analysed by liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS).

LC–MS/MS

The samples were analysed on an Q Exactive HF-X or Orbitrap Fusion Lumos Tribrid mass spectrometer. The samples were injected directly onto a 25 cm, 100 μm (inner diameter) column packed with BEH 1.7 μm C18 resin. The samples were separated at a flow rate of 300 nl min−1 on an EASY-nLC 1200 UHPLC. Buffers A and B were 0.1% formic acid in 5% and 80% acetonitrile, respectively. The following gradient was used: 1–25% B (over 160 min for HF-X and 120 min for Lumos), an increase to 40% B (over 40 min), an increase to 90% B over another 10 min and at 90% B (30 min for HF-X and 10 min for Lumos) for a total run time of 240 min for HF-X (180 min for Lumos). The column was re-equilibrated with solution A before the injection of sample. Peptides were eluted from the tip of the column and nanosprayed directly into the mass spectrometer by application of 2.8 kV at the back of the column. The mass spectrometer was operated in a data-dependent mode. Full MS1 scans were collected in the Orbitrap at 120,000 resolution for HF-X (60,000 for Lumos). The ten most abundant ions per scan were selected for HCD MS/MS at 25 NCE (30 NCE for Lumos). Dynamic exclusion was enabled with exclusion duration of 10 s and singly charged ions were excluded.

Data processing

Protein and peptide identification were done using the Integrated Proteomics Pipeline (IP2). Tandem mass spectra were extracted from raw files using RawConverter83 and searched with ProLuCID84 against a database comprising UniProt-reviewed (Swiss-Prot) proteome for Homo sapiens (UP000005640), UniProt amino acid sequences for Endo H (P04067), PNGase F (Q9XBM8) and Proteinase K (P06873), amino acid sequences for the examined proteins, and a list of general protein contaminants. The search space included no cleavage-specificity. Carbamidomethylation (+57.02146 C) was considered a static modification. Deamidation in the presence of H218O (+2.988261 N), GlcNAc (+203.079373 N), oxidation (+15.994915 M) and N-terminal pyroglutamate formation (–17.026549 Q) were considered differential modifications. Data were searched with 50 ppm precursor ion tolerance and 50 ppm fragment ion tolerance. Identified proteins were filtered using DTASelect285 and using a target–decoy database search strategy to limit the false discovery rate to 1%, at the spectrum level86. A minimum of 1 peptide per protein and no tryptic end per peptide were required, and the precursor delta mass cut-off was fixed at 10 ppm. Statistical models for peptide mass modification (modstat) were applied. Census287 label-free analysis was performed based on the precursor peak area, with a 10 ppm precursor mass tolerance and 0.1 min retention time tolerance. ‘Match between runs’ was used to find missing peptides between runs. Data analysis using GlycoMSQuant82 was implemented to automate the analysis. GlycoMSQuant summed precursor peak areas across replicates, discarded peptides without NGS, discarded misidentified peptides when N-glycan remnant-mass modifications were localized to non-NGS asparagines and corrected/fixed N-glycan mislocalization where appropriate.

Antigenic profile

Biolayer interferometry experiments were performed on an Octet RED96 instrument (Sartorius) at 24 °C with 1,000 rpm shaking. ProA biosensors (Sartorius, 18-5012) were prehydrated for 10 min in HBS-EP+ buffer (Teknova, H8022) containing 0.1% BSA (Rockland, BSA-30). Black 96-well microplates (Greiner, 655209) were prepared with 200 μl per well. The plate layout included assay buffer in columns 1 and 12, HIV trimers (1 μM) in columns 2–4, antibodies (5 μg ml−1) in columns 5–10 and regeneration solution (0.85% phosphoric acid, Sigma-Aldrich, 345245) in column 11. Binding kinetics were measured with baseline, association and dissociation phases, and biosensors were regenerated between measurements. Antibody binding was quantified as the ratio of the dissociation signal relative to baseline shifts. Raw data were exported in .csv format and analysed using Octet Data Analysis software, with binding values visualized in Prism.

Thermostability measurements

The melting temperature was measured using nano differential scanning fluorimetry (nanoDSF) on the Prometheus Panta (NanoTemper Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Data analysis was carried out using the PR.Panta analysis software.

ELISA analysis of antigen-specific plasma responses

ELISA plates (Corning 96-Well Half-Area, 3690) were coated with streptavidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, AAJ624288PL) at 2 µg ml−1 in PBS (Gibco, 10010023) for 2 h at room temperature, then washed once and blocked overnight at 4 °C with 2% BSA (Sigma-Aldrich, A3294) in PBST (PBS + 0.05% Tween-20, Thermo Fisher Scientific, AAJ63314AP). The plates were washed with PBST (0.2% Tween-20) and incubated with ELISA antigens at 0.5 µg ml−1 in 1% BSA/PBST for 1 h at room temperature. After a single wash, the plates were blocked for 1 h at room temperature with PBST containing 5% skimmed milk (BD Difco, 232100) and 1% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 16000044), then washed three times. Serum samples were serially diluted 1:600 in blocking buffer (PBST + 1% FBS), added at 25 µl per well, and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C under 80% humidity. The plates were washed four times and incubated with 25 µl per well of Peroxidase AffiniPure Donkey Anti-Human IgG (H+L) (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 709-035-149) at 1:5,000 in PBST + 1% FBS for 1 h at room temperature. After three washes, TMB substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 002023) was added, and the reaction was stopped after 6 min with 25 µl 0.5 M H2SO4. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm with 570 nm background subtraction on a VersaMax plate reader (Molecular Devices). Data were analysed using Prism v10 (GraphPad Software) to generate dose–response curves and calculate half-maximum effective concentration values. The area under the curve was computed using the trapezoidal method, summing the areas beneath straight-line connections between adjacent points.
