Leveraging large language models for information extraction from free-text liver MRI reports and assessment of clinical utility: a multicenter study
Authors: Jiahui Jiang, Jingwei Wei, Jiapei Lin, Qingwen Yang, Jun Lu, Kun Liu, Hui Xu, Yuelang Zhang, Haoran Sun, Hongjie Hu, Zhenchang Wang, Zhenghan Yang, Dawei Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLarge language models (LLMs) have shown considerable potential for extracting information from free-text radiology reports, enabling efficient data use, large-scale data mining, and a wide range of secondary analyses and clinical applications. This study aimed to evaluate the performance of LLMs in extracting diagnostically relevant information from multicenter free-text liver magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) reports, explore the clinical utility of LLM-generated structured reports, and investigate optimal prompting strategies for multicenter data.MethodsIn this retrospective multicenter study, 800 free-text liver MRI reports from four medical centers (Beijing Friendship Hospital, Tianjin Medical University General Hospital, The Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University, Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital) were collected to evaluate the information extraction performance of two LLMs—DeepSeek-V3 and ChatGPT-4o—using radiologist-annotated structured data as the reference standard. Three prompting strategies were applied: zero-shot prompting, global few-shot prompting (using shared examples across centers), and center-specific few-shot prompting (using examples specific to each center), with example counts set to 2–12. Model performance was evaluated using field-level F1 scores, and report-level extraction success was defined as the correct extraction of ≥80% report fields. Additionally, an exploratory clinical evaluation was conducted using 20 reports from one center, in which 10 radiologists and 10 clinicians evaluated the readability and clinical usability of free-text, manually structured, and LLM-generated reports on a 5-point Likert scale.ResultsFew-shot prompting significantly outperformed zero-shot prompting for both LLMs, with the largest gains in macro F1 observed when k was increased from 0 to 2 (∆DeepSeek-V3: global 0.106, center-specific 0.127; ∆ ChatGPT-4o: global 0.086, center-specific 0.107). Performance plateaued at k=4 [DeepSeek-V3: global 0.848 (0.838–0.858), center-specific 0.865 (0.856–0.875); ChatGPT-4o: global 0.835 (0.824–0.845), center-specific 0.861 (0.851–0.870)], with adjacent-k gains <0.01. Center-specific prompting consistently outperformed global prompting (∆F1: 0.017–0.024 for DeepSeek-V3; 0.014–0.026 for ChatGPT-4o). In the exploratory clinical evaluation, structured reports received higher scores for clarity and communication than free-text reports (both P<0.001), while LLM-generated reports received scores comparable to those of manually structured reports (both P>0.05).ConclusionsLLMs demonstrated strong performance in extracting diagnostically relevant information from Chinese multicenter liver MRI reports. In the exploratory clinical evaluation, the LLM-generated structured reports showed the potential to improve report clarity and facilitate clinical communication. Global prompting showed good performance across centers, while center-specific prompting further improved accuracy by adapting to local reporting styles.Keywords: Large language model (LLM), information extraction, structured report, liver, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)

Introduction

Radiology reports are a crucial communication tool between radiologists and clinicians, systematically documenting radiologists’ interpretations and conclusions while providing detailed descriptions of imaging findings (1,2). These reports are generally presented in either structured or free-text formats. Because of their flexibility and unrestricted expression, free-text reports are widely used, particularly in complex cases (3,4). However, their unstructured nature makes it time-consuming for clinicians to extract clinically relevant information, thereby increasing the interpretation burden and hindering efficient multidisciplinary communication. Moreover, free-text reports contain rich medical knowledge and hold significant potential as training data for medical artificial intelligence (AI); however, their lack of a standardized format and precise alignment with imaging content makes it difficult for machines to parse diagnostic details, limiting their utility as high-quality data resources for AI development. Consequently, efficient clinical approaches for more rapidly extracting and using key information from free-text reports urgently need to be established to reduce the interpretation burden, enhance communication efficiency, and unlock the potential value of these reports in medical AI.

Natural language processing (NLP) techniques offer a promising solution to this challenge by enabling the automated extraction of key information from free-text radiology reports (5,6) and converting them into structured formats. This transformation not only improves clinical readability and communication efficiency, supporting rapid access to critical imaging evidence for diagnostic decision-making and multidisciplinary collaboration, but also facilitates the construction of large-scale, fine-grained image–text paired datasets. Such datasets are essential for advancing multimodal foundation models in medical imaging (7), thereby promoting the efficient use and secondary analysis of report data.

Previous studies (8,9) have employed rule-based, machine learning, and deep learning approaches for information extraction from free-text radiology reports. However, these approaches are limited by poor generalizability across diverse writing styles and the need for labor-intensive annotation. Recently, large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT and DeepSeek have emerged, leveraging large-scale pre-training and in-context learning to rapidly adapt to new tasks without task-specific fine-tuning (10-13). LLMs have already shown promising performance in certain radiology report domains (14-19). Nonetheless, liver magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) reports are more complex, as they require detailed descriptions of hepatic morphology, lesion characteristics across multiple sequences and contrast phases, and vascular anatomy, all of which are critical for accurate diagnosis and therapeutic decision-making. The application of LLMs to multicenter free-text liver MRI reports has yet to be systematically investigated.

This study aimed to systematically evaluate the capability of LLMs to extract diagnostically relevant information from multicenter free-text liver MRI reports and to further explore their value in improving report readability and enhancing communication. In addition, the study investigated optimal prompting strategies for handling data heterogeneity across multiple centers. We present this article in accordance with the STARD-AI reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1-0198/rc).

Methods

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Board of Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University (No. 2025-P2-088-01), which waived the requirement of individual consent for this retrospective analysis. All participating hospitals were informed of and agreed to the study.

Report collection and preparation

Free-text liver MRI reports were retrospectively collected from four independent tertiary medical centers (Beijing Friendship Hospital, Tianjin Medical University General Hospital, The Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University, Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital) between January 2023 and December 2023. All reports were written in Chinese and contained detailed documentation of imaging findings and diagnostic impressions. No translation to English or other languages was performed. If multiple reports were available for a patient during the study period, only the first liver MRI report was included. A total of 800 reports were randomly sampled (200 reports from each center). The mean age of the patients was 58±14 years, and 59.6% of the patients were male. Additionally, 12 example reports that did not overlap with the 800 reports used for model evaluation were selected from each center for few-shot prompting.

All personally identifiable information was manually removed in accordance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Safe Harbor method by two trained radiology residents. Each report was interpreted by at least two radiologists with varying levels of experience, representing more than 40 distinct readers in total. Information extraction from free-text reports

To extract structured liver disease-related information from free-text liver MRI reports, with a focus on focal liver lesions, an information extraction schema was developed based on the Liver Imaging Reporting and Data System (LI-RADS) (20) and expert consensus. The consensus was reached through discussion among six senior radiologists, each with over 10 years of experience in liver imaging diagnosis. The target fields included two major categories: (I) non-lesion-specific fields, which serve as integral references for diagnosis, including liver background characteristics, such as liver morphology and parenchymal features, as well as the portal venous system, the biliary system, and regional lymph nodes; and (II) lesion-specific fields, including lesion location, number, size, morphology, margin, signal characteristics across multiple MRI sequences (T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI)), and contrast enhancement patterns. For reports describing multiple lesion types, information for each lesion type was extracted separately.

All included reports were independently reviewed by two radiologists (with 5 and 3 years of experience, respectively) to establish the reference standard. Fields not mentioned in the report were labeled as negative and marked as “NA”. In cases of disagreement between the two radiologists, a third senior radiologist (with 15 years of experience) was consulted, and consensus was reached through discussion. The radiologists establishing the reference standard were blinded to the LLM outputs during the annotation process.

Prompt design

This study used ChatGPT-4o and DeepSeek-V3, accessed between June 1 and June 25, 2025. To evaluate the impact of different prompting strategies on information extraction performance, three experimental settings were designed: zero-shot prompting, global few-shot prompting, and center-specific few-shot prompting. In all settings, the instruction presented to the model was identical and explicitly described the information extraction task; only the presence and selection of example demonstrations varied across conditions.

Zero-shot prompting provided only the instruction and target report, without examples. Global few-shot prompting used k=2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 examples randomly drawn from all centers and applied uniformly across test samples. Center-specific few-shot prompting used the same k values, but the examples were drawn from the same center as the target report. No test-set reports were used during prompt design or example selection. All prompts followed the format (Instruction) + (k examples, if any) + (Target report), were written in Chinese to preserve clinical expression, and were submitted via an application programming interface (API). The LLMs generated structured outputs with field labels, which were manually converted to standardized JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) for evaluation. Each report was tested once under both zero- and few-shot conditions. Details of the prompt instructions are provided in the Appendix 1.

Evaluation metrics

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of the LLMs in extracting information from free-text radiology reports, both field-level and report-level analyses were conducted.

In the field-level analysis, each field was classified as true positive (TP; present and matched), false negative (FN; present but missing), false positive (FP; absent but extracted), true negative (TN; absent and not extracted), or both FP and FN (present in both but mismatched). Based on these classifications, recall, precision, and F1 score were calculated to quantify extraction performance. Detailed criteria are provided in Table S1.

The report-level accuracy analysis was defined as follows: a case was considered successfully extracted if the proportion of correctly extracted fields (i.e., the total number of TPs and TNs) exceeded 0.8 of all reference (gold standard) fields within a single report. In addition, a sensitivity analysis based exclusively on positive fields was performed to further evaluate the ability of the LLMs to correctly extract clinically relevant information. Specifically, the proportion of correctly extracted positive fields [TP/(TP + FN)] was calculated at the report level, and successful extraction was defined as a threshold of >0.8.

Exploratory clinical evaluation

A total of 20 liver MRI cases were randomly selected from Center 1. Each case included three report types: a free-text report, a manually structured report (expert-extracted), and a LLM-generated structured report (DeepSeek-V3 with center-specific few-shot prompting, k=4). The reports were divided into three groups, with the three versions of each case assigned to different groups to avoid memory bias. Report order within each group was randomized to minimize sequence effects.

Ten radiologists and 10 clinicians participated. Each reader reviewed all three groups in three separate sessions, with a two-week washout period between sessions. The respondents were blinded to the source of the structured reports. For each report, the respondents rated four dimensions—information completeness, clarity of key findings, ease of clinical communication, and report type preference—using a 5-point Likert scale. Details of the evaluation criteria are provided in the Appendix 2.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.11.7). Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and percentages, were reported where appropriate. No formal sample size calculation was performed, as this was a retrospective exploratory study. The sample size was determined based on data availability and the need to ensure representation of heterogeneous reporting styles across multiple centers. Subjective scores were reported as medians and interquartile ranges. Precision, recall, and F1-score were calculated for the field-level analysis. Bootstrap analyses were performed by sampling reports (case-level resampling) 1,000 times. For each bootstrap sample, the F1 score and corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated for each individual field to estimate the distribution of field-level macro F1 scores. In the case-level analysis, multiple comparisons were conducted using the Chi-square test, with p values adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. In the exploratory clinical evaluation, a cumulative link mixed model (CLMM) was employed, specifying report format as the fixed effect (free-text as the reference), with random intercepts for report and reader to account for within-report and within-reader clustering. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using estimated marginal means with Bonferroni adjustment. For all tests, a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Field-level extraction performance across different prompting strategies and LLMs

Macro F1 scores were compared across prompting strategies and few-shot sample sizes (k=0–12) for DeepSeek-V3 and ChatGPT-4o (Table 2 and Figure 2). At the same number of examples, center-specific prompting consistently outperformed global prompting in both models. Among all settings, DeepSeek-V3 with center-specific prompting achieved the highest macro F1 score (0.889; 95% CI: 0.880–0.899) at K=12. Compared to the zero-shot setting, few-shot prompting led to substantial performance gains across models, particularly from k=0 to k=2. Although macro F1 peaked at k=12, performance began to plateau as early as k=4.

Report-level extraction performance of LLMs under center-specific few-shot prompting at k=4

Given that both DeepSeek-V3 and ChatGPT-4o outperformed global prompting with center-specific prompting at k=4, we further analyzed report-level performance under this setting. In the report-level accuracy analysis (Table 3), DeepSeek-V3 and ChatGPT-4o achieved report-level extraction success rates of 85.4% (683/800) and 83.3% (666/800), respectively, with no significant overall difference (P=0.090). In the subgroup analyses, DeepSeek-V3 demonstrated superior performance in reports with an average sentence length <25 words (91.0% vs. 82.5%, P=0.031). Within-model comparisons showed that both models performed best in lesion-free reports, with significantly lower performance in reports containing lesions (DeepSeek-V3: 93.3% vs. 80.7%, P=0.001; ChatGPT-4o: 93.3% vs. 77.0% for reports with one lesion type and 84.7% for reports with multiple lesion types, overall P<0.001). Additionally, ChatGPT-4o exhibited significant variability across centers, particularly between Center 2 and Center 4 (89.0% vs. 78.0%, P=0.032), whereas DeepSeek-V3 showed no such difference. No significant differences were observed across subgroups defined by report text length or average sentence length for either model. In the sensitivity analysis (Table S4), the report-level extraction success rates decreased to 75.9% (607/800) for DeepSeek-V3 and 73.4% (587/800) for ChatGPT-4o. No statistically significant difference was observed between the two models (P=0.096). The relative performance between the models remained consistent with that observed in the accuracy-based analysis.

Exploratory clinical evaluation of LLM-generated structured reports

In the exploratory study, four metrics were evaluated: information completeness, clarity of key findings, ease of clinical communication, and report type preference (Table 4 and Figure 3). For information completeness, free-text reports received the highest scores from both radiologists and clinicians, significantly outperforming manually structured reports (both P<0.001) and LLM-generated structured reports (both P<0.001), indicating that when reading free-text reports, readers may be less likely to detect certain omissions in information. For clarity of key findings and ease of clinical communication, both structured report types (manually structured reports and LLM-generated structured reports) scored significantly higher than free-text reports (both P<0.001), with no significant differences between the two structured formats (clarity of key findings: radiologists P=0.858, clinicians P=0.346; ease of clinical communication: radiologists P>0.99, clinicians P=0.357). For report type preference, radiologists favored free-text reports (P<0.001), whereas clinicians preferred structured reports (P<0.001).

Discussion

In this multicenter study, we systematically evaluated the ability of LLMs to extract diagnostically relevant information from Chinese free-text liver MRI reports and to assess their clinical utility. Our findings demonstrated that LLMs can accurately extract information from unstructured radiology narratives, with performance shaped by prompting design: center-specific prompting consistently outperformed global prompting, and the number of examples showed a nonlinear effect, with gains plateauing beyond four. Moreover, the exploratory clinical evaluation showed that LLM-generated structured reports were comparable to manually structured reports in clarity and communication value, underscoring their potential clinical utility.

Most reports in our dataset were long (>200 words) and covered diverse liver lesion types and multi-sequence features. Their semantic complexity and structural variability made information extraction challenging. At the field level, expression diversity was high, with the number of unique values often exceeding 100 and coverage of the top three most frequent values below 0.5, indicating dispersed terminology and substantial semantic variability.

The substantial variability in writing style, terminology, and structural expression across centers may influence extraction performance. We compared two strategies—center-specific and global prompts—under the same few-shot sample sizes. Center-specific prompts generally achieved higher macro F1 scores, as their semantic and stylistic alignment reduced contextual drift and irrelevant interference, improving model focus on center-specific expressions (21). Notably, for fields such as liver background and biliary system, global prompts performed better at very low k values (k=2, 4), likely due to the broader linguistic diversity providing useful reference cues (22). However, as the number of examples increased, the center-specific prompts gradually outperformed the global prompts, likely due to their consistency in context, terminology, and structural alignment, which collectively contribute to improved extraction performance (23). Overall, global prompting was well suited for the rapid processing of heterogeneous multicenter data, offering solid performance with minimal tuning. Conversely, center-specific prompting was better suited for precision-oriented tasks, where alignment with local reporting styles enabled refined optimization. Hence, prompting strategies should be chosen based on task objectives and data characteristics rather than relying on a single universal approach.

The lesion-centric analysis revealed heterogeneous performance across features. Higher accuracy was observed for structured attributes such as location, size, and signal intensity (T1WI/T2WI), while lower accuracy was observed for enhancement, morphology, margin, and DWI. This may be attributed to the greater variability and semantic complexity of these features, which are often described using diverse and less standardized expressions. In addition, the LLMs showed relatively lower accuracy in extracting numerical information expressed in Chinese, suggesting additional challenges in handling linguistic variability.

Few-shot prompts consistently improved model performance under both center-specific and global strategies. Performance further increased with more examples, demonstrating that example-based guidance effectively enhanced the LLMs’ understanding of task objectives and semantic structure, consistent with previous findings (16,24,25). At k=4, performance gains plateaued with only slight improvement, reflecting diminishing returns (26): a few examples sufficed to capture task format and key semantic patterns, while additional examples contributed less incrementally. For semantically complex and lengthy radiology reports, excessive examples may also introduce distraction or redundancy due to context window limitations (27). Moreover, key expression patterns were often captured in the initial examples, limiting the added value of later ones. Therefore, k=4 appears to strike a practical balance between model performance and prompting efficiency, making it a practical choice for real-world applications.

At k=4, we compared DeepSeek-V3 and ChatGPT-4o under center-specific prompting to evaluate robustness in few-shot, low-resource settings. A threshold of 0.8 was applied in both the accuracy- and sensitivity-based analyses to define report-level successful extraction, balancing strict evaluation with practical tolerance. Performance varied based on the number of lesion types, with the highest performance observed in reports without lesions and the lowest performance observed in reports describing a single lesion, likely due to limited contextual information. In some cases, the models also misattributed abnormalities from other organs to the liver, resulting in semantic errors.

Notably, ChatGPT-4o exhibited variable performance across centers, particularly between Center 2 and Center 4, while DeepSeek-V3 maintained relatively consistent performance across centers, indicating superior cross-center robustness. However, as TNs typically constitute a large proportion of all fields, this metric may partially dilute the impact of FNs. Consequently, in certain cases, reports containing a small number of FPs or FNs may still be classified as successfully extracted. Therefore, a TP-based sensitivity analysis was further conducted. The observed decrease in report-level extraction success rates in the sensitivity analysis suggests that the accuracy-based metric may be influenced by the predominance of negative fields, potentially leading to an overestimation of model performance. Moreover, these findings indicate that, while the models performed well in identifying negative information, their ability to accurately extract (clinically relevant) positive fields requires further improvement.

In an exploratory study, we compared free-text, manually structured, and LLM-generated structured reports across four domains: information completeness, clarity of key findings, ease of clinical communication, and report preference. “Information completeness” primarily refers to actual completeness, defined as whether the report objectively includes key elements for lesion characterization (e.g., location, number, size, signal, and enhancement). The free-text reports scored highest for completeness, likely because their narrative style conveys a sense of comprehensiveness even when details are missing. Conversely, structured reports explicitly list key elements, making omissions more apparent; the LLM-generated structured reports received the lowest completeness scores due to occasional extraction omissions. Actual completeness reflects the objective quality of information by assessing whether the report provides detailed and comprehensive content. However, it differs from perceived completeness, which reflects the reader’s subjective judgment of information completeness. Despite variability among readers, perceived completeness may still indicate whether information coverage meets clinical needs.

For clarity of key findings and ease of clinical communication, both the structured formats were rated higher than the free-text reports, with the manually structured reports and LLM-generated reports performing comparably. Regarding preference, radiologists favored free-text reports, reflecting both habitual use and the flexibility of nuanced descriptions, while clinicians preferred structured reports for their concise presentation of key findings and improved access to actionable information.

This study had several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, and all reports were in Chinese, which may limit the broad applicability of our findings to other languages or clinical contexts; therefore, further validation with larger, diverse datasets is warranted to confirm these findings across different languages and clinical settings. Additionally, most extracted information involved non-standardized fields with diverse expressions and inconsistent semantics. Future work could apply terminology normalization to map heterogeneous expressions to unified terms, supporting the construction of structured, high-quality databases and providing standardized training data for multimodal foundation models. Second, in the exploratory clinical evaluation, only DeepSeek-V3 was used to reduce experimental complexity and ensure consistency across evaluations. The exclusion of ChatGPT-4o may limit the generalizability of the findings. Third, this study relied solely on LLMs accessed via APIs, which, despite data de-identification, may raise privacy concerns. Future work may explore locally deployable models, such as DeepSeek-V3, combined with techniques such as Retrieval-Augmented Generation or fine-tuning to enhance extraction performance, data privacy, and deployment control. Further validation with larger, diverse datasets is warranted to confirm these findings across different languages and clinical settings.

Conclusions

LLMs effectively extracted diagnostic information from Chinese multicenter liver MRI reports. LLM-generated structured reports may further enhance report clarity and facilitate clinical communication. Global prompting demonstrated consistent performance within this multicenter study, while center-specific prompting achieved higher accuracy by adapting to local reporting styles. These findings highlight the importance of prompt design in optimizing LLM performance for heterogeneous clinical data and provide preliminary insights for future multicenter applications.
