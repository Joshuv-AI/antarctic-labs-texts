Bladder wall abscess revealing squamous cell carcinoma in an adult with neurogenic bladder: A rare case report
Authors: Viktoriya Boncheva-Henderson, Katie Mallalieu, Omid Yassaie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bladder wall abscesses are rare and present a diagnostic challenge, particularly in high-risk patients. We report the first known case of a bladder wall abscess in an adult female with a neurogenic bladder using clean intermittent self-catheterisation (CISC), ultimately diagnosed with squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) of the bladder. Infective symptoms and imaging consistent with abscess delayed the diagnosis of moderately differentiated SCC. This case highlights the diagnostic difficulty of bladder abscesses and the importance of early histological evaluation in atypical or non-resolving lesions. Malignancy should always be considered in high-risk individuals with neurogenic bladder and long-term CISC.

Introduction

Abscess of the bladder wall is a very uncommon condition and as such, its management remains controversial owing to the lack of literature on the subject. There have been a few cases of a urachal and intramural space abscesses, but the occurrence of a true bladder wall abscess remains rare even in the presence of recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs).1 To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of bladder wall abscess in an adult female with a neurogenic bladder, reliant on clean intermittent self-catheterisation (CISC), with squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) as its underlying pathology. This highlights the diagnostic challenges and emphasizes the need for thorough evaluation of atypical bladder wall lesions, particularly in high-risk patients.

Initial presentation and management

A 43-year-old Caucasian woman presented to the emergency department (ED) with a three-day history of suprapubic pain, fever (39 °C) and lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS). She has never smoked and had a background of spina bifida occulta which was picked up during her first year of life because of slow progress attaining milestones. Her only spina bifida-associated long-term complication was a neurogenic bladder for which she had been performing CISC several times a day since childhood. The patient had no previous pelvic or urological surgeries and did not report issues with recurrent UTIs. However, in the preceding three months, she had several UTIs with multiple courses of antibiotics and one hospital admission for pyelonephritis. For this reason, she had been started on prophylactic low dose Nitrofurantoin.

The patient's initial assessment revealed soft abdomen with moderate suprapubic tenderness but no palpable masses. Her laboratory tests demonstrated elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) at 80mg/L, leukocytosis of 19.2 (109/L) and urinalysis notable for pyuria and microscopic haematuria. Her Haemoglobin A1C and her creatinine were normal. The patient had a computed tomography (CT) scan of the abdomen and pelvis which described a thickened bladder wall and a rim enhancing fluid collection at the anterior bladder wall, measuring 50x45x54mm (Fig. 1.). Its density measured 27 HU, compared to 7 HU at the bladder lumen and there was no evidence of hydronephrosis or pyelonephritis. The collection was closely related to the sigmoid colon which also showed segmental wall thickening but no overt diverticulitis. A retrograde CT cystogram showed no communication between the bladder and the rim enhancing collection. For completion, a flexible cystoscopy was performed which revealed an inflammatory change of the anterior bladder wall mucosa, with protrusion into the bladder. The patient was counselled on our recommendation for percutaneous drainage which was performed the following day under CT guidance. A 10 French pigtail drain was inserted into the anterior bladder wall abscess and 25ml pus was aspirated. Post-procedurally, urine cultures returned pan-sensitive Enterococcus faecalis and the abscess aspirates returned pan-sensitive Streptococcus dysgalactiae (Group C/G). The patient recovered well and was discharged home within a few days with a 10-day course of Amoxicillin 1gr and Clavulanic acid 125mg three times daily. She was advised to continue to perform CISC and was closely monitored in the outpatient setting with weekly clinic appointments. Unfortunately, the drain was removed accidently four weeks after insertion whilst the daily output was still around 25–45ml. A week later, the patient re-presented to ED with fevers, increasing suprapubic pain and elevated CRP at 207mg/L and leukocytosis of 12.7 (109/L). A repeat CT abdomen and pelvis demonstrated re-accumulation of anterior bladder wall abscess (60x57x60mm) with bilobar morphology and surrounding inflammatory stranding in the anteroinferior abdomen/upper pelvis (Fig. 3). This was initially managed with antibiotics (intravenous Ceftriaxone and oral Metronidazole) and urgent percutaneous drainage - 12 French pigtail drain inserted by interventional radiology. The patient was discussed with the infectious diseases team, given that aspirates from the abscess cultured Serratia marcescens. The drain remained in situ but continued to discharge. Following discussion at a multidisciplinary meeting (MDM), a decision was made to proceed with elective exploratory surgery and possible cystectomy, hysterectomy, and colectomy due to colonic thickening seen on previous CT scans. The operation was carried out by a team of urologists and general surgeons 6 months following insertion of the last pigtail percutaneous drain. Initially, diagnostic laparoscopy was performed which demonstrated significant dense adhesions between the sigmoid colon and the anterior abdominal wall and bladder. Intraoperative colonoscopy was also performed which revealed large inflammatory mass in the sigmoid colon but no other pathology. Decision was made to proceed with laparotomy, radical cystectomy and formation of ileal conduit, hysterectomy, and sigmoid colectomy with colonic anastomosis. An acute abscess at the right ureteric orifice and squamous metaplasia of the uterine cervix were also noted. All 13 lymph nodes examined were negative for malignancy. The case was discussed at an MDM and adjuvant chemotherapy was not recommended. Instead, the patient was to be followed-up with 3-monthly CT chest, abdomen and pelvis scans and MDM review. The team recommended that systemic treatment should be initiated in the case of recurrence.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a bladder wall abscess in an adult female with neurogenic bladder requiring CISC, and notably, the first in which histopathology revealed underlying SCC of the bladder. Bladder wall abscesses are exceedingly rare in both adults and children, and the existing literature is sparse. There has been only a handful of reports of spontaneous bladder wall abscesses in healthy adults – a 20-year-old male with suprapubic pain and fever2; a 37-year-old woman with LUTS and suprapubic pain for seven days,3 and a 60-year-old female with right inguinal pain and fevers.4 There are also several adult case reports of a bladder wall abscess in those with predisposing factors - diabetes mellitus,5 recurrent UTIs,6 infected urachal cysts,7,8 and immunosuppression.9 There has been one case associated with a neurogenic bladder requiring CISC in a child.10

The pathophysiology of bladder wall abscess development is not clear. Recurrent UTIs may cause disruption of the bladder epithelium, allowing uropathogens to enter the bloodstream cause bacteraemia and potential abscess formation.11 Catheterisation may predispose the same process by mechanically disrupting the urothelium. Evidence suggests that catheter-induced inflammation may prompt the release of fibrinogen from the urothelium, facilitating the adhesion and growth of otherwise non-adherent organisms such as Enterococcus faecalis.11 The isolation of E. faecalis from a urine culture in this case supports its potential role in both recurrent UTIs and in bladder wall abscess formation.

As in previous reported cases,2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 this patient presented with suprapubic pain and leukocytosis, prompting cross-sectional imaging, and revealing a bladder abscess. Treatment strategies vary but include radiology-guided percutaneous drainage, endoscopic de-roofing or open surgical intervention, typically accompanied by intravenous antibiotics. In this case, management involved repeat percutaneous drainage and courses of antibiotics, culminating in radical cystectomy, hysterectomy, and sigmoid colectomy. The diagnosis of pT3bN0 SCC was unexpected and carries significant prognostic implications. Reported 5-year overall survival rates for this stage of SCC range between 15 % and 48 %, with median survival of 12–24 months, even following radical cystectomy.12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 These outcomes are significantly poorer than for urothelial carcinoma of similar stage.18,19 The aggressive nature of bladder SCC, limited chemosensitivity, and its frequent diagnosis at an advanced stage all contribute to the unfavourable prognosis.19

Unfortunately, the initial symptoms, imaging, cystoscopic and microbiology findings were misleading and therefore, impeded the accurate diagnosis. Several months after initial presentation, histopathological analysis of the surgical specimens confirmed a low grade, moderately differentiated bladder SCC (pT3bN0). This rare histological finding accounts for only 2–5 % of all bladder cancers in non-endemic regions.12,13 It is most frequently associated with chronic bladder irritation from prolonged indwelling catheterisation, recurrent UTIs, neurogenic bladder dysfunction and urinary stasis.13,14,20 Although, the literature suggests that CISC reduces the risk of SCC in long-term catheter users, there have been several reported cases of bladder SCC following CISC.20 Our patient had been performing CISC since childhood, likely contributing to an increased risk of bladder SCC. The exact aetiology in this case will remain unclear. Recurrent UTIs, CISC, and neurogenic bladder are independent factors known to contribute to squamous metaplasia and progression to bladder SCC.12,14 Furthermore, CISC is likely to have masked some of the initial cancer symptoms in this case such as, haematuria, frequency, and UTIs. To our knowledge, there are no prior documented cases of SCC initially masquerading as bladder wall abscess in an adult patient with neurogenic bladder, performing CISC. This case underscores the diagnostic complexity and highlights the critical role of histological evaluation in non-resolving or atypical bladder lesions – especially in high-risk individuals with long-term catheter use.

Conclusion

Bladder wall abscesses carry substantial morbidity, as illustrated by this patient's need for repeat hospitalisations, percutaneous drainage procedures, and prolonged antibiotics. Early recognition and tailored management are essential. Crucially, in patients with neurogenic bladder and chronic catheter use, malignancy—particularly SCC—should be considered, even when initial presentations suggest infection. The presence of E. faecalis in the urine sample may have contributed to abscess formation but it also obscured the underlying malignancy. This case underscores the importance of early histological assessment in persistent or atypical bladder lesions, particularly in high-risk populations.
