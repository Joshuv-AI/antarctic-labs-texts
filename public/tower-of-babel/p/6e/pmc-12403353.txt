Strengthening country ownership of national antimalarial policymaking: insights from Laos, Pakistan and the Solomon Islands
Authors: Varunika S. H. Ruwanpura, Vilayphone Phongchantha, Ric N. Price, Koen Peeters Grietens, Kamala Thriemer, Caroline A. Lynch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe concept of “ownership” in global health policymaking broadly assumes that external donors and advisors expect recipient countries to actively steward their national programmes when assistance is provided. This study specifically explored country ownership of national policy decision-making for Plasmodium vivax malaria using three case studies: Laos, Pakistan and the Solomon Islands.MethodsYin’s comparative case study model, Kingdon’s Multiple Streams policy theory guided this analysis. From 2021 to 2024, interviews were undertaken with 29 national stakeholders, external partners and relevant global stakeholders.ResultsThere were four main findings: firstly, national malaria programme respondents expressed strong ownership of internal antimalarial policy processes but acknowledged nuanced influence of external donors on the national policymaking space. Second, in countries relying on external funding, antimalarial policy change is mainly triggered by the World Health Organization’s (WHO) recommendations and WHO national support was identified as a key enabler of policy change. Third, external donors often influence, or outline policy directions aligned with WHO recommendations, but policy change is also driven by antimalarial tools procurement constraints. Fourth, timing of updated WHO recommendations may not align with countries’ needs. Yet many malaria endemic countries face pressure from external funders, that resource national policy decision-making, to adhere to WHO recommendations, although the WHO considers its recommendations as advisory only. Overall, these external influences moderate country ownership of national policy change.ConclusionsThis study indicates that there is potential to strengthen country ownership of national antimalarial policymaking. This may require application of targeted policy acceleration levers such as better alignment of timing of global guidance with country needs and an understanding between external donors and national stakeholders that WHO guidance is advisory only and should be understood as one perspective relative to country needs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05510-7.

Background

Country ownership of policymaking can be interpreted in different ways depending on the policy agenda and type of donor-recipient relationship. For aid-funded initiatives, it is envisioned that stronger country ownership of these initiatives will lead to more successful public policy and health outcomes [1]. Broadly, the concept of country ownership stems from the idea that donors expect recipients to not simply receive a donation (financial or otherwise) but to also “own” it; that is, to proactively steward what donors help establish, and play an invested role in an initiative’s long-term outcomes [2].

In this paper ownership in the context of national decision-making and policy change regarding Plasmodium vivax malaria was explored. Most malaria endemic countries rely on the World Health Organization’s (WHO) recommendations to develop national antimalarial policy. Likewise, many donor organizations working in the global health space rely on WHO recommendations to guide their investments ensuring adherence to internationally recognized WHO Prequalification standards [3].

The WHO Global Malaria Programme’s (GMP) recommendations are generally broad with the intention that they are tailored by countries, to their own contexts [4]. However translation of global guidelines into national guidelines is often complex and challenging for many reasons, including disease specific factors such as differing endemicity and prevalence, health system structure and capacity, cost implications, political, governance and socio-economic factors [5].

In the broader malaria context, the ‘big push 2030’ is being advocated for by the Roll Back Malaria Partnership and driven by a suite of new tools predicted to become available from the research and development pipeline. The latter include new first line antimalarial therapies to overcome drug resistance, malaria vaccines, long-acting injectable drugs and new tools for combatting malaria transmission [6, 7]. In the Asia Pacific region, unlike in many African countries, the most dominant malaria species is P. vivax malaria, and the region aims to eliminate malaria by 2030. After nearly 70 years of relying on one treatment option to prevent relapsing P. vivax malaria, novel tools are now available with major potential to improve treatment outcomes. Notable new tools include shorter more effective primaquine regimens [8, 9], single-dose tafenoquine [10, 11] and quantitative testing for Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency at point of care [12, 13]. A significant number of countries in the Asia Pacific are in the pre-elimination phase and considering strategies for the prevention of re-establishment of transmission at sub-national level. Timely policy decisions regarding these new tools are essential to enable this region to accelerate their progress towards the malaria elimination goals.

There is very limited research on regional and national stakeholders’ views on the key external factors impacting country ownership of antimalarial policy change in the Asia Pacific region and how global P. vivax malaria recommendations are translated into national guidelines [14]. This study, therefore, investigated factors that moderate country ownership of policy change, when translating global P. vivax malaria guidelines into national recommendations, using national, regional and international stakeholders’ perspectives with reference to national policy documents and policy process maps.

Methods

Context

Despite new streamlined processes between WHO prequalification and global antimalarial policy formulation, policy change triggers such as new evidence, and new drugs and diagnostic tools registered by stringent regulatory authorities did not immediately initiate these processes for vivax malaria treatment policies [14]. Until recently, international donors like the Global Fund were unable to procure antimalarial drugs and tools without WHO prequalification of these and unwilling to procure these items without a global policy recommendation, resulting in complex and extended time lags in providing any new antimalarial tools to endemic countries [15].

In 2018, two stringent regulatory agencies the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the Australian Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA), approved adult tafenoquine for relapse prevention in P. vivax [16, 17]. Tafenoquine was only included in WHO guidelines in 2024, approximately six years after regulatory approval, with a geographic restriction to South America and a recommendation for controlled use and further research for other locations [18]. A specific prerequisite for tafenoquine use is quantitative G6PD testing to guide treatment. So far only one commercially available point of care test was submitted for WHO prequalification in 2020 [19]. This test was provisionally approved by the Expert Review Panel for Diagnostics (ERPD) in July 2019 [20] enabling its procurement by donors adhering to WHO guidelines and stipulations. Aligned with the revisions of the treatment recommendation this received WHO prequalification in December 2024 [21].

In 2024, the WHO also announced that it would begin a parallel policy development and drugs/tools regulatory pathway to shorten time taken for any updated policy recommendations to around 12 months [22]. National Drug Regulatory Authorities in Brazil, Thailand, Pakistan, Vietnam, Myanmar, Peru, Costa Rica and Columbia have registered tafenoquine [23, 24], and Brazil and Thailand have introduced it into national treatment guidelines based on feasibility studies undertaken by their National Malaria Programmes (through external support) [25, 26]. However, few other countries outside of South America are expected to introduce tafenoquine without WHO endorsement of its global use [27].

In the case of 7-day high dose primaquine (1.0 mg/kg/day) [9, 28] any national policy change towards this new regimens was initially affected by a conditional WHO recommendation against it in 2022, due to concerns over gastro-intestinal tolerability and the risk of haemolysis at this higher daily dose [29]. This recommendation was subsequently reversed in late 2024 opening the way for endemic countries to adapt this regimen [18].

Study countries were Laos, Pakistan and the Solomon Islands. They were selected because they differ regarding P. vivax malaria epidemiology and burden, risk factors such as G6PD deficiency, health system structure, political and governance systems, economic status, population size and socio-cultural environment (Table 1). Theoretical frame

Yin’s comparative case study model was used as the overall method for study design and analysing data gathered from the country case studies [43]. This approach uses findings from individual case studies to draw final cross-case conclusions. It requires adoption or development of a suitable theory to guide the case studies. Preliminary cross-case conclusions are then used to modify the adopted analytical frame if needed, deduce final themes, and develop a cross-case report.

Kingdon’s [44–47] widely adopted mainstream policy analysis approach of Multiple Streams Theory was used as the theoretical frame to guide the country case studies [48]. Mainstream policy analysis considers what values are at stake and whose voices are heard in the policy cycle. It also considers how political priority is generated. Kingdon’s theory suggests that “policy change occurs when advocates—‘policy entrepreneurs’—match problems with policy solutions in a politically favourable environment” [48]. The assumption is that the ‘problem,’ ‘policy’ and ‘politics’ streams run separately and concurrently, but when a policy entrepreneur brings these three streams together, a ‘policy window’ opens to action change. Politics of policy change relates to who influences the process and at what stage in the process [49].

Data were first inductively coded and then categorised and analysed by the themes of problems, policies and politics to understand and identify potential policy entrepreneurs and policy windows. Other a priori theories and concepts used in previous related studies [14, 50, 51] also informed the overall analysis and assisted in identifying the main theme of ‘country ownership’. These included Walt’s policy triangle framework [52], Clar et al.’s barries and enablers of uptake of evidence into health policy [53], the 3i policy framework [54] and Shiffman’s four factors influencing global and national health policy [55].

Definitions

While there is no universally accepted definition of ‘country ownership’ [2, 56, 57], a systematic review on ‘country ownership in development aid’ assumes that with recipient countries’ interest and participation, “owning” aid-funded initiatives will lead to more successful outcomes [2]. For this study, country ownership for decision-making on antimalarial policy change for P. vivax malaria was assessed and how factors external to national processes could enhance or moderate these processes. A ‘policy entrepreneur’ refers to “individuals who introduce and promote their ideas in many different fora and invest time and energy to increase the chances for an idea to be placed on the decision agenda” [58]. ‘Internal policy processes’ refers to processes within the control of the national government and the National Malaria Programme. ‘External influences’ on national policy processes refers to factors and processes beyond national government or National Malaria Program control that have an impact on national policy processes.

Data collection

Data was collected through a total of 50 in-depth interviews with 29 stakeholders. These included national malaria stakeholders, national drugs regulatory authority representatives, Ministry of Health representatives, representatives of international and local malaria partners such as donors and technical advisory organizations, and relevant global malaria stakeholders. Of these individuals, 16 were respondents working and residing in the study countries (Table 2). Respondents were provided a participant information sheet and written, or verbal consent was recorded for interviews.

In-depth interviews were conducted with stakeholders via Zoom [60] or in person. First-round interviews were held from 17th May to 24th October 2021. Second round interviews were held from 9th May to 11th May 2022 at a regional conference. Further interviews were conducted via Zoom [60] and in person from 1 st June 2022 to 8th January 2024. In-person interviews were undertaken in the Solomon Islands from 6th December 2023 to 14th December 2023. Discussions of between 30 and 60 min were conducted based on a topic guide developed by VSHR. The topic guide was adjusted to suit specific expertise of each interviewee. Up to three follow-up interviews were conducted if required to obtain further insights and allowing to capture temporal changes in perspective. Interviews were transcribed verbatim to clearly document responses to questions posed and checked by VSHR for accuracy. Interviews conducted in English were led by VSHR. Interviews in Laotian were conducted by VP with guidance from VSHR. VP transcribed and translated Laotian transcripts into English.

National Strategic Plans for malaria control and related policy literature provided by respondents such as reports on national malaria technical working group processes and national health system structure were reviewed (Supplementary Files 1 & 2). Policy change process diagrams (provided by National Malaria Program respondents) were used to compare information provided at interviews (Supplementary File 3).

Data analysis

Interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim using Otter.ai [61], edited for accuracy and uploaded to NVivo12Plus for analysis [62]. Reflexive memos were written for each interview with a new respondent before formal coding and to understand the primary researcher’s (VSHR) own positioning. Country case studies were developed from gathered interview data and individual country findings on the policy change process compared as a cross-case report in the first stage of the study (Supplementary File S1). These case studies were inductively coded to identify initial themes. Then, second cycle coding was undertaken to interpret how themes related to the adopted theoretical frame: Kingdon’s Multiple Streams [58]. At this point a significant new theme specific to this study was identified: country ownership of policy processes.

Respondents’ statements were used to illustrate findings. Respondents were de-identified when attributing responses and direct quotations as follows: National policy makers, including Ministry of health representatives, by a N#, malaria researchers by a R#, WHO national, regional or global representatives by a W#, external donor representative by a D# and regional network respondents as a RN#.

Policy process documents and reports, particularly the National Strategic Plans, were compared with interview responses. Comparing first-person accounts of policy processes with documented national policy guidelines and processes enabled a more nuanced understanding of these processes. Reviewed documents informed themes identified from interviews. COREQ guidelines for qualitative research reporting were referred to when collecting and interpreting data [63].

Ethics and consent to participate

The study was reviewed and approved by the Office of Research and Innovation, Charles Darwin University, Australia (H19081) and received a waiver of full ethical review by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Australian Northern Territory. The study was also approved by the Solomon Islands Ministry of Health’s research and ethics review board (HRE005/23) for the in-country case study. All participants provided consent before being included in the study.

Results

Results are presented through the lens of country ownership that emerged from the analysis. Under this theme, four identified moderating factors are presented: i) reliance on WHO recommendations to trigger national processes, ii) local and regional WHO offices’ capacity to assist with policy change, iii) reliance on external donors for procurement and policy implementation, and iv) availability of country-specific evidence and need for local feasibility studies.

Diverging views on country ownership

National Malaria Programme respondents felt that they had strong ownership of their internal antimalarial policy change processes (based on their expertise). However, in countries relying strongly on external funding, external influences on these processes moderate country ownership, de facto, reducing national decision-making ability and choices (N1- N10, W2, W4, W8, R1, D4, D5, M2). WHO and external donors however often regard countries as having ‘sufficient ownership’ over their national policy change processes. This reflects a potential gap in understanding of what constitutes “true or sufficient ownership” between national and global malaria stakeholders (N6, W7 to 9). The WHO perceives its role as one of providing global policies that countries should adapt to their own contexts.“WHO’s job is not to make country policies. And that’s why the language has changed. Before it used to be WHO policies. Now we have WHO recommendations and then country policies.” (WHO respondent)

International donors often consider WHO recommendations as non-negotiable for countries (D5, D6), indicating that while are supposed to be recommendations only, these recommendations may be adhered to and implemented more stringently by other external partners working with countries.

Reliance on WHO recommendations to trigger national processes

WHO treatment recommendations are considered the gold standard by most national stakeholders such as Ministry of Health’s and provincial health authorities (N1-N2, N5-N6) and are used to convince national decision makers of the reliability of evidence presented (N1-N3, N5-N6). Once it hits the WHO guidelines, then that sort of is all the evidence that we need to support, to support our, our local findings. So, in terms of what drugs to, to use as alternatives would be guided by WHO.” (National Malaria Programme respondent)

Both countries also rely on their WHO country and regional offices’ support for tailored technical advice to assist with triggering national policy change and developing National Strategic Plans for malaria (N2-N3, N5, N8). For example, in Pakistan and the Solomon Islands, policy change for P. falciparum malaria was triggered by the WHO’s recommendation to use artemether lumefantrine as a first line drug for malaria instead of artesunate sulfadoxine/pyrimethamine and chloroquine in 2018 and 2009, respectively (N5, N8). Historically, policy change triggered by therapeutic efficacy studies (TES) only resulted in treatment change for P. falciparum malaria (N2-N3, N5). This is mainly because national TES are routinely funded by external partners like the WHO (usually with Global Fund support) for P. falciparum malaria, but not for P. vivax malaria. Local and regional WHO offices’ capacity to assist with policy change

NMPs from all three study countries consult their local and regional WHO office when changing policy, with these working relationships established over many years (N1-N2, N6). However, the capacity of local WHO offices to assist with policy change varies between countries (depending on staffing levels), moderating national policy change viability and extent of policy ownership. This is highlighted in the translation of the 2022 WHO guidance on low dose short course primaquine to national guidelines, where rapid policy changes were made in Pakistan and Laos immediately after the WHO announcement. In Pakistan, collaboration between consultants of the National Malaria Programme, Global Fund principal recipient representatives and advice from WHO regional office enabled this policy change (N3, N8, W1). In Laos, a trusted long-term working relationship between the National Malaria Programme, the WHO Mekong Malaria Elimination programme, and WHO country office, including provincial level staff, enabled a quick policy change (N2, W2).“WHO is like our big brother who has worked with us closely.” (National Malaria Programme respondent)

In Laos, tailored advice from WHO to change P. vivax malaria radical cure policy came from country and regional level representatives (W1-W2, W4, N2, N6, N8), reflecting the significant role of WHO local and regional offices in progressing national policy change. Overall, national decisionmakers’ trust in the WHO as a global technical agency was identified as a policy change enabler in study countries. Reliance on external donors to procure new antimalarial tools, directly influences policy processes in Pakistan and the Solomon Islands, that both rely heavily on external support for high malaria burden provinces (N3, N5), thereby reducing country ownership of national policy change processes. Availability of country-specific evidence and need for local feasibility studies

Study countries prefer to have country-specific evidence, as well as global evidence, to inform policy change. However, studies equivalent to TES for falciparum malaria are not conducted for P. vivax malaria (N1-N2, N5) for several reasons including the need for longer follow up and difficulty in discerning relapse from re-infection. Countries therefore mostly rely on global evidence to guide national P. vivax malaria treatment policy decision-making, and this reliance was seen by respondents as moderating country ownership of national policy change.“As much as possible, we would like to have local evidence. However, like I mentioned, it’s very much dependent on finances to actually expand what we regularly do for efficacy studies. So, yes, we can rely on other evidence. And first and foremost, as long as it’s within WHO guidelines in terms of approval, then, then everything that follows on after that is easier.” (National Malaria Programme respondent)

Discussion

This study identified strong perceptions of country ownership over internal policy change processes. However, there are nuanced instances when external donors influenced the national policy decision-making space. External donors often influence or outline policy options to National Malaria Programmes, mainly in line with WHO recommendations, but also guided by donors’ procurement policies. This data also reflects the key role that WHO plays in triggering national policymaking, due to its role as the technical leader in global health and since the WHO was established in 1948 [65].

The precepts on which the WHO was created and its caretaker role to World Health Assembly countries, forming its member states, largely remained unchanged until recently [66]. External donors rely on WHO recommendations, often using WHO norms and standards to determine what antimalarial interventions will be funded in countries that that they support. Khan et al. [67] identified that when it came to setting policy goals and targets for monitoring and evaluation, donors’ influence was strengthened by linking their goals to global initiatives and norms. This indicates that taking global recommendations as the de facto standard could constrain and potentially delay national programmes by requiring them to choose from a narrower set of interventions.

There are however some examples of recent national treatment policy changes without a prior WHO recommendation, when a suitable “policy window” opened [49]. These include Indonesia which adopted artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) as a first-line treatment for falciparum malaria in 2006 [63] before a WHO recommendation on ACT was issued [64]. More recent examples of countries revising their treatment guidelines before updated WHO recommendations include Brazil and Thailand, both less reliant on external funding, introducing tafenoquine in 2024 [25, 26]. This indicates that WHO recommendations may not be in pace with country needs.

Gulbrandsson et al. [68] tested Kingdon’s theory of ‘policy windows’ opening when three streams influencing public policy–problem, policy and politics converge. Merging of these three streams leads to a window during which policy change can be triggered. Results from this study highlight that a policy window for national policy change is often opened through new global malaria recommendations. A recent example is the 2022 WHO recommendation for low dose short course primaquine [29] which was subsequently quickly adopted in Pakistan, Laos and other countries. However, the timing of updating global recommendations is often not suitably aligned with regional or even global malaria elimination targets.

Results from this study indicate that national malaria programmes perceive ownership over their national decision-making processes despite external donors’ influences in this space. Respondents from Laos even described WHO national counterparts as their ‘big brother’ indicating both influence in this decision-making space, and that WHO is an intrinsic part of national decision-making processes instead of being an ‘outside’ influence. However, and similar to Khan et al. [67] and Salisbury et al. [69], the overall findings suggest that national decisionmakers were still concerned that external donors may influence their antimalarial policy, leading to policies which may misalign with local needs and capacities.

Several studies indicate that WHO and global funders have significant power in shaping health priorities in low to middle income countries [69–71]. This influence can be directly through purchasing or provision of health care interventions, investment in research and technical assistance or indirectly, through their roles as global standards and norms setters [69, 72]. External donors relying primarily on global recommendations also means that they don’t have to consider the ability and institutional capacity of National Malaria Programmes to synthesize and assess evidence; this particularly applies to donors who are primarily financing partners rather than technical agencies [73]. Institutional capacity is a complex idea—and often, countries that have ‘capacity’ do not need external support and move ahead with policy change without external funding support [73]. External donors may not appreciate the recent strategic shift within WHO to move away from providing global ‘guidance’ to ‘recommendations’, encouraging countries to determine their own policies irrespective of WHO recommendations [14].

Study countries mostly rely on global evidence but delay changing policy, due to a perceived need for local ‘feasibility studies’. This is particularly the case for the introduction of tafenoquine. While it may be ideal to conduct ‘feasibility studies’ before introducing new policies, this should be weighed with cost implications and consideration of available global and regional evidence that might be sufficient to support policy change [74, 75]. However more clarity on what type of feasibility studies are required is needed. Feasibility or pilot studies are defined differently by various stakeholders. Clinical trial specialists or regulatory bodies, consider a feasibility study as research that assesses whether an intervention is appropriate for further testing [76, 77]. These studies are conducted before a main study and help researchers estimate important parameters for designing the main study. Contrastingly, for implementors, such as Malaria Programmes (and perhaps national regulators), a feasibility study can be either a real-world effectiveness study or more akin to phased implementation of a new intervention to gauge its acceptability within a health system, to understand what additional training and support might be required for health workers and to assess perceptions of new tools that could require specific messaging to ensure uptake [78, 79]. While external funders are often reluctant to invest in multiple high quality evidence generating studies such as clinical trials beyond what is required to move global policy forward, early implementation experience is an ideal and robust way to identify how best to introduce new interventions. It remains however unclear, whether those early experiences are required to support policy change, or whether they should be part of policy implementation once national policies have been introduced [80–82].

The emphasis by the WHO that global policies are ‘recommendations’ only, does not align with many external donors’ stipulations that a WHO recommendation is required to support funding. Nor does it address most countries’ perception that a WHO global recommendation is required to move ahead with a policy change. This poses challenges for countries where domestic resources are unavailable to support decision-making or there is a national perception of the need for an external ‘more legitimate’ source of technical knowledge.

Regional variability in policy decision-making approaches within the Asia Pacific indicates that there is room to strengthen decision-making capacity, raising awareness about new treatment options that are in the development pipeline and how they may suit a given epidemiological, political and socio-economic context [83, 84]. National decision-making strengthening mechanisms that could be applied for this purpose include the WHO affiliated Structured Operational Research and Training initiative [85] and more regionally specific programs like the Health intervention and Technology Assessment Program employed by the Thai Ministry of Health and the Options Assessment Toolkit developed specifically to support the policy decision-making process [84, 86].

There are several limitations to this work. Firstly, this is a three-country case study, and findings may not be generalisable to other P. vivax malaria endemic countries. Yet previous studies support the concept that country ownership of policy decision-making depends on domestic capacity to act independent of external donors [67, 69]. Secondly, there were limitations in ‘observing’ policy change in action as interviews were mostly conducted online. This was mitigated by undertaking multiple interviews over time with key respondents to understand how national policy change processes developed over the study period. Thirdly, a limited number of respondents were interviewed, and more in-depth national-level analysis and mapping of regional and national stakeholder power dynamics required to further explore findings. Fourth, potentially differences between the depth during face-to-face interviews and online interviews could have influenced the results. Fifth, the investigators own biases may have influenced data analysis. By engaging in interviews and discussions with national policymakers and discussion between the authors regarding study findings this was limited, and inherent biases were explored. Lastly, respondents’ accounts might be influenced by social desirability bias and ‘saving face’. This was mitigated by multiple interviews to build trust between the authors and study respondents.

There is room to strengthen country ownership in the context of national antimalarial policy development and external stakeholders can assist with this process. This is likely relevant not only for the study countries but broadly for many malaria endemic countries. This could be supported by better aligning the timing of global policymaking with regional needs and mutual understanding between donors and national stakeholders that WHO recommendations are advisory only to support national policymaking rather than binding. Furthermore, there is a need for better definition of what evidence is required for initiating national policy change versus more effective policy implementation.
