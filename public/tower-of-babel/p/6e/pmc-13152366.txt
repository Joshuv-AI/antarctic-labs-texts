Changes in cerebral oxygenation during sit-to-stand test in COPD patients compared to controls and the effect of oxygen supplementation

To the Editor:

COPD is a leading cause of morbidity, mortality and disability worldwide. While it is primarily characterised by persistent, non-fully reversible airflow limitation, COPD is now recognised as a systemic disease with extrapulmonary manifestations [1]. Among these, impaired functional performance – reflected in difficulty standing from a seated position, stair climbing or fast walking for everyday chores – leads to higher fall risk, reduced independence and poorer quality of life [2]. Importantly, these deficits often occur even in the absence of resting hypoxaemia. Contributing factors include skeletal muscle weakness, impaired balance and coordination [3]. While peripheral mechanisms are well documented, recent evidence suggests central nervous system involvement even in COPD patients without resting hypoxaemia. Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET)-based studies have revealed blunted cerebral oxygenation during exercise in this population [4, 5]. Unlike maximal exertion on a stationary bicycle, the 30-s sit-to-stand test (STST) assesses lower-limb function and dynamic balance, mimicking daily physical demands. Previous studies report impaired STST performance in COPD patients [3]. Therefore, the aims of the study were: 1) to assess whether non-hypoxaemic COPD patients exhibit reduced cerebral oxygenation during a functional activity test (the 30-s STST), compared with controls; 2) to determine whether the 30s-STST can detect differences in functional performance and symptom perception, between COPD patients and healthy controls; and 3) to explore whether acute oxygen supplementation can improve cerebral oxygenation and alleviate exertional symptoms, in COPD patients.

In this prospective case control study, 22 stable COPD patients without resting hypoxaemia and 19 matched controls were recruited. COPD diagnosis was based on the presence of post- bronchodilation forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)/forced vital capacity ratio <0.7 in patients with chronic respiratory symptoms and/or exposure to known COPD risk factors. The patients who were included in the study were followed up in a specialised COPD clinic of a tertiary hospital, so they had all undergone thoracic computed tomography and Doppler echocardiography during routine follow-up. Patients with recent COPD exacerbation, musculoskeletal or balance problems, interstitial lung disease, and/or disproportionate pulmonary hypertension were excluded [3]. The study was approved by the institution's Scientific Review Board Committee.

Participants completed two visits. The first visit included medical history, post-bronchodilation spirometry and arterial blood gases sampling. Within 2 weeks, participants underwent a 30-s STSΤ [6] under continuous pulse oximetry. The number of repetitions performed, Borg dyspnoea scale score and rate of perceived exertion (0–10 scales) were recorded. Cerebral oxygenation was continuously monitored at the prefrontal lobe using near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) (OxyMon; Artinis Medical Systems, the Netherlands) during seated rest (3 min), STST (30 s) and seated recovery (3 min). A subgroup of COPD patients (n=14) repeated the test, after 20 min of rest, under oxygen (liquid oxygen source via nasal cannula at a rate of 3 L·min−1) or medical air (sham oxygen) supplementation in a single-blind, randomised order. The control group conducted the measurements once, breathing room air. Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29). For comparisons between groups, the t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test was applied, depending on the normality of distribution of values. Within-group comparisons in the COPD subgroup were conducted using paired t-tests or the Wilcoxon test. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

22 COPD patients (mean±sd 67.5±8.2 years old; 17 males, five females; body mass index (BMI) 26.7±3.9 kg·m−2) and 19 age-, sex- and BMI-matched controls completed the study. Among COPD patients, FEV1 was 58.5±20.6% predicted. One (4.5%) patient was grouped in Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) stage 1, 10 (45.5%) patients in GOLD stage 2, eight (36.4%) patients in GOLD stage 3 and three (13.6%) patients in GOLD stage 4. All patients presented with emphysematous lung disease. Baseline arterial blood gases indicated no hypoxaemia for any of COPD patients (oxygen tension 65.2±3.7 mmHg, carbon dioxide tension 41.3 mmHg, pH 7.39±0.03 and bicarbonate concentration 26.2±0.6 mEq·L−1). Compared to controls, COPD patients performed fewer STST repetitions (13.50±3.63 versus 17.11±4.91, p<0.05) and reported higher levels of perceived dyspnoea (median (95% CI) 3.0 (1.0–4.0) versus 0.0 (0.0–0.0), p<0.05) and fatigue (3.0 (2.0–4.0) versus 2.0 (1.5–2.0), p<0.05) at exercise termination. During STST under medical air, COPD patients exhibited lower average responses in oxygenated haemoglobin (O2Hb) (p<0.01), in total haemoglobin (tHb, p<0.05) and haemoglobin difference (Hbdiff) (p<0.001), compared to controls (figure 1a–d), while no group differences were detected in deoxygenated haemoglobin (HHb). In COPD patients, oxygen supplementation during the STST also resulted in reduced dyspnoea scores when compared to medical air (1.3 (0.5–3.0) versus 2.5 (1.0–4.0), p<0.01), a trend towards reduced leg fatigue (2.0 (1.0–3.0) versus 3.0 (1.5–4.0), p=0.05), while STST repetitions were not significantly different (14.1±4.6 versus 13.6±4.5). Oxygen supplementation, compared to medical air, also resulted in increased O2Hb average responses (p<0.01), tHb (p<0.05) and Hbdiff (p<0.001) (figure 1e–h), while no differences were detected in HHb. It has to be noted that, during the STST, no patient in any protocol established hypoxaemia (peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) <90%) and the average SpO2 values for the two protocols were as follows. 1) Air protocol: SpO2 at rest 96.1±2.7%; SpO2 at end of test 92.4±3.1% (p<0.001). 2) Oxygen protocol: SpO2 at rest 98.3±1.3%; SpO2 at end of test 96.4±0.2% (p>0.05).

In summary, our data show that COPD patients exhibited a blunted rise in O2Hb and Hbdiff during the STST compared to controls, suggesting lower increases in cerebral oxygenation during this functional task. These impairments were possibly the result of lower oxygen delivery as no differences were detected between groups in oxygen extraction, as suggested by the similar HHb response between groups. When COPD patients received oxygen supplementation, cerebral oxygenation responses during STST significantly improved, reaching the response observed in controls. As no significant differences in HHb were detected under oxygen supplementation or medical air, these findings also suggest that cerebral oxygenation is blunted in COPD patients during the STST, mainly due to reduced oxygen delivery. Despite its short duration, the STST can sufficiently stress multiple physiological systems, due partly to its anaerobic nature and rapid muscle recruitment, and in our study, it elicited a significant drop in SpO2 (from 96.0±2.7% at rest to 92.3±3.2% at end test, p<0.001). While oxygen supplementation is not the only means to improve functional capacity, our findings suggest that enhancing oxygen delivery to the brain, whether through exercise training or supplemental oxygen, could enhance oxygen delivery, lessen cerebral oxygenation impairments and reduce exertional dyspnoea in COPD patients.

The majority of studies examining cerebral oxygenation during exercise in COPD patients using NIRS reported augmented brain O2Hb during maximal exercise [4, 7–10], but still lower than in controls [4, 7, 8], while one reported no differences at rest and during aerobic exercise [11]. However, incremental CPET [4, 9, 10] or steady-state workload cycling [5, 7, 8, 11] may not reflect functional limitations in everyday life. Our study using STST adds to this literature by assessing a task that mimics daily physical demands, and includes dynamic balance and postural control. Potential pathophysiological mechanisms of cerebral oxygenation impairment in COPD include hypocapnia-induced vasoconstriction due to hyperventilation [12], endothelial dysfunction driven by systemic inflammation [13] and lower haemoglobin affinity for oxygen caused by hypocapnia and lactic acid accumulation [12].

Regarding STST performance, COPD patients performed fewer repetitions and reported higher levels of perceived dyspnoea than controls, in agreement with the current literature [2, 3]. Interestingly, following oxygen supplementation, although the number of repetitions remained relatively unchanged, dyspnoea was significantly reduced. STST performance in COPD patients is related to quadriceps strength and endurance [6]. Oxygen supplementation improves exercise tolerance by alleviating ventilatory constraints and enhancing oxygen delivery to locomotor muscles [14]; however, it is still unclear whether it can be partly attributed to increased cerebral oxygenation. Higashimoto et al. [7] reported a link between exertional dyspnoea and decreased HHb in the pre-motor cortex of hypoxaemic COPD patients suggesting that cortical activation may be correlated with increased respiratory motor drive, while a recent meta-analysis showed that the use of oxygen generally relieves exertional dyspnoea in many, but not all, non-hypoxaemic COPD patients [15]. Thus, assessing cerebral oxygenation and cortical activation may help tailor oxygen therapy more effectively, though further research is needed.

This study is strengthened by the real-time noninvasive monitoring of cerebral oxygenation via NIRS, and the utilisation of a functional test that challenges balance, coordination and postural control. The protocol design included matched healthy controls and COPD patients blinded to the type of gas administered. Limitations include the relatively small sample size, cerebral oxygenation measurements only on the prefrontal cortex and lack of researchers’ blinding to gas type administered (oxygen versus air). It could be suggested that the short duration of STST may not allow sufficient time for the haemodynamic autoregulatory mechanism to increase cerebral tHb to compensate for any potential impairment in cerebral oxygenation. Nevertheless, the rapid transition from sitting to standing during the 30-s STST induces a rapid shift in blood volume distribution and challenges the cardiovascular system; this is why in individuals with compromised cardiovascular or autonomic nervous system control, this test can unmask dysfunctions that are not evident at rest, such as orthostatic hypotension, autonomic dysfunction and blunted haemodynamic responses [16].

In conclusion, COPD patients without resting hypoxaemia exhibit impaired prefrontal cerebral oxygenation during the STST compared to healthy individuals. These impairments, along with perceived dyspnoea and fatigue, improved after acute administration of oxygen supplementation. This could be interpreted as pointing toward a possible role of cerebral oxygenation in symptom experience during everyday tasks and a potential beneficial effect of oxygen supplementation during exercise. The 30-s STST appears to be a practical tool to detect both physiological and perceptual impairments in COPD. Longitudinal studies, assessing the long-term effects of oxygen therapy during exercise in this population are needed.
