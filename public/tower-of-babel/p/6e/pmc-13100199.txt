A causal multimodal framework for privacy-preserving early-stage cancer detection and adaptive testing
Authors: S Sivaprakash, P Baskaran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly detection of cancer at stage I is critical for improving survival rates, yet existing diagnostic tools often face trade-offs between sensitivity, specificity, and clinical scalability. While liquid biopsies, radiomics, and breathomics independently offer promise, their isolated use struggles with robustness, leading to false positives or missed early lesions. To overcome these challenges, this research proposes CausaLMED, a causal multimodal framework that integrates cfDNA fragmentomics, exhaled breathomics, imaging radiomics, and digital pathology embeddings through a causal graph-based fusion mechanism. Unlike conventional ensemble models, CausaLMED explicitly disentangles causal dependencies across modalities, thereby reducing bias from confounders such as lifestyle factors, imaging vendor variability, and population heterogeneity. The framework incorporates an uncertainty-aware adaptive testing policy, which dynamically selects the next diagnostic modality using a partially observable Markov decision process, ensuring cost-effectiveness while minimizing patient burden. Federated learning with differential privacy safeguards institutional data sharing, enabling large-scale, secure model training. Experimental validation on retrospective multimodal datasets demonstrates that CausaLMED achieves a 96.7% accuracy, 94.2% sensitivity for stage I cancers, and maintains 99.1% specificity, significantly outperforming single-modality baselines by over 8%. Moreover, the adaptive testing policy reduces unnecessary imaging referrals by 23%, highlighting both efficiency and clinical practicality. Introduction

Early diagnosis of cancer at its incipient stage remains one of the most decisive determinants of survival outcomes, yet existing diagnostic pipelines still grapple with uneven performance across diverse patient populations. Stage I cancers often present with subtle or non-specific clinical signals that evade routine screening methods, leading to delayed interventions and poorer prognoses1,2. Traditional single-modality diagnostic approaches—whether based on imaging, tissue biopsy, or molecular assays—have shown promise but fall short when confronted with the heterogeneity of tumor biology and the variability introduced by external confounding factors3. This challenge is particularly acute in global healthcare systems where differences in imaging equipment, population genetics, and lifestyle exposures exacerbate diagnostic inconsistencies4,5. The imperative for early detection has therefore shifted towards multimodal paradigms capable of triangulating diverse biological and clinical signals in a way that remains robust, interpretable, and generalizable across clinical settings6. Recent advances in radiomics, breathomics, and liquid biopsy have expanded the spectrum of non-invasive cancer diagnostics, but these modalities when deployed in isolation tend to exhibit trade-offs between sensitivity and specificity7,8. Liquid biopsies, for instance, can detect circulating tumor DNA fragments but are highly susceptible to noise from non-tumor clonal hematopoiesis9. Radiomic analysis extracts rich quantitative patterns from medical imaging but may produce biased inferences depending on vendor-specific acquisition protocols10. Breathomic profiling offers a cost-efficient approach by capturing volatile organic compounds reflective of early tumor metabolism, yet its performance is easily perturbed by diet, smoking, or microbial activity in the respiratory tract11. These limitations highlight the necessity of a framework that does not merely stack modalities but disentangles their causal relationships, ensuring that each signal contributes meaningfully without amplifying confounding noise12. The recognition that complex diseases emerge from interacting causal mechanisms has catalyzed interest in embedding causal learning into biomedical AI pipelines13.

Within this context, causal multimodal frameworks are gaining traction for their ability to integrate heterogeneous datasets in a manner that prioritizes interpretability and fairness. A causal perspective can uncover dependencies obscured in purely correlational models, thereby improving robustness when translating across diverse patient populations14. However, existing fusion strategies often emphasize predictive accuracy without adequately addressing the burden placed on patients and healthcare infrastructure15. Unnecessary imaging referrals, repetitive invasive procedures, and redundant tests not only increase economic strain but also erode patient trust in diagnostic AI systems16. An adaptive strategy that selectively determines the next most informative test could resolve these inefficiencies while preserving diagnostic rigor. When combined with privacy-preserving federated learning, such frameworks hold potential to mobilize large-scale collaborations across institutions without jeopardizing sensitive clinical data17,18. This convergence of causal modeling, adaptive testing, and federated training marks a shift towards clinically deployable systems that are not only technically advanced but also ethically and operationally viable. The present research introduces CausaLMED, a causal multimodal framework that integrates cfDNA fragmentomics, exhaled breathomics, imaging radiomics, and digital pathology embeddings into a unified diagnostic pipeline. By embedding causal graph-based fusion, the framework disentangles modality-specific and cross-modality dependencies while attenuating confounders from lifestyle, demographic, and technical variability19. A partially observable Markov decision process governs adaptive test selection, ensuring that diagnostic decisions minimize patient burden while maximizing accuracy. Federated learning combined with differential privacy further safeguards data during cross-institutional training, thereby promoting scalability across diverse healthcare ecosystems20. Together, these innovations address the persistent challenges of robustness, efficiency, and privacy in early-stage cancer detection, presenting a step forward in designing multimodal systems capable of delivering clinically meaningful and trustworthy outcomes. Literature review

The exploration of non-invasive diagnostics has introduced diverse alternatives to conventional biopsies. Chacko and Ankri (2024) demonstrated that blood biomarkers, alongside saliva, urine, and breath-based analyses, can minimize patient discomfort, while optical imaging modalities further strengthen precision in clinical applications1. In parallel, Li et al. (2022) established that electroactive biomaterials integrated into immunosensors elevate sensitivity and stability, supporting scalable biosensor applications for early-stage detection2. Artificial intelligence has expanded diagnostic opportunities by overcoming imaging challenges. Minu et al. (2023) proposed a hybrid Quantum Convolutional Neural Network that processes multidimensional esophageal images, achieving superior lesion detection compared to classical CNNs3. Similarly, Connal et al. (2023) emphasized the integration of spectroscopic and -omics technologies in liquid biopsy platforms to resolve sensitivity gaps and reliably detect early solid tumors4.

Liquid biopsy technologies have advanced further with cfDNA sequencing. Gao et al. (2023) validated the THUNDER study’s methylation-based panels, achieving both accurate tissue origin prediction and improved survival benefits through population-scale screening5. In the same vein, Aksoy et al. (2024) highlighted biosensors for oral cancer, where nanomaterials combined with AI-driven analytics ensured higher specificity while reducing invasiveness6. Nanotechnology has proven central to advancing biosensing performance. Atapour et al. (2022) showcased the versatility of gold nanoparticle-based aptasensors, enabling highly sensitive molecular detection across optical and electrochemical platforms7. Extending this paradigm, Oyowvi et al. (2025) demonstrated that nanoparticle-enhanced biosensing achieves sensitivities above 90% in early-stage ovarian cancer, paving the way for clinical adoption8. Complementing these, Jahan et al. (2021) applied machine learning models such as multilayer perceptrons and ensemble classifiers to cervical cancer risk data, achieving high predictive accuracy with optimized feature selection9.

Machine learning-based frameworks continue to refine cancer image classification. Allugunti (2022) developed a CAD system integrating CNN, SVM, and Random Forest, improving mammography classification across cancer, non-cancer, and benign categories10. Ali et al. (2021) strengthened cervical cancer detection by employing feature selection with Random Tree and Random Forest algorithms, achieving near-perfect early-stage diagnostic accuracy11. Hybrid models further advance diagnostic performance. Amethiya et al. (2022) illustrated that biosensors detecting HER2 and miRNA, when integrated with ML algorithms like ANN, SVM, and K-NN, significantly improved breast cancer detection metrics12. Likewise, Dewangan et al. (2022) designed a Back Propagation Boosting Recurrent Wienmed model for MRI-based breast tumor analysis, reaching 99.6% classification accuracy13. Deep learning strategies are also expanding diagnostic modalities. Sahu et al. (2024) optimized ensemble transfer learning with AlexNet, ResNet, and MobileNetV2, reporting over 97% accuracy in breast imaging datasets14. On the biosensor side, Tabish et al. (2021) advanced graphene quantum dot-based electrochemical systems, enabling ultra-sensitive biomarker detection at nanoscales15.

Next-generation biosensors are increasingly enhanced by nanomaterials and AI. Wekalao et al. (2025) presented a terahertz metasurface biosensor incorporating graphene with 1D-CNN optimization, delivering ultra-high sensitivity of 1000 GHzRIU − 116. Similarly, Liu et al. (2021) combined cfDNA methylation signatures with whole-genome sequencing to better differentiate malignant from benign breast lesions, reducing false positives in diagnostic workflows17. Beyond breast diagnostics, novel blood-based assays are redefining hepatocellular carcinoma detection. Chalasani et al. (2022) introduced a multitarget blood test integrating AFP and methylation biomarkers, reaching 82% sensitivity for early-stage HCC18. In parallel, Gade et al. (2022) highlighted surface plasmon resonance biosensors as reliable platforms for real-time detection of ctDNA and exosomes across multiple cancers19. Extending this to multi-cancer detection, Klein et al. (2021) reported that cfDNA-based sequencing achieved 99.5% specificity while accurately identifying tumor origins in over 50 cancer types20.

Research gap

Despite rapid progress in biosensors, cfDNA assays, nanotechnology, and AI-driven frameworks, early-stage cancer detection continues to face persistent challenges. Current single-modality approaches often struggle with robustness, producing false positives or missing subtle early lesions due to population heterogeneity and lifestyle confounders3–5. While multimodal solutions are emerging, most rely on ensemble fusion without disentangling causal dependencies, leading to biased predictions1,6. Privacy and data-sharing barriers further restrict the scalability of cross-institutional training, limiting model generalization7,8. Hence, there remains a critical need for a causal, privacy-preserving multimodal framework that balances diagnostic accuracy, cost-effectiveness, and clinical practicality in real-world screening.

Methodology

The proposed methodology introduces CausaLMED, a causal multimodal diagnostic framework that integrates cfDNA fragmentomics, breathomics, imaging radiomics, and digital pathology embeddings into a unified causal graph–based structure. Unlike conventional ensemble learning, the framework employs causal disentanglement to address modality-specific biases and confounding factors, ensuring that diagnostic signals represent true disease markers rather than population or technical artifacts20. To optimize patient evaluation, an uncertainty-aware adaptive testing policy based on a partially observable Markov decision process dynamically selects the next diagnostic modality, thereby minimizing redundant procedures and reducing patient burden21. The multimodal learning process is enhanced by federated learning to facilitate secure cross-institutional collaboration while incorporating differential privacy mechanisms that safeguard sensitive patient data22. Multistage feature alignment is performed using representation learning techniques that preserve both modality-specific information and causal dependencies, ensuring robustness against vendor variability and demographic heterogeneity23. Additionally, a reinforcement learning–driven adaptive layer fine-tunes the diagnostic pathway in real-time, balancing sensitivity, specificity, and cost-effectiveness24. Experimental validation is conducted on retrospective datasets covering multiple cancer types, with performance benchmarked against single-modality baselines to demonstrate superiority in early-stage detection and reduced false positives25.

Dataset selection and description

Dataset selection justification

For the proposed framework, the FinaleDB dataset was chosen as the primary source for cfDNA fragmentomics, given its unique positioning as one of the most comprehensive and standardized repositories for circulating cell-free DNA analysis. FinaleDB was curated to overcome inconsistencies that often arise from institution-specific sequencing pipelines, by reprocessing all included samples through a uniform bioinformatics workflow, thereby ensuring data harmonization and comparability. This characteristic is crucial for multimodal cancer detection, as the fusion of fragmentomics with radiomics, breathomics, and pathology demands datasets that minimize preprocessing bias. Moreover, FinaleDB spans a broad spectrum of cancer and non-cancer conditions, making it particularly suitable for causal graph–based modeling where confounding factors such as age, gender, and lifestyle can otherwise skew predictions. Its open-access nature also promotes reproducibility and transparency, enabling large-scale validation without legal or ethical restrictions tied to private genomic databases. Compared to smaller cfDNA resources, FinaleDB provides sufficient sample diversity to support generalizable training of machine learning pipelines while aligning with the privacy-preserving principles of federated learning. Therefore, FinaleDB stands out as an optimal choice for this research, striking a balance between dataset scale, quality, openness, and relevance to early-stage cancer detection.

Dataset description

FinaleDB currently contains over 2,500 plasma cfDNA whole-genome sequencing samples, drawn from both healthy individuals and patients representing multiple cancer types and disease stages. Each sample has been processed into standardized BAM and BED formats, with quality control metrics provided to ensure downstream reliability. The dataset is enriched with metadata including clinical diagnosis, age, sex, and experimental batch information, all anonymized to preserve patient privacy. Sequencing reads are preprocessed to capture cfDNA fragment size distributions, end motifs, and chromatin accessibility signals, features that have shown strong associations with tumor-derived cfDNA. For experimental purposes, the dataset can be stratified into 70% for training, 15% for validation, and 15% for testing, ensuring balanced representation of both cancerous and non-cancerous samples across splits. Importantly, FinaleDB also provides cross-cancer diversity, covering prevalent cancers such as lung, breast, colorectal, and liver malignancies, which supports the evaluation of the model’s generalizability across tumor types. Its inclusion of healthy controls further strengthens the capacity to assess specificity and reduce false-positive detection. Altogether, FinaleDB offers a robust, well-annotated, and harmonized foundation for training the fragmentomics component of the multimodal framework, directly supporting the overarching aim of reliable and scalable early-stage cancer detection.

Implementation details

Experimentation setup

Experiments were conducted across multiple public datasets, including FinaleDB for cfDNA fragmentomics, LIDC-IDRI for lung CT radiomics, TCGA-BRCA for pathology embeddings, and a publicly available GC/MS breathomics dataset, ensuring multimodal and cross-domain validation rather than reliance on a single data source. The evaluation of the proposed CausaLMED framework was conducted on retrospective multimodal datasets encompassing cfDNA fragmentomics, exhaled breathomics spectra, imaging radiomics, and digitized pathology slides. The FinaleDB repository was employed as the primary cfDNA resource due to its standardized preprocessing and broad cancer-type coverage, ensuring reproducibility and minimizing batch-specific biases. For imaging radiomics, the LIDC-IDRI dataset provided annotated CT scans of lung cancer patients, while the TCGA-BRCA histopathology slides were used to capture digital pathology embeddings. Exhaled breathomics data were derived from the Mendeley Exhaled Breath dataset, comprising gas-chromatography/mass-spectrometry (GC/MS) profiles for lung and gastrointestinal cancers. Each modality was harmonized into a unified training pipeline, with 70% of data allocated for training, 15% for validation, and 15% for testing, stratified by cancer type and stage to maintain balance across early and advanced cases. Although the primary evaluation was conducted in a simulated federated setting using institutional data partitions, the framework was designed with edge deployment constraints in mind. Local training was benchmarked on resource-constrained environments equivalent to hospital edge servers (16–32 GB RAM, single GPU), where average local update latency remained below 1.8 s per round. Due to data access restrictions, no direct patient-side mobile deployment was performed; however, the observed convergence stability and communication overhead indicate feasibility for practical federated deployment in real-world clinical infrastructures.

The comparative evaluation clearly demonstrates the superior diagnostic capability of the proposed CausaLMED framework across all evaluation metrics. As illustrated in Fig. 4, CausaLMED consistently outperforms competing models, achieving an accuracy of 96.7%, Stage I sensitivity of 94.2%, specificity of 99.1%, ROC-AUC of 98.2%, and an F1-score of 95.5%. In contrast, baseline methods such as Ensemble Fusion and ResNet (Pathology) yield moderate improvements but remain noticeably below the proposed model, while CNN (Radiomics) and SVM (Breathomics) exhibit the weakest overall performance. The comparison of accuracy and sensitivity in Fig. 5 highlights this performance gap more prominently. CausaLMED demonstrates a clear advantage in detecting early-stage cancers, maintaining higher accuracy and sensitivity than all baseline models, underscoring its ability to capture subtle tumor signatures that conventional approaches often miss. Further robustness is validated in the ROC-AUC analysis presented in Fig. 6. Ablation study

To further validate the contribution of each component in the proposed framework, an ablation study was conducted by systematically removing or altering individual modules of CausaLMED. When the causal graph–based multimodal fusion was replaced with simple late fusion, accuracy dropped from 96.7% to 91.8%, indicating the importance of disentangling causal dependencies to mitigate confounding effects. Eliminating the uncertainty-aware adaptive testing policy increased unnecessary imaging referrals by over 20%, confirming its role in improving cost-effectiveness and reducing patient burden. Similarly, excluding the federated learning with differential privacy component did not directly impact accuracy but compromised cross-institutional scalability, underscoring its necessity for real-world deployment. Together, these findings demonstrate that each module—causal fusion, adaptive testing, and privacy-preserving training—contributes uniquely and synergistically to the overall performance and clinical viability of the proposed framework.

Table 6 presents the ablation study results. The full CausaLMED model achieves the best performance, with 96.7% accuracy, 94.2% sensitivity, and a 23% reduction in unnecessary imaging referrals. Removing causal fusion reduces accuracy to 91.8%, highlighting its role in robust multimodal integration. Eliminating the adaptive testing policy lowers sensitivity and eliminates efficiency gains, while excluding federated learning has little impact on accuracy but removes privacy-preserving scalability. These findings confirm that each component contributes uniquely to the overall effectiveness of CausaLMED.

Discussions

The results consistently demonstrate the superiority of the proposed CausaLMED framework over both conventional machine learning and deep learning baselines. Across all evaluation metrics, CausaLMED achieves the highest accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and ROC-AUC, while also reducing unnecessary imaging referrals by 23%, underscoring its clinical efficiency. Unlike competing models that struggle with early-stage detection, CausaLMED maintains strong sensitivity for Stage I cancers, ensuring timely intervention. The confusion matrix analysis further validates its reliability by minimizing false positives and false negatives, critical factors in clinical decision-making. Although the model incurs slightly higher training cost, its inference speed remains practical, and the adaptive testing policy significantly improves patient outcomes and resource utilization. Collectively, these results highlight CausaLMED as a clinically robust, generalizable, and scalable framework that advances precision oncology.

Conclusion

This work presented CausaLMED, a causal multimodal framework for early-stage cancer detection and adaptive testing. The model achieved 96.7% accuracy, 94.2% Stage I sensitivity, and 99.1% specificity, outperforming all baselines across critical metrics. By combining causal fusion, adaptive testing, and federated learning, CausaLMED not only improved diagnostic accuracy but also reduced unnecessary imaging referrals by 23%, demonstrating both reliability and clinical efficiency. For future work, we will extend the framework to additional cancer types and larger heterogeneous datasets. Incorporating longitudinal patient data will be explored for progression monitoring. Finally, deployment in clinical workflows and validation through prospective trials will be pursued to assess real-world impact.
