Outcomes of mitral valve repair in ischemic mitral regurgitation: A two-decade experience
Authors: Lokeswara Rao Sajja, Gopichand Mannam, Aditya Koppula, Satya Bhaskara Raju Dandu, Satyendranath Pathuri, Venkata Satya Siva Saikiran Krishnamurthy, V Ramachandra Raju Pusapati, Ramesh Gudapati, Jyothsna Guttikonda, Sriramulu Sompalli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe study objective was to evaluate long-term survival outcomes after mitral valve repair in patients with moderate or moderate-to-severe ischemic mitral regurgitation undergoing concomitant coronary artery bypass grafting.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 212 consecutive patients who underwent mitral valve repair with restrictive annuloplasty and concomitant coronary artery bypass grafting for ischemic mitral regurgitation and coronary artery disease at a tertiary center between January 2005 and May 2025. Survival at 1, 5, 10, 12, and 20 years was assessed using Kaplan–Meier estimates and Cox proportional hazard models. Differential survivals were compared using hazard ratios for the following variables: age more than 65 years, sex, diabetes, residual postoperative mitral regurgitation, incomplete revascularization, left ventricular ejection fraction less than 40%, left ventricular end-systolic diameter 45 mm or greater, and preoperative renal dysfunction (serum creatinine ≥1.5 mg/dL).ResultsOf 212 patients, survival at 1, 5, 10, 12, and 20 years was 97.6%, 94.3%, 88.7%, 87.3%, and 81.1%, respectively. At 12 years, survival was significantly lower in patients with diabetes (82.1% vs 93% in nondiabetic patients; hazard ratio, 2.79 [1.18-6.61], P = .0197), residual mitral regurgitation (51.7% vs 92.9%; hazard ratio, 9.29 [4.30-20.04], P < .0001), and incomplete revascularization (38.9% vs 92.3%; hazard ratio, 11.19 [5.14-24.33], P < .0001). At 20 years, survival remained lower in diabetic patients (71.4% vs 93%; hazard ratio, 3.73 [1.71-8.12], P = .000933), patients with residual mitral regurgitation (51.7% vs 89.1%; hazard ratio, 8.38 [4.46-15.74], P < .0001), patients with incomplete revascularization (33.4% vs 86.1%; hazard ratio, 7.86 [3.97-15.55], P < .0001), and patients with left ventricular end-systolic diameter 45 mm or greater (65.9% vs 88.1%; hazard ratio, 5.29 [2.81-9.96], P < .0001). Age more than 65 years, sex, left ventricular ejection fraction less than 40%, and preoperative renal dysfunction did not significantly affect 12- or 20-year survival. On multivariate analysis, residual mitral regurgitation and incomplete revascularization independently predicted 12-year mortality, whereas at 20 years, residual mitral regurgitation, incomplete revascularization, and left ventricular end-systolic diameter 45 mm or greater were independent predictors.ConclusionsThis 2-decade experience shows that mitral valve repair with restrictive annuloplasty combined with coronary artery bypass grafting yields favorable long-term survival in moderate or moderate-to-severe chronic ischemic mitral regurgitation, particularly when residual mitral regurgitation is avoided, revascularization is complete, and preoperative left ventricular end-systolic diameter is less than 45 mm.Key Words: ischemic mitral regurgitation, mitral valve repair, survival analysis

The 20-year mortality is higher with residual MR, incomplete revascularization, and LVESD 45 mm or greater.

Central Message

With proper candidate selection and repair strategy, durable outcomes are achievable in moderate or moderate-to-severe chronic IMR with concomitant CABG.

Perspective

Residual MR, incomplete revascularization, diabetes mellitus, and LVESD 45 mm or greater are key predictors of reduced long-term survival in patients with moderate or moderate-to-severe chronic IMR undergoing repair with concomitant CABG, necessitating close attention to these factors for patient selection and procedural optimization.

Ischemic mitral regurgitation (IMR) is a heterogeneous complication of myocardial infarction, occurring in 13% to 50% of patients.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Mitral regurgitation (MR) after myocardial infarction is broadly categorized into acute and chronic IMR based on the underlying mechanism. Acute IMR is uncommon and typically results from papillary muscle rupture, leading to sudden volume overload and severe hemodynamic instability. In contrast, chronic IMR is functional in nature and characterized by left ventricle (LV) remodeling and subsequent valvular distortion. Its primary mechanism is adverse remodeling of the ischemic LV, which subsequently causes papillary muscle displacement, annular dilatation, and leaflet tethering, collectively contributing to progressive MR.7,8

Although robust evidence supports mitral valve repair (MVr) over replacement for primary (organic) MR, significant uncertainty remains regarding the optimal surgical strategy for secondary (ischemic) MR.7,8 Although mild IMR generally does not necessitate mitral valve intervention, severe IMR has been shown to have better 2-year outcomes with mitral valve replacement compared with MVr in a randomized trial.9 Restrictive annuloplasty remains the most commonly used technique for patients with moderate or moderate-to-severe IMR.7 However, this approach is limited by a high recurrence rate, with MR returning in 58.8% of patients at 2 years,9 and has a potential negative influence on long-term survival. Although repair is known to improve heart failure symptoms, there is a significant paucity of evidence on the long-term survival benefit of repair for chronic moderate or moderate-to-severe IMR. In this study, we report the long-term outcomes from a 2-decade retrospective cohort of patients who underwent MVr combined with coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) for significant coronary artery disease and chronic IMR. We also report on the salient factors affecting long-term survival.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

This observational study included 212 consecutive patients with moderate or moderate-to-severe chronic IMR and coronary artery disease defined as 70% stenosis or greater in 1 or more epicardial coronary arteries or 50% or greater stenosis of left main coronary artery on angiography. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee, Star Hospitals, Hyderabad (Approval No: SH/CT/IEC (60)/2025-26/04, dated: May 17, 2025), and the Institutional Ethics Committee granted a waiver for informed consent because the study is based on retrospective data analysis. All patients fulfilling the following selection criteria formed the study cohort. Inclusion criteria were patients referred for CABG with concomitant MVr and patients with preoperative moderate or moderate-to-severe MR (as defined by Lancellotti and colleagues 200310). Exclusion criteria were preoperative mild/severe MR; any MR more than mild after repair; concomitant valve intervention other than MVr; acute IMR; papillary muscle rupture; organic/primary MR; severe aortic, pulmonary, tricuspid valve disease; and prior mitral valve surgery or coronary bypass surgery. All eligible patients were recruited between January 2005 and May 2025. Clinical, operative, and follow-up data were retrospectively collected from the institutional database.

Echocardiographic Evaluation

Echocardiographic data at 3 time points were collected for the study: the preoperative period, the postoperative period (at the time of discharge), and the last follow-up. Preoperative MR severity was quantified according to contemporary guidelines.10, 11, 12, 13 Patients were eligible if the MR severity met any of the following 2 quantitative thresholds for moderate or moderate-to-severe MR: effective regurgitant orifice area 0.20 to 0.39 cm2, regurgitant volume 30 to 59 mL, regurgitant fraction 30% to 49%, vena contracta width 0.3 to 0.8 cm, or ratio of the mitral regurgitant jet area to the left atrial area: 20% or less mitral regurgitant jet area to the left atrial area less than 40% and jet area 4 to 8 cm2.13 Patients with mild or severe MR (outside these ranges) were excluded. Left ventricular end-diastolic and end-systolic dimensions, as well as ejection fraction, were measured using standard transthoracic echocardiography. MR severity was classified as grade I (mild), grade II (moderate), grade III (moderately severe), or grade IV (severe). All patients demonstrated Carpentier type IIIb MR, and a minority also exhibited type I MR, confirmed by transthoracic echocardiography, intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography, or direct surgical inspection. Seven patients showed localized type II (prolapse of P1/P3 segments) lesion in addition to type IIIb. Adequacy of mitral repair was assessed with intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography, and patients with more than mild residual MR were returned to cardiopulmonary bypass for conversion to valve replacement.

Surgical Technique

All operations were performed via a median sternotomy and using standard cardiopulmonary bypass with dual venous and ascending aortic cannulation. Myocardial protection consisted of antegrade cold blood cardioplegia or del Nido cardioplegia under moderate hypothermia as reported earlier.11 CABG was completed before addressing the mitral valve. The mitral valve was approached through the standard left atriotomy or via a superior septal incision in patients with a small left atrium. Comprehensive functional and segmental valve analysis guided the repair. Restricted mitral annuloplasty was performed in all cases using a Carpentier-Edwards Classic annuloplasty ring (Edwards Lifesciences LLC) or Carpentier-Edwards Physio annuloplasty ring (Edwards Lifesciences LLC). When mild residual MR persisted, suture commissuroplasty (n = 11), secondary chordal cutting (n = 4), or placement of polytetrafluoroethylene chordae (n = 7, polytetrafluoroethylene chordal placement done for a single chordal elongation of the P3 segment in 4 patients and the P1 segment in 3 patients) for elongated chordae was performed as appropriate. No subvalvular procedures, such as papillary muscle relocation or approximation of papillary muscles, were required. In the present study, residual MR is defined as any MR that persists after repair. Only patients with mild or trivial MR were included in the study cohort. All cases with more than mild residual MR were converted to mitral valve replacement intraoperatively.

Statistical Analysis

The primary end point was all-cause mortality. Follow-up extended from January 2005 to May 2025 and included both early and late survival. Unadjusted survival was expressed as frequencies and percentages. Adjusted survival (adjusted for number-at-risk after removal of dead/censored patients) at 1, 5, 10, 12, and 20 years was represented graphically using Kaplan–Meier plots. Predictors of mortality, including age more than 65 years, sex, preoperative renal dysfunction (serum creatinine ≥1.50 mg/dL), left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVESD) 45 mm or greater, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) less than 40%, postoperative residual MR, incomplete revascularization, and diabetes mellitus, were analyzed using a Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression model. The significant predictors in univariate modeling were combined in multivariate Cox models. The effect of a predictor in the model was expressed as a hazard ratio (HR) with 95% CIs. All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio software (R version 4.5.2).14 Kaplan-Meier analysis and Cox regression were done using the in-built functions in ggsurvfit 1.2.015 and survival 3.8-316,17 R packages.

Thirty-day mortality occurred in 3 patients (1.4%), resulting in an early survival of 98.6%. The number of individuals who survived at 1, 5, 10, 12, and 20 years was 207 (97.6%), 200 (94.3%), 188 (88.7%), 185 (87.3%), and 172 (81.1%), respectively. Patients aged more than 65 years (n = 64) had survival of 84.4% (54/64) at both 12 and 20 years, whereas patients aged 65 years or less (n = 148) had survival of 88.5% (131/148) at 12 years and 81.1% (120/148) at 20 years. Age more than 65 years was not associated with mortality at 12 years (HR, 1.29; 95% CI, 0.59-2.83; P = .523) or 20 years (HR, 1.59; 95% CI, 0.79-3.20; P = .196). Female patients (n = 44) had survival of 84.1% (37/44) at both 12 and 20 years, and male patients (n = 168) had survival of 88.1% (148/168) at 12 years and 82.8% (139/168) at 20 years. Sex was not significantly associated with mortality at 12 years (HR, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.31-1.75; P = .49) or 20 years (HR, 0.67; 95% CI, 0.34-1.35; P = .264).

Diabetic patients (n = 112) had survival of 82.1% (92/112) at 12 years and 71.4% (80/112) at 20 years. Nondiabetic patients (n = 100) had survival of 93% at both intervals (93/100). Diabetes mellitus was associated with increased mortality on univariate analysis at 12 years (HR, 2.79; 95% CI, 1.18-6.61; P = .0197) and 20 years (HR, 3.73; 95% CI, 1.71-8.12; P = .000933). Diabetes was not statistically significant in multivariate analysis at 12 years (HR, 1.68; 95% CI, 0.60-4.27; P = .280) or 20 years (HR, 1.97; 95% CI, 0.84-4.65; P = .119). Mild residual MR was present in 29 patients, with survival of 51.7% (15/29) at both 12 and 20 years. Patients without residual MR (n = 183) had survival of 92.9% (170/183) at 12 years and 89.1% (163/183) at 20 years. Residual MR was associated with significantly increased mortality on univariate analysis at 12 years (HR, 9.29; 95% CI, 4.30-20.04; P < .0001) and 20 years (HR, 8.38; 95% CI, 4.46-15.74; P < .0001). Multivariate analysis remained significant for both 12 years (HR, 6.76; 95% CI, 2.86-16.02; P < .0001) and 20 years (HR, 3.86; 95% CI, 1.82-8.19; P = .000443). Incomplete revascularization was present in 18 patients, with survival of 33.4% (6/18) at 12 and 20 years. Complete revascularization (n = 194) showed survival of 92.3% (179/194) at 12 years and 86.1% (167/194) at 20 years. Incomplete revascularization was associated with markedly increased mortality in univariate analysis at 12 years (HR, 11.19; 95% CI, 5.14-24.33; P < .0001) and at 20 years (HR, 7.86; 95% CI, 3.97-15.55; P < .0001). Multivariate analysis confirmed significance at both 12 years (HR, 7.90; 95% CI, 3.00-20.81; P < .0001) and 20 years (HR, 5.82; 95% CI, 2.81-12.04; P < .0001). A total of 60 patients had preoperative LVEF less than 40%, with survival of 85% (51/60) at both 12 and 20 years. Patients with LVEF 40% or greater (n = 152) had survival of 88.2% (134/152) at 12 years and 82.9% (126/152) at 20 years. Preoperative LVEF less than 40% was not associated with mortality at 12 years (HR, 1.25; 95% CI, 0.56-2.79; P = .588) or 20 years (HR, 1.61; 95% CI, 0.84-3.09; P = .152). Eighteen patients had serum creatinine 1.5 mg/dL or greater, with survival of 83.4% (15/18) consistently from 5 to 20 years. Patients with normal creatinine (n = 194) had survival of 87.6% (170/194) at 12 years and 80.9% (157/194) at 20 years. Renal dysfunction did not significantly predict mortality at 12 years (HR, 1.32; 95% CI, 0.39-4.40; P = .656) or 20 years (HR, 2.17; 95% CI, 0.65-7.28; P = .21). Forty-four patients had LVESD 45 mm or greater, with survival of 65.9% (29/44) at both 12 and 20 years. Patients with LVESD less than 45 mm (n = 168) had survival of 92.9% (156/168) at 12 years and 88.1% (148/168) at 20 years. LVESD 45 mm or greater was associated with increased mortality on univariate analysis at 12 years (HR, 4.45; 95% CI, 2.08-9.51; P = .000117) and 20 years (HR, 5.29; 95% CI, 2.81-9.96; P < .0001). LVESD 45 mm or greater remained significant in multivariate analysis only at 20 years (HR, 2.36; 95% CI, 1.15-4.81; P = .0186).

The results of echocardiographic follow-up are elaborated in the Appendix E1. Ejection fraction (%) at 3 time points was preoperative LVEF (46.5% ± 10.2%), postoperative LEVF (46.8% ± 9.3%), and LVEF (47.1% ± 8.2%) recorded at the end of the follow-up period. Repeated-measures analysis of variance with Greenhouse-Geisser correction did not show a statistically significant difference in LVEF (P = .202) through the follow-up period. During follow-up, most patients demonstrated stable or improved LVEF compared with preoperative values (Table E1), consistent with reverse remodeling of the left ventricle and enhanced systolic function. A post-operative decrease in LVEF was noted in a minority of patients and was associated with a higher risk of mortality (Table E2).

To summarize, both 12- and 20-year survivals were significantly lower with diabetes mellitus, residual MR, incomplete revascularization with CABG, and a preoperative LVESD 45 mm or greater when these variables were analyzed individually with univariate models. On the other hand, multivariate testing for the combined influence of all the significant univariate predictors found that only residual MR and incomplete revascularization were significant predictors for 12-year survival. In contrast, 20-year survival significantly depended on a combination of residual MR, incomplete revascularization, and a preoperative LVESD 45 mm or greater. Diabetes mellitus showed a nonsignificant trend in the multivariate model. There was no statistically significant difference in outcomes between patients with normal versus impaired renal function and moderate LV systolic dysfunction versus mild dysfunction/preserved LV function at 12 and 20 years.

Discussion

Our 20-year experience with restrictive mitral annuloplasty plus CABG in a cohort of moderate and moderate-to-severe (excluding both mild and severe cases) chronic IMR showed a survival of 97.6% at 1 year, 94.3% at 5 years, 88.7% at 10 years, 87.3% at 12 years, and 81.1% at 20 years. In contrast to previous studies, the high 20-year survivals we have reported (∼81%) can be due to careful patient selection criteria, which resulted in a cohort with inherently lower risk, in addition to the standardized protocols and high-volume surgical expertise, among others. Multivariate analysis identified residual or recurrent MR, incomplete revascularization, and diabetes as independent predictors of mortality. Patients discharged with residual MR had markedly lower 12- and 20-year survival (51.7%) than those with durable repairs (93% and 89.1%), and incomplete revascularization had a similar detrimental effect. These observations align with contemporary studies showing that incomplete revascularization is a significant determinant of mortality in ischemic cardiomyopathy; Kusunose and colleagues found that incomplete revascularization increased mortality (61% vs 28%) and was a stronger predictor than MR severity.17 Most patients in our cohort underwent repair with a 28-mm ring (152/212), whereas smaller (26 mm) or larger (30-32 mm) rings were used in selected patients. Stable or increased LVEF in the follow-up after restrictive mitral annuloplasty with concomitant revascularization in this cohort is consistent with reverse remodeling of the left ventricle.18

The long-term survival in our study exceeds that reported in several randomized and observational studies. In the STICH sub-study of 1212 patients, CABG plus mitral repair reduced mortality compared with CABG alone (adjusted HR, ≈0.41)19; however, the trial was not randomized with respect to the valve intervention, and the follow-up was limited. A small, randomized trial of patients with moderate IMR reported survival of 83% versus 77% at 12 years and 80% versus 72% at 15 years for CABG with restrictive annuloplasty versus CABG alone,20 suggesting that durable repair may provide a modest long-term benefit. In contrast, the Cardiothoracic Surgical Trials Network randomized study of severe IMR found no significant difference in survival at 2 years between repair (19% mortality) and replacement (23% mortality) but noted a high recurrence of moderate/severe MR after repair (59% vs 3.8% after replacement).9

An extensive Medicare analysis of 10,471 beneficiaries reported a 20-year restricted mean survival time of 6.02 years with CABG with MVr versus 5.57 years with CABG with MV replacement, a difference of only 5.4 months after risk adjustment.21 When surgeon preference was used as an instrumental variable to control for selection bias, the survival difference disappeared, suggesting that unmeasured factors, rather than the operation itself, explained the apparent advantage.21 A meta-analysis of 9 observational studies found that replacement was associated with significantly higher short-term and long-term mortality than repair (HR, 1.35 [long-term mortality]; odds ratio, 2.67 [operative mortality]).7 These data support the notion that repair can be superior to replacement when performed by experienced surgeons and when durable.

Magne and colleagues compared repair and replacement in 370 patients and found lower operative mortality after repair (9.7% vs 17.4%) but no difference in 6-year survival (73% vs 67%).7 Meyer and colleagues observed 1-, 5-, and 10-year survivals of 96%, 88% and 69%, respectively, in a mixed cohort of mitral repairs and identified functional MR, poor preoperative ejection fraction, and residual MR as predictors of late death.22 Timek and colleagues studied 128 patients with IMR repaired using the etiology-specific GeoForm ring; they reported 1-, 5-, and 10-year survivals of 91%, 77%, and 44%, respectively, and freedom from recurrent moderate/severe MR of 98%, 94%, and 80%, respectively.23 Although LV volumes improved, survival beyond 10 years was poor, highlighting the importance of durable repair and possibly reflecting complex patient selection. The 10-year (88.7%) and 20-year (81.1%) survivals in our study exceed these figures.

Several smaller series provide insight into early and mid-term results. Refaie and colleagues reported no deaths at 1 year and an early mortality of 5% after mitral reduction annuloplasty for moderate IMR; 1-year survival was 95%, and LVEF improved significantly, but MR recurred in many patients by 1 year.24 Furukawa and colleagues documented freedom from recurrent MR of 97% at 3 years and 83% at 5 years, and freedom from cardiac death of 81% and 74% in patients undergoing mitral annuloplasty with or without subvalvular repair.25 Sajja and colleagues reported satisfactory early- and mid-term results (mean follow-up of 19 months) in 127 patients with chronic IMR but noted the absence of long-term data.26 An off-pump CABG study showed that patients with untreated mild/moderate IMR had 5-year survival of 78% and 8-year survival of 42% compared with 88% and 77% in patients without IMR27; these findings emphasize that leaving even moderate IMR uncorrected during CABG is associated with worse long-term outcomes.

Multiple studies have identified factors influencing outcomes after mitral repair. In our series, residual MR and incomplete revascularization were associated with HRs of 5.4 to 8.9 for late death. Kusunose and colleagues corroborated the importance of incomplete revascularization, showing it to be an independent predictor of mortality in patients with severe ischemic cardiomyopathy (61% vs 28% survival).17 Diabetes and older age were also associated with increased mortality in their study, and we found a similar trend, with diabetic patients having lower 20-year survival (71.4% vs 93%). Meyer and colleagues reported that poor preoperative ejection fraction, larger LV size, and absence of a ring predicted recurrence and mortality.22 These data underscore the need for comprehensive preoperative assessment, including ventricular geometry and viability, and complete revascularization of all viable territories.

Our findings support current guidelines recommending repair of moderate–severe IMR at the time of CABG. Durable repair offers the potential for near-normal longevity when combined with complete revascularization. Conversely, leaving MR untreated during CABG or accepting residual MR at discharge results in substantially worse survival. The high recurrence rates seen in randomized trials of restrictive annuloplasty alone reinforce the value of adding subvalvular procedures when tethering is severe.9 However, the success of our program suggests that high-volume center expertise, standardized protocols, and careful patient selection are pivotal. Centralization of complex IMR surgery to specialized centers may improve outcomes. Future research should focus on refining techniques to minimize recurrence (eg, papillary muscle approximation, hybrid valve–ventricular devices) and on developing tools to stratify which patients will benefit most from repair versus replacement.26

Limitations

This study has several limitations. As a retrospective, single-center observational study, it is subject to inherent selection bias and unmeasured confounding factors. The cohort spans more than 2 decades, during which surgical techniques and perioperative care evolved, and there is no contemporaneous control group for direct comparison, limiting generalizability to current practice. Most patients received a single ring size, and the excellent results from this high-volume center may not translate to smaller centers or those using different devices. Also, differential follow-up and censoring rates for the comparison groups can potentially bias the Kaplan–Meier estimates and HRs.

Conclusions

In this 2-decade follow-up study of patients undergoing MVr with concomitant CABG for moderate or moderate-to-severe chronic IMR and coronary artery disease, good (∼80%) long-term survival at 20 years was observed. Residual MR, incomplete revascularization, diabetes mellitus, and LVESD 45 mm or greater emerged as the salient predictors of reduced long-term survival. Careful patient selection, high-volume surgical expertise, and standardized treatment protocols translate into good long-term survival.

Long-Term Echocardiographic Follow-up of Patients

For all patients, the ejection fraction (%) was documented at 3 time points: preoperative LVEF (46.5 ± 10.2%); postoperative LEVF (46.8% ± 9.3%); and LVEF (47.1% ± 8.2%) recorded at the end of the follow-up period. The distribution of the LVEF at these time points is shown in Figure E1.

The distribution of LVEF at the 3 time points: Preoperative, postoperative, and last follow-up values. Patients were followed up for 4 to 243 months. The mean period of follow-up was 103.5 ± 60.5 months. Repeated-measures analysis of variance with Greenhouse-Geisser correction did not find a statistically significant difference in LVEF [F(2, 418) = 1.64, P = .202] through the follow-up period.
