Atypical presentation of Acrodermatitis enteropathica in a child: later onset with life-threatening severe extensive dermatitis and septic shock

Introduction

AE is a rare and severe autosomal recessive disease caused by a mutation of the SLC39A4 gene, resulting in a zinc absorption disorder [1]. Zinc, a vital micronutrient in over 200 metalloenzymes, is essential for the body’s metabolic and biochemical pathways [2, 3]. The presentation typically occurs in breastfed newborns after weaning, while formula-fed infants experience it sooner. The delay in the onset of symptoms in breast-fed children is due to the higher bioavailability of zinc in human milk [4].

The skin manifestations of AE include eczematous, vesiculobullous, pustular, or erosive dermatitis, acral and scaly erythematous plaques that are generally periorificial on the face or anogenital area, and hair loss. Other rare manifestations include paronychia, onychodystrophy, angular stomatitis, cheilitis, conjunctivitis, photophobia, and prolonged wound healing. The upper lip is frequently spared [5–7]. Extracutaneous complications include diarrhea, irritability, lethargy, anorexia, delayed puberty, male hypogonadism, anemia, growth retardation, neuropsychiatric issues, prenatal morbidity, hyposmia, and hypogeusia [8].

While the classic triad of AE is well-documented, progression to life-threatening complications like septic shock is exceptionally rare due to the good response to medical treatment when timely diagnosed and managed. Considering this rare condition, early diagnosis is critical. This case report presents an 8-year-old child who reported a history of skin manifestations and was admitted to the hospital in a severe condition with septic shock.

Case presentation

An eight-year-old Iranian boy was born to consanguineous parents and was referred to the hospital’s medical emergency room. He was immediately admitted to the intensive care unit with a diagnosis of septic shock. He had a history of dermatitis from the age of two years old. He was the family’s fourth kid, with three healthy sisters, born via natural vaginal delivery at 40 weeks’ gestational age, weighing 2900 g at birth, and with a normal perinatal and developmental history. He was exclusively breastfed from birth until the age of two, following which complementary solid foods were introduced according to the standard nutritional schedule. After weaning at two years old, he developed pustular lesions, erosions, and multiple ulcers in the genital area, followed by lesions on the face. With the first diagnosis of allergic contact dermatitis, he was placed on a diet to address food allergens, but there was no improvement. Subsequently, he received a course of zinc supplements and multivitamins, which led to some improvement in the lesions, but the ulcers persisted and did not completely heal. At the age of seven, he discontinued zinc and multivitamin supplements and underwent treatment with Chinese herbal medicines. After one year, in addition to worsening skin lesions, the patient was in impaired psychosocial engagement, and he was in septic shock by the time of admission.

Upon initial physical examination, vital signs indicated blood pressure (BP) of 70/40 mmHg, pulse rate of 180 per minute, respiratory rate of 30 per minute, oxygen saturation measured by pulse oximetry of 94% in room air, and body temperature of 36.5 °C. The distal pulse of all extremities was weak, and the urine output was decreased. There was generalized hemorrhagic and erosive dermatitis predominantly affecting the periorofacial, genital, and limb areas. The child appeared thin and had generalized alopecia of his scalp, eyelashes, and eyebrows (Fig. 1). The patients presented with an inability to walk, but the history and physical exam were negative for joint inflammation or arthritis. His weight, height, and BMI were 15 kg (Z score: −5.11; <5th percentile), 121 cm (Z score: −1.25; 10th percentile), and 10.25 (< 5th percentile), respectively. He also had poor social interaction. Initially, he received serum fluid therapy, antibiotics, and epinephrine. Based on the critical condition and septic shock, the empirical broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotic therapy was initiated. Ceftriaxone (100 mg/kg/day) and Vancomycin (60 mg/kg/day) were started and continued for 14 days. Epinephrine drip was also initiated and titrated to 0.3 µg/kg/min for 48 h. The laboratory data upon admission are presented in Table 1. The blood culture was also positive for Staphylococcus Epidermis. Given the suspicion of nutritional dermatoses, immunodeficiency, and metabolic conditions, the immunoglobulin, amino acid, and metabolic panel tests were conducted.

After emerging from shock, vitamin level assessments were conducted with the suspicion of nutritional dermatosis. Plasma zinc level was initially insufficient (initially insufficient, then 60 µg/dL on the second day of the treatment), alkaline phosphatase (ALP) was normal, and high-dose elemental zinc sulfate treatment (3 mg/kg/day) was initiated with a suspicion of AE on the second day of hospitalization. Simultaneously, a high-calorie and high-protein diet was initiated to adjust vitamin and micronutrient intake through nutritional consultation. Considering the risk of refeeding syndrome, we initiated a lower protein and calorie diet (75 kcal/kg) and gradually increased it based on the clinical condition and tolerance over 10 days. After 14 days of completing the antibiotic therapy, improving per os (PO) tolerance with the high-calorie and high-protein diet, and significant improvement in lesions, the child was discharged with a prescription for Iron sulfate and zinc supplements at day 14. A skin biopsy from the acral lesions and a hair biopsy were sent during the hospitalization, which revealed the pathologic results of compact hyperkeratosis with acanthosis and mild spongiosis. In the dermis, infiltration of lymphocytes, admixed with some neutrophils, was noted as well. Notable finding in this case is the sub-corneal pustule containing numerous neutrophils, which can be seen in the pustular lesions of AE (Figs. 2 and 3). At the time of discharge, due to suspicion of metabolic and genetic disorders, amino acid tests and genetic tests were requested. Although the family history was negative, the hereditary autosomal dominant disease was considered and confirmed by genetic testing. Clinical whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed on a patient sample using the Twist Bioscience V2 kit and sequenced on a NextSeq500, with alignment to the GRCh37/hg19 genome. Variants were called using GATK and annotated with an in-house software, followed by filtering and prioritization based on public databases and predictive scores. Amino acid tests were normal, and the genetic test reported a frameshift deletion on the SLC39A4, which highly confirmed the diagnosis of AE (Supplementary Table 1). We identified a homozygous truncating variant in SLC39A4 (NM_130849.4:(9/12): c.1465_1474 + 4delCTGAGCCCAGGTGA) in the patient’s WES data.

After discharge, the patient was followed up at two weeks, two months, and four months later. The response to treatment was assessed based on the resolution of clinical symptoms. The serum zinc level was normal two months after treatment. During a four-month follow-up visit, all the skin lesions and erosions resolved, the child’s weight, height, and BMI also improved to 23 kg (Z score: −0.78; 20th percentile), 123 cm (Z score: −0.9; 18th percentile), and 15.2 (50th percentile), respectively, which represents a normal catch-up growth. His psychological condition had improved significantly. There was also evidence of the regrowth of eyebrows, scalp hair, and eyelashes. The patient tolerated the supplementation, and no complications were observed during follow-up assessments. The clinical course and recovery of the patient are evident in Fig. 4.

Discussion

In this case report, we described a case of AE in an 8-year-old boy who presented with severe skin manifestations and septic shock. Upon admission, the child’s weight and BMI were below the 5th percentile, and he had poor social interaction. Two months after treatment with a therapeutic zinc supplement (3 mg/kg/day), his skin lesions began to heal, his hair regrew, and during follow-ups, he showed significant improvement in both weight and social engagement.

Zinc is an essential element for proper cellular function, protein and carbohydrate metabolism, and vitamin A activity. It also acts as a cofactor for several metalloenzymes, including ALP, RNA polymerase, and alcohol dehydrogenase [8].

Cutaneous manifestations usually appear as symmetrical erythematous and eczematous plaques that may progress to bullae, vesicles, and pustules, typically around the mouth and genitalia. In severe cases, desquamation occurs [8]. Untreated lesions may spread to the face, trunk, and extremities. Diffuse alopecia, characterized by the loss of eyelashes, eyebrows, and scalp hair, is another common feature [9]. Diarrhea, another key symptom, may be intermittent or absent in some cases. When present, it is often associated with neuropsychological symptoms, growth retardation, anemia, and weight loss [10]. Other systemic manifestations include photophobia, delayed physical and mental development, delayed puberty, poor wound healing, and hypogonadism in severe cases [8]. Secondary bacterial or fungal infections may complicate the lesions, making diagnosis more challenging [10]. The clinical presentation of AE is highly variable, and the classic triad is seen in only a minority of cases. Overlapping symptoms with other metabolic and malabsorption disorders further complicate diagnosis and would require an astute index of suspicion [8]. Nutritional deficiencies, metabolic disorders, and immunodeficiency syndromes, chronic inflammatory bowel disease, hyper IgE syndrome, HIV, Netherton syndrome, and infectious and inflammatory dermatosis should be considered as differential diagnoses.

There is another case of a 6-month-old exclusively breastfed boy who presented with sharply demarcated erythematous plaques on the face, genitalia, and extremities since 4 months of age. His weight and height were below the third percentile. Lab tests revealed low levels of serum zinc, 24-hour urinary zinc, and ALP. Treatment with 3 mg/kg/day zinc led to rapid improvement within two weeks [11]. In another report, an 11-month-old boy had recurrent fever, intermittent diarrhea, infections, and erythematous symmetric lesions on the hands, feet, knees, and perioral area since 6 months old. His serum zinc was 27 µg/dL (normal: 50–120), while ALP was normal. Treatment with 2 mg/kg/day oral zinc resolved his symptoms quickly [12]. A 21-month-old boy born to consanguineous parents developed periorificial and acral lesions at 3 months, which were also reported. His brother had died at 14 months of age with similar symptoms. Serum zinc was 12 µg/dL. Treatment with 40 mg/day zinc sulfate resolved his symptoms [13]. There is another study that reported a 14-month-old premature boy (birth weight: 1800 g) who presented with skin lesions since 1 month of age. His sister had died at 5 months with similar lesions. Due to superinfection, the diagnosis was delayed. Lab results showed serum zinc of 23 µg/dL (normal: 65–118) and 24-hour urinary zinc of 11.4 mg (normal: 150–1200). Treatment with 3 mg/kg/day zinc led to complete resolution within two months [9].

We identified a homozygous truncating variant in the SLC39A4 gene. Mutations in this gene have been reported to be associated with AE, a rare autosomal recessive disorder. The variant is a frameshift variant that also affects the canonical splice donor site and the splice region, impacting intron 9 of the gene. This variant has been observed in the general population with a frequency of 0.0034% as reported in gnomAD, with no homozygous individuals identified. Due to the loss-of-function mutation, a complete or near-complete loss of ZIP4 activity is expected as a result of this mutation. The phenotypes affected by this mutation typically present as severe and classic forms of AE. Computational evidence supporting the predicted deleterious effect of the variant includes a combined annotation-dependent deletion (CADD) score of 25.9. The variant has previously been observed in a few patients with AE [14, 15].

Most AE cases manifest in infancy or days to weeks after weaning from breastfeeding. However, our patient remained undiagnosed until the age of 8. The delayed diagnosis of the disease and its manifestation with septic shock due to the secondary infection of skin lesions are among the rare aspects of this case. In this case, the manifestation of symptoms was delayed due to two reasons: first, breastfeeding until the age of two years. And second, the patient presented with cutaneous manifestations, which were misdiagnosed as dermatitis, and he was taking multivitamins and zinc supplementation until the age of seven years old. Although a correct diagnosis was not established initially, this intervention led to some improvement in the lesions; however, the ulcers persisted and failed to heal completely.

Due to the negative family history and the patient’s condition at the time of admission, this case presented significant diagnostic challenges. Lab data findings in our case included low serum zinc (initially insufficient, then 60 µg/dL on the second day of the treatment) and normal ALP. Genetic testing revealed a frameshift mutation in SLC39A4 (ZIP4 protein defect), which confirmed the diagnosis of AE. In previous care reports, genetic testing was either not performed or not reported. Limitations included no tests to rule out malabsorption disorders (celiac, Crohn’s) or HIV-related immunodeficiency.

Conclusion

Due to the variations in clinical presentations of AE and overlap with other metabolic conditions, it is recommended to consider the clinical diagnosis of AE in any patient presenting with mucocutaneous erosive lesions, especially those with a periorificial distribution and positive familial history. Timely intervention is advised to prevent severe complications, including infections and sepsis of cutaneous origin.
