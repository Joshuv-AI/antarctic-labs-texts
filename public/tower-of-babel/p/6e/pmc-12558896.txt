Rare Case of Long Retained Button Foreign Body in Esophagus Removed by Esophagotomy: A Case Report From Muhimbili National Hospital, Tanzania
Authors: Rajabu Athumani Bakari, Georvin Marco Haule, Alfred Chibwae, Charles Oswald Ruboha, Diana Batuel Ngulo, Gerald Mpemba, Hilda Makungu, Abdulrahman Abdallah Amin, Ally Hamisi Mwanga, Zaitun M. Bokhary, Kitembo Salum Kibwana, Charles Komba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChallenges in the management of dysphagia from a patient with a long‐retained esophageal foreign body, unveiling the diagnostic and treatment challenges. Reported a rare case of an esophageal foreign body from a button presenting with long‐standing dysphagia; the button was retrieved by thoracotomy with esophagotomy. This is the rare case of an esophageal foreign body managed by surgery.

Introduction

Esophagotomy is a surgical option in esophageal foreign body retrieval and is rarely done as it accounts for < 1% of cases [1, 2]. However, 80%–90% of ingested foreign bodies that pass beyond the upper esophagus typically move through the gastrointestinal tract without incident; 10%–20% require medical or surgical intervention [3, 4]. Surgical management is necessary in approximately 1% of cases. In rare instances, an esophagotomy may be required to extract an impacted esophageal foreign body, particularly if there is evidence of life‐threatening complications or a high likelihood of such complications due to the object's size, shape, or location [5, 6].

Case History and Examination

An 8‐year‐old male, came with chief complaint of difficulty in swallowing for 1 year as reported by his grandmother, It was gradual and progressive in nature as initially it was difficult to swallow solids then semi‐solids like porridge; currently he could swallow liquids. It was associated with painful swallowing and regurgitation but no history of choking. This symptom had no specific periodicity and was not relieved or aggravated by cold or hot food. He has significant weight loss but no B‐symptoms. No history of foreign body ingestion, instrumentation, corrosive ingestion or chest surgery performed. He attended several health facilities where he was diagnosed with adenotonsillitis, with the symptoms reported to be relieved for a while after surgical treatment (tonsillectomy) but they recurred necessitating another admission.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

On examination, he had the only positive finding of reduced breath sounds on the left side. Investigations done were OGD which showed a foreign body (thought to be a coin) covered with food content and partially buried within mucosa making a fistula; an air bubble was seen at 15 m from the upper incisor and could not progress further, The conclusion was a foreign body with an esophagotracheal fistula.

CT scan of the chest was done which showed a round metallic FB suggestive of a coin that lodged in the thoracic esophagus at the level of the aortic arch, The coin measured about 21 × 19 × 2 mm with inflammation and swelling of the esophagus wall around it, The esophagus is dilated upstream of the coin and the coin is exerting pressure on the trachea pushing it slightly to the right and causing narrowing of the trachea lumen, although there was no evidence of tracheoesophageal fistula Figure 1.

Contrasted chest CT scan showing a foreign body (button in green arrows) in the esophagus.

Intra operatively, there was extensive esophageal and periesophageal fibrosis approximately 5–6 cm long, with no fistula or air leakage noted Figure 2. Azygous vein was divided then the esophagus was mobilized, An esophagotomy was done involving the fibrotic part, and a button measured 4 cm in diameter was extracted, An NGT was inserted by direct visualization, irrigation was done then tube thoracostomy was performed, and the incision site was closed in layers after the correct swab and instruments count. Post operatively the patient improved as suggested by the CT scan (Figure 3) and on examination (Figure 4).

Conclusion and Results

Esophageal foreign body ingestion if not managed promptly may increase morbidity and mortality for the patient; early diagnosis and management are crucial, Management varies from conservative, to medical, and surgical approaches may be the last option in indicated cases, unveiling the multidisciplinary approach in management.

Discussion

Surgical management in esophageal foreign body is rarely performed; different types of surgery can be employed viably from esophagotomy and sometimes esophagectomy can be done, The level of obstruction is always the corner stone in the determination of the approaches [7].

Foreign body ingestion is particularly prevalent in the upper gastrointestinal tract, accounting for 75%–85% of such cases in pediatric patients [8, 9, 10]. Commonly ingested items include food items such as bones, meat, fruits (especially pits), and vegetables, as well as non‐food items like coins, batteries, plastic toys, and dental devices [11].

Children are at a higher risk for foreign body ingestion especially those under the age of 3–6 years due to their exploratory behavior. In some cases, ingestion may be intentional, occurring during suicide attempts or occupational accidents.

Once ingested, foreign bodies may become lodged at anatomical narrowing such as the cricopharyngeal muscle, esophageal strictures, or diverticula [12, 13]. Their presence often triggers an inflammatory response, leading to mucosal edema, rupture, or necrosis [12, 14]. If the obstruction is prolonged, it can result in esophageal perforation and mediastinitis—conditions that may be life‐threatening if not promptly managed.

Dysphagia is a common presentation in cases of foreign body ingestion. While it is often an acute emergency and may also be a late presentation, presentations vary depending on the size, shape, and location of the object [12, 15]. It may be associated with odynophagia, drooling, inability to manage secretions, chest discomfort, regurgitation, and signs of aspiration such as coughing, wheezing, or shortness of breath. In some cases, foreign bodies may remain undetected for extended periods, particularly if the symptoms are mild or misinterpreted [16]. In our case, the patient was initially diagnosed with adenotonsilitis and underwent a tonsillectomy before the actual cause was identified.

Diagnosis of foreign body ingestion requires a comprehensive approach with history taking and physical examination being critical for clinical suspicion. Radiographic imaging, particularly plain X‐rays, is typically the first diagnostic step, although it is limited to detecting radiopaque objects [17]. In cases involving radiolucent materials, computed tomography (CT) or ultrasound may be necessary [15, 18]. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) remains the primary diagnostic and therapeutic tool, allowing for direct visualization and possible retrieval of the object [19]. Urgent endoscopy is indicated within 6 h in cases involving sharp objects, complete esophageal obstruction, or battery ingestion [6, 20]. In other scenarios, endoscopy should be performed within 24 h, with flexible endoscopy being the preferred modality [21, 22].

When treating foreign bodies in the esophagus, timing is crucial because waiting longer than 24 h increases the risk of complications [23, 24]. When endoscopy is not an option, the Foley catheter approach may be useful in certain situations to remove foreign bodies, but it must be used carefully [25]. For children, rigid endoscopy under anesthesia offers the finest airway control, and it is advised to be performed within 24 h [26]. Conservative care is safe if the youngster is asymptomatic and past the esophagus. For symptomatic patients, it is advised to perform imaging, maintain NPO, administer IV fluids, and consult an ENT or GI surgeon. Repeat X‐rays after 2 weeks are advised if there are no symptoms, and endoscopy if symptoms remain [20, 27, 28]. In contrast to asymptomatic items that may be passed into the stomach with stool monitoring and follow‐up, symptomatic esophageal objects require immediate endoscopy. For foreign bodies that can't be removed by endoscopy, then surgery is indicated for removal of the foreign bodies in the esophagus [20, 29]. Esophagotomy is the standard approach in the management of esophageal foreign bodies, though evaluation of the patient is essential before surgery as reports suggest that 10%–20% of esophageal foreign bodies require endoscopic removal and < 1% progress to surgical intervention such as esophagotomy. The surgical approach depends on the location of the foreign body as a left lateral neck incision is used for the cervical esophagus, a right thoracotomy at the fourth intercostal space for the upper thoracic esophagus, and a left thoracotomy at the seventh or eighth intercostal space for the lower thoracic esophagus [30].

Common indications for esophagotomy include failed endoscopic retrieval, the presence of complex or sharp objects, and foreign bodies located in high‐risk areas such as near the aortic arch or an aberrant right subclavian artery [31, 32]. Continued endoscopic attempts in such situations may increase the risk of perforation. Long‐retained foreign bodies associated with significant fibrosis or necrosis also warrant surgical management. In addition to conventional methods, advanced techniques such as balloon‐assisted endoscopy, Holmium laser fragmentation, and combined endoscopic‐surgical approaches have emerged to enhance retrieval success.

Esophagotomy performed in the context of foreign body ingestion is associated with several potential complications including surgical site infections, anastomotic leaks, empyema thoracis, stricture formation due to fibrosis, and esophageal retained material [32]. Other risks include hemorrhage and respiratory complications such as atelectasis or pneumonia. Timely identification and management of these complications are essential to improve clinical outcomes and ensure optimal recovery.
