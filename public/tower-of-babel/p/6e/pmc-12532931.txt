Prevalence and risk factors of intimate partner violence among pregnant women: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jiandi Ma, Shuai zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntimate partner violence (IPV) has adverse physical and psychological effects on pregnant women. With regard to the influencing factors of IPV among pregnant women, discrepancies still exist in clinical studies. By identifying these factors, more targeted and effective interventions can be developed for this population. Consequently, this review aims to examine the impact of IPV on pregnant women and provide an empirical basis for the development of preventive strategies in clinical practice.MethodBibliographic databases like PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Science Direct, Cochrane Library, CBM, VIP, CNKI, and Wan Fang Data were employed in the investigation for retrieving articles. A thorough evaluation of all study designs was conducted to understand the impact of IPV on pregnant women, focusing on articles published before May 2025. In total, 36,214 pregnant women were included in the studies reviewed.ResultsA total of 33,355 articles were retrieved, and 37 articles were systematically screened to evaluate the influencing factors of IPV among pregnant women. The meta-analysis revealed that the prevalence of IPV in pregnant women was 34%[95%CI (27%-41%)]. Sixteen significant influencing factors were identified: Age of pregnant women<25, Partner’s smoking habit, Unemployed, Low level of education, Undesired pregnancy, Partner’s Khat chewing habit, Partner’s alcohol drinking habit, household income, Experience of violence before the current pregnancy, Parental exposure to IPV, Living alone, Rural dwellers, Income loss due to COVID-19, Household decisions made by husband alone, partner controlling behaviors, and partner has more than one wife.ConclusionIPV has detrimental effects on the physical and mental well-being of pregnant women. Consequently, prompt implementation of prevention and intervention strategies is essential to decrease the prevalence of IPV and enhance the quality of life for pregnant women. Numerous factors contribute to IPV during pregnancy. Healthcare providers should systematically identify these high-risk factors and offer targeted preventive guidance to effectively reduce its incidence at its root cause.Trial registrationCRD420251046017.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08209-4.

Background

The World Health Organization identifies intimate partner violence (IPV) as a widespread issue affecting women, including physical, sexual, and emotional abuse, as well as controlling behaviors by partners [1]. Globally, IPV during pregnancy is estimated to affect 6.5% to 15.9% of women, though actual rates may be higher due to underreporting [1]. In the U.S., 22% to 39% of pregnant women experience IPV [2], while a study in Hunan Province, China, found a 22.2% prevalence [3]. Infertile women in low-income countries face a 36.0% IPV rate over 12 months [4]. These statistics highlight the urgent need for global public health interventions to address IPV during pregnancy.

IPV during pregnancy is linked to severe health issues [5], including psychological problems like depression [6], suicidal thoughts [7], and PTSD [8]. It also negatively impacts fetal and neonatal health, with abdominal trauma posing a significant risk to the fetus. Women experiencing partner violence face higher neonatal and postnatal mortality rates [9]. Studies have explored factors affecting IPV in pregnant women, such as racial differences [6] and unintended pregnancies [10], with prevalence varying from 2.1% [11] to 93.1% [12]across regions. The prevalence of IPV among pregnant women varies by region due to differences in screening methods and regional focus on IPV. Variations in measurement tools across studies make direct incidence comparisons difficult, and some studies focus only on physical violence, ignoring psychological and sexual violence [13].

Existing reviews have explored factors influencing IPV among pregnant women in Ethiopia [14] and its link to trauma care during pregnancy [15]. However, there is limited global data on IPV incidence and factors among pregnant women. Our study advances the field by using a multi-dimensional framework that includes individual, partner/household, and macro-level factors. We conducted a meta-analysis to determine the global prevalence and subtype distribution of IPV during pregnancy and identified significant risk factors to support targeted prevention and intervention strategies.

Objective

To thoroughly assess the occurrence and contributing factors of IPV among pregnant women.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

This systematic review and meta-analysis were carried out in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [16]. This study is registered with PROSPERO, CRD420251046017. The criteria for eligible participants were outlined as follows: pregnant women who have experienced intimate partner violence, which encompasses physical, psychological, emotional, or sexual violence. Participants were not affected by factors that increase the risk of high-risk pregnancies, such as being older, having hypertension, diabetes, or other related health issues. The primary subject of investigation was the occurrence of IPV in pregnant women and the related causes. Excluded were studies in languages other than English and Chinese, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and articles where the full-text was not available.

Titles and abstracts identified through both electronic and manual searches were organized using EndNote 20 reference management software. The reference lists of all relevant articles were thoroughly reviewed to identify any additional studies. Scholarly literature in both Chinese and English, concerning factors influencing IPV among pregnant women, was sourced from databases including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Science Direct, Cochrane Library, China Knowledge Network (CNKI), Vipers (VIP), Wan Fang, and Sinomed (CBM). The search period extended from the inception of each database to May 2025. The search terms utilized were: “Pregnancy/mother*/maternal/Gestation” AND “Intimate partner violence/Domestic violence/Gender-Based Violence/Physical abuse/Exposure to violence/Sex Offenses/Emotional Abuse/Aggression” AND “risk factors/predict*/screen*/probability*”. For a detailed search algorithm, please refer to Table 1. Ethical approval was not required for this study because no human subjects were directly involved.

Search strategy

Study selection and quality assessment

In this study, one author was tasked with conducting the literature search and eliminating duplicate entries. Subsequently, two other authors independently evaluated the titles and abstracts according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. If at least one reviewer deemed an abstract to satisfy the inclusion criteria, the full-text article was retrieved for further analysis [17]. The two reviewers then independently assessed each full-text article to determine its eligibility for inclusion in the final analysis. In instances where consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted for resolution. The full-text articles were reviewed by two independent evaluators based on the following inclusion criteria: (a) Study population: Pregnant women with clearly defined diagnostic criteria for intimate partner violence (IPV), formally diagnosed with IPV. IPV was characterized as the experience of one or more acts of physical, sexual, and/or emotional violence by an intimate partner during pregnancy; (b) Study focus: Factors influencing IPV among pregnant women, with the original manuscript providing odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI); (c) Study design: Cross-sectional studies, cohort studies, and case-control studies; and (d) Language of publication: Chinese or English. Any discrepancies encountered during the screening of titles, abstracts, and full texts were resolved through consultative discussions with a third reviewer until consensus was reached.

The assessment of scholarly quality was independently performed by two researchers, with the results subsequently cross-verified. In cases of disagreement, a third researcher was consulted to mediate. Cross-sectional studies were evaluated using the criteria established by the American Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) [18]. This evaluative framework comprises 11 items, typically employing a three-tier scoring system of “Yes/No/Uncertain” or “Satisfied/Partially Satisfied/Not Satisfied.” Total scores ranging from 8 to 11 indicate high quality, 4 to 7 indicate moderate quality, and 0 to 3 indicate low quality. Cohort and case-control studies were assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [19], which is divided into three major categories, each corresponding to different items. The scale includes four items for selection, two items for comparability, and three items for outcome/exposure, totaling nine items. Scores of 7–9 denote high quality, 5–6 indicate moderate quality, and 0–4 signify low quality.

Data extraction and outcome of interest

Data from each study in the review were collected according to criteria defined beforehand, using the standardized JBI data extraction instrument [20]. Data extraction was performed by two authors, and the results were compared; any discrepancies were settled through consensus. If consensus was not possible, a third researcher was called upon to assist in the decision-making. By reviewing the entire text, we extracted the following information: the author and publication year, the country where the study was conducted, the research methodology (including cross-sectional, cohort, and case-control studies), the type of IPV, the sample size, the prevalence of IPV among pregnant women, and its associated influencing factors.

Data analysis

Data were systematically collated and recorded in Excel spreadsheets, and proactive efforts were made to contact the original authors for supplementation of missing information. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.1). Composite effect sizes and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant in the z-test results. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 and χ2 tests; significant heterogeneity was indicated when I2 exceeded 50% and the P value was less than 0.05, prompting the use of a random-effects model for subsequent analyses. Conversely, a fixed-effects model was applied. Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding individual studies or modifying the analytical framework to evaluate the robustness of the findings. Furthermore, publication bias was evaluated using the Egger test.

Results

Review process

Initially, 33,355 manuscripts were obtained, and 20,559 duplicates were eliminated. In the first screening phase, which involved reviewing titles and abstracts, 286 manuscripts were chosen. After an extensive examination of the full texts, 37 manuscripts were ultimately included (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

Among the 37 manuscripts analyzed in this research, 2 were in Chinese and 35 were in English. The studies included 1 case-control, 1 cohort, and 35 cross-sectional studies. A total of 36,214 pregnant women were part of the study, with 8,637 experiencing IPV. For the 35 cross-sectional studies, the AHRQ score was utilized, with 9 manuscripts receiving a high-quality rating and 26 a moderate-quality rating. The other 2 manuscripts used the NOS score and were both rated as high quality (See Supplementary Table 1).

Weighted mean difference of IPV among pregnant women

The 37 retained manuscripts underwent a heterogeneity test, which indicated substantial heterogeneity among the studies (I2 = 99.8%, P < 0.001), prompting the application of a random-effects model for analysis. The meta-analysis indicated that the rate of IPV among pregnant women was 34%, with a 95% confidence interval between 27% and 41%(Fig. 2). The analysis further demonstrates that the rates of Physical IPV, Sexual IPV, and emotional or psychological IPV among pregnant women are 13% [95% CI (9% − 16%)](Fig. 3), 14% [95% CI (9% − 18%)](Fig. 4), and 26% [95% CI (18% − 33%)](Fig. 5), respectively.

Assessment of publication bias of IPV among pregnant women

The analysis of IPV incidence among pregnant women showed some publication bias, as suggested by the funnel plot and Egger’s test [z = 1.655, P = 0.098 (95% CI: 0.018, 0.379)] (Fig. 7). The funnel plot demonstrates slight asymmetry, with small-sample studies (n < 200) clustering toward higher prevalence estimates. Visual inspection suggests potential publication bias, although the majority of studies fall within the pseudo 95% confidence limits. Egger’s test (z = 1.655, P = 0.098) indicates borderline statistical significance.

Subgroup (Region) analysis for IPV among pregnant women

The study used Africa as a benchmark (OR = 0.35, 95%CI 0.27–0.46, p < 0.001) and analyzed 37 observational studies (Table 2; Fig. 8). Subgroup analysis revealed that the effect size in Europe was significantly lower than in Africa (OR = 0.22, 95%CI 0.11–0.45, p < 0.001), showing that the association strength in Europe was only 22% of that in Africa. No significant effect size differences were found between the Americas (p = 0.401), Asia (p = 0.251), and Africa. Regional factors accounted for 28.12% of the heterogeneity (QM = 16.55, p = 0.0009), but residual heterogeneity was still very high (I²=99.58%).

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias of factors influencing IPV among pregnant women

For the contributory variables discussed above, a sensitivity analysis was performed, and the combined effect magnitudes were independently evaluated by adjusting the two effect models. Discussion

In a comprehensive and systematic manner, the current meta-analysis thoroughly explored the relationship between multiple predisposing factors and IPV in pregnant women. We aimed to provide comprehensive empirical evidence on predictive variables associated with IPV onset in pregnant women. The findings from the meta-analysis indicated that the prevalence of IPV among pregnant women was 34% [95% CI (27%−41%)], aligning with the results of prior studies [25]. Further analysis revealed that the incidence rates of physical IPV, sexual IPV, and emotional or psychological IPV among pregnant women were 13% [95% CI (9%−16%)], 14% [95% CI (9%−18%)], and 26% [95% CI (18%−33%)], respectively. The substantial heterogeneity observed in this study (I² = 99.8%) can be ascribed to several factors. Firstly, there are variations in the definitions of intimate partner violence (IPV), encompassing physical, psychological, and sexual dimensions. Furthermore, diverse screening instruments were employed, including the WHO questionnaire and researcher-designed tools. Regional disparities may also contribute to this heterogeneity, as evidenced by the differing prevalence rates, with Asia reporting a prevalence of 24% and Africa 41%.

Intimate partner violence during pregnancy is a significant public health issue that can have severe consequences for both the mother and the unborn child. Various studies have identified several risk factors associated with IPV among pregnant women, with age being a notable determinant. Specifically, younger pregnant women, particularly those under the age of 25, Low level of education, and unemployed are often at a higher risk of experiencing IPV. The reason for this is that young pregnant women may not have completed their education or be fully employed, economically dependent on their partners, increasing the likelihood of enduring violence [53]. Young pregnant women may not have established stable social networks, making them more vulnerable to becoming targets of violence when isolated [54]. Besides, there may be more significant age or maturity gaps between young partners, leading to unequal decision-making power [55]. Young pregnant women may lack experience in dealing with relationship conflicts, exacerbating the risk of violence [56]. In some regions, early marriage customs are common, and young brides have limited autonomy in marriage, making them more prone to violence. Societal stereotypes of young women (such as being submissive and patient) may condone violent behavior [57].

The study found that a partner’s smoking, khat chewing, alcohol consumption, sole decision-making, controlling behavior, and polygamy are linked to IPV against pregnant women. Smoking, alcohol, and khat use often reflect a lack of self-discipline and correlate with violence [2]. Smokers may dominate relationships through control, increasing violence risk. Khat’s stimulants can lead to irritability and aggression, while addictive behaviors strain finances and heighten conflict [58]. Alcohol abuse lowers inhibitions, leading to violence, and often causes financial strain and neglect of family duties, worsening household conflicts [59]. Husbands’ control over decision-making highlights gender inequality, limiting women’s autonomy and increasing violence risk. Control is exerted through resource restriction and social isolation, often leading to physical violence. Dominance in relationships is maintained through surveillance and social restrictions [60]. Polygynous relationships contribute to IPV among pregnant women due to conflicts over unequal resource distribution [61]. Counseling for smoking, alcohol, and caterweed addiction should combine behavioral therapy with pharmacological treatments like nicotine replacement and alcohol dependence medications [62]. Additionally, workshops for couples now teach nonviolent communication and emphasize shared decision-making. Laws also ban authoritarian control in families and outline legal consequences for violating partners’ rights.

Key factors linked to IPV during pregnancy include unintended pregnancy, pre-pregnancy violence exposure, parental IPV exposure, living alone, rural residency, and income loss from the COVID-19 pandemic. Unintended pregnancies can spark partner conflicts over responsibilities and finances, raising violence risks. Partners may use violence to influence pregnancy outcomes [63]. Rural women often depend on partners’ income due to limited job opportunities. The pandemic’s economic impact has reduced women’s financial independence, increasing their vulnerability to violence [64]. Experiencing or witnessing parental violence in childhood can lead to adopting violent behaviors and reduced resistance to violence in adulthood [65]. Prolonged exposure to violence can lower self-esteem and lead to learned helplessness. Violence may force women to live alone, such as escaping abuse, and lacking support can make pregnant women more vulnerable to partner control [66]. To address this, economic empowerment through cash assistance or job training for low-income pregnant women can reduce financial dependence. Promoting gender equality education is vital to breaking the cycle of violence. Strengthening social support with anonymous helplines and shelters, especially for isolated women, is crucial. Even after the COVID-19 pandemic, remote psychological counseling remains important for supporting pregnant women during health crises.

Conclusion

Intimate Partner Violence has detrimental effects on the physical and mental well-being of pregnant women. Consequently, prompt implementation of prevention and intervention strategies is essential to decrease the prevalence of IPV and enhance the quality of life for pregnant women. Numerous factors contribute to IPV during pregnancy. Healthcare providers should systematically identify these high-risk factors and offer targeted preventive guidance to effectively reduce its incidence at its root cause.

Limitation of this review

Firstly, the study excluded literatures with fewer than two studies on the same influencing factor or those whose data were unsuitable for comprehensive analysis, which might have introduced selection bias. Second, significant heterogeneity was observed in the meta-analysis of IPV incidence among pregnant women. This heterogeneity stemmed from variations in IPV assessment methods, publication years of the included studies, and geographical contexts, thereby impacting the generalizability of the findings. Future research could further explore the three subtypes of IPV incidence and their associated influencing factors. Lastly, due to the limited sample size, some influencing factors were underrepresented in the literature, and publication bias could not be adequately assessed. Future studies could validate these preliminary results through large-scale, multicenter prospective cohort studies.
