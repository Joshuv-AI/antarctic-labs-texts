ABSTRACT
AbstractGenomic sequencing (GS) for patients and families with rare disease creates the opportunity for precise diagnosis as well as the option to learn about medically actionable secondary findings (SF). Debate persists internationally on how to manage the analysis and disclosure of SF, especially in settings where service delivery models for GS are still in development and outcomes are preliminary. This study aimed to understand parents' decision to opt‐out of learning about their child's SF for adult‐onset conditions and whether an SF identified in a child was inherited. All children received GS as part of their diagnostic work‐up for a rare disease. Sixteen semistructured interviews were conducted with parents. Guided by a qualitative approach, interviews were analyzed with inductive content analysis. Participants reported that the timing of decision‐making, competing priorities, anticipated adverse psychological outcomes, and health‐related beliefs and worldviews contributed to their decision to opt‐out of receiving SF. An emphasis was placed on a preference for flexible SF delivery models to aid in reducing the burden of SF decision‐making at the time of indication‐based testing. Genetic service delivery models that separate the timing of decisions about indication‐based test results and SF may address patient preferences. Findings from this work may inform clinical practice and policy in jurisdictions where SF delivery models are still being developed and optimized.

INTRODUCTION

Genomic sequencing (GS), inclusive of exome and genome sequencing, is improving diagnostic yield for patients and families with rare disease (100,000 Genomes Project Pilot Investigators, et al., 2021). In addition to identifying disease‐causing variants (i.e., primary findings), GS can identify secondary and incidental findings. While secondary and incidental findings are both unrelated to the indication for testing and generally related to medically actionable disease risks, secondary findings (SF) stem from a recommended gene list and incidental findings are identified unexpectedly through the process of genome analysis (Bowling et al., 2022; Green et al., 2013). As the list of genes in which SF are sought continues to expand and laboratories identify a growing number of incidental findings, opportunities and challenges related to preventive care also expand. Herein, we refer to secondary and incidental findings as SF.

Importantly, approaches to SF vary across sequencing programs and jurisdictions. The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) currently recommends that SF in 84 genes (ACMG v. 3.3) be returned to families, given the opportunity this knowledge presents for preventive care (Lee et al., 2025). In contrast, other professional societies such as the Canadian College of Medical Genetics (CCMG) and the European Society of Human Genetics (ESHG) recommend a more cautious approach to reporting SF and call for additional evidence related to the benefits, harms, and cost consequence of SF before the offer and return of SF become routinized (Boycott et al., 2015; de Wert et al., 2021). More cautious approaches to SF are fueled by the desire for a better understanding of the benefits and risks of reporting SF, especially with respect to reporting adult‐onset SF in children. Moreover, individuals with a SF reported in the context of opportunistic screening may be less likely to have a recognized family history or develop the associated disease, compared to those for whom genomic variants are identified through diagnostic testing (Brothers et al., 2019; Houdayer et al., 2019). Importantly, positive predictive values for index cases and their family members have often been reported to be much lower for opportunistic screening than for diagnostic testing (Black & Welch, 1997).

Mindful of the international debate on SF choice structures and reporting best practices (Majeed et al., 2024), our provincial GS program began to track SF uptake and decline rates at the time that local GS became clinically available (Hayeems et al., 2023). To date, the local decline rate for optional SF is 10% (Hayeems et al., 2023). While a limited body of research from Australia, the United States, and the United Kingdom has identified health worry, feeling overwhelmed, misalignment with religious beliefs, and privacy concerns as core reasons for declining SF (Clift et al., 2015; Martyn et al., 2025; Nolan et al., 2024; Rego et al., 2022; Schupmann et al., 2021), less is known in pediatric settings and in jurisdictions like Canada where SF policy and models of care to support SF reporting are still evolving (Hayeems et al., 2022; Mackley, Weisz, et al., 2025; Mackley, Shickh, et al., 2025). In pediatric settings, there is ongoing debate surrounding the disclosure of SF associated with adult‐onset conditions. Parents have previously reported that learning about SF associated with adult‐onset conditions for their child may help them to be proactive about surveillance and early intervention (Goehringer et al., 2025). However, anxiety about disease risk and upholding the child's autonomy have been reported as concerns (Pereira et al., 2023). As described by Martyn et al. (2025), some parents are not interested in learning about SF for their child because they are concerned about additional worry that SF results may cause and prefer to address health problems as they arise. Furthermore, parents have expressed concerns that learning about SF for their child may be detrimental to the parent–child relationship due to fears about the possible onset of disease in their child (Houdayer et al., 2019). The age of the child undergoing GS can also influence the desire to learn about SF, as parents of younger children may be less likely to consent to SF (Downie et al., 2020). In a previous study where families learned about SF, parents explained that their child's capacity to understand GS and SF will guide how and when they disclose their SF results to their child (Miner et al., 2020). Caution with respect to learning about SF related to adult‐onset conditions in children also aligns with long‐standing policy that advises against carrier testing in children (Moore & Richer, 2022; Ross et al., 2013). Given the absence of consensus on the optimal approach to and impact of reporting SF associated with adult‐onset conditions in pediatrics, the objective of this study was to explore the decisional factors that impacted SF decision‐making among those who declined to receive optional SF.

METHODS

The protocol for this qualitative description (Sandelowski, 2000) was reviewed and approved by Clinical Trials Ontario, a provincial research ethics board (CTO #3655). Informed consent was obtained from all study participants and study methods aligned with the COREQ reporting guidelines, where applicable (Tong et al., 2007). AI was not used in this research study or in the preparation of this manuscript.

Setting

The Ontario Ministry of Health services Ontario's population of approximately 15 million people, and since 2014 has facilitated clinical ES for Ontarians through the Out‐of‐Country/Out‐of‐Province Prior Approval Program (Hayeems et al., 2025). In response to the growing demand for clinical ES, in 2020 Ontario Health conducted a comprehensive health technology assessment of GS for unexplained developmental disabilities and multiple congenital anomalies (Hayeems et al., 2025). Based on this assessment and the recommendation for public funding for ES for these clinical indications, in 2021 Genome‐wide Sequencing Ontario (GSO) (https://www.gsontario.ca/) was launched in Ontario, Canada. GSO services all clinical genetics centers in Ontario and represents the first clinical‐grade GS service for probands and biological parents for rare disease diagnosis in Canada (Hayeems et al., 2022; Hayeems et al., 2025). At the time of enrollment into GSO, the possibility of identifying and disclosing SF is offered to patients and families. It is explained that for patients ≤ 18 years of age, SF associated with conditions that are actionable in childhood will be disclosed automatically to the patient/their parents. Parents of pediatric patients can choose whether their child's report will include SF associated with conditions that are actionable in adulthood. Parents of these pediatric patients are also given a choice to learn about the presence or absence of their child's SF in themselves, as having the SF could have health implications for them. Patients >18 with capacity can choose for themselves. The option to identify and report SF after the primary GS result is reported by the laboratory is not provided to families. While the CCMG position statement advocates for a restrictive approach to reporting SF, GSO's approach to SF aligned with ACMG guidance on SF since GS for Ontarians was performed in US clinical laboratories until 2021 and the Ontario clinical geneticists, when consulted, voiced a preference for retaining this model of care.

Sample and recruitment

At the time data were collected, probands were eligible for GS through GSO when a baseline genetics evaluation had been completed and was noninformative (chromosomal microarray and/or targeted genetic testing) and a genetic etiology was the most likely explanation for the phenotype (e.g., moderate to severe developmental or functional impairment, multisystem involvement) (Boycott et al., 2015). Two types of individuals were eligible to participate: (i) adult probands of any age who declined to receive SF, and (ii) parents of pediatric probands who declined to receive optional SF for their child or SF inheritance for themselves. Through email or telephone‐based invitations following the disclosure of GS results, eligible individuals were notified of their eligibility and informed that the study aimed to learn about the factors that influenced their decision to decline receiving SF for themselves or their child. They were invited to participate between June 2023 and October 2024, until data saturation was reached (Saunders et al., 2017). Interpreter services were used to invite eligible non‐English speaking individuals over the phone.

Data collection

Semistructured qualitative interviews were conducted virtually with individuals who expressed interest in participating in the study. The interview guide was informed by conducting a search for relevant articles using key words that included but were not limited to: SF, pediatrics, adult‐onset conditions, patient/parent preferences (Anderson et al., 2017; Houdayer et al., 2019; Rego et al., 2022) and developed by the study team (supporting information). Prior to participation in the interview, there was no relationship between the participants and the interviewer, and the participants knew that the researcher was conducting this interview to better understand the decision to opt‐out of learning about medically actionable SF to inform SF practice and policy. The interview explored participants' understanding of GS and SF, as well as factors informing the decision to opt‐out, such as perceived downsides and benefits, cultural or religious beliefs, and privacy/insurance concerns. The first two interviews were conducted by two study team members (SL and AH), one of whom was an experienced qualitative interviewer. Following training, the remaining 14 interviews were conducted by AH. As a female clinical research project assistant with a bachelor's degree and professional training in qualitative methods, AH recognizes that her position as an interviewer without lived experience of parenting a child with a rare disease or the experience of making a decision about receiving SF may have shaped both the questions she asked and the interpretations she drew from participants' accounts. To mitigate this influence, she engaged in ongoing reflexive practice throughout data collection and analysis, including memo writing and consultation with the research team to ensure that participants' perspectives remained central. Demographic information was collected for all study participants during the session. Only the participant and interviewer(s) were present during the interviews. All interviews were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim by a professional transcription service. Field notes were taken when required. Interviews ranged from 20 to 50 min in duration. Transcripts were not returned to participants.

Data analysis

Using content analysis (Sandelowski, 2000; Hsieh & Shannon, 2005), transcripts were coded inductively with a lens toward understanding participants' reasoning for declining optional SF. A codebook was developed by members of the study team (AH, TC, SL, RH) and refined through the process of double coding a subset of transcripts (AH and TC) and discussions with the team to resolve coding discrepancies. The codebook, including a coding tree to characterize categories and subcategories, was applied to the full dataset using Dedoose software (Dedoose Version 9.0.107, 2023). Related codes were grouped into salient categories. Coded data were labeled according to whether the parent opted out of learning about adult‐onset SF for their child and/or whether they opted out of learning about the inheritance of an SF identified in their child. Throughout the analytic process, a larger group of study team members met regularly to discuss and refine generated categories (RH, SL, RBH, OM, AH, TC). Participants did not provide feedback on findings.

RESULTS

One hundred and twenty‐nine eligible individuals were invited to participate in interviews: 16 interviews were completed with no repeat interviews, 35 individuals actively declined, and 78 did not respond. Of the seven eligible adult probands invited, none agreed to participate in an interview. All participants lived in Ontario, spoke English, and were parents of children who received GS. Interviewees ranged from 18 to 69 years old, and the majority (62%) identified as White (Table 1). Among participants, seven (44%) completed an undergraduate, graduate, or professional degree, 10 (62%) worked full‐time for pay, and 15 were mothers (94%). Three key thematic categories regarding the decision to opt‐out of receiving medically actionable SF were identified. First, the timing of the option to identify SF impacted decision‐making, with participants reporting that learning about SF was not a priority at the time of offer and asserting a preference for more flexibility in SF decision‐making. Second, participants shared that they opted out of learning about SF due to concerns about unwanted worry and disruption. Finally, participants declined SF as the receipt of this information was not aligned with their values, beliefs, or worldviews.

Timing matters

Not a priority

In the context of an acute care admission or an outpatient visit where parents were searching for an answer to their child's primary health concerns, parents conveyed that they did not want more to think about and could not process another decision. They were focused on managing their child's current health concerns and preferred not to think about what might be relevant in the future. As such, SF were not considered to be a priority at this highly sensitive time. For example, this parent, whose child was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit at the time of the decision to opt‐out of learning about SF, explains: So, it was kind of a life and death situation for us, we didn't know, would he recover fully, would he get off dialysis, so thinking about a future for him, like what would happen 10 years later or after he's 18, had us getting more worried.” Later in the interview, this participant says, “maybe this is not the time we can handle it, we need to focus on [child] right now, and not on what the future holds. Preference for flexibility

Participants provided some reflections on the current GSO approach to SF, suggesting that families: (i) be notified of decisions related to SF in advance of their genetics consultation to provide time for them to think about it, and/or (ii) be provided with the option to make decisions about SF at a later date, when acute stressors such as hospitalization or medical complications from a primary diagnosis have subsided. A participant who did not know they would have to decide if they wanted to learn about SF at their appointment shared: I would have liked to have known ahead of time, in an email, before you show up for your genetic testing, here's what's going to happen, and you will be asked this question…, it just gives you a little bit more time to think about it, maybe consult with other people… so that you're kind of doing that thinking, and if you had any questions before you made that decision, you'd have them ready instead of just trying to process, oh my god, do I want to know this? Like this is a big life decision. So, it would've been nice just to have a heads‐up… (CH001)

Not aligned with values, beliefs, or worldview

For some, the decision to decline SF was tied to cultural norms or religious beliefs. Participants explained, for example, that in their culture, medical illness or risk of illness generates fear and it is therefore common to avoid receiving medical information. Others shared how religious or spiritual beliefs impacted their decision to decline SF: Another factor is that my culture…we do not really go a lot to a doctor, you know…a simple flu or a cold, we usually just handle it at home because culturally, we are also afraid of what's happening within us. So, knowing that you may have this disease in the future, it scares us a lot…. I also believe if it's God's will, it's God's will, so if you get like, if you get a disease, or a sickness, it's God's will, and it has a purpose… that's also one of our principles. (SK006)

Some expressed the belief that health risks are not only determined by genetics, but by a confluence of other factors including the environment, lifestyle, and aging; “genetics loads the gun, but it doesn't always pull the trigger…” (SK003). DISCUSSION

Our findings provide a novel reflection on reasons parents decline SF in a pediatric setting and align with existing literature that highlights the plausibility of flexible models which allow for more time to consider this decision. Two‐step models of care, in which opportunistic screening is offered at a separate appointment that follows the disclosure of primary findings, have been proposed as one such approach (Martyn et al., 2019). Our findings also foreshadow challenges that are likely to become apparent as SF reporting is increasingly employed in genetic and nongenetics clinics. Specifically, our data suggest that the decision to opt‐out of receiving SF in pediatric settings is complex and personal. Timing was a key factor that influenced SF decision‐making, as some parents were not interested in learning about SF while they were focused on their child's acute or primary health concerns. Parents also declined SF due to concerns that they would cause additional worry or disruption to their or their child's day‐to‐day routine. For other families, SF were simply not aligned with their personal values, beliefs, or worldviews. While limited understanding of SF benefits and concerns about discrimination have been reported in related work (Clift et al., 2015; Rego et al., 2022), these factors were not described by participants in our study. Overall, participants' views aligned with the use of flexible models of care that allow families to consider SF after they receive indication‐based test results. A flexible model of this kind may provide families with time to gather and prepare for SF specific information, reflect upon their decision, engage children with emerging capacity in the decision, and change their mind over time.

While families shared a reluctance to learn SF during their child's admission to hospital or while experiencing a stressful health event, they conveyed interest in learning SF when the stress of their situation subsided. This aligns with the idea that receiving genetic information requires consideration of the right time and place (Ashtiani et al., 2014). Specifically, through qualitative interviews with parents of children who received genetic diagnoses, Ashtiani et al. (2014) found that ‘poor timing’ of result disclosure (i.e., during a hospital admission) was associated with an overall negative experience for families. Specific to SF, Houdayer et al. (2019) found that participants were reluctant to learn about medical risks many years prior to possible disease onset, whereas later in life they felt better equipped to learn such risks as they approached the typical age of onset. As in our work, previous research has considered the timing of SF decision‐making in relation to the child's cognitive capacity. For example, Kleiderman et al. (2014) found that parents preferred to be informed of SF as soon as possible but planned to share this information with their children at a later time, suggesting that the child's evolving capacity is another factor that warrants consideration in determining the timing of SF disclosure.

In addition to the influence of timing on SF decision‐making, our findings suggest that learning about SF does not align with some families' values and beliefs. For example, some participants preferred not to learn about SF owing to a spiritual belief that “everything happens for a reason” and that a higher power would “take care” of their health risks. Others did not view genetics as an important enough contributor to their health outcomes to justify the anticipated burden of this knowledge and its impact on their day‐to‐day life. These views align with related work by Clift et al. (2015) who conducted interviews about the return of SF with families undergoing genomic testing. In this study, some participants shared that religious beliefs impacted their decision to avoid learning SF, as they felt there were aspects of their health that they were not supposed to know (Clift et al., 2015). In other related work, Mighton et al. (2020) interviewed patients who had a personal or family history of cancer about their hypothetical perspectives on receiving medically actionable and nonactionable SF. Participants who felt that SF would be detrimental to their quality of life associated SF with negative psychological effects, including increased anxiety related to disease onset, especially if the participant felt they had no control over whether or when they would develop the condition (Mighton et al., 2020). Among participants surveyed from a large National Institutes of Health environmental study in which they were offered the opportunity to learn medically actionable SF, approximately one‐third of the individuals offered SF declined (Schupmann et al., 2021). Among those who declined, half anticipated that these results would have made them worried or sad, one‐third were not curious about the finding, and one‐fifth felt learning about SF was not useful to their family (Schupmann et al., 2021). In a study exploring GS and learning about SF for newborns with congenital deafness, decisions about the information desired from GS were noted to vary depending on personal values and circumstances (Downie et al., 2020).

Given the importance of timing, flexibility, and unique values and beliefs that underpin SF decision‐making, our findings align with flexible (i.e., two‐step) models of care as a strategy for managing SF. In a model described by Martyn et al. (2019, 2024) for example, patients and their families can make decisions regarding SF after primary results have been disclosed. In this model, adults who receive GS results are offered the opportunity for reanalysis of their GS data specifically for adult‐onset SF and consent to SF after primary results have been disclosed. If interested in learning SF, individuals receive a decision‐aid (i.e., online self‐administered tool) that provides them with information about SF and a values clarification exercise to facilitate decision making. Following this, individuals meet with a genetic counselor to discuss their SF decision and result. Of those approached for reanalysis, 41% agreed to a reanalysis of stored genomic data for SF (Martyn et al., 2024). Families who received ultrarapid GS through this two‐step model responded in a post‐test survey that they preferred separating the offer of diagnostic testing from screening for adult and childhood‐onset SF (Martyn et al., 2025). In addition, flexible models allow patients to be counseled about different types of test results in a stepwise manner. In one study, when patients were recounseled about SF after primary results had been disclosed, it was found that some patients changed their mind about the receipt of SF. Specifically, 41/83 respondents changed their minds from declining to accepting SF and six people changed their minds from accepting to declining SF, a net decrease in refusals of 42.2% (Schupmann et al., 2021). In the study by Houdayer et al. (2019) about hypothetical preferences for SF, participants endorsed providing a period of reflection prior to deciding about the receipt of SF. This additional time and opportunity to ask questions may also improve patients' understanding of SF and promote informed decision‐making. Flexible models of care like this may better support families, like those interviewed in our study for whom learning SF was not a priority at the time of consenting to genetic testing and would like to consider SF at a later time. Separating these decisions in time may also reduce confusion about the reanalysis of genomic data, specifically for SF, in the years after receiving primary results.

Despite the plausible benefits of a two‐step model for SF decision‐making, implementing a model of this kind requires clinical and laboratory time, resources, and expertise. As reported by Martyn et al. (2024) a two‐step model in which SF are discussed at a separate counseling appointment after primary results are disclosed may be more resource‐intensive as it requires multiple counseling sessions within an already constrained system. The potential for families to opt‐out of receiving SF as a consequence of not wanting to attend an additional appointment with genetics has also been described (Martyn et al., 2024). In addition, Driessnack et al. (2013) caution that following up with families over time regarding SF may be logistically challenging, especially if a SF is identified in a child who will need a follow‐up appointment later in life, once they have the capacity to understand the findings. Preference‐sensitive approaches to SF may also add complexity to mainstreaming models of care and be perceived as barriers by nongenetics providers (Mackley, Shickh, et al., 2025; Mackley, Weisz, et al., 2025). Our finding that patients prefer a flexible approach to SF‐related decision‐making suggests that, while more complex models may require careful planning, capacity building among nongenetics providers, and novel educational/decision‐making tools (e.g. patient facing platforms), a two‐step model for SF consenting and reporting may improve patient centered care for families facing this decision.

LIMITATIONS

Despite our efforts to include non‐English‐speaking participants using interpreter services, a limitation of this study was that all participants were English‐speaking. We also aimed to include adults who declined SF for themselves, but due to the limited response rate and sample size (n = 7), we were only able to include parents. Most of the participants were mothers from urban settings and all participants had received at least some post‐high school education. This lack of diversity in participant demographics may limit the perspectives reported and warrants further study. Due to the limited sample size, exploring the data according to the types of SF declined was not informative.

CONCLUSION

In a pediatric setting this study identified factors related to SF decision‐making that include timing, concerns regarding worry or disruption, and misalignment with personal values, beliefs, and worldviews. Results from this study aim to inform clinical practice and policy decisions around SF in genomic diagnostics. Further work to consider flexible models of care that separate the decision about receiving primary results from the decision to receive SF may be warranted. Despite resource implications of a two‐step model, this might be important to consider in jurisdictions that present a range of SF‐related decisions to patients and their family members and particularly important in acute care and/or pediatric settings. Ultimately, the decision to not learn about medically actionable SF is multifaceted and requires specific attention when developing GS related models of care.
