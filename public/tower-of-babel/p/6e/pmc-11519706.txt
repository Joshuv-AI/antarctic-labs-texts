Protocol for isolating extracellular vesicles from pancreatic cancer cells for liver metastatic niche research
Authors: Anthony Casabianca, Mélanie Bellina, Crissy Dudgeon, Muhammad Sadeqi Nezhad, Chris Harris, Stephany Fiore, Agnes Bernet, Wade Narrow, Orjola Prela, Lan Wang, Igor Astsaturov, Patrick Mehlen, Darren R Carpizo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryExtracellular vesicles (EVs) are secreted, cell-derived, membrane-bound compartments implicated in various diseases for their ability to influence distant targets and as carriers of biomarkers. Here, we present a protocol for separating EVs from mammalian pancreatic cancer cells and their characterization using western blot and electron microscopy. We then demonstrate how they are utilized to affect tumor development in a murine model of metastatic pancreatic cancer including a method to quantify hepatic tumor burden in histologic samples.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Dudgeon et al.1Subject areas: Cell Biology, Cancer, Molecular Biology

Before you begin

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are secreted, cell-derived, membrane bound compartments capable of delivering cell components and bioactive compounds. EVs have been implicated in various disease processes for their ability to influence distant targets and as carriers of pathologic biomarkers.2 In a recent publication, we demonstrate Netrin-1 is present on the surface of EVs and facilitates pancreatic cancer metastases through long- and short-range intracellular communication.1 EVs comprise a heterogeneous group of membrane bound structures categorized by their origin. These include exosomes, and microvesicles which are physiologically secreted products from the endosome, as well as autophagosomes and byproducts of cellular stress.2,3 EVs have been implicated in cancer progression and epithelial to mesenchymal transition, as well as in metastatic niche priming through their ability to affect tissues at distant sites from a primary tumor and influence the tumor microenvironment.4 Specifically, tumor derived EVs have been characterized in the bloodstream and influence metastatic niche formation.5

Here we describe steps for separation and characterization, of extracellular vesicles harvested from pancreatic cancer cells using two protocols, as well as steps for systemic treatment to examine EV effects on liver metastasis. Two methods of separation are shown including isolation by immunoprecipitation, as well as a modified ultracentrifugation method, both from cell free culture media.6 While both methods are sufficient to generate EV’s, we find the immunoprecipitation method easier when preparing for in vivo injection. In our recent study we utilize these methods to characterize Netrin-1 and investigate its role in pancreatic cancer metastases.1 However, these methods can be utilized to investigate any molecule suspected of being secreted in EVs to affect distant sites.

Prior to beginning this protocol, investigators will have prepared cell lines which produce their molecule of interest which they suspect is secreted in EVs and any controls needed for comparison. Investigators will also have identified genetically appropriate murine model compatible with their cell lines for the generation of metastatic tumors.

Expected outcomes

For every 30 mL of cell culture media, 30 μL of immunoprecipitated EVs are generated. The amount of EV’s contained within this volume will vary depending on the specific cell line used. In our experience with our ink4a cell line, 60 mL of starting culture media would result in 1–1.5 mg/mL protein concentration in our EV solution. Intrasplenic injection of tumor cell lines should result in all mice developing liver metastases. When completed, this protocol should result in liver tissue containing varying amounts of metastases burden. In our recent publication, we demonstrate that preconditioning mice with netrin-1 containing EVs resulted in greater metastatic tumor burden.1

With the ultracentrifugation method, for any given amount of cell culture media, 30–50 μL of secreted EVs are separated and concentrated. The amount of EVs contained within this volume will vary depending on the initial amount of cell culture medium collected, the number of cells that were plated in culture dished, and the specific cell line used. The diameter of EVs should peak around 100 nm, thus the differential centrifugation steps should be adjusted to obtain a desired EV diameter [8]. Western Blot analysis of cultivated cells and their secreted EVs should show expression of specific membrane protein in both samples. Transmission Electron Microscopy should allow to visualize the expression of these membrane proteins at the surface of EVs in amounts corresponding to the expression levels of the protein in the cell line (Figure 2). In our recent publication, we demonstrated that netrin-1 expression was observed at the surface of EVs thanks to the co-labeling of vesicles with anti-Netrin-1 and anti-CD63 antibodies, in proportions that matched protein expression in the cells : a greater proportion of netrin-1 expressing EVs was observed in EVs from the Met38 cell line in which the expression of netrin-1 is upregulated, compared to the EV from the Ink4a cell line in which netrin-1 expression is not upregulated.1

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data analysis for this protocol involves the image-based quantification of liver metastases using digital pathology software such as QuPath. Other digital tools such as Visiopharm also exist and can accomplish this task. This is done by first generating stained tissue sections from harvested mouse livers. In our recent publication, hematoxylin and eosin staining was sufficient to distinguish metastases from surrounding stroma (Figure 1).1 However, users may wish to utilize specific immunohistochemistry in their attempts. Once images of whole slides are scanned, areas of stroma, tumor, and slide background are highlighted for the software’s pixel classification learning. Subsequently this is used to determine the of total tumor occupied area a percentage of tissue occupied space. This can be done for multiple sections of liver from each mouse for complete surveillance. This data was then compared for statistical significance using the student’s t-test.

Limitations

This protocol is limited in several ways. Firstly, this protocol does not isolate specific EVs from cell culture media, and confounding results may occur if isolates contain unaccounted protein and EVs. EVs are a heterogeneous mix of endosomal derived secreted membrane bound vesicles, as well as various autophagy and apoptosis associated vesicles which may contain varying amounts of the molecule of interest, as well as confounding molecular components. This limitation may be partially overcome with the use of genetically modified cell lines which do not express the molecule of interest to act as negative controls.

Additionally, this protocol utilizes a splenic injection model of metastases. While this generates consistent liver tumor deposits, it differs from the mechanisms underlying orthotopic and endogenous tumor spread.

This protocol does not allow the separation of subtypes of extracellular vesicles by ultracentrifugation. However, TEM analysis permits the colocalization of target proteins with specific EV marker proteins (such as CD63 or CD9 which are enriched in exosomes (5).

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Immunoprecipitation yields low quantity of EV protein. Step 1 through 9.

Potential solution

The quantity of EVs produced will vary based on growing conditions, cell type, cell passage, and starting volume of cell media used in generating a 10× media concentrate. In our experiments, our ink4a cell line can produce up to 1.5 mg/mL protein in EV solution when grown to 80% confluence in 30 cm dishes containing 30 mL of media. Cells are washed and maintained in serum free media for 24 h at 37°C prior to concentration. These parameters may need to be adjusted based on users’ specific cell lines and empiric testing.

Problem 2

Splenic injection does not result in liver metastases. Steps 22–37.

Potential solution

Not all cell lines are capable of generating tumors in vivo. Our Ink4a and met38 cell lines have been empirically shown to produce robust tumor growth in vivo in our FVB mouse strain. All cell lines should be tested for tumor formation and growth rate prior to using this protocol. Additionally, cell lines should be genetically compatible with their murine model into which they are being injected to avoid immune mediated tumor cell destruction.

Problem 3

Ultracentrifugation yields low quantity of EVs. Steps 43–55.

Potential solution

The quantity of EVs produced will vary based on growing conditions, cell type, cell passage, and starting volume of cell media used. In our experiments, our ink4a cell line can produce up to 20 million particles/mL when platted at 50% confluency in 150 cm2 dishes containing 10 mL of media. Cells are washed and maintained in serum free media for 72 h at 37°C prior to ultracentrifugation. These parameters may need to be adjusted based on users specific cell lines and empiric testing.

Problem 4

Ultracentrifugation yields high quantity of EVs and Western blot shows clear expression of target protein in cells but not in EVs. Steps 56–66.

Potential solution

Poor washing of cells before the 72 h incubation with serum free media may lead to the presence of additional EVs that were not secreted by the cells but present in the plating culture media, therefore enhancing the total amount of particles detected by NTA. Make sure to wash cells properly and thoroughly before incubating with serum free culture media.
