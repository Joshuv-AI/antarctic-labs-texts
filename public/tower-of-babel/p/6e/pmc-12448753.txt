Primary care for recipients of kidney transplants
Authors: Christie Rampersad, Jason Bau
Published: 2025

Patients with kidney transplants have increased risk of other diseases and benefit from primary care involvement

Kidney transplant offers patients survival benefits up to age 75 years and better quality of life than with dialysis.1,2 Patients who have transplants require lifelong immunosuppression, which can contribute to cardiovascular disease, malignancy, and infection, from which transplant recipients are at a higher risk of death than the general population.3

Risk of serious infection is high

Five-year incidence of any diagnosed infection is about 80%, doubling risk of death and contributing to about one-fifth of patient deaths after kidney transplant.4 Urinary tract and respiratory infections predominate.4 Vaccinations should be given before transplant, per public health guidelines, as live vaccines (e.g., measles, mumps, and rubella) are contraindicated after transplant. Inactivated vaccines can be given beginning 3–6 months after transplant — except influenza vaccines, which may be administered at 1 month.3 Annual vaccination of household contacts for respiratory viruses is advised.

Cancer screening and prevention are critical

Patients with kidney transplants have a 2–3 times higher risk of malignancy and 10 times higher risk of cancer-related death than the general population.5 Primary care involvement is linked to higher screening adherence.5 Given high risk of nonmelanoma skin cancers, dedicated use of sunscreen rated higher than SPF50, skin self-checks, and annual skin and lip examinations are indicated.3 Post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD) may present with constitutional symptoms, lymphadenopathy, or cytopenias. Epstein–Barr virus DNAemia is a risk factor for PTLD and testing is warranted.5

Cardiovascular risk factors should be actively managed

Diabetes mellitus occurs in 30% of transplant recipients and is linked to glucocorticoid use. Post-transplant metabolic targets are low-density lipoprotein levels lower than 2.0 mmol/L and glycated hemoglobin lower than 7.5%.3 A blood pressure target of lower than 130/80 mm Hg balances general health while avoiding hypotension precipitating kidney hypoperfusion.3 Smoking cessation and lifestyle counselling (diet, physical activity) are essential.3

Fertility may quickly improve after transplant

Pregnancy should be considered only after 1 year of graft function stability and with early involvement of specialists.6 Contraception and preconception counselling are essential, given the teratogenic risks of common post-transplant medications (e.g., mycophenolic acid, trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system inhibitors).6
