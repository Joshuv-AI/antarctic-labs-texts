Development and preclinical evaluation of a hybrid stereoelectroencephalographic–laser depth electrode for magnetic resonance imaging‐guided interstitial thermal therapy in drug‐resistant epilepsy
Authors: Bertrand Mathon, Karima Mokhtari, Damien Galanaud, Alexandre Carpentier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study was undertaken to design and validate a hybrid depth electrode combining stereoelectroencephalographic (sEEG) recording and magnetic resonance‐guided laser interstitial thermal therapy (MRgLITT) under real‐time magnetic resonance thermometry, to streamline the transition from invasive localization to focal ablation in patients with drug‐resistant focal epilepsy.MethodsWe engineered a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)‐compatible depth probe that integrated intracerebral EEG contacts and a central optical fiber for laser delivery. The contact materials and geometry were optimized to reduce susceptibility artifacts and preserve the proton resonance frequency (PRF) thermometry. Preclinical testing included MRI artifact screening in phantoms, thermal performance testing, PRF thermometry validation against temperature sensors in phantoms and ovine brain, artifact quantification versus clinical depth electrodes, electrophysiologic signal quality assessment before/after thermal stress, and in vivo canine feasibility with serial MRI and histology. MRI compatibility was confirmed for a next generation contact variant.ResultsThe optimized contact design produced small MRI artifacts and preserved PRF thermometry outside an approximately 2‐mm pericontact exclusion zone. Thermal testing showed localized heating with rapid postlaser decay, modulation by coolant flow, and performance comparable to that of clinical LITT applicators. In dipole‐phantom testing, baseline electrophysiological recordings from the new hybrid electrode were comparable to clinical depth‐electrode controls, whereas a previously heated hybrid electrode showed increased noise under low‐amplitude conditions. In vivo, MRgLITT produced sharply demarcated lesions that scaled with the delivered energy without hemorrhage, edema, midline shift, or device damage. Histological examination revealed coagulative necrosis with a narrow perilesional zone and no carbonization at the contacts.SignificanceThis patented hybrid sEEG‐laser electrode supports a “diagnose–model–treat–verify” strategy along a single stereotactic trajectory, enabling sEEG confirmation followed by MRgLITT without a second stereotactic implantation in selected patients. These data support progression to first‐in‐human evaluation and integration into epilepsy surgery workflows, particularly for MRI‐negative focal epilepsies, where minimally invasive strategies are favored.

Key points A single hybrid depth electrode combines intracranial EEG mapping with MR‐guided laser ablation under real‐time PRF thermometry.Optimized contact materials and geometry minimize MRI susceptibility artifacts and preserve thermometry fidelity beyond an approximately 1–2‐mm pericontact exclusion zone in the tested preclinical models.Phantom and ovine brain experiments demonstrated controlled, localized heating with cooling‐dependent temperature stabilization and lesion profiles comparable to those of a clinical LITT applicator.In vivo canine experiments showed dose‐scalable, MRI‐confirmed lesions with preserved device integrity and histology consistent with coagulative necrosis without contact carbonization.The electrode is designed to streamline clinical workflows by reducing the need for sequential “sEEG then separate LITT” procedures, supporting a single‐implantation—and in selected cases, same‐procedure—pathway from localization to ablation.

INTRODUCTION

Drug‐resistant epilepsy is a major public health challenge. For focal epilepsies, when medications fail, the current standard includes open surgical approaches, which can yield substantial seizure reduction but at the cost of craniotomy, inpatient recovery, and limited repeatability. 1 Less invasive strategies, including stereotactic radiosurgery, have shown lower effectiveness than open resection, whereas neuromodulatory therapies (e.g., deep brain stimulation or vagus nerve stimulation) are promising yet largely palliative rather than ablative. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5

Magnetic resonance‐guided laser interstitial thermal therapy (MRgLITT) has matured into a minimally invasive alternative that couples focal energy delivery with real‐time magnetic resonance (MR) thermometry and prospective thermal dose control. 6 , 7 Contemporary series and reviews report clinically meaningful seizure freedom and responder rates in appropriately selected mesial temporal and lesional epilepsies, alongside shorter hospital stays, and favorable recovery profiles compared with open surgery. 8 , 9 , 10 Importantly, early clinical experiences with real‐time MR thermometry‐guided laser ablation in intracranial pathology demonstrated the feasibility of precise, controlled lesion creation with an acceptable perioperative seizure profile, supporting broader application of MR‐guided thermal therapy in the epilepsies. 11 , 12

In parallel, stereoelectroencephalography‐guided radiofrequency thermocoagulation (sEEG‐RFTC) leverages intracranial electrodes to perform targeted lesioning at the time of diagnostic mapping; however, it lacks real‐time MR imaging (MRI) thermometric safety monitoring and affords limited visualization of thermal spread. 13 , 14 Addressing these limitations, we developed a hybrid depth electrode that integrates sEEG recording and laser delivery within a single, internally cooled probe engineered for MR compatibility and minimized artifact, thereby enabling continuous EEG acquisition, prospective modeling of the ablation, real‐time thermal monitoring, and immediate verification, all along a single stereotactic trajectory under MRI guidance. This “diagnose–model–treat–control” workflow is designed to consolidate what has traditionally required multiple tools and stages into a single streamlined, minimally invasive procedure.

Here, we present the design rationale and preclinical validation of this hybrid sEEG‐LITT device across benchtop, ex vivo, and in vivo models, including systematic assessments of MR artifact behavior, thermometric accuracy, electrode durability under thermal load, and the safety and feasibility of creating well‐circumscribed intracerebral lesions under real‐time MR monitoring.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Device design

A custom MRI‐compatible depth electrode was engineered with (1) multiple circumferential platinum–iridium (Pt‐Ir; and later gold [Au]) contacts for intracerebral EEG, (2) a central optical channel for laser fiber delivery, and (3) an internal closed‐loop cooling lumen. During development, several prototype configurations were designed with different contact layouts and adjustable intercontact spacing, to balance electrophysiological sampling, MRI compatibility, and the need to preserve an adequate laser‐emission window between contacts for the intended ablation geometry. The representative clinical prototype illustrated in Figure 1A comprised an outer insulated shaft with an overall diameter of approximately 1.62 mm, circumferential metallic EEG contacts (Pt‐Ir in the principal prototype series and Au in a later compatibility variant) with a contact length of 2 mm, and configurable intercontact spacing (typically 5 mm, adjustable to 10 mm when a larger laser‐treatment window was required). The distal shaft incorporated a central optical channel for insertion of the laser fiber and an internal cooling circuit with dedicated lumen(s) for closed‐loop coolant circulation (Figure 1B). The metallic contacts were integrated into the outer shaft while remaining electrically connected to a proximal EEG interface. Figure 1 depicts one representative prototype configuration and its main engineering components, rather than all prototype variants tested during development.

Proposed clinical workflow and proximal handling

The intended clinical use of the device is sequential (Figure 1C). First, the hybrid electrode is implanted stereotactically and fixed at the skull entry site using a standard sEEG anchoring interface (bolt/cap‐type fixation or equivalent platform‐compatible cranial anchor). During the diagnostic phase, the proximal electrical connector is attached to the EEG recording system; the optical/cooling access remains sealed by a protective cap. If ablation is subsequently indicated, the EEG connector is disconnected under sterile conditions, the proximal cap is opened, the laser fiber is introduced through the central optical channel, and the cooling lines are connected to establish the closed‐loop cooling circuit before MR‐guided ablation. In the current prototype concept, EEG recording and laser‐fiber use are therefore intended to be sequential rather than simultaneous functions. After ablation, reconfiguration for further recording may be conceptually possible, but this reconnection workflow was not formally tested in the present preclinical study.

With respect to infection control, the intended workflow relies on sterile preparation of the proximal interface, use of sterile single‐use laser and cooling components, maintenance of a closed cooling circuit, and resealing/protection of the proximal access when the fiber is not in place. These handling principles are described here as the intended translational workflow; they were not formally evaluated in a dedicated human‐factors or infection‐control study.

Experimental program

MRI artifact screening in phantoms

We conducted bench testing in closed‐bore 1.5‐T MRI to screen candidate Pt‐Ir (90/10) sEEG contact geometries for MRI compatibility and thermometry performance using melon and yogurt phantoms. Arrays of tubular contacts spanning inner/outer diameters and lengths were mounted alongside platinum foils (5.3 × 2.0 mm, .01–.05 mm thick) approximating the unrolled surface area of a 1.7‐mm‐diameter, 2‐mm‐long contact, and then embedded in a melon. Morphologic artifacts were assessed using three fast spoiled gradient echo (FSPGR) localizer slices (3‐mm thickness). For thermometry, an endoluminal laser fiber was positioned between the first and second contacts and activated without cooling in melon and yogurt to test whether contact‐related susceptibility altered MR thermal mapping or automated thermal‐dose computation. The endpoints included qualitative artifact extent, artifact stability during thermometry, accuracy of MR temperature calculations near contacts, and posttest device integrity.

Thermal performance in water and opaque gel phantoms

Using two perpendicular thermistors—T1 abutting an electrode contact and T2 at a 3–12‐mm offset—we applied stepwise laser power (≈2–15 W) with predefined on/off cycles. We compared the temperature increases and cooling dynamics between the hybrid probe and a reference interstitial laser applicator and characterized the impact of coolant flow rate on peak and steady‐state temperatures.

MRI thermometry validation

We performed proton resonance frequency (PRF)‐based MR thermometry during laser delivery (e.g., 5–10 W for 30–60 s) in yogurt phantom and freshly explanted ovine brain, comparing MR‐derived temperatures against thermocouple readings at positions along the probe (contact, sheath, 8–12‐mm radial offsets). The system safety cutoffs and necrosis prediction algorithms were evaluated.

Comparative MRI artifact quantification across electrode models

To compare susceptibility‐related MRI artifacts across electrode designs, a fresh ovine brain was immersed in water and implanted with three depth electrode models positioned in parallel within the same imaging plane: the hybrid sEEG‐laser electrode, a clinical depth electrode (ADTech), and a microdepth electrode (MICRODEEP, DIXI Medical). MRI was performed on a 1.5‐T clinical system using a panel of common clinical sequences (T1‐weighted, inversion recovery (IR)‐FSPGR/FSPGR three‐dimensional [3D] axial/coronal, T2* gradient‐recalled echo [GRE], fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery [FLAIR], T2 fast spin echo [FSE], and diffusion). Detailed artifact‐imaging acquisition parameters are provided in Table S1. In the archived ex vivo ovine‐brain protocol, T1 was acquired axially (slice thickness = 5 mm, gap = 1 mm, field of view [FOV] = 260 × 260 mm, matrix = 320 × 224, pixel size = .8 × 1.2 mm), IR‐FSPGR 3D and FSPGR 3D axially/coronally (slice thickness = 1.5 mm, FOV = 240 × 180 mm, matrix = 256 × 192, voxel size = .9 × .9 × 1.5 mm), T2 GRE axially (slice thickness = 5 mm, gap = .5 mm, FOV = 240 × 180 mm, matrix = 256 × 224, pixel size = .9 × .8 mm), FLAIR axially (slice thickness = 5 mm, gap = .5 mm, FOV = 240 × 240 mm, matrix = 320 × 192, pixel size = .75 × 1.25 mm), T2 FSE axially (slice thickness = 5 mm, gap = .5 mm, FOV = 240 × 180 mm, matrix = 320 × 256, pixel size = .75 × .7 mm), and diffusion axially (slice thickness = 5 mm, gap = .5 mm, FOV = 240 × 180 mm, matrix = 128 × 96, pixel size = 1.9 × 1.9 mm). For each sequence, artifact metrics were measured on DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) images using electronic calipers: radial artifact diameter (maximal transverse signal void/distortion around the electrode), longitudinal artifact length (maximal extent along the electrode axis), and geometric deformation (maximal local displacement/distortion of adjacent anatomy attributable to the field perturbation).

MRI compatibility of next generation Au contacts

We tested a next generation Au‐contact variant in a separate standalone MRI compatibility experiment in water at 1.5 T to reconfirm artifact behavior and thermometry compatibility. This Au dataset was not acquired in the same side‐by‐side ex vivo comparator setup used for the Pt/Ir artifact quantification study.

Electrophysiologic signal quality

In a magnetically shielded magnetoencephalography room, we recorded responses to a standardized electrical dipole phantom using four electrode conditions: (1) a routine clinical sEEG electrode (AdTech Medical), (2) a microdepth electrode (DIXI Medical), (3) a new hybrid sEEG‐laser electrode, and (4) a previously heated (“used”) hybrid sEEG‐laser electrode. Each electrode was positioned at approximately 5 cm from the dipole, and recordings were obtained from the contact closest to the tip. Dipole stimulation was triangular at 7 Hz, with three excitation voltages (.5, .05, and .039 V), and each acquisition lasted 60 s. The full experiment was repeated once to confirm the consistency of the recordings. Because this archived dataset was exploratory, the analysis focused on qualitative waveform fidelity and relative noise level across electrode types and excitation amplitudes, rather than on a prespecified quantitative impedance or spectral‐power endpoint.

In vivo canine feasibility and safety

Two adult beagles underwent stereotactic implantation and laser treatment under general anesthesia. Dog #1 underwent one frontal hybrid sEEG‐laser ablation (5 W, 30 s; cooling setting 1), followed by MRI at approximately 2 h, day 8, and day 41. Dog #2 underwent four acute intracerebral ablations at bilateral frontal/parietal targets: three with the hybrid probe (5 W/30 s, 5 W/60 s, and 10 W/60 s; cooling setting 1) and one with a reference Biotex laser applicator without cooling (10 W/60 s), followed by postprocedure MRI and immediate brain harvest for histological analysis. Animals were maintained under general anesthesia (e.g., xylazine premedication and intravenous ketamine), and all efforts were made to minimize pain and distress during the procedure. Euthanasia was performed at the planned endpoints using intracardiac thiopental under deep anesthesia. The primary endpoints were procedural feasibility, device integrity, adverse events, and MRI/histological lesion characterization. Lesion size was assessed from the available postprocedure MRI measurements. True 3D lesion segmentation was not performed. For descriptive purposes only, approximate ellipsoid volumes were estimated from the available lesion diameter and length using the formula V = π/6 × diameter2 × length, assuming that the unmeasured second transverse diameter was similar to the measured transverse diameter.

Statistics

Artifact diameter and length were analyzed as paired, nonparametric repeated measures across the MRI sequences. Analyses were limited to sequences with complete data across the three‐electrode models. The Friedman test was used to assess the differences across the electrodes. For Friedman tests, we report the test statistic (χ2), degrees of freedom, and exact two‐sided p‐value. When significant, post hoc paired Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests were performed for each electrode pair, with Holm correction for three comparisons. Data are summarized as median (interquartile range [IQR]) across sequences. For effect size, we reported mean paired differences (comparator−sEEG‐laser) with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (CIs; resampling across the sequences). When applicable, the association between the laser power and stabilized temperature was assessed using Spearman rank correlation. All tests were two‐sided (α = .05).

The electrophysiology experiment was exploratory and descriptive. Because the archived dataset did not include standardized impedance measurements, spectral‐band decomposition, or a sufficiently structured quantitative signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR) dataset linked to a standardized thermal‐history protocol, no formal inferential statistics were performed for this experiment.

RESULTS

Optimized contact geometry on phantoms

Thinner platinum foils produced less susceptibility artifact in a constant‐surface surrogate experiment. In parallel, qualitative MRI screening of multiple Pt/Ir tubular contact geometries showed that a low‐artifact group included contacts around diameter (Ø) 1.6 × 1.7 mm (length = 1.27 mm), Ø1.53 × 1.68 mm (length = 1.14 mm), and Ø.79 × .86 mm (length = 2 mm). The final Pt/Ir contact geometry (Ø1.62 × 1.70 mm, length = 2 mm) was selected empirically as a compromise between low artifact, thermometry compatibility, and integration into the final cooled hybrid electrode design (Figure 2A–C). During MR thermometry with the laser on, artifact magnitude/distribution were unchanged versus morphological imaging; temperature estimates were reliable except within ~2 mm of each contact, and neither the thermal‐management algorithm nor necrosis prediction on the laser console was perturbed. After laser shutoff, the metallic contacts cooled more slowly than the sheath/phantom (greater thermal inertia); in noncooled prototypes, we observed partial sheath melting and detachment of one contact, attributed to the absence of active cooling and provisional assembly, motivating cooled designs for subsequent experiments.

Thermal phantoms: Localized heating with rapid decay

In a water bath, the temperature increase measured during laser activation increased with the delivered power for both the hybrid probe and reference applicator (5–15 W; Figure S1A,B). Despite a lower measured coolant flow for the hybrid probe (≈6 mL/min vs. ≈9 mL/min for the reference applicator at identical pump settings), temperature increases at the probe interface (T1) and at 3 mm from the probe (T2) remained limited and comparable between devices. In this highly convective model, these findings should be interpreted descriptively as reflecting lower net thermal elevation under the tested conditions, rather than isolated evidence of better cooling efficiency.

In an opaque gel phantom, laser delivery resulted in a marked local temperature increase at the probe interface, whereas the temperature elevation rapidly decreased with radial distance. At 12 mm from the probe, the temperature remained near the baseline, whereas a modest increase was observed at 8 mm, indicating a steep radial thermal gradient and highly localized heating (Figure S1C).

During stabilized heating in the opaque gel, the sheath temperature increased monotonically with the laser power (2–10 W; Figure S1D). A strong monotonic association was observed between the laser power and stabilized temperature at both the sheath and contact levels (Spearman rank correlation, ρ = 1.00 for both, n = 3). High‐temperature endurance tests demonstrated stable temperature plateaus at ≥90°C for several minutes without visible device alteration, supporting the robustness of the cooling strategy.

PRF thermometry validation

With internal cooling (flow setting = 1, safety threshold = 54°C), PRF MR thermometry reproduced the expected heating/cooling dynamics when the regions of interest (ROIs) were placed on the sheath and outside the immediate pericontact artifact zone. In the yogurt phantom, the sheath temperature increased from 34 to 70°C during 5 W/30 s (temperature increase relative to baseline [ΔT] = 36°C) and from 37 to 78°C during 5 W/60 s (ΔT = 41°C; Movie S1, Figure 3A). At 10 W, the sheath temperature increased from 37.3 to 107°C (ΔT = 69.7°C) before the automatic cutoff at 42 s. In fresh ovine brain, sheath temperature rose from 12 to 40°C during 5 W/30 s (ΔT = 28°C) and from 13 to 76°C during 10 W/60 s (ΔT = 63°C; Movie S2, Figure 3B). Data are descriptive; in yogurt, ΔT increased monotonically with delivered energy (power × time; Spearman ρ = 1.00, n = 3, using 42 s for the cutoff run; Figure 3C).

Temperature sampling on metallic contacts produced spurious values (~140°C). In the ovine brain, each contact generated a persistent central signal void (“black hole”) of ~3–4 mm diameter; PRF values were unstable within ~1 mm and overestimated at approximately ~1–2 mm, whereas measurements obtained beyond approximately 2 mm were qualitatively consistent with prior thermistor‐based experiments (Figure 3D). These observations support placing thermal safety control points at least 2 mm radially away from each contact center as a conservative preclinical operational rule for PRF ROI placement. This threshold should not be interpreted as a universally validated clinical safety margin for all target types or anatomical situations.

Artifact comparison

Across the evaluated sequences, the sEEG‐laser electrode produced small, well‐bounded susceptibility artifacts with limited geometric distortion beyond the immediate perielectrode region (Figure 4A–D). Sequence‐by‐sequence radial artifact diameter, longitudinal artifact length, geometric deformation, and acquisition parameters are summarized in Table S2. The radial artifact diameter was measurable across seven sequences with complete data and differed significantly among electrodes (Friedman χ2[2] = 10.2, p = .006; Figure 4E). The median artifact diameter was 4.7 mm (IQR = 4.1–4.7) for the sEEG‐laser electrode, 6.0 mm (IQR = 5.4–6.5) for the ADTech electrode, and 4.7 mm (IQR = 4.2–5.2) for the DIXI Medical MICRODEEP electrode. Post hoc Wilcoxon testing (Holm‐adjusted) showed a larger artifact for ADTech versus sEEG‐laser (p adj = .047), whereas DIXI Medical MICRODEEP versus sEEG‐laser was not significant (p adj = .14); ADTech versus DIXI Medical MICRODEEP showed a trend (p adj = .055; Figure 4F). Estimation analyses yielded mean paired differences of +1.80 mm (ADTech−sEEG‐laser; 95% CI = 1.1–2.5; n = 7) and + .47 mm (DIXI Medical MICRODEEP − sEEG‐laser; 95% CI = .06–1.1; n = 7).

Geometric deformation on FLAIR/T2 FSE remained limited for the sEEG‐laser electrode (≤2 mm) and was more apparent for ADTech (up to ~3–4 mm) under similar conditions.

In a separate standalone 1.5‐T water experiment, the Au‐contact electrode variant produced no appreciable artifact and a ~1.7‐mm signal void limited to the implant diameter, supporting MRI compatibility and robust thermometry behavior. Because this Au evaluation was performed independently from the ex vivo comparator experiment, it should be interpreted as a compatibility dataset rather than a direct quantitative head‐to‐head comparison with clinical depth electrodes.

EEG signal quality

In the dipole‐phantom experiment, four electrode conditions were tested (ADTech, DIXI Medical MICRODEEP, new hybrid sEEG‐laser, and previously heated hybrid sEEG‐laser), each at three excitation voltages (.5, .05, and .039 V), with 60‐s acquisitions repeated in a second full run to verify consistency. The resulting dataset was descriptive. Across conditions, baseline recordings from the new hybrid electrode were broadly comparable to those of the ADTech reference and the MICRODEEP electrode. At the lower excitation levels (.05 and .039 V), the new hybrid electrode appeared somewhat noisier than ADTech, but the archived report judged this difference not to be practically significant. In contrast, the previously heated (“used”) hybrid electrode showed clearly increased noise relative to the new hybrid electrode at the same low‐amplitude settings, suggesting degradation of recording quality after prior thermal exposure (Figure 5). Electrode impedance, spectral‐band power, and the effect of a standardized repeated‐ablation protocol were not formally measured in this experiment. Accordingly, no examples of clinically interpreted postablation EEG are available from this dataset.

In vivo canine feasibility and outcomes

The canine feasibility study included two adult beagles and a total of five intracerebral ablations: four with the hybrid sEEG‐laser probe and one with a reference Biotex laser applicator (Table S2). The procedures were stereotactically feasible with a standard workflow (Figure 6A,B). No unexpected clinical adverse events were observed. In Dog #1, a single frontal hybrid ablation (5 W, 30 s) was followed by serial MRI at approximately 2 h, day 8, and day 41; MRI showed a sharply demarcated lesion measuring approximately 6 mm in diameter and 8 mm in length, without hemorrhage, midline shift, or significant edema (Figure 6C). In Dog #2, acute postprocedure MRI was obtained after three hybrid ablations and one noncooled Biotex reference ablation. The three hybrid lesions measured approximately 4.6 × 6.7 mm, 6.5 × 8.2 mm, and 8.7 × 12.2 mm, respectively, whereas the single noncooled Biotex lesion measured 11 × 15.2 mm (Figure 6D). Across these descriptive data, lesion size increased with delivered energy among the hybrid ablations. Because true 3D MRI segmentation was not performed, lesion volumes were not used as formal endpoints. As an exploratory descriptive estimate, assuming an ellipsoid geometry and a second transverse diameter similar to the measured lesion diameter, the approximate volumes of the hybrid lesions were 151 mm3 for Dog #1 at 150 J, and 74 mm3, 181 mm3, and 445 mm3 for Dog #2 at 150 J, 300 J, and 600 J, respectively. The single noncooled Biotex reference lesion at 600 J had an approximate ellipsoid volume of 963 mm3 (Table S2). These values are provided only to contextualize the dose–size relationship and should not be interpreted as validated 3D volumetry.

DISCUSSION

Key findings

Table 1 summarizes the staged experiments and key outcomes of this study. We demonstrated the preclinical feasibility of a hybrid depth probe that unifies intracranial EEG recordings with MR‐guided laser ablation. The central technical achievements were as follows: (1) minimizing susceptibility artifacts through iterative optimization of the contact material and geometry; (2) preserving the PRF thermometry fidelity outside a narrow region around each contact (approximately a 2‐mm radius from the contact center); and (3) preventing contact‐adjacent overheating through internal cooling. Thermal testing confirmed highly localized energy deposition with rapid postlaser decay and modulation by coolant flow, whereas in vivo experiments showed dose‐dependent lesioning without acute hemorrhage or edema and histopathology typical of controlled thermal coagulation.

Beyond technical feasibility, the main translational message is the workflow. A hybrid sEEG‐laser electrode was designed to consolidate a “diagnose–model–treat–verify” pathway along a single stereotactic trajectory. In contemporary practice, many candidates who require sEEG to localize seizure onset undergo two distinct stereotactic interventions if they proceed to MRgLITT: first, diagnostic EEG electrode implantation, and later, a second stereotactic implantation of a dedicated laser applicator after electrode explantation and target selection. In contrast, a hybrid electrode enables treatment through the same implanted device used for localization, eliminating the need for a second stereotactic retargeting step. This opens the door to a true single‐intervention strategy—sEEG confirmation followed immediately by MRgLITT in the same procedure for selected patients—and, more broadly, a single‐implantation pathway where ablation can be performed without device exchange once the target is defined. In practical terms, this can translate into fewer operating room episodes, reduced postoperative risks 15 and cumulative anesthesia exposure, fewer stereotactic planning steps, and less opportunity for trajectory mismatch between diagnostic and therapeutic devices, while preserving the MR thermometry‐based safety control that underpins the MRgLITT.

Clinical context

MRgLITT has matured into a widely used, minimally invasive option for focal epilepsy. Its defining advantage over other ablative approaches is prospective, real‐time temperature monitoring with thermal dose modeling, which allows titration of lesion extent while protecting adjacent critical structures. 6 , 7 , 16 National utilization studies confirm that LITT use in epilepsy has increased substantially over the past decade, in parallel with a relative decline in some open procedures. 17 At the same time, sEEG‐RFTC has expanded as a minimally invasive therapeutic option that can be delivered through diagnostic electrodes during invasive evaluation, particularly in complex epileptogenic networks or when targets are deep and/or adjacent to eloquent structures. 18 However, sEEG‐RFTC lacks real‐time MR thermometry feedback and thermal dose modeling, and its therapeutic effect is often limited or transient, in part because the technology typically allows only small‐volume lesions and provides comparatively limited ablative power, which may be insufficient to achieve durable disconnection of broader epileptogenic tissue. 14 , 19 , 20 Taken together, these limitations mean that sEEG‐RFTC is currently used at many centers primarily as a pretherapeutic or “test” intervention; a favorable short‐term clinical response to thermocoagulations can support the causal role of the targeted contacts in the epileptogenic network and, in turn, help refine and justify a subsequent definitive resective or disconnective surgical strategy when appropriate. 13 , 21

A hybrid sEEG‐laser electrode aims to bridge these approaches by combining electrophysiologic targeting (including stimulation‐based functional mapping) with MR‐verified ablation control. This combination is particularly relevant for nonlesional (MRI‐negative) focal epilepsy. In such patients, sEEG is often required to delineate the seizure onset zone and to map a distributed epileptogenic network; this diagnostic uncertainty may also temper enthusiasm for irreversible open resection. 22 Nevertheless, sEEG‐informed MRgLITT has been reported in MRI‐negative focal epilepsies and epilepsy associated with periventricular nodular heterotopia, supporting the principle that electrophysiology‐defined targets can be treated using stereotactic laser energy. 23 , 24 , 25 However, the typical workflow still requires the exchange of devices (diagnostic electrodes and a separate laser applicator). A combined sEEG‐LITT device directly targets this bottleneck.

Technical considerations

The principal imaging constraint observed in our program—PRF thermometry unreliability within approximately 1–2 mm of metallic contacts—was predictable from susceptibility‐related phase distortions and was consistent across phantoms and ex vivo tissues. Operationally, this limitation can be managed by positioning safety control points and thermal‐dose contours slightly away from each contact, outside the exclusion zone, while maintaining accurate monitoring in the surrounding parenchyma where tissue‐injury thresholds are clinically relevant. However, the present data support this approach only as a conservative preclinical operational strategy for ROI placement, not as a universally validated clinical safety margin. This distinction becomes important for targets in which treatment planning may require submillimeter precision, such as some insular targets or hypothalamic hamartomas adjacent to critical structures. In such settings, the contact‐adjacent exclusion zone may reduce the practical usefulness of thermometry exactly where spatial precision is most demanding, and the current preclinical data should therefore be interpreted with caution. In addition, our artifact‐imaging experiment suggests that visible topologic image deformation becomes negligible beyond approximately 3 mm radially around the hybrid probe, but this should not be conflated with thermometry accuracy at submillimetric distances from the contacts.

An additional translational consideration is tissue heterogeneity. All validation experiments in this study were performed in healthy phantoms, ex vivo normal brain, or healthy canine brain, whereas clinical epilepsy targets often consist of structurally abnormal tissue such as focal cortical dysplasia, periventricular nodular heterotopia, gliosis, or previously operated parenchyma. These substrates may differ from healthy brain in cellular architecture, extracellular matrix composition, water content, and local vascularity. As a result, both thermal spread and lesion geometry may differ from the patterns observed here, because conductive heat transfer, local heat‐sink effects, and tissue coagulation thresholds may not be identical. The relationship between true tissue heating and PRF thermometry may also vary in abnormal tissue if the local microenvironment changes the spatial distribution of heating or the stability of the surrounding MR signal. Accordingly, the lesion dimensions and thermometry behavior reported in this study should be interpreted as proof‐of‐feasibility in healthy tissue rather than as a definitive prediction of ablation geometry in all epileptogenic substrates.

Cooling is the second cornerstone of safe translation. Internal cooling is standard for modern LITT applicators and is essential to prevent overheating at the probe–tissue interface of the probe. Our early noncooled prototypes showed sheath degradation and contact detachment after laser exposure, whereas cooled designs maintained structural integrity and produced no carbonization at the contacts in vivo. Clinically, robust cooling should reduce char‐related impedance changes that can destabilize heating, minimize unintended tract injury, and preserve the predictable lesion geometry during prolonged or repeated applications. It may also facilitate multisegment ablation along a trajectory, which is attractive for elongated targets or network‐informed strategies requiring several small lesions rather than one large ablation.

Electrophysiological performance after thermal stress merits cautious interpretation. Beyond thermal and imaging performance, successful translation will also depend on the usability of the proximal workflow. The present study validates the engineering feasibility of the hybrid concept but does not constitute a formal evaluation of fixation hardware, sterile connector exchange, or MRI‐suite handling during transition from sEEG recording to ablation. In the current design concept, these steps are intended to follow a sequential workflow in which EEG recording is disconnected before laser‐fiber insertion and cooling‐circuit connection. Dedicated human‐factors and procedural validation will be required before routine clinical implementation. The available data come from an exploratory dipole‐phantom experiment comparing one new and one previously heated hybrid electrode against routine clinical comparators, rather than from clinical postablation EEG recordings. Baseline responses from the new hybrid electrode were broadly comparable to clinical comparator electrodes in this controlled setting, whereas the previously heated hybrid electrode showed increased noise at low excitation amplitudes. These findings support preservation of baseline phantom‐recording capability before ablation but do not establish that postablation EEG remains clinically interpretable in vivo. Therefore, in early clinical translation, definitive electrophysiological mapping and stimulation should be performed before ablation. Postablation recordings, if obtained, should initially be considered exploratory verification signals rather than validated clinical endpoints. Formal testing of impedance stability, spectral signal quality, insulation integrity, and repeated‐ablation effects on contacts will be required before stronger claims can be made about postablation recording durability.

Limitations

This study was an engineering and translational feasibility program. The sample sizes were small, and the in vivo component was not designed to estimate complications, compare outcomes, or reproduce diverse epilepsy targets (mesial temporal structures, insula, periventricular nodules, or focal cortical dysplasia). Likewise, the contact‐adjacent PRF exclusion zone was defined from preclinical phantom and ex vivo data and should not be overinterpreted as a definitive clinical safety margin for targets requiring submillimeter precision. This issue may be particularly relevant for very small or eloquent targets, including some insular trajectories or hypothalamic hamartomas adjacent to critical structures, where the practical monitoring corridor may be narrower than in the experimental settings studied here. The canine brain anatomy and thermal properties differ from those of the human epileptogenic cortex, and lesions were created in healthy tissue rather than in epileptogenic zones with variable gliosis, dysplasia, heterotopia, vascularity, extracellular matrix remodeling, or surgical change. This limits generalizability, because abnormal epileptogenic tissue may not dissipate heat, deform under coagulation, or interact with MR thermometry in the same manner as healthy parenchyma. In particular, differences in tissue composition and vascular heat‐sink effects could modify lesion geometry, thermal dose propagation, and the practical interpretation of PRF thermometry near the treatment zone. Future translational studies should therefore include disease‐relevant tissue settings, or at minimum more heterogeneous tissue models, to determine how these substrate‐dependent factors influence safety margins, lesion predictability, and thermometry‐guided treatment planning.

We did not perform systematic 3D MRI lesion segmentation or thermometry–histology coregistration. The approximate ellipsoid volumes reported here were derived retrospectively from available 2D MRI measurements and are intended only as descriptive estimates. They cannot substitute for prospective volumetric lesion segmentation, thermal‐dose mapping, or histology‐coregistered dose–volume modeling, which will be required to establish reproducible dose–volume relationships and refine safety margins around contacts and vascular structures. In the canine experiment, the in vivo sample comprised only two animals and one unmatched reference‐applicator ablation. Thus, variability between animals and devices can only be described, not meaningfully estimated or compared statistically. In addition, the original pathology report states that tissue deformation during specimen handling and sectioning made histologic lesion measurements unreliable; histologic lesion sizes/volumes were therefore not reported. Finally, because the single Biotex lesion was performed without cooling and not within a matched control arm, the study does not support formal claims of equivalence versus standard LITT applicators. Likewise, the electrophysiology substudy was not designed as a formal quantitative validation of postthermal or postablation recording performance. Although the phantom recordings suggested acceptable baseline behavior and increased noise after prior thermal exposure, we did not prospectively measure electrode impedance, longitudinal impedance stability, spectral‐band power, quantitative SNR, insulation integrity, or clinical interpretability of postablation EEG against a standardized ablation protocol with controlled peak temperature, heating duration, cooling parameters, and repeated cycles. These questions remain important for future translational testing.

The intended “single intervention” advantage requires further discussion. Many patients require days of sEEG recording to capture seizures and characterize networks; thus, the main benefit of the hybrid electrode is a single stereotactic implantation rather than a single‐day diagnostic‐and‐treat session. Nevertheless, reducing two stereotactic procedures to one can be clinically meaningful, as it may lower procedural risk, 15 , 26 reduce resource utilization, 27 and simplify the patient pathway.

In addition, the proximal handling workflow was not formally validated in this preclinical program. Although the device was designed for a sequential pathway from sEEG recording to MRgLITT, we did not perform a dedicated study of anchoring hardware, sterile connector exchange, infection‐control procedures, or simultaneous versus sequential EEG connectivity during laser‐fiber insertion. These practical workflow components will require dedicated evaluation during first‐in‐human translation.

Selected scenarios (strong a priori hypothesis, lesional epilepsy with short confirmation recordings, or acute intraoperative confirmation paradigms) may leverage the technology toward the same procedure, sEEG confirmation and MRgLITT treatment, as experience and infrastructure evolve.

Another important point is electrical stimulation. The probe was designed to preserve the usual diagnostic functionalities of a standard sEEG electrode, and electrical stimulation is therefore expected to be feasible. However, this study was not designed to formally evaluate stimulation performance, thresholds, charge delivery, or stimulation safety. Our focus here was preclinical validation of MRI compatibility, thermometry behavior, thermal control, recording quality, and ablative feasibility. Dedicated studies will be required to characterize stimulation performance in detail and to define how this functionality may be integrated into future network‐guided or tract‐oriented ablative strategies.

Implications and next steps

These preclinical data support progression toward first‐in‐human evaluation of our patented sEEG‐laser concept (Assistance Publique–Hôpitaux de Paris; FR2955477, EP2528501, US8597338B2). A prospective early phase study should address three sets of questions: first, safety and technical performance: implantation feasibility with standard stereotactic or robotic techniques, MR artifact and thermometry fidelity in vivo, cooling stability, device integrity after ablation, and rates of hemorrhage, infection, and contact‐adjacent injury were assessed; second, workflow feasibility: the proportion of patients in whom the hybrid device allows treatment without second stereotactic implantation, impact on planning and MRI suite logistics, and ablation delivery reproducibility across centers; and third, preliminary clinical signals: seizure outcomes appropriate to etiology and target, neuropsychological profiles, and concordance between electrophysiology‐defined targets, MR thermal dose, and postablation imaging.

The broader relevance of this study is demonstrated by the adoption of MRgLITT. The neurosurgical literature indicates LITT is no longer confined to early adopters and is now implemented at many centers. 28 In epilepsy, single‐center experience and national datasets show increasing LITT utilization. 10 , 17 , 29 , 30 This procedural ecosystem suggests that if a hybrid sEEG‐LITT workflow proves safe and effective, clinical translation could be rapid, as core MRI capabilities for targeting, PRF thermometry, and thermal‐dose monitoring exist in many institutions.

From clinical and market perspectives, nonlesional focal epilepsy is a key segment in the epilepsy market. These patients often undergo sEEG, and when a focal seizure onset zone is identified, minimally invasive ablation can be attractive when open resection is high‐risk, the epileptogenic zone is deep or eloquent, or uncertainty favors stepwise “test‐and‐treat” strategies. 25 , 31 By enabling sEEG‐guided ablation without second stereotactic implantation, the hybrid electrode addresses a key barrier: the transition from invasive diagnosis to therapy. Beyond patient benefits (fewer procedures, faster treatment access), this consolidation has system‐level implications: reducing duplicated stereotactic workflows, decreasing device‐exchange steps, and potentially increasing throughput in cases where MRI suite time is limited.

Successful translation will require integration with contemporary epilepsy surgery infrastructure, including robotic stereotaxy for implantation, standardized image‐based contact localization, and software linking sEEG analysis to planned ablation segments and MR thermometry quality assurance. Combined with rigorous outcome tracking and safety reporting, a hybrid sEEG‐laser depth electrode could move focal epilepsy care toward an efficient pathway in which localization and ablation are achieved through one implanted device while preserving the advantages of MR‐guided therapy.

CONCLUSIONS

In this staged preclinical program, we demonstrated the feasibility of hybrid sEEG‐laser depth electrode‐coupled intracranial EEG recording with MRgLITT under real‐time PRF thermometry. Across phantoms, ex vivo brain tissue, and an in vivo canine model, the device exhibited limited MRI susceptibility artifacts and preserved thermometry performance outside a narrow exclusion zone around each metallic contact, whereas internal cooling prevented contact‐adjacent overheating and carbonization, enabling sharply demarcated lesions with dose‐dependent scaling.

The main procedural implication is that this electrode streamlines sEEG‐guided focal ablation by enabling a single implantation workflow in which seizure localization and MRgLITT treatment can be performed without exchanging devices or repeating stereotactic targeting. This consolidation could particularly impact patients with MRI‐negative focal epilepsies or deep/eloquent targets, where sEEG is frequently required and minimizing the procedural burden is crucial.

These data support progression to first‐in‐human evaluation to establish clinical safety, workflow feasibility, and preliminary effectiveness, alongside integration with contemporary stereotactic implantation pathways and safety reports.
