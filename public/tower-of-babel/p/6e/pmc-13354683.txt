Unicompartmental knee arthroplasty and total knee arthroplasty in patients with 15 or more degrees of varus deformity: age- and gender-matched comparative study of radiographic and clinical outcomes
Authors: Arun Mullaji, Sitaram Chopperla, George K George, Rufiat Kazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTo date, few studies have directly compared matched cohorts of patients with severe varus deformity undergoing UKA versus TKA. The current study seeks to address this gap. The hypothesis was that, in patients with deformity of > 15°, postoperative PROMs following UKA would be inferior to those achieved with TKA.MethodsRecords of patients operated for UKA and TKA from January 2016 to December 2021 were evaluated, and the data for patients with deformity of ≥ 15° were selected. The UKA patients were then matched for age and gender with TKA patients. 52 matched pairs of patients were obtained. The HKA, mLDFA, MPTA were measured. OKS, SF12, and FJS were collected. The magnitude of change in the mLDFA and MPTA was assessed using the Estimated Marginal Means.ResultsThe mean follow-up times for UKA and TKA were 3.7 years and 5.5 years, respectively. The mean deformity correction (ΔHKA) was significantly greater in TKA (16.3° ± 4.5°) than UKA (10.6° ± 3.1°) (p < 0.001), with significant changes in mLDFA and MPTA (p < 0.001). In UKAs, 68% of patients demonstrated a postoperative HKA between 171.2° and 175.7°, whereas in TKAs, values clustered between 174.5° and 179.8°. Greater deformity correction was achieved with TKA; however, UKA also resulted in substantial correction despite persistence of some residual varus in certain patients with severe preoperative deformity. Introduction

Traditionally, patients with advanced medial compartment osteoarthritis and a pronounced varus alignment, defined by a Hip-Knee-Ankle (HKA) angle exceeding 15°, have been managed with total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [1]. Unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) has historically been considered contraindicated in such large deformities, owing to concerns that increasing varus alignment is associated with a higher likelihood of disease in the contralateral tibiofemoral compartment, involvement of the patellofemoral joint, and compromise of the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL). Supporting this concern, Mullaji et al. reported that 73% of knees with varus deformity exceeding 15° showed severe grade degeneration of ACL [2]. Similarly, Matsuura et al. reported that medial compartment osteoarthritis with severe varus deformity is frequently associated with ACL insufficiency, and consequently recommended avoiding UKA in patients with marked varus alignment, given the critical role of an intact ACL in the long-term success of UKA [3].

The technique of deformity correction in TKA aims to correct deformity while achieving a balanced knee, using optimal bone resections and varying degrees of soft-tissue and ligament releases. On the other hand, UKA is essentially a resurfacing procedure with no soft tissue releases. Optimal re-tensioning of the medial collateral ligament is the aim, with no attempt made to completely correct the deformity. Therefore, large preoperative deformities may leave a postoperative residual varus in UKA [4]. The outcomes for knees with large deformity and the impact of residual varus have been described in detail for TKA [5–8]. However, there is limited data for outcomes in patients with large deformities undergoing UKA. Itou showed that perioperative varus alignment does not affect short-term outcome measures following UKA, on analysing the mean preoperative deformity of 8.5°±2.9° and the postoperative mean residual varus of 5.1 ± 3.3 [9]. Kleeblad et al. opined that, based on a limited experience with UKAs in 8 knees with > 15° varus, a cautious approach is needed in such deformities [10].

To date, few studies have directly compared matched cohorts of patients with deformity of > 15°undergoing UKA versus TKA [11]. The current study seeks to address this gap. The hypothesis was that, in patients with deformity of > 15°, postoperative patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) following UKA would be inferior to those achieved with TKA. We sought to determine our objectives by evaluating age- and gender-matched cohorts in two groups undergoing UKA or TKA for varus deformity of > 15° and analysing:

The magnitude of deformity correction obtained in both groups.

The clinical and radiological outcomes in both groups.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This was a retrospective study, where records of patients who underwent UKA and TKA from January 2016 to December 2021 were evaluated, and the data for patients with deformity of ≥ 15° were selected. The UKA patients were then matched for age and gender with TKA patients.

For UKAs, the inclusion criteria were patients who fulfilled the Oxford criteria [12] and showed a deformity ≥ 15° varus. All patients had correctable deformity with valgus stress in flexion, less than 10° of flexion deformity, and intact ACL confirmed on preoperative MRI.

For TKA, the inclusion criteria were varus deformity ≥ 15° and correctable with valgus stress. The exclusion criteria for both groups were patients with inflammatory arthritis, post-traumatic arthritis, incomplete records, inadequate radiographs, those lost to follow-ups, and deceased patients.

A 1:1 matching without replacement was performed for patient selection. No bilateral cases were included in the analysis. 52 matched pairs of patients were obtained (Fig. 1) using the case control matching command in Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS version 25.0, IBM Corporation, New York, USA) for MS Windows. All data for PROMs and radiographic measurements were entered in an Excel spreadsheet version 16.66.1 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, Washington, USA).

Demographic characteristics

Table 1 summarises the demographic characteristics of the study cohorts. Each group comprised an identical proportion of female and male patients (84% female, 16% male), minimising gender-related confounding in comparative outcome analyses. The mean age at surgery was comparable between the UKA and TKA cohorts (64.3 ± 8.7 vs. 64.7 ± 8.3 years), indicating a similar age profile at the time of intervention. The overall cohort had a mean age of 64.5 ± 8.5 years.

The mean duration of follow-up ranged between 3 and 5 years between groups, with longer follow-up in the UKA cohort (5.5 years) compared with the TKA cohort (3.7 years), resulting in an overall mean follow-up of 4.3 years.

Surgical technique

All surgeries were performed by the senior surgeon (AM). For UKA, the Oxford partial knee (Zimmer Biomet, Bridgend, United Kingdom) was used. A standard minimally invasive medial UKA surgical procedure was followed using Oxford partial knee microplasty instrumentation. Optimal medial soft tissue tension was achieved with minimal bony cuts and an appropriate insert. There was no attempt made to correct the deformity using any releases.

For TKA, the Sigma PFC (DePuy Synthes, Warsaw, Indiana, USA) was used. Standard TKA surgical procedure was followed, and all cases were performed using the computer-assisted Brainlab 2.0 navigation system (Brainlab AG, Munich, Germany). Soft tissue balancing was done using a soft tissue tensioner and confirmed with navigation. Final postoperative alignment (HKA) was aimed to be between 177°and 180°. No superficial medial collateral ligament releases were performed in any of the cases.

Outcome measures

PROMs collected in this study included the Oxford Knee Score (OKS), the Short Form-12 Health Survey (SF-12), and the Forgotten Joint Score (FJS). Preoperative PROMs were obtained at the time of hospital admission before surgery. Postoperative PROMs were collected annually from the date of surgery through structured telephonic interviews conducted by an independent professional agency that was provided with patient contact details and was experienced in PROM data collection.

Patients in both groups were categorised based on their Oxford Knee Score into four outcome categories: excellent (> 41), good (34–41), fair (27–33), and poor (< 27).

Radiological measures

The preoperative and postoperative radiological measurements for all patients were measured from full-length standing hip-knee-ankle radiographs. Patients were instructed to stand straight with both knees fully extended and to equally distribute their body weight between both limbs. The patella was aligned in the direction of the X-ray beam. The X-ray beam was centred at the distal pole of the patella, aligning the image parallel to the tibial joint line in the frontal plane. The HKA (Hip-knee angle), mLDFA (mechanical Lateral Distal Femoral Angle), MPTA (Medial Proximal Tibial Angle) were measured. HKA is measured as the medial angle between the mechanical axes of the femur and tibia. mLDFA is the lateral angle between the joint line of the distal femur and the mechanical axis of the femur. MPTA is the medial angle between the joint line of the proximal tibia and the mechanical axis of the tibia [13] (Fig. 2a and b). The magnitude of change in the mLDFA and MPTA was assessed using the Estimated Marginal Means module. Radiographic measurements were performed independently by two fellowship-trained arthroplasty surgeons, and the same observers evaluated all radiographs. The measurements were conducted in a blinded manner. Interobserver reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), which demonstrated excellent agreement between observers (ICC = 0.91).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v25.0 (IBM Corporation, USA). Normality of continuous variables was assessed separately for each group (TKA and UKA) using the Shapiro-Wilk test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered indicative of no significant deviation from normality. Among the radiological parameters, pre-operative HKA showed significant deviation from normality in both groups (TKA: p < 0.001; UKA: p = 0.009). Postoperative HKA values were normally distributed (TKA: p = 0.876; UKA: P -0.323). ΔHKA showed borderline deviation from normality in the TKA group (p = 0.047) but not in the UKA group (p = 0.987). The other radiological parameters, namely – mLDFA, MPTA, KJLO and their respective deltas were found to be normally distributed (p < 0.05 in all). Paired comparisons of preoperative and postoperative radiological measurements within each group were performed using paired t-tests, and between-group differences in radiological change (Delta(Δ)) were assessed using independent t-tests.

Repeated-measures ANOVA using the general linear model was used to analyse the radiological outcomes, with time (pre- vs. postoperative) as the within-subjects factor (2 levels) and group (TKA vs. UKA) as the between-subjects factor. This approach was retained for HKA, despite the non-normal distribution of preoperative HKA because of the large and balanced sample sizes (n = 52 per group), and the established robustness of repeated-measures ANOVA to mild-to-moderate non-normality in balanced designs (particularly in the presence of large effect sizes) [14].

As the within-subject factor comprised only two time points (preoperative and postoperative), sphericity was not applicable [15, 16]. Homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test, and equality of covariance matrices was assessed using Box’s M test. To ensure consistency across analyses and because of its relative robustness to violations of variance-covariance assumptions, repeated-measures effects for all radiological parameters were interpreted using Pillai’s Trace from the multivariate model. Between-subject group effects were obtained from the between-subjects tests. All post-operative PROMs – OKS, SF12 and FJS showed significant deviation from normality in both TKA and UKA groups (p < 0.001) and were analysed non parametrically using the Mann-Whitney U test. Correlations of preoperative deformity and postoperative residual varus with the postoperative outcomes were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation test. To account for multiple testing, the Bonferroni correction was applied. Adjusted significance thresholds were calculated by dividing 0.05 by the number of tests in the relevant family.

A post-hoc power analysis performed in G*Power 3.1.9.4 using the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test (two-sided α = 0.05) showed that the available PROM sample size provided 69% power for the observed between-group comparison. This indicates limited power to detect small between-group differences; therefore, the absence of statistically significant PROM differences should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence. Larger studies are needed to confirm these findings.

Correlation analysis demonstrated that no statistically significant correlation was detected between the magnitude of preoperative varus deformity and any postoperative outcome measure following UKA. Postoperative residual varus alignment similarly showed no statistically significant correlation with SF-12 or FJS. However, a weak but statistically significant relationship was observed between residual postoperative varus and functional scores: OKS demonstrated a weak negative correlation (r = − 0.308).

In the TKA cohort, no statistically significant correlation was observed between the severity of preoperative varus deformity and postoperative functional outcomes, including OKS, SF-12, and FJS.

Greater residual varus was associated with lower OKS (Spearman’s rho = − 0.312, p = 0.006) and lower SF-12 scores (Spearman’s rho = − 0.345, p = 0.002). After Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, only the association between postoperative HKA and SF-12 remained statistically significant, while the other correlations did not retain significance. These correlations were weak and exploratory and should be interpreted with caution. In contrast, no statistically significant correlations were observed in the TKA group.

Discussion

Principal findings

The main findings of our study were that the mean deformity correction was significantly greater in the TKA group (16.3° ± 4.5°) than in the UKA group (10.6° ± 3.1°). Although there was no statistically significant difference detected between the UKA and TKA cohorts across all assessed PROMs (OKS, SF-12, and FJS with p > 0.05 in all), equivalence cannot be concluded.

TKA achieved significantly greater deformity correction, as reflected by the change in HKA angle (ΔHKA), compared to UKA (16.27 ± 4.45 vs. 10.57 ± 3.12, respectively; p < 0.001). This likely reflects the differing alignment philosophies of the two procedures: TKA aimed for more complete deformity correction, whereas UKA typically involves controlled under-correction to maintain a more personalized alignment [17]. Despite the greater radiographic correction achieved with TKA, there were no significant differences in PROMs between groups. Alternative alignment philosophies, including functional, kinematic, inverse kinematic, and restricted kinematic alignment, typically result in residual varus compared with traditional mechanical alignment; current literature consistently demonstrates that PROMs with these approaches are non-inferior to those achieved with mechanical alignment [18–22]. In our study, patients who underwent UKA are often left with greater residual varus than in mechanically aligned TKA. This results from an absence of soft-tissue releases, combined with gap balancing and restoration of constitutional alignment, which may possibly contribute to non-inferior PROMs compared with mechanically aligned TKA.

Excellent or good OKS scores were obtained in 98% of the UKA cohort and 94% of the TKA cohort. TKA produced a larger mean HKA correction, driven predominantly by greater tibial correction as noted in the ΔMPTA (7.74+/-3.4), with all radiological parameters showing significant postoperative improvement. These findings reflect the differing alignment strategies inherent to UKA and TKA. In the UKA group, the postoperative HKA ranged between (171.17°-175.7°), whereas the TKA group had postoperative HKA values clustered between (174.5°-179.8°).

The estimated marginal means are predictions from a statistical model and estimate what the mean response would be for each group if the study were balanced and all covariates were equal. They reveal that TKAs produce a greater effect on the tibial side and substantially alter MPTA versus UKAs. In contrast, UKAs largely influence the femoral side and markedly alter mLDFA compared to TKAs.

Comparison with the literature

Elderly patients often present with multiple medical comorbidities, and an increasing comorbidity burden has been shown to adversely affect survival following TKA [23, 24]. In this context, studies have demonstrated that UKA is associated with lower perioperative morbidity, faster functional recovery, and improved cost-effectiveness when compared with TKA [25].

Traditional contraindications to UKA have also been increasingly challenged [26]. Historically, an intact ACL was considered mandatory for UKA. However, contemporary evidence suggests that a functionally competent ACL, rather than an anatomically intact ACL, is sufficient. Multiple studies have reported that ACL deficiency does not necessarily compromise clinical outcomes or implant survivorship following UKA [27].

Furthermore, studies have shown that UKA can yield favourable clinical and radiological outcomes even in patients with deformities exceeding 15° [28], provided appropriate patient selection and surgical technique are employed.

These findings support the use of UKA as a viable alternative to TKA in patients with medial osteoarthritis who are contraindicated for TKA. In such patients, where TKA may be associated with potentially increased morbidity, UKA offers the advantages of reduced surgical burden, hastened recovery, and comparable functional outcomes [25], even in the presence of significant deformity.

Itou et al., on analysing mean preoperative deformity of 8.5° ± 2.9°, found that the perioperative varus alignment does not affect short-term patient-reported outcome measures of FJS, knee injury and osteoarthritis outcome score and Knee Society Score [9]. This suggests that postoperative alignment may not be a major contributor to postoperative outcomes.

Sung-Mok Oh et al. reported that postoperative varus malalignment increases proportionally to the severity of preoperative varus deformity in TKA [6]. Aydin et al. reported satisfactory outcomes in TKA patients with residual varus, where the mean postoperative residual varus was 8.3° ± 3.6° [8]. Meta-analysis done by Wan et al. [7] showed that residual mild varus alignment after TKA may lead to similar or superior outcomes than neutral alignment, while a meta-analysis by Han et al. showed limited benefit for residual varus in TKA [5]. Neutral alignment was retained as the gold standard in both the meta-analysis. Vasso et al. reported that UKA patients with slight postoperative varus (5°-7°) had better outcomes than those with lesser varus [29]. Mullaji et al., on studying the determinants of postoperative limb alignment in UKA, showed that the more severe the preoperative varus deformity, the greater the tendency for residual postoperative varus [30]. Kleeblad et al. showed that large preoperative varus deformities (7°-18°) were corrected to optimal or acceptable alignment in 98% patients, but that a cautious approach is needed in deformities > 15° [10]. The ideal postoperative alignment target remains unknown.

Traditionally, it is believed that varus deformity of > 15° indicates a lack of ACL. Hence, even a correctable varus deformity of > 15° is not considered for UKA, and the patients undergo TKA. UKA patients are shown to achieve superior clinical and functional outcomes compared to TKA [11]. Matsuura et al. [3] investigated intraoperative ACL assessment and identified a cutoff varus angle of 11.3° as being associated with a high risk of ACL insufficiency. They concluded that UKA may be preferable for patients with medial compartment osteoarthritis and varus alignment of < 15°. In our series, UKA was performed only after confirmation of ACL integrity through both magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and intraoperative evaluation. TKA is associated with a higher rate of postoperative infection, thrombo-embolism, dissatisfaction, need for blood transfusion, higher 3-month mortality, more difficult revision for failed TKAs, and longer recovery compared to UKA [3]. Hence, patients should be counselled about the advantages and potential risks of both procedures so that an informed decision may be made.

Clinical implications

Our study demonstrates that patients with correctable varus deformities of > 15° should be carefully assessed for anteromedial osteoarthritis, ACL integrity, and fulfilment of the Oxford criteria when determining candidacy for UKA. UKA demonstrated comparable short- to mid-term PROMs in this selected cohort; however, definitive non-inferiority conclusions cannot be drawn from the current study design. Deng et al. [11], in a meta-analysis of 944 UKAs and 944 TKAs for medial compartment osteoarthritis, had reported that UKA patients achieved superior early and mid-term clinical outcomes compared with TKA, with no significant difference in revision rates. These findings support our observation that UKA may be considered a potential alternative in carefully selected patients with medial osteoarthritis. However, it is important to note that the meta-analysis did not evaluate long-term outcomes, nor did it specify the magnitude of deformity in the included cases.

The strengths of our study include the use of a matched-pair cohort design to compare UKA and TKA in patients with deformities of > 15°, thereby addressing an important gap in the literature and exploring the potential role of UKA in larger deformities of > 15°. Additionally, no revisions were observed in either cohort during the study period.

Limitations

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. A key limitation of this study is that matching was restricted to age and gender. Confounders such as BMI, comorbidities, baseline functional status, and activity level were not considered, as it would result in a smaller sample size and lead to an underpowered study. Consequently, residual confounding cannot be excluded and may have influenced the observed outcomes. A further limitation of this study is the unequal follow-up duration between the two groups, with a mean follow-up of 5.5 years in the UKA group and 3.7 years in the TKA group. This difference may have influenced the PROMs between cohorts. Although the overall mean follow-up was 4.3 years, longer-term follow-up is necessary to more accurately evaluate durability, revision risk, and long-term functional outcomes, and such follow-up is currently ongoing. Another limitation is the exclusion of alternative deformity-correction procedures, such as high tibial osteotomy, which may also be considered in similar patient populations. In addition, the retrospective design of the study introduces inherent limitations, including potential selection bias in the surgical groups. There is also a possibility of recall bias associated with telephone-based follow-up assessments. A potential ceiling effect was observed, particularly for FJS and OKS scores, which may have reduced the ability of these PROMs to detect subtle clinical differences between the UKA and TKA groups. The UKA group in this study represented a carefully selected subgroup of patients, including those with an intact ACL, correctable deformity, and suitability according to established Oxford criteria. In contrast, TKA is generally performed in a broader and more heterogeneous patient population. This difference in patient selection introduces inherent selection bias and should be considered when interpreting the findings, as it limits the direct comparability of the two procedures in the general population.

Future research

Further studies involving larger patient cohorts, with appropriate matching for potential confounders, are required to validate these findings and to evaluate long-term clinical outcomes, including Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) and Patient Acceptable Symptom State (PASS), and implant survivorship.

In conclusion, UKA performed in carefully selected patients with large varus deformities (> 15°) can provide satisfactory short- to mid-term outcomes. Although no statistically significant differences were observed in postoperative PROMs between the UKA and TKA groups, the current study was not designed or sufficiently powered to establish equivalence or detect subtle clinically meaningful differences between procedures. Greater deformity correction was achieved with TKA; however, UKA also resulted in substantial correction despite persistence of some residual varus in certain patients with severe preoperative deformity. Neither the severity of preoperative deformity nor the degree of postoperative residual varus demonstrated a statistically significant correlation with postoperative outcomes in this cohort.
