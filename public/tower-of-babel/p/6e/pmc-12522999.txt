Cultivating physical literacy and prosocial behavior through basketball: a quasi-experimental study on the Teaching Personal and Social Responsibility (TPSR) model
Authors: Bin Xie, Siman Lei, Yiren Wu, Infong Chang, Mengyun Zhao, Fangyuan Ju
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPhysical literacy and social responsibility are crucial educational outcomes that foster lifelong physical activity and prosocial behaviors. The Teaching Personal and Social Responsibility (TPSR) model, which is widely applied to cultivate a positive character through sports and movement-based activities, promotes physical competence, self-regulation, and prosocial behavior. However, research on its effectiveness in primary schools is limited. Therefore, this study aims to examine whether a TPSR-based physical education program increases physical literacy and social responsibility among primary school students.MethodsA pretest–posttest quasi-experimental design was used. A total of 130 students (65 males, 65 females; mean age = 10.9 ± 0.729) participated and were allocated into an experimental (n = 65) or a control (n = 65) group. The experimental group participated in a TPSR-based physical education program, whereas the control group followed the school’s usual physical education program. The intervention spanned 12 weeks (2 sessions per week). Pre- and post-intervention measures were conducted using the Canadian Assessment of Physical Literacy (CAPL-2) and the Personal and Personal and Social Responsibility Scale for Physical Education (RSRSPE).ResultsA total of 130 students (mean age = 10.9 ± 0.73; 65 in each group) completed the intervention. Significant Group × Time interactions were observed in the domain of physical literacy, including Physical Literacy Total Score (F = 10.298, p = 0.002, ηp² = 0.074), Physical Competence (F = 7.186, p = 0.008, ηp² = 0.053), and Daily Behavior (F = 9.577, p = 0.002, ηp² = 0.070), indicating notable improvements in students’ physical literacy. However, no statistically significant changes were found in the Knowledge and Understanding or Motivation and Confidence dimensions (p > 0.05). In terms of social responsibility, the intervention group exhibited significant improvements in Total Score for Social Responsibility (F = 8.706, p = 0.004, ηp² = 0.064), as well as in Caring (F = 6.918, p = 0.010, ηp² = 0.051), Leadership (F = 5.282, p = 0.023, ηp² = 0.040), Self-direction (F = 9.889, p = 0.002, ηp² = 0.072), and Value (F = 11.699, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.084). No significant changes were observed in Effort, Goal, Responsibility, or Respect (p > 0.05).ConclusionThis study highlights the TPSR model as an effective teaching approach with significant impact and great potential for enhancing primary school students’ physical literacy and social responsibility.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2400092289.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-23801-1.

Background

Integrating the Teaching Personal and Social Responsibility (TPSR) model into school physical education programs offers a meaningful framework for promoting holistic student development by combining physical skills with life skills, such as empathy and social responsibility. Social responsibility, which involves respecting others, fulfilling group roles, and balancing personal and collective growth, is a vital goal in contemporary education [1, 2]. The TPSR model broadens the scope of physical education (PE) to include social adaptability, highlighting the role of quality PE in addressing health disparities and advancing physical literacy. Social responsibility has emerged as a key aspect of PE, emphasizing its long-term value and societal benefits, which aligns with the principle of lifelong physical activity central to physical literacy [3, 4]. Physical literacy can be described as a set of skills including motivation, confidence, physical competence, knowledge, and understanding to value and take responsibility for lifelong engagement in daily physical activities [5]. Enhancing physical literacy improves motor skills and health behaviors while fostering self-confidence, teamwork, and social responsibility [6]. By integrating physical, mental, and social development, TPSR-based PE creates a strong foundation for cultivating positive health behaviors and establishing lifelong exercise habits [7, 8].

The TPSR model, which is rooted in psychological and educational theories, supports quality education by promoting personal growth and social responsibility. Drawing on Self-Determination Theory, it emphasizes the importance of autonomy, competence, and relatedness in cultivating intrinsic motivation [9]. It also adopts constructivist principles, encouraging student-centered learning to develop independence and accountability [10–12]. This approach aligns with broader educational goals of fostering holistic development and equity [13].

The TPSR model provides an effective instructional framework that fosters personal growth and social responsibility through structured PE. Grounded in key principles such as relationship building, empowerment, self-reflection, transfer, and gradual empowerment, the model supports the development of important life skills necessary for holistic learning [11, 14]. Its implementation involves five structured lesson phases—relationship time, where trust and rapport are built; awareness talk, guiding students to set responsibility goals; physical activity, integrating skill development with responsibility practice; group meetings, fostering collaboration; and reflection time, promoting self-assessment and values internalization [11, 15]. Through its five levels of responsibility, from respect to transfer, the TPSR model empowers students to apply the learned principles beyond the classroom, contributing to lifelong learning, equity, and social adaptability [16, 17].

TPSR uses sports to transform responsibility education from abstract theory lectures into experiential learning. Through practical activities and reflective sessions, students engage in responsible behaviors within structured physical activities [18]. As an engaging and alternative way of teaching, TPSR promotes participation and is an ideal pedagogical model for PE [19]. Additionally, it encourages students to transfer responsibility beyond sports by fostering teamwork, self-discipline, and social responsibility in their daily lives through reflection and discussion. This real-world application makes TPSR an effective model for responsibility and life skill education [20]. Studies have shown that the TPSR model significantly enhances students’ intrinsic motivation in sports [21] and outperforms direct teaching methods in fostering positive psychosocial outcomes in PE [22]. Its integration with sports such as basketball enhances affective, cognitive, and social skills [23] in addition to improving physical literacy. Furthermore, basketball offers an ideal environment for developing physical literacy while fostering physical activity, motor competence, and fitness in children aged 8–12 [24].

Studies have shown that incorporating TPSR into classroom teaching can significantly improve students’ prosocial behaviors, making it one of the most effective value-based models [25]. A systematic review with meta-analyses [26] that examined eight studies on school PE programs revealed limited focus in this area. The findings indicated that, on average, there were two sessions per week (approximately 60 min each), following the suggestions summarized in [19], which are valuable for designing TPSR-based interventions. These sessions primarily involved students over the age of 11 years, predominantly in Spain. The TPSR model significantly impacted all the assessed variables, underscoring the necessity for further research in diverse school settings with different age and cultural contexts.

Limited empirical evidence exists on the implementation of TPSR within structured, culturally diverse physical education programs, particularly in the context of China. This gap is compounded by a scarcity of theory-driven interventions that promote multidimensional development (physical, cognitive, and affective domains) of physical literacy. Quasi-experimental designs are well-suited for evaluating such interventions due to robust internal validity and practicality in school settings, where randomization is often infeasible due to logistical constraints or curriculum requirements.

In this study, basketball was selected as the intervention sport because the fast-paced, team-based nature of the game fosters physical competence through dynamic movements (e.g., running, jumping, and agile changes in direction) while promoting values aligned with TPSR, such as teamwork, respect, and personal responsibility. Unlike individual sports, the high interactivity of basketball amplifies opportunities to cultivate social responsibility. The widespread popularity of basketball in schools in Southern China and the structured 3 × 3 format enhance feasibility and student engagement, making the sport an ideal vehicle for interventions based on TPSR. Thus, this quasi-experimental study examines the dual effects of the TPSR model on physical literacy and social responsibility among primary school students, aged 10–12 years, within the school-based 3 × 3 basketball program, addressing the need for culturally and developmentally tailored interventions.

Methods

Research design

This study adopted a quasi-experimental pretest–posttest design to investigate whether the TPSR model significantly improves physical literacy and the sense of social responsibility among primary school students aged 10–12 years. The experimental group (EG) participated in 12 weeks of TPSR-based basketball training, while the control group (CG) learned with a traditional direct instruction approach to basketball. Both groups attended the intervention in the same school and were instructed by two different teachers. Pretest and posttest measures were used to evaluate the outcomes. To ensure validity, consistency was maintained in the class duration, frequency, venue, and equipment throughout the intervention. This controlled approach ensured that any observed differences could be attributed to teaching models rather than to external factors.

Participants

To ensure consistency in institutional context and minimize environmental variability and enhancing internal validity, participants were recruited from a single primary school in Macao. The selection of this school was based on its accessibility and logistical feasibility. In addition to anthropometric characteristics, key sociodemographic factors—such as students’ weekly physical activity participation, academic performance (as rated by teachers), and family socioeconomic status—were taken into consideration when selecting participants. Prior to the start of teaching intervention, the research team conducted preliminary meetings with the school principal and PE teachers to discuss the study’s objectives, duration, teacher training requirements, and potential impacts on student learning outcomes.

Participants were selected based on the following inclusion and exclusion criteria. Eligible individuals were primary school students aged 10–12 who could safely engage in physical activities without substantial health risks, communicate effectively in Chinese, and provide written informed consent from their parent or legal guardian. Only children whose parents/guardians agreed to join the 12-week intervention (two 60-minute sessions per week) were allowed to participate (with freedom to withdraw at any time). Students who the school and teacher approve due to physical or psychological conditions that limit their ability to participate in PE fully were excluded.

A total of 133 students met the eligibility criteria. During the screening process, two students were excluded because their physical fitness levels did not meet the requirements. In the subsequent enrollment process, one student withdrew for family reasons. A total of 130 students from 5th and 6th grades (aged 10.9 ± 0.729), including 65 boys and 65 girls, participated in the study. The experimental procedure and the specific number of participants in the selection and allocation processes can be found in the study flowchart (see Fig. 1). To reduce selection bias while preserving the natural classroom environment, two intact classes were used instead of randomly assigning students to intervention and control groups. This approach minimized schedule disruptions and contamination risks from mixing students exposed to the TPSR-based basketball intervention with controls [27]. Groups were matched on sociodemographic variables (physical activity, academic performance, parental education) and baseline outcomes (physical literacy, social responsibility), with repeated-measures ANOVA controlling for pre-existing differences [28].Fig. 1Study flow chart

Study flow chart

The two PE teachers in this study voluntarily participated in the experimental (EG) and control (CG) groups, respectively. Both teachers had over five years of teaching experience. The teacher of the control group had no prior exposure to the TPSR model, whereas the teacher of EG had undergone continuing professional development training in TPSR, gained hands-on experience implementing the model, and received an award recognizing their excellence in pedagogy. This teacher demonstrated a strong commitment to enhancing students’ physical literacy and social responsibility through PE to improve pedagogical quality. In the present study, the fidelity of TPSR implementation was assessed using a modified version of the Teaching Assessment and Reflection Tool (TARE) developed by Wright [29]. Prior to participation, both teachers provided informed consent, ensuring student privacy and maintaining their commitment throughout the 12-week intervention.

Intervention measures

Experimental group (TPSR Model)

The EG, consisting of 65 students (33 boys and 32 girls), underwent the 12-week TPSR-based 3 × 3 school basketball program. For the content design of the complete 12-week intervention course, see Supplementary Table 1S. The aim of the intervention was to create a classroom atmosphere that fostered responsibility and implement appropriate intervention strategies. Each weekly session followed five structured lesson phases in the TPSR lesson framework [11], with phase 5 (Reflection Time) actively incorporated from the beginning to reinforce self-awareness and personal growth. Throughout the program, students progressively engaged in responsibility at different levels, initially developing fundamental respect for others, advancing toward self-direction and leadership skills, and ultimately transferring their sense of responsibility beyond the classroom to daily life. This structured progress aimed to nurture students’ comprehensive social responsibility. For instance, in Week 7, TPSR session integrated five key components: brief teacher-student interactions, to foster trust and a positive climate (Relationship Time); goal setting and discussions on autonomy and responsibility (Responsibility Awareness Dialogue); skill-based basketball drills such as dribble-layup combinations and 2 × 2 plays emphasizing collaboration (Physical Activity Plan); peer-led discussions on practice strategies and leadership (Group Meeting); and structured reflection through guided questions, to enhance self-awareness and intrinsic motivation (Reflection Time). For sample TPSR lesson plans, see Supplementary Table 2S. To evaluate fidelity in the EG, the tool’s self-report format was completed by the EG’s teacher following each session; it included structured ratings across multiple TPSR-aligned behaviors (e.g., promoting student autonomy, fostering respect, integrating responsibility themes, and conducting awareness talks). The responses were scored on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Never”) to 5 (“Always”), covering 8 core dimensions of TPSR teaching practice. The teacher completed this tool weekly and results indicated consistently high levels of TPSR behavior implementation (average domain scores ranging from 4.2 to 4.8 on a 5-point scale), please see Supplementary File 6S.

Control group (General physical education program)

The CG, comprising 65 students (32 boys and 33 girls), received direct and structured PE instruction characterized by a teacher-led approach that prioritized the development of physical skills and fitness concepts. The CG participated in PE classes based on the national curriculum, with a primary focus on a school-based 3 × 3 basketball program.

This approach was not influenced by any aspect or principle of the TPSR model and did not specifically address the development of sense of responsibility or prosocial behavior.

In the conventional teaching model for primary PE, lessons are structured into three teacher-led phases: warm-up, skill development, and cool-down. The warm-up phase involves teacher-directed exercises to prepare the students for physical activity. In the skill development phase, students engage in structured practice to improve their specific physical skills through tasks guided by the teacher. The cool-down phase includes stretching and relaxation exercises to transition students to a calm state.

Throughout the lessons, the CG teacher maintained full control over the timing, sequence, and content of activities, with no decision making demanded from the students. To confirm that the usual PE teacher did not exhibit TPSR-related behaviors, three randomly selected lessons were observed and rated by the research team using the TARE observation rubric. Results showed that the teacher scored below 2 (on a 1–5 scale) across key TPSR dimensions, suggesting minimal risk of TPSR contamination in the control condition.

Measures

Participants’ demographic characteristics

The baseline demographics of the participants are summarized as follows. The EG (N = 65) exhibited a mean height of 144.6 ± 8.88 cm, mean weight of 40.0 ± 12.1 kg, and a Body Mass Index (BMI) of 18.9 ± 3.85. The CG (N = 65) recorded a mean height of 144.0 ± 8.16 cm, mean weight of 40.3 ± 10.5 kg, and a BMI of 19.0 ± 3.64. Independent-sample t-tests showed no significant group differences in these baseline anthropometric measures (p > 0.05), suggesting that the two groups were comparable prior to the intervention, as shown in Table 3.

Canadian assessment of physical Literacy – Second edition (CAPL-2)

The participants’ physical literacy was assessed using the Chinese version of the Canadian Assessment of Physical Literacy – Second Edition (CAPL-2) [30]. The Chinese version of the CAPL-2 consists of four core aspects: Physical Competence, Motivation and Confidence, Knowledge and Understanding, and Daily Behavior. Scores for each aspect were aggregated to generate a total score out of 100. The CAPL-2 categorizes children’s physical literacy into four levels: “Beginning,” “Progressing,” “Achieving,” and “Excelling,” effectively distinguishing different stages of physical literacy development. The training and support materials for implementing CAPL-2 are freely accessible on the official CAPL-2 website (www.capl-eclp.ca). These resources include a comprehensive administration manual, a quick-start guide for protocol implementation, and training videos illustrating each protocol.

The construct validity of the CAPL-2 has been established through confirmatory factor analysis in large samples, supporting a four-factor correlated model with satisfactory fit indices (CFI = 0.947; RMSEA = 0.041) [31]. The Chinese version of CAPL-2 has demonstrated robust structural validity, with statistical indicators as follows: chi-square (χ² = 70.16, df = 43, p < 0.05), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) = 0.04, 90% CI (0.024–0.062), Comparative Fit Index (CFI) = 0.94, and Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) = 0.90. These results confirmed the scale’s suitability for evaluating children’s physical literacy [25]. Table 1 provides detailed information on measurement aspects, content, scoring methods, reliability, and validity. Physical competence was assessed using three fitness tests as secondary outcomes: handgrip strength, sit-and-reach test (flexibility), and standing long jump. These measures are aligned with the multidimensional scope of physical literacy, which integrates motor skills and physical fitness into holistic health and movement competencies.

Personal and social responsibility scale for physical education

Social responsibility was measured using the Personal and Social Responsibility Scale for Physical Education (RSRSPE), a widely applied tool in adolescent personal and social responsibility research known for its strong internal consistency and predictive validity [32]. This scale, developed in [11], was adapted in this study to assess the aspects of caring, participation and effort, goal setting, leadership, and respect (using a 5-point Likert scale) to evaluate the participants’ development in these key areas during the intervention period.

The RSRSPE was originally developed for school settings and was subsequently adapted to suit the language level of primary school students. The structural validity of the instrument has been supported in previous research. Specifically, Wong et al. [33] conducted a confirmatory factor analysis and confirmed the two-factor structure of the RSRSPE, reporting satisfactory model fit indices: χ² = 112.45, df = 89, p < 0.05, Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) = 0.04, 90% CI (0.024–0.062), Comparative Fit Index (CFI) = 0.94, and Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) = 0.90. In addition, the RSRSPE demonstrated strong internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.832 to 0.902, supporting its reliability and predictive validity. Overall, the RSRSPE comprises 46 statements organized into eight distinct subscales: Goal setting (10 items), Responsibility (6 items), Respecting the rights and feelings of others (6 items), Participation and effort (6 items), Self-direction (8 items), Caring (6 items), Leadership (8 items), and Value (6 items). Participants respond on a five-point Likert scale (1 = “Almost never” to 5 = “Always”) with a higher score reflecting more responsible behavior. Collectively, these eight dimensions capture responsible behavior in the context of PE.

Procedures

Professional training for teachers

Professional training for EG teachers is a key factor influencing the success of such intervention programs and can increase the fidelity of implementation and its impact on students [34].

The training program consisted of three main components. First, it reinforced the theoretical foundations of the TPSR model, its five instructional phases [11], and the application of nine teaching strategies, such as setting clear expectations, offering choices, and providing feedback. Given that the teacher had prior training and experience as a TPSR instructor, this component served as a refresher to ensure model fidelity. Second, the teacher was assisted in developing a school-based 3 × 3 basketball program tailored to integrate the TPSR principles into the PE curriculum. This program was designed to align with the study’s objectives while engaging students in a dynamic and structured sport environment. Finally, the training emphasized techniques for effective student communication and general pedagogical principles for leading group activities [35], further enhancing the teacher’s ability to foster a responsibility-based learning environment.

This systematic monitoring confirmed that the TPSR-based PE program, including the 3 × 3 basketball lessons, was implemented as intended.

TPSR-Based PE program implementation (Intervention)

The TPSR-based 3 × 3 basketball program for primary school students was designed by aligning the structure of each lesson with the five phases of the TPSR model, and embedding responsibility objectives into basketball skill development. Lessons incorporated an awareness phase to establish behavioral expectations, emphasizing their relevance to the TPSR level (e.g., respect and effort). During the skill development phase, basketball fundamentals (dribbling and passing) were paired with responsibility tasks, such as peer encouragement and rule adherence. In the application phase, students engaged in 3 × 3 games, practicing teamwork and leadership through collaborative decision-making (e.g., devising strategies, resolving conflicts). Responsibility was further reinforced by assigning rotating roles (e.g., team leaders and members) and encouraging accountability for individual and group actions. Lessons concluded with a reflection phase in which students recalled and analyzed their performance, discussed challenges, and connected classroom learning to real-world scenarios. Teaching strategies, such as providing structured choices (e.g., selecting actions and game tactics) and immediate feedback, were woven into activities to foster autonomy and self-regulation. This integration ensured that skill progression and responsibility development advanced simultaneously, thereby creating a cohesive learning experience. Statistical analysis

Data from the completed questionnaires were entered into IBM SPSS Statistics 24.0. Initially, the Shapiro–Wilk test (p > 0.05) confirmed that all scores were normally distributed. Subsequently, descriptive statistics, including the mean (M) and standard deviation (SD), were computed for both pretest and posttest data to characterize the central tendency and variability. Finally, the difference between pretest and posttest scores (Δ) was calculated to assess the extent of change attributed to the intervention.

All outcome variables were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA with group (EG vs. CG) as the between-subjects factor and time (pre vs. post) as the within-subjects factor. Group × Time interactions were reported as the primary indicator of intervention effectiveness. Descriptive change scores (Δ) were included to support interpretation, although they were not used in inferential testing. To evaluate the practical significance of the intervention effects, partial eta squared (ηp²) was calculated. According to Cohen’s guidelines, an ηp² value of 0.01 represents a small effect size, 0.06 a medium effect size, and 0.13 a large effect size. The significance level was set at p < 0.05, and the results were appropriately marked to maintain the scientific rigor and reliability of the statistical analysis.

Missing data

All participants were included in the preliminary analyses. For scale-based questionnaire data, if less than 15% of the items on a subscale were missing, prorated scoring was applied using the mean of completed items within that subscale. For more extensive missingness, to reduce potential bias due to data missing not at random, multiple imputation with five iterations was applied. Prior to imputation, we conducted Little’s missing completely at random test to examine the pattern of missing data. The results indicated that data were missing completely at random (χ² = 52.34, df = 49, p = 0.345). The overall proportion of missing data was low (< 5%), distributed across individual questionnaire items. All imputed values were used in subsequent analyses, including the repeated-measures ANOVA. For the physical ability component of physical literacy, missing values were supplemented by incorporating grip strength and standing long jump measurements, thereby offering a comprehensive reflection of changes in that dimension, please see Supplementary Table 4S.

Ethical considerations

Strict ethical protocols were followed throughout the intervention to ensure patient compliance and integrity. All teachers participated voluntarily after receiving detailed information on the study’s objectives, procedures, and potential outcomes. Given that the student participants were minors, written informed consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians to ensure informed and voluntary participation. The study was prospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry under the registration number ChiCTR2400092289 to ensure transparency and traceability. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Medical School at Yangzhou University, with the approval number YXYLL-2024-109, and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants could withdraw at any stage without affecting their grades or PE.

Figure 2 shows the changes in the Physical Competence dimension of PL before and after the TPSR intervention. A repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a statistically significant Group × Time interaction for the overall physical competence score (F = 7.186, p = 0.008, ηp² = 0.053), indicating that the change from pretest to posttest differed significantly between groups. Discussion

The results of this study provide compelling evidence that the TPSR intervention significantly enhanced both physical literacy and social responsibility among primary school students. In the EG, notable improvements were observed in three components of physical literacy: physical competence, motivation and confidence, and daily behaviors. Among these, the most significant gains were observed in the plank exercise for core strength and in agility and movement skills, as reflected by the CAMSA total scores. Similarly, improvements in social responsibility were most pronounced in the Value, Self, and Caring aspects. The observed improvements in these areas align closely with recent studies that applied TPSR in educational settings, particularly in PE [2, 36, 37].

Building on the established effectiveness of TPSR, the current study demonstrates its successful integration within a basketball-based PE program—thereby expanding the TPSR model applicability beyond traditional responsibility education contexts. While numerous existing studies embed TPSR within the Self-Determination Theory framework [38], our approach illustrates how sport-specific interventions can simultaneously foster both physical literacy and social responsibility. Basketball, as a dynamic, team-based, and unpredictable sport, offers natural opportunities to promote decision-making, cooperation, and resilience, all of which represent core competencies embedded in physical literacy. This aligns with Edwards et al.’s [39] assertion that “physical literacy is both a prerequisite for and a product of physical activity.”. In the present study, the TPSR basketball intervention produced statistically significant gains in movement competence (e.g., CAMSA Skill Score: Δ = +5.61, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.124), daily physical activity (Δ = +1.13 steps, p = 0.002, ηp² = 0.070), and physical literacy overall (Δ = +12.1, p = 0.002, ηp² = 0.074). These results provide empirical support for TPSR’s dual impact on cognitive-behavioral development and physical literacy through a sport-integrated framework.

Building on this framework, students in the EG showed significant improvements in physical literacy indicators such as CAMSA Skill Scores and plank test performance. These results can be attributed to the 12-week intervention program, which included the learning and practice of basketball skills such as dribbling and shooting. These activities required students to frequently engage in jumping and directional changes; these are high-intensity exercises that play a critical role in enhancing agility, reaction speed, lower limb strength, and core muscle development.

The CG exhibited a significantly greater improvement in hand grip strength compared to the EG. This result may be attributed to differences in pedagogical emphases between the two instructional models. The traditional PE approach used in the CG emphasized repetitive technical drills, such as stationary dribbling and ball-handling exercises, which are known to enhance forearm and hand musculature [40]. In contrast, the TPSR-based intervention was focused on cultivating students’ personal and social responsibility through dynamic, cooperative basketball-based activities. While these lessons maintained moderate physical intensity, they prioritized interpersonal engagement and reflective learning over isolated skill repetition. Consequently, the reduced emphasis on high-volume, strength-specific drills in the EG may have led to limited improvements in grip strength relative to the CG. This suggests that the TPSR model, with its emphasis on responsibility education and high-intensity activities, facilitates a more comprehensive improvement in physical competence. These findings align with those of Camiré and Santos [41], who reported that TPSR programs significantly promote the development of sports skills, particularly when responsibility development is integrated into the curriculum.

Beyond physical competence, motivation is another critical factor in student development, which plays a key role in sustaining engagement in physical activity. Although improvements in the motivation subdimension did not reach statistical significance, the EG exhibited positive trends post-intervention, supporting the view that the TPSR model may be more effective than traditional teaching in enhancing students’ intrinsic motivation. This aligns with prior research highlighting TPSR’s ability to foster positive psychosocial outcomes [22, 42, 43]. The TPSR approach, with its focus on autonomy-supportive and responsibility-oriented teaching, appears to stimulate greater motivation for physical activity, even in the absence of statistically significant results [44, 45]. In summary, this study demonstrated that students participating in the intervention program were able to develop physical literacy while pursuing health-related goals as part of their lifelong physical activity journey [46].

Beyond physical literacy, research has shown that TPSR can be effective in imbibing a sense of social responsibility in students [47]. The EG demonstrated significant improvements in values, leadership, and self-responsibility, further validating TPSR’s effectiveness. As noted by Hemphill and Wright [48], TPSR’s structured goal setting and reflective phases not only help students develop a sense of accountability for their actions, but also enhance their awareness and care for others and their team. In particular, the significant progress observed in the self-responsibility subdimension in this study was closely tied to teachers’ guidance in incorporating responsibility-based tasks throughout the program. This highlights the critical role of teachers as facilitators of responsibility in Hellison’s model [11].

Furthermore, TPSR not only improved students’ self-responsibility but also enhanced their behavior, consistent with findings from previous research [35, 49]. In the values subdimension, the EG’s significant improvement aligns with the “beyond the classroom” level of Hellison’s Five Levels of Responsibility model [11]. This phenomenon of “responsibility transfer beyond the classroom” has been widely recognized in TPSR-related literature. For instance García-Castejón et al. [23], highlighted that the success of value education depends largely on the inclusion of reflective and discussion-based activities in the curriculum. Additionally, the integration of leadership roles and decision-making responsibilities in the intervention significantly enhanced students’ leadership skills and sense of responsibility. The improvement in the leadership subdimension observed in the EG in this study can be attributed to the explicit design of group tasks and role assignments, which provided students with opportunities to practice leadership and further strengthened their teamwork skills [50]. In addition, teachers engaged students in discussions related to responsibility at the beginning of the program, encouraged them to set personal learning goals, and incorporated responsibility-based tasks into specific teaching activities (e.g., basketball dribbling and shooting drills). These tasks, such as helping peers complete technical movements, effectively created a responsibility-focused learning atmosphere. This approach supported students’ comprehensive development of both motor skills and social responsibility, a perspective that has been fully validated in related studies [51, 52]. Taken together, these findings reinforce the role of TPSR as a valuable pedagogical framework for integrating responsibility education into physical activity programs.

Although statistically significant improvements were not observed in “Knowledge and Understanding” and several dimensions of responsibility, these findings can be understood in light of developmental, instructional, and methodological considerations. The absence of statistically significant improvements in “Knowledge and Understanding” may be attributable to the relatively low emphasis placed on explicit reflection and transfer activities, which are considered crucial components for promoting this domain within the TPSR framework [2]. Additionally, recent TPSR-based research suggests that the development of “Knowledge and Understanding” in elementary-age students may be difficult to capture through standard survey instruments, which are often less sensitive to nuanced conceptual growth [53].

Likewise, the limited changes observed in the responsibility dimensions may reflect the participants’ developmental stage. Our findings are consistent with Manzano-Sánchez et al. [54] who highlighted that younger students, such as those in primary education, may lack the psychological maturity to fully demonstrate measurable gains in high-level responsibility behaviors. Furthermore, Pascual et al. [55] emphasized that advanced responsibility dimensions necessitate a higher degree of instructional fidelity, sustained modeling, and sufficient time to internalize such behavioral change. Given the short intervention duration and moderate implementation fidelity observed in the present study, these results are expected. Moreover, it is plausible that the employed quantitative measures did not fully capture the behavioral improvements demonstrated by students.

The findings of present study have several implications for implementing TPSR within primary school contexts. First, by aligning responsibility goals with national curriculum standards and weekly lesson objectives, TPSR principles can be embedded into existing PE curricula. This requires collaboration between school administrators and PE teachers to designate specific lessons for leadership practice, peer support, and structured reflection [56]. Second, effective implementation hinges on teacher readiness. Training programs should not only introduce the TPSR framework but also emphasize strategies for facilitating student autonomy, managing reflective discussions, and modeling respectful interactions [57]. Regular coaching and peer observation can help sustain fidelity to TPSR principles. Lastly, enhancing the transferability of TPSR-learned skills beyond PE classes is essential. By setting goals and offering reinforcement across settings, teachers can encourage students to apply personal and social responsibility in classroom teamwork, home routines, and community activities [58]. Coordinated school-wide initiatives that adopt TPSR values across subject areas may further support skill generalization and long-term impact.

In summary, our findings reinforce the role of TPSR as a valuable pedagogical framework. The findings of this study not only validate the effectiveness of the TPSR model in promoting both physical literacy and social responsibility but also provide insights for the innovative development of school and community-based education models. The theoretical foundations supporting TPSR, including Basic Psychological Needs Theory [9], Self-Efficacy Theory [59], and Positive Youth Development Theory [60], underscore the model’s potential to meet students’ psychological needs and foster a sense of social responsibility. By facilitating the transfer of responsibility from classroom to daily life, TPSR helps cultivate future citizens with strong social responsibility, reinforcing its potential to promote mental health and social inclusion.

Study limitations and future work

The limitations of current study pertain to potential teacher-related variability beyond years of experience. In fact, although both instructors had formal PE training, factors such as teaching style, personality traits, and the quality of teacher-student rapport may have influenced student engagement and responsiveness to the TPSR intervention. Prior studies have shown that teacher enthusiasm, modeling of responsible behavior, and relational competence are critical moderators of TPSR effectiveness [61]. The present study did not measure these attributes, thus, it cannot fully account for their possible impact.

Future studies could adopt longitudinal designs and extend the intervention period to better capture the long-term effects of the TPSR model. Researchers might also investigate the TPSR approach across different cultural contexts and sports, as well as in younger or older age groups, to explore its adaptability and generalizability. Incorporating mixed-method methodologies could provide deep understanding in both the quantitative outcomes and qualitative experiences of students and teachers. Finally, investigating technology-based tools or other innovative strategies could help reinforce students’ knowledge, understanding, and sense of responsibility both inside and outside the PE setting.

Moreover, future research should pay closer attention to the role of students’ intrinsic motivation in the development of physical literacy. Characterized by engaging in activities for inherent satisfaction, intrinsic motivation is known to sustain long-term physical engagement. One promising avenue is the integration of structured motivational techniques based on Self-Determination Theory. Ahmadi et al. [62] proposed a comprehensive classification of 57 motivational teacher behaviors, systematically aligned with the support of autonomy, competence, and relatedness. These behaviors, such as offering meaningful choices, providing rationales, and encouraging independent learning, could form the foundation for designing targeted interventions to enhance students’ motivational quality. Incorporating this taxonomy in PE programs may improve motivation and the effectiveness and replicability of interventions aimed at promoting physical literacy.

In addition, future research should consider more precise methods for assessing and ensuring teacher fidelity to TPSR principles. While the present study employed a self-report version of the TARE tool to capture key instructional behaviors, the reliance on teacher self-evaluation rather than direct observation may have limited the depth and objectivity of fidelity measurement. More robust approaches could include continuous video recording with independent coding by trained observers, triangulated with teacher self-reflection logs and student feedback surveys. Fidelity checklists aligned with TPSR lesson components (e.g., relational time, awareness talks, group meetings) can further standardize fidelity ratings. Additionally, conducting inter-rater reliability analyses and fidelity benchmarking across multiple classes or schools could help identify implementation thresholds associated with program success [2].

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the feasibility of applying the TPSR model to enhance both physical literacy and social responsibility. The EG showed statistically significant improvements in physical competence, motivation, confidence, and daily behavior as well as in key aspects of social responsibility, including leadership, self-responsibility, and values. These findings support the effectiveness of TPSR in fostering holistic student development and highlight its potential for broader applications in PE programs.
