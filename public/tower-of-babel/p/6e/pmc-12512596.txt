Morphometric changes in overweight horses following 10-week weight loss programs
Authors: Anna Garland, David A van Doorn, Robin van den Boom, Ellen Roelfsema, Lola Jung, Madeline Boast, Katerina Papadakis, Mary Margiotta, Samara Wafelbakker, Morgan Briggs, Persephone McCrae, Wendy Pearson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUp to 70% of horses in domestic equine populations are overweight. Promotion of weight loss is an important component of limiting the detrimental co-morbidities associated with excess adipose tissue. The use of a body condition score (BCS) can help equine caregivers track their horse’s weight-status. However, this single score doesn’t reflect changes in individual adipose depots. The purpose of this study was to characterize morphometric changes (including 8 anatomic BCS measurements that make up the whole-BCS) in horses during 3 different weight-loss programs over 10 weeks.MethodsThirty-two horses were randomly assigned to one of three weight loss groups [exercise (E; fed to 100% digestible energy (DE) requirements, exercise 5x/week), feed restriction (FR; fed to 85% DE requirements, no exercise), or feed restriction and exercise (FRE; 85% DE, exercise 5x/week)], or a weight-maintained control (C; 100% DE, no exercise)] for 10 weeks. Horses were fed individualized diets formulated to meet their nutritional requirements. Outcome measures were recorded at weeks 1, 5 and 10, and included 11 morphometric measurements [body weight (BW) via weight tape, cresty neck score (CNS), and 8 individual BCSs (neck, shoulder, leg, withers, ribs, back, haunches, and tailhead) and a whole-BCS)]. Data were analyzed using a 2-Way RM ANOVA with respect to treatment group and week.ResultsAll 4 groups (including controls) had a significant reduction in BW and whole BCS. At week 10 whole BCS was lower in FRE than controls. All groups had a reduction in back, haunches, leg and shoulder; FR, FRE and E horses had reductions in rib BCS; E and FRE horses had reductions in tailhead BCS and CNS.ConclusionIn conclusion, all weight loss strategies produced significant weight loss, but only those strategies which included exercise reduced fat deposits known to be implicated in metabolic dysregulation in obese horses. Future studies should explore the optimum exercise load (duration and intensity) to facilitate loss of fat from these areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05032-z.Keywords: Equine, Body condition scoring, Adipose tissue, Weight-loss, Exercise, Feed restriction

Background

Approximately 20–70% of domesticated horses are overweight [1, 2]. Excess accumulation of adipose tissue (AT) is a risk factor for co-morbidities such as chronic inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and osteoarthritis [3]. Therefore, refining strategies to reduce excess body fat is an important step to protect robust health in horses.

In humans, the AT phenotype can be influenced by both diet and exercise, with regular exercise contributing to an anti-inflammatory tissue profile and excess AT accumulation contributing to a pro-inflammatory profile [4–7]. There are two primary classifications of white AT, based on where the fat is stored, i.e. visceral (surrounding internal organs) or subcutaneous (SAT; under the skin) depots. In horses, body condition scoring (BCS) is a simple, non-invasive system used to track individual horses’ conditioning status (i.e. underweight, healthy weight, overweight). This assessment is done by palpating the horse’s SAT depots and giving the horse an overall score. While the whole-body BCS is a useful tool, it does not reflect changes in individual SAT depots. Horses have a few unique SAT depots, including the nuchal ligament and tailhead fat. The horse’s nuchal crest [measured as an increase in cresty neck score (CNS)] and tailhead have both been previously associated with increased risk for metabolic dysregulation [8, 9]. Nuchal ligament AT is composed of white adipocytes, and an increase in CNS is associated with increased inflammation, demonstrated through hypertrophic adipocytes and infiltrating macrophages and leukocytes [10, 11]. An increase in tailhead AT is also associated with metabolic dysregulation [9, 12, 13].

Weight loss strategies typically focus on feed restriction, exercise, or a combination of both. Previous studies have used a variety of weight-loss strategies, with program length ranging from 4 to 12 weeks, restricting energy consumption to 82.5–85% digestible energy (DE), or 70% metabolizable energy (ME) of requirements, and exercising horses in low, moderate or intense workloads, from 3 to 7 days a week [14–18]. These studies have all focused on general morphometric changes, including changes in body weight (BW), a whole body BCS, CNS, and/or fat mass. Results in these previous studies have all shown programs were successful in reducing these measurements. Weight loss programs comparing feed restriction and exercise with similar target energy losses have a similar effect on morphometric measurements [14]. Contrasting results have been found when comparing a feed restriction only program with a combination of low-intensity exercise and feed restriction program, with one study showing no significant differences between group reductions in morphometric measurements [16], while another showed the combined feed restriction + exercise group had greater declines in morphometric measurements compared to the feed restriction alone [17]. However, a single BCS does not reflect changes in individual adipose depots, which may have implications for metabolic health of horses during and following weight loss Therefore, the purpose of this study was to characterize morphometric changes in response to 3 different weight-loss programs including changes in body weight (BW), CNS, an overall BCS, individual BCS for 8 anatomical locations (neck, shoulder, leg, withers, ribs, back, haunches and tailhead) over 10 weeks.

Material & methods

Horses & housing

Thirty-two mix breed horses, including 4 geldings and 28 mares, aged 7 to 21 years from the Arkell Equine Research Station (University of Guelph, Guelph, ON, Canada) were enrolled in the 10-week study [Table 1]. Horses were conditioned to become overweight (determined as a BCS of ≥ 7/9, on a 9-point scale) during a 16-week weight-gain program immediately prior to enrollment as outlined in a thesis by Garland [19]. During the weight gain period, horses were fed a nutritionally balanced diet, as determined by a commercial online feed evaluation program (FeedXL; FeedXL; Tamworth New South Wales, Australia) consisting of ad libitum hay, Trimax, Horse Plus (both from Purina, Strathroy, Ontario, Canada), trace mineral salt (Canadian Stockman, Mississauga, ON, Canada), and Canola Oil (No Name, Loblaws, ON, Canada) such that horses were provided with 120% of their DE for maintenance. The first cohort (n = 15 horses) was studied from May to July 2022 and the second cohort (n = 17 horses) from September to November 2022. During the weight-gain program, horses were categorized into a weight classification (class) by how quickly they reached the desired BCS. Class 1 started the conditioning period at a BCS of at least 7/9, class 2 met the desired BCS of 7/9 during the first 6-weeks, and class 3 met the desired BCS of 7/9 after the first 6-weeks [19].

Horses were group-housed in a large loafing barn bedded with deep-litter straw. The barn was freely accessed by the horses from an outdoor dry lot of approximately 2,000 m2. All horses had no forced exercise for at least 12 months prior to starting the 10-week weight loss trial. Horses had ad libitum access to water and trace mineral salt blocks, and were individually fed hay three times a day (600 h, 1200 h, 1800 h), with a meal supplement at 1200 h (see Table 2 and Feeding Program below).

Treatment groups

Horses were blocked by cohort, sex and class, then randomly placed into one of four groups (n = 8 horses/group). Treatment groups included: Control (C), Feed Restriction (FR), Exercise (E), and Feed Restriction plus Exercise (FRE) (Table 3). C and E horses were fed 100% of their Digestible Energy (DE) requirements (as estimated by FeedXL) and FR and FRE horses were fed 85% DE. Horses in the E and FRE groups were exercised 5x/week (expending ~15% DE)[20-22], C and FR horses did not undergo forced exercise. To minimize the number of animals required for this study, the four C horses from cohort 1 were re-used in cohort 2, with each being placed into one of the four groups.

Feeding program

Diets were formulated for each individual horse using FeedXL. First cut grass hay was fed as 75% of dry matter (DM) intake of the balanced diet. Horses were individually fed hay three times a day (at 600 h, 1200 h, and 1800 h), and received a meal at 1200 h, which included Trimax, Equilizer, Horse Plus (all from Purina, Strathroy, Ontario, Canada) and trace mineral salt (Canadian Stockman, Mississauga, Ontario, Canada). Nutrient content of the hay was determined at a commercial laboratory (SGS Agrifood Laboratory, Guelph ON Canada) and nutrient contents of the trace mineral salt, Trimax, Equilizer, and Horse Plus were sourced directly from the manufacturers (Table 2). Horses were offered their hay or hay + meal for a total of 90 min per feeding, after which time any uneaten meal and/or hay were removed and weighed. The weight of Trimax and hay in each horse’s diet was reduced every two weeks, the magnitude of reduction determined by the amount of weight the horse had lost in the previous 2-week period. Actual amounts offered to each horse are included as Supplementary File 1.

Exercise program

Exercising horses (E, FRE) underwent submaximal unridden exercise, with a target working heart rate (HR) of 150 bpm, five days a week. Horses wore Polar Equine FT1 (Polar Electro Oy, Kempele, Finland) heart rate monitors once a week during their daily exercise to match workload with heart rate. Horses were hand-walked for 15 min, followed by trotting on a lunge line for 20 min (10 min in both directions) at the targeted HR of 150 bpm, and then hand-walked for a cool-down of 10 min.

Measurements

At the beginning of each week, horses underwent 11 morphometric measurements. All measurements and scores were performed by a single experienced individual. All measurement data are presented for weeks 1, 5 and 10. Morphometric measurements included body weight (BW; kg) via a weight tape (Easy Measure, Sharpe Farm Supplies, Guelph, ON, Canada), cresty neck score (CNS;/5) [23], an average whole body BCS (/9) [24], and 8 individual anatomical BCS (/9; neck, behind shoulder, inner front legs, withers, ribs, crease down back, crease down haunches and tailhead). Weight tape measurements were obtained in triplicates and averaged for a final weight.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in SAS OnDemand for Academics (SAS Studio, SAS Institution Inc, Cary, NC, USA) using proc glimmix. A 2-Way RM ANOVA was run to determine interactions between and within treatment and week. Student residuals were analyzed to ensure a proper fitting model. A Tukey-Kramer post-hoc test was used for multiple comparisons in the simple effect model, sliced by week and by treatment. Least squares means was run to assess each individual treatment time point mean and standard error. To account for any seasonal effect of when the study was conducted, cohort was included as a statistical block and covariance tests were run for cohort and weight classification. An additional individual random statement was run for weight classification. Significance was accepted at P ≤ 0.05. Data are presented as mean ± SEM.

Results

Horses

Four horses were removed from the study. The first horse was removed during cohort 1 week 1 due to a lameness present at the trot. Two horses were removed during cohort 2 week 1 as they did not reach the desired BCS of 7/9. The fourth horse was removed due to a congenital condition unrelated to this study. Horses removed from the trial were not included in any statistical analyses. An updated sample population can be found in Table 4. Additionally, there was no significant effect of cohort (i.e. season when the study was conducted) on any outcome measure, therefore, data from both cohorts was pooled confidently and presented as such herein.

Morphometrics

Body weight

All four groups decreased BW from week 1 to 5 (P < 0.001), and E and FRE had a further decrease between weeks 5 and 10 (P < 0.05). Body weight was not significantly different between groups at any time point.

Cresty neck

There was no significant change in cresty neck score in C or FR groups at any time during the 10-week weight-loss period. Horses in the E and FRE groups had a decreased CNS between week 1 and 10 (P < 0.01), and horses in the E group also significantly decreased between week 5 and 10 (P < 0.01). At week 10, horses in the C group C had significantly higher CNS than horses in the E group (P = 0.05).

Whole-body BCS

Whole BCS was significantly reduced between weeks 1 and 10 in all groups (P < 0.01) (Fig. 1). Groups C, E and FRE decreased whole BCS between weeks 1 and 5 (P < 0.05), and groups E and FRE decreased score from week 5 to 10 (P < 0.05). At week 10, horses in the C group had higher whole BCS than horses in the FRE group (P = 0.01).

BCS-shoulder

Horses in the FRE group had a decrease in shoulder BCS between week 1 and 5 (P ≤ 0.01), while all groups had a decrease in shoulder BCS between weeks 1 and 10 (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2). Horses in group C also had a decrease in shoulder BCS between weeks 5 and 10 (P < 0.05). At week 5, shoulder BCS was higher in C horses than FRE horses (P < 0.01).

BCS-leg

Horses in groups C and FRE had a decrease in leg BCS between weeks 1 and 5 (P < 0.01), and horses in groups C, E and FRE had a decrease in leg BCS between weeks 1 and 10 (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2).

BCS-withers

Horses in the FRE group had a decrease in withers BCS between weeks 1 and 5 (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2). Groups E and FRE had a decrease in withers BCS between week 1 and 10 (P < 0.0001), and horses in group E had a decrease in score from week 5 to 10 (P < 0.01).

BCS-ribs

Horses in groups E, FR and FRE had a decrease in ribs BCS between weeks 1 and 10 (P < 0.05), and horses in groups E and FRE had a decrease in ribs BCS between weeks 5 and 10 (P ≤ 0.05) (Fig. 2). At week 10, group C had higher ribs BCS than group FRE (P = 0.01).

BCS-back

Horses in groups E, FR and FRE had a decrease in back BCS between weeks 1 and 5 (P ≤ 0.05), and all four groups had a decrease in back BCS between weeks 1 and 10 (P < 0.05).

BCS-haunches

Horses in groups C, FR and FRE had a decrease in haunches BCS between weeks 1 and 5 (P < 0.05), and all four groups had a decrease in haunches BCS between weeks 1 and 10 (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2).

BCS-tailhead

Horses in groups E and FRE had a decrease in tailhead BCS between weeks 1 and 5 (P ≤ 0.05) and weeks 1 and 10 (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2). Horses in the FRE group had a decrease in tailhead BCSbetween weeks 5 and 10 (P = 0.01). At week 10, tailhead BCS was significantly higher in group C than group FRE (P < 0.01).

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to quantify morphometric changes during a 10-week weight loss program using feed restriction, daily exercise, or both.

All 4 groups (including control horses) had a significant decline in BW and whole BCS during the first 5 weeks, but only horses undergoing daily exercise (E and FRE groups) continued significant reduction in BW and whole BCS for the final 5 weeks. Furthermore, only the FRE and E groups had a significant reduction in CNS.

Whilst caloric intake of our control horses was estimated using an online ration formulation program to meet requirements for maintaining body weight, we observed reductions in BW, whole BCS, and BCS of 4 anatomical locations (shoulder, leg, back, and haunches) in this group. This phenomenon has been reported by others [17] and may have resulted initially from the homeostatic transition from ad libitum feeding during the weight-gain period (prior to the start of this trial) to controlled individual feeding during this 10-week weight loss trial [16, 25], and later to an increase in free movement because they were no longer standing for long periods of time at a hay bale. Sedentary, free-feeding ponies consume between 2.3 and 2.9% of their body weight [26] and many horses in free-feeding situations consume around 2.4% of their body weight per day [27], exceeding the recommended intake of 2% of body weight for maintenance [20]. Therefore, reducing feed intake to 2% of BW by controlled-access feeding can be expected to reduce body weight in overweight horses with ad libitum access to feed without requiring imposed exercise.

While the shift from free-feeding to controlled feeding of a maintenance diet produces significant improvements in body weight and whole body condition score, observation of changes in the individual SAT deposits demonstrates that this strategy may not be enough to provoke improvements in adipose tissue sites most often associated with metabolic dysfunction such as the nuchal crest and tailhead [28, 29]. Adipose tissue sites at the shoulder, back, leg and haunches are readily responsive to calorie restriction as they are anatomically superficial and tend not to require adrenergic stimulation for mobilization [26]. However, accumulation of fat in these regions is not typically associated with metabolic dysregulation primarily because, unlike regions like the nuchal crest and tailhead, adipose tissue in these regions has lower expression of pro-inflammatory adipokines and cytokines [28, 29]. Indeed, our data suggest that switching from free-feeding (prior to beginning this trial) to controlled-access feeding (during this 10-week trial) is as effective as a 15% restriction in DE, as there was no significant difference between horses in the FR and C groups with respect to BW, CNS and whole BCS. The only incremental benefit of including feed restriction over providing controlled access to a maintenance diet measured in the current study was a reduction in rib BCS in the FR group. As this fat deposit is deeper and less vascularized than that of the shoulder, leg, back, and haunches [30], it is possible that a more pronounced caloric deficit is required to mobilize fat in this area. To the authors knowledge there are no equine studies which inform the health and/or metabolic implications of reducing rib fat in addition to fat over the shoulder, leg, back and haunches; however, as the ribs are the area most commonly palpated by horse owners to assess fatness [31], loss of fat in this area may provide a more readily visible response to feed restriction compared to simply switching from free-feeding to a controlled-access maintenance diet.

Imposition of a daily exercise regimen did not provide significant benefit over controlled feeding or 15% restriction of DE with respect to losses in BW or whole BCS, though the combination of feed restriction and exercise resulted in significantly lower whole BCS at week 10 compared with control horses (but not different from E, or FR). What our data show, however, is that exercise is essential to mobilization of fat from locations known to be implicated in metabolic diseases in obese horses, including the nuchal crest and tailhead [9]11– [13, 28, 29]32– [36]. Tailhead and nuchal crest fat contains a greater abundance of lipogenic markers including adiponectin receptors and stearoyl-coA desaturase than that of more superficial SAT deposits [36], suggesting that this fat depot is more metabolically active and pro-inflammatory. Both problematic fat deposits were completely unaffected by controlled maintenance feeding or 15% DE feed restriction in the current study, which is consistent with reports from others [14, 16, 37].

Study limitations

As with all experiments, data from the current study should be interpreted with inherent study limitations in mind. We unfortunately did not have access to an equine scale and thus relied on estimates of body weights via weight tape and BIA. It has been previously reported that equine body weight estimated by use of a weight tape is not significantly different from body weight measured on a calibrated scale [38]. However, it is possible that our results were influenced by use of a weight tape; thus future studies in this area should use a calibrated scale. Additionally, it is possible that horses in the energy-restricted groups may have attempted to offset their calorie deficit through consumption of straw bedding. Future studies should use bedding that is less likely to be consumed, such as wood shavings and/or wood pellets. Furthermore, due to the protracted nature of weight loss programs, it is possible that environmental factors may also have influenced our results. We included ‘cohort’ as a block in the statistical model, which accounted for differences between Summer and Fall, but it is possible that there were seasonal effects within each cohort, the effects of which on our results are unknown.

Conclusions

In the current study, all treatments (including controls) produced significant improvements in BW and whole BCS in our overweight horses. However, by quantifying changes in individual SAT sites, our data highlight important metabolic differences between these different weight loss strategies. All groups had reductions in superficial SAT deposits, but only the exercise-based interventions produced significant improvements in fat deposits implicated in equine metabolic disorders. These data provide further evidence for the importance of including exercise in weight loss programs for overweight horses. Additional research is needed to determine the optimum load of exercise (i.e. duration and intensity) to promote fat loss and protect against metabolic consequences of obesity in horses.
