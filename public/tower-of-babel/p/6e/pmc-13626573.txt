Is psoas muscle index an effective predictor of outcomes following robotic total hip arthroplasty?
Authors: Olivia Schaffer, Braden V. Saba, Bryce A. Saba, Anna Cohen-Rosenblum, Ran Schwarzkopf, Diren Arsoy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Sarcopenia, the loss of muscle mass and function, is closely linked to poor health and has been associated with inferior outcomes in total hip arthroplasty (THA). Psoas Muscle Index (PMI) is a radiographic metric inversely correlated with sarcopenia. It was hypothesized that low PMI patients would have worse outcomes following THA.Methods:A retrospective review of 515 patients who underwent robotic primary THA between 2014 and 2022 at a single, urban, academic health system with 2-year follow-up data was performed. Psoas Muscle Index was calculated from preoperative computed tomography imaging of the pelvis. Patient baseline demographics, laboratory values, perioperative data, and outcomes were collected from chart review.Results:Low PMI occurred in 56 (10.8%) of 515 patients. There were no differences in readmissions or reoperations (P = 0.51; P = 0.58). Low PMI was associated with higher HOOS, JR score at 2 weeks and greater improvement from baseline (P = 0.034; P = 0.037), as well as lower Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System Pain Intensity at 3 months and greater reductions from baseline (P = 0.038; P = 0.013).Conclusion:Psoas Muscle Index did not reliably predict surgical outcomes following THA, and low PMI was associated with better patient-reported outcome measures after surgery. Further investigation into other means of diagnosing sarcopenia may be warranted.

Introduction

As the volume of total hip arthroplasties (THA) performed in the United States continues to grow, the procedure is increasingly being performed in more frail and medically complex patients.1,2 Among these populations, sarcopenia, the progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass and function, has been associated with poor surgical outcomes. Patients with sarcopenia may be at an elevated risk of postoperative complications, including delayed recovery, increased morbidity, and higher health care utilization.3 With surgical contraindications becoming more permissive and operative techniques advancing,4 optimization of patient outcomes requires a closer examination of the role sarcopenia plays in surgical risk stratification and recovery.

Sarcopenia has been associated with poor health and mobility outcomes, particularly among older individuals.5 Low muscle mass often coincides with frailty, reduced physical performance, increased fall risk, delayed recovery, and higher morbidity in surgical populations.3,6 Its pathogenesis is multifactorial, driven by age-related deconditioning, inflammation, nutritional deficits, and impaired anabolic signaling.7 These same mechanisms are implicated in the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis and musculoskeletal decline.7–10 Sarcopenia confers a heightened risk of knee osteoarthritis,11 suggesting that muscle loss may contribute both to the progression of joint disease and to poorer surgical outcomes. The prevalence of sarcopenia among patients undergoing total joint arthroplasty (TJA) may be as high as 15% in some practices.12

In TJA populations, awareness of sarcopenia as a relevant risk factor is increasing. Patients undergoing TJA have been shown to exhibit lower appendicular lean mass and reduced protein intake compared with the general population, raising the possibility that sarcopenia may affect outcomes in surgery.13 Sarcopenic patients have been shown to have higher risks of perioperative complications such as urinary tract infections, prosthetic loosening, and dislocation, in addition to longer hospital stays and increased costs.3,6

Despite its clinical significance, sarcopenia remains underdiagnosed in orthopedic practice and is not routinely assessed during preoperative workup. However, the increasing use of computed tomography (CT) imaging for surgical planning provides a unique opportunity for identification and risk stratification. Beyond improving surgical execution, preoperative imaging may incidentally uncover previously unrecognized risk factors.14 The Psoas Muscle Index (PMI) is a sarcopenia metric derived from cross-sectional area of the psoas muscles on axial CT.5,15,16 Although PMI correlates with skeletal muscle index and has demonstrated prognostic utility in hospitalized patients, its use in orthopedic surgery has not yet been demonstrated.17 Within this context, PMI may represent a promising and practical tool for identifying sarcopenic patients at risk for poor outcomes following THA.

It is important to acknowledge that no single metric is likely sufficient to predict outcomes following THA. Surgical outcomes are shaped by multiple variables, including comorbidity burden, disease severity, nutritional status, and functional reserve, that extend well beyond muscle mass at a single anatomical level. Psoas Muscle Index should therefore be understood as one candidate variable within a broader risk stratification framework rather than a comprehensive standalone predictor.

The aim of this study was to evaluate whether sarcopenia, defined by low PMI on preoperative CT imaging, is associated with adverse outcomes following primary THA. It was hypothesized that patients with sarcopenia would experience higher rates of perioperative complications, longer length of stay, and worse early functional recovery compared to nonsarcopenic patients.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective study included 515 consecutive patients who underwent robotic-assisted primary THA at a single urban academic health system between 2014 and 2022. Patients undergoing bilateral procedures or nonelective THA were excluded. Inclusion criteria required availability of preoperative pelvic CT imaging, which was used to calculate the PMI. All patients were stratified by sarcopenia status, defined as a PMI less than 7 for men and 5.5 for women.18 Clinical outcomes, including revision surgery and reoperations, were compared between groups. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained before study initiation.

Data collection and outcome measures

Patient demographics, laboratory values, and perioperative clinical variables were extracted from the electronic medical record (Epic Systems, Verona, WI). Collected demographic variables included age, sex, race, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, American Society of Anaesthesiologists (ASA) classification, and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). Preoperative vitamin D levels were also obtained when available as shown in Table 1. Operative details included surgical duration, length of hospital stay, and discharge disposition. The Activity Measure for Post-Acute Care “6 Clicks” Inpatient Short Form (AM-PAC) scores on postoperative days 0 and 1 were recorded to assess early functional recovery.19 “Perfect” AM-PAC score refers to a raw score of 24.

Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) were collected, including Hip dysfunction and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score for Joint Replacement (HOOS, JR) and Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Pain Intensity Scores. A priori power analysis found that at least 48 sarcopenic patients would be adequate to detect a medium effect size in PROMs (Cohen d = 0.5) with 80% power and α = 0.05.

Psoas Muscle Index assessment

Preoperative CT imaging of the hip and pelvis was obtained as part of routine planning for robotic-assisted THA. If multiple scans were available, the CT scan performed closest to the date of surgery was selected for analysis. Patients with spinal instrumentation generating excessive metal artifact that precluded accurate measurement (n = 3) were excluded.

All CT scans were reviewed using the institutional picture archiving and communication system (Visage 7 CloudPACS, Visage Imaging, San Diego, CA). Axial, noncontrast images were accessed, and the cross-sectional area of the bilateral psoas muscles was measured at the level of the third lumbar vertebra (L3), corresponding to the most cranial slice of the scan field of view. Using the freehand region-of-interest (ROI) tool, the outline of each psoas muscle was manually traced, as shown in Figure 1. The PACS software automatically calculated the cross-sectional area of each ROI in square centimeters. Data analyses

All descriptive statistics were presented as means and standard deviations (SDs) for all normally distributed data. The t test statistic was used to compare continuous variables with a significance set at P = 0.05. Group comparisons were performed using 1-way analysis of variance. Chi-square tests were used for the analysis of categorical variables. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed using R. All other statistical analysis was performed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences for Windows V24.0 (SPSS, IBM, Armonk, New York), and P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Patient-reported outcome measures analyses were performed using complete-case data without imputation, as missing values were not assumed to be missing at random. Multivariable regression analysis was not performed, as the low rates of adverse events in this cohort produced insufficient outcome counts to construct statistically stable models.

Results

Patient cohort

A total of 515 patients undergoing robotic primary total hip arthroplasty (THA) with preoperative planning CT scans were included. Fifty-six patients (10.8%) were classified as sarcopenic based on PMI.

Sarcopenic patients were significantly older than nonsarcopenic patients (mean age 68.0 vs. 62.5 years, P = 0.001) and had significantly lower BMI (27.8 vs. 30.6 kg/m2, P = 0.002). There were no statistically significant differences in sex, race, ASA classification, CCI, preoperative vitamin D levels, smoking history, or follow-up duration.

Clinical and perioperative outcomes

Operative time and hospital length of stay did not differ significantly between groups. The majority of patients in both groups were discharged home (91.1 and 94.6% for sarcopenic and nonsarcopenic patients, respectively), and revision rates were not significantly different (3.6% in sarcopenic vs. 2.0% in nonsarcopenic, P = 0.46). One sarcopenic patient developed sepsis. Although this contributed to a nominally significant difference (P = 0.023), this finding is based on a single event and should be interpreted with caution.

Sarcopenic patients demonstrated similar early functional recovery based on time-to-perfect AM-PAC scores. On postoperative day 0 (POD0), 20.0% achieved a perfect AM-PAC mobility score compared with 25.8% in the nonsarcopenic group (P = 0.466). By postoperative day 1 (POD1), 51.4% of sarcopenic patients had achieved a perfect AM-PAC score compared with 58.6% of nonsarcopenic patients (P = 0.371).

Diagnostic performance of PMI

Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis (Figure 2) was performed to evaluate the ability of PMI to predict surgical outcomes, with an area under the curve (AUC) greater than 0.8 considered acceptable. Psoas Muscle Index showed modest discrimination for 90-day readmissions (AUC 0.64) and all-cause revisions (AUC 0.66), indicating limited predictive utility. It performed slightly better for aseptic revisions (AUC 0.69). The highest discrimination was observed for septic revisions (AUC 0.90). However, given the absence of septic revision events in the sarcopenic group and the very low overall incidence of this outcome, this AUC value is statistically fragile and should not be interpreted as a clinically meaningful finding.

Patient-reported outcome measures

Patient-reported outcome measures were available for 180 patients in the cohort. Preoperative HOOS, JR and PROMIS Pain scores were similar between groups (HOOS, JR: 45.2 ± 20.3 vs. 49.3 ± 15.6, P = 0.297; PROMIS Pain Intensity: 53.4 ± 8.2 vs. 53.9 ± 6.6, P = 0.756). At 2 weeks postoperatively, sarcopenic patients showed greater improvement in HOOS, JR (Δ preoperative to 2 weeks: 27.5 ± 21.7 vs. 16.9 ± 16.5, P = 0.037; absolute score: 73.1 ± 9.1 vs. 65.7 ± 13.0, P = 0.034) and larger reductions in pain intensity at 3 months (Δ preoperative to 3 months: −19.0 ± 8.3 vs. −10.8 ± 8.7, P = 0.013; absolute 3-month score: 38.8 ± 4.3 vs. 44.0 ± 7.3, P = 0.038). No significant differences were observed at later time points.

Discussion

In this retrospective study of patients undergoing robotic-assisted THA, sarcopenia, as defined by a low PMI on preoperative CT, was associated with impaired early functional recovery but not with increased surgical complications, revisions, or readmissions. Although sarcopenic patients were older, had lower BMI, and were more likely to have a smoking history (Table 1), most perioperative outcomes were similar between groups (Table 2). Although PMI was not associated with short-term surgical morbidity in this study, outcomes following THA are determined by multiple interacting factors, including comorbidity burden, severity of hip osteoarthritis, nutritional status, and functional reserve, that a single imaging-derived metric cannot adequately capture. Other measures of muscle health may be more strongly correlated with mobility and recovery.

Although existing literature demonstrates an association between frailty, reduced physiologic reserve, and delayed postoperative rehabilitation.3,6,13 Psoas Muscle Index demonstrated limited diagnostic utility for predicting key outcomes. As shown in Table 2, sarcopenic patients had similar AM-PAC scores at postoperative day 0 and 1. Receiver operating characteristic analysis (Figure 2) showed modest discrimination for 90-day readmission (AUC 0.64), all-cause revision (AUC 0.66), and aseptic revision (AUC 0.69). Psoas Muscle Index demonstrated the most accurate discrimination for septic revisions, with a higher PMI predicting greater odds of periprosthetic joint infection (AUC 0.90); however, the absence of events in the sarcopenic group and the low overall incidence make this a fragile finding that should be interpreted with caution.

Analysis of PROMs revealed that sarcopenic patients experienced greater early improvements in HOOS, JR scores at 2 weeks and larger improvements in PROMIS Pain at 3 months compared with nonsarcopenic patients (Table 3), contrary to the expected pattern of inferior early outcomes. One possible explanation might be that BMI was significantly lower in sarcopenic patients relative to nonsarcopenic patients, as this was not a matched analysis, which may have contributed to faster short-term recovery. Previous literature points to sarcopenic obesity as a risk magnifier for patients undergoing surgery, which the study was not powered to detect.7–12 Furthermore, patients may have adequate muscle mass in other regions that are more crucial for postoperative recovery. It is also possible that sarcopenic patients had greater baseline hip disease severity or pain burden that was not fully reflected in preoperative PROMs scores, which may have created greater potential for perceived improvement following a successful arthroplasty. Total hip arthroplasty may be sufficiently effective as an intervention that even physiologically vulnerable patients experience meaningful early pain relief, particularly when preoperative disease burden is high.

These results highlight a critical limitation of PMI as a standalone surrogate for sarcopenia. Although it provides a convenient, imaging-based measure of muscle quantity, it does not capture muscle quality, strength, or physical performance; these factors may be more closely tied to surgical resilience and postoperative function than radiographic metrics of sarcopenia. Measures such as grip strength, gait speed, or chair rise time may offer better discrimination of functional vulnerability.8 Several complementary approaches to sarcopenia assessment have demonstrated prognostic value in surgical populations. Grip strength is recommended as a primary sarcopenia indicator by the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People and has been associated with postoperative outcomes across multiple surgical specialties.5 Physical capacity measures including gait speed and walking tests have been shown to predict functional outcomes after THA and may more directly reflect the demands of postoperative rehabilitation.20 The Short Physical Performance Battery integrates balance, gait speed, and chair rise performance into a composite score with established predictive validity in older surgical patients.21 Dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry remains a reference standard for appendicular lean mass quantification, while bioelectrical impedance analysis offers a more accessible clinical alternative.5 Biochemical markers including serum albumin and prealbumin reflect nutritional status and catabolic burden and have been associated with complications in TJA populations.22 Thus, it is recommended to combine low muscle mass with evidence of reduced strength or physical performance to confirm a diagnosis of sarcopenia to improve both sensitivity and specificity compared with imaging metrics alone.5

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. This study was conducted at a single institution and was retrospective in design, which may limit generalizability. The number of major adverse events was low, and the expected effect size was calculated based on PROMs differences, reducing the ability to draw robust conclusions about the relationship between sarcopenia and rare outcomes such as septic revision. In addition, while sarcopenia was the focus of this study, few patients had both sarcopenia and obesity, limiting the ability to explore the additive or synergistic effects of sarcopenic obesity: a phenotype that may confer greater risk. A further technical limitation of CT-based PMI measurement is that standard imaging does not reliably distinguish contractile muscle tissue from intramuscular and intermuscular fat infiltration. The presence of myosteatosis can lead to overestimation of functional muscle mass if Hounsfield unit-based filtering or dedicated muscle density analysis is not applied, as the traced cross-sectional area may include both lean tissue and lipid deposits.23 This further limits the utility of PMI as a sarcopenia screening tool, particularly in patients with significant fat infiltration.

Conclusions

These findings do not support the hypothesis that PMI is an effective preoperative risk stratification method for THA. Validation of sarcopenia assessments that integrate imaging, functional, and biochemical markers would likely contribute to an improved understanding surgical risk. In particular, prospective investigations with larger sample sizes are needed to better characterize the combined effects of sarcopenia and obesity, identify thresholds for intervention, and evaluate whether targeted preoperative rehabilitation or nutritional strategies can improve outcomes in this vulnerable subgroup.
