Effect of Cognitive Behavioral Music Therapy on Noise-Induced Perceived Stress and Anxiety Among University Students
Authors: Temitope Ayodeji Falade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:University campuses are often noisy environments, and prolonged exposure to campus noise has been linked to increased stress and anxiety among students. Although many studies focus on engineering or policy solutions to manage noise, far fewer examine psychological interventions that help students cope with unavoidable noise. In particular, evidence on approaches that combine cognitive-behavioral strategies with music-based techniques remains limited.Objective:This study examined whether a structured cognitive behavioral music therapy (CBMT) program could reduce noise-induced perceived stress and noise-related anxiety among undergraduate students.Methods:A pretest–posttest control-group study was conducted at the University of Nigeria. Sixty undergraduate students with elevated levels of noise-related stress or anxiety participated (CBMT = 29; control = 31). The intervention group received eight weekly 60-minute CBMT sessions that integrated cognitive restructuring, graded exposure, attentional training, and guided music-based relaxation. The control group continued with usual care. Outcomes were measured using the adapted Noise-Induced Perceived Stress (PSS-N) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item Noise-Related Anxiety Scale (GAD-7-N) scales. Postintervention group differences were analyzed using analysis of covariance, while controlling for baseline scores.Results:Students who participated in CBMT experienced marked reductions in both noise-related anxiety and PSS-N compared with those in the control group. These improvements were observed in both male and female students, with no meaningful gender differences in treatment response. In contrast, participants receiving usual care showed little change over the study period.Conclusion:The findings provide preliminary evidence that CBMT may offer a practical, nonpharmacological approach for helping university students manage stress and anxiety associated with chronic campus noise. Further research using randomized designs, longer follow-up periods, and objective physiological measures is warranted to strengthen and extend these findings.

INTRODUCTION

University campuses are inherently noisy spaces. Students move daily through environments filled with traffic, construction, mechanical systems, and dense social activity. Repeated or chronic exposure to elevated noise levels activates the body’s autonomic and neuroendocrine systems, particularly the sympathetic branch and the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis.[123] These stress systems trigger measurable physiological responses such as transient increases in heart rate and blood pressure, alongside altered cortisol secretion and disrupted diurnal rhythms.[45] Over time, these processes can undermine sleep, attention, and cognitive performance, linking noise exposure not only to discomfort but also to wider nonauditory outcomes such as fatigue, anxiety, and impaired academic functioning.[467] In university settings, this exposure often coincides with periods of heightened academic strain, thereby exacerbating students’ psychological vulnerability to persistent environmental noise.

In this study, stress is conceptualized as the psychological and physiological response to environmental demands that exceed an individual’s adaptive capacity.[3] Anxiety, by contrast, refers to a more persistent state of worry, tension, and heightened arousal that may continue even in the absence of the immediate stressor.[8] Although closely related, stress is typically linked to a specific trigger such as environmental noise, whereas anxiety may become anticipatory and enduring. This distinction is important when examining noise-related psychological outcomes among university students.

The mental health burden among university students is both substantial and well documented.[910] Global reviews show that roughly one-third of students experience clinically meaningful anxiety symptoms, with pooled estimates clustering between 30% and 40%.[11121314] These high baseline rates mean that even a modest contribution from modifiable factors, such as ambient noise, constitutes a serious population-level concern. Addressing this link through scalable, evidence-based interventions could therefore yield significant benefits for student well-being.[151617]

Despite broad recognition of student stress and anxiety, the specific role of noise-related perceived stress and anxiety remains poorly understood. Numerous surveys and audits show substantial noise annoyance, sleep disturbance, and concentration difficulties in campus or urban settings, and epidemiological studies link chronic noise exposure to poorer mental well-being.[18192021] Yet few studies quantify the proportion of stress or anxiety that can be directly attributed to noise or explore dose–response relationships within campus environments.[1922] This lack of targeted epidemiological evidence makes it difficult for institutions to recognize noise as a distinct mental health risk factor and to design interventions tailored to this form of distress. Most campus noise interventions to date have focused on environmental and engineering solutions: soundproofing, traffic management, or scheduling noisy activities. These are essential but often costly, slow, and difficult to implement on older or resource-constrained campuses.[23] More importantly, they do not equip students with psychological strategies to cope with or reframe unavoidable noise exposure. Recent evidence syntheses, including those by the World Health Organization, highlight the limited number of interventions targeting nonauditory outcomes such as coping, resilience, and emotion regulation, and call for individual-level approaches that complement physical noise-control measures.[6]

Although the adverse effects of environmental noise on mental health are increasingly acknowledged, notable gaps persist in the literature. Research and interventions in university settings have largely prioritized environmental engineering, policy regulation, and urban planning approaches to noise reduction. While these strategies are undeniably important, they are often expensive, slow to implement, and of limited value in settings where noise cannot be fully eliminated. By contrast, relatively few empirical studies have examined psychologically grounded, individual-level interventions that support students in coping with ongoing noise exposure. In particular, evidence remains limited for interventions that combine cognitive-behavioral approaches with structured, music-based techniques to alleviate noise-related stress and anxiety.

Music therapy represents one such promising approach. Structured music interventions have repeatedly demonstrated reliable physiological and psychological relaxation effects. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicate that music has the potential to alleviate state anxiety and physiological arousal, including reductions in heart rate, blood pressure, and cortisol levels, in both clinical and nonclinical populations.[2425] Music acts through multiple mechanisms by synchronizing with the body’s rhythms, engaging neural reward and affiliative circuits, and providing an emotionally meaningful focus for attention and guided imagery.[26] As such, music offers an accessible, low-risk, and engaging way to support relaxation and emotional regulation in therapeutic and educational contexts.

Integrating music therapy within a cognitive-behavioral framework to form cognitive behavioral music therapy (CBMT) offers a conceptually sound and practical intervention for noise-related stress. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) helps individuals identify and restructure maladaptive thoughts about noise, such as beliefs of uncontrollability or exaggerated consequences for concentration. At the same time, music facilitates rapid physiological downregulation and provides a safe setting to practice relaxation, attentional refocusing, and exposure.[242728] These complementary mechanisms combine cognitive change with bottom-up physiological calming, creating a dual pathway for reducing distress. The biological plausibility of noise as a stressor (HPA and autonomic activation), the high baseline prevalence of anxiety and stress among students, the documented adverse effects of noise on academic and psychological functioning, and the synergistic mechanisms of CBT and music therapy form a compelling rationale for testing CBMT as an accessible, nonpharmacological intervention to reduce noise-induced perceived stress and anxiety among university students.[672429] Identifying effective and scalable psychological interventions for noise-related distress is especially important for campus mental health services, particularly in low-resource or high-density university environments where structural approaches to noise control are limited.

Objective

To examine whether a structured CBMT program can reduce noise-induced perceived stress and noise-related anxiety among university students exposed to elevated campus noise, compared with usual care.

Research Hypotheses

H01: There will be no significant difference in the reduction of noise-induced perceived stress from baseline to postintervention between students who receive CBMT and those in the control group.

H02: There will be no significant difference in the reduction of noise-related anxiety from baseline to postintervention between students who receive CBMT and those in the control group.

H03: There will be no significant interaction effect between group assignment (CBMT vs. control) and gender on postintervention noise-induced perceived stress.

H04: There will be no significant interaction effect between group assignment (CBMT vs. control) and baseline noise sensitivity on postintervention noise-related anxiety.

Review of Empirical Studies

A growing body of evidence supports the effectiveness of CBT for conditions in which maladaptive responses to sound are central. Randomized and controlled clinical trials have demonstrated that CBT significantly reduces sound-related distress and improves coping in disorders such as misophonia, hyperacusis, and tinnitus. For example, a study found that CBT helped individuals with misophonia reduce emotional reactivity and avoidance behaviors related to specific sounds. In contrast, another study reported improved tolerance and reduced distress among patients with hyperacusis following CBT.[530] Similarly, another study observed that CBT led to meaningful reductions in tinnitus-related annoyance and improved emotional functioning.[31] These findings demonstrate that targeting unhelpful appraisals, avoidance tendencies, and catastrophic beliefs about sound through CBT can meaningfully decrease sound-induced anxiety and distress. Beyond these clinical applications, researchers have explored combining CBT with sound-based interventions, particularly music therapy, to enhance emotional regulation and coping. School- and university-based programs that integrated CBT elements with structured music activities have shown that such combinations are both feasible and effective in reducing anxiety and test-related stress. For instance, another study reported that students who participated in CBT-plus-music interventions experienced greater emotional stability and engagement than those in conventional counseling conditions.[26] Studies confirm that guided music listening and structured musical engagement reliably lower subjective anxiety and physiological arousal, including heart rate, blood pressure, and cortisol levels.[2432] These findings suggest that music can serve as a complementary mechanism to CBT, offering a rapid, bottom-up pathway to emotional regulation that supports the cognitive restructuring process central to CBT. Recent environmental and health psychology reviews extend these findings to noise-related stress more broadly. Research highlights that prolonged exposure to environmental noise triggers biological stress mechanisms, notably the HPA axis and the autonomic nervous system, potentially resulting in heightened stress and anxiety over extended periods.[333] However, these reviews also note that most intervention research has focused on environmental or engineering solutions, such as noise insulation and urban design, while neglecting psychological coping and resilience-building strategies. This imbalance points to an important research gap: the need for empirically tested, psychologically grounded interventions that help individuals reinterpret and regulate their responses to noise exposure. These empirical findings build a coherent foundation for testing a CBMT approach in university settings. The existing literature demonstrates that CBT effectively modifies maladaptive thought patterns about sound and that music interventions reliably reduce physiological and emotional arousal. Integrating both approaches could offer a uniquely powerful intervention for reducing noise-induced perceived stress and anxiety among university students, a population particularly vulnerable to both high noise exposure and high stress levels.

METHODS

Study Design

The study used a pretest–posttest control-group design. Eligible participants were allocated to the CBMT arm or a usual-care control arm using a matched, nonrandom allocation procedure. Baseline assessments were completed before the first session, and postintervention assessments were obtained within 1 week after the final session.

Setting and Participants

The study was conducted at the University of Nigeria, Nsukka, Enugu State, Nigeria. Recruitment targeted undergraduate students who resided on campus or frequently used campus facilities during sandwich studies.

Inclusion Criteria

Participants were registered full-time and sandwich undergraduate students at the University of Nigeria, Nsukka, aged 18 to 35 years, representing the typical university student population. Eligibility was determined using two adapted screening instruments: the Noise-Induced Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-N) and the Noise-Related Anxiety Scale (GAD-7-N). The PSS-N contains 10 items scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 = “never” to 4 = “very often,” yielding a total score of 0 to 40. To ensure that only students with severe noise-related stress were included, participants qualified if they scored between 30 and 40 on the PSS-N. The GAD-7-N consists of 7 items scored on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 = “not at all” to 3 = “nearly every day,” producing a total score of 0 to 21. Students were eligible for inclusion based on the anxiety criterion if they scored between 14 and 21 on the GAD-7-N, reflecting moderate-to-severe noise-related anxiety. Only students meeting at least one of these high-symptom thresholds were included to ensure that the CBMT intervention targeted individuals most affected by noise-induced perceived stress or anxiety. Additionally, eligible participants had to be able to attend all eight weekly 60-minute CBMT sessions and provide written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Exclusion Criteria

Students were excluded if they had a current diagnosis of psychotic disorder, bipolar disorder, or active suicidal ideation requiring urgent psychiatric care, as these conditions required specialized treatment beyond the scope of the study. Those already receiving structured psychological treatment for stress or anxiety at the time of recruitment were also excluded to avoid overlapping interventions that could confound the results. Students with a hearing impairment that would interfere with music-based activities were not eligible because the intervention required active participation in listening and rhythmic exercises. Finally, students taking medications known to substantially affect physiological stress responses, such as antidepressants, anxiolytics, beta-blockers, or systemic corticosteroids, were excluded unless their dosage had remained stable for at least 3 months and was approved by a qualified healthcare provider.

Sample Size

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power version 3.1.9.4 (Franz Faul, Universität Kiel, Kiel, Germany) for an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA, F-test; fixed effects, main effects, and interactions) to detect a large effect size (f = 0.40) with α = 0.05 and power (1 − β) = 0.80. The analysis, assuming two groups and one covariate, produced a noncentrality parameter (λ) of 8.32, a critical F value of 4.04, a denominator df of 49, and an estimated total sample size of 52 participants to achieve the desired power (actual power = 0.807).[34] In order to account for an anticipated attrition rate of 24%, the recruitment target was increased to 64 participants, with 30 participants assigned to the CBMT group (experimental) and 34 participants assigned to the control group. The CONSORT flow diagram [Figure 2] shows that 29 participants in the experimental group and 31 in the control group were analyzed due to attrition during the study period. It is important to note that this small reduction in the final sample size was the result of participant dropouts and did not significantly compromise the statistical power of the study, as the final sample size (N = 60) remained close to the required sample size.

Recruitment and Allocation

Participants were recruited through multiple channels, including campus announcements, flyers displayed in residence halls and lecture areas, class mailing lists, and collaboration with student welfare officers. Interested students participated in face-to-face screening interviews using a structured interviewer-administered noise exposure checklist, and ambient sound levels were recorded where feasible. Participants were matched using an approximate 1:1 matching strategy based on baseline PSS-N and noise sensitivity scores. The minor imbalance between groups (30 CBMT vs. 34 control) resulted from the availability of matched pairs and participant consent to group allocation following screening. This matching approach helped reduce baseline differences between groups and improved comparability prior to the intervention.

During screening, participants described their main sources of campus noise, such as road traffic, generators, construction activities, hostel noise, and social activities. They also reported how long they were typically exposed each day, how intense the noise felt, and when it most interfered with studying and sleep. Interviewers verified participants’ primary exposure locations and, where feasible, recorded ambient sound levels using a handheld sound-level meter. Students who consistently reported regular exposure to moderate-to-high levels of campus noise for several hours each day were considered eligible. Because all participants were drawn from the same campus environment and allocation was matched on baseline stress and noise sensitivity, overall noise exposure was considered reasonably comparable between the intervention and control groups. The anticipated trial start date was August 25, 2025; however, participant recruitment commenced on September 1, 2025. The anticipated final follow-up date was October 7, 2025, and the trial was completed on October 13, 2025.

Measures

The primary outcomes were PSS-N and Noise-Related Anxiety (GAD-7-N).

Noise-Induced Perceived Stress

The PSS-N was adapted from the original 10-item Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) developed by Cohen et al.[35] to specifically measure stress resulting from environmental noise within the campus setting. Each statement was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“never”) to 4 (“very often”). Possible scores, therefore, ranged from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating higher levels of noise-related stress. The original PSS-10 has consistently demonstrated strong psychometric performance, with Cronbach’s alpha values typically ranging from 0.78 to 0.91, and has demonstrated solid construct validity across a wide range of populations.

The adapted PSS-N maintains the original item structure, response format, and scoring procedures of the PSS-10 while adjusting item wording to better reflect noise-related stressors commonly experienced in campus environments. This adaptation improves the relevance of the scale for the present population without altering its underlying measurement properties. Because the PSS framework is widely used in intervention research and is sensitive to change over time, the adapted scale is well-suited for detecting changes in perceived stress specifically related to environmental noise.

Noise-Related Anxiety (GAD-7-N)

The Noise-Related Anxiety Scale (GAD-7-N) was adapted from the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7) by Spitzer et al.[36] to evaluate anxiety symptoms that arise or intensify due to noise exposure. Responses were recorded on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”), yielding a total score between 0 and 21. Higher scores reflected greater levels of noise-related anxiety. The original GAD-7 is widely recognized for its high reliability (Cronbach’s α ≈ 0.92) and strong criterion validity, with a clinical cut-off score of 10 commonly used to indicate moderate anxiety. Demographic variables collected included age, sex, academic level, tribe, occupation, and religion, obtained through a structured self-report questionnaire administered in person at baseline alongside the PSS-N and GAD-7-N.

To ensure the psychometric integrity of the adapted tools, the item structure, response format, and scoring procedures of the original PSS-10 and GAD-7 were retained. Both instruments underwent expert review for face and content validity. A pilot study involving 20 undergraduate students (who were not part of the main study) was conducted to evaluate item clarity, cultural suitability, and preliminary reliability. The pilot yielded internal consistency coefficients of Cronbach’s α = 0.75 for the PSS-N and Cronbach’s α = 0.76 for the GAD-7-N, confirming their acceptability for research application.

Intervention

The intervention was a manualized CBMT program adapted from the Provider’s Guide to Brief Cognitive Behavioral Therapy,[37] which was modified to integrate structured music therapy procedures at every stage of treatment. The adaptation preserved the original brief-CBT framework (orientation, problem formulation, cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments/exposure, relaxation training, homework, and relapse prevention) while embedding music-based components designed to (1) accelerate autonomic down-regulation, (2) scaffold graded auditory exposure, (3) provide an affective corrective experience to pair with cognitive restructuring, and (4) produce portable self-management tools for use in campus settings. The core manual and session templates from the uploaded Provider’s Guide were used as the structural backbone for session agendas, therapist scripts, and fidelity checklists. These were reviewed and modified in collaboration with a credentialed music therapist to produce the final CBMT manual. The intervention consisted of group CBMT delivered in 8 weekly 60-minute sessions. Each group comprised 8 to 12 students to maximize peer learning while allowing opportunities for individual practice. The manual supports brief CBT formats of 4 to 8 session and provides case conceptualization and session templates to adapt content.

Session 1: Orientation, Case Conceptualization, and Psychoeducation

The interventions began by orienting students to brief CBT (goals, homework expectations) as described in the manual (Module 7: orienting patient to CBT). A brief group assessment of noise-related problems (when and where noises occur and its functional impact) was conducted. The cognitive-behavioral model as applied to noise (situation → thoughts about noise → emotional/physiological response → behaviors such as avoidance or hypervigilance) was then introduced. Participants collaboratively set two to three concrete goals (e.g., “reduce intrusive anxiety during late-night study in the dormitory”), and the rationale for CBMT was introduced: CBT for reappraisal and music for regulation and practice. The session concluded with a 6- to 8-minute baseline guided music relaxation exercise (receptive listening to a slow-tempo instrumental track, tempo 60–70 bpm) to provide participants with an immediate experience of in-session down-regulation and learn the “music tool.”

Session 2: Identifying Automatic Thoughts and Music-Assisted Relaxation Skills

Participants were taught to use three-column thought records (situation, automatic thought, and alternative balanced thought) adapted to noise-related examples (e.g., “I can’t study with any noise” → testable evidence). Participants practiced one thought record during the session using a recent noise-related episode. They were then taught progressive muscle relaxation (PMR), accompanied by an abridged music track designed for physiological entrainment. They were instructed to pace breathing/exhalation to the music (breathing at 6–7 breaths/min where music provides tempo cues). Homework included daily 10- to 15-minute guided music and PMR practice, together with one three-column noise-related thought record each day.

Session 3: Cognitive Restructuring and Music Imagery

Homework was reviewed, and participants were introduced to cognitive distortions and guided Socratic questioning to evaluate catastrophic noise appraisals. A music-guided imagery exercise was then conducted. While listening to a calm instrumental track, students were guided to imagine a predictable, controllable situation (e.g., studying in a quiet library) and compare their catastrophic thoughts with evidence during the imagery. This paired cognitive reappraisal with an affective corrective experience by using music to reduce arousal while the students rehearsed alternative appraisals. Homework included music-guided imagery practice twice weekly, with a thought record.

Session 4: Attention Training, Mindfulness of Sound, and Graded Auditory Exposure with Music Support

Participants were introduced to attentional control strategies, including focused attention, attention shifting, and labeling. They then collaboratively developed a small noise hierarchy (e.g., distant traffic → louder traffic → construction noise). Instead of pure silent exposure, participants completed music-scaffolded graded exposure. Recorded campus noise at low intensity was presented alongside calming music [music at comfortable volume, noise at low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)], and participants practiced attentional refocusing and cognitive restructuring during the exposure. The intensity of the noise was gradually increased or the music was reduced, allowing participants to practice tolerating the noise with minimal or no music. This method reduced initial distress and provided a supported in-session exposure experience.

Session 5: Behavioral Strategies (Sleep/Study Hygiene), Problem Solving, and Music Cues for Sleep/Study Routines

Participants were taught behavioral activation and problem-solving strategies for managing noise-controllable behaviors (e.g., rearranging study times and using quiet rooms). They were also introduced to music cueing by assigning specific playlists for particular contexts (e.g., “study playlist” for sustained attention, “pre-study relaxation playlist” to downshift arousal). Participants were encouraged to pair music with behavioral routines so that it became a conditioned cue for focused study or relaxation. Homework included implementing one behavioral plan and using the assigned playlists daily.

Session 6: Advanced Cognitive Restructuring and Active Music Exercises

Participants were taught to use the seven-column record for detailed evaluation of evidence and development of alternative plans. They were engaged in active music-making (simple rhythm exercises using hand percussion) to practice attention switching, behavioral activation, and group cohesion. These activities also helped participants remain focused when unpredictably distracted by noise. Participants continued graded exposure tasks and practiced CBT strategies during music-supported exposure exercises. Homework included practicing active music rhythm exercises or recorded guided sessions twice weekly while completing a thought record after exposure.

Session 7: Consolidation, Relapse Prevention Planning, and Real-World Simulations

Participants completed simulated real-world practice sessions using recordings of their most problematic noises. They applied the full CBMT sequence, including appraisal check, breathing with music-assisted down-regulation, cognitive challenge, and attentional refocusing in vivo. Participants were taught relapse prevention strategies, including identifying warning signs, maintaining music practice, and scheduling booster sessions. Homework included creating a personal CBMT plan comprising a playlist, two cognitive strategies, and an exposure schedule.

Session 8: Review, Maintenance, Booster Planning, and Transfer

Progress was reviewed, remaining barriers were addressed, and self-management booster sessions (e.g., one booster at 4 weeks) were scheduled. Participants were provided with a take-home “CBMT workbook” containing guided playlists, exposure steps, thought records, and contact and support resources.

Treatment Fidelity

To promote consistent delivery of the intervention, facilitators followed a session-by-session manual and completed standardized checklists adapted from the CBMT guide. Participant attendance was monitored throughout the program, and any missed sessions were recorded. In addition, facilitators engaged in weekly supervisory meetings to reinforce adherence to the protocol, address implementation challenges, and ensure consistency in session delivery across groups.

Therapists

The CBMT sessions were conducted by licensed mental health clinicians (clinical psychologists or counselors) who had completed specialized training in CBT and were further trained in the CBMT intervention model. Each facilitator had experience working with stress- and anxiety-related presentations among university students and received specific instruction in the use of music-assisted relaxation and exposure techniques. Therapists met weekly for supervision with a senior clinical psychologist experienced in CBT to review session fidelity, discuss challenges, and ensure adherence to the intervention manual. This process maintained professional standards, therapeutic consistency, and participant safety throughout the study.

Control Group

Control participants received usual care, defined as continued access to routine university counseling services. They were assessed at the same time points as the CBMT group.

Data Collection Procedures

Baseline data, including demographics as well as scores on the PSS-N and GAD-7-N, were collected 1 week before the first intervention session. Postintervention data for these same measures were obtained within 1 week after participants completed the final session.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as means ± standard deviations for demographic variables and baseline (PSS-N, GAD-7-N) and post-intervention scores. Intervention effects were evaluated using separate ANCOVAs for each primary outcome (postintervention PSS-N and GAD-7-N scores). The interaction effects were also analyzed. Model assumptions (normality of residuals, homogeneity of variances, homogeneity of regression slopes, and absence of multicollinearity) were examined and addressed as required. Effect sizes were reported as partial η2 and interpreted in percentages. Adjusted (estimated marginal) means and 95% confidence intervals were reported for group comparisons. Although paired sample t-tests can be used to examine within-group pre–post changes, ANCOVA was adopted as the primary analytic approach because it adjusts for baseline differences, reduces bias associated with nonrandom group allocation, and yields a more accurate estimate of the between-group intervention effect. In addition to the complete-case analysis, an additional analysis, called Intent-to-Treat (ITT) analysis using the last observation carried forward (LOCF) method was performed to evaluate the treatment effects despite the existence of dropouts. All tests were two-tailed with α = 0.05, and analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Ethical Approval and Informed Consent

The study protocol received approval from the Research Ethics Committee of the Department of Counseling and Human Development Studies, University of Nigeria, Nsukka (Approval No REC/UNN/CHDS/2025/000055). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. All data were deidentified for analysis and stored in accordance with institutional data protection requirements. The trial was registered retrospectively due to completion of data collection prior to registry submission, in line with Pan-African Clinical Trial Registry (PACTR) provisions for ongoing and completed studies. The study was retrospectively registered with the PACTR (Registration No. PACTR202601613296413).

RESULTS

Descriptive Analysis of the Demographics Variables

Table 1 presents the descriptive analysis of the demographics variables. At baseline, participants reported elevated noise-related anxiety and noise-induced perceived stress across demographic subgroups. The overall mean anxiety score for the sample was 16.65 ± 1.69, whereas the overall mean stress score was 32.45 ± 1.81. At baseline, males had a mean anxiety score of 16.39 ± 1.99 and a stress score of 33.00 ± 2.05, whereas females had corresponding mean scores of 16.81 ± 1.47 and 32.11 ± 1.58, respectively, indicating minimal gender differences. By age group, participants aged 18 to 25 years had slightly higher mean scores (16.69 ± 1.78 for anxiety; 32.63 ± 1.82 for stress) than those aged 26 to 35 years (16.38 ± 0.92 for anxiety; 31.25 ± 1.28 for stress), although the differences were small. In terms of ethnicity, the Tiv subgroup (n = 55) had mean anxiety and stress scores of 16.64 ± 1.73 and 32.60 ± 1.78, respectively. The Igbo subgroup had mean scores of 17.33 ± 1.53 for anxiety and 31.00 ± 1.73 for stress, showed slight variations but without substantial disparities. For religion, the majority of participants were Christians (n = 59), with mean anxiety and stress scores of 16.64 ± 1.70 and 32.44 ± 1.82, respectively, closely aligning with the overall sample means. In all, baseline results indicate that anxiety and stress levels were fairly homogeneous across demographic groups, supporting comparability between subgroups before the intervention and strengthening the validity of subsequent group comparisons.

Pretest–Posttest Changes in Noise-Related Anxiety and Perceived Stress

Table 2 presents the descriptive analysis conducted to examine changes in noise-related anxiety (GAD-7-N) from baseline to postintervention across treatment groups and gender. At baseline, the CBMT group had an overall mean anxiety score of 16.14 ± 1.30, indicating moderate-to-severe anxiety. After the 8-week CBMT program, the group’s mean anxiety score decreased markedly to 6.90 ± 3.76, reflecting a mean reduction of Δ = 9.24 points. Both genders demonstrated substantial improvements. Mean anxiety scores in males decreased from 15.64 ± 0.92 at baseline to 7.73 ± 3.35 post-test (Δ = 7.91), whereas females showed as reduction from 16.44 ± 1.42 to 6.39 ± 4.00 (Δ = 10.05). In contrast, the control group showed minimal changes in anxiety scores. The overall mean anxiety score decreased slightly from 17.13 ± 1.88 at baseline to 16.39 ± 1.43 post-test, yielding a negligible mean reduction of 0.74 points. In the control group, mean anxiety scores in males decreased from 17.08 ± 2.47 to 16.25 ± 1.66 (Δ = 0.83), whereas females showed a reduction from 17.16 ± 1.46 to 16.47 ± 1.31 (Δ = 0.69). These descriptive results highlight a substantial decrease in anxiety among students who received CBMT, whereas anxiety levels in the control group remained largely unchanged.

The large effect size highlights the robust impact of CBMT on reducing noise-related anxiety.

DISCUSSION

Cognitive Behavioral Music Therapy and Noise-Related Anxiety

Findings from this quasi-experimental study revealed that students who received CBMT experienced significantly greater reductions in noise-related anxiety than those who received usual care. This result aligns with a growing body of evidence demonstrating that cognitive behavioral interventions, whether delivered alone or integrated with music and sound-based strategies, are highly effective in alleviating anxiety and sound-related distress. Randomized clinical trials have shown that structured CBT produces robust short- and long-term improvements in disorders characterized by aversive sound responses, such as misophonia.[30] Similarly, another study found that CBT led to clinically meaningful improvements in hyperacusis (heightened sound sensitivity), reinforcing its suitability for addressing sound-triggered anxiety.[5]

Over the past decade, research has increasingly recognized environmental noise as a public health concern that extends beyond hearing-related effects to include mental health outcomes. Accumulating evidence shows that long-term exposure to environmental noise is associated with elevated levels of anxiety, depression, and general psychological distress among adults.[338] Consistent with this evidence, the World Health Organization’s Environmental Noise Guidelines for the European Region highlight that noise exposure contributes to nonauditory outcomes such as annoyance, sleep disturbance, and mental health effects, and provide evidence-based recommendations for protecting health in community and occupational settings.[6] These findings reinforce the importance of addressing environmental noise as a meaningful psychological stressor and support the focus of the present study on interventions that target the psychological processes linking noise exposure with stress and anxiety.

The findings in the present study are consistent with earlier research showing that structured CBT interventions can reduce distress associated with sound sensitivity and noise-related conditions. Likewise, studies in music therapy and music-based relaxation have consistently reported reductions in physiological arousal and perceived stress, particularly when music is used to support emotional regulation and attentional control. What distinguishes the current study is the deliberate integration of these two approaches within a single manualized CBMT protocol, allowing cognitive restructuring to occur alongside music-supported down-regulation and graded auditory exposure. This combined approach may help explain the magnitude and consistency of the observed effects and suggests that pairing cognitive strategies with embodied regulation techniques can enhance engagement, skill transfer, and real-world applicability in nonclinical student populations.

Comprehensive reviews of CBT applications for tinnitus, hyperacusis, and misophonia consistently conclude that the approach reliably reduces distress and enhances daily functioning.[31] Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of music-based interventions report medium-to-large effects on stress and anxiety, confirming that structured music therapy can produce measurable psychological and physiological benefits.[2432] Studies that directly combine CBT with music or sound components often show enhanced outcomes compared with either method alone. For example, a study found that Music-CBT led to greater behavioral and neurophysiological improvements than music therapy or CBT delivered in isolation among patients with tinnitus.[28] Likewise, in nonclinical student populations, CBT augmented with music significantly reduced test-related anxiety compared with usual care or no intervention,[26] demonstrating the feasibility and value of integrating music into CBT protocols within educational settings similar to this study’s context.

The mechanisms underlying these effects support the dual-pathway rationale of CBMT. The CBT component primarily targets maladaptive appraisals, avoidance patterns, and worry, representing top–down cognitive-behavioral change. In contrast, the music-based component fosters bottom-up regulation by facilitating autonomic down-regulation, parasympathetic recovery, and attentional reorientation away from perceived threats.[2432] Neurophysiological evidence from Music-CBT research further indicates that combining these modalities produces complementary neural effects in auditory and prefrontal emotion-regulation circuits, offering a plausible explanation for the enhanced anxiety reduction observed in CBMT participants.[28]

The absence of a significant group × gender interaction in this study suggests that CBMT was equally effective for both male and female students, consistent with earlier findings showing that CBT and music-augmented therapies benefit diverse groups regardless of gender.[2431] Therefore, the present findings align closely with prior empirical work, reinforcing that (a) CBT is a validated treatment for sound-related distress, (b) music-based interventions independently yield strong anxiolytic and physiological benefits, and (c) the integration of these two evidence-based approaches (CBMT) can achieve anxiety reductions that are comparable or superior to those achieved through usual care or single-component interventions.

Cognitive Behavioral Music Therapy and Noise-Induced Perceived Stress

This study revealed a significant difference in noise-induced perceived stress between students who participated in CBMT and those who received usual care. Students in the CBMT group showed marked reductions in PSS-N scores, whereas the control group demonstrated little or no improvement. Participation in CBMT appeared to help students reinterpret their thoughts about persistent campus noise and apply music-assisted relaxation strategies to regulate their emotional and physiological stress responses more effectively. These findings are consistent with cognitive-behavioral theory and prior evidence showing that modifying catastrophic appraisals and strengthening adaptive coping skills can meaningfully reduce perceived stress.[39] They also align with previous studies that have combined CBT and music-based approaches. School- and university-based programs integrating CBT elements with structured music activities have produced significant decreases in stress and anxiety,[2426] while systematic reviews of music interventions consistently document reliable short-term reductions in both autonomic arousal and subjective stress.[252632]

The CBMT model likely produced its effects by pairing cognitive reappraisal with physiological regulation through music. The use of rhythmic entrainment, paced breathing, and attentional refocusing may have helped create a calmer internal state, allowing students to practice coping and exposure skills in a more manageable emotional context. These results also reflect findings from research on sound-triggered disorders, such as misophonia and hyperacusis, where randomized CBT trials have demonstrated substantial reductions in sound-related distress.[530] This suggests that the cognitive and regulatory mechanisms targeted in those clinical populations are transferable to managing noise-related stress in everyday campus environments. Notably, CBMT was effective for both male and female students, with no evidence of a treatment × gender interaction. This indicates that the stress-reducing effects of CBMT generalized across sexes, consistent with previous reports showing that CBT-based stress interventions tend to benefit men and women equally.[40] Therefore, these findings support CBMT as a feasible and nonpharmacologic strategy for mitigating noise-related stress among university students. By integrating cognitive restructuring with structured music-based relaxation, CBMT offers a culturally adaptable, accessible, and scalable intervention that could enhance campus mental health efforts to address the growing problem of chronic environmental noise. The agreement between the results of the primary and ITT analyses indicates the strength of the efficacy of CBT music therapy, and the effects remained consistent despite attrition.

Recommendations

In light of these findings, it is recommended that CBMT programs be incorporated into university counseling and wellness services as a standard, nonpharmacological approach to managing noise-related distress. Counselors and clinical psychologists should be trained to deliver structured CBMT sessions that combine cognitive restructuring with music-assisted relaxation techniques to enhance emotional regulation and resilience in university students. University administrators and public health policymakers are encouraged to recognize the mental health implications of environmental noise and to provide institutional and financial support for the implementation of CBMT alongside conventional noise-control strategies. In addition, future research should include long-term follow-up assessments to determine the durability of treatment effects and explore the applicability of CBMT among other noise-sensitive populations. Future research would benefit from incorporating objective physiological indicators, such as cortisol levels, heart rate variability, or electrodermal activity, to better understand the mechanisms through which CBMT influences stress and anxiety responses.

Limitations

This study is not without limitations. First, the absence of a follow-up assessment limits the ability to determine whether the observed reductions in stress and anxiety were sustained over time. Second, due to practical constraints within the university setting, participants were not fully randomized to the treatment or control groups, which may have introduced selection bias and limited the generalizability of the findings. Despite these limitations, the study provides strong preliminary evidence that CBMT can serve as an effective and accessible intervention for mitigating the psychological impact of chronic noise exposure in university students. The sample in this study was drawn from a single university within a specific cultural and environmental context, which may differ from other populations in important ways, including typical noise exposure patterns, environmental density, daily demands, and available coping resources. For instance, industrial workers, healthcare personnel, and residents of highly urbanized areas may experience more persistent or higher-intensity noise and may respond to these exposures differently than university students. However, the observed reductions in anxiety and perceived stress suggest possible broader relevance; replication across diverse cultural, occupational, and environmental settings is needed before the findings can be confidently generalized beyond university populations.

CONCLUSION

This study provides preliminary evidence that a manualized CBMT may reduce noise-related anxiety and perceived stress among university students. Given the quasi-experimental design and the reliance on self-reported measures, the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Nevertheless, the results support further investigation of CBMT as a potentially accessible, nonpharmacological approach for managing noise-related psychological distress in campus settings. Future studies employing randomized designs, longer follow-up periods, and objective physiological indicators are warranted to clarify long-term effectiveness, underlying mechanisms, and generalizability across diverse populations.
