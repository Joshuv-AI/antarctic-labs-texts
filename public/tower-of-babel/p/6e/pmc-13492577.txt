Should H2‐Blockers Be Used for the Treatment of Laryngopharyngeal Reflux?
Authors: Sophia Linguiti, W. Jack Palmer, Janvi J. Shukla, Kathleen Tibbetts, Colin Huntley, Joseph Spiegel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLaryngopharyngeal reflux (LPR) is a highly prevalent condition in otolaryngology practice, characterized by reflux of gastric contents into the pharynx and larynx. Although proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) remain first‐line therapy, many patients report persistent symptoms. In refractory cases, histamine‐2 receptor antagonists (H2RAs) are often employed as adjunctive or standalone therapy due to their rapid onset and accessibility. However, their utility as daily therapy is limited by tachyphylaxis, with significant loss of acid suppression within days of continuous use. Despite longstanding evidence from gastroenterology literature discouraging daily H2RA therapy, these agents remain frequently prescribed in otolaryngology practice. Contemporary ear, nose, and throat (ENT)‐specific guidance on their role in LPR is lacking. In patients with ongoing laryngopharyngeal symptoms despite optimized PPI therapy, a systematic, phenotype‐driven approach is recommended, incorporating objective testing with hypopharyngeal‐esophageal pH‐impedance monitoring, alternative agents including alginates, and consideration of surgical or endoscopic interventions. Greater adherence to evidence‐based recommendations may improve outcomes.

Laryngopharyngeal reflux (LPR) is a highly prevalent disorder in otolaryngology practice, characterized by retrograde movement of gastric contents into the pharynx and larynx, producing symptoms such as hoarseness, throat clearing, globus sensation, and chronic cough. 1 , 2 Historically conceptualized as an extraesophageal manifestation of gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), LPR is now recognized as a heterogeneous symptom complex with distinct pathophysiology and diagnostic challenges. 2 , 3 Unlike GERD, which typically presents with heartburn and regurgitation, LPR often occurs without classic esophageal symptoms and involves unique mechanisms of laryngeal injury. 1 , 2 The nonspecific nature of laryngopharyngeal symptoms, overlap with functional and inflammatory laryngeal disorders, and absence of universally accepted diagnostic criteria contribute to substantial variability in diagnosis and treatment response. 3 , 4 Despite these uncertainties, LPR accounts for up to 10% of otolaryngology visits and carries a substantial healthcare burden. 3 Empiric acid suppression with proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) therefore remains a common initial strategy in suspected LPR, although response is frequently incomplete. 3 , 4 In this context, histamine‐2 receptor antagonists (H2RAs) are often introduced as adjunctive or alternative acid‐suppressive therapy. Given increasing recognition of the pharmacologic limitations of H2RAs with repeated dosing, it may be time to reconsider their role in contemporary LPR management.

H2RAs act by reversibly inhibiting histamine binding at parietal cell H2 receptors, thereby reducing gastric acid secretion. 1 However, continuous daily dosing leads to a marked reduction in antisecretory efficacy within days, with studies demonstrating significant loss of acid suppression by approximately day 5 of therapy. 5 This tachyphylaxis is attributed to compensatory increases in endogenous histamine release and enterochromaffin‐like cell activity and cannot be overcome by dose escalation. 5 A 2002 study from Fackler et al demonstrated that the initial benefit of adding a bedtime H2RA to PPI therapy for nocturnal acid breakthrough is lost within approximately 1 week, with no sustained improvement in gastric pH control after 28 days of continued administration. 6 Although most tachyphylaxis data derive from GERD populations, this pharmacologic phenomenon is directly applicable to LPR, as the mechanism of acid suppression is identical regardless of clinical indication. Consistent with these physiologic data, contemporary gastroenterology guidance highlights the limited role of H2RAs as daily adjuncts and supports their use primarily on an intermittent or as‐needed basis. 7

Despite these limitations, H2RAs remain commonly employed in otolaryngology practice for suspected LPR. Practice‐pattern analyses suggest that laryngology‐trained otolaryngologists are more likely to prescribe combination reflux regimens, including concurrent PPI and H2RA therapy, underscoring the persistence of this approach in contemporary ENT care. 8 Although empiric PPI therapy is widely regarded as first‐line treatment for suspected LPR, there remains a lack of contemporary otolaryngology‐specific guidance addressing the optimal role and duration of adjunctive H2RA therapy, representing an important gap in specialty consensus. 3 , 4

Increasing evidence suggests that laryngopharyngeal injury may result from non‐acid refluxate components, including pepsin and bile acids, or from non‐reflux mechanisms, helping to explain the limited and variable response to acid‐targeted therapies in some patients. 9 For patients with persistent laryngopharyngeal symptoms despite initial acid‐suppressive therapy, contemporary LPR paradigms increasingly emphasize systematic reassessment rather than indefinite escalation of empiric pharmacologic treatment. Recommended strategies include confirmation of medication adherence and dosing, reconsideration of the working diagnosis, and objective reflux testing when clinically appropriate to define reflux burden and phenotype. 3 , 7 Hypopharyngeal‐esophageal multichannel intraluminal impedance‐pH monitoring can characterize both acid and non‐acid reflux events and may facilitate more individualized therapeutic decision‐making in patients with refractory symptoms. 3 , 7

At the same time, the multifactorial nature of LPR has prompted growing emphasis on adjunctive and non‐acid‐targeted therapies. Barrier agents such as alginates may reduce laryngopharyngeal exposure to refluxate through mechanical raft formation and have demonstrated benefit in selected patient populations. 4 , 7 Neuromodulatory or behavioral interventions may be appropriate in patients whose symptom profile suggests laryngeal hypersensitivity or functional laryngeal disorders. 3 , 7 In carefully selected individuals with objective evidence of persistent pathologic reflux, procedural anti‐reflux interventions may be considered within a comprehensive phenotype‐directed management framework. 3 , 7

In summary, while H2RAs remain commonly employed in otolaryngology practice for the management of LPR, their effectiveness as continuous daily therapy is intrinsically limited by rapid tachyphylaxis with repeated dosing. In a condition characterized by heterogeneous mechanisms and variable response to acid suppression, prolonged dual acid‐suppression strategies, including chronic H2RA therapy, should be approached judiciously. Greater alignment of LPR management with contemporary reflux principles, including intermittent rather than continuous H2RA use and phenotype‐directed evaluation of persistent symptoms, may improve outcomes for patients with laryngopharyngeal complaints. 3 , 4 , 7
