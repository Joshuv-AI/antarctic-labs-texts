Balloon-occluded Retrograde Transvenous Obliteration for Gastric Varices Draining into the Gastrocaval Shunt
Authors: Shiro Miyayama, Masashi Yamashiro, Takumi Sugiura, Nobuhiko Ogawa, Fumito Ozaki, Naoko Sakuragawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe left inferior phrenic vein has two orifices and consists of transverse and vertical parts. A total of 10%-15% of fundal gastric varices drain into the gastrocaval shunt, a hypertrophied transverse part of the left inferior phrenic vein. The outlet of the gastrocaval shunt is occasionally kinked and stenotic, and the transverse part of the left inferior phrenic vein is usually connected to several vessels. Additionally, portopulmonary venous anastomosis is occasionally accompanied by gastric varices draining into the gastrocaval shunt without developing a gastrorenal shunt. These anatomical characteristics make it difficult to embolize gastric varices draining into the gastrocaval shunt with balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous obliteration. We describe fundamental techniques for catheterizing and obliterating gastric varices draining into the gastrocaval shunt using 5% ethanolamine oleate.Keywords: gastric varices, balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous obliteration, gastrocaval shunt

Introduction

The left inferior phrenic vein (LIPV) has a unique configuration and it usually has two main orifices [1-3]: one enters the inferior vena cava (IVC) or the left hepatic vein (LHV) via the subdiaphragmatic transverse part of the LIPV under the left diaphragm; and the other enters the left renal vein via the vertical part of the LIPV and the left adrenal vein. The vertical part of the LIPV develops as a gastrorenal shunt (GRS) in patients with gastric varices due to portal hypertension [1-9]. A total of 80%-85% of gastric varices located at the fundus drain into the GRS, and 10%-15% drain into the IVC through a gastrocaval shunt (GCS), which is a hypertrophied transverse part of the LIPV [4, 5]. Additionally, several vessels are connected to the transverse part of the LIPV (Figure 1) [3, 10-19].

These anatomical characteristics make it difficult to embolize gastric varices that drain into the GCS with balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous obliteration (BRTO). Therefore, the purpose of this paper was to describe fundamental techniques of BRTO using 5% EOI (mixture of 10% ethanolamine oleate [Oldamin; Takeda, Osaka, Japan] and the same amount of iopamidol 300 mgI/mL [Iopamiron 300; Bayer, Osaka, Japan]) for gastric varices draining into the GCS.

Anatomy of LIPV

The transverse and vertical parts of the LIPV typically connect through one or more anastomotic veins and form a continuous vein [1]. The vertical part of the LIPV is typically a single trunk; however, two vertical parts (ring type) are observed in 3% of cases of gastric varices (Figure 2) [7]. The transverse part of the LIPV also consists of a single trunk; however, we encountered a patient with two separate trunks (Figure 3). The outlet of the transverse part of the LIPV is occasionally kinked and may also show stenosis (Figures 4 and 5) [13, 20].

The transverse part of the LIPV is typically connected to the pericardiacophrenic and intercostal veins (Figures 2-10), as well as to the internal thoracic vein (Figures 4, 6, and 9). Furthermore, portopulmonary venous anastomosis (PPVA), which connects the paraesophageal and pulmonary veins as a collateral pathway due to portal hypertension [17-19, 21, 22], is occasionally accompanied by gastric varices that drain into the GCS without developing a GRS (Figure 10) [17-19]. Therefore, several collateral pathways are usually demonstrated under balloon occlusion of the transverse part of the LIPV (Figures 4-10) [3, 10-16]. Additionally, the GRS is frequently demonstrated through one or more communications (Figures 6-8).

BRTO for gastric varices draining into the GCS

Selection of an access route

We access the gastric varices via the right femoral vein. When contrast-enhanced CT shows that the GCS is the main outlet of the gastric varices, catheterization into the transverse part of the LIPV is attempted first. When a hypertrophied GRS is also demonstrated on CT, a balloon catheter is first introduced into the GRS because this is usually easier than advancing one into the transverse part of the LIPV.

Under balloon occlusion of the main draining vein, another part of the LIPV is frequently demonstrated as a collateral outlet (Figures 2, 3, and 5-8). Therefore, two catheters are occasionally needed to complete BRTO for gastric varices draining into the GCS (Figures 5-8). We typically insert the second catheter into the right femoral vein just below the first catheter, if necessary. When a balloon catheter has already been introduced into the GRS, it is easier to advance another catheter into the transverse part of the LIPV using fluoroscopic guidance with contrast injection through the balloon catheter. However, we encountered a patient in whom catheterization into the LIPV entering the LHV was unsuccessful. This patient had a small vessel directly entering the IVC (considered to be the independent branch of the pericardiacophrenic vein), which was connected to the transverse part of the LIPV, and a microcatheter could be navigated into the transverse part of the LIPV through the anastomosis. The LIPV and this vessel were subsequently embolized with metallic coils, and a sclerosant was then injected into the gastric varices via the balloon catheter placed in the GRS (Figure 5).

We usually select a balloon catheter to occlude another outlet of the LIPV when it is large and there is a possibility of injecting a sclerosant into the varices through the catheter. Coil embolization is performed when the outlet is relatively small and injection of a sclerosant is unnecessary, or when insertion of a balloon catheter is impossible.

Catheterization into the transverse part of the LIPV

We use a 5-F shepherd hook catheter to catheterize the GCS (Figures 4 and 5). However, modification of the catheter shape with steam heat is occasionally required because the diameter of the IVC is frequently larger than the configuration of the pre-shaped catheter. A 5-F cobra-type catheter is also used when the shepherd hook catheter fails to select it. The LIPV orifice is typically located on the front left wall of the IVC or the left wall of the LHV (Figures 4-6, 8, and 9). Rapid backflow of injected contrast material into the IVC can confirm that the catheter tip is in the LIPV (Figure 4). A 0.035-In hydrophilic guidewire is then advanced deeply into the transverse part of the LIPV. Thereafter, the catheter is exchanged for an angiographic catheter or occlusion balloon catheter over the guidewire. An angled-tip catheter makes it easier to traverse the kinked outlet of the LIPV. If the pre-shaped angled-tip balloon catheter cannot traverse it, additional bending of the catheter tip using steam heat can help with catheterization (Figure 4). However, steam heat can also damage the balloon and cause it to rupture. Therefore, it is necessary to prevent steam from contacting the balloon during shaping. The use of an angioplasty balloon catheter for occlusion of the transverse part of the LIPV has also been reported [16]. This technique is worth trying when the occlusion balloon catheter cannot traverse there because the friction between the vessel wall and the angioplasty balloon may be lower than that between the vessel wall and the occlusion balloon. When a microballoon catheter >6 mm in diameter is used, an appropriate guiding catheter is advanced into the LIPV.

Embolization of collateral pathways, including communication between the transverse and vertical parts of the LIPV

When collateral pathways are demonstrated on a venogram, it is important to confirm whether they connect to the systemic circulation. If not, they can safely be embolized, even with liquid and/or particle embolic agents.

Small low-flow vessels can be occluded by injecting a small amount of 5% EOI, 50% glucose solution, absolute ethanol, and/or gelatin sponge particles. We encountered a patient with cerebral embolism related to BRTO for gastric varices draining into the GCS, possibly resulting from the migration of clots or gelatin sponge particles into the pulmonary or systemic circulation during collateral embolization (Figure 9) [15]. Although the responsible vessel and causative agent could not be identified in this case, gelatin sponge particles of approximately 2 mm in diameter were used. When a small collateral vessel is embolized with gelatin sponge particles, particles >2 mm should be prepared to prevent systemic embolization [15].

Medium-sized low-flow vessels can also be obliterated with EOI; however, a relatively large amount of EOI is needed. Therefore, embolization of these vessels with metallic coils can reduce the total volume of EOI necessary [3]. High-flow vessels should be embolized with metallic coils, and additional injection of a small amount of sclerosant can reduce the number of metallic coils needed by enhancing their embolic effect. However, absolute ethanol should not be used for embolization of the pericardiacophrenic vein because serious complications, such as coma or ventricular fibrillation, may occur when it flows into this vessel [3]. The double-balloon catheterization technique is also effective for BRTO of gastric varices draining into both the GRS and GCS (Figure 6) [3, 9].

When the collateral pathway connects to the systemic circulation, it should be embolized using metallic coils and/or vascular plugs.

Injection of sclerosant into gastric varices

When the main efferent vein of the gastric varices is opacified after embolization of collateral vessels, the sclerosant is injected until filling the entire gastric varices (Figures 4-11). If coil embolization of a medium-sized low-flow collateral vessel is impossible, it is necessary to advance a microcatheter beyond its orifice before sclerosant injection. We infuse EOI for 30 minutes until the varices are filled, and infusions are repeated until the main efferent vein and varices, as well as the main afferent vein, are filled with EOI and clots, and additional EOI does not fill the varices [23].

To minimize the total volume of sclerosant needed, a microcatheter should be advanced as close as possible to the gastric varices [3, 8]. This can also prevent the migration of embolic agents and/or clots to the right atrium through collateral pathways because they may cause systemic embolization in cases without a PPVA due to hepatopulmonary syndrome, which is characterized by pulmonary microvasculature dilation, the existence of a foramen ovale, and pulmonary arteriovenous malformation (Figure 9) [15].

Obliteration of the gastric varices with selective injection of a mixture of n-BCA (Histoacryl; B.Braun, Tuttlingen, Germany) and iodized oil (Lipiodol 480; Guerbet Japan, Tokyo, Japan) has been reported in a case of unsuccessful embolization of high-flow collateral veins [3]. In this technique, two microcatheters are used. First, one microcatheter is advanced into the varices through the main draining vein. Second, another microcatheter is also advanced into the main draining vein beyond the orifice of the collateral vessel, and the main draining vein is then embolized with microcoils. Finally, the mixture of n-BCA and iodized oil is injected via the microcatheter placed in the varices until the varices are entirely opacified [3]. The sclerosant can also be changed to EOI when contrast material successfully stagnates in the varices after coil embolization. If the microcatheter is turned back in the main draining shunt, the same steps can be completed only via one access route [13].

Unsuccessful advancement of a balloon catheter into the transverse part of the LIPV

With outlet narrowing and/or kinking of a GCS [13, 20], a balloon catheter or guiding sheath for a microballoon catheter occasionally cannot traverse it [13]. In such a situation, the transverse part of the LIPV can be embolized with metallic coils when the gastric varices have a GRS. In cases where the varices have no GRSs, advancement of two microcatheters into the GCS, one for coil deployment and the other for injection of sclerosant, is usually necessary [24]. Successful variceal obliteration with coil-assisted RTO via a single access route using a steerable microcatheter has also been reported [13].

We encountered a patient with unsuccessful advancement of either a 5-F balloon catheter or a 4-F guiding sheath for a microballoon catheter into the GCS due to severe outlet stenosis. In this case, the flow of the LIPV could be blocked by the mass effect of a 5-F shepherd hook catheter placed in the stenosed portion. Consequently, the sclerosant injected via the GRS under balloon occlusion could be successfully stagnated in the varices without leakage (Figure 5). We also encountered a small paraesophageal vein connected to the azygous vein that drained from the gastric varices, although this case did not match the focus of the present paper because of the absence of a GCS. Advancement of a 5-F catheter into this vessel could stall its flow by the catheter's mass effect itself and stretching the tortuous vessel portion. By injecting the sclerosant via another balloon catheter placed in the GRS, BRTO was then successfully performed without complications (Figure 10). This technique can reduce the procedural time and costs compared with coil embolization. If the collateral flow cannot be stopped by catheter insertion alone, additional coil embolization should be performed.

Identification and embolization of PPVA

PPVA is known as one of the causes of systemic embolization following the treatment of gastroesophageal varices. The reported diameter of PPVA is 2-7 mm [22], and CT and MRI can depict most PPVAs. However, PPVA can arise from various sites of the paraesophageal vein, and Sano et al. [21] reported PPVA arising at the level of the hilus of the left lung. Therefore, thoracic CT is also necessary to check for the presence of PPVA. When a PPVA is demonstrated on preprocedural CT and/or MRI or during the BRTO procedure, embolization of the PPVA with metallic coils or distal advancement of a balloon catheter beyond the PPVA is required (Figure 11) [17, 18]. If a transvenous approach to PPVA is difficult, coil embolization via a percutaneous transhepatic portal venous approach has been reported [19, 25].

Conclusions

BRTO is an effective treatment for gastric varices. However, certain techniques must be employed to ensure the safe and effective use of BRTO for gastric varices draining into the GCS. We should be familiar with these techniques.
