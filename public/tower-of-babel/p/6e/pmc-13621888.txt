Incremental yield of genome sequencing after exome sequencing for nonimmune hydrops fetalis spectrum
Authors: Rozlyn CT Boutin, Billie R Lianoglou, Nuriye N Sahin Hodoglugil, Katie Tick, Carmen MA Santoli, Ugur Hodoglugil, Pierre Martin, Jessica Van Ziffle, Patrick Devine, Neil Risch, Mary E Norton, Teresa N Sparks
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOBJECTIVE:Exome sequencing (ES) detects a monogenic disorder in approximately 30% of pregnancies with unexplained nonimmune hydrops fetalis spectrum (NIHFS), defined as the presence of one or more pathologic fetal fluid collection. Since genome sequencing (GS) can identify additional genetic variants not detectable by ES, we sought to determine the proportion of cases with new reportable findings on GS following non-diagnostic standard of care testing, including ES, for NIHFS.STUDY DESIGN:Cohort study of pregnancies with NIHFS that had nondiagnostic results of karyotype and/or chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) and ES. Eligible fetal fluid collections included at least one of: ascites, pleural or pericardial effusions, skin edema, cystic hygroma, and/or nuchal translucency ≥3.5 mm. Pregnancies with NIHFS that did not receive a clear diagnosis with prior testing underwent additional testing with GS. ES and GS were performed in a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments–approved laboratory, and genetic variants were classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines. Primary outcome was the proportion of new reportable pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) variants identified on GS and resulting in a new diagnosis. Secondary outcome was the incremental yield of GS for detecting new secondary findings and variants of uncertain significance (VUSs).RESULTS:Overall, 118 fetal samples underwent GS following negative (n=102) or inconclusive (n=16) ES. GS identified four P/LP copy number variants previously not detected by karyotype, CMA, or ES that led to a new genetic diagnosis in three pregnancies, providing a new positive finding yield of 2.5% (3/118). Additionally, one secondary finding and four VUSs were identified by GS in four different pregnancies. In contrast to the primary outcome, these additional findings were largely the result of updated analytical tools and literature rather than GS-specific technology.CONCLUSION:GS has a small but clinically relevant increased diagnostic yield over chromosome studies and ES for NIHFS, particularly for identifying copy number variants. As interpretation of noncoding regions and complex genomic variations improves, the incremental positive finding yield of GS is expected to further increase.Keywords: copy number variants, exome sequencing, fetal fluid collection, genetic testing, genome sequencing, nonimmune hydrops fetalis, prenatal diagnosis, single-gene disorders

Introduction

The landscape of prenatal genetic testing has evolved rapidly in recent decades, increasing our ability to identify fetal genetic diseases, optimize multidisciplinary management, and pave the way for emerging fetal therapies. Karyotype and chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) have traditionally been the first-line prenatal genetic tests and can detect large chromosomal abnormalities and smaller subchromosomal copy number variants (CNV), respectively.1,2 Over the past few years, exome sequencing (ES) has become increasingly clinically available and utilized to identify fetal single-gene disorders not detected by first-line testing in anomalous pregnancies.3–5 ES interrogates the 1% to 2% of the genome that codes for proteins6,7 and can detect single nucleotide changes and intragenic insertions and deletions (indels) less than 50 to 100 base pairs (bp).8–13 Since the implementation of ES for prenatal diagnosis, the analytical pipelines, variant calling algorithms, and knowledge of gene-disease relationships have improved substantially. More recently, genome sequencing (GS) has emerged as a diagnostic tool with a faster turnaround time than ES14 and the ability to assess nearly the entire genome. GS has improved coverage of some protein-coding regions,15,16 examines nonprotein-coding regions, and can detect aberrations difficult to ascertain on ES such as moderately sized CNVs ≥50 bp.17,18

Developments in genetic testing technology have been particularly impactful for the diagnosis and management of pregnancies with nonimmune hydrops fetalis spectrum (NIHFS), for which single-gene disorders are a frequent cause. Many of the same underlying single-gene disorders are known to underlie pregnancies with single versus multiple fluid collections; the term NIHFS represents this diagnostic overlap and highlights the importance of a complete evaluation for pregnancies with one abnormal fluid collection. The term NIHFS was recently recognized in a consult from the Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine.8,9,19–22 Several studies utilizing ES have identified single-gene disorders in approximately 30% of pregnancies with NIHFS after nondiagnostic first-line testing.8–13 The types of single-gene disorders leading to NIHFS are broad and associated with variable long-term prognoses, including those affecting the lymphatic, cardiac, hematologic, metabolic, and other pathways. Little has been published to date on the diagnostic yield of GS for prenatal diagnosis in the context of fetal anomalies, with findings of small incremental positive finding yields over ES and only one study that focused on NIHFS specifically.14,23 GS offers the potential to uncover novel genetic variants for pregnancies with NIHFS that are not explained by karyotype, CMA, and ES, and has important implications for diagnosis and management of these complex pregnancies.

This study addresses a critical gap in the literature by evaluating the incremental positive finding yield of GS in pregnancies with unexplained NIHFS. Specifically, we aim to quantify the incremental positive finding yield of GS for identifying disease-causing variants in pregnancies with NIHFS that underwent deep phenotyping and received nondiagnostic ES results after an uninformative karyotype and/or CMA. We hypothesized that GS would provide an increase in positive finding yield over ES, primarily through identification of structural and nonprotein-coding variants undetectable by other genetic tests.

Materials and methods

This is a cohort study of pregnancies with NIHFS that remained unexplained after standard of care genetic testing, including ES. Pregnancies were enrolled from across the United States between 2018 and 2022 and underwent ES after nondiagnostic CMA and/or karyotype and other standard evaluations for NIHFS.19,22 ES results and methodology for this cohort have been previously described.8,9 We enrolled pregnancies with NIHFS, including at least one of the following: fetal ascites, unilateral or bilateral pleural effusion, pericardial effusion, and/or skin edema. We also enrolled pregnancies with cystic hygroma (CH) or increased nuchal translucency (NT≥3.5 mm). Pregnancies were eligible for inclusion if the abnormal fetal fluid collections were documented at any time during pregnancy, including prior to stillbirth, termination of pregnancy, or delivery of a liveborn. Pregnancies with concurrent structural anomalies were also included. Pregnancies with an identified etiology for NIHFS such as alloimmunization, congenital infection, placental chorioangioma, or twin–twin transfusion syndrome were excluded. Pregnancies were additionally excluded if insufficient high-quality DNA remained from the original fetal samples. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF) Institutional Review Board (IRB 17-21662 and 22-36344).

Participants who did not receive a clear diagnosis following consent to the initial ES study and who consented to future research were eligible for inclusion in the current study and underwent GS. Those with clear pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) variants identified on ES that established an explanation for the NIHFS were excluded, as were those with insufficient or poor quality fetal or infant DNA samples available for GS. Prior to initiation of GS, charts were systematically reviewed, and additional records were obtained to extract any new phenotypic information identified during and/or after pregnancy to inform variant filtering and interpretation.24,25 This included collection of postnatal phenotype where available and for as long as possible among survivors. Using the updated phenotype, ES reanalysis was requested for all cases within one year prior to initiating GS. Pregnancies for which ES reanalysis clearly identified one or more diagnostic variant did not undergo GS. We allowed inclusion of pregnancies with variants of uncertain significance (VUSs) on ES given the clinical uncertainty of those variants. Extracted DNA remaining from the original ES was used to perform GS.

Medical records were reviewed to extract obstetric and family history; laboratory results, including infectious and genetic studies; imaging reports from throughout the pregnancy (antenatal ultrasound, fetal echocardiogram, and magnetic resonance imaging); pre and postnatal pathology results; pregnancy complications; delivery outcomes; and postnatal clinical data including laboratory studies, imaging reports, dysmorphic features, and comorbidities. Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms were used to describe pre and postnatal phenotypes to inform variant interpretation for ES reanalyses and GS.26–28

Pregnancies were categorized according to the type(s) of abnormal fetal fluid collections observed across the entirety of each pregnancy: (1) increased NT or CH without other abnormal fluid collections; (2) single abnormal fetal fluid collection including any one of: pleural effusion, pericardial effusion, ascites, or skin edema; or (3) two or more abnormal fetal fluid collections, consisting of pleural effusion, pericardial effusion, ascites, and/or skin edema. The presence or absence of additional structural anomalies was also documented, including those affecting the abdominal wall, central nervous system, face, gastrointestinal tract, genitalia, heart, lungs, renal system, or musculoskeletal system.

GS was performed between 2022 and 2024 at the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments program (CLIA)-certified UCSF Genomic Medicine Laboratory. For each sample, paired-end sequencing (Illumina NovaSeq 6000) was done after DNA was prepared using the Roche KAPA EvoPlus PCR-free prep kit. The data were aligned to the human reference genome build GRCh38 (hg38) utilizing Illumina’s DRAGEN (Dynamic Read Analysis for GENomics) algorithm. This streamlined method takes raw sequencing data (FASTQ files) and converts it into fully genotyped variant call formats (VCF/gVCF) with high precision and sensitivity. DRAGEN employs a suite of integrated algorithms to accurately identify the entire spectrum of genetic variations. It simultaneously calls single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), insertions/deletions (Indels), structural variants (SVs), and CNVs from a single dataset. The average read depth of samples was 40×. The VCF files (SNV, SVS, and CNV) were uploaded to Moon (Diploid) or Emedgene (Illumina) for analysis. Variants were classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Medical Pathology recommendations.29 Laboratory clinical bioinformatic experts performed an initial manual variant curation, and variant classification was finalized during a weekly multidisciplinary meeting with experts in clinical informatics, bioinformatics, statistical/population genetics, clinical genetics, pathology, perinatology, pediatrics, and other relevant clinical specialties at UCSF. Variants were reviewed in the context of the clinical history and phenotype to provide an overall case classification. See Supplemental Methods for additional details on ES and GS protocols, variant calling, and analysis.

Primary findings were biallelic or single variants classified as LP or P that resulted in a new diagnosis and were thought to explain the abnormal fluid collection(s). VUSs were reported if gene- or variant-level evidence was suggestive of potential clinical significance but insufficient for a classification of LP. Incidental findings were defined as variants with health implications that did not clearly explain the abnormal fluid collection(s), and these were reported due to inherent limitations of prenatal phenotyping and the potential for phenotypic expansion. Secondary findings were variants in genes associated with actionable health conditions as defined by the ACMG Secondary Findings List v3.2 and were only reported if participants originally consented to receiving these findings.30 All reportable variants were confirmed with orthogonal testing in the form of in-house Sanger sequencing or multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification at an independent CLIA-certified lab (University of Pittsburgh Medical Center). GS was run as proband-only, and inheritance of newly identified variants was clarified with follow-up targeted testing of parental samples at the time of orthogonal confirmation of the variant in the proband. In some cases, results of this parental testing did affect variant classification.

The primary outcome was the incremental positive finding yield of GS for detecting P/LP variants not identified by prior ES and associated with a new diagnosis. In addition to primary variants that explained the NIHFS, incidental GS findings were also considered part of the primary outcome either because they resulted in a new diagnosis or they offered the potential for phenotype expansion. Secondary outcome was the incremental diagnostic yield for new secondary findings and VUSs. Proportions with exact (Clopper–Pearson) 95% confidence intervals calculated using the binomial distribution were reported for primary and secondary outcomes. Demographic and phenotypic variables were summarized as proportions or median values with interquartile ranges as appropriate. Data were analyzed using R version 4.5.1 and RStudio software.31

Results

In total, 141 pregnancies with NIHFS and nondiagnostic results of karyotype and/or CMA as well as ES were potentially eligible. Of these, three were positive on ES reanalysis, and 20 had insufficient high-quality DNA for GS. GS was therefore completed in 118 cases (Figure). Among the cases that underwent GS, 16 had previously inconclusive ES results with at least one VUS that was not clearly the cause of the NIHFS, whereas 102 had negative ES results.

With regards to pregnancy outcomes, miscarriage or stillbirth occurred in 27.1%, pregnancy termination or selective reduction in 27.9%, and neonatal or childhood death in 14.4% of pregnancies. Overall, GS detected four new disease-causing variants not previously identified with ES in three pregnancies (Table 3), providing an incremental positive finding yield of 2.5% (3/118; 95% CI, 0.5%–7.3%) over previously performed standard of care genetic testing, including ES. All new diagnostic variants detected by GS were CNVs ranging in size from 135 to 387,976 bp, and all were located in protein-coding regions of the genome. In all three cases, previous CMA and ES had not detected these variants due to the size of CNVs being below and above the resolution of these tests, respectively. Three of the new variants detected by GS were inherited.

In terms of types of fluid collections, the first fetus with a new finding on GS had two abnormal fluid collections, the second had a CH, and the third had a single abnormal fluid collection (pleural effusion), respectively. All three fetuses with a new diagnosis on GS had additional structural anomalies beyond abnormal fetal fluid collections. The first fetus with a new diagnosis identified by GS had a paternally inherited primary finding; this was a contiguous deletion encompassing HBA1 and HBA2 that was not previously seen with ES. In combination with a maternally inherited missense HBA2 variant (hemoglobin Adana) in trans that was previously seen with ES (and again detected with GS), this led to a diagnosis of hemoglobin H-Adana-related alpha thalassemia. The second fetus had two new disease-causing primary variants that were identified by GS; this fetus was found to have biparentally inherited compound heterozygous deletions encompassing the LARGE1 gene associated with autosomal recessive muscular dystrophy-dystroglycanopathy (MDD). The third fetus had a de novo duplication of SMC1A and HUWE1 on the X chromosome, estimated on orthogonal testing to be mosaic at a level of 1.5 to 1.6X. This duplication is associated with X-linked nonsyndromic intellectual disability based on overlap of the involved region with the Xp11.22 microduplication syndrome (MIM 300705) and emerging evidence of triplosensitivity for HUWE1.32,33 Given the lack of known phenotypic overlap between this duplication and abnormal fetal fluid collections, this new molecular diagnosis was categorized as a likely incidental finding. Pregnancy and family history for two of the three cases were unremarkable; in the hemoglobinopathy case, there was a family history of intrauterine fetal demise at 24 weeks’ gestation in the maternal grandmother, and the patient had a history of two previous spontaneous abortions. Additional details of the variants and genetic conditions detected on GS are outlined in Table 3.

In contrast to the new molecular diagnoses corresponding to primary and incidental findings, all five variants in four pregnancies that were newly identified with GS and categorized as secondary findings and VUSs were SNVs that could be seen with ES. They became reportable with GS due to updated literature and/or new ClinVar entries in the intervening time between the ES reanalysis and later GS (Table 4 and Supplemental Table 3). New VUSs were reported in 3/118 (2.5%; 95% CI, 0.5%–7.3%) pregnancies. These included one fetus with two VUSs in RYR1 associated with an autosomal recessive primary finding, and two cases each with a single VUS (PRPF8 and FBXO11) associated with autosomal dominant disease that were categorized as likely incidental findings due to the lack of clear phenotypic overlap (Table 4). Among the 118 cases that underwent GS, 104 consented to disclosure of secondary findings. Only one LP variant associated with an autosomal dominant secondary finding of vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome was reportable following GS (1/104, 1.0%; 95% CI, 0.0%–4.6%). Finally, all VUSs that were originally seen with ES in 16 cases were again seen with GS.

Comment

Principal findings

In this large, national cohort of 118 pregnancies with NIHFS that remained unexplained following standard of care genetic testing, including ES, GS had a small but clinically important incremental positive finding yield of 2.5%. Specifically, GS detected deletions and duplications too small to be detected on CMA but too large to be detected on ES in three pregnancies, all in protein-coding regions of the genome. These variants are associated with a broad range of disease categories, including alpha thalassemia, MDD, and X-linked intellectual disability. In addition, the incremental yield for secondary findings and VUSs was 2.5% and 1.0%, respectively, although these were largely due to updated analytical tools and literature rather than GS-specific technology. The majority (5/9; 56%) of reportable variants following GS with known inheritance were inherited, highlighting the reproductive implications for biological parents of fetuses with NIHFS.

Results in the context of what is known

Two of the new genetic diagnoses made by GS in our study, alpha thalassemia due to hemoglobin H-Adana and MDD, have been previously reported in association with NIHFS.34–37 However, prior reports of MDD in association with NIHFS document genes other than LARGE1,36,37 and our case expands the MDD genes known to be associated with NIHFS. Additionally, microduplication of SMC1A and HUWE1 associated with nonsyndromic intellectual disability has not been previously reported in association with NIHFS. Based on current evidence, this molecular diagnosis most likely represents an incidental finding given the lack of clear phenotypic overlap and uncertain postnatal outcomes.32 However, microduplications involving HUWE1 have been identified in patients with seizures, abnormal neuroimaging findings, fetal growth restriction, and hypotonia.38,39 Additionally, there is increasing literature suggesting a potential association between neurodevelopmental disorders and NIHFS,40–42 making it possible that NIHFS represents a phenotypic expansion of HUWE1-associated duplication disorders.

Notably, all three new genetic diagnoses made with GS were the result of GS technology and its ability to identify deletions and duplications not detectable by CMA and ES. In contrast, the additional secondary findings and VUSs that became reportable following GS were largely the result of updated analytical tools and literature rather than GS-specific technology. It is well known that sequencing technology has continued to advance rapidly,43,44 but the value of increasing knowledge about gene- and variant-disease relationships over time is also critical to highlight.

Consistent with previous studies, the primary value of GS in our study was its ability to identify disease-causing SVs that were not detected by CMA and ES.45–48 The CNVs we detected exclusively with GS were below the reporting threshold for CMA and larger than typically detected with ES. ES typically detects smaller insertions and deletions (<20–50 bp), but alignment and variant resolution are hindered by incomplete genomic coverage and short captured fragments (~150–300 bp) that limit the ability of this technology to detect larger SVs and precisely define breakpoints that lie outside targeted exons. No new complex structural rearrangements, intronic single-nucleotide changes, or exonic single-nucleotide changes were detected by GS in our cohort. Likewise, while we did not detect any repeat expansion disorders, conditions such as congenital myotonic dystrophy type I have been associated with NIHFS49–51 and can be detected with GS.

Few studies to date have investigated the diagnostic utility of GS following uninformative karyotype, CMA, and ES in the context of fetal anomalies. Based on limited data, the incremental positive finding yield of GS is approximately 1% to 3% for unselected cohorts with single or multiple fetal anomalies.14,46–48 It is clear from the literature that the diagnostic yield of ES for NIHFS is relatively high compared to anomalous pregnancies without NIHFS.8–13 GS appears to have a similar incremental positive finding yield for NIHFS relative to what has been reported with other fetal anomalies, and the genetic variants uncovered with GS that we identified were all in protein-coding regions of the genome. It is likely that over time, the yield of GS for NIHFS and other fetal abnormalities will increase as more becomes understood about the relationship between nonprotein-coding regions of the genome and fetal disease.52–57

Clinical implications

These data contribute valuable information about the modest incremental positive finding yield of GS over standard of care genetic testing, including ES, expanding our knowledge of the genetic variants leading to fetal disease and the types of abnormal fluid collections indicative of specific underlying diseases. ES has already been transformative for prenatal diagnosis in the setting of fetal anomalies, with particularly high diagnostic yields for NIHFS that approximate 30%.8–13 GS has the potential to detect variants that would be seen with both ES and CMA and contributes a small but meaningful incremental yield over these tests with implications for clinical management.

Balancing risks and benefits, it is reasonable to offer GS as a follow-up test for information-seeking patients whose pregnancies are complicated by NIHFS when prior testing with karyotype and/or CMA is nondiagnostic.22 While further studies are needed to generate additional evidence for prenatal testing utilizing GS as a first-line genetic test along with its cost-effectiveness,58 it may soon serve as an effective test to more efficiently arrive at a diagnosis for these complex pregnancies. Overall, given the diagnostic utility of both ES and GS, it is increasingly important to offer these tests for prenatal diagnosis and to increase equitable access to these technologies for patients with NIHFS.

Research implications

Despite the high burden of single-gene etiologies underlying NIHFS, the incremental positive finding yield of GS following nondiagnostic ES is comparable to that observed for other fetal structural anomalies.14,46,48 As our understanding of gene-disease relationships and nonprotein-coding regions of the human genome expands, the incremental positive finding yield of GS is likely to increase. Indeed, the number of known disease-associated genes has nearly quadrupled since the first diagnosis of human disease using ES over 15 years ago, and there is increasing evidence of the role for the “dark genome” (ie, nonprotein-coding DNA) in human disease.59–62 Even within our cohort, we observed a similar incremental positive finding yield for ES reanalysis and GS, emphasizing the rapid evolution of knowledge in gene-disease relationships. Furthermore, emerging technologies such as long-read GS deserve further study for uncovering complex structural rearrangements, abnormal methylation, and other aberrations associated with NIHFS but not detected by other genetic testing, including the short-read GS as performed in our study.63,64

Moving forward, research focused on the implementation of GS clinically for pregnancies with NIHFS and other anomalies will be important. Performing a karyotype and/or CMA before ES as currently practiced as standard of care22 can add delay to a timely diagnosis when a single-gene disorder is present. GS can detect genetic variants that would be identified through karyotype and/or CMA as well as ES, in addition to other genomic abnormalities such as the disease-causing CNVs that we report.41,47,48,65–67 GS uses smaller amounts of DNA and can return results faster than ES47 and is likely that in the future, GS will replace the sequential first-line tests currently performed. Furthermore, our data suggest that with careful variant interpretation, GS does not lead to an unmanageable increase in secondary findings or VUSs. However, additional studies are needed to confirm the validity and cost-effectiveness of GS as a first-line test before it can be routinely implemented for pregnancies with NIHFS and other anomalies.

Strengths and limitations

Our study adds valuable data on the incremental positive finding yield of GS for NIHFS following nondiagnostic karyotype and/or CMA as well as ES, including a systematic approach to ES reanalysis prior to GS. We report not only primary findings, but also secondary findings, incidental findings, and VUSs. These data are beneficial for counseling given the lack of data published on the utility of GS for NIHFS and provide direction for future study. Our use of detailed phenotype with standardized HPO terms over the course of pregnancy and after delivery in bioinformatic analyses optimized the likelihood of identifying disease-causing variants.68 Additionally, we used a robust multidisciplinary and iterative process to review candidate variants for reporting in order to determine their classification and clinical significance.

Limitations include the small number of new findings with GS despite the relatively large sample size for a rare condition, and further study will allow for a deeper view of the types of variants and conditions that GS has the capacity to uncover in the context of NIHFS. Prenatal phenotyping is inherently limited as it is unable to identify features such as neurodevelopmental disorders or vision impairment. In addition, the unique fetal phenotypes of some genetic diseases are incompletely understood, and not all genotypes capable of causing fetal disease are known.14 As a result, certain variants may have been overlooked on ES or GS due to an apparent mismatch with the genotype and phenotype. Furthermore, in cases of pregnancy termination, intrauterine fetal demise, or stillbirth, it was not possible to evaluate phenotypic evolution, and this may have limited our ability to identify relevant disease-causing variants. The proband-first GS design and the unavailability of all parental samples to determine the inheritance of genetic variants could have affected the identification or classification of some variants. Finally, although we requested ES reanalysis within a year of completing GS, a study design comparing CMA plus ES against concurrent GS using the most up to date high-resolution CMA technology and ES variant calling algorithms that include CNV calling would have allowed for a more direct comparison of the diagnostic yield of these tests. Future research will be required to systematically compare the turnaround time and costs of GS versus ES.

Conclusions

In this large, national study of pregnancies with NIHFS, we identified an incremental positive finding yield of 2.5% for GS following previous nondiagnostic standard of care genetic testing, including ES. GS detected clinically relevant SVs associated with autosomal recessive and X-linked conditions, which were not identified by prior genetic tests. Despite the ability of GS to detect variants in nonprotein-coding regions of the genome, all new diagnoses were the result of SVs in protein-coding regions, which highlights the need for further research to explore the role of intronic and intergenic genomic regions in fetal disease. In contrast, the new secondary and uncertain variants we identified were largely the result of updated analytical tools and literature, rather than GS-specific technology. Additional studies are needed to further characterize the utility of GS for detecting disease-causing variants underlying NIHFS, determine the validity and cost-effectiveness of GS as a potential first-line prenatal diagnostic tool, and inform guideline development for the use of GS in pregnancies with NIHFS.

AJOG MFM at a Glance

Why was this study conducted?

Single-gene disorders represent an important cause of nonimmune hydrops fetalis and single abnormal fetal fluid collections (ie, nonimmune hydrops fetalis spectrum; NIHFS).

Genome sequencing has the potential to identify novel genetic variants not detected by karyotype and/or chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) and exome sequencing.

The diagnostic potential of genome sequencing for NIHFS and other fetal anomalies has not been well studied.

What are the key findings?

Genome sequencing has an incremental positive finding yield of 2.5% for NIHFS following nondiagnostic karyotype and/or CMA plus exome sequencing, identifying clinically relevant copy number variants (DNA insertions or deletions ≥50 base pairs).

What does this study add to what is already known?

Genome sequencing offers the ability to detect additional structural variants underlying NIHFS, allowing for improved clinical diagnosis and management.
