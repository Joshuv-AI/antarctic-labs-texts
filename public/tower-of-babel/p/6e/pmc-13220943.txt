Knowing your worth: a review of initial pulmonary and critical care academic offers
Authors: Laura Hinkle
Published: 2026

Searching for an initial faculty position in academic medicine and then negotiating its associated contract can be anxiety provoking for many trainees.1 This is the first opportunity many trainees have had to actively select their future employer and negotiate a contract, and they often find themselves ill-prepared for this task.2 A major knowledge gap trainees face is a lack of understanding of their worth. What is fair compensation for the value they add to an institution? What types of things should they negotiate for beyond salary? While the Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) publishes an annual Faculty Salary Report providing data for total compensation broken down by academic rank, specialty, practice location, etc,3 it is prohibitively expensive for trainees to access and does not provide information about nonmonetary aspects of compensation such as protected time for academic work, startup packages, and other important items important for success in academic medicine. In this issue of ATS Scholar, Olson et al. attempt to fill this void by describing first academic job offers for 60 recently graduated pulmonary and critical care medicine (PCCM) physicians.4

Graduates of PCCM training programs between 2020 and 2025 whose first job was in academic medicine were eligible to participate. Based on data that only 20% of all PCCM graduates enter academic medicine, the authors estimated that 720 physicians met inclusion criteria. They developed a survey and distributed it broadly, using a snowball sampling technique to maximize the response rate. A total of 60 participants completed the distributed survey, with 28 (47%) identifying as physician scientists and the remainder identifying as clinician educators. Respondents were able to provide quantitative data about a maximum of 3 job offers and provide comments. This work yielded information about 103 distinct offers.

Most respondents (57%) had accepted positions at the institution where they did their fellowship training. The median salary across all offers was $150K to $199K for physician scientists and $250K to $299K for clinician educators. Those who stayed at their home institution were offered a significantly higher salary compared with those who did not, while no differences in salary were noted based on gender or geographic location. Physician scientists who had received an early career development award were not offered higher salaries than those who did not, but were awarded higher startup packages. Less than half of those identifying as clinician educators (42.4%) were given protected time to devote to nonclinical work. A recurring theme in the free-text comments centered on the perceived lack of leverage or negotiating power for those who took positions at their home institution.

I believe some of the authors’ findings regarding compensation data deserve special attention: the impact of a career development award on startup packages, the lack of a gender compensation gap, and the lack of geographic difference in salary. The knowledge that physician scientists receiving an early career development award during fellowship were offered higher startup packages should challenge division and training program leadership to ensure that individuals on this career pathway are given the tools they need for long-term success during training: protected time to work, opportunities for skill development, and above all, excellent mentoring.5 Given the importance of physician scientists to the mission of academic medicine and the fact that they are widely recognized as an endangered species, every opportunity that presents to foster their growth and development should be taken advantage of. Another interesting finding was the lack of gender difference in the amount of startup packages awarded to physician scientists or in salaries as a whole for both physician scientists and clinician educators. While this data represents a small number of individuals, it is encouraging and differs from older published data.6 This may signal that efforts to close the gender-based compensation gap in our specialty are working, and future studies should evaluate whether this finding holds true on a broader scale. Finally, the lack of significant difference in salary across geographic regions is very surprising. The AAMC Faculty Survey data continues to demonstrate differences in academic medicine salaries based on geographic location.7 The authors appropriately collected geographic data based on region rather than at the state level to preserve anonymity of their participants. However, given the way the states are grouped, I suspect this led to the compensation data being skewed and that significant geographic differences in compensation actually still exist.

Another interesting finding centered on how offers divided the proposed full-time-equivalent (FTE). Less than half of respondents identifying as clinician educators had protected time for nonclinical work built into their contracts. Many of these individuals reported that the division of their FTE changed in their first year as faculty, although the authors did not describe whether this represented an increase or decrease in the amount of protected time for academic work. If increased, it may represent individuals gaining formal educational roles, such as course director, that provide funding for protected time. For those with no protected time, I question how many of them were not interested in having such time built into their contract in the first place. Reviewing comments in the supplementary data, one person described their job as the “[B]est of both worlds. I work with trainees without having the burden that comes with academic medicine.” In future work, defining more clearly what is meant by clinician educator to separate clinicians who work in an academic setting from clinician educators who have specific roles in the education realm would help define the resulting data set further, making it more useful to future first-time job seekers.

This study was both ambitious and very well executed. It meets its stated goal of providing physicians negotiating their first contract essential knowledge to be better advocates for themselves at the negotiating table and highlights the importance of nonmonetary aspects of job compensation, which may be more negotiable. The authors point out that collecting this data at a national level would be extremely helpful and provide better clarity for candidates in the future, and I agree this effort should be strongly considered by national organizations, with the results freely provided to trainees.
