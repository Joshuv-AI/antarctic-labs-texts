Unraveling the role of distinct cytoskeletal motility structures in Mycoplasma pneumoniae relatives
Authors: Jiaxin Chen, Yalan Jiang, Yifei Wang, Gao Zeng, Peng Liu, Jindou She, Keming Zhong, Baihuan Duan, Hong Huang, Yating Wen, Wenxin Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mycoplasma represents a unique genus of prokaryotic bacteria distinguished by the absence of a cell wall, a characteristic that sets it apart from other bacteria. Within the Mollicutes class, phylogenetic analysis reveals three distinct categories: Spiroplasma, Mycoplasma and Acholeplasma. Mycoplasmas within Pneumoniae are recognized for their capacity to induce a range of diseases in both humans and animals, frequently impacting respiratory and reproductive health. The representative strains in Pneumoniae group, particularly the M. pneumoniae clusters, have garnered significant attention due to their remarkable ability to adhere to, invade, and traverse host cells. This ability is facilitated by specialized structures known as attachment organelles, which possess a unique cytoskeletal structure that supports a distinctive gliding motility mechanism. This mode of motility is distinct from that observed in eukaryotes and the majority of bacteria. The gliding machinery of Mycoplasma is a complex assembly consisting of both surface and internal components, including a terminal button, paired plates, and a structure resembling a bowl or wheel. The internal architecture of the attachment organelles provides the essential scaffold for the operation of this sophisticated motility system. Mycoplasma’s gliding motility is crucial for its infection process and its capacity to evade the host immune defenses. Understanding the role of this motility to immune evasion can offer profound insights into the pathogenesis of these bacteria, could pave the way for the development of more effective therapeutic strategies against diseases caused by Mycoplasma and related species.

Introduction

The Mollicutes class are the smallest self-replicating, wall-less bacteria. Most Mollicutes are ubiquitous in nature and exist as commensals, although some are pathogenic to humans, animals and plants [1]. Phylogenetically, Mollicutes are classified into three phylogenetical categories: Spiroplasma, Mycoplasma and Acholeplasma [2]. Spiroplasma owns unique motility and helical morphology, and infects a diverse host range, including insects, plants, and aquatic crustaceans [3]. For instance, S. eriocheiris sp. nov infects the Chinese mitten crab, causing spasms and tremors [4, 5]. Mycoplasma is another typical genus of the class Mollicutes [6]. Evolving from gram-positive bacterial ancestors, Mycoplasmas have lost their peptidoglycan layer and undergone genome reduction, resulting in their distinctive wall-less structure (7, 8). These microorganisms exert a significant impact on the health of humans, animals, and plants. For example, M. pneumoniae is a common causative agent of atypical pneumonia [9, 10]. M. hominis [11], M. genitalium [12, 13], and Ureaplasma urealyticum (U. urealyticum) [14] are implicated in urinary and reproductive tract infections, often associated with various pregnancy complications [15, 16]. Consequently, elucidating the pathogenic mechanisms of Mycoplasma holds considerable promise for safeguarding human health and conferring significant socio-economic benefits [9].

Generally, motile bacteria move to acquire nutrients and evade waste and predators [17]. The form of motility varies among the species. Some use flagella for swimming in liquid, and others use the cell-surface machinery to move over solid surfaces. If a bacterium has motility, it will use the ability to survive and thrive. For many pathogenic species, their motility is a crucial virulence factor. Mycoplasmas in Pneumoniae cluster almost are motile bacteria [18]. These pathogens cause a variety of respiratory and urogenital diseases in humans and animals, as exemplified by M. pneumoniae being a significant causative agent of ‘walking pneumonia’ [19]. Most species in the Pneumoniae cluster have gliding capability on solid surface or host cell with the support of attachment organelle [18, 20, 21]. However, the proteins involved in the actual mechanisms underlying this motility is complex [22]. In this review article, we summarized the attachment organelles, cytoskeletal structures and gliding mechanisms of M. pneumoniae relatives, aiming to provide valuable insights into the mechanisms of motility that facilitate the Mycoplasma invasion.

The motility of M. Pneumoniae cluster

Mycoplasma species exhibit a remarkable polymorphism in morphology, encompassing spherical, rod-like, filamentous, and branching forms. Numerous Mycoplasma species, including M. mobile, M. penetrans, M. genitalium, and M. pneumoniae, are characterized with a cell protrusion, known as attachment organelle which is also referred to various in different species (e.g., apical structures, terminal organelles, and membrane processes) [23–25]. Currently, eight species are assigned to the M. pneumoniae cluster based on 16S rRNA sequence similarity [18, 22, 26]. The members of the M. pneumoniae cluster with gliding movement include M. pneumoniae, M. genitalium, M. gallisepticum, M. amphoriforme, M. imitans, M. testudines and M. pirum (Fig. 1A) [18].

Microorganisms, including Mycoplasma, promote their proliferation through movement to seek benefits and avoid harm [22]. Motility is essential for the survival and pathogenicity of many bacterial species, and certain Mycoplasma species have evolved a unique form of gliding motility, enabling biological movement on solid surfaces or host cells without the reliance on conventional appendages such as flagella [27]. Unlike flagellated bacteria, Mycoplasma species lack a general chemotactic signaling system to control their movement direction [28]. This gliding motility is characterized by continuous unidirectional movement, with the cell oriented parallel to the direction of travel, devoid of directional changes or backward motion [28]. High-precision measurements using optical microscopy have revealed rheotaxis in three Mycoplasma species, a gliding movement either toward or away from fluid flow. The morphology of Mycoplasma itself appears to function as a sensor, enabling the bacteria to recognize rheological properties akin to those found on host cell surfaces [28, 29]. This motility is thought to play a significant role in host colonization and infections [30, 31]. The motility of these motile Mycoplasma species is underpinned by their intracellular cytoskeleton within the attachment organelles and an adhesion complex anchored to the cell membrane. These adhesin complexes, connected to the internal core structure, execute a repetitive catch-pull-release cycle interacting with sialylated oligosaccharides on the host cell surface to propel the bacterium forward (Fig. 1B).

Energy source for gliding in the M. pneumoniae cluster

All motile behavior of bacteria requires energy source. Both thermal and chemical energies serve as direct energy sources for cell motility [34]. Many eukaryotic cells form membrane projections via the local aggregation of actin, primarily driven by thermal energy in the form of a Brownian ratchet [35]. The rotation of bacterial flagella, which propels swimming and swarming movements, is typically powered by the proton motive force [36] or the sodium motive force [37]. Most bacterial motility systems, including bacterial flagella, Myxococcus gliding, and Flavibacterium gliding, rely on the membrane potential rather than on ATP. As an exception, the pili motor system uses ATP energy [38].

Within the genus Mycoplasma, M. mobile has been systematically studied, revealing that its motility is driven by ATP energy. This was demonstrated by the use of membrane-destroying ‘ghost’ cells, which could be ‘reactivated’ by the addition of ATP [20, 39–41]. In studies of M. gallisepticum, gliding ‘ghost’ were successfully generated, and ATP was identified as the direct energy source for gliding [42]. Although experiments to reactivate the motility of M. pneumoniae ‘ghost’ have failed, M. pneumoniae may still rely on ATP for its motility. The Ser/Thr protein kinase (PrkC; MPN248) and phosphatase (PrpC; MPN247) genes influence the frequency and speed of gliding and the level of phosphorylation of the component proteins in attachment organelle. This suggests that altered local charges coupled with phosphorylation have a role in the gliding mechanism [43]. Additionally, a protein in the attachment organelle of M. pneumoniae, identified as an ATP-dependent protease, Lon, may function as a gliding motor [22]. These findings indicate that M. pneumoniae relatives rely primarily on ATP as their energy source. Interestingly, M. penetrans, which is phylogenetically distant from the M. pneumoniae cluster, appears to rely primarily on thermal energy for gliding [34]. If the gliding movement in Mycoplasma is powered by the Brownian ratchet mechanism, which converts heat into forward motion, this would represent a unique feature among prokaryotes. This suggests the presence of a yet-to-be-identified cytoskeletal component capable of polarization, polymerization, and depolymerization.

The attachment organelles in M. pneumoniae cluster species

Mycoplasma gliding is principally dependent on attachment organelles, which exhibit significant morphological variations across different species. The M. pneumoniae cluster serves as a prominent example of motile Mycoplasma. This cluster comprises eight described species, seven of them possess an attachment organelle (Fig. 1B) [18]. This organelle, intricately linked to cell division and DNA replication, performs essential functions in cellular activities, including cytoadherence and gliding motility [18, 19, 28]. M. pneumoniae surface proteins form tightly ordered rows, with thicknesses of up to 5.5 nanometers, on the extracellular surface of the attachment organelle [44]. These protein arrays correspond to a protruding nap structure visualized via negative-staining electron microscopy [25]. During gliding, conformational changes in this nap structure are synchronized with receptor binding and release events, producing consistent unidirectional movement toward the attachment organelle [45].

M. pneumoniae cluster species revealed substantial differences in the dimensions and ultrastructural features of the attachment organelles and their associated cell bodies. These differences included variations in attachment organelle length, width, and curvature, as well as cell body dimensions such as length and width. Studies have shown that correlation between cell width and attachment organelle width, but no relationship was found between cell length and other features. The attachment organelle is divided into substructures, namely the distal portion is referred to as the “knob” and the proximal region the “shaft” (Fig. 2A). M. gallisepticum (Fig. 2B), M. imitans (Fig. 2C), M. amphoriforme (Fig. 2D), and M. testudinis (Fig. 2E) display relatively short, wide attachment organelles, with mean lengths ranging from 120 to 150 nm and widths from 130 to 150 nm. The knob is distinct from the shaft in M. gallisepticum and M. amphoriforme, occasionally discernible in M. testudinis, but not in M. imitans. The M. pirum attachment organelle, also featuring a prominent knob, is elongated (250 nm) and moderately wide (100 nm), uniquely exhibiting three or four parallel striations approximately perpendicular to the long axis. (Fig. 2F). M. genitalium (Fig. 2G) and M. pneumoniae (Fig. 2H) possess narrow shafts. The M. genitalium structure (170 nm) is considerably shorter than that of M. pneumoniae (290 nm), which represents the longest within the cluster (Fig. 2G). Additionally, M. pneumoniae features a trailing filament, and species like M. genitalium and M. testudinis have curved attachment organelles with circular gliding patterns [18]. These morphological differences do not correlate with gliding speed or phylogenetic relatedness, indicating that the morphology of the attachment organelle is not a direct determinant of gliding motility.

Cytoskeletal structures in M. pneumoniae cluster species

Lacking a peptidoglycan layer, Mycoplasma relies on its cytoskeleton, often referred to as the gliding machinery, to support the attachment organelle. This gliding machinery can be broadly categorized into surface and internal structures (22, 25). The internal cytoskeleton is further divided into core and translucent regions. Most studies have concentrated on the cytoskeleton, which is insoluble in the nonionic detergent Triton X-100 [39, 44]. Variations in the fine structure of the cytoskeleton within the M. pneumoniae cluster may indicate its importance in motility [44]. Research has unequivocally demonstrated the essentiality of the cytoskeleton for Mycoplasma motility [21, 22, 25]. While all members of the M. pneumoniae cluster possess an cytoskeletal architecture within the attachment organelle varies among different phylogenetic clusters. The cytoskeleton of the attachment organelle, comprising the terminal button, rod, and base, is highly conserved across different species. However, the base structure exhibits significant variation. While the core structure is essential for the formation of the attachment organelle, its specific ultrastructural features appear to have evolved independently of gliding motility mechanisms [18]. These findings suggest that while the core is essential for cellular functions like cytoadherence, further research is needed to elucidate its precise role in cellular activities.

Internal cytoskeletal components

M. pneumoniae

M. pneumoniae, a significant cause of community-acquired pneumonia in children and young adolescents [46–48], has been extensively studied with regard to its cytoskeleton, and its gliding model is well-established [22]. The M. pneumoniae cytoskeleton consists of 25 proteins, measures over 220 nanometers in length and 50 nanometers in thickness, and occupies the central region of the attachment organelle [25]. As shown in Fig. 3, this core structure consists of a 53 nm terminal button at the cell’s anterior end, 212 nm paired plates, and a 51 nm bowl-shaped complex. The terminal button, which interacts with the inner layer of the peripheral membrane protein, is primarily composed of P65 (MPN309) and HMW3 (MPN452) [25], with HMW3 localized at the interface with the paired plates [49, 50]. The paired plates consist of two striated plates separated by a 7 nm gap (Fig. 3A). The HMW2 protein, with its N-terminus localized at the terminal button and C-terminus at the bowl complex, forms a dimer and a parallel bundle [22]. Within the thicker plate, striations likely correspond to the eleven coiled-coil regions interspersed throughout the HMW2. The thinner plate, characterized by a stable hexagonal lattice, is likely composed of HMW1 (MPN447) and CpsG (MPN066) [21]. These paired plates likely serve as a scaffold for attachment organelle formation, as both HMW1 and HMW2 are essential for the organelle’s early development. They are also thought to play a critical part in gliding motility [21, 22]. The striated paired plate is connected at its proximal end to a “wheel” (or bowl) complex featuring fibrils that link the complex to the cell body [44, 51]. A similar structure, termed the “bowl” has been observed at the site of the wheel complex in M. pneumoniae [21]. MPN387, the C-terminus of HMW2 (MPN310), P41 (MPN311), P200 (MPN567), TopJ (MPN119), P24 (MPN312), and Lon (MPN332) appear to be localized on the bowl complex [25]. The bowl complex likely has a role in connecting the attachment organelle to other parts of the cell, because in mutant strains of M. pneumoniae without P41, the attachment organelle occasionally detaches from the cell body and glides independently [23]. The bowl complex may also be responsible for force generation or transmission [22]. Furthermore, MPN387-depleted mutants, while retaining cytoadherence, are incapable of gliding [52]. The possible position of the MPN387 protein suggested that it interfaces with the anterior side of the bowl complex [25], potentially bridging the complex to the posterior side of the paired plates for force transmission. Intriguingly, the gliding force itself may originate within the bowl complex (Fig. 3A) [22, 53]. While an inchworm-like model was initially proposed in 2006 to explain cytoskeletal conformational changes and the associated gliding mechanism, subsequent research has led to a revised model [44]. In this refined model, the force necessary for gliding is generated at the bowl complex and transmitted through the terminal button to both the paired plates and the adhesin complex. The adhesin complex, comprising proteins like Nap, undergoes repetitive catch-pull-release cycles with sialylated oligosaccharides on the host cell surface. Concurrently, the attachment organelle draws the remainder of the cell towards the posterior end of the bowl complex, thus effectuating gliding motility [22].

M. genitalium

M. genitalium, a close phylogenetic relative of M. pneumoniae, is recognized for its minimal genome and ability to thrive in axenic cultures [13]. This sexually transmitted pathogen is implicated in nongonococcal, non-chlamydial urethritis in men and various inflammatory reproductive tract syndromes in women [54, 55]. Its characteristic bottle-shaped morphology arises from the protrusion of the attachment organelle [56]. The structure and composition of its cytoskeletal core closely mirrors that of M. pneumoniae, featuring a terminal button, rod, and wheel complex. The terminal button contains proteins MG217 and MG317; the rod structure comprises MG312 and MG218; and MG491, MG386, MG269, and MG200 are situated within the wheel complex (Fig. 3B) [57]. Research into the gliding mechanism of M. genitalium has primarily focused on protein function and mutant analysis. Studies suggest that the adhesin complex plays a central role in gliding motility, while the rod-shaped element within the cytoskeletal core does not function as the primary molecular motor [58]. A double-spring ratchet model has been proposed, implicating the wheel complex of the electron-dense core and the terminal button in directional control. Mutations within proteins of the wheel complex can impact both the speed and direction of movement [56].

Despite their phylogenetic proximity and conserved cytoskeletal structure and composition, M. pneumoniae and M. genitalium exhibit some divergence in their attachment organelle components [59]. For instance, in mutant strains of M. pneumoniae lacking P41, a protein at the base of the attachment organelle, these attachment organelles are detached from the cell body and glid independently, implying that the organelles themselves act as the engines of cellular motility [23]. Conversely, M. genitalium cells with a point mutation in the MG491 gene, which encodes a protein analogous to P41, exhibit significantly reduced motility [60].

M. gallisepticum

M. gallisepticum, an avian pathogen, is distinguished by its membrane protrusion, composed of a bleb and infrableb, which enable gliding motility [61, 62]. Phylogenetically, M. gallisepticum is more distantly related to M. pneumoniae than M. genitalium. Interestingly, M. gallisepticum possesses a terminal bleb analogous to the attachment organelle of M. pneumoniae (Fig. 3C) [63]. Electron microscopy studies in 1964 revealed this membrane protrusion along with intracellular electron-dense structures (65). Treatment with 0.3% Triton X-100 and DNase exposes the asymmetric, dumbbell-shaped cytoskeletal structure of M. gallisepticum [59]. This structure consists of a cap (MGC2), a small oval (PlpA), a connecting rod (Hlp), a large oval (MGA_0306 and MGA_1203), and a bowl (MGA_0205). The large oval is connected to the bowl by a few filaments, whereas the small oval is linked to the cap by a more extensive network of filaments [61]. Small oval and cap together form a structure analogous to the terminal button of M. pneumoniae; rod and large oval are equivalent to the curved rods of the M. pneumoniae attachment organelle, with large oval representing a bulge at the rod’s terminus; and bowl resembles the bowl complex of M. pneumoniae (Fig. 3C).

M. amphoriforme

Identified in 1999, M. amphoriforme has been associated with congenital immune deficiency, and implicated as an underlying cause of bronchopneumonia [65]. M. amphoriforme is motile, but its gliding operates at a slower speed than that in M. pneumoniae and M. gallisepticum. These species share similar overall cell morphologies characterized by short and wide attachment organelles measuring approximately 120 to 150 nanometers in length and 130 to 150 nanometers in width [66]. However, they display significant differences in terminal button and rod structure. In contrast to other species, M. amphoriforme possesses a Triton X-100 insoluble discrete structure that resembles the electron-dense core found in M. pneumoniae, consisting of elements such as terminal buttons, rods, bases, and fibers, with each element larger than those present in other species. The rod-shaped portion of M. amphoriforme consists of a pair of parallel rod-shaped structures with an average width of approximately 82 ± 15 nanometers, which is almost twice as wide as that observed in M. pneumoniae. The total length of the terminal button measures 254 ± 20 nanometers, which is slightly longer than that in M. pneumoniae [66].

Overall, although most in-depth research has been conducted on the attachment organelle of M. pneumoniae, the attachment organelle of different species still needs to be further studies because there seem to be differences in their attachment organelles (Fig. 2). Among the Mycoplasma genus, M. pneumoniae exhibits the strongest pathogenicity [9]. Differences in the gliding ability of Mycoplasma may lead to different pathogenicity. Interestingly, M. insons, a bacterium found in the respiratory tract and blood of healthy green iguanas, possesses gliding motility but its unique cytoskeleton lacks a distinct attachment organelle [67]. The undifferentiated cytoskeleton of this bacterium extends throughout the entire cell, presenting a unique organization that differs significantly from that of any previously described Mycoplasma. This novel cytoskeletal arrangement represents a distinct paradigm for determining Mycoplasma cell polarity. Additionally, M. mobile has a ‘jellyfish’ cytoskeleton structure (69). These observations indicate that the evolution of polarity in Mycoplasma species occurred via multiple independent pathways [69, 70].

Adhesion mechanisms and gliding motility in the M. pneumoniae cluster

The gliding motility of Mycoplasma is closely related to its adhesion mechanism, as both processes have a crucial role in the pathogenicity and virulence of Mycoplasma [71]. Although the attachment organelle varies among M. pneumoniae relatives, its structure and function are essential for both cell adhesion and gliding [72]. Adhesion, the first stage of host invasion, is a prerequisite for infection. While adhesion mechanisms in the M. pneumoniae cluster are predominantly associated with the attachment organelle, some closely related species possess similar polar structures, the roles of which in adhesion remain to be elucidated [73].

The representative adhesin protein P1 is considered the ‘leg’ of the attachment organelle of M. pneumoniae [74]. Its C-terminus possesses a proline-rich repeating region implicated in adhesion. M. pneumoniae mutants deficient in P1 or displaying P1 mislocalization exhibit diminished adhesion, underscoring its importance in this process [75]. M. pneumoniae P1 adhesin bound to the Fab fragment of monoclonal antibody P1/MCA4, the epitope of P1/MCA4 involves residues only from the small C-domain of P1 and is accessible to antibodies only in the “closed conformation” of the adhesion complex, not in the “open” conformation. Polyclonal antibodies against the large N-domain of P1 or the whole ectodomain of P40/P90 have little or no effect on adhesion or motility [76]. The proteins HMW1, HMW3, P30, P90, P40, and P65 collectively constitute an interconnected adhesion network (Table 1) [22, 77]. During gliding, P1 adhesins capture, pull, and release sialylated oligosaccharides anchored to the substratum [78]. Sialic acid oligosaccharides, prevalent on host cell surfaces, act as ligands for binding in a “lock and key” fashion. The binding affinity is modulated by structural variations in the sialic acid compounds [43]. While both α-2,3 and α-2,6 lactosyl sialate support M. pneumoniae adhesion, gliding motility is observed only with α-2,3 lactose sialate. Gliding initiation requires a higher density of sialic acid receptors than adhesion alone [79]. During extrapulmonary infections, Mycoplasma adherence to erythrocytes can induce deformation and denting [80]. Furthermore, surface-displayed subunits of the M. pneumoniae pyruvate dehydrogenase complex can bind and activate human plasminogen, potentially contributing to pathogenesis [81].

The adhesion protein sequence in M. genitalium closely resembles that of M. pneumoniae P1 adhesin. The nap protein in M. genitalium is composed of two P140-P110 heterodimers with a loosely associated extracellular interface, suggesting that subunit function relies primarily on intracellular and transmembrane interactions. Each heterodimer appears to transition between “open” and “closed” conformations for receptor binding and release; the “open” conformation primes P110 for binding to sialylated cell receptors, whereas the “closed” conformation facilitates rapid dissociation [54]. The intracellular regions anchor the tetrameric complex to allow the transition between the ‘open’ and ‘closed’ states [82]. The highly conserved Engelman motifs in the transmembrane helix of M. genitalium P110 adhesin also alter adhesion and motility [76]. M. genitalium also binds to host cell-associated mucins via surface glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase and utilizes neuraminidase receptors on erythrocytes [83, 84]. The M. genitalium protein of adhesion (MgPa) plays a pivotal role in M. genitalium adhesion to host cells. Cyclophilin A was previously identified as a cellular receptor of MgPa using the virus overlay protein-binding assay. Additionally, histone H2B has been reported to act as a potential cellular receptor of MgPa, mediating M. genitalium adhesion to host cells [85]. The amino acid sequences of the adhesin complex of M. gallisepticum are similar to those of P110 and P140, suggesting that the adhesin complex has two sialylated oligosaccharide binding sites, namely, CrmA and GapA [61, 86]. However, the adhesion protein sequence in M. gallisepticum is more distantly related to that of M. pneumoniae. This relationship in the adhesion molecules aligns with the evolutionary distance observed in the 16S rRNA sequences of these bacteria.

Essential cytoskeletal proteins in the attachment organelle for gliding and adhesion

Cytoskeletal proteins in the attachment organelle

Cytoskeletal proteins of the attachment organelle are fundamental to cellular architecture and participate in diverse cellular processes, including adhesion and gliding [21, 44, 58, 97, 100]. In M. pneumoniae, several proteins, including the P1 adhesin, P90, P40, P65, P41, HMW1, HMW2, HMW3, and P200, have been identified as integral components of this organelle (Table 1) [25]. The nap adhesin complex of M. pneumoniae, comprising the P1 adhesin, P40, and P90 proteins, is essential for both cell adhesion and gliding [54, 87, 101]. P1 adhesin, a 170 kDa transmembrane protein, facilitates the repeated capture and release of host cell surface structures. Monoclonal antibodies targeting P1 demonstrably diminish both gliding speed and the capacity for surface binding [102]. Studies examining P1’s conformational changes and displacement have further illuminated its role in gliding [103]. Recent investigations have localized the sialic acid receptor binding site, crucial for Mycoplasma adhesion, to a loop within the P90 polypeptide of the P40/P90 heterodimer [87]. P41 and P24 are other key proteins implicated in gliding motility in M. pneumoniae. P24 is responsible for the formation of the attachment organelle and the regulation of gliding motility [94, 95]. P41 participates in P24 localization and may coordinate gliding initiation and termination [95, 96]. Notably, MG491, a protein in M. genitalium similar to P41, exhibits reduced gliding activity when mutated, despite maintaining normal levels of attachment organelle proteins [60, 98]. Intriguingly, P24 homologs are absent in M. genitalium, making P24 a protein unique to M. pneumoniae [104].

Many cytoskeletal proteins involved in attachment organelle formation are associated with both adhesion and movement of Mycoplasma. For example, P1 and P30 in M. pneumoniae have dual functionalities in both adhesion and gliding [52, 105–107]. Given the close spatial and functional relationship between P30 and P65, P65 likely interacts with the internal domain of P30 [22], the C-terminal region of P65 is responsible for multimerization, while the intrinsically disordered N-terminal region forms a filament [108]. This interaction may facilitate the tight binding of the terminal button to the front side of the membrane. P30 may anchor dozens of P1 adhesin complexes at the front end to form the initial core of the P1 adhesin complex array covering the organelle through lateral interactions among the complexes. In contrast, P65 may determine the gliding direction by modifying the angle of the organelle relative to the cell axis. This is supported by the observation that deletion of MG217 in M. genitalium, the ortholog of P65, changes the gliding direction [109]. The N- and C-termini of HMW2 (MPN310), a coiled-coil protein, are mapped to the terminal button and bowl complex, respectively [25]. Many bacterial scaffold proteins contain abundant coiled-coil domains, which are important for oligomerization and close protein‒protein interactions. These proteins are termed coiled-coil-rich proteins. For example, the α-helical coiled-coil-rich intermediate filament-like protein crescentin from Caulobacter crescentus is associated with the establishment of cell shape [110].

Functional roles of cytoskeletal proteins in gliding and adhesion

HMW1 and HMW2 participate in the early stages of attachment organelle formation and form or stabilize paired plates [77]. HMW2 is a specific component of thick rods [25, 96]. HMW3 is located at the terminal button of the attachment organelle, where P30 and P65 also reside [25]. P30 and P65 are predicted to have α-helical coiled-coil regions. Many microorganism-derived proteins, such as HMW1 (MPN447), HMW2 (MPN310), P24 (MPN312), and P41 (MPN311) in M. pneumoniae, and their homologs in M. genitalium (MG217, MG218, MG491, MG312, and MG200), share α-helical coiled-coil properties. This suggests that one solution to forming a polar cytoskeletal structure, independently developed in M. pneumoniae and M. genitalium, involves the assembly of large, highly charged proteins that interact with each other over an extensive surface area via α-helical coiled coils (Table 1). At least four components in M. pneumoniae form the wheel complex that connects the attachment organelle to the cell body and membrane: TopJ, P24, P200, and P41. The homologous genes of these proteins in M. genitalium (MG200, MG219, MG386, and MG491) also seem to constitute the wheel complex (Table 1).

The MG312 protein in M. genitalium is homologous to the HMW1 protein. MG312-deficient cells exhibit poor adhesion and limited movement on surfaces. This protein comprises an N-terminal domain, which is not directly involved in surface attachment or attachment organelle development but is clearly implicated in cell movement. It contains a conserved ATP binding site known as the P-loop or Walker A box, suggesting its active involvement in the gliding mechanism [111]. MG218 and MG317 in M. genitalium are homologous to the cytoskeletal proteins HMW2 and HMW3 in M. pneumoniae. Mutations in these proteins result in impaired gliding. The N-terminal region of these proteins is particularly crucial for maintaining the structure of the attachment organelle [112]. MG217 contributes to attachment organelle bending, acting as a motor and guiding the direction of gliding [91]. These roles highlight the complexity and diversity of proteins involved in Mycoplasma gliding motility.

Interestingly, two gliding-associated genes in M. genitalium, MG200 and MG386, do not directly contribute to cell adhesion. These genes encode proteins containing the conserved EAGR (enriched in aromatic and glycine residues) box motif, which has a specific function in Mycoplasma cell motility [93, 113]. Mutant M. pneumoniae without the P200 protein can still bind erythrocytes and the A549 human lung adenocarcinoma cell line, but their ability to glide and colonize differentiated bronchial epithelium is impaired [96]. This observation aligns with the finding that the homologous gene MG386 is involved in gliding rather than adhesion. TopJ in M. pneumoniae, localized at the base of the attachment organelle in the vicinity of the bowl complex, is a predicted J-domain cochaperone essential for attachment organelle function [97, 114]. It stabilizes the P24 protein, which is crucial for the initiation of attachment organelle formation and knob enrichment [97, 114]. MG200 and MG386 homologs have been found in M. pneumoniae (MPN119 and MPN567) and M. gallisepticum (MGA_1228 and MGA_0205), respectively.

The gliding machinery and mechanism of M. gallisepticum are similar to those of M. pneumoniae. The gliding machinery of M. pneumoniae and M. gallisepticum share 8 cytoskeletal protein homologues with amino acid similarities ranging from 20 to 45% [115]. These important components of the cytoskeleton coordinate the complex association between adhesion and sliding in Mycoplasma strains. Although many cytoskeletal proteins are strongly correlated with gliding in Mycoplasma species, direct evidence of their involvement in movement is still lacking. The generation and preliminary identification of gliding mutants have provided insights into the mechanism of Mycoplasma gliding. Thus, the foundation for future research on the engineering and regulation of the gliding machinery has been established.

Essential role of Motility-Related cytoskeletal proteins in Mycoplasma host immunity evasion

Immune evasion is paramount for Mycoplasma survival and persistence [116]. Motility is a crucial factor for nutrient acquisition, evasion of toxins and predators, and the exchange of genetic material through mating, and also plays a vital part in immune evasion [22]. The unique morphology of Mycoplasma cells enables them to act as rheological sensors, effectively recognizing the properties of host cell surfaces [28, 29]. This motility facilitates colonization in host and subsequent infection [30, 31]. Invasion by Mycoplasma spp. is mediated by a combination of factors, including surface adhesins and their accessory proteins, capsular polysaccharides, invasive enzymes, and biofilms [117–119]. For instance, M. pneumoniae infects respiratory epithelial cells using adhesin P1, with HMW1, HMW2, P30, and P65 jointly participating in this process [120, 121]. Meanwhile, M. genitalium usually infects urogenital epithelial cells [122–125]. Although these two bacteria have different tissue tropisms, they are evolutionarily related and display highly similar pathogenic mechanisms.

Some invasion-related enzymes, such as proteases, nucleases, and antioxidant enzymes, play key roles in Mycoplasma pathogenesis by degrading specific substrates [126–128]. For example, MPN491 of M. pneumoniae has nuclease activity that can degrade neutrophil extracellular traps, thereby evading the killing effect of neutrophils [129]. Mycoplasmas-derived proteases evade the host immune system by degrading immunoglobulins. Pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), such as lipoproteins, can be recognized by pattern recognition receptors on monocytes, macrophages, and natural killer cells [130, 131]. Through a series of related signaling pathways, these PAMPs upregulate the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, as well as cytokines like IFN-γ, causing tissue damage [131]. Some reports indicate that Mycoplasma can survive within cells [8, 132–134], and its toxin molecules and secondary metabolites, such as community-acquired respiratory distress syndrome toxins (CARDS), hydrogen peroxide, and superoxide ions, can cause pathological damage to host mucosal epithelial cells or red blood cells [135–137]. In addition to activating the innate immune response, Mycoplasma also triggers adaptive immune responses in the host. Antigens such as lipoproteins and membrane proteins induce the host organism to produce antibodies, particularly secretory IgA, to prevent infection of local mucosal surfaces [138, 139]. However, Mycoplasma can also activate CD4+ Th1 cells to secrete cytokines such as IL-2, TNF-α, and IFN-γ, further exacerbating tissue damage [140, 141].

Mycoplasma employs diverse strategies to evade immune damage, enabling the bacteria to proliferate and disseminate within the host. The primary tactics involve modulating the host immune responses and modifying the host, its own structures, or the location within the host [142]. Mycoplasma achieves immune escape through antigen mutation. First, Mycoplasma has multiple genomic loci encoding major lipoprotein variants and may selectively express different variants through genetic mechanisms such as gene conversion and recombination [143, 144]. Second, Mycoplasma is the only known bacterium carrying editing deficient aminoacyl tRNA synthetases (AARSs) [145]. AARSs acylate incorrect amino acids on tRNA, often resulting in antigen mutations. The M. pneumoniae genome harbors repetitive genetic elements designated RepMPs. Notably, 75% of these RepMPs exhibit homology with MPN141 (P1) and MPN142 (P40/P90). Homologous recombination between RepMPs and either MPN141 or MPN142 introduces variability within the antigenic regions of P1 and P40/P90, respectively. This variability provides an essential strategy for evading the host immune system [145–147]. Polymorphisms in M. pneumoniae adhesion antigens further diminish the effectiveness of specific antibodies [148]. Moreover, intracellular invasion represents an evasion mechanism employed by M. genitalium to circumvent attacks from cellular and humoral immunity, as well as those from antibiotics [149, 150].

In addition to variations in Mycoplasma motility and adhesion-related proteins involved in immune escape, specific cytoskeletal structures in M. pneumoniae can tightly adhere to the surface of host cells to avoid phagocytosis before accurate antibody regulation occurs [151]. The flexible gliding mechanism exhibited by Mycoplasma cells, which is closely associated with attachment organelles, may also contribute to host invasion and immune evasion. Although this aspect has received limited attention, it warrants further investigation.

Perospective

Mycoplasma species exhibit a broad host range, encompassing humans, animals, plants, and diverse other organisms [9, 153–155]. M. pneumoniae infections manifest in a spectrum of clinical presentations [156]. These presentations are contingent upon the specific species, host immunity, and the presence of co-infections [157]. As a ubiquitous pathogen in children globally, M. pneumoniae is a leading cause of childhood pneumonia [158, 159]. M. genitalium causes non-gonococcal urethritis in men and cervicitis, pelvic inflammatory disease, and infertility in women [160]. In avian species, such as chickens and turkeys, M. gallisepticum causes chronic respiratory disease and infectious sinusitis, impacting egg production, increasing mortality rates, and incurring significant economic losses [161].

Elucidating the pathogenic mechanisms of Mycoplasma will enhance the health of humans, animals and plants, and provide socio-economic benefits. Despite extensive research on the virulence factors of Mycoplasma, its unique gliding mechanism has been virtually neglected. The representative species M. pneumoniae can glide over solid and semisolid surfaces, primarily through the use of cytoskeleton and surface adhesion proteins, via the repetitive catch-pull-release cycle of adhesin with sialylated oligosaccharides on the host surface [162, 163].

Given the role of the attachment organelle in the gliding and adhesion of Mycoplasma, further investigation into Mycoplasma’s internal structures and surface proteins may unveil insights into its pathogenicity. Much about the pathogenesis of M. pneumoniae and its related diseases remains to be elucidated, including the factors that interact closely with respiratory or urogenital epithelial cells, evade host immune responses, and promote bacterial spreading. The structural information on Mycoplasma cytoskeletal proteins can help identify key functional areas, such as binding or catalytic sites, or structural domains involved in gliding. Knowledge of protein structures can guide the development of inhibitors or vaccines targeting these sites, providing new strategies for treating Mycoplasma infections. A combination of techniques can be applied to study protein structures to overcome the challenge of the lack of detailed structural information on the cytoskeletal components. Potential techniques include X-ray crystallography, cryo-electron microscopy, and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy [163, 164]. Detailed structural information on the cytoskeletal component proteins of Mycoplasma will be crucial for elucidating gliding mechanisms and their role in the pathogenesis of Mycoplasma species. Many of the proteins involved in gliding are specific to each bacterial species such as those in M. pneumoniae and M. genitalium. Therefore, caution is needed when extrapolating attachment organelle information from M. pneumoniae to other species.

Conclusions

Adhesion is an essential first step in pathogen infection. Gliding and adhesion are closely intertwined functions with a significant role in the pathogenicity of these Mycoplasma cluster strains [72]. On the one hand, Mycoplasma binds tightly to the host epithelium through adhesive structures, facilitating its colonization and subsequent proliferation. On the other hand, these structures also evade host immune responses through antigenic variation. Adhesive structures act as “legs” promoting the spread of Mycoplasma within the host. Moreover, they help these microorganisms move and escape the action of antibodies or complement molecules, thereby delaying pathogen clearance. These mechanisms, together with others such as the cleavage of antibodies and the inhibition of lymphocyte proliferation via virulence factors, promote Mycoplasma immune evasion.

Advances in genetics, genomics, biochemistry, and electron microscopy have provided valuable insights into the genes associated with Mycoplasma gliding. Key proteins, including P1, the P40/P90 complex, and HMW proteins (HMW1, HMW2, and HMW3), play crucial roles in these processes. Mutations in proteins such as MG217, MG218, and MG312 have effects on gliding [93, 112, 152]. Structural information on these proteins can help elucidate gliding mechanisms. By clarifying the three-dimensional structures of these proteins, researchers can better understand how they interact with each other and with other cellular components during gliding. This knowledge is essential for developing inhibitors or vaccines targeting these sites, providing new strategies for treating Mycoplasma infections.
