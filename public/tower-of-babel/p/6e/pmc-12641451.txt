FgNdk1 Promotes Effector Secretion to Scavenge ROS During Fusarium graminearum Infection
Authors: Manli Sun, Yiming Zhang, Yichen Yang, Xinyue Xie, Huawei Zheng, Pengbo Dai, Zhiyan Cao, Jingao Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPathogens secrete numerous effectors to overcome plant‐derived reactive oxygen species (ROS), but how pathogens modulate effector secretion during infection remains unclear. In this study, we showed that the nucleotide diphosphate kinase Ndk1 in Fusarium graminearum plays important roles in vegetative growth, conidiation, sexual reproduction and pathogenicity. The species‐specific N‐terminus and three active sites of FgNdk1 functioned in the development and virulence of F. graminearum and contributed to its enzymatic activity. Protein structure results showed that the N‐terminus is rich in proline, and subcellular localisation and enzymatic activity assays confirmed that it was responsible for endoplasmic reticulum (ER)‐anchoring; additionally, the proline‐rich feature contributed to the role of the N‐terminus in enzymatic activity. We further revealed that the N‐terminus of FgNdk1 exhibited a loosened structural conformation, likely facilitating the activity of FgNdk1 anchored in the ER. Moreover, FgNdk1 significantly suppressed ROS in planta. Comparative transcription analysis showed that 16 effector genes were differentially expressed in the Fgndk1 mutant, particularly during the infection stage. FgNdk1 greatly promoted the secretion of effectors FgSp10, FgSp16 and FgSp24 FgSP, which were important for the virulence of F. graminearum and ROS detoxification. Overall, FgNdk1 contributes to virulence by promoting effector secretion to scavenge ROS in planta, and its proline‐rich species‐specific N‐terminus enhances enzymatic activity, further influencing the development and virulence of F. graminearum. These findings elucidate the mechanism by which effector secretion is modulated by an ER‐anchored protein during plant fungal pathogen invasion.Keywords: effector secretion, Fusarium graminearum, nucleotide diphosphate kinase, ROS in planta, species‐specific N‐terminus, virulence

Introduction

Fusarium graminearum is a devastating soil‐borne fungal pathogen that threatens the production of wheat, barley and maize worldwide, causing Fusarium head blight in wheat and barley and Gibberella stalk rot in maize, which result in huge economic losses (Walter et al. 2010). Similar to other plant fungal pathogens, F. graminearum secretes effectors to facilitate successful invasion. For example, in the systematic characterisation of 50 orphan secreted protein (OSP) genes, Osp24 has been identified as a critical factor for infectious growth and plays a role in suppressing BAX‐ or INF1‐induced cell death (Jiang et al. 2020). Cysteine‐rich common in fungal extracellular membrane (CFEM) domain proteins are required for full virulence in maize stalk infection (Zuo et al. 2022). Similarly, the effector FgNIs1 promotes the virulence of F. graminearum and suppresses BAX‐induced cell death (Hao et al. 2023), and another effector, Fg03600, is translocated into plant cells and accumulates in chloroplasts as a virulence factor to promote fungal invasion (Jin et al. 2024).

To survive, plants have evolved a multilayer immune system to combat pathogens (Jones and Dangl 2006; Yuan, Bruno Pok Man, et al. 2021). Among the earliest plant responses to pathogen invasion, reactive oxygen species (ROS) are rapidly generated. The localised ROS burst induces cell death near infection sites by damaging DNA, proteins and cell membranes, restricting the nutrients available for pathogen proliferation. In addition, ROS function as signalling molecules that trigger the activation of defence‐related gene networks (Mittler et al. 2011; Camejo et al. 2016). Consequently, ROS are a pivotal element of plant immunity that pathogens must neutralise for infection. To overcome ROS toxicity, pathogens secrete effectors, which are highly expressed during the infection process but are present at low levels during other developmental stages (Yan et al. 2023; Gao et al. 2023). Many effectors target the host's ROS generation machinery to suppress the plant immune response (Gao et al. 2021; Qi et al. 2022; Shang et al. 2024). Additionally, some effectors interfere with the plant immune response through multiple strategies, including inhibiting endoplasmic reticulum (ER)‐mediated plant immunity and targeting plant susceptibil genes (Fan et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2023, 2022). Whether host‐derived ROS restrict F. graminearum invasion and how this pathogen orchestrates effector activities to suppress ROS are poorly understood. Nucleoside diphosphate kinases (Ndks) have NDP kinase activity, catalysing the interconversion from ATP/GTP to ADP/GDP in purine metabolic pathways (Sun et al. 2024). It is a highly conserved enzyme and primarily functions in the maintenance of intracellular NTPs. These essential building blocks are required for DNA and RNA synthesis and act as mediators of signal transduction pathways (Zrenner et al. 2006). In Saccharomyces cerevisiae , Ynk1 (an Ndk1 orthologue) is important for nucleotide pool homeostasis but is dispensable for vegetative growth, sporulation, mating efficiency and cellular morphology (Fukuchi et al. 1993). Neurospora crassa NcNdk1 is required for mycelial growth, conidiation and sexual reproduction and regulates catalase activity and perithecial polarity (Lee et al. 2006). MoNdk1 in the rice blast fungus Magnaporthe oryzae is not only required for metabolic homeostasis but is also essential for biotrophic growth, thereby promoting rice blast disease. Furthermore, the Mondk1 mutant elicits a strong ROS burst in host cells (Rocha and Wilson 2020), indicating that Ndk1 contributes to ROS detoxification during fungal infection. However, the mechanisms of Ndk1 contributing to virulence and ROS detoxification remain elusive.

In this study, we reveal that the deletion of FgNDK1 leads to defects in vegetative growth, conidiation, sexual reproduction and virulence. The proline‐rich species‐specific N‐terminus and active sites of Ndk1 are important for its enzymatic activity and associated phenotypes. Moreover, the Fgndk1 deletion mutant elicits a strong ROS burst during the infection stage. FgNdk1 promotes the secretion of effectors FgSp10, FgSp16 and FgSp24, which are critical for the virulence of F. graminearum and ROS scavenging in planta. These findings indicate that FgNdk1 with its species‐specific N‐terminus scavenges ROS in planta by promoting effector secretion during F. graminearum infection, thereby providing a potential fungicide target for the effective management of Fusarium head blight and maize stalk rot caused by F. graminearum.

Results

The Expression of FgNDK1 Is Significantly Upregulated During the Early Infection Stage

Upon examining the expression levels of genes involved in the purine metabolic pathway using RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) data of infection stage (Jiang et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2016), we found that the gene encoding nucleoside diphosphate kinase (Ndk1) was significantly upregulated (Figure S1a). Notably, the expression of FgNDK1 was significantly higher during the infection stage compared to the developmental stages (Figure S1b), indicating that FgNDK1 plays a crucial role in virulence. By assessing the FgNDK1 expression profile during the hyphal and infection stages, we found that the FgNDK1 transcript level significantly increased during the early infection stage at 1 and 2 days post‐inoculation (dpi) (Figure S1c), suggesting that FgNDK1 is important in the early infection stage of F. graminearum.

Sequence analysis revealed that FgNDK1 has an open reading frame of 717 bp, encoding a 238 amino acid (aa) protein with a molecular weight of 26.02 kDa and an isoelectric point of 9.10. Multiple sequence alignment analysis showed that Ndk1 orthologues contain a conserved nucleoside diphosphate kinase (NDPK) domain with three active sites (R189, H202 and D205). Among the 17 selected species including model fungi, biotrophs, hemibiotrophs and necrobiotrophs, only seven species of plant‐pathogenic fungi, namely F. graminearum, Fusarium verticillioides, Fusarium oxysporum, M. oryzae, Colletotrichum fructicola, Botrytis cinerea and Sclerotinia sclerotiorum exhibited a species‐specific N‐terminus. This characteristic distinguishes them from model organisms and biotrophic pathogens (Figure S2a,b). Phylogenetic analysis confirmed that Ndk1 proteins are highly conserved among filamentous fungi (Figure S2c). The structure predicted by AlphaFold showed that FgNdk1 contains a transmembrane helix (31–50 aa) and an NDPK domain, which constitutes the central region of the active enzyme with three active sites (Figure S2d).

FgNDK1 Plays Important Roles in Vegetative Growth, Conidiation and Sexual Reproduction

To determine the biological function of FgNDK1, we constructed knockout mutants using a split‐marker approach (Figure S3a). At least three deletion mutants with the same phenotypes were identified; however, only one mutant is described in detail (Figure S3b). When cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA), complete medium (CM) and minimal medium (MM) plates, the growth rate of the Fgndk1 mutant was significantly reduced by over 60% compared to the wild type (WT) (Figure 1a). In liquid carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) cultures, the conidiation of the Fgndk1 mutant was reduced by 70% compared to the WT, and the conidial morphology of the Fgndk1 mutant was abnormally varied, exhibiting 0–4 septa (Figure 1b). In addition, the Fgndk1 mutant rarely formed perithecia, whereas the WT strain produced abundant and normal‐sized perithecia with cirrhi (Figure 1c). The complemented strain Fgndk1/FgNDK1 was verified by PCR (Figure S3c) and fully rescued all defects observed in the Fgndk1 mutant, suggesting that FgNDK1 plays important roles in vegetative growth, conidiation and sexual reproduction.

FgNDK1 Is Critical for the Virulence of F. graminearum

To determine the role of FgNDK1 in virulence, conidial suspensions of the WT, the Fgndk1 mutant and complemented strains were used to inoculate flowering wheat heads and maize stalks. Culture blocks were used to inoculate maize silks to determine virulence. The Fgndk1 mutant caused symptoms only on inoculated spikelets, resulting in a 90% decrease in virulence on wheat heads compared to the WT (Figure 2a,b). Virulence assays on maize stalks and silks showed that the virulence of the Fgndk1 mutant was reduced by approximately 70% and 90%, respectively, compared to the WT (Figure 2c–f), thereby confirming the critical role of FgNDK1 in the virulence of F. graminearum. Similar results were observed for detached maize leaf sheath tissues. However, lesions produced by the Fgndk1 mutant showed over 60% type 1, 25% type 2% and 15% type 3, with type 4 infectious growth rarely observed. In contrast, the WT displayed 18% type 1, 28% type 2, 37% type 3% and 17% type 4 (Figure 2g,h). These findings are consistent with the high expression level of FgNDK1 observed during the infection stage (Figure S1b).

Exogenous ATP/GTP or ADP/GDP Does Not Rescue the Vegetative Growth Defect of the Fgndk1 Mutant

To determine whether ATP/GTP or ADP/GDP recovers the vegetative growth defect of Fgndk1, exogenous ATP, GTP, ADP and GDP at concentrations of 0.1, 1 and 5 mM were supplied to minimal medium (MM). The results showed that none of the exogenous supplements tested rescued the vegetative growth defect of Fgndk1 (Figure S4a,b). Based on these findings, we propose that purine transporters responsible for the absorption of exogenous purine may be non‐functional in the absence of FgNDK1. Reverse transcription‐quantitative real‐time PCR (RT‐qPCR) analysis revealed that the expression levels of five randomly selected purine transporter genes (FGSG_12024, FGSG_03523, FGSG_11439, FGSG_05112 and FGSG_12980) were significantly reduced in the Fgndk1 mutant compared to the WT (Figure S4c). Thus, the vegetative growth defect of the Fgndk1 mutant is not solely attributable to the lack of ATP/GTP and ADP/GDP, but is also relevant to the expression of purine transporter genes. These results suggest that FgNDK1 deletion impairs the expression of purine transporter genes, thereby further diminishing the absorption and utilisation of exogenous purine compounds.

The Species‐Specific N‐Terminal Region and Active Sites of FgNdk1 Are Crucial for Vegetative Growth, Conidiation, and Virulence in F. graminearum

The sequence alignment results indicated that seven plant‐pathogenic fungi among the selected organisms possess an N‐terminal region in common (Figure S2a,b). In addition to the NDPK domain with three active sites (R189, H202 and D205), FgNdk1 featured an 84‐aa N‐terminus that is also present in F. verticillioides, F. oxysporum, M. oryzae , C. fructicola , B. cinerea and S. sclerotiorum (Figure S5a).

To determine the contributions of the N‐terminal region and active sites of FgNdk1 during development and infection stages, we constructed full‐length FgNdk1 without the N‐terminal region, full‐length FgNdk1 with arginine 189 mutated to alanine and full‐length FgNdk1 with histidine 202 and aspartic acid 205 simultaneously mutated to alanine, into pFL2 under the native promoter control and transformed into the protoplasts of the Fgndk1 mutant to generate the transformants Fgndk1/FgNDK1 −N, Fgndk1/FgNDK1 R189A and Fgndk1/FgNDK1 H202AD205A (Figure S3d). The strains Fgndk1/FgNDK1 R189A and Fgndk1/FgNDK1 H202AD205A exhibited similar growth defects to the Fgndk1 mutant, whereas Fgndk1/FgNDK1 −N showed a higher growth rate than the Fgndk1 mutant (Figure 3a,b). Compared to the WT, conidiation of the Fgndk1/FgNDK1 R189A and the Fgndk1/FgNDK1 H202AD205A strains was similar to that of the Fgndk1 mutant, but Fgndk1/FgNDK1 −N had significantly higher conidiation than the Fgndk1 mutant (Figure 3c). After maize stalks were inoculated with conidia of the WT and the aforementioned strains, dark brown lesions were observed at 14 dpi. Similar to the Fgndk1 mutant, the virulence of Fgndk1/FgNDK1 R189A and Fgndk1/FgNDK1 H202AD205A was significantly reduced compared to the WT, but Fgndk1/FgNDK1 −N exhibited a higher virulence than the Fgndk1 mutant (Figure 3d,e). These results suggest that the active sites of FgNdk1 are crucial for vegetative growth, conidiation and virulence in F. graminearum and that the N‐terminal region, independent of the kinase domain, significantly contributes to the development and virulence of F. graminearum.

The Species‐Specific N‐Terminus and Active Sites of FgNdk1 Are Important for Enzymatic Activity

Based on the results of sequence alignment analysis and the phenotypic functions of the N‐terminal region, we hypothesised that this region may confer specific functions to FgNdk1. Examination of green fluorescence protein (GFP) signals by epifluorescence microscopy on N‐terminal GFP fused FgNdk1 and FgNdk1−N revealed that GFP‐FgNdk1 partially coincided with the ER marker FgKar2‐mCherry, while GFP‐FgNdk1−N was distributed throughout the entire cell, including the cytoplasm and nucleus (Figure S5b). This observation is consistent with the prediction of the transmembrane helix (a TM helix, 31–50 aa) (Figure S5c). These results indicate that FgNdk1 is partially functional in the ER and that its N‐terminus is responsible for ER anchoring.

To further elucidate the detailed function of the N‐terminus of FgNdk1, we analysed the FgNdk1 protein sequence and found that this region is rich in proline, an unusual amino acid that affects the protein structure and function (Figure 4a). Initially, we constructed glutathione S‐transferase (GST)‐tagged full‐length FgNdk1, both with and without the N‐terminal, as well as mutants with arginine 189, histidine 202 and aspartic acid 205 mutated to alanine, and a mutant with 10 prolines mutated to alanine. The GST‐tagged variants, namely FgNdk1, FgNdk1−N, FgNdk1H202AD205A, FgNdk1R189A and FgNdk110PA, were successfully purified from Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) cells, with FgNdk1−N exhibiting higher concentrations under the same conditions (Figure 4b,c). The luminescent kinase assay demonstrated that GST‐tagged FgNdk1 exhibited kinase activity, but both FgNdk1H202AD205A and FgNdk1R189A showed significantly reduced activity compared to FgNdk1 (Figure 4d). The activities of FgNdk1−N and FgNdk110PA were similarly lower than that of FgNdk1 but higher than that of the active site mutated proteins (Figure 4d). Additionally, we measured their enzymatic activity using a classically coupled pyruvate kinase‐lactate dehydrogenase method (Wang et al. 2019). Consistent with the findings from the kinase activity assay (Figure 4d), the results revealed that FgNdk1 exhibited enzymatic activity; however, both FgNdk1H202AD205A and FgNdk1R189A showed significantly reduced activity compared to FgNdk1. FgNdk1−N and FgNdk110PA exhibited lower activity than FgNdk1, yet higher than the active site mutated proteins (Figure 4e). Based on the protein structure of FgNdk1, we constructed FgNdk1‐N (1–84 aa) and FgNdk1‐C (85–238 aa) in both the yeast GAL4‐binding domain vector pGBKT7 and the GAL4‐activation domain vector pGADT7. The yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) assay revealed that the C‐terminus of FgNdk1 formed a homodimer, but it did not interact with the N‐terminus, indicating that the N‐terminus did not inhibit the enzymatic activity of its C‐terminal portion, which formed a homodimer to promote activity (Figure 4f). The structure predicted by AlphaFold for FgNdk1 showed that 10 prolines in the N‐terminal region maintained a stable structure at the amino acid sites, which likely exhibited a loosened N‐terminal conformation to promote the catalysis of FgNdk1 anchored in the ER (Figure 4g). However, more α‐helices present in the FgNdk110PA protein structure resulted in the attenuated flexibility of the N‐terminus, which was detrimental to the activity of FgNdk1 (Figure 4h). FgNdk1−N lacking the N‐terminal region had a completely exposed NDPK domain, but its ER localisation was abolished, which was detrimental to its enzymatic activity (Figure 4i and Figure S5b). These results indicate that the active sites of FgNdk1 are critical for its enzymatic activity, and the N‐terminal region positively contributes to this activity, likely through its proline‐rich feature and ER localisation.

FgNdk1 Is Important for ROS Scavenging In Planta

Due to the upregulation of FgNDK1 during the early stages of infection (Figure S1c), we hypothesised that FgNdk1 plays a role in mitigating early plant defence responses, including ROS scavenging. To determine its sensitivity to exogenous H2O2, the WT, the Fgndk1 mutant and the complemented strains were cultured on PDA plates containing 0.1, 1, 2, 5 and 10 mM H2O2. Compared to the WT and complemented strains, the Fgndk1 mutant exhibited a significantly lower growth inhibition rate under exogenous H2O2 treatment (Figure 5a,b), indicating that FgNdk1 is sensitive to exogenous ROS and may be crucial for ROS scavenging. To further validate this hypothesis, 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining and NADPH oxidase inhibitor diphenylene iodonium (DPI) treatment methods were applied to maize leaf sheath tissues inoculated with conidia of the WT, Fgndk1 and Fgndk1/FgNDK1 strains. ROS accumulated in maize leaf sheath cells infected with the Fgndk1 mutant; however, no ROS accumulation was observed after inoculation with the Fgndk1 mutant and treatment with DPI. Similar results of no ROS accumulation were found in leaf sheath cells infected by the WT and complemented strains (Figure 5c), indicating that FgNdk1 is functional during the early stages of infection and further inhibits ROS to facilitate fungal growth in planta.

FgNdk1 Affects the Expression of Effector Genes During the Infection Stage

To determine the function of FgNdk1 in the virulence of F. graminearum in more detail, RNA‐seq analysis was performed with mycelia of the WT and the Fgndk1 mutant collected at 12 h post‐inoculation (hpi) as well as inoculated maize stalk samples collected at 3 dpi. Compared to the WT, 2193 and 2097 genes were up‐ and downregulated, respectively, in the mycelia of the Fgndk1 mutant (Figure S6a), and 82 and 73 genes were up‐ and downregulated, respectively, in the Fgndk1 mutant during the infection stage (Figure S6b). Only 91 genes were specifically differentially expressed in the Fgndk1 mutant compared to the WT during the infection stage. Among the 91 genes, 33 were identified as secreted protein‐encoding genes by the SignalP v. 6.0 algorithm and subcellular localisation prediction (Figure 6a, Table S2). Of the 33 secreted proteins, 24 were upregulated in the Fgndk1 mutant compared to the WT during the infection stage (Figure S6c). Prediction with EffectorP v. 3.0 and the DeepRedEff algorithm showed that 16 and 7 of the 33 secreted protein genes encoded effectors, respectively, with 12 of the 16 effector genes (75%) being specifically upregulated in the Fgndk1 mutant during the infection stage (Table S2, Figure S6c). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that genes related to catalytic or binding activities were significantly enriched (Figure 6b). Conservation analysis demonstrated that most of these genes are conserved among different species especially in Fusarium (Table S3). Because the majority of effector genes were upregulated in the Fgndk1 mutant compared to the WT, we randomly selected seven effector genes (over 20%) regulated by FgNDK1 for verification and found that all were indeed upregulated in the Fgndk1 mutant compared to the WT during the infection stage (Figure 6c). RT‐qPCR confirmed that all effector genes were significantly upregulated in the Fgndk1 mutant compared to the WT strain during the infection period (Figure 6d). These results indicate that FgNdk1 affects the expression of effector genes during the infection stage, likely further contributing to the secretion of effectors.

FgNdk1 Enhances the Secretion of FgSp10, FgSp16 and FgSp24, Which Contribute to the Virulence and ROS Scavenging in F. graminearum

Among the effector genes affected by FgNdk1, FgSP10, FgSP16 and FgSP24 were selected for verification. We first examined their expression levels during conidiation, 24 h hyphae, sexual reproduction (8 days post‐fertilisation, dpf) and the infection period (3 dpi). Our analysis revealed that all three genes were specifically upregulated during the infection stage, in accordance with the expression profiles of the effector genes (Figure 7a). Subsequently, we assessed their secretory function, and the resulting yeast transformants grown on YPRAA exhibited secreted invertase activity (Figure 7b), indicating that the signal peptides (SPs) of FgSp10, FgSp16 and FgSp24 are functional in yeast.

Total proteins were isolated from the culture supernatant and detected with anti‐GFP antibody. Coomassie Brilliant Blue (CBB) staining indicates protein samples loaded. To determine whether FgNdk1 affects the secretion of FgSp10, FgSp16 and FgSp24, we fused them with a GFP tag and transformed them into the protoplasts of WT and Fgndk1 mutant. The total protein was extracted from the mycelia and subjected to immunoblot analysis with a GFP antibody. The GFP‐tagged proteins were detected in both the WT and the Fgndk1 mutant; however, significant accumulation was observed for FgSp10, FgSp16 and FgSp24 in the Fgndk1 mutant compared to the WT strain (Figure 7c,d). Notably, interactions between FgNdk1 and FgSp10, FgSp16 or FgSp24 were not detected by Y2H assays (Figure S7). These findings are consistent with the results of RNA‐seq and RT‐qPCR (Figure 6c,d). We further examined the GFP‐tagged proteins in the culture supernatant and found that the secretion of FgSp10, FgSp16 and FgSp24 from the Fgndk1 mutant was significantly reduced compared to the WT (Figure 7e,f). This result indicates that FgNdk1 is essential for the secretion of effectors. To further evaluate the contributions of FgSp10, FgSp16 and FgSp24 to virulence, we generated deletion mutants and complemented strains (Figure S8a). The deletion of FgSp10, FgSp16 and FgSp24 did not significantly affect vegetative growth or conidiation (Figure S8b–d); however, it resulted in attenuated virulence (Figure 7g,h). The complemented strains completely rescued the virulence defects, suggesting that FgSp10, FgSp16 and FgSp24 are important for the virulence of F. graminearum. To verify the role of FgSp10, FgSp16 and FgSp24 on ROS scavenging in planta, we measured the H2O2 content of maize leaf sheath tissues infected with F. graminearum strains. The results showed that leaf sheath tissues exhibited over 2.5‐fold higher accumulation of H2O2 after inoculation with the Fgsp10, Fgsp16 and Fgsp24 mutants compared to the WT and the complemented strains at 3 dpi. In contrast, the uninfected plants (CK) exhibited a lower H2O2 content (Figure 7i), suggesting that FgSp10, FgSp16 and FgSp24 play a crucial role in ROS scavenging in planta.

Discussion

In this study, we show that FgNDK1 is required for colony growth, conidiation, sexual reproduction and pathogenicity. Its proline‐rich species‐specific N‐terminus and active sites are important for these phenotypes and enzymatic activity. In addition, the Fgndk1 deletion mutant elicited a strong ROS burst during F. graminearum infection. Comparative transcriptomic analysis revealed that 16 effectors were specifically differentially expressed in the Fgndk1 mutant compared to the WT during the infection stage including FgSP10, FgSP16 and FgSP24, which are important for virulence and ROS scavenging in F. graminearum. In comparison with the WT, FgSp10, FgSp16 and FgSp24 significantly accumulated in the mycelia but reduced in the culture supernatant of the Fgndk1 mutant, resulting in secretion defects during F. graminearum infection These findings illustrate that FgNdk1 facilitates effector secretion to scavenge ROS in planta, likely by affecting effector regulatory genes, leading to the successful invasion of F. graminearum (Figure 8).

A working model depicting endoplasmic reticulum (ER)‐anchored FgNdk1 promotes effector secretion to overcome reactive oxygen species (ROS) in planta. During the early infection stage, Fusarium graminearum upregulates FgNDK1 expression to affect the effector regulatory gene for enhancement of effector secretion, including FgSp10 (endo‐1,4‐β‐xylanase 1 precursor), FgSp16 (glycoside hydrolase) and FgSp24 (hypothetical effector). Secretion of effectors contributes to scavenging plant‐derived ROS and facilitates F. graminearum invasion of host plants.

Ndk1 is evolutionarily conserved and widely present in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes, playing crucial roles in maintaining intracellular nucleotide homeostasis, regulating protein histidine phosphorylation, gene expression and responding to host ROS accumulation (Rocha and Wilson 2020; Yu et al. 2017). The ndk1 disruption mutant of Schizosaccharomyces pombe exhibits no detectable phenotypic differences but displays significantly reduced cellular NDK activity compared to the WT (Izumiya and Yamamoto 1995). In Escherichia coli , His117 of Ndk1 is essential for self‐phosphorylation, phosphotransferase, and histidine kinase activities (Yu et al. 2017). Conversely, in the fungus Aspergillus flavus, three key sites (Arg‐104, His‐117 and Asp‐120) of Ndk1 are crucial for spore and sclerotia development (Wang et al. 2019). Similar defects have been observed in M. oryzae and F. graminearum. MoNdk1 modulates intracellular nucleotide pools to maintain redox balance and virulence in M. oryzae (Rocha and Wilson 2020). In F. graminearum, FgNdpk has been reported to be required for vegetative growth, conidiation, sexual reproduction and pathogenicity, and it regulates toxisome formation and deoxynivalenol (DON) biosynthesis (Mao et al. 2024). This is consistent with our observations of the Fgndk1 mutant. We further confirmed the enzymatic activity of FgNdk1 and revealed that its N‐terminus and active sites are not only important for the development and pathogenicity of F. graminearum but also essential for its enzymatic activity. Therefore, the enzymatic activity of Ndk1 may be conserved across evolutionarily distant species, with its biological functions varying slightly concerning the virulence of plant‐pathogenic fungi.

The function of the species‐specific N‐terminl region of FgNdk1 prompted us to further investigate its molecular mechanism contributing to enzymatic activity and virulence. We found that this region contains 10 prolines, an unusual amino acid that significantly affects protein structure and function. Notably, virulence was completely abolished in the VdTRP variant of Verticillium dahliae, in which all proline residues in the repeat motif were mutated to alanine (Liu et al. 2024). Thus, we explored the protein structure of full‐length FgNdk1, in which all 10 prolines in the N‐terminus were simultaneously mutated to alanine. The structure of FgNdk110PA exhibited a greater number of α‐helices compared to that of FgNdk1, thereby restricting the flexibility of the N‐terminal region. The localisation of FgNdk1 and FgNdk1−N revealed that the N‐terminus constrained the functionality of FgNdk1 within the ER. The enzymatic activity assay indicated that the proline‐rich feature was responsible for the function of the N‐terminus and enhanced the enzymatic activity of FgNdk1 in the ER. This finding has not been reported in other fungi, and future studies are needed to confirm whether such functional restrictions are common among Ndk1 orthologues.

Fusarium graminearum is a hemibiotrophic fungal pathogen that initiates its infection cycle as a biotroph by penetrating plant tissues, subsequently transitioning to a necrotrophic phase (Kazan and Gardiner 2018). In response, plants have evolved a two‐layered innate immune system to defend against pathogenic attack (Yuan, Jiang, et al. 2021). One of the earliest manifestations of a plant immune response is the ROS burst, which is highly toxic to the infectious agents (Heller and Tudzynski 2011). Previous studies have demonstrated that ROS significantly accumulate in plant cells following infection by various biotrophs, hemibiotrophs and necrotrophs (Liu et al. 2020; Xu et al. 2019; Yang et al. 2022). Consequently, all plant pathogens strive to overcome the detrimental effects of ROS to establish successful infections. Although ROS production has been observed in Arabidopsis thaliana and wheat in response to the mycotoxin DON produced by F. graminearum, which exhibits elicitor‐like activity (Desmond et al. 2008; Wang et al. 2020), it is unclear whether ROS are generated in plant tissues to hinder infection by this pathogen. In this study, we observed that the Fgndk1 deletion mutant elicited a pronounced accumulation of ROS in the infected maize leaf sheath cells. Consistent with our results, MoNdk1 has been shown to suppress plant innate immunity in the biotrophic stage, facilitating successful infection of host cells, indicating that the interference of Ndk1 in ROS scavenging may be conserved among pathogenic fungi (Rocha and Wilson 2020). Considering the role of ROS in the activation of plant defence and/or the attenuation of F. graminearum virulence, we hypothesise that ER‐anchored FgNdk1 interferes with the functions of secreted proteins that suppress plant ROS burst.

In F. graminearum, secreted proteins have been reported to elicit and further suppress plant immune responses (Jiang et al. 2020). For example, five subtilisin‐like family proteases (FgSLPs) trigger cell death in Nicotiana benthamiana and significantly contribute to virulence (Xiong et al. 2024). The secreted ribonuclease Fg12 induces cell death and promotes the pathogenicity of F. graminearum (Yang et al. 2021). Small secreted protein Fg02685 contributes to the virulence of F. graminearum, and its transient expression leads to plant cell death. Furthermore, its N‐terminal 32‐amino‐acid peptide induces host resistance to Fusarium and Phytophthora pathogens (Xu et al. 2022). Among the secreted proteins inducing plant defensive responses, effectors are key virulence factors in almost all plant pathogens. In this study, three effector proteins, FgSp10, FgSp16 and FgSp24, contributed to virulence and ROS scavenging in F. graminearum. These results underscore the importance of small secreted proteins, including effectors, in promoting fungal virulence and inhibiting the ROS burst in planta. Thus, pathogens must orchestrate effector activities to suppress plant immune responses and further promote virulence during the infection stage.

A regulator Rgs1 in M. oryzae represses over 60 temporally coregulated effector genes to prevent their transcription during the pre‐penetration stage (Tang et al. 2023). The histone H4K8 acetyltransferase FolSas2 in F. oxysporum is a transcriptional regulator of early effector gene expression (Song et al. 2025). The COPII cargo receptor MoErv29 is required for the secretion of many proteins, including extracellular laccases and apoplastic effectors in M. oryzae (Qian et al. 2022). Deacetylation of B. cinerea BcTol1 enhances the secretion of the peroxidase BcCcp1 to scavenge plant‐derived ROS (Yang et al. 2022). These findings confirm that plant pathogens employ a variety of molecular mechanisms to intricately modulate the expression and secretion of effectors. Our results demonstrate that FgNdk1 plays important roles during the early infection stage and modulates the secretion of effectors FgSp10, FgSp16 and FgSp24. FgSp10 is annotated as endo‐1,4‐β‐xylanase 1 (XylA), which has been reported to be important for the virulence of F. graminearum. Deletion of XylA cannot be compensated for by other members of the xylanase family (Tini et al. 2020). FgSp16 is a member of the glycoside hydrolase family, among which PsGH12 has been previously reported to be a virulence factor and recognised as a pathogen‐associated molecular pattern (PAMP) in Phytophthora sojae (Ma et al. 2015). FgSp24 is a hypothetical secreted protein; however, its secretion is greatly affected by FgNdk1. Future studies should consider whether other FgSps exhibit similar expression patterns and whether their expression and secretion are modulated by FgNdk1.

This study identified that FgNdk1 promotes the expression and secretion of effectors, including endo‐1,4‐β‐xylanase and glycoside hydrolase, during the infection stage of F. graminearum. The results elucidate the mechanism by which FgNdk1 promotes ROS scavenging in planta, providing a novel target for designing small molecules against F. graminearum. As a multifunctional protein, the role of Ndk1 in phosphorylation has only been explored in M. oryzae . Ndk1 phosphorylates and interacts with the regulator of G‐protein signalling (RGS) protein Rgs3, which has emerged as a key regulator that senses intracellular oxidation during appressorium formation (Zhang et al. 2024). Because direct interactions between FgNdk1 and the effectors were not detected in this study, a possible mechanism by which FgNdk1 regulates the expression and secretion of effectors is through its interaction with or phosphorylation of a transcription factor, such as Rgs1, to promote the expression of effector genes and their subsequent secretion (Figure 8). Therefore, further evidence of molecular interactions and phosphorylation are needed to verify these hypotheses.

Experimental Procedures

Strains and Culture Conditions

The F. graminearum WT strain PH‐1 and its derived transformants were routinely cultured on PDA at 25°C. Colony morphology and growth rates were assessed on PDA, CM and MM plates incubated at 25°C, as described (Yang et al. 2015). Conidiation assays were performed as described (Wang et al. 2011). For sexual reproduction induction, aerial hyphae on 6‐day‐old carrot agar plates were gently pressed with sterile 0.1% Tween 20 and incubated under black light at 25°C (Wang et al. 2011).

Construction of the Fgndk1 Mutant, Fgndk1/ FgNDK1 , Fgndk1/ FgNDK1 −N, Fgndk1/ FgNDK1 R189A and Fgndk1/ FgNDK1 H202AD205A Strains

For transformation, protoplasts were prepared from 12 h germlings collected from yeast extract peptone dextrose (YEPD). Gene deletion constructs for Fgndk1 were generated using the split‐marker approach (Catlett et al. 2003) and transformed into PH‐1 protoplasts. For selection, hygromycin B (MDbio) and geneticin (Sigma‐Aldrich) were added at final concentrations of 300 and 400 μg/mL, respectively. At least three independent hygromycin‐resistant deletion mutants displaying consistent phenotypes were selected for further analysis. For complementation, the full‐length FgNDK1 gene driven by its native promoter was cloned into plasmid pFL2 by gap repair and transformed into the Fgndk1 mutant to generate the Fgndk1/FgNDK1 strain. Transformants resistant to both geneticin and hygromycin were verified by PCR and further assayed for phenotype complementation.

To generate truncated or mutated variants, three constructs were created: (1) Fgndk1/FgNDK1 −N: full‐length FgNDK1 lacking the N‐terminal 84 aa, (2) Fgndk1/FgNDK1 R189A: full‐length FgNDK1 with R189A substitution and (3) Fgndk1/FgNDK1 H202AD205A: full‐length FgNDK1 with H202A and D205A substitutions. All strains were confirmed by PCR and epifluorescence microscopic examination (Zeiss, LSM710), and the phenotypes were further characterised. The primers used are listed in Table S1.

Infection Assays for the WT, Deletion Mutants and Transformants

Flowering wheat heads of the cultivar XiaoYan22 (Kang et al. 2008) were inoculated with 10 μL of a conidial suspension (2 × 105 conidia/mL) at the fifth spikelet from the base and examined for disease symptoms and disease index, as previously described (Gale et al. 2007; Sun et al. 2021). Infection assay with maize stalk was performed as previously described (Zuo et al. 2022). At least nine maize stalks were used for each independent infection assay. The stalks were harvested at 14 dpi, and the inoculated internodes were split longitudinally to assess rot symptoms (Zuo et al. 2022). The lesion length was measured using ImageJ software. For maize leaf sheath tissue infection, 15 μL of a conidial suspension (106 spores/mL) in a 0.2% gelatin solution was used to inoculate the excised leaf sheath tissues. To maintain humidity, the excised maize leaf sheath tissues were wrapped with sterile cotton. Experiments in which maize leaf sheath tissues were treated with or without 0.5 mM DPI dissolved in dimethyl sulpoxide were conducted at least three times. Samples were harvested and observed at 36 hpi. The infection sites (n = 100) of F. graminearum were observed, and invasive hyphae (IH) were rated from type 1 to 4 (type 1, only one IH infected a cell; type 2, IH with 1 or 2 short branches; type 3, IH with at least three branches but short and extending within a plant cell; type 4, IH that has numerous branches and fully occupies the plant cell or even extends to an adjacent plant cell). Infection assays with maize silks and wheat coleoptiles were performed as previously described (Zhang et al. 2012; Liu et al. 2015). The inoculation experiments on wheat heads, maize stalks, maize silks, maize leaf sheath tissues and wheat coleoptiles were repeated at least three times.

Exogenous Supplementation With ATP/GTP or ADP/GDP in M Plates

Exogenous ATP (Sangon Biotech, A600020), GTP (Sangon Biotech, A620250), ADP (Sangon Biotech, A610016) and GDP (Sangon Biotech, A415892) at final concentrations of 0.1, 1 and 5 mM were added to MM plates, on which the WT and the Fgndk1 mutant were cultured for 6 days. For the control (CK), no exogenous substance was added to MM plates. This experiment was repeated at least three times.

Epifluorescence Microscopy

Fusarium graminearum cells (8 h germlings) expressing GFP‐tagged proteins were collected and visualised using epifluorescence microscopy (Zeiss, LSM710). Constructs GFP‐FgNdk1 and GFP‐FgNdk1−N were transformed into the Fgndk1 mutant for visualisation.

ROS Observation and Sensitivity Assay

Leaf sheath tissues were detached from 3‐week‐old maize plants, disinfected with 75% ethanol, and inoculated with 15 μL of a 2 × 106 spores/mL conidial suspension. The wounds were covered with wet cotton and cultured in the dark for 48 h in a glasshouse under 12‐h light/12‐h dark cycles at 25°C. For DAB staining, after the maize leaf sheaths were incubated with 15 μL of a conidia suspension for 48 h, the leaf sheath tissues were immersed in a 1 mg/mL DAB solution (pH = 3.8) and incubated for 8 h in the dark. The leaf sheath tissues were then bleached with 95% ethanol until the samples became colourless. To assess the ROS sensitivity of FgNDK1, the WT, Fgndk1 and Fgndk1/FgNDK1 strains were cultured on PDA plates for 3 days with varying concentrations of H2O2 (0.1, 1, 2, 5 and 10 mM). PDA plates without H2O2 served as negative controls (CK). The growth inhibition rates were calculated using the following formula: (growth diameter of the WT with 0.1 mM H2O2 treatment − growth diameter of the WT)/growth diameter of the WT. ROS observations were performed in at least 10 leaves, and the ROS sensitivity experiment was repeated at least three times.

RNA‐seq Analysis and RT‐qPCR Assays

The expression levels of genes involved in the purine metabolic pathway, including FgNDK1, were examined based on published RNA‐seq data (Liu et al. 2016; Jiang et al. 2019). For the RNA‐seq assay, vegetative hyphae samples of the WT and the Fgndk1 mutant strains were collected at 12 h and frozen in liquid nitrogen. At 3 dpi, maize stalks infected with the WT and the Fgndk1 mutant were collected and subsequently frozen in liquid nitrogen. Three biological replicates of 12 h vegetative hyphae and 3 days maize stalk samples were assessed. Total RNA was extracted using a spin column fungal total RNA purification kit (Sangon Biotech, B518659‐0100). High‐throughput sequencing was conducted using HISAT2, and the resulting clean RNA‐seq reads were aligned to the F. graminearum reference genome (King et al. 2015). The number of reads mapped to each gene was quantified using featureCounts (Liao et al. 2014). Gene expression counts were normalised using the transcripts per kilobase million (TPM) method (Wagner et al. 2012). Differentially expressed genes were identified using a fold‐change threshold of > 2 and p < 0.05. GO enrichment analysis was conducted to investigate molecular function classification. For RT‐qPCR, total RNA was isolated and purified using an RNA Extraction Kit (Sangon Biotech, B518659‐0100). All‐in‐One 59 RT Master Mix (Abm, G492) was used for cDNA synthesis, following the manufacturer's instructions. The relative expression levels of the target genes were determined using the SYBR qPCR Master Mix Kit (TaKaRa, G3325) on a Bio‐Rad Real‐Time machine. The GzUBH gene was used as the internal control. The transcript abundance of the candidate genes was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001) and repeated three times independently. The mean and standard deviation (SD) were calculated from three biological replicates. The primers used for RT‐qPCR are listed in Table S1.

Expression, Purification of Recombinant Proteins and Enzymatic Activity Measurement

The cDNA fragments of FgNdk1, FgNdk1−N, FgNdk1R189A and FgNdk1H202AD205A were amplified and cloned into the vector pGEX4T1. The GST‐fused FgNdk110PA vector was constructed by Genscript Biotechnology Company (Nanjing, China). The correctly sequenced GST‐fused vectors were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) cells (TransGen Biotech, CD601) for in vitro protein expression. When the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached 0.6, then 0.8 mM isopropyl β‐D‐1‐thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added to induce the expression of GST‐fused proteins except for FgNdk1−N. The cultures were incubated at 25°C with shaking for 6 h. The GST‐fused vector of FgNdk1−N was transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) cells, and 0.4 mM IPTG was added under the same OD600 condition. The cultures were incubated at 25°C with shaking for 8 h. The cells were harvested and lysed by sonication in lysis buffer (300 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 1 mM PMSF, and protease inhibitor cocktail). For protein purification, the clarified lysate was applied to a nickel column and GST‐tag resin (Beyotime, P2251) and incubated for 2 h at 4°C. The nickel column was washed with five column volumes of lysis buffer containing 20 mM imidazole, and the GST beads were washed with five column volumes of phosphate‐buffered saline. GST‐fused proteins were eluted gradually using an elution buffer supplemented with either 100 mM imidazole. To measure the kinase activities, the Kinase‐Glo luminescent kinase assay platform (Promega) was used to quantify the amount of ATP remaining in solution. The kinase activity assay was repeated at least three times. The enzymatic assays of recombinant proteins used a classically coupled pyruvate kinase‐lactate dehydrogenase method, as previously described (Wang et al. 2019).

Protein Secretion Assays

The predicted SPs of FgSp10, FgSp16 and FgSp24 were cloned into the pSUC2 vector. Constructs with the correct sequence were transformed into the yeast suc2 mutant YTK12, and growth was assessed on CMD−W and YPRAA plates. The invertase enzymatic activity was detected by the reduction of 2,3,5‐triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) to insoluble red‐coloured 1,3,5‐triphenylformazan (TPF). YTK12 transformants carrying pSUC2‐Avr1bSP were used as the positive controls, and YTK12 transformants containing the empty pSUC2 vector served as the negative controls. This experiment was repeated at least three times.

Western Blotting Analysis

The GFP fusion pFL2 constructs of FgSp10, FgSp16 and FgSp24 were transformed into WT F. graminearum (PH‐1) and the Fgndk1 mutant. Strains were cultured in liquid CMC medium for 3–5 days and then moved into YEPD for 12 h. The collected mycelia were ground into powder in liquid nitrogen and resuspended in 1 mL RIPA lysis buffer II (Sangon Biotech, C510006) with 2 mM PMSF (Beyotime, ST506‐2) for total protein extraction. The cell lysates were kept on ice for 30 min, shaken every 10 min to facilitate protein extraction, and centrifuged at 13,680 g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant lysates were collected as total proteins. Samples were analysed using 10% sodium dodecyl sulphate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE), followed by western blotting with either the GFP antibody (Abmart, 293967) or the actin antibody (TransGen, HC201) for protein analysis.

For secreted FgSp‐GFP detection, the conidia of WT/FgSp10, Fgndk1/FgSp10, WT/FgSp16, Fgndk1/FgSp16, WT/FgSp24 and Fgndk1/FgSp24 were incubated with 4‐week‐old maize seedlings in YEPD medium at 25°C for 36 h in a shaker. Then, the liquid cultures from equal weights of mycelia were harvested, and cold acetone was added to achieve a final concentration of 80%. The mixtures were centrifuged at 13,680 g for 20 min at 4°C after incubation at −20°C overnight to separate the secreted proteins. The FgSp‐GFP level was determined with an anti‐GFP antibody. Western blot assays of mycelia and culture supernatant were repeated at least three times, respectively. Coomassie Brilliant Blue (CBB) staining was used to verify equal loading of the protein samples. The protein concentration was estimated using a BCA protein assay kit (Solarbio, PC0020).

Y2H Assays

To construct plasmids for the Y2H assays, cDNA was used to clone the coding sequence of each tested gene with the primers listed in Table S1. The cDNA fragments of FgNdk1‐N (1–84 aa) and FgNdk1‐C (85–238 aa) were inserted into the yeast GAL4‐binding domain vector, pGBKT7, and the GAL4‐activation domain vector, pGADT7 (Clontech), respectively. The cDNA fragment of FgNDK1 was inserted into the yeast GAL4‐binding domain vector pGBKT7, while the genes encoding FgSp10, FgSp16 and FgSp24 were constructed into the GAL4‐activation domain vector pGADT7. The pairs of Y2H plasmids were transformed into S. cerevisiae AH109. The Leu+ and Trp+ transformants were isolated and assayed for growth on SD−Trp−Leu−His−Ade medium. Plasmids pGBKT7‐53 and pGADT7‐T, and plasmids pGBKT7‐Lam and pGADT7‐T were used as positive and negative controls, respectively. The Y2H assay was repeated at least three times.

Quantification of H2O2

Maize leaf sheath tissues were inoculated with 15 μL spore suspensions of different strains (106 spores/mL) and grown in a plant growth chamber at 25°C under 90% relative humidity (RH). The infected leaf sheath tissues were harvested at 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 dpi, and washed with deionised water. H2O2 content was determined using a Hydrogen Peroxide Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0038) according to the manufacturer's instructions (Yin et al. 2018). Briefly, test tubes containing 50 μL of supernatant and 100 μL of test solution were placed at 30°C for 30 min and measured immediately with a spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 560 nm. Absorbance values were calibrated against a standard curve generated with known concentrations of H2O2.

Bioinformatic Analyses

The molecular weight and isoelectric point were determined using the Expasy algorithm (https://www.expasy.org/). Ynk1 (YKL067W) from S. cerevisiae was used as a query to search for Ndk1 orthologues in the Ensembl database. Sequence alignment of Ndk1 orthologues from F. graminearum, F. verticillioides, F. oxysporum, M. oryzae, Colletotrichum fructicola, B. cinerea, S. sclerotiorum, V. dahliae, Ustilago maydis , Puccinia striiformis f.sp. tritici, Neurospora crassa, Aspergillus nidulans, Schizosaccharomyces pombe, Candida albicans , Arabidopsis thaliana and E. coli was constructed by Clustal Omega. IQ‐TREE was used to build maximum‐likelihood trees with 1000 bootstrap replicates. The protein structure of FgNdk1 was modelled using AlphaFold with default settings (Bryant et al. 2022). Secreted proteins were identified using SignalP (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/SignalP‐6.0/). Proteins containing transmembrane domains were screened with TMHMM‐2.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM‐2.0/) and Phobius (http://phobius.sbc.su.se/). The remaining proteins underwent analysis with WoLF PSORT II (https://www.genscript.com/tools/wolf‐psort) and big‐PI Fungal Predictor (https://mendel.imp.ac.at/gpi/fungi_server.html) to remove proteins localised within cells and those with GPI modification sites. Effector‐like protein genes were predicted using EffectorP and DeepRedEff algorithms, as previously described (Kristianingsih and MacLean 2021; Sperschneider and Dodds 2022). A heat map with coloured squares was used to describe the average TPM of each gene.
