Site-specific profiling of structure and function of Igµ B cell receptor glycans
Authors: M D Holborough-Kerkvliet, L Hafkenscheid, S Kroos, R Biersteker, R van de Wetering, O Singh, E Fadda, R T N Tjokrodirijo, P A van Veelen, M Wuhrer, D Falck, R E M Toes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough N-linked glycans play pivotal roles in the regulation of antibody effector functions, little is known about the composition and functional impact of glycans attached to human B cell receptors (BCR). Here, we describe the site-specific glycosylation profiles of all 4 N-linked glycosylation sites of human Igµ BCRs from primary naive and memory B cells. Our data indicate that the Igµ BCR glycans do not undergo structural changes during transition from naive to memory cells, as healthy donor-derived BCRs from both B cell subtypes carry similar glycans. Conversely, serum IgM antibodies display distinct glycosylation features to Igµ BCRs, which are in part explained by the different molecular form of Igµ BCRs and IgM antibodies. Moreover, using B cell lines expressing defined BCRs, we show that, although the absence of the Igµ BCR N209 glycans reduces antigen binding, the individual glycosylation sites are nonessential for cell surface expression and BCR internalization following BCR triggering. Collectively, we show high conservation of Igµ BCR glycosylation across B cell subsets and limited contribution to BCR expression and function, suggesting BCR glycans may have evolved to support IgM antibody functions.Subject terms: Glycobiology, Cell signalling, B-cell receptor, Antibodies

Introduction

B cells contribute to humoral immunity by secreting immunoglobulins (Ig) (i.e., antibodies) that can neutralize pathogens and engage effector functions, such as Fc receptor and complement activation. Diversity in Ig isotypes allows for differential activation of antibody (Ab) effector functions1. An additional layer of functional diversification of Ig that has garnered substantial interest in recent years are the conserved carbohydrates (i.e., glycans) that functionalize Igs2–4. Enzymatic or genetic removal of the conserved IgG Fc glycans at position N297 completely abrogates the ability of IgG to activate effector functions5,6. Furthermore, the importance of IgG Fc glycan composition is exemplified by findings that show that afucosylated IgG binds the FcγIII receptor with 20-40 times higher affinity than fucosylated IgG7,8.

Recently, more studies into the relationship between the glycosylation profiles and effector functions of other Ab isotypes have been reported2,3,9. In the case of IgA for example, data suggest that the more pro-inflammatory effects of IgA2 on neutrophils and macrophages, relative to IgA1, are dependent on its lower sialic acid content2. Furthermore, IgM glycosylation is suggested to be dynamically regulated in health and disease states, such as following infection with Covid-199,10 and IgM glycans have been implicated in modulating IgM effector functions in a glycosylation site-specific manner3.

Conversely, although it was shown that the IgG1 B cell receptor (BCR) glycans do not intrinsically affect B cell biology11, both glycosylation profiles of other B cell receptor classes and the role of BCR glycans in B cell biology remain largely unknown. The relative lack of insights into BCR glycosylation and its function likely originates from the technical challenges associated with the isolation of sufficient amounts of BCRs from selected B cell populations, and the methodologies to analyze N-linked BCR glycans site-specifically. BCRs are membrane-anchored Ig that play pivotal roles in initiating antigen-specific humoral immune responses via the activation and proliferation of B cells12. Due to the central role of the BCR in B cell biology, studying its glycosylation profiles and potential site-specific glycan functions may reveal essential insights on the regulation of BCR function. In contrast to IgG1 BCRs, which harbor only 1 conserved glycosylation site, Igµ BCRs have 4 N-linked glycosylation sites. The Igµ BCR carries 1 glycan in its CH1 and CH2 domains (N46 and N209) and 2 glycans in its CH3 domain (N272 and N279). Moreover, IgM Abs, which in circulation consist of five IgM monomers, a joining chain (J-chain) and CD5L13, harbor an additional glycosylation site that is not present in their Igµ BCR counterparts due to the alternative splicing events that µ heavy chain mRNA transcripts undergo10,14,15. This process results in the replacement of the Igµ BCR transmembrane domain with a glycosylated IgM Ab tailpiece.

Given the lack of insight into the structure and function of the N-linked glycans expressed by the 4 conserved Igµ BCR N-linked glycosylation sites of human Igµ BCRs, we here analyze their structural composition and impact on BCR-mediated B cell internalization and activation. Our data reveal that Igµ BCRs from healthy donor-derived naive and memory B cells express similar glycans, while serum IgM Abs differ in glycosylation. We find that Igµ BCR glycans are nonessential for surface expression and are not involved in the intrinsic regulation of BCR internalization and the early signaling cascade following BCR triggering. However, we observe that the removal of the N209 glycan reduces antigen binding to the Igµ BCR. We conclude that IgM glycans may have evolved predominately to support IgM Ab (effector) functions.

Results

Isolated naive Igµ BCRs show distinct site-specific glycosylation profiles

Igµ BCRs harbor glycosylation sites that are shared with IgM Abs at position N46 (CH1), N209 (CH2), N272 (CH3) and N279 (CH3) (Fig. 1C)10,16. We, aimed to characterize the N-glycosylation profiles of naive and memory B cell-derived Igµ BCRs (hereinafter referred to as naive and memory Igµ BCR). To this end, we isolated PBMCs from whole blood and sorted naive and memory IgM B cells (Supplementary Fig. 1A). B cells were gated based on CD19 and CD20 positivity, while naive and memory B cells were discriminated on CD27 expression. IgM B cells were collected by selecting IgG and IgA double-negative naive and memory B cells, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1A). To isolate Igµ BCRs, B cells were immediately lysed after sorting and incubated with anti-IgM affinity matrices. Samples were run by SDS-PAGE and silver stained followed by in-gel proteolytic cleavage (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Samples were subsequently analyzed by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), showing glycopeptide clusters corresponding to the 4 conserved N-glycopeptides and allowing the site-specific annotation of N-glycans (Fig. 1A, B). Glycopeptide tandem MS spectra of 1-2 glycopeptides per glycosylation site were analyzed to derive glycan structures and structural features such as the presence of bisecting GlcNAc (Supplementary Fig. 2a–g)17. To further confirm the specific capture of Igµ BCRs and not IgM Abs, prior to inclusion in our analysis, Igµ BCR samples were vetted for the presence of IgM Ab tailpiece peptides (Supplementary Fig. 1D). Relative abundances of all identified N-glycans per glycopeptide can be found in Supplementary Data 1 and glycopeptide retention times, isotope dot products (idotp) and mass errors can be found in Supplementary Data 2–4.

Igµ BCR glycosylation is highly conserved between naive and memory B cells

After establishing the LC-MS workflow to analyze conserved IgM glycans, we wished to define the glycan types carried by Igµ BCRs and assess whether Igµ BCR glycosylation changes upon the transition of naive B cells to the memory B cell compartment. To this end, we site-specifically quantified the relative abundance of complex, hybrid and oligomannose-type glycans of naive and memory B cell-derived Igµ BCRs (Fig. 2). Serum-derived IgM Abs (hereinafter referred to as serum IgM Abs) were isolated from the same donor’s buffy coat and included as a glycosylation comparator (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Naive Igµ BCR glycans at N46 carried slightly lower levels of complex-type glycans and higher levels of hybrid-type glycans compared to memory Igµ BCR and serum IgM Abs (82%, 88% and 91% complex-type glycans and 14%, 7% and 6% hybrid-type glycans, for naive and memory Igµ BCRs and IgM Abs, respectively). No significant differences were detected in oligomannose-type glycan levels (Fig. 2A). N209 was completely occupied by complex-type glycans for naive and memory Igµ BCR as well as serum IgM Abs (Fig. 2B). At N272, naive and memory Igµ BCRs carried slightly less complex-type glycans and more oligomannose-type glycans than IgM Abs (96%, 97% and 100% complex-type glycans and 3%, 3% and 0% oligomannose-type glycans for naive and memory Igµ BCRs and IgM Abs, respectively)(Fig. 2C). Lastly, at N279, naive and memory Igµ BCRs carried less complex-type glycans and more oligomannose-type glycans compared to IgM Abs (0%, 0% and 9% complex-type glycans and 84%, 83% and 75% oligomannose-type glycans for naive and memory Igµ BCRs and IgM Abs, respectively). No differences were found for hybrid-type glycans at this position (Fig. 2D). These data indicate that the glycan-types expressed on human naive Igµ BCRs are conserved in memory Igµ BCRs, and although not identical, largely resemble the glycan-types observed on IgM Abs.

Sialylation and GlcNAc bisection are differentially expressed on Igµ BCRs and serum-derived IgM Abs

After comparing the glycan types of naive and memory Igµ BCRs, we next wished to compare their respective derived glycosylation traits. Again, serum IgM Abs from the same donor were included as a comparator. Accordingly, for each glycan site we calculated the glycosylation traits for the predominant glycan-types as presented in Fig. 2. Namely, fucosylation, galactosylation, sialylation and bisection were determined for the complex-type glycans at positions N46, N209, N272 and the number of mannoses was determined for the oligomannose-type glycans at position N279 (Fig. 3). Interestingly, although Igµ BCRs and IgM Abs generally displayed comparable fucosylation and galactosylation levels, increased sialylation was observed at N46, of Igµ BCRs (1.15, 1.14 and 0.85 sialic acids per glycan for naive and memory Igµ BCRs and IgM Abs, respectively). In contrast, a decrease in bisection was noted compared to IgM Abs (0.09, 0.12 and 0.36 bisecting GlcNAcs per glycan for naive and memory Igµ BCRs and serum IgM Abs, respectively)(Fig. 3A). These differences in sialylation and bisection were recapitulated at N209 (1.96, 1.98 and 1.1 sialic acids, and 0.01, 0.06 and 0.66 bisecting GlcNAcs per glycan for naive and memory Igµ BCRs and serum IgM Abs, respectively)(Fig. 3B). Similar, but more profound differences between Igµ BCRs and serum IgM Abs were found at N272 (1.78, 1.82 and 0.81 sialic acids and 0.05, 0.075 and 0.75 bisecting GlcNAcs per glycan for naive and memory Igµ BCRs and serum IgM Abs, respectively)(Fig. 3C). The higher sialylation observed for Igµ BCRs was found to be explained by higher levels of disialylated (S2) glycans relative to monosialylated (S1) glycans as consistently observed on all three sites expressing complex-type glycans (Supplementary Fig. 3A–C). At N279, Igµ BCRs were found to carry more mannoses per oligomannose-type glycan (6.69, 6.68 and 5.88 mannoses per glycan for naive, memory and serum IgM Abs, respectively) (Fig. 3D). Thus, together, these data indicate that, compared to naive and memory Igµ BCR, serum IgM Abs harbor lower levels of sialic acids and increased levels of bisecting GlcNAcs at N46, 209 and 272, while at N279, lower levels of mannoses are observed.

Differential expression of sialylation and bisection of Igµ BCR and serum-derived IgM Ab glycans is recapitulated in IgM-expressing B cell lines

Intrigued by the differences in sialylation and bisection at positions N46, N209 and N272 between Igµ BCRs and serum IgM Abs, we aimed to elucidate to what extent these findings could be explained by properties intrinsic to the IgM protein. Therefore, we next addressed the question whether similar findings are obtained in case a monoclonal IgM is expressed as either a BCR or Ab. To this end, we transduced a Ramos Burkitt’s lymphoma B cell line (from here on referred to as Ramos) deficient in endogenous IgM and IgD heavy chains, lambda light chains and activation-induced cytidine deaminase (MDL-AID-KO) with plasmids encoding either an anti-myeloperoxidase (anti-MPO) IgM expressed as a membrane-anchored Igµ BCR or as a secreted IgM Ab. This resulted in the generation of 2 distinct IgM-expressing Ramos cells (i.e., Igµ BCR-expressing and secreted IgM Ab-expressing) with the same antigen specificity (Fig. 4A)18,19. We next affinity-captured cell line-derived Igµ BCRs or IgM Abs from cell lysates and cell supernatant (hereinafter referred to as Ramos Igµ BCR or Ramos IgM Abs)(Supplementary Fig. 4A–C), respectively, and analyzed the glycosylation profiles in a site-specific manner. Determination of glycosylation traits showed that at N46, while fucosylation, galactosylation and levels of bisection were similar, Ramos Igµ BCRs displayed increased sialylation levels compared to Ramos IgM Abs (1.16 versus 1.01 sialic acids per glycan for Ramos Igµ BCR and IgM Abs, respectively) (Fig. 4B). Likewise, also at N209, sialylation was increased on Ramos Igµ BCRs, compared to Ramos IgM Abs (1.87 versus 1.44 sialic acids per glycan at N209 for Ramos Igµ BCR and IgM Abs, respectively) (Fig. 4C, D). Interestingly, levels of bisection expressed by Ramos Igµ BCRs trended towards an increase at N209 (0.62 versus 0.82 bisection for Ramos Igµ BCRs and IgM Abs, respectively) and were significantly increased at N272 compared to Ramos Igµ BCRs (0.62 versus 0.87 bisection for Ramos Igµ BCRs and IgM Abs, respectively)(Fig. 4C, D). Although less striking than for primary Igµ BCRs and human serum IgM Abs, Ramos Igµ BCRs carried relatively more S2 glycans at N46, N209 and N272 than Ramos IgM Abs (Supplementary Fig. 4D–F). Furthermore, we calculated the differences in the mean number of sialic acids and bisecting GlcNAcs per N-linked glycan between primary Igµ BCRs and serum IgM Abs, and Ramos Igµ BCRs and Ramos IgM Abs, at N46, N209 and N272. These data indicate that although the magnitude of the difference in sialylation and GlcNAc bisection may differ between primary Igµ BCR and serum IgM Abs, and Ramos Igµ BCR and Ramos IgM Abs, the glycophenotype differences we observe in primary Igµ BCR and serum IgM Abs are largely recapitulated in Ramos Igµ BCR and Ramos IgM Abs (Supplementary Fig. 4G–I). Lastly, we found a small increase in the number of mannoses at N279 in Ramos Igµ BCRs (5.61 versus 5.03 mannoses per glycan for Igµ BCRs and IgM Abs, respectively) (Fig. 4E), which, again, mirrors our finding in primary Igµ BCRs and serum IgM Abs. Summarizing, these data indicate that Ramos Igµ BCRs carry more sialic acids and mannoses, and less bisection per glycan, findings that are similar to what we observed comparing peripheral blood-derived naive and memory B cell Igµ BCRs and serum IgM Abs. Taken together, these data suggest that differences in the number of sialic acids and level of bisection are intrinsic to the molecular scaffold of IgM.

Removal of the Igµ BCR N209 glycans reduces antigen binding but is nonessential for surface expression, BCR internalization and early signaling

Following our site-specific investigation of Igµ BCR glycosylation, we next aimed to investigate whether the 4 conserved Igµ BCR glycans were relevant for specific aspects of B cell biology, such as BCR expression, antigen binding, BCR internalization following BCR triggering and phospho-Syk signaling. To this end, we generated Ramos cell lines expressing a defined anti-MPO BCR harboring asparagine-to-glutamine (N-to-Q) mutations in the 4 conserved glycosylation sites, N46Q, N209Q, N272Q and N279Q. As N-linked glycosylation requires the presence of an asparagine in a glycosylation consensus sequence, these N-to-Q mutations render the site deficient in glycosylation11.

Successful introduction of the respective site-specific mutations in the plasmids was validated by PCR amplification and subsequent sequencing of Igµ BCR DNA, and LC-MS analysis of isolated WT and N-to-Q Igµ BCRs further confirmed the presence of the N-to-Q mutations on the level of the Igµ BCR protein (Supplementary Fig. 5A–C). All Igµ BCR N-to-Q mutants were expressed on the cell surface, indicating that none of the conserved Igµ BCR glycans was essential for cell surface expression (Fig. 5A). We assessed the association of the Igµ BCR heavy and light chains on nonreducing SDS-PAGE and found no evidence to suggest lack of the conserved glycans impacted heavy chain – light chain pairing (Fig. 5B).

Next, we assessed antigen binding of the Igµ BCRs to their cognate antigen, MPO. Therefore, we co-incubated the anti-MPO B cell lines with AF700-labeled MPO and IgM F(ab’)2-AF647 and calculated the ratio of the MPO signal to IgM signal. All Igµ BCRs retained their ability to bind MPO (Fig. 5C), although the absence of the N209 glycans resulted in a 20% reduction of MPO binding compared to the WT Igµ BCR (Fig. 5D, E). To validate this phenotype, we subsequently generated cell lines expressing WT and N209Q Igµ BCR sequences directed against tetanus toxoid (anti-TT) and citrullinated proteins (anti-citrullinated protein antibody (ACPA; ACPA are the hallmarking autoantibodies in rheumatoid arthritis) derived from rheumatoid arthritis patients (Supplementary Fig. 5E, F). Titration of labeled cognate antigen (tetanus toxoid and citrullinated antigens, respectively) on WT and N209Q Igµ BCR-expressing B cells again showed diminished antigen binding, suggesting a phenotype that is not dependent on MPO-reactivity (Fig. 5F). Taken together, our data indicate N209 is required for maximum antigen binding and show that Igµ BCR glycans are nonessential for Igµ BCR cell surface expression.

Igµ BCR glycans do not intrinsically affect BCR internalization

BCR internalization is an essential cellular response to (surrogate) antigen-mediated BCR engagement12. Therefore, we aimed to investigate if the 4 conserved Igµ BCR glycans affect BCR internalization. To this end, we stimulated WT and the N-to-Q mutant Igµ BCR-expressing B cell lines with kappa light chain-specific F(ab’)2 and assessed the subsequent BCR internalization by detecting the remaining (i.e., noninternalized) Igµ BCRs with anti-IgM F(ab)-AF647). Stimulation with lambda light chain-specific F(ab’)2 was used as a negative control (Supplementary Fig. 5H). No differential internalization of any of the N-to-Q mutant anti-MPO Igµ BCRs was observed in response to titration of kappa light chain-specific F(ab’)2 or trialing the stimulation time (Fig. 5G and Supplementary Fig. 5I). Moreover, these findings were recapitulated in WT and N209Q ACPA Igµ BCR (Fig. 5H). From these data, we conclude that Igµ BCR glycans are likely not involved in modulating internalization of the BCR following BCR triggering.

N209 Igµ BCR glycans do not intrinsically affect BCR phospho-Syk signaling

The data presented above indicate that the BCR glycans are not directly involved in regulating BCR internalization, suggesting they do not directly impact BCR activation. To follow-up this notion, we assessed the phosphorylation of Syk, a tyrosine kinase that is involved in the early B cell signaling cascade20. We focused on the WT and N209Q Igµ BCR, as we observed that the N209 glycans were instrumental in antigen binding (Fig. 5D–F). Accordingly, we stimulated Ramos B cell lines expressing either anti-MPO or ACPA (citrullinated antigen-directed) WT or N209Q Igµ BCRs with kappa light chain-specific F(ab’)2 (Fig. 5I). For both anti-MPO and ACPA B cells, concentrations of 1 µg/ml of kappa light chain-specific F(ab’)2 and higher lead to robust Syk phosphorylation, while 10 µg/ml a-lambda light chain-specific control F(ab’)2 did not. In agreement with the observations made on BCR internalization, no differences between WT and N209Q Igµ BCR-expressing B cells were observed. Together, these data indicate that the N209 glycans, similarly to the lack of N209-dependent BCR internalization phenotype, are not directly involved in the regulation of the early BCR signaling cascade.

The Igµ N209 glycans are essential for the structural integrity of the Igµ BCR

To gain a better understanding of how the N209 glycans affect the antigen binding of the Igµ BCR, we used GlycoShape ReGlyco-Ensemble (https://glycoshape.org), a tool designed to reconstruct glycoproteins with glycan 3D structures from molecular dynamics (MD) weighted conformational ensembles21. Recently, the cryo-EM structure of the Igµ BCR was solved22,23. We utilized the published structure (PDB entry no. 7XQ8), removed the C79α/β co-receptors, and linked the glycan ensemble corresponding to the major N209 glycoform determined experimentally, i.e., H5N4F1S2 (GlyTouCan ID G54258NG), to the structure. The results show that N209 glycans retain a high degree of flexibility, filling a relatively large exclusion volume at the intersection of the IgHµ v and C chains (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 6). These data suggest that the dynamics of the N209 glycans support the structural integrity of the Igµ BCR and the extension and orientation of the Fabs relative to the Fc around the hinge, preventing collapse of the Fab regions towards the Fc and cell membrane.

Discussion

Here, we elucidate the glycosylation profiles of the conserved glycosylation sites of Igµ BCRs on primary human B cells and the biological functions of Igµ BCR glycans in in vitro B cell lines. We quantified the glycosylation of Igµ BCR and IgM Ab by applying a label-free quantitative LC-MS approach. Due to its label-free nature, this approach cannot provide information on absolute abundances, which may vary due to differences in glycopeptide ionization efficiencies, but allows for the calculation of the within-glycopeptide relative abundances of glycans. Our findings show that both naive and memory B cell-derived Igµ BCRs carry mainly core-fucosylated, diantennary, galactosylated and sialylated glycans at N46, N209 and N272, and predominately oligomannose-type glycans at N279. Naive and memory Igµ BCRs are remarkably comparable in their glycosylation profiles, although naive Igµ BCRs were found to carry subtly more hybrid-type glycans and less complex-type glycans at N46. Comparing Igµ BCRs to serum IgM Abs, we found that Igµ BCRs display more sialylation but less bisection at N46, N209 and N272. At position N279, Igµ BCRs are increased in the number of mannoses per oligomannose-type glycan. As Igµ BCRs are membrane-anchored, monomeric Ig and IgM Abs are secreted, pentameric Ig, we wondered whether differences in the molecular “form” or protein scaffold could underlie the differences in glycosylation. To investigate to what extent these differences were protein scaffold-dependent, we assessed the site-specific glycosylation of Ramos B cell line-derived Igµ BCRs and IgM Abs. These data recapitulate our previous findings on primary Igµ BCRs and serum IgM Abs, and show that Igµ BCRs exhibit more sialylation, less bisection, and at N279, carry more untrimmed mannoses. Thus, differences observed in sialylation, bisection and mannosylation between Igµ BCRs and IgM Abs may in part be dependent on the respective protein scaffold properties (e.g., membrane-bound versus secreted and monomeric versus pentameric).

Comparison of cryo-EM structures of Igµ BCRs and IgM Abs reveals structural similarities in the shared Cµ3 and Cµ4 domains13,16. However, although Igµ BCRs and IgM Abs share similar tertiary structures, differences in quaternary structure, such as multimerization or membrane-anchoring, could modulate the accessibility for glycosylation enzymes such as sialyltransferases, N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase-III and mannosidases, to IgM glycans. Solvent accessibility has been suggested to correlate with the efficiency of N-glycan processing enzymes, but also, membrane-bound glycoproteins are typically more processed24. Increased glycan processing of membrane-bound BCR is line with higher sialylation. In contrast, lower bisection at N46, N209 and N272, and a higher number of mannoses per oligomannosidic glycan at N279 on Igµ BCRs compared to IgM Abs suggests more processing of the latter. Thus, the degree of processing seems to depend on the glycosylation feature. A confounding factor underlying the glycosylation differences observed between Igµ BCRs and serum IgM is that glycosylation may further be modified by circulating glycosidases, and half-life differences between Igµ BCR and serum IgM Abs may also contribute to the different glycophenotype differences observed25. Importantly, however, although clearly more subtle, similar differences were found between well-defined Igµ BCRs and IgM Abs produced by Ramos B cells, suggesting that the impact of effects such as glycan processing mediated by glycosidases in serum is limited. Similarly, it can also not be excluded that the glycosylation machinery of plasma cells differs from that of the studied B cell types, or that metabolic limitations result in different glycosylation due to differences in protein turnover26. Importantly, differences between Igµ BCRs and IgM Abs may be affected by pathophysiological states such as autoimmunity, which has been linked to altered B cell glycosylation27, and states which, for example, affect antibody oligomerization28. Nonetheless, although differences between Igµ BCRs and IgM Abs are present in healthy donors, these differences appear relatively small as similar glycan types were expressed by both IgM entities, with oligomannosidic glycans expressed at N279 and a predominance of complex-type glycans at all three other sites.

In addition to investigating the glycosylation profiles of Igµ BCRs, we also investigated the potential functional roles of Igµ BCR glycans on Igµ B cell biology. It has been reported that memory Igµ B cells show increased tyrosine-phosphorylation of molecules in the BCR signaling cascade and more rapid Ca2+ mobilization upon BCR triggering compared to naive Igµ B cells29. Hence, conceivably, differential naive and memory Igµ BCR glycosylation may explain the differences in Igµ B cell activation due to direct effects of the Igµ BCR glycans on BCR function. Our LC-MS data did not show differences in naive and memory Igµ BCR glycosylation profiles that could suggest a glycosylation phenotype-dependent differential regulation of BCR signaling in naive and memory Igµ BCRs. However, these data did not rule out a mechanism in which the presence of the conserved Igµ BCR glycans modulates B cell biology in a site-specific manner. To explore this possibility, we generated B cell lines expressing Igµ BCR N-to-Q mutants that were each devoid of one of the conserved glycosylation sites. Although the expression of BCRs was not dependent on the expression of N-linked glycans, we observed less maximal antigen binding in anti-MPO Igµ BCRs lacking the N209 glycosylation sites. This phenotype was recapitulated in B cell lines expressing anti-TT and ACPA Igµ BCRs. A limitation of these findings is that they were observed by flow cytometry and calculated using bulk median fluorescent intensity (MFI). Although this calculation corrects the antigen signal for the Igµ BCR expression, which may vary slightly per cell line and experiment, it does not provide information on antigen binding at the (molecular) level of a single antigen and Igµ BCR. Nevertheless, we do not consider it likely that the reduced maximal binding is explained by an intrinsic change in the affinity of the N209Q Igµ BCR for antigen (for example, via disruption of paratope-epitope interactions), as the N209 glycans are positioned distally from the antigen-binding domains. The N209 glycans are located in the Cµ2 domain, close to the Cµ3 border16. Moreover, they are located at the interface of the 2 Cµ2 chains. Based on the 3D reconstruction of the N209 glycan conformational ensemble of the Igµ BCR cryo-EM structure with GlycoShape ReGlyco-Ensemble, we suggest that the N209 glycans play an important structural role in the Igµ BCR, preventing the collapse of the Fabs over the Fc and stabilizing the functional, extended fold of the Igµ BCR. Interestingly, light chain-directed F(ab’)2 B cell stimulation does not result in differential internalization of the N209Q Igµ BCR compared to the other Igµ BCRs. This indicates that the structure of the Fab arms of N209Q Igµ BCRs are still intact. We propose that, consistent with our binding and functional data, the N209Q Igµ BCR resides in two structural states, of which one state allows for antigen binding, whereas the other state precludes antigen binding. Structurally, these states could be characterized by an Igµ BCR form that is extended, resembles a canonical Igµ BCR structure and is able to bind antigen, and a form that is collapsed relative to the Fc-domain, thus affecting the ability to bind antigen. Due to the orientation of the collapsed form of the N209Q Igµ BCR, the binding of cognate antigens may be disturbed, while the binding of light-chain directed F(ab’)2 may remain unaffected. Although our insights regarding our in vitro functional data and in silico structural data are highly suggestive of only the N209 N-glycans being relevant for Igµ BCR function, future experiments, for example, involving in vivo murine models, could help in gaining a more in-depth and comprehensive view of Igµ BCR glycans in B cell biology, such as understanding the role of the N209 glycans in B cell competitiveness.

Recently, it was reported that nonconserved, somatically introduced IgG Fab-glycans are enriched on the rheumatoid arthritis-specific ACPA B cells and have been shown to intrinsically affect B cell biology by affecting BCR internalization30. Both ACPA IgG Fab glycans and Igµ CH1 (N46) glycans are complex-type, biantennary sialoglycans. Furthermore, both are located in the Ig variable domains, as ACPA IgG Fab glycans are predominately found in the CDR1 and FR3, while IgM carries a conserved CH1 glycosylation site31. Considering these similarities, it is interesting that their effects on B cell biology appear to be different. These differences may be explained by the different isotypes (i.e., IgG versus IgM), or may be due to the different locations of these variable domain glycans.

Our investigation focused on elucidating Igµ BCR glycosylation and its intrinsic effect on B cell biology. In particular, we assessed the role of Igµ BCR glycans on BCR heavy chain–light chain pairing, BCR surface expression and antigen-mediated BCR internalization and early signaling, and thus, due to the scope of our work, we can only comment on these specific factors of B cell biology. Whether Igµ BCR glycans affect other factors such as spontaneous BCR internalization or B cell metabolism, for example, remains to be investigated. To this point, multiple accounts of intrinsic or extrinsic roles for IgM-(pre-)BCR glycans have been reported in the literature. For example, it has been reported that the pre-BCR N46 glycan is essential for the joining of the pre-BCR heavy chain and surrogate light chain32. Furthermore, Galectin-9 has been reported to bind and reorganize Igµ BCR cell surface nanoclusters in a glycan-dependent manner33. The putative glycan site(s) and glycoforms that mediated the reported interactions were not described. Although Galectin-9 has been shown to bind to terminally galactosylated biantennary N-glycans, it is unclear if Galectin-9 is able to bind to biantennary disialylated N-glycans, the predominant glycoform we observed on Igµ BCRs34,35.

Besides the reduction in antigen binding to N209Q Igµ BCRs, we do not find intrinsic biological roles for the other Igµ BCR glycans. These findings mirror the redundancy of the conserved IgG Fc N297 glycan on IgG BCR function11. However, recent studies investigating the role of IgM Ab glycans indicate that they may affect IgM Ab effector functions3,9. In complement-dependent cytotoxicity experiments, IgM N46Q mutations increased the complement activity 2-fold compared to WT IgM3. Conversely, the IgM N209Q, N272Q and N440Q (tailpiece glycan knockout) showed a 6-fold reduction in complement activity. In addition, binding to pIgR, FcαµR and FcµR were not affected by these IgM mutants. Another recent study reported high levels of sialylation and a low mannosylation of total IgM in Covid-19 patients that correlated with disease severity9. In in vitro assays, enzymatic removal of sialic acids from patient-derived IgM reduced deposition of complement component C3. Taken together, these data point towards a role for IgM glycosylation in the modulation of Ab effector functions, and not for intrinsically modulating B cell biology.

Concluding, we report the site-specific glycosylation profiles of Igµ BCRs derived from naive and memory IgM B cells. Our data indicate that the Igµ BCR N-linked glycans expressed by IgM B cells have not undergone significant structural changes during the transition from naive to memory cells, as the compositions of the Igµ BCR glycans in memory IgM B cells are similar to those observed in naive IgM B cells. Functionally, we demonstrate that individual Igµ BCR glycosylation sites are nonessential for Igµ BCR cell surface expression or for BCR internalization, and in the case of the N209 glycans, do not affect phospho-Syk signaling. Collectively, these findings indicate that, aside from the N209 glycan, which might be involved in allowing optimal antigen-binding by the BCR, Igµ BCR glycosylation does not serve BCR-intrinsic roles, implying that it may have evolved to support the functions of IgM antibodies, similarly to IgG Fc glycans11.

Methods

Healthy donor samples

Our research complies with all relevant ethical regulations, and all uses of human material have been approved. Buffy coats from healthy donors were obtained after written informed consent (Sanquin Blood Bank, Amsterdam, The Netherlands, protocol NVT0131.01). No sex and gender information of the healthy buffy coat donors was provided.

B cell enrichment, FACS, B cell sorting and Igµ BCR and IgM antibody isolation

Prior to B cell sorting, B cells were enriched from Buffy coats using magnetic assisted cell sorting B cell isolation kit II (Miltenyi Biotic, 130-091-151). Post enrichment, B cells were stained with Live/Dead Aqua (1:8000) (L34966, ThermoFisher) and CD3 (1:100) (Biolegend, #300488), CD14 (1:100) (Biolegend, #301841), CD19 (1:50) (#557791), CD20 (1:300) (Biolegend, #302332), CD27 (1:60) (Biolegend, #562513), IgD (1:20) (Biolegend, #348206), IgG (1:150) (Biolegend, #564230) and IgA (1:800) (Miltenyi Biotic, #130-113-477) antibodies for 30 min on ice in the dark. IgM B cells were sorted based on IgG and IgA double-negativity. After sorting naive and memory IgM B cells, cells were lysed in Triton-X (1%) at 37 ⁰C for 1 h. Cell lysates were incubated overnight at 4 ⁰C with 2 µl of POROS CaptureSelect IgM-XL Affinity Matrix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 2812892005). The next day, the beads were washed 3 times with PBS and 2 times with MilliQ prior to boiling in the presence of Laemmli buffer at 95 ⁰C. The boiled samples were run on SDS-Page and fixed overnight at 4 ⁰C in 40% EtOH, 10% acetic acid and 50% MilliQ. SilverQuest Silver Staining Kit (Thermo Fisher, LC6070) was used to stain the gels. Stained gels were further processed prior to LC-MS analysis. IgM Abs were isolated from the plasma fraction of Buffy coats by incubating 2 µl of POROS CaptureSelect IgM-XL Affinity Matrix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 2812892005) overnight at 4 ⁰C with 2 ml of plasma. Subsequently, IgM Ab samples were processed as mentioned for Igµ BCR samples.

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry

For MS analysis, gel bands were reduced with 10 mM dithiothreitol and alkylated with 50 mM iodoacetamide. In-gel trypsin digestion was performed using a Proteineer DP digestion robot (Bruker) in 25 mM sodium bicarbonate, pH 8.4. Trypsin (TRTPCK, CAS: 9002-07-7; Source: Bovine Pancreas) and Glu-C (non-sequencing grade, CAS: 66676-43-5, Source: Staphylococcus aureus V8) were purchased from Worthington Biochem. Per gel slice, 384 ng of trypsin and Glu-C were used. Tryptic peptides were extracted from the gel slices using 50/50/0.1 water/acetonitrile/formic acid (v/v/v). Samples were then lyophilized, and endo Glu-C was added for a two-step digestion in 25 mM sodium bicarbonate, pH 8.4. The samples were lyophilized again, dissolved in water/formic acid (100/0.1 v/v) and analyzed by online C18 nanoHPLC MS/MS with a system consisting of an Ultimate3000nano gradient HPLC system (Thermo Fisher, Bremen, Germany) and an Exploris480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher). Peptides were injected onto a cartridge precolumn (300 μm × 5 mm, C18 PepMap, 5 μm, 100 A, and eluted via a homemade analytical nano-HPLC column (50 cm × 75 μm; Reprosil-Pur C18-AQ 1.9 μm, 120 A (Dr. Maisch, Ammerbuch, Germany). The gradient was run from 2% to 40% solvent B (20/80/0.1 water/acetonitrile/formic acid (FA) v/v) in 30 min. The nano-HPLC column was drawn to a tip of ∼ 10 μm and acted as the electrospray needle of the MS source. The mass spectrometer was operated in data-dependent Top 20 MS/MS mode, with a HCD collision energy at 30% and recording of the MS2 spectrum in the Orbitrap, with a quadrupole isolation width of 1.2 Da. In the master scan (MS1) the resolution was 120,000, the scan range 400–3500, at standard AGC target @maximum fill time of 50 ms. A lock mass correction on the background ion m/z = 445.12003 was used. Precursors were dynamically excluded after n = 1 with an exclusion duration of 10 s, and with a precursor range of 20 ppm. Charge states 1–7 were included. For MS2, the first mass was set to 110 Da, and the MS2 scan resolution was 30,000 at an AGC target of 100% @maximum fill time of 60 ms. An additional MS2 scan was triggered if the HexNAc oxonium ion 204.087 was present in an MS2 spectrum. The same precursor was again selected, now with a stepped collision energy of 20, 35 and 50%, the maximum fill time was adjusted to 200 ms at a normalized AGC target of 500%, a mass resolution of 30,000 and a mass range of 110–3500. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE36 partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD062559.

Data analysis

In a post-analysis process, Byonic V5.7.11 (Protein Metrics) was used with 10 ppm and 20 ppm deviation for precursor and fragment mass, respectively, and no enzyme was specified37. Oxidation on methionine and acetylation on the N-terminus were set as a “common” modification; carbamidomethyl on cysteine was set as a “fixed” modification. N-glycan modification was set to “rare”. Two common and one rare modification were allowed, since all BCR glycopeptides contained a single glycosylation site. Settings were derived empirically and established previously17. LC-MS data was further quantified using Skyline (V22.2.0.527, ProteoWizard). The identified glycopeptides were combined with a comprehensive list of IgM Ab glycopeptides, extracted from literature10,17, and imported into Skyline. Extracted ion chromatograms were generated for the first three isotopologues of each glycan. Glycopeptides were quantified if they had a matching isotopologue pattern (idotp above 0.9), a mass accuracy between − 10 and 10 ppm, and retention times did not deviate more than 180 s from other glycopeptides with the same peptide backbone. Identifications were extended to other samples more leniently by manual inspection, if retention time differed by ≤ 30 s. Manual MS/MS analysis was used to validate at least one peptide-glycan combination for each of the 4 conserved glycosylation sites (See Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Data 2–4). Furthermore, MS/MS analyses allowed for the annotation of core fucosylation and bisection. Next, quantification of the analytes was done by integrating the MS1 isotopologue peaks and summing the charge states. Relative quantification of glycoforms per glycopeptide was done in Microsoft Excel by normalizing the intensity per glycoform to the total area intensity.

Annotation of MS2 spectra

MS2 spectra were annotated using Annotator (v1.2.1), a spectrum annotation framework for complex peptidoforms and glycopeptides, applied in accordance with the published workflow38. Raw data files were imported directly into Annotator, and MS2 scan numbers were selected based on Byonic identifications (Supplementary Data 2), supported by idotp scoring and precursor mass accuracy evaluation in Skyline. MS1 precursor signals were additionally manually inspected in Xcalibur (v2.2). Peptide assignments assumed a tryptic digest with no missed cleavages. Fragment ion assignment in Annotator was performed using a 10 ppm mass tolerance for MS2 spectra. Glycan-related fragment interpretation was supported by the GlyGen Glycan Identifier (https://glygen.org/), which was used to detect diagnostic glycan-specific marker ions indicative of glycan bisection, hybrid structures and high mannose glycans, where present.

Generation of B cell lines

Retroviral particles encoding single vector Igµ BCR or IgM Ab plasmids were produced as described in refs. 11,30,39. In short, retrovirus-producing cell lines (Phoenix-Eco) were transfected with single-vector plasmids encoding Igµ BCR or IgM Ab heavy and light chains, pKat vector and PolyJet In Vitro DNA Transfection Reagent (SignaGen, SL100688). To transduce Igµ BCR or IgM Ab DNA into B cell lines, viral particles were captured onto RetroNectin-coated Corning 24-well plates (3506, Polystyrene), overnight at 4 ⁰C. The next day, RetroNectin-coated plates were blocked with 2% BSA before incubation with 500 µl Phoenix-Eco supernatant for 20–30 min. After removing supernatant, 200.000 Ramos Burkitt’s Lymphoma B cell lines devoid of IgM and IgD heavy chains, lambda light chains and activation-induced cytidine deaminase (MDL-AID-KO) and incubated at 37 ⁰C overnight. Incorporation of single-vector plasmids was assessed by staining for IgM and GFP expression by flow cytometry. B cells positive for IgM and GFP-expression were bulk-sorted on a CytoFlex SRT (Beckman Colter). To verify the sequence of the transduced N-to-Q mutant Igµ BCRs, the genomic DNA of 5E6 Ramos B cells was isolated using the ISOLATE II Genomic DNA Kit (Bioline, BIO-52066). The Igµ BCR DNA was subsequently amplified by PCR amplification, resolved by gel electrophoresis, and Sanger sequenced. Primers used for Igµ BCR DNA amplification and Sanger sequencing were ordered from IDT (forward primer (TCCTCCCTTTATCCAGCCCTCAC) and reverse primer (TCACATTGCCAAAAGACGG)).

3D structural reconstruction analysis

ReGlyco-Ensemble

ReGlyco-Ensemble (https://glycoshape.org/ensemble) extends the Re-Glyco glycoprotein builder by allowing users to integrate structural information of proteins, from the RCSB PDB, or Alphafold Protein Structure Database, or own, with weighted conformational ensembles obtained from molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the free glycans in solution21,41,42. The GlycoShape database provides the major conformational clusters along with their corresponding population statistics (Supplementary Fig. 6A, B), and ReGlyco-Ensemble integrates this information within the protein 3D structures environment, maintaining distribution consistency obtained from the MD simulations. To ensure the correct geometry of the glycosidic linkages, torsion angles (Ψ [CB-CG-ND2-C1] and Φ [CG-ND2-C1-O5]) are sourced from Privateer43 and modeled as von Mises distributions for efficient sampling (Supplementary Fig. 6C, D).

The method rapidly generates glycoprotein ensembles that closely mirror distributions obtained from extensive MD simulations, significantly reducing computational time to a matter of seconds. For glycosylation sites with spatial constraints, if the targeted ensemble size is unattainable within the set computational budget, a difficulty score is computed. This score quantitatively reflects site accessibility and glycan flexibility, providing valuable insight into the structural constraints of glycosylation sites.

Igµ BCR modeling with GlycoShape ReGlyco-Ensemble

To characterize the glycan conformational ensemble at the N209 glycosylation site on IgM B-cell receptor (BCR), we used the 3D structure from cryo-EM22 deposited in the PDB (PDB ID: 7XQ8, 10.2210/pdb7XQ8/pdb). From this structure, chains C and v were used for the reconstruction. The type of N-glycan at N209 was established via mass spectrometry in this work, and the corresponding structure for reconstruction was selected from the GlycoShape database as GlyTouCan ID: G54258NG, for consistency. We used ReGlyco-Ensemble to generate the corresponding conformational ensemble with 200 frames from the MD simulation, reflecting the conformational distribution observed in free glycans, yet constrained by the structural environment of the Igµ BCR framework (Supplementary Fig. 6E). The calculated difficulty metric scored 0.01 for the N209 glycosylation site, suggesting a near-complete accessibility of the glycosylation site, with minimal steric hindrance.
