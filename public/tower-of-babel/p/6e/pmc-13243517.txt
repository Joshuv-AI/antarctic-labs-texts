Associations of higher protein intake with diet quality and diabetes-related metabolic health indicators in Korean adults without diabetes: analysis of Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) 2019–2021
Authors: Ye-Jin Kim, Soo-Kyung Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUND/OBJECTIVESRising pre-diabetes in Korean adults is an emerging concern, with notable increases observed even among younger adults. The interest in high-protein diets is also increasing in Korea, while concerns about excess intake remain. This study examined the associations of protein intake, diet quality, and diabetes-related metabolic indicators in Korean adults.SUBJECTS/METHODSThis study analyzed 8,254 adults aged 19–64 yrs without diabetes from the Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2019–2021, stratified by sex and pre-diabetic status. The protein intake was assessed using a 24-h recall, expressed as a percentage of the total energy intake, and divided into quintiles. The diet quality was examined using the Korean Healthy Eating Index. The diabetes-related metabolic indicators were waist circumference, triglyceride, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), blood pressure, homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance, and triglyceride-glucose index. The associations were analyzed using complex sample general linear models and logistic regression, adjusting for sociodemographic factors, lifestyle, and total energy intake.RESULTSThe protein intake was positively associated with the diet quality, peaking at Q4, then declining. Pre-diabetic men showed a decreasing trend in the elevated triglyceride risk with higher protein intake (Q4: odds ratio [OR], 0.697 [0.489–0.995]; P for trend = 0.041). In women, higher protein intake was significantly associated with an increased risk of low HDL-C in the non-diabetic group (Q5: OR, 1.544 [1.141–2.088]; P for trend = 0.020) and with the pre-diabetic group showing a lower risk of elevated blood pressure (Q5: OR, 0.657 [0.458–0.944]; P for trend = 0.012).CONCLUSIONThese findings suggest that in Korean adults without diabetes, maintaining an appropriate, balanced protein intake rather than simply increasing consumption may help manage the metabolic alterations associated with diabetes.Keywords: Dietary proteins, healthy eating, prediabetic state, metabolic syndrome, nutrition surveys

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes mellitus is one of the most prevalent and serious chronic diseases worldwide and represents a major global public health challenge [1]. In Korea, an estimated 15.5% of adults aged ≥ 30 yrs had diabetes, and 41.1% were in a pre-diabetic state in 2021–2022. Specifically, the prevalence of diabetes has been increasing among younger adults aged 19–39 yrs, with 21.8% reported to have pre-diabetes during 2019–2022, underscoring the growing importance of diabetes prevention as a national public health priority [2]. Pre-diabetes is characterized by elevated blood glucose levels that do not meet the diagnostic criteria for diabetes and is associated with a substantially increased risk of progression to diabetes, as well as a higher long-term burden of cardiovascular disease and other complications [3]. The expanding pre-diabetic population reflects a widespread distribution of metabolic abnormalities within the population and highlights the need for preventive strategies aimed at improving and maintaining diabetes-related metabolic health before disease onset.

Diet is a key modifiable factor in diabetes prevention [1]. In recent years, protein intake has attracted increasing interest, and high-protein diets have attracted attention as strategies for weight management and metabolic health. Protein is an essential macronutrient required for growth and tissue maintenance, playing a key role in muscle protein synthesis, immune cell function, and the provision of essential amino acids for hormones, enzymes, and neurotransmitters. Compared with carbohydrates or fat, dietary protein induces greater satiety and diet-induced thermogenesis and may improve insulin sensitivity and weight control through increases in fat-free mass [4,5,6,7,8,9,10].

Along with these physiological benefits and heightened public interest in immune health, dietary patterns emphasizing the protein intake and the market for protein-rich foods and amino acid supplements have expanded rapidly [11,12]. Correspondingly, protein-centered dietary patterns and the consumption of high-protein foods and supplements have increased globally and in Korea in the general population, as part of usual dietary practices [13,14].

Despite the growing interest, evidence regarding the association between high protein intake and diabetes risk remains inconsistent [15,16]. A clinical trial showed that a low-calorie diet combining high-protein intake with low-glycemic-load foods improved insulin sensitivity and reduced the inflammatory markers among overweight and obese women with polycystic ovary syndrome compared with a conventional hypocaloric diet [17]. Several studies based on the Almased Concept against Overweight and Obesity and Related Health Risk (ACOORH) trial reported that high-protein, low-glycemic index meal replacements had beneficial effects on the regression from pre-diabetes to normoglycemia and on the metabolic markers, including fasting insulin and inflammation [18,19,20]. Nevertheless, the findings were inconsistent even within the same protein sources. Large population-based studies have shown that higher total and animal protein intakes are associated with increased insulin resistance and a higher risk of type 2 diabetes, whereas plant protein intake was not associated with these risks or was inversely associated [21,22,23,24]. In contrast, a randomized controlled trial reported lower postprandial insulin and glucagon responses and improved insulin sensitivity with a high–animal protein diet than with a high–plant protein diet [25], and a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials found no significant effects of red meat intake on glycemic control or inflammatory markers [26]. Collectively, the relationship between high protein intake and type 2 diabetes risk is complex and may depend on the level and source of protein intake, the nutrients displaced by protein, and overall diet quality [16].

Importantly, diets high in protein or heavily focused on specific protein sources may compromise the balance of other nutrients and potentially compromise the overall diet quality. Therefore, understanding the role of higher protein intake in diabetes prevention requires a consideration of the protein intake levels and overall diet quality. On the other hand, studies addressing these factors simultaneously remain limited, and most existing evidence has been derived from Western populations. In Korea, where the prevalence of pre-diabetes has been increasing across all age groups, including younger adults, and the interest in high-protein foods and supplements continues to grow, data representing the associations of higher protein intake with diabetes-related metabolic indicators in conjunction with diet quality are scarce. The diabetes-related metabolic health indicators examined in this study, such as fasting glucose, waist circumference, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and blood pressure, are well-established components of metabolic syndrome and recognized as the early markers of insulin resistance and predictors of future type 2 diabetes risk [27]. Therefore, this study examined the associations between the protein intake levels, overall diet quality, and diabetes-related metabolic health indicators in Korean adults without diabetes in their daily diets, stratified according to sex and pre-diabetic status.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Study design and data source

This cross-sectional study analyzed the data from the eighth Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) 2019–2021 to examine the associations between protein intake, diet quality, and metabolic health indicators at the population level. KNHANES is a nationally representative survey designed to assess the health and nutritional status of the Korean population. The survey uses a complex, multistage, stratified cluster sampling design based on the most recent Population and Housing Census to ensure national representativeness. The survey consists of a health interview, a health examination, and a nutrition survey. The health interview, examination components, and the nutrition survey were conducted in mobile examination centers. Detailed information on collected data and collection methods can be found elsewhere [28,29].

KNHANES is conducted and managed by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. The data are publicly released as de-identified secondary data in accordance with the Personal Information Protection Act and the Statistics Act. The eighth KNHANES was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (approval numbers: 2018-01-03-C-A, 2018-01-03-2C-A, and 2018-01-03-5C-A). Based on the use of publicly available, de-identified data, this study was also granted an exemption from the IRB review on April 29, 2025.

Study population

Among the 22,559 participants in the eighth KNHANES (2019–2021), adults aged 19–64 yrs were considered eligible for this study (n = 10,288). The participants with diabetes were excluded. Diabetes was defined among nonpregnant individuals who had fasted for at least 8 h as meeting one or more of the following criteria: fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%, a self-reported physician diagnosis of diabetes, current use of glucose-lowering medications, or insulin therapy. This study further excluded pregnant or lactating women and participants with missing data on dietary intake (total energy intake and diet quality), diabetes-related metabolic health indicators (waist circumference, triglycerides, HDL-C, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, and insulin), anthropometric measures (body weight and body mass index [BMI]), or covariates (household income, education level, alcohol consumption, smoking status, and physical activity). Participants with an implausible total energy intake (< 500 or > 5,000 kcal/day) were also excluded. After these exclusions, 8,254 adults without diabetes were included in the final analytic sample.

The participants were stratified according to sex (men and women), glycemic status (non-diabetic and pre-diabetic), yielding 4 subgroups: non-diabetic men, pre-diabetic men, non-diabetic women, and pre-diabetic women. Within each subgroup, the participants were further categorized into quintiles of protein intake (% of total energy). Pre-diabetes was defined as the absence of diabetes with fasting plasma glucose levels of 100–125 mg/dL or HbA1c levels of 5.7–6.4%. The non-diabetic group was defined as individuals without diabetes or pre-diabetes, with fasting plasma glucose < 100 mg/dL or HbA1c < 5.7% [30,31]. This stratification was conducted to understand the associations between protein intake, diet quality, and metabolic indicators within each group so that implications could be drawn directly for the people in each group. Dietary intake and assessment of diet quality

The dietary intake data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained interviewers using a single 24-h dietary recall, representing dietary intake in a general setting, not a clinical or research setting. The food and nutrient intakes were calculated based on the reported data using the Korean Food Composition Table (10th revision; Rural Development Administration, 2022).

The protein intake was assessed as the proportion of total energy intake from protein. The participants were categorized into quintiles (Q1–Q5) based on the distribution of protein intake as a percentage of the total energy. The highest quintile (Q5) reflects the relatively higher protein intake within the study population. All analyzable nutrient intakes were standardized and expressed per 1,000 kcal of total energy intake to minimize the influence of total energy intake and facilitate comparisons across groups.

The diet quality was evaluated using the Korean Healthy Eating Index (KHEI), a composite measure designed to assess the overall dietary patterns and diet quality. The KHEI consists of 14 components, including 8 adequacy components (have breakfast; mixed grains intake; total fruits intake; fresh fruit intake; total vegetables intake; vegetable intake, excluding kimchi and pickled vegetables intake; meat, fish, eggs, and beans intake; and milk and milk products intake), 3 moderation components (percentage of energy from saturated fatty acid; sodium intake; and percentage of energy from sweets and beverages), and 3 balance of energy intake components (percentage of energy from carbohydrate; percentage of energy from fat; and energy intake). Component scores were assigned based on the Korean dietary guidelines, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 100 [32].

In addition, a modified KHEI was calculated by excluding the protein-source food component (meat, fish, eggs, and beans; 10 points) to minimize conceptual overlap with the primary exposure variable (protein energy intake). The total score of the modified KHEI ranged from 0 to 90.

Diabetes-related metabolic health indicators

Diabetes-related metabolic health indicators were selected based on the established associations with insulin resistance and future diabetes risk. The 6 indicators were as follows: waist circumference, triglycerides, HDL-C, blood pressure, homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), and triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index. Each indicator was dichotomized according to predefined cutoff values and included as a binary outcome in logistic regression analyses. The fasting plasma glucose and HbA1c levels were used to classify the glycemic status (non-diabetic and pre-diabetic groups) rather than as outcome variables.

The 4 components of metabolic syndrome were defined according to the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III criteria [27], with Korean-specific waist circumference cutoffs [33]. An elevated waist circumference was defined as ≥ 90 cm in men and ≥ 85 cm in women. Elevated triglycerides were defined as triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL or current use of lipid-lowering medication. Reduced HDL-C was defined as HDL-C < 40 mg/dL for men and < 50 mg/dL for women, or the current use of lipid-lowering medication. An elevated blood pressure was defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg, a diastolic blood pressure ≥ 85 mmHg, or the current use of antihypertensive medication.

The indices of insulin resistance included the HOMA-IR and the TyG index. The HOMA-IR was calculated as [Fasting Insulin (µU/mL) × Fasting Plasma Glucose (mg/dL)]/405, and a cutoff value of 2.34 was applied based on previous studies in Korean adults [34]. The TyG index was calculated as ln [Triglycerides (mg/dL) × Fasting Plasma Glucose (mg/dL)/2], and a cutoff value of 8.81 was applied [35,36,37].

General characteristics

Potential confounding was controlled by including age and total energy intake as continuous variables and education level, household income, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity as categorical covariates in the analyses.

The education level was categorized as elementary school or less, middle school, high school, and college or higher. The household income was classified into 4 categories (lowest, lower-middle, upper-middle, and highest). The smoking status was categorized as current smoker or former/non-smoker. Alcohol consumption was defined as drinking alcohol at least once per month during the past year or less than once per month (including non-drinkers). The physical activity was assessed based on the adherence to aerobic physical activity guidelines, incorporating moderate- and vigorous-intensity activities. The participants were classified as meeting the aerobic physical activity recommendations if they engaged in ≥ 150 min per week of moderate-intensity activity, ≥ 75 min per week of vigorous-intensity activity, or an equivalent combination of moderate- and vigorous-intensity activities (with 1 min of vigorous activity equivalent to 2 min of moderate activity); all others were classified as not meeting the recommendation. These variables were considered potential confounders of the associations between dietary intake and diabetes-related metabolic health indicators.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were carried out accounting for the complex sampling design of the KNHANES, including sampling weights, stratification, and clustering. The data from the 2019–2021 survey cycles were pooled for analysis. The participant characteristics are presented as the weighted percentages with SEs [% (SE)], and were compared using Rao-Scott χ2 tests for the categorical variables. For continuous variables, the group means were compared using complex survey-weighted general linear models, and statistical significance was assessed using Wald F tests. When the overall differences were significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons between the protein intake quintiles (Q1–Q5) were conducted, with P-values adjusted for multiple comparisons using the sequential Bonferroni (Holm) method. The results are presented as the weighted means ± SE. Comparisons of the diet quality (KHEI scores) and nutrient intakes across protein intake quintiles were conducted using the same analytic approach. Tests for the linear trend were conducted using the weighted median value for each protein intake group, modeled as a continuous variable.

The associations between the protein intake and diabetes-related metabolic health indicators, including waist circumference, triglycerides, HDL-C, blood pressure, HOMA-IR, and TyG index, were evaluated using complex survey-weighted logistic regression analyses, and the results are presented as the odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). The protein intake and diet quality (modified KHEI score) were first modeled as continuous variables along with their interaction term to determine whether these associations varied by diet quality. The interaction terms were not significant; therefore, further analyses were conducted without interaction terms. The protein intake was then categorized into quintiles to estimate the associations across intake levels, and 3 sequentially adjusted models were specified. Model 1 (basic model) was adjusted for age, education level, household income, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and total energy intake. Model 2 was further adjusted for the total KHEI score. Model 3 (final model) was further adjusted for the modified KHEI score in place of the total KHEI score. Additional comparisons across quintiles were adjusted for multiple testing using the sequential Bonferroni (Holm) method where applicable.

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and a 2-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Table 1 lists the participant characteristics according to sex and pre-diabetes status. The final analytic sample included 8,254 adults without diabetes. The mean age of the overall population was 41.3 yrs: 40.6 yrs among men and 42.0 yrs among women (P < 0.001). In men and women, age (P < 0.001) and BMI (P < 0.001) were significantly higher in the pre-diabetic group than in the non-diabetic group, whereas participation in physical activity was more common in the non-diabetic group (men: P < 0.001; women: P = 0.002). The participant characteristics according to protein energy intake quintiles were also examined (data not shown). The BMI was not significantly associated with the protein-energy intake level.

An examination of the baseline characteristic differences between participants included in the final analysis and those excluded because of missing values revealed no significant differences in protein energy intake or the total KHEI score (data not shown). Protein and nutrient intakes

Table 2 lists the macronutrient energy contributions across quintiles of protein energy intake, stratified according to sex and pre-diabetes status. Across all sex- and prediabetes-specific subgroups, the proportion of energy intake from protein increased progressively from approximately 10% at Q1 to 22% at Q5. Consistent and significant increasing trends were also observed for the total protein intake (g/day), protein intake per kilogram of body weight (g/kg), and protein intake per 1,000 kcal (all P for trend < 0.001). In all groups, higher protein energy intake was associated with a higher proportion of energy from fat (men in the non-diabetic group: P for trend = 0.001; men in the pre-diabetic group and all female subgroups: P for trend < 0.001) and a lower proportion of energy from carbohydrates (all P for trend < 0.001).

DISCUSSION

Using data from the KNHANES 2019–2021, this cross-sectional study examined the associations between protein intake and diet quality and diabetes-related metabolic health indicators among 8,254 Korean adults without diabetes. Regardless of sex and pre-diabetic status, a higher protein intake was positively associated with diet quality, but the association was not linear and was attenuated at Q3 or Q4. The protein intake and diet quality were significantly associated with selected diabetes-related metabolic health indicators, but no significant interaction between the 2 was observed. Higher protein intake was inversely associated with elevated triglycerides among pre-diabetic men and with elevated blood pressure among pre-diabetic women, whereas it was positively associated with a reduced HDL-C among non-diabetic women.

The level of protein intake observed in this study was consistent with the recent trends reported among Korean adults. Over the past decade, the proportion of energy derived from protein has steadily increased in Korea, accompanied by a growing proportion of individuals whose protein energy intake exceeds the upper level of Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Ranges (AMDR) for protein, 20% [38]. The protein energy intake has also been reported to be higher in younger age groups and among men. This trend has been largely attributed to the increased consumption of animal-based protein, particularly from meat, while the relative contribution of plant-based protein sources has remained stable or declined [39]. The estimated average requirement (EAR) for protein among Korean adults is 0.73 g/kg/day. In this study, all quintiles of women except the lowest (Q1) exceeded the EAR. This finding is consistent with previous reports indicating that most Korean adults already meet the EAR for protein [38]. Although the highest protein intake group (Q5) exceeded the upper level of AMDR for protein (20%) specified in the 2025 Dietary Reference Intakes for Koreans (KDRI), the overall distribution of protein intake in the study population did not deviate markedly from the KDRI-recommended range of 10–20% and largely reflected typical dietary patterns in Korea.

As the protein intake increased, a corresponding reduction in the proportion of energy derived from carbohydrates was observed. In the highest protein intake group (Q5), carbohydrate intake approached the lower bound of the recommended range and, although not excessively low, was significantly lower than that in Q4. This pattern suggests that higher protein intake may be accompanied by a relative displacement of carbohydrates. The KDRI recommends balanced energy contributions from carbohydrates, protein, and fat to reduce the risk of chronic diseases associated with the nutritional imbalance [38]. Although high-protein, low-carbohydrate dietary patterns may be beneficial for short-term glycemic control [40,41], their long-term metabolic effects remain uncertain [42]. Potential mechanisms underlying these associations have been proposed. Elevated circulating amino acid levels associated with higher protein intake may influence glucose metabolism through their effects on insulin action and glucagon secretion, as well as by serving as substrates for gluconeogenesis [15]. Nevertheless, the overall metabolic impact of these processes may vary depending on an individual’s insulin sensitivity and overall metabolic status. In addition, the metabolic effects of such dietary patterns may depend on the type and quality of carbohydrates reduced. For example, reductions in fiber-rich, complex carbohydrates have been associated with unfavorable changes in insulin sensitivity, whereas adequate dietary fiber intake has been associated with more favorable metabolic profiles [16].

An increase in protein intake up to a certain level may help improve diet quality, but higher protein intake may not always be associated with overall dietary balance. In this study, diet quality generally improved as the protein intake increased, but a nonlinear pattern emerged, with the diet quality declining in the highest intake group (Q5). In Q5, lower scores were observed for the percentage of energy derived from carbohydrates and for sodium moderation. Among women, the scores for total fruit and fresh fruit intake were also lower. These findings suggest that higher protein intake does not necessarily correspond to improved overall diet quality and may be accompanied by shifts in dietary composition. Diets characterized by greater diversity across food groups have been associated with more favorable metabolic profiles and lower diabetes risk in previous studies, through mechanisms including gut microbiota-mediated effects, synergistic interactions among nutrients, and efficient nutrient provision [43,44,45,46]. In this context, an approach emphasizing balanced intake across diverse food groups, as reflected in existing dietary guidelines [47,48], may provide a useful framework for interpreting these findings rather than focusing solely on single nutrients such as protein.

In the present study, interactions between protein intake and diet quality were not statistically significant for most diabetes-related metabolic health indicators. This finding suggests that the observed associations between protein intake and metabolic health indicators may occur largely independent of the overall diet quality. Nevertheless, considering that increases in protein intake are, to some extent, accompanied by broader changes in overall dietary composition, the implications of these structural relationships for metabolic health merit more detailed examination. In particular, potential nonlinear patterns and mediating effects should be explored carefully in future studies.

Previous studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding the relationships between protein intake and lipid profiles and blood pressure, with some suggesting beneficial effects and others reporting neutral or mixed results [49,50,51,52]. Consistent with these findings, heterogeneous associations were observed across subgroups in this study. For several indicators, the ORs decreased as the protein intake increased up to the fourth quintile (Q4) and then increased slightly in the highest quintile (Q5), suggesting a potential nonlinear relationship rather than a linear pattern.

The glycemic status may influence the interpretation of the associations between protein intake and metabolic health indicators. Pre-diabetes represents a stage during which the progression to diabetes may be delayed or mitigated through lifestyle modification, including dietary changes and physical activity [45,53], and individuals in this stage may exhibit greater metabolic sensitivity to dietary exposures compared with those without pre-diabetes [54]. In the present study, the inverse associations with certain indicators, such as triglycerides in men and blood pressure in women, were observed primarily among individuals with pre-diabetes, whereas such patterns were not evident in the non-diabetic group. In contrast, among non-diabetic women, a tendency toward an increased risk of reduced HDL-C was observed.

The increased risk of reduced HDL-C observed exclusively among non-diabetic women warrants further consideration. In this subgroup, the association between higher protein intake and reduced HDL-C remained significant across multiple models, including those adjusted for diet quality (KHEI and modified KHEI), indicating that this relationship may be independent of the overall diet quality. Although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, several explanations may be considered. Previous studies have suggested that high protein intake may influence lipid metabolism in a sex-specific manner, potentially related to hormonal regulation [50]. In addition, although the KHEI scores tended to increase as the protein intake increased among non-diabetic women, not all KHEI components improved consistently. Some components, such as fresh fruit intake scores, showed a decreasing trend as the protein intake increased. Furthermore, in the highest quintile (Q5), non-diabetic women had a higher protein intake per kilogram of body weight (1.6 g/kg/day) compared with pre-diabetic women (1.4 g/kg/day). This higher intake was accompanied by a tendency toward lower vitamin C intake, a nutrient that contributes to the antioxidant function of HDL particles [55]. Overall, these findings suggest that higher protein intake may be associated with broader shifts in dietary composition not fully captured by composite diet quality indices such as the KHEI. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted with caution, given the cross-sectional design and the potential for type I error due to multiple subgroup analyses.

This study had several limitations. First, the causal relationships among protein intake, diet quality, and diabetes-related metabolic health indicators cannot be established due to the cross-sectional design. Second, assessments of protein intake and diet quality were based on a single 24-h dietary recall, which may not fully capture the habitual dietary patterns and is subject to recall bias. Third, although protein source-specific nutritional composition and physiological effects may differ, this study focused on the associations between the overall protein intake and metabolic health indicators according to sex and pre-diabetic status and did not include source-specific analyses. In addition, the KNHANES dataset provides limited information on the major protein sources, such as high-protein processed foods and protein or amino acid supplements, which constrained more detailed source-based analyses. Fourth, the KHEI used to assess diet quality includes components related to protein-source foods. Therefore, potential overadjustment can occur when the protein energy intake and the KHEI score are included simultaneously in analytical models. To address this concern, this paper presented the results from a basic model, a model also adjusted for the total KHEI score, and a final model adjusted for a modified KHEI score excluding protein-related components. Nevertheless, the modified KHEI may not fully capture the overall diet quality, and direct comparability with previous studies using the standard KHEI remains limited. In addition, sociodemographic differences were observed between the included and excluded participants for some variables. Although the key exposure variables, including protein intake and diet quality, were comparable, the potential impact of selection bias due to missing covariate data cannot be excluded. Finally, given the multiple subgroup analyses and outcomes examined, the possibility of type I error cannot be excluded. Despite these limitations, this study has notable strengths. This is the first study to systematically examine the associations among protein intake, diet quality, and diabetes-related metabolic health indicators in Korean adults without diabetes, with stratification according to sex and pre-diabetic status. Using nationally representative KNHANES data, the findings may apply to the broader population, rather than being limited to individuals with a particular interest in protein intake.

In conclusion, the protein intake among Korean adults largely fell within or close to the KDRI-recommended range, despite the increasing interest in higher protein consumption. Significant associations of the protein intake were found with one different indicator in each sex and pre-diabetic status group, independent of the diet quality. Therefore, among Korean adults, a “high protein intake” per se may not necessarily reflect a healthier dietary pattern. Rather, maintaining an appropriate level of protein intake while avoiding imbalances in overall dietary composition may be associated with a similar level of metabolic health relative to the higher protein intake, without potential adverse effects. This study provided insight into the relationships between protein intake, diet quality, and diabetes-related metabolic health indicators in Korean adults in their usual daily life and highlights the complexity of these associations. These findings may inform future longitudinal studies aimed at clarifying the long-term implications of high-protein diets for metabolic health and their potential relevance to the diabetes-related outcomes.
