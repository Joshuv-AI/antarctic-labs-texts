Increased plasma ceramide levels are associated with arterial stiffness and vascular ageing: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jiachi Ren, Guan Wang, Ying Wang, Lin Li, Jingru Sun, Shengmin Liu, Xiaofen Wu, Cuntai Zhang, Lei Ruan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular (CV) events pose a substantial threat to human health. Plasma ceramides have been shown to exhibit predictive value for CV events; however, its correlation with arterial stiffness had yet to be fully elucidated. This study aimed to explore the associations of plasma ceramide levels and their ratios with arterial stiffness.MethodsThis retrospective study involved participants whose ceramide levels were measured and whose arterial stiffness was assessed. Multivariate regression was used to assess the association between plasma ceramides and arterial stiffness. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was used to assess the correlation between plasma ceramide levels and arterial stiffness. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to assess the diagnostic value of plasma ceramides for arterial stiffness. Subgroup analyses were also performed.ResultsThis study included 503 patients with a mean age of 43.7 ± 10.8 years. The results showed that plasma ceramides 24:0 and 22:0 were significantly associated with arterial stiffness, as supported by multivariate regression analysis. RCS analysis revealed a potential linear relationship between plasma 24:0 ceramide and arterial stiffness (P for nonlinearity > 0.05). In contrast, a significant nonlinear correlation was found for plasma 22:0 ceramide (P for nonlinearity = 0.04). ROC analysis revealed optimal cut-off values of 1.035 µmol/L for plasma 22:0 ceramide (68.0% specificity, 67.3% sensitivity) and 3.335 µmol/L for plasma 24:0 ceramide (64.1% specificity, 74.8% sensitivity), with area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.702 and 0.724, respectively, demonstrating acceptable diagnostic performance. Additionally, subgroup analyses revealed a marked connection between serum ceramide levels and arterial stiffness, especially in male participants and individuals with normal lipid profiles.ConclusionsPlasma ceramide levels are significantly correlated with arterial stiffness, which suggests that they play a role as biomarkers for vascular ageing assessments and highlights their potential value for early prevention strategies against CV events.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02706-3.Keywords: Ceramides, Ageing, Arterial stiffness, Biomarkers

Background

The vascular system undergoes a complex ageing process that is characterised by structural and functional remodelling, and increased arterial stiffness is a significant manifestation of this process [1]. Remodelling is essential in the progression of chronic cardiovascular disease (CVD), which continues to be a primary cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide [2, 3]. An increasing number of studies have aimed to delay the progression of CV ageing by ameliorating vascular stiffness. The current approach focuses primarily on preventing the damaging effects of CV risk factors caused by vascular ageing [4]. Pharmacological agents, including statins and antihypertensive medications, have been shown to be effective for decreasing arterial stiffness, especially among individuals with hypertension [5–7]. However, existing approaches have been proven to be insufficient, and in clinical practice, effective strategies for the prevention and treatment of arterial stiffness have yet to be established [8]. Therefore, early identification and targeted therapeutic modulation of vascular senescence have emerged as crucial approaches for the prevention of cardiovascular complications.

Ceramides, which are bioactive lipids composed of sphingosine and fatty acids [9], have been identified as significant biomarkers of adverse cardiac events [10]. Ceramides have strong predictive capabilities; for example, plasma ceramide ratios are significantly correlated with coronary artery disease (CAD) and insulin levels [11] and serve as important predictors of CV mortality in both stable CAD patients and those with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) [12]. Furthermore, plasma ceramides can predict both the mortality rate of cardiovascular diseases [13] and the residual risk in individuals with stable coronary artery disease who have received optimal medical care [14]. The current evidence provides a potential rationale for the adverse effects of plasma ceramides on cardiac events, which may include impaired insulin signalling and glucose transport, insulin resistance [9, 11], escalation of oxidative damage [15, 16], inflammatory stimulation [16], and apoptosis [9, 17, 18].

Despite considerable progress in understanding the role of ceramides in CV events, their direct association with arterial stiffness has not been sufficiently investigated. In particular, the relationships between specific ceramide species or ratios and quantifiable arterial stiffening have not been systematically examined, which limits the clinical utility of certain ceramides in early vascular ageing assessments. Based on the pivotal role of ceramides in the vasculature, this study hypothesises that distinct plasma ceramide species and their ratios are significantly associated with arterial stiffness, thus suggesting their potential role as biomarkers of vascular ageing. Accordingly, the primary aim of this study was to elucidate the relationship between plasma ceramides and arterial stiffness, thereby clarifying the role of these bioactive lipids in vascular ageing and their potential utility in clinical assessments.

Methods

Population and study design

This study retrospectively enrolled adult patients who underwent arterial stiffness evaluation by brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV) and plasma ceramide measurements during health checkups at Tongji Hospital, affiliated with Tongji Medical College of Huazhong University of Science and Technology, between April 2021 and August 2022. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) the use of lipid-lowering medications; (2) the administration of glucose-lowering medications; (3) the presence of malignant neoplasms or the occurrence of postoperative complications; or (4) a lack of adequate baseline data. This study received approval from the Medical Ethics Committee at Tongji Hospital (TJ-IRB202406039) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Assessment of plasma ceramide

Plasma samples were initially treated with ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) to inhibit coagulation and then stored at -80 °C for a maximum of two weeks. The levels of ceramide species in the plasma were determined using LC–MS/MS using an AB Sciex Triple Quad™ 4500MD system (Sciex, Framingham, MA, United States) according to a previously detailed protocol [13, 15]. Protein precipitation was performed with the use of a deuterated internal standard during sample preparation, which improved the precision of ceramide quantification. The analysis of plasma ceramides was performed by liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry, which used positive electrospray ionisation and multiple reaction monitoring for the detection of precursor and product ion mass‒charge ratios. Detailed information regarding the fragment ions for each analyte is presented in Table 1. The measurements were conducted by individuals blinded to the patients’ clinical details [13].

Arterial stiffness measurements

The Omron Colin BP-203RPE III device (Omron Health Care, Kyoto, Japan) was used to measure the baPWV. The plethysmographic and oscillometric pressure sensors were linked to four pneumatic pressure cuffs affixed to the upper arms and ankles of the participants. Patients fasted overnight for at least 12 h and were subjected to a 5-minute period of rest in the supine position. The final baPWV value was defined as the maximum baPWV between the left and right sides [19]. Arterial stiffness was defined as baPWV ≥ 1400 cm/s, which is a validated threshold for vascular ageing in accordance with current guidelines [4]. All testing was performed by qualified physicians in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Covariates

Upon enrolment, supplementary data were collected, including body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), the levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), uric acid (UA), and creatinine (Cr) as well as estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), a history of tobacco and alcohol use, and medical history. The criteria for the diagnosis of dyslipidaemia included TC > 6.2 mmol/L, TG ≥ 2.3 mmol/L, LDL-C ≥ 4.1 mmol/L, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) ≤ 1.0 mmol/L, or treatment with lipid-lowering drugs per the 2016 Chinese guidelines [20]. Considering the impact of lipid-lowering medications, patients who received lipid-lowering therapy were excluded from this study. Hypertension was defined according to the JNC-7 criteria: SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, DBP ≥ 90 mmHg, current antihypertensive medication use, or self-reported hypertension [21].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed with R version 4.3.3, and the threshold for significance was P < 0.05. Categorical data are expressed as frequencies with percentages and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables are summarised as either means ± standard deviations (SDs) (for normally distributed data) or as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) (for nonnormally distributed data). Normally distributed continuous data were compared using the t test, whereas nonnormally distributed continuous data were compared using the Mann‒Whitney U test. Multiple linear regression and logistic regression models were employed to analyse continuous and categorical dependent variables, respectively.

In the multivariate analysis, three adjusted models were developed to address confounding variables: Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, SBP, DBP, FPG, eGFR, alcohol consumption, smoking status, and BMI; and Model 3 was adjusted for age, sex, SBP, DBP, FPG, eGFR, alcohol consumption, smoking status, BMI, HDL-C, and TG level. The nonlinear and dose‒response relationships between plasma ceramide levels and arterial stiffness were evaluated using RCS analysis. All confounding variables in Model 3 were included when the RCS curves were generated. Using ROC analysis, optimal diagnostic cut-off values for plasma ceramides for the identification of individuals with arterial stiffness were determined. This preliminary evaluation quantified the diagnostic performance of ceramides by calculating sensitivity, specificity, and the Youden index (J = sensitivity + specificity − 1) and reported AUC values with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The threshold for statistical significance was P < 0.05. As reported by Hosmer et al. [22], the discriminative ability of a model is classified as follows: (1) outstanding, AUC values ≥ 0.9; (2) excellent, AUC values ≥ 0.8 and < 0.9; (3) acceptable, AUC values ≥ 0.7 and < 0.8; (4) poor, AUC values ˃ 0.5 and < 0.7; and (5) no discrimination, AUC = 0.5. Additionally, subgroup analyses were performed based on age, sex, and the presence of dyslipidaemia and hypertension.

Results

A total of 527 patients were identified for the assessment of plasma ceramide levels. Among these participants, 503 successfully completed the arterial stiffness evaluation, which ultimately formed the basis for the final analysis (Fig. 1). The patients were categorised into two distinct groups according to their arterial stiffness measurements. Table 2 provides an overview of the baseline clinical characteristics of both cohorts. Compared with the nonarterial stiffness group, the arterial stiffness group presented significantly elevated blood pressure (P < 0.001). Furthermore, except for HDL-C, noteworthy disparities were identified in other blood lipoprotein level, age, sex, BMI, smoking status, FPG, UA concentration, eGFR, and Cr level. Moreover, although the plasma levels of 14:0 ceramide did not significantly differ (P > 0.05, Table 2), the levels of ceramides with other chain lengths and ratios significantly differed between the two groups.

Multivariate regression was used to examine the connection between plasma ceramide levels and arterial stiffness. Baseline characteristics such as age, sex, SBP, DBP, FPG, eGFR, alcohol consumption, smoking status, BMI, HDL-C, and TG level were systematically included as confounding variables in the regression analyses. As shown in Table 3, in Model 2, the plasma levels of 24:0 ceramide (multivariate linear Model 2; standardised β = 11.70 ± 5.85, P = 0.046) and 22:0 ceramide (multivariate linear Model 2; standardised β = 53.31 ± 20.65, P = 0.010) were positively associated with arterial stiffness. The results revealed that in the fully adjusted logistic regression Model 3, the plasma levels of 24:0 and 22:0 ceramide were significantly associated with arterial stiffness (Table 3). The odds ratios were significant for both ceramides: 1.52 (95% confidence interval: 1.15–2.01; P = 0.003) and 3.29 (95% confidence interval: 1.26–8.59; P = 0.015), for 24:0 and 22:0, respectively. In stark contrast, the plasma levels of ceramides 14:0, 16:0,18:0, 20:0, and 24:1 were not significantly associated with arterial stiffness (P > 0.05) in Model 3. This study delved deeper into the intricate relationships between various ceramide ratios—specifically, 16:0/24:0, 18:0/24:0, and 24:1/24:0—and their impact on arterial stiffness. Notably, higher levels of the 16:0/24:0 and 24:1/24:0 ceramide ratios were significantly associated with a lower likelihood of arterial stiffness.

To further elucidate these relationships, a dose‒response analysis was performed to examine the associations between plasma levels of ceramide 22:0 and 24:0, along with the aforementioned ceramide ratios, and arterial stiffness. RCS curves were employed for this assessment (Fig. 2). The findings revealed a substantial nonlinear relationship between plasma levels of ceramide 22:0 (B) and arterial stiffness, with a P value for nonlinearity of < 0.05. In contrast, the relationships involving plasma 24:0 ceramide (A), the 16:0/24:0 ratio (C), and the 24:1/24:0 ratio (D) appeared to follow a linear trend, as suggested by the P value for nonlinearity, which was ˃ 0.05.

The dynamic association between ceramide levels and arterial stiffness. OR odds ratio, CI confidence interval

ROC curves were generated to evaluate the discriminative capacity of specific plasma ceramide species and ratios for identifying arterial stiffness (Fig. 3), while the AUC was calculated to quantify diagnostic accuracy. Specifically, the plasma levels of 24:0 ceramide demonstrated acceptable clinically discriminative power (AUC = 0.724, 95% CI: 0.671–0.777), with an optimal threshold of 3.335 µmol/L (74.8% sensitivity, 64.1% specificity). At this threshold, the investigation correctly identified 74.8% of individuals with arterial stiffness and 64.1% of individuals without arterial stiffness. Similarly, the plasma levels of 22:0 ceramide demonstrated a comparable performance (AUC = 0.702, 95% CI: 0.647–0.757; cut-off of 1.035 µmol/L, 67.3% sensitivity, 68.0% specificity). At this threshold, 67.3% of individuals with arterial stiffness and 68.0% of individuals without arterial stiffness were correctly identified. Furthermore, the Youden index values of 0.352 for the plasma levels of 22:0 ceramide and 0.389 for the plasma levels of 24:0 ceramide confirmed their diagnostic utility. In contrast, ceramide ratios exhibited suboptimal diagnostic utility: the 16:0/24:0 ratio yielded an AUC < 0.70 at a cut-off of 0.139 (70.1% sensitivity, 57.3% specificity), whereas the 24:1/24:0 ratio at 0.229 showed high specificity (78.3%) but low sensitivity (37.4%) (Table 4).

Table 5 provides an overview of the findings from the subgroup analyses that examined the relationship between plasma ceramides and arterial stiffness. After multivariable adjustment, the results revealed a substantial correlation between specific ceramide species and arterial stiffness, particularly in male participants. This analysis indicated that 24:0 ceramide demonstrated a statistically significant association with arterial stiffness, with an odds ratio of 1.81 (95% CI: 1.26–2.60; P = 0.001). Similarly, 22:0 ceramide had a pronounced association, with an odds ratio of 4.16 (95% CI: 1.25–13.58; P = 0.020), whereas the ratio of 24:1 to 24:0 ceramide also demonstrated a significant relationship, with an odds ratio of 9.13e− 03 (95% CI: 2.54e− 04–0.33; P = 0.010). Furthermore, these associations were also pronounced in populations with normal blood lipid levels. For example, the odds ratio for 24:0 ceramide in this patient subgroup was 2.35 (95% CI: 1.28–4.34; P = 0.006), while 22:0 ceramide had a high odds ratio of 14.28 (95% CI: 1.65–123.43; P = 0.016). The ratio of 24:1/ to 24:0 ceramide in this context also indicated a significant correlation, with an odds ratio of 1.08e− 03 (95% CI: 3.86e− 06–0.03; P = 0.018). In contrast, the analysis indicated that the 22:0 ceramide and the 24:1/24:0 ceramide ratio were not significantly associated with arterial stiffness when participants were stratified by age (P > 0.05). Interestingly, the 16:0/ to 24:0 ceramide ratio demonstrated a potential inverse association, which was only pronounced in the older age groups, with an odds ratio of 3.53e− 05 (95% CI: 1.48e− 09–0.84; P = 0.046). Additionally, in both the younger and older age groups, elevated plasma levels of 24:0 ceramide were significantly positively associated with arterial stiffness. Notably, ceramide levels were significantly associated with arterial stiffness among normotensive participants (P < 0.05), whereas no statistically significant association was observed in hypertensive individuals.

Discussion

The results of this study revealed that plasma ceramides –specifically 22:0 and 24:0 – are significantly associated with arterial stiffness and demonstrate acceptable diagnostic performance. The correlation between specific ceramide species and arterial stiffness varied based on sex. Additionally, this correlation was statistically significant among individuals without dyslipidaemia and those with normal blood pressure.

LDL-C and TG are widely recognised as important lipids that are present in the bloodstream and serve as essential biomarkers for predicting adverse cardiac events [23]. These lipids have long been the focus of cardiovascular risk assessment [12], but recent advancements in lipidomics technology and related research have revealed a new player in this area: plasma ceramides. Ceramides are emerging as novel and critical CV risk factors [24] and belong to a complex family of bioactive lipid molecules, which are formed by the combination of sphingosine and a fatty acid [25]. The unique biological effects of ceramides, including their potential to elicit opposing physiological responses, can be largely attributed to the diversity of ceramide species. These species are characterised by variations in both the length of their fatty acid chains and their degree of saturation, which significantly influence their biological activity and health implications [9]. Recent studies have revealed the role of ceramides in the pathogenesis of CVDs and have suggested that they may act as driving factors in the development of these conditions. Specifically, ceramides promote CVD through mechanisms such as oxidative stress, which can lead to cellular damage and inflammation—key contributors to heart disease [24]. Moreover, ceramide levels tend to increase with age, thus highlighting their potential role in age-related CV risk [26]. In a comprehensive community-based study involving elderly participants, elevated levels of ceramides were correlated with a heightened risk of mortality [27]. However, it remains unclear whether ceramide is independently linked to arterial stiffness. Emerging evidence suggests that there is a significant correlation between plasma ceramides and vascular endothelial dysfunction, a condition that plays a critical role in CVD progression [18]. Given that arterial stiffness is closely intertwined with endothelial dysfunction, this research posits that plasma ceramide could serve as a key factor that influences the health of the arterial endothelium [18].

Arterial stiffness is increasingly recognised as a significant predictor of CVD risk [28, 29], as arterial stiffness is correlated with various risk factors and serves as an independent prognostic indicator of CV mortality [29]. In this study, baPWV was used as a metric for the evaluation of arterial stiffness, providing an assessment of vascular health [30]. Ceramide, a type of sphingolipid, has been shown to potentially exert multifaceted effects on arterial stiffness. One of the most critical factors that contributes to the development of arterial stiffness is endothelial dysfunction [31], which is characterised by an impaired ability of the endothelium to regulate vascular tone and maintain homeostasis. Ceramide plays a pivotal role in promoting endothelial dysfunction through the activation of inflammasomes, which are complexes that trigger inflammatory responses, within endothelial cells [32]. By strategically lowering ceramide levels, it may be possible to reduce vascular inflammation, thereby enhancing endothelial function and potentially mitigating the progression of arterial stiffness [33]. Interestingly, while ceramide is often associated with negative vascular outcomes, emerging studies suggest that it may also play a protective role under certain conditions. For example, sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P), an intermediate product of ceramide metabolism, has been shown to activate endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) via G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) [34]. This activation leads to increased production of nitric oxide (NO), a crucial molecule for vascular dilation [35]. A deficiency in NO production can result in diminished vascular relaxation, which triggers coagulation and inflammatory pathways and exacerbates oxidative stress [9]. This investigation revealed that higher plasma 16:0/24:0 and 24:1/24:0 ceramide ratios were significantly associated with reduced arterial stiffness. This study further revealed a significant nonlinear correlation between the levels of ceramide 22:0 (B) and arterial stiffness. This finding suggests a complex dose‒response relationship, which indicates that certain levels of ceramide may exert a potential protective role against the stiffening of arteries.

Furthermore, the evaluation of the associations between plasma ceramides and arterial stiffness revealed a degree of heterogeneity across the subgroups. Given that the average age of the study population is 43.7 years, 45 years was selected as the age cut-off for subgroup analysis to ensure sufficient participant numbers in both age groups for statistical analysis. This age criterion is also substantiated by epidemiological literature, as CAD becomes a significant cause of morbidity and mortality after the age of 45 years [36]. Additionally, another study of adults aged ≥ 45 years without a history of cardiovascular disease demonstrated that the ageing process progressively led to increased arterial and ventricular stiffness [37]. These findings suggest that 45 years may be a threshold for the onset of age-related cardiovascular changes. However, when stratified by age using a cutoff of 45 years, subgroup analysis revealed a trend in which, apart from 24:0 ceramide levels showing significance in both age groups and the 16:0/24:0 ceramide ratio being significant in the subgroup above 45 years old, no other ceramides or ratios showed significant differences in the age-based subgroups. First, this study suggests that the lack of significance in subgroup analyses may stem primarily from a reduced sample size, which reduces the statistical power. Second, for individuals aged < 45 years with relatively healthy vascular systems, specific ceramide species are insufficient to independently drive pronounced arterial stiffness. Conversely, in those aged > 45 years, specific ceramide species likely participate in synergistic interactions with age-related risk factors to significantly impact arterial stiffness, which potentially overshadows their apparent individual contributions. Additionally, the relationship between specific ceramides and arterial stiffness may be nonlinear, and the 45-year age cut-off potentially delineates different phases of this relationship. Moreover, the associations involving 22:0 ceramide, 24:0 ceramide, and the 24:1/ to 24:0 ceramide ratio exhibited significance among males and individuals without dyslipidaemia. A potential explanation for these findings may be that under conditions of dyslipidaemia, oxidative stress and inflammatory responses caused by excessive lipids may play a dominant role, overshadowing the effects of ceramides. The observed sex differences in the plasma levels of 22:0 ceramide, 24:0 ceramide, and the 24:1/24:0 ceramide ratio may be partially explained by the following mechanisms. First, males exhibit elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) production via mitochondrial pathways, which promotes ceramide accumulation and endothelial dysfunction [9, 38–40]. Second, sex hormones directly modulate sphingolipid metabolism through enzyme-specific regulation. Sukocheva et al. [41] revealed that oestrogen acts as a potent activator of sphingosine kinase (SphK) in human breast cancer cells, where it catalyses the phosphorylation of sphingosine to S1P, which subsequently exerts vascular regulatory effects [34, 35]. Conversely, testosterone may influence ceramide synthase, leading to higher levels of ceramide 20:0 in males than in females [42]. These sex-regulatory mechanisms may collectively play a role in sex differences. Additionally, in the normotension group, ceramide was significantly associated with arterial stiffness, but this relationship was not evident in the hypertension group. This phenomenon may stem from the bidirectional risk potentiation between arterial stiffness and hypertension [5, 43]. This confounding effect may obscure the impact of ceramide on arterial stiffness. These findings highlight the importance of investigating the relationship between ceramide and arterial stiffness in a wider population.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. This study is the first to systematically explore the relationship between plasma ceramides and arterial stiffness, thus offering a new perspective on vascular ageing. Moreover, the analysis of various ceramide species and their ratios revealed potential nonlinear relationships between 22:0 ceramide and quantifiable arterial stiffness. Finally, the detailed records and meticulous documentation of the vascular ageing data platform, coupled with its precision, significantly enhance the rigor and validity of this study’s results. However, this study also has several limitations. Given the cross-sectional nature of the study, the causal relationship between ceramide levels and arterial stiffness could not be established, and the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms could not be fully elucidated. Moreover, the dynamic nature of this relationship over time was not accurately characterised. Furthermore, the generalizability of the findings may be restricted, as this study was performed at a single centre. The verification of these findings through future multicentre cohort studies with extended follow-up is essential. Next, although a wide range of confounders were controlled, the possibility of the impact of residual confounding from unmeasured factors, such as environmental and genetic influences, cannot be eliminated. Future studies should incorporate more comprehensive covariate data to further validate these findings.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study revealed that elevated plasma levels of ceramides 22:0 and 24:0 were significantly associated with arterial stiffness. These specific species of ceramides may serve as potential biomarkers for vascular ageing assessment, which could contribute to improvements in the early detection and management of cardiovascular diseases. These findings suggest the potential utility of plasma ceramide measurements as a potential risk factor in health assessment, which may provide a basis for more optimised interventions and support for healthier ageing.

While this study provides novel insights, further research is essential to elucidate the underlying mechanisms through which ceramides influence arterial stiffness, thereby revealing new therapeutic targets. Longitudinal and clinical trials are necessary to determine how ceramide-targeted interventions affect cardiovascular health.
