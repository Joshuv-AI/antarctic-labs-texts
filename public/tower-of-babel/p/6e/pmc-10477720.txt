Acute calcific periarthritis of acromioclavicular joint: A case report
Authors: Shila Awal, Nabaraj Acharya, Lila Awal, Nabin Prajapati, Kunal Gupta, Ayushi Srivastava
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractKey Clinical MessageIt is of utmost importance to be aware of acute calcific periarthritis, as it may confuse the clinicians leading to misdiagnosis. Furthermore, patients may be subjected to various diagnostic and invasive procedures due to a lack of familiarity with this condition.AbstractDeposition of hydroxyapatite crystals in the para‐articular soft tissues is a well‐known condition, often involving the shoulder joint. However, the deposition of calcium salts in the para‐articular soft tissue of acromioclavicular joint has rarely been described. We report a case of a 55‐year‐old otherwise healthy man, presented with acute onset of pain and restriction of movement of left shoulder, who was diagnosed based on radiographic findings with calcific periarthritis of the acromioclavicular joint. As suggested by the literatures, he was managed conservatively, and no reoccurrence was reported after the episode.Keywords: acromioclavicular joint, calcific periarthritis, shoulder

INTRODUCTION

Hydroxyapatite deposition disease, characterized by the deposition of calcium crystals around para‐articular soft tissues, is often monoarticular and can involve various joints, the shoulder being the most common. Other joints like elbow, wrist, hip, knee, ankle, foot, hand, and spine can also be affected. 1 However, the deposition of calcium salts as an acute calcification around the acromioclavicular joint is a rare condition. 2 This condition is usually self‐limiting. Unfortunately, with no radiographic evidence of improvement, some patients remain symptomatic. 3 This report presents a case of acute calcific periarthritis of the acromioclavicular joint, presented with acute pain of the shoulder, managed conservatively.

CASE REPORT

A 55‐year‐old male presented with a complaint of pain over the left shoulder for 1 week. The pain started a day after lifting a heavy load and was continuous, progressive, non‐radiating, and aggravated by movement and sleeping on that side. He reported pain to be severe enough to prevent him from performing daily activities. He denied a history of trauma, fever, and any systemic or metabolic disorders.

On examination, there was a local rise in temperature over the left acromioclavicular joint, which was tender to touch. There was no swelling or redness of the overlying skin. Both active and passive range of motion was limited due to pain. There was no lymphadenopathy, and the neurological examination was normal.

Plain radiographs of the region revealed the presence of calcific deposits in the acromioclavicular joint. There was no alteration of bone structures of the joint (Figure 1A,B). Complete blood count was within normal range.

Plain radiograph of the shoulder. (A) Anterior posterior view (B) Scapular Y view.

After clinical examination and radiological findings, a diagnosis of calcific periarthritis of the acromioclavicular joint was made. The patient was advised for relative functional rest for 10 days, taking care not to use the left upper limb to move heavy loads and to avoid overhead activities. He was also prescribed NSAIDs for 7 days. The patient was followed after 2 months. He reported the disappearance of the pain and a recovery of full range of movement of the shoulder, both active and passive. Radiographic findings supported the disappearance of the calcific deposits in the joint. (Figure 2).

Complete disappearance of calcific deposit in 2 months follow up.

When the patient was contacted after 7 months, he reported that there had been no recurrence of symptoms of pain or functional limitation of the shoulder.

DISCUSSION

Acromioclavicular pain is a common condition usually arising due to traumatic causes and less commonly due to atraumatic causes such as infections and arthritis. 2 Acute calcific periarthritis is a rarely diagnosed condition of the acromioclavicular joint and is frequently misdiagnosed as septic arthritis and fractures. This is especially the case when calcification involves joints other than the shoulder. 4 Calcific periarthritis is equally distributed between males and females and mostly present between the fourth and sixth decades of life. 1

Acute calcific periarthritis can present with varying signs and symptoms including pain, redness, swelling, and limited movements of the joint. 5 The pain can be severe enough to limit activities of daily living similar to our case. The pathophysiology can be described as the deposition of hydroxyapatite along with inflammatory cells, mostly neutrophils. 6 Hydroxyapatite crystals are thought to be deposited around tendons and joints as a precipitate owing to local hypoxia. 7 The pathogenesis of the hydroxyapatite deposition disease can be divided into three phases, namely, precalcific, calcific, and postcalcific stages. 8 In precalcific stage, the tenocytes undergo metaplasia into chondrocytes. This stage is usually avascular; however, the glycosaminoglycans are prominent in the tissue. The next stage i.e., calcific stage is characterized by the deposition of calcium crystals in matrix vesicles which later coalesce to form foci of calcification. These foci are gradually bordered by fibrocartilagenous tissue. With disease inactivity, these foci of calcification undergo resorption. Finally, in the postcalcific stage, the granulation tissue replaces the area of resorption. 8

Several imaging modalities help diagnose the condition. Radiography is the key diagnostic modality for evaluating calcific deposition. Our case was also diagnosed with an X‐ray of the shoulder. Sequential X‐ray films are cost‐effective and help in recognizing a change in the size and location and spontaneous disappearance. 9 , 10 Ultrasound is useful for both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes of acute calcific periarthritis, particularly in the shoulder. 11

Treatment options include conservative and surgical therapy. Nonsurgical therapy is the mainstay of treatment, with NSAIDs, physical therapy, local hot or cold compression, corticosteroid injections, and extracorporeal shockwave therapy comprising first‐line treatment. 1 , 3 , 12 NSAIDs are the mainstay of therapy for pain reduction in acute phases. 3 In a similar case reported by Iovane et al., 2 ultrasound‐guided percutaneous treatment with needles resulted in the disappearance of the pain and recovery of functional status when reassessed 3 weeks after the treatment. Open or arthroscopic surgery for the removal of calcium deposits is considered after the failure of conservative therapy. 3
