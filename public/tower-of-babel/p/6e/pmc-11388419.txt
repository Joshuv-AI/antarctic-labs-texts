Multifunctional biomolecular corona-inspired nanoremediation of antibiotic residues
Authors: Jie Hou, Yuqi Lu, Qiqi Chen, Xinyi Liao, Xinyue Wu, Kaijian Sang, Jason C. White, Jorge L. Gardea-Torresdey, Jiang Xu, Jianying Zhang, Kun Yang, Lizhong Zhu, Daohui Lin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceSurface engineering of nanomaterials is a promising strategy to achieve niche-targeting remediation technologies. However, the multifunctional modification of nanomaterials is still limited by the trial-and-error cost and the trade-off between desired nanoscale properties. We propose that biota are endowed with material modification abilities via biomolecular coronas, which can provide quick and tunable strategies to augment nano-enabled remediation. Under the antibiotic contamination, biomolecular coronas derived from highly pollutant-tolerant worms modified and enabled nZVI multi-functionality, including enhanced responsiveness, enrichment, and degradation. This biomolecular-enabled nanoremediation is precisely regulated by biochemical syntheses and targeted secretion of proteins and metabolites with specific functions. This work highlights a paradigm shift towards ecologically-mediated nanomaterial modification strategies that significantly augment the potential of nanoremediation technologies.

The boom of the chemical industry over the past century has led to a complex and evolving threat of emerging pollutants, which presents a significant challenge to the global community. Antibiotics, perhaps the greatest achievement toward improved human public health, have also become an emerging issue of great environmental concern. Developing countries are the largest producers and consumers of antibiotics (1, 2). In China, the annual use of antibiotics has increased to 210,000 tons, with 46.1% used in healthcare and livestock breeding (3). The low bioavailability and overuse of antibiotics results in significant release into the environment, triggering the dual risk of ecotoxicity and antibiotic resistance (4). Unlike many conventional pollutants, a number of antibiotics are halogenated molecules (HAs) that are not only structurally stable but also able to induce strong inhibitory effects on microorganisms, making them resistant to traditional wastewater treatment strategies that rely heavily on chlorine disinfection and biodegradation (5). Considering the complexity and variability of this global risk, there is an urgent need to develop safe and effective technologies for the efficient removal of antibiotics from environmental matrices.

Engineered nanomaterials offer significant potential for the alleviation of antibiotic pollution. Nano zero-valent iron (nZVI) has demonstrated efficacy for the removal of halogenated contaminants by adsorption, reduction, and oxidation (6, 7), potentially reducing their bioactivity, exposure burden, and overall risk (8). However, the performance of nZVI in practical scenarios, e.g., soil/groundwater remediation, is often inefficient (6, 9). To improve material stability, selectivity, and reactivity, several physicochemically based strategies for nZVI surface functionalization have been developed during the last three decades, including carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) coating and oxalate modification (10, 11). However, the traditional development of functional materials is a “trial-and-error” process that requires years of time and economic costs (12, 13). For example, the development of polyelectrolyte coatings to enhance the colloidal stability and mobility of nZVI during groundwater remediation has taken 10 to 20 years since the reductive dehalogenation ability of Fe0 was reported in the early 1990s (14, 15). Moreover, there is an inherent trade-off between the different desirable properties (10, 16), such as CMC coating-enabled stability and reactivity (17), making the realization of coordinated multifunctional modifications face significant technical challenges.

One strategy to overcome the property trade-offs and trial-and-error costs may involve leveraging interactions between nanomaterials and natural processes. For example, the biomolecular corona is a fundamental form of eco-mediated nanomaterial modification (17) which contains a variety of proteins and metabolites that could provide significant potential for designing multifunctional nanomaterials (18). Current studies have primarily focused on the formation of the biomolecular corona in vivo for medical applications or nanotoxicity assessments (19–21), while the ex vivo biomolecular corona mediated by indigenous biota, particularly under contaminated conditions, has received little attention. Recently, it has been noted that certain organisms, particularly pollution-tolerant species, possess unique capacity to enhance nanoscale remediation strategies (nanoremediation) through biochemical augmentation. For example, soil nematodes can enhance the reductive degradation of pentachlorophenol by nZVI through the secretion of cysteine and other reductive components (22). Plants such as rice (Oryza sativa L.) and alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.) can exude versatile metabolites such as tyrosine and phenazine to promote the redox capacity of iron-based nanomaterials for pentachlorophenol and polychlorinated biphenyls (23–25). However, current understanding of the interfacial interaction processes between these biomolecules and nanomaterials is still lacking (26); the dynamic composition of biomolecular corona under contaminated conditions, as well as its regulatory mechanisms on the structure and function of nanomaterials, remain to be elucidated.

The current study explored the synergistic remediation of antibiotics-contaminated water by a biomodified nZVI with a pollutant-tolerant worm (Tubifex tubifex)-derived biomolecular corona. We hypothesize that the biomolecular corona would endow nZVI with a much more versatile and tunable response, interfacial enrichment, and high degradation efficiency for antibiotics. Using florfenicol (FF) as a model HA, the mechanisms of synergistic remediation and interfacial interaction with a composited nZVI–worm system were determined. The composition of proteins and metabolites in the biomolecular corona was systematically analyzed using orthogonal multiomics techniques. The FF-responsive activation of nZVI was characterized at the gene transcription level and molecular simulations with associated validation experiments were conducted to clarify how key biological metabolites enhance the pollutant responsiveness, enrichment, and reduction of nZVI via surface modification. These findings provide an important understanding of the rational design of multifunctional nanomaterials for environmental applications.

Results and Discussion

nZVI–Worm Interactions Synergistically Removed HAs from Wastewater.

Globally, aquaculture production facilities are known to be prone to significant antibiotic contamination, presenting significant public and environmental health risks (27, 28). Our group has previously observed that sludge worms (Tubifex tubifex) are naturally distributed in the outlet ponds of aquaculture farms (SI Appendix, Fig. S1); therefore, wastewater from a fish farm was used to test the feasibility of nZVI–worm bionanocomposite for antibiotic remediation. Seventeen types of antibiotics were detected in the wastewater (SI Appendix, Table S1), with chloramphenicol, FF, and thiamphenicol being the major compounds (0.83 μmol/L in total, accounting for 92.4%). In a microcosm experiment, the antibiotic removal performance of 104 per liter worms, 1 g/L four nZVIs including 50 nm (nZVI50), 100 nm (nZVI100), a microscale ZVI (mZVI, 7.7 μm), and CMC-coated nZVI20 (CMC-nZVI), along with their combinations, were determined (Fig. 1A). Only chloramphenicol, FF, and thiamphenicol were detected at 7 d. Without worms, the HAs concentration was 0.67 to 0.75 μmol/L after a 7-d treatment of different nZVIs, with the removal rates ranging from 10 to 19%. In addition, HAs removal rates with worms alone was 13%, showing no significant difference compared to the control value (10%). Importantly, the co-occurrence of nZVIs and worms significantly reduced the concentration of HAs by 19 to 58%. Specifically, nZVI100 significantly removed 58% (P < 0.01) of HAs, which is likely due to the integrated advantages of lower hydrodynamic diameters (about 895 nm) compared to the nZVI50 alone (about 1,190 nm), the higher specific surface area (6.2 m2/g) than that of mZVI (5.6 m2/g) (SI Appendix, Text S1), and the blocking effect of CMC coating on HAs degradation (29).

To further optimize the ratio of nZVI–worm composites for the synergistic remediation of HAs, 3 μmol/L FF (approximately 1 mg/L) was used, and a 4 × 4 factorial experiment was designed with additional doses of nZVI100 and worms in microplate-based experimental design. The result showed that 0.3 to 3 g/L nZVI100 and 104 to 105 per liter worms effectively and synergistically removed FF (P < 0.01) (Fig. 1B and SI Appendix, Table S2), with the optimum ratio being 1 g/L nZVI100 and 104 worms per liter, achieving 86% FF removal in 7 d. The potential contribution of symbiotic bacteria was excluded by measuring the coremediation potentials of nZVI100 with the mixed symbiotic bacterial components and the dominant species (Flavobacterium sp. and Aeromonas sp.) identified in the metagenomics (SI Appendix, Texts S2 and S3 and Figs. S2–S4). It was also evident that nZVI100 particles were initially adsorbed by the worm epidermis within 1 d, forming “nZVI-augmented worms” and then were gradually depassivated along with the production of yellow-brown iron oxides during the 7-d exposure (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Previous studies have shown that invertebrate epidermal secretions are rich in proteins and that amino acids such as glycine and alanine can chelate with iron, thus adhering to iron-containing particles (22, 30) and leading to the adsorption of nZVI on the worm epidermis. These findings suggest that nZVI can be coupled with worms to create composites that resist passivation and exhibit significant potential for the remediation of HAs-contaminated water (Fig. 1C).

The Nano–Bio Interface as a Focal Point for HAs Enrichment and Reduction.

The relationship between nZVI–worm interfacial interactions and FF degradation was further investigated through a 7-d microplate experiment. Notably, the worms responded specifically to FF exposure in conjunction with nZVI100 activation, which was accompanied by the release of Fe3+ ions and colored the solution brownish-yellow (Fig. 2A). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectral analysis showed characteristic peaks such as C─H (2962 to 2850 cm−1), CO─NH (1690 to 1630 cm−1), and C─O (1,275 to 1,025 cm−1) on the surface of nZVI100 after 7 d (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S3), particularly in the nZVI100-FF cotreatment group, indicating biomolecule-mediated modification of nZVI100 (31). Scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) further confirmed that the nZVI100 particles near the worm epidermis (within 62.6 ± 16.4 μm) in the cotreatment group were highly oxidized after 7 d, while the more distant particles were not thoroughly oxidized (Fig. 2C). These findings demonstrate that worm-secreted biomolecules directly mediate the activation of nZVI100 under FF exposure.

The levels of FF and its degradation products in water, at the nano–bio interface, and in the organisms were determined (Fig. 2 D–F). Due to the blocking effect of the surface passivation layer, 1 g/L nZVI100 alone was unable to effectively adsorb or degrade FF within 7 d (Fig. 2D), which is consistent with the findings of Xu et al. (12). Treatment with 104 per liter worms resulted in the slow degradation of FF into florfenicolamide (FFA), with the in vivo content of FFA being only 0.32 μmol/kg and nondetectable levels in solution at 7 d (Fig. 2F). Importantly, worm secretions at the nano–bio interface were extracted using ammonia and ethyl acetate (14), and a large quantity of FF dechlorination products, including deschloro flofenicol (FF-Cl) and dideschloro flofenicol (FF-2Cl), was detected in the secretions during 1 to 7 d (Fig. 2E). The total concentration of dechlorination products reached 2.60 μmol/kg after 7 d, which was 1.3 times (P < 0.05) and 4.16 times (P < 0.01) higher than those in the water and in the worms, respectively; this demonstrates that the nano–bio interface is a focal point for FF enrichment and reduction (Fig. 2G). Somewhat analogous to the worms, plants such as Oryza sativa can transfer nZVI into iron plaques via the rhizosphere secretion and use the iron plaques as barriers for the immobilization-degradation of pentachlorophenol in soil (23). To identify and understand the mode of action of key biomolecules at the worm epidermis that mediate pollutant enrichment and nZVI activation, the components of the biomolecular corona were investigated using multiomics techniques.

Structural and Functional Proteins Mediate Biomolecular Corona Formation.

Proteins are known to be the most important components of the biomolecular corona, creating a microenvironment that subsequently alters material uptake, biocompatibility, and nanotoxicity (17). Using proteomic analysis, 52 proteins were identified from the worm secretions (Fig. 3A), of which 42 were annotated (SI Appendix, Table S3). It was found that both structural proteins including tubulin, actin, and MYOsin and functional proteins including ATP synthase, propionyl-CoA carboxylase (Pccb), and thioredoxin were present in the secretion on the nZVI100 surface. By comparison, proteins related to DNA binding, ATP energy binding mechanisms, and metabolic processes were identified from Daphnia magna-generated eco-corona on silver, titanium, and polystyrene nanomaterials (20, 21). These findings indicate that biomolecular coronas acquired from different species during nanomaterial exposure have a certain level of biological conservatism.

Considering the conservativeness of proteins identified through proteomics and the accessibility of related antibodies, the distribution of tubulin (green), actin (red), Pccb (blue), and thioredoxin (pink) as representative structural and functional proteins was further investigated using immunofluorescence. These structural and functional proteins were located in the outer and inner parts of the worm epidermis, respectively (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, exposure to nZVI100 induced the secretion of structural proteins (tubulin and actin) that formed the biomolecular coronas, whereas functional proteins (Pccb and thioredoxin) were released under the nZVI-FF coexposure (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Western blot results (Fig. 3 C and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S7) also confirm that nZVI exposure increased tubulin and actin detection by more than 2.0-fold (P < 0.01) and that the nZVI-FF coexposure significantly increased both structural and functional protein content by 2.2 to 2.8-fold. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) observations demonstrate that the 7-d nZVI-FF coexposure induced functional protein accumulation in precursor vesicle structures in vivo (SI Appendix, Fig. S8), which were secreted as tubulin-coated vesicles that subsequently interacted with nZVI agglomerates on the epidermis (Fig. 3E). Tubulin is a heterodimeric protein that self-assembles into microtubules and is deeply involved in vesicle transport (32, 33). Therefore, it appears that FF induced the production of functional proteins and that were then assembled into vesicles (Fig. 3F), likely explaining the responsiveness of the nZVI–worm composite to FF contamination. Although a protein itself may be relatively devoid of chemical reactivity, it may catalyze reactions that lead to metabolites that could then induce nZVI modification. Therefore, we further analyzed the biomolecular corona by metabolomics.

Organic Acids and Amines are Major Metabolites in the Corona.

Metabolomics revealed that the major metabolites in the worm secretion were amino acids, carboxylic acids, saccharides, alcohols, and amines (Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Table S4), of which over 50% were organic acids (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). These components were similar to the predicted metabolites in the biomolecular coronas in human bodily fluids and Daphnia magna (34, 35). The most prominent components were succinic acid, lactic acid, urea, and alanine; importantly, the nZVI100-FF cotreatment increased organic acid and urea in the secretion (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). Interestingly, these components are similar to the metabolic wastes found in sweat that play an important role in the protection of stratum corneum (36).

Analysis of the enriched pathways reveals that these metabolites were mainly involved in the glucose-alanine cycle, amino acid (alanine, etc.) metabolism and degradation, and the urea cycle (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Fig. S11). To explore the pattern of metabolite synthesis and secretion, the most abundant components, including succinic acid, lactic acid, urea, and L-alanine, were further investigated in the worm body and in the skin secretion (Fig. 4 C–F). In vivo evidence shows that the lactate content increased from 245 µmol/g protein to 312 (P < 0.05), 485 (P < 0.01), and 399 µmol/g (P < 0.01) protein after the exposure to nZVI100, FF, and their combination, respectively; in addition, the urea content was increased from 131 µmol/g protein to 306 µmol/g protein after the nZVI-FF coexposure. This indicates that in vivo synthesis of lactate and urea can be induced by the nZVI-FF coexposure. While the biomolecular corona analysis shows that the FF exposure significantly increased the secretion of succinic acid (4.4 folds, P < 0.01) and lactic acid (3.4 folds, P < 0.01), the secretion of urea (2.2 folds, P < 0.05) and L-alanine (1.9 folds, P < 0.05) was induced by nZVI100, and importantly, all these biomolecules were highest (1.7 to 9.0 folds) under nZVI-FF coexposure. The above findings suggest that the synthesis and secretion of metabolites are is somewhat dependent on exposure to nZVI100 and FF. Specifically, the biochemical regulation of organic acid secretion was FF-dependent manner, which could be associated with nZVI100 activation under antibiotic exposure.

As skin secretion of animals is primarily controlled by acetylcholine/adrenergic receptor-mediated neuromodulation (36–38), we compared the expression patterns of the relevant genes in the worms in response to nZVI100, FF, and their coexposure (Fig. 4G and SI Appendix, Table S5). No homologous genes for adrenergic receptors were detected in the worms, although achr, gsk, and cmd exhibited a significant FF-dependent upregulation (2.9 to 4.5 folds, P < 0.01), with greatest expression under nZVI-FF coexposure (4.1 to 7.6 folds, P < 0.01). This suggests that FF exposure activated neuroexcitation and signaling, and thereby, mediated metabolite secretion through the acetylcholine receptor (32). Some glycolysis-related genes such as pdh and enol showed FF-dependent upregulation (1.5 to 2.8 folds, P < 0.05), while most genes involved in transamidation (atr), the TCA cycle (ldh, frd), and vesicle assembly (bactin, tbb) showed nZVI-dependent upregulation (4.1 to 7.6 folds, P < 0.01), suggesting that the responsiveness of nZVI–worm composite to FF contamination is highly dependent on precise regulation of metabolic synthesis and secretion (Fig. 4H).

Distinct Roles of Metabolites in nZVI Multifunctionalization.

An independent gradient model based on Hirshfeld partition (IGMH) analysis of metabolite-FF interactions was applied to understand how metabolites in the biomolecular corona promote FF interfacial enrichment and nZVI activation (Fig. 5A and SI Appendix, Fig. S12). The oxygen atoms on lactic acid and urea were found to bind the hydrogen atoms in FF via strong electrostatic interactions (Fig. 5A), suggesting the presence of multiple hydrogen bonds.

The ITC binding curves of (B) lactic acid and (C) urea onto FF; the red area shows the 95% CI. (D) Deformation charge density of succinic/lactic acid-nZVI calculated by DFT calculations. (E) FF residual rates within 24 h after the treatments with 1 g/L nZVI100 and 10−5 mol/L lactic acid, succinic acid, urea, L-alanine, and HCl; the pie chart shows electron transport efficiencies of 1 g/L nZVI100 after the treatments with 10−5 mol/L HCl, lactic acid, and succinic acid at 24 h. Error bars: ±SE, n = 3.

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) binding curves show that although the reactions of lactic acid-FF and urea-FF were exothermic and endothermic (as indicated by the ΔH), respectively, the ΔG values of lactic acid-FF and urea-FF were −24.35 and −8.10 kJ/mol, respectively, indicating spontaneous chemical association (Fig. 4 B and C) (39). Strong interactions, such as hydrogen bonds between two molecules, are known to modify the solubilities of their complexes (31, 40). Further analysis confirmed that with the increase of lactic acid and urea concentrations (SI Appendix, Fig. S13 A and B), the solubility of FF was increased by 7.1 and 7.5 times (P < 0.01), respectively, which explains the FF enrichment at the nonhomogeneous interface.

It was initially considered that the ionized H+ from succinic/lactic acids corroded the passivation layer and activated nZVI. However, molecular simulations suggest that succinate and lactate also played an important role in nZVI modification. Density functional theory (DFT) calculation results show that although the interaction between FF and nZVI was weak (SI Appendix, Fig. S14), succinate and lactate tended to conjugate with the hydroxyl groups on the surface of the passivated nZVI100 through carboxyl groups, with the adsorption energy (Eads) being −1.85 eV and −0.94 eV, respectively (Fig. 5D). Such conjugation on the material surface may lead to a higher density of electron cloud distribution than that of the passivated nZVI, which could then promote electron transfer from the internal Fe0 to FF (11). In a 24-h simulation test, the adsorption of FF by nZVI was negligible, while succinic/lactic acids exhibited more significant enhancement of nZVI-based FF degradation than HCl at the same molar concentration (Fig. 5E). Specifically, although the activation of nZVI100 by HCl was faster than that of succinic/lactic acids (6 h and 12 h, respectively), the FF residual rates in the presence of these organic acids were lower than that with HCl at 24 h. More importantly, the electron efficiencies of nZVI100 with succinic and lactic acid were as high as 39.4% and 55.6%, which are 6.5- and 9.1-fold of that with HCl (P < 0.01), respectively. This result indicates that slow-activation and conjugation induced by weak organic acids can benefit nZVI reaction with target pollutants, which is consistent with recent studies using oxalate and phosphate to modify nZVI surfaces (41, 42). Notably, more than two decades have passed since the first report of Fe0-based pollutant reduction with oxygenated acid-modified nZVI (14, 42), while such eco-mediated nZVI modification is largely unreported. Furthermore, the functional molecules in the biomolecular corona are biologically compatible metabolites (43), and their synthesis and secretion mechanisms are highly conserved among various invertebrate and vertebrate species (44). Therefore, similar modification strategies may be possible between widely distributed indigenous organisms and nanomaterials; this should become a topic of active investigation. Importantly, this unique approach of exploiting the biomolecular corona as a tool to enhance nano-enabled technologies offers a highly tunable platform for contaminant remediation in complex environmental matrices, which will greatly shorten the time required for nanotechnology development, lower the R&D costs, and facilitate the advancement of materials science.

Materials and Methods

Materials.

nZVIs, antibiotics, and other reagents were purchased from commercial sources as described in SI Appendix, Text S1. nZVIs, including nZVI50, nZVI100, mZVI, and CMC-nZVI were characterized in our previous study (20, 21). In a microcosm experiment, wastewater from an aquaculture farm in Huzhou, China, was collected for investigating the synergistic remediation performance of nZVI–worm composites. The basic water quality information is provided in SI Appendix, Text S4. Microplate experiments were further designed, and florfenicol (FF) was used as a targeted antibiotic due to its wide applications in livestock and aquaculture. The reported environmental concentrations of FF range from 120 ng/L to 10 mg/L (45); thus, 3 μmol/L (approximately 1 mg/L) was set as the target concentration. The nZVI concentration was set as 1 g/L in the microcosm experiment and at 0.3, 1, and 3 g/L in the microplate experiment according to previous studies (13, 16). Healthy adult worms (Tubifex tubifex) were purchased from an online aquarium market and were kept for a 14-d adaptation period in preparation for experiments.

Experimental Design.

A microcosm experiment focusing on the synergistic removal of antibiotics from wastewater was conducted in a 1-L tank with circulating water system (20 ± 1 °C in the dark). Based on the abundance of Tubifex tubifex in natural sediments, a density of 104 per liter was placed into each tank, with three replicates per treatment. The antibiotic removal performance of 1 g/L nZVI50, nZVI100, mZVI, and CMC-nZVI with or without worms was determined after a 7-d exposure period.

The microplate experiment was conducted in the 6-well plates based on 4 × 4 factorial experiment design according to our previous study with modifications (22). Worms at a density of 0, 104, 3 × 104, and 105 per liter were placed into each well containing 0, 0.3, 1, and 3 g/L nZVI100 along with 3 μmol/L FF. After a 7-d exposure (20±1 °C in the dark, pH = 7.0), water samples were collected and filtered and the FF content was determined. Since the optimized ratio obtained from this initial experiment was 1 g/L nZVI100 and 104 per liter worms (see Results), a second microplate experiment was conducted under similar conditions with individual 1 g/L nZVI100, 104 per liter worms and their combination. There was no worm mortality during the exposure. Water, skin secretion, and worms were separately collected after 7 d for determination of FF and its degradation products, as well as analysis of multiomics, key metabolites, and the gene expression.

Chemical Analyses.

Chemical analyses of antibiotics in wastewater, water, worm secretions, and the worm body were conducted according to previous methods (46–48). In the microcosm experiment, wastewater samples were filtered through 0.22-μm hydrophilic polyethersulfone membranes and were five-fold diluted for the determination of 38 commonly used antibiotics in aquaculture (49), including 18 quinolones, 9 sulfonamides, 5 macrolides, 3 chloramphenicols, and 3 other antibiotics. According to the chemical characteristics of these antibiotics, solid phase extraction (quinolones, sulfonamides, and macrolides) and liquid–liquid extraction (chloramphenicols and other antibiotics) were conducted, followed by cleanup and high-performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS) analysis. All antibiotics were analyzed in the positive mode, except for chloramphenicols, which were analyzed in the negative mode. Detailed protocols are provided in SI Appendix, Text S5.

In the microplate experiment, FF and degradation products [deschloro florfenicol (FF-Cl), dideschloro florfenicol (FF-2Cl), and florfenicolamide (FFA)] in water were determined by HPLC-MS/MS after filtration. To determine FF and degradation products in the worm secretions (representing the concentration at the nano–bio interface) and worm body (representing the concentration in vivo) after different treatments, the secretion along with nZVI particles were carefully separated from the worm skin according to previous methods (19, 20, 50). The secretion samples and the worms were separately homogenized, centrifuged, and extracted with ammonia and ethyl acetate, and then were analyzed using HPLC-MS/MS. FFA were analyzed with the positive mode, while FF, FF-Cl, and FF-2Cl were analyzed with the negative mode. Detailed protocols are provided in SI Appendix, Text S5.

Multiomics and Physiochemical Analyses.

After the 7-d exposure, secretion samples in each group were collected from the worm skin as described above, and nZVI particles were magnetically removed from the mixed samples. Samples were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) containing a protease inhibitor at 4 °C, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was aliquoted and protein concentration was determined using a BCA kit (Beyotime, China). Proteomic analysis was conducted by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The detailed procedure of protein extraction, digestion, quantification, and identification are provided in SI Appendix, Text S6.

Western blot analysis was performed to determine the levels of several proteins, including tubulin, actin, Pccb, and thioredoxin, in the biomolecular corona; three replicates were used in each treatment group. After extraction, 30 μg total proteins of each sample were separated by electrophoresis in 10% SDS-PAGE gels. After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes at 200 mA for 2 h at 4 °C. The membranes were then blocked with TBST (20 mM Tris, 500 mM NaCl, and 0.05% Tween 20) containing 5% skim milk power for 2 h at room temperature, then incubated with the corresponding antibodies of tubulin, actin, Pccb, and thioredoxin (CL488-66240, CL594-60008, 11139-1-AP, and 14999-1-AP, respectively; 1:1000 dilution; Proteintech Group Inc., China) overnight at 4 °C. Anti-GAPDH antibody (60004-1-Ig, 1:1000 dilution) was used as the internal reference. After washing, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Blots were washed in TBS:Tween twice for 10 min, incubated in commercial enhanced chemiluminescence reagents, and exposed to photographic film. Band density was calculated by AlphaEaseTM FC 32-bit software.

To analyze the key metabolites in the worm secretions, gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS)–based nontargeted metabolomics were employed (51). Briefly, the secretion was carefully removed from the worm skin, and nZVI particles were magnetically removed; 100 worms from each treatment were used to obtain one sample and 3 replicates were conducted. The detailed procedures of metabolite extraction, derivatization, and analysis are provided in SI Appendix, Text S7. After succinic acid, lactic acid, urea, and L-alanine were detected as the main components, their contents in the worm body were measured using commercial kits (ab204718, ab65331, ab83362, ab83394, respectively; Abcam). Detailed protocols and detection mechanisms are provided in SI Appendix, Text S8.

Gene transcription analyses were conducted after total RNA extraction, cDNA reverse transcription, and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) (SI Appendix, Text S9). The degenerate primers of 10 genes (achr, acetylcholine receptor; gsk, glycogen synthase kinase; cmd, calmodulin; pdh, pyruvate dehydrogenase; enol, enolase; atr, aminotransferase; ldh, lactate dehydrogenase; frd, fumarate reductase; bactin, β-actin; tbb, tubulin beta) related to neuromodulation, glycolysis, and vesicle secretion were designed using PRIMER 5.0 software based on the common sequences of similar species (Eisenia fetida, Lumbricus rubellus, Caenorhabditis elegans, etc.) from the NCBI database (SI Appendix, Table S6). The relative abundance was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method (52).

Characterization of Biomolecular Corona-Modified nZVI.

For the characterization of original nZVI100, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis showed that it contained 71.1% Fe0 along with 28.9% Fe3O4 (SI Appendix, Text S1 and Fig. S15A); X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (SI Appendix, Text S1 and Fig. S15B) showed that the material surface consisted primarily of 83.21% Fe(III) and 14.6% Fe(II), with only 2.18% of unoxidized Fe(0). After 7 d of exposure, FTIR was used to investigate the functional groups of molecules on the nZVI surface. nZVI100 particles in the secretion from two treatments (nZVI, nZVI-FF) were recovered using a magnet and then freeze-dried. The original nZVI100 was used as the control, and the functional groups of FF were also measured (SI Appendix, Fig. S16). The samples were weighed according to the ratio of sample: potassium bromide as 0.5 mg:100 mg, ground and pressed, and then measured by FTIR (Nicolet 6700, Thermo Fisher Scientific) (53). To determine the distribution and oxidation state of nZVI particles at the nano–bio interface, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Hitachi S-3400N) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDSP) (Bruker XFlash 5010) analyses were performed. Worms in the nZVI-FF coexposure group at the 7th d were air-dried on a glass slide for SEM observation in the backscattered electron mode, and the Fe, O, and C elements of the sample were measured by EDS (22). In addition, the distribution of tubulin, actin, Pccb, and thioredoxin as representatives of structural and functional proteins in the biomolecular corona were investigated using antibody-based immunofluorescence. Detailed protocols are provided in SI Appendix, Text S10.

Molecular Interaction Calculation.

Calculations were performed with density functional theory (DFT) implemented in a Gaussian 09 program package. The ground state equilibrium geometries of all compounds were computed using DFT with the B3LYP functional at 6-31G(d) level under long-range dispersion correction (em = gd3bj). Electrostatic potential distribution on the molecular surface was calculated, and the independent gradient model based on Hirshfeld partition (IGMH) method was used to visualize weak interactions by Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD) software 1.9.3 (54). The CASTEP module in Materials Studio Software 2020 was also used to analyze the interfacial interaction between passivated nZVI and metabolites (55). Detailed protocols are provided in SI Appendix, Text S11.

Simulation Experiments for Synergistic Degradation.

The potential promoting effects of lactic acid or urea on FF solubility were investigated. Specifically, FF-saturated solutions were prepared at 60 °C. Lactic acid and urea with different concentrations (0, 1, 2, 4, 6, and 8 mol/L) were prepared in the FF saturated solutions, and the mixtures were placed in a thermostat (120 r/min, 20 °C) for 2 d. Ten mL of the solution was then filtered through a 0.22-μm microfiber filter and analyzed by HPLC-MS/MS as described in the Chemical Analyses section.

ITC was used to confirm the interactions between lactic acid and FF and between urea and FF by measuring the absorption or release of heat (56). In detail, titration experiments were conducted by injecting 2 µL lactic acid (20 mmol/L) or urea (8 mol/L) into 450 µL FF (2 mmol/L) per 300 s for 24 instances at 25 °C, using deionized water as a blank titration. The thermodynamic parameters, including enthalpy (ΔH), entropy (ΔS), and binding free energy (ΔG) were determined by NANOITC (automated version, Malvern analytical) and Launch Nano Analyze software.

To investigate the role of metabolites in nZVI-mediated FF degradation, a series of 1 mmol/L (based on our detected interfacial concentrations) lactic acid, succinic acid, urea, and L-alanine were combined with 1 g/L nZVI100 in a 3 mmol/L FF solution under anaerobic conditions. After shaking for 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24 h in the dark (150 rpm, 25 °C), the obtained suspensions were filtered through 0.22-μm hydrophilic polyethersulfone membrane and diluted for the quantification of FF and its degradation products using HPLC-MS/MS. Specifically, at the 24-h time point, FF, FF-Cl, FF-2Cl along with H2 were determined to calculate the electron transport efficiencies as described in Hu et al. (13) and SI Appendix, Text S12.

Statistical Analysis.

Statistical analysis of experimental data was performed using the SPSS 20.0 statistical software. Normality and homoscedasticity were assessed by one-way analyses of vauriance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s HSD post hoc test. Significance levels were reported at *P < 0.05 and **P < 0.01. Metabolic pathway enrichment was conducted using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 tool (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/). Sankey diagrams, circle networks, and heatmaps were visualized by an online platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn). The symbols and error bars in the graphs represent the averages and SE, respectively.
