Trends and inequalities in statin use for the primary and secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease between 2009 and 2021 in England: a series of cross-sectional and cohort studies
Authors: Rutendo Muzambi, Krishnan Bhaskaran, Helen Strongman, Tjeerd van Staa, Liam Smeeth, Anne Marie Suffel, Emily Herrett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPrevious studies suggest that inequalities in statin prescribing exist for the primary and secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease (CVD), but it is unclear where along the pathway, from recognition of risk through to prescribing and continuation, these inequalities manifest. We aimed to investigate trends and inequalities in statin use for primary and secondary prevention of CVD.MethodsWe conducted a historical cohort study of 5 million adults aged 25 years or older, randomly sampled from the Clinical Practice Research Datalink linked to Hospital Episode Statistics in England between 2009 and 2021. We calculated the monthly proportion of current statin users. Using logistic regression and Cox modelling, we calculated adjusted ORs (aORs) for statin initiation and adjusted HRs (aHRs) for CVD, statin discontinuation and reinitiation.ResultsIn our primary prevention prevalence cohort (n=4 597 500), the overall monthly proportion of individuals prescribed statins increased from 22.3% in 2009 to 35.6% in 2021 among those aged 70+ years and was stable in other age groups. In our secondary prevention prevalence cohort (n=406 746), the monthly proportion of statin users was higher in all age groups (eg, increasing from 68.1% to 73.7% over the same period in those aged 70+ years). Between 2009 and 2021, women (aOR 0.70, 95% CI 0.68 to 0.72) and people of Black ethnicity (aOR 0.71, 95% CI 0.65 to 0.77) were less likely to be initiated on statins compared with those of White ethnicity for secondary prevention. Statin discontinuation was most likely among women (aHR 1.08, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.11), Black people (aHR 1.76, 95% CI 1.65 to 1.89) and the most deprived group (aHR 1.08, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.12) compared with men, White people and the least deprived group, respectively, for secondary prevention, with similar associations seen for primary prevention for ethnicity and deprivation.ConclusionsStatin use remains suboptimal, and inequalities, particularly among women, people of Black ethnicity and those in the most deprived socioeconomic groups, persist across multiple stages of statin use for both primary and secondary prevention. Strategies are needed specifically targeting these patient groups to reduce these inequalities.Keywords: Epidemiology, Pharmacoepidemiology, Cardiovascular Diseases, Public Health, Preventive Medicine

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, accounting for 32% of all global deaths in 2019 and a quarter of all deaths in the UK.1 2 Statins have been proven to be effective in reducing cardiovascular events and are widely prescribed, with atorvastatin the most commonly dispensed medication in England between 2022 and 2023.3,5

Despite the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidelines recommending the use of statins in those at risk of CVD and for all individuals with a prior CVD event, evidence suggests that statins are under-prescribed, with previous studies showing that only 30%–50% of adults at high risk of CVD in the UK were receiving statins.6,9 Coverage of CVD risk assessment is low, with limited evidence on recent trends and a lack of data on inequalities in its uptake.10

Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have highlighted that age, gender, ethnicity and socioeconomic deprivation are associated with poor statin adherence globally.11,14 However, fewer studies have explored where along the pathway, from identification of eligible patients, initiation among those at high risk, discontinuation and reinitiation of statins, these inequalities manifest, particularly in recent years, including after the 2014 NICE guideline recommendation to offer statins to all individuals with a 10% or higher 10-year risk of CVD for both primary and secondary prevention and across different CVD phenotypes.15,18 While patterns in statin prescribing before and during the COVID-19 pandemic have been explored in regions, including Europe,19 20 the USA,21 Canada22 and Australia,23 there remains a lack of studies investigating statin use in England over the same period.

To better understand and address health inequalities, it is important to understand where along the pathway of statin use inequalities manifest as missed opportunities for treatment, which are associated with worsening of clinical outcomes, mortality and higher healthcare costs.24,26 Therefore, we aimed to comprehensively examine trends and factors (age, gender, ethnicity and deprivation) associated with statin prevalence, cardiovascular risk scoring, initiation (among those with an above-threshold CVD risk score for primary prevention or an existing CVD event for secondary prevention), discontinuation and reinitiation of statins between 1 April 2009 and 31 December 2021.

Methods

Data and participants

We conducted several cross-sectional and historical cohort studies using data from the Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD Aurum) linked to Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) Admitted Patient Care.27 28 The CPRD Aurum database consists of pseudonymous electronic health records from UK primary care practices using the Egton Medical Information Systems (EMIS) general practice (GP) electronic health record software. The CPRD Aurum January 2022 build was used to extract the cohort for this study. CPRD Aurum contained over 40 million individuals and 1489 GPs.29 The database includes information on patient and practice demographics, diagnoses and symptoms, medication prescriptions and referrals. Our study followed the Reporting of Studies Conducted Using Observational Routinely Collected Data reporting guidelines.30

Our overall study population included 16 650 277 individuals aged 25 years or older between 1 April 2009 and 31 December 2021, eligible for HES linkage and with at least 12 months of research-standard follow-up in CPRD Aurum (online supplemental figure 1). From this population, we extracted data from a random sample of 5 million individuals, and details of the random sample can be found in online supplemental table 1. Our study period was from April 2009, as this corresponds to when National Health Service (NHS) Health Checks, which assess CVD risk and can inform statin prescribing, were first introduced.31 Start of follow-up for the overall cohort was defined as the latest of 1 April 2009, the 25th birthday and 12 months after current registration in the CPRD practice. The end of follow-up was the earliest of 31 December 2021, the death date or the end of follow-up in CPRD.

We used Systematised Nomenclature of Medicine Clinical Terms (SNOMED) codes in CPRD and International Statistical Classification of Diseases 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes in HES to identify CVD diagnoses (myocardial infarction, stroke, angina, peripheral arterial disease (PAD) and transient ischaemic attack) and covariates. Statin prescription information was obtained in CPRD using Dictionary of Medicines and Devices (dm+d) codes.

Study population and statistical analyses

The overall prevalence of statin use for primary and secondary prevention is the result of the identification of eligible individuals, initiation and continued use of statins. Our study seeks to determine where, and in whom, along this pathway there are inequalities in statin treatment and thus where opportunities for CVD prevention are being missed. Online supplemental figure 2 shows a conceptual pathway of statin use. We used data from certain subpopulation cohorts to estimate CVD risk scoring, prevalence, initiation, discontinuation and reinitiation of statins (table 1). Our overall study population included all individuals aged 25 years or older in accordance with NICE guidelines for primary and secondary prevention of CVD. Our analyses on factors associated with CVD risk scoring and statin initiation in primary prevention were based on the age criteria for NHS Health Checks, which are carried out among individuals aged 40–74 years.

CVD risk scoring

We described the characteristics of individuals formally risk-scored by their GP using all available CVD risk assessment tools (QRISK, QRISK2, Framingham and Joint British Society).32,35 Then, we described the monthly proportion of individuals with a recorded cardiovascular risk score in the last 5 years, between May 2009 and December 2021, and stratified by CVD risk tool, CVD risk category as assessed by the GP (<10%, 10%–19% and 20%), age, gender, ethnicity (White, South Asian, Black, mixed and other) and deprivation (using linked patient-level Townsend deprivation scores categorised into quintiles). The denominator included all individuals eligible for CVD risk assessment according to NICE guidelines (ie, individuals aged 25–84 years with no history of CVD, chronic kidney disease (CKD), type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) and familial hypercholesterolaemia) and individuals with no contraindications to statins prior to the start of follow-up. Individuals were excluded from a monthly denominator when they were diagnosed with a CVD event, became ineligible for CVD risk assessment (eg, CKD, T1DM or familial hypercholesterolaemia diagnosis) or were initiated on statins. The numerator included individuals with an up-to-date CVD risk record, defined as having a recorded risk score in the last 5 years (corresponding to the recommended frequency of CVD risk assessment).31 If individuals had multiple CVD risk scores on the same date, we kept the highest score.

To determine which individuals were eligible for CVD risk assessment, we included individuals aged 40–74 years (in accordance with the NHS Health Check age criteria), with no history of CVD, CKD, T1DM, familial hypercholesterolaemia and no contraindications to statins. Cox proportional hazards regression models, with the start of follow-up as the time scale, were used to estimate HRs for the association between demographic variables (age at start of follow-up, gender, ethnicity and deprivation) and receiving a CVD risk score from the GP. The start of follow-up was defined as the latest of 1 April 2009, the 40th birthday and 12 months after current registration in CPRD. Individuals were followed until the earliest of CVD risk assessment, the 75th birthday, CVD event or ineligibility for CVD risk assessment. We adjusted for demographic variables mentioned above, as these factors are likely to be fixed. We had insufficient time-updated information to adjust for time-dependent variables.

Statin prevalence

To describe the prevalence of statin use for primary CVD prevention, we included individuals without prior CVD (eg, past or current history of myocardial infarction, angina, revascularisation procedures, stroke, transient ischaemic attack or PAD). For secondary prevention, our cohort included individuals with existing CVD. We excluded individuals contraindicated to statins (using SNOMED codes for liver disease or codes explicitly stating statin contraindication) in both cohorts. An individual was defined as a current statin user if they had an ongoing statin prescription based on the number of days prescribed, allowing for a grace period of 28 days between prescriptions.

We described the proportion of individuals with a current statin prescription each calendar month for primary and secondary prevention. Individuals left both prevalence cohorts when they had a statin contraindication and left the primary prevention cohort when they were diagnosed with a CVD event.

Statin initiation

To assess initiation of statins for primary prevention, we included individuals aged 40–74 years who were eligible for the NHS Health Check and had a recorded CVD risk score above the threshold for statin initiation, with no history of CVD, CKD, T1DM or familial hypercholesterolaemia. We included the first recorded CVD risk score, within follow-up, that was eligible for statin initiation, where there was no previous statin prescription. Consistent with NICE guidelines, those with a 20% or higher 10-year risk of CVD before 2014 and a 10% or higher 10-year risk of CVD after 2014 were considered above the treatment threshold and eligible for statin initiation.36 Statin initiation was defined as having a first statin prescription within 28 days of a first recorded CVD risk score above the treatment threshold. We included only those with a risk score above the threshold to identify which groups of individuals are missing out on statins despite being eligible.

Our secondary prevention initiation cohort comprised individuals with a CVD event who remained alive and under follow-up within 60 days of a CVD event and with no statin contraindications. Statin initiation was defined as having a first statin prescription within 60 days of a CVD event. We chose a 60-day grace period between the first cardiovascular event and subsequent statin prescription to account for individuals who may be initiated on statins in secondary care and may wait until they have completed their prescription before obtaining a statin prescription in primary care.

We used logistic regression to examine the factors associated with initiating a statin for primary and secondary prevention within 28 days of becoming eligible. We adjusted for age at risk assessment (primary prevention) or age at CVD diagnosis (secondary prevention), gender, ethnicity, deprivation, Body Mass Index (BMI, kg/m2) (underweight, normal weight, overweight, obese or morbidly obese), smoking status (people who do not smoke, people who currently smoke and people who used to smoke), treated hypertension (defined as antihypertensives prescribed on the date of or after hypertension diagnosis), type II DM, atrial fibrillation and rheumatoid arthritis. We did not adjust for high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), total cholesterol and systolic and diastolic blood pressure due to moderate/high levels of missingness (online supplemental table 2) and CKD (in primary prevention analyses) as CVD risk assessment is not recommended in this population for primary prevention.

Statin discontinuation

We included all individuals aged 25 years or older who had initiated statins for primary prevention (all individuals without a pre-existing CVD event) and secondary prevention (all individuals with a first CVD event during the study period). After individuals reached the end of their prescription based on the date of prescription, dose and quantity prescribed, statin cessation was defined as reaching the end of a 90-day grace period without a new statin prescription, to allow time for those with overlapping prescriptions to use their excess supply.

We used Kaplan-Meier curves to examine the time from statin initiation to discontinuation. Cox proportional hazards regression models, with date of statin initiation as the time scale, were used to estimate HRs for the association between demographic variables (age at statin initiation, gender, ethnicity and deprivation) and statin discontinuation, adjusted for these demographic variables.

Statin reinitiation

Our reinitiation cohort included all individuals who had stopped taking statins (from our discontinuation analyses) based on the criteria mentioned above. For our primary prevention cohort, we additionally excluded individuals with a CVD diagnosis prior to statin cessation. Statin reinitiation was defined as receiving a subsequent statin prescription after a 90-day period without a statin prescription, starting from the time when the last prescription ran out (estimated based on the date of the previous prescription, dose and quantity of tablets prescribed).

We used Kaplan-Meier curves to examine the time from statin discontinuation to first subsequent statin prescription, and we used Cox proportional hazards regression models, with date of statin discontinuation as the time scale, to estimate HRs for the association of age, gender, ethnicity and deprivation with statin discontinuation, adjusting for these demographic variables.

Additional secondary and sensitivity analyses

We performed a range of secondary and sensitivity analyses. First, we stratified our initiation, discontinuation and reinitiation analyses by the pre-pandemic (before 1 March 2020) and pandemic periods (from 1 March 2020) to examine the effect of the COVID-19 pandemic. Second, we expanded our definition of statin initiation from a first statin prescription within 28 days after an above-threshold risk score to 90 days for primary prevention and from a statin prescription within 60 days after a first CVD event to 90 days for secondary prevention. The rationale for this was to minimise misclassification of statin initiators. Third, to minimise misclassification of statin discontinuation, we expanded our definition of cessation from a 90-day grace period to 180 days.

Model checking

We found evidence of non-proportionality when we tested the Cox proportional hazards assumption using the Schoenfeld residuals test and through visual inspection of Schoenfeld residual plots and log-log plots for our analyses on factors associated with receiving a CVD risk score, statin discontinuation and statin reinitiation. To deal with this, we performed sensitivity analyses in which we added interaction terms between non-proportional demographic factors and time.

We handled competing risks such as mortality using cause-specific Cox regression models, which are appropriate for handling aetiologically focused research questions such as those posed in our study.37 38 The cause-specific hazards approach is implemented by stopping follow-up at the occurrence of a competing event.

All analyses were conducted in R Studio, V.4.3.2. Numerical estimates of monthly proportions of statin users and individuals with CVD risk assessments, as well as the number of people at risk and events for the Kaplan-Meier plots, can be found on GitHub (https://github.com/RutendoMuzambi/trends_statin_use).

Patient and public involvement statement

No patients were involved in setting the research question, outcome measures, design or implementation of this study.

Results

In our random subsample of 5 million individuals aged 25 years or older from a total population of 16 650 277 individuals eligible for inclusion in the present study (online supplemental figures 3 and 4), 4 606 274 and 416 431 individuals were included in the primary and secondary prevention cohorts, respectively, specifically on statin use (table 2). We present demographic, lifestyle and comorbid characteristics of individuals included in four subcohorts for prevalence, initiation, discontinuation and reinitiation analyses in table 2 and online supplemental table 3. Overall, individuals included in the primary prevention cohorts were younger (median: 39.0 years (IQR 29.0 to 53.0) vs 70.0 years (IQR 59.0 to 79.0)), and there was a higher proportion of women (50.8% vs 43.1%) than in secondary prevention cohorts. The initiation primary prevention cohort only included individuals aged 40–74 years, while all other cohorts in table 2 included individuals aged 25 years or older. 303 292 and 122 743 people in total initiated statins during the study regardless of CVD risk score; of these individuals, 116 468 (38.4%) and 27 257 (22.2%) discontinued statins for primary and secondary prevention, respectively (online supplemental figures 3 and 4). Of these, 89 796 (77.1%) and 24 876 (91.3%) reinitiated statins for primary and secondary prevention, respectively. Baseline characteristics of individuals included in the CVD risk assessment subcohorts, monthly prevalence of individuals with a CVD risk assessment and factors associated with a CVD risk assessment are presented in online supplemental table 4.

Trends across time in statin prevalence

Between May 2009 and November 2021, there was a small increase in the monthly proportion of statin users, ranging from 8.5% to 10.7% and from 67.4% to 71.8% for primary and secondary prevention, respectively (figure 1). The increase in statin prevalence for primary prevention appears to be driven largely by the 70+ years age group, whose monthly proportion of statin users increased from 22.3% to 35.6% between May 2009 and November 2021. During this period, an increase in this age group from 68.1% to 73.7% was seen for secondary prevention. In both cohorts, the monthly proportion of statin users was lowest among women, 25–39 years age group, in those of Black, mixed and other ethnic groups and in the most deprived group (primary prevention only), but highest in those with a history of myocardial infarction (secondary prevention only).

Trends across time in cardiovascular risk scoring

The monthly proportion of individuals with a GP-recorded CVD risk assessment, regardless of eligibility for risk assessment, rose steadily from 13.7% in May 2009 to 35.3% in February 2020 and declined to 31.8% by November 2021 (online supplemental figure 5). The proportion of CVD risk assessments performed by GPs was highest in the 40–74 years age group and among those with less than 10% CVD risk score. Online supplemental figure 6 shows that CVD risk assessment was most likely among the 70–74 years age group compared with the 40–49 years age group (adjusted HR (aHR) 2.62, 95% CI 2.60 to 2.65) and among those of South Asian ethnicity (aHR 1.39, 95% CI 1.38 to 1.40), Black ethnicity (aHR 1.28, 95% CI 1.27 to 1.29) and mixed ethnicity (aHR 1.15, 95% CI 1.13 to 1.17) compared with White ethnicity. While there was no apparent trend across the four lowest quintiles of deprivation, the most deprived group was more likely to receive a CVD risk assessment than the least deprived group (aHR 1.23, 95% CI 1.22 to 1.24).

Initiation

11.5% (n=49 877) of individuals with a recorded risk score above the threshold initiated a statin within 28 days (primary prevention) while 79.0% (n=115 968) of individuals with a CVD diagnosis were initiated on statins within 60 days of the first CVD event (secondary prevention). Figure 2 shows that among individuals with an above-threshold recorded CVD risk score, statin initiation was least likely in the 70+ years age group than the 40–49 years age group (adjusted OR (aOR) 0.75, 95% CI 0.72 to 0.79), people of Black ethnicity than White ethnicity (aOR 0.82, 95% CI 0.77 to 0.88), but statin initiation was more likely in those in the most deprived group than in the least deprived group (aOR 1.16, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.20) and among women compared with men (aOR 1.06, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.08). In contrast, for secondary prevention, statin initiation within 60 days of a first CVD event was highest among the 60–69 years age group than the 25–39 years age group (aOR 3.19, 95% CI 3.02 to 3.38) and among people of South Asian ethnicity compared with those of White ethnicity (aOR 1.53, 95% CI 1.42 to 1.64). On the other hand, statin initiation was less likely among women compared with men (aOR 0.70, 95% CI 0.68 to 0.72) and in the Black group compared with the White group (aOR 0.71, 95% CI 0.65 to 0.77). We also found that the factors associated with initiation were similar across CVD subtypes (online supplemental figure 7).

Discontinuation

Among people who initiated statins for primary prevention, 26.3% and 37.2% had discontinued statins by 1 year and 5 years of initiation, respectively. For secondary prevention, 10.0% and 19.9% had discontinued statins by 1 year and 5 years of initiation, respectively. We found that statin discontinuation was least likely in the 70+ years age group compared with the 25–39 years age group (aHR 0.63, 95% CI 0.61 to 0.65) and slightly less likely among women compared with men (aHR 0.98, 95% CI 0.96 to 0.99) after adjusting for age, gender, ethnicity and deprivation in the primary prevention cohort (figure 3). Conversely, statin discontinuation was higher in the Black group than the White group (aHR 1.66, 95% CI 1.61 to 1.70) and in the most deprived group compared with the least deprived group (aHR 1.16, 95% CI 1.14 to 1.19). In line with primary prevention findings, statin discontinuation among those with a history of CVD was most likely among people of Black ethnicity compared with those of White ethnicity (aHR 1.76, 95% CI 1.65 to 1.89) and the most deprived group compared with the least deprived group (aHR 1.08, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.12). However, women were also more likely to discontinue than men (aHR 1.08, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.11). Findings were also consistent with those according to CVD subtypes (online supplemental figure 8). Reinitiation

For both primary and secondary prevention, older people, particularly those in the 70+ years age group compared with the 25–39 years age group, people of South Asian, Black and mixed ethnicity compared with White people and people in the most deprived groups compared with the least deprived groups were most likely to restart statins, with statin reinitiation highest in the 70+ years age group compared with the 25–39 years age group (aHR 1.39, 95% CI 1.25 to 1.55) for secondary prevention (online supplemental figure 11). Women were less likely to reinitiate statins compared with men, particularly for secondary prevention (aHR 0.87, 95% CI 0.85 to 0.90). Similar associations were seen for all CVD subtypes except PAD (online supplemental figure 12), and Kaplan-Meier curves depicting time from statin discontinuation to reinitiation are presented in online supplemental figures 13 and 14.

Additional secondary and sensitivity analyses

Secondary analyses stratifying our main analyses by pre-pandemic and pandemic periods (online supplemental figures 15–18) were broadly similar in the pre-pandemic and pandemic periods. Our sensitivity analyses were consistent with our main analyses, as we observed minimal changes to our effect estimates (online supplemental figures 19 and 20). Due to evidence of non-proportionality (Schoenfeld residuals test, p<0.05) for our Cox proportional hazards analyses on the association of demographic variables and risk of receiving a CVD risk assessment, statin discontinuation and statin initiation, we performed additional sensitivity analyses shown in online supplemental tables 5−8. We found that the risk of receiving a CVD risk assessment was higher in the first 5 years from the start of follow-up, while individuals were less likely to receive a CVD risk assessment more than 5 years after the start of follow-up. Statin discontinuation was less likely in the first 6 months of statin initiation in older age groups compared with more than 6 months, and statin discontinuation was more likely among ethnic minority groups in the first 6 months than after 6 months for primary prevention, with similar findings seen for secondary prevention. Analyses on reinitiation were consistent with our primary analysis.

Discussion

In our comprehensive population-based cohort study of 5 million adults in England, we report the following key findings: (1) between May 2009 and November 2021, we saw an increase in statin prevalence for primary and secondary prevention among the 70+ years age group, while CVD risk assessment rose steadily over time from 15% to 32%; (2) the monthly prevalence of statins was lowest among Black, mixed and other ethnicities and those in the most deprived socioeconomic quintile; however, these groups, including people of South Asian ethnicity, were more likely to receive a CVD risk assessment compared with the White ethnic group and least deprived groups, respectively; (3) statin initiation for secondary prevention was highest among people of South Asian ethnicity compared with those of White ethnicity but least likely among the Black ethnic group compared with the White ethnic group and among women compared with men and (4) Black people, the most deprived socioeconomic group and women (secondary prevention only) were most likely to discontinue statins compared with the White people, the least deprived group and men, respectively. These same groups, including people of South Asian ethnicity and the 70+ years age group, compared with the White and 25–39 years groups, respectively, were most likely to reinitiate statins for both primary and secondary prevention.

Comparison with other studies

Our prevalence estimates are in line with a UK primary care study, which observed a slower rate of increase in statin prescription rates from 2007 onwards.39 Globally, studies in the USA,40 Denmark41 and Australia42 have also seen an increase in the prevalence of statin use over time.

Cardiovascular risk scoring increased over time in our study, consistent with previous studies; however, we observed a decline in recorded risk scores during the pandemic period.7 43 The COVID-19 pandemic severely impacted primary care services, with a substantial reduction in primary care contacts for CVD after the introduction of population-wide restrictions.44 In agreement with our findings, between 2019–2020 and 2020–2021, the proportion of people invited for an NHS Health Check declined by 82%, with the proportion of people taking up an NHS Health Check invite also declining by 84%, leading to a backlog and suboptimal care.45,47 Prior to the pandemic, findings from a systematic review showed uptake of the NHS Health Check was higher among older age groups and females between 2009 and 2016.48 Similarly, we found that women and older age groups were more likely to receive a CVD risk assessment.

We found that statin initiation was less likely in older age groups compared with the 40–49 years age group in our primary prevention cohort among people with a recorded CVD risk score. This finding is in conflict with a primary care English study between 2004 and 2012, which found that statin initiation rate increased with age up to 70–74 years.49 Unlike our study, this study did not restrict the study population to those with a CVD risk score above the threshold for statin initiation. Conditioning on the statin eligibility threshold in our study may have resulted in other factors associated with statin initiation being more predictive of initiation than age, which may explain the differences in findings. Additionally, restricting our analyses to only those eligible for statin initiation limits the generalisability of our findings, as it is likely that those with below-threshold risk scores may be initiated on statins. Among people with CVD, our findings were consistent with studies which found that women and Black people were less likely to initiate lipid-lowering drugs than men and White people, respectively, while the South Asian ethnic group is more likely to initiate statins compared with the White group in the UK.50 51

We found that a high proportion of those who discontinued statins reinitiated, which is in agreement with a CPRD study that found that 72% and 75% of those who discontinued for primary and secondary prevention, respectively, also reinitiated statins.52 Reinitiation was lower in studies from Scotland,18 Finland,53 Canada15 and the USA.16 54 This lower reinitiation of statins could be due to differences in the definition of reinitiation, with some studies defining reinitiation after a 180-day treatment gap versus 90 days in our study, or reinitiation only assessed within a year of discontinuing statins, while our definition of reinitiation was not limited to a specific duration. Additionally, the study period of these studies ranged from 1997 to 2017.

Consistent with our findings, a study in the UK and the USA reported that women and Black, South Asian and other ethnic minority groups were more likely to discontinue statins compared with men and the White ethnic group, respectively, while women and younger age groups were less likely to restart statins in these studies.52 55 Reasons for statin discontinuation have been explored in previous studies and include fear of or experience of adverse events.56 57

Ethnic differences observed in our study may be a result of residual confounding, as we were unable to account for factors such as cholesterol levels due to a high level of missingness or understand the reasons for under-prescribing and discontinuation of statins. A previous UK study found that most patients initiated on statins had no previous CVD risk score. In the absence of a CVD risk score, cholesterol levels were found to be the main predictor of statin initiation.7 58 Prior studies have reported that people of Black ethnicity are associated with lower than expected rates of access to and use of NHS cardiovascular care.59 Evidence suggests that healthcare professionals experience considerable uncertainty in supporting ethnically diverse patients, which leads to hesitance and contributes to ethnic healthcare inequalities.60 Structural racism, discrimination and distrust have also been identified as key factors that negatively impact healthcare.61 Deprivation has also been associated with CVD, with people living in the most deprived areas four times more likely to die prematurely from CVD.62

Addressing these missed opportunities for statin treatment is important in cost-effectively improving patient outcomes. Previous studies have shown that optimising statin use is associated with reduced cardiovascular events, gains in quality-adjusted life years, improved life expectancy and increased healthcare cost savings to the NHS.63 64

Over the course of our study period, several key developments may have influenced statin use, including the 2011 patent expiration of atorvastatin leading to a greater affordability and availability of high-intensity statins, the intense media coverage over the safety of statins between October 2013 and March 2014, the 2014 NICE guidance on CVD prevention, the introduction of proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitors recommended by NICE in 2016, the NHS long-term plan published in 2019 which outlined the need for early detection and treatment of CVD and the COVID-19 pandemic.3644 65,67

Strengths and limitations

Our large cohort of 5 million individuals is the first to comprehensively assess inequalities across five stages of statin use in England for primary and secondary prevention, to the best of our knowledge. Our study extends existing literature by examining trends and inequalities throughout each stage of statin use across a long follow-up period, including before and during the COVID-19 pandemic. Other strengths of our study include the use of linked primary and secondary electronic records broadly representative of the UK population in terms of age, gender, ethnicity and geographical location and well-recorded GP prescription data.68

There were several limitations in our study. First, in our time-to-event analyses (cardiovascular risk assessment, discontinuation and reinitiation analyses), we did not adjust for time-dependent covariates such as BMI and smoking status due to a lack of time-updated information on these factors. Furthermore, data on cholesterol and blood pressure measurements had a high proportion of missing data; thus, these factors were not adjusted for in our initiation analyses. As a result, we cannot rule out residual confounding. Second, low-intensity statins can be purchased over the counter, and thus we were not able to capture information on statin use outside of primary care. Third, the reasons for statin discontinuation are not well captured in primary care records; thus, we were unable to explore this. Fourth, there are no data on whether individuals actually collected their statin prescription or took their medication; thus, misclassification of statin discontinuation is possible, as we cannot be certain of when they discontinued statins. Fifth, although we assessed changes in statin prescribing, we did not investigate whether there was a change in the incidence of cardiovascular events over time, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic, though this has recently been reported.44 We assessed this for primary prevention by observing changes in the CVD risk category over the 13-year study period. Sixth, although we explored changes in statin prescribing before and during the COVID-19 pandemic, we cannot assess the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic from our analyses, as this would require an interrupted time series analysis. Lastly, while our study did not focus on adherence, we explored inequities in statin adherence for primary prevention of CVD in England in our recent study.69

Conclusions

We observed inequalities by age, gender, ethnicity and deprivation across multiple stages of statin use, including statin prevalence, CVD risk scoring, initiation, discontinuation and reinitiation. Optimising statin use among those eligible could lead to reduced CVD events, improved life expectancy and substantial savings to the NHS. Further research is needed to understand the reasons for under-prescribing and discontinuation of statins, particularly among women, Black ethnic groups and the most deprived socioeconomic groups.

Our study suggests that there is an opportunity to optimise statin treatment through improving CVD risk assessment. Reductions in CVD risk assessment during the COVID-19 pandemic highlight the urgent need to improve uptake of routine CVD risk assessment to minimise missed opportunities for statin treatment, thereby reducing preventable CVD events and additional costs to the NHS. This also has implications for pandemic planning to ensure important health services can be maintained during periods of reduced in-person contact. Future qualitative studies are also needed to explore drivers of variation in statin adherence to understand which groups of patients are less likely to adhere to treatment and thus should be specifically targeted through education and prescribing strategies to reduce preventable CVD events in these groups.
