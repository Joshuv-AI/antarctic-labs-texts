Prognostic value of valve calcification and high cardiothoracic ratio for major adverse cardiovascular events and mortality in hemodialysis patients
Authors: Wan-Lun Hsu, Chen-Lien Hong, Yukiko Ono, Vy-Khanh Nguyen, Chung-Kuan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMaintenance hemodialysis (MHD) patients are at high risk of valve calcification (VC) and cardiomegaly, defined as cardiothoracic ratio (CTR) ≥0.5. CTR is a widely used indicator of volume overload and left ventricular dysfunction and predicts adverse cardiovascular outcomes. However, the combined prognostic impact of VC and high CTR on cardiovascular mortality remains unclear. We conducted a retrospective, single-center, exploratory study enrolling MHD patients from October to December, 2018. Patients were stratified into four groups based on VC and CTR (<0.5 or ≥0.5). Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models and the Kaplan-Meier method were used to evaluate risks and cumulative incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs), cardiovascular mortality, and overall mortality among groups. Of 312 MHD patients, 37 had no VC with CTR <0.5, 47 had no VC with CTR ≥0.5, 96 had VC with CTR <0.5, and 132 had VC with CTR ≥0.5. Compared to MHD patients without VC and CTR <0.5, those with VC and CTR ≥0.5 had higher risks of MACEs, cardiovascular mortality and overall mortality (adjusted hazard ratios: 4.174, 6.426, and 4.031; 95% confidence intervals: 1.766–9.868, 1.503–27.48, and 1.407–11.55, respectively). During 3-year follow-up, the cumulative incidence of aforementioned outcomes was highest among MHD patients with VC and CTR ≥0.5 (log-rank test, p < 0.05). The coexistence of VC and CTR ≥0.5 may identify a high-risk subgroup, suggesting potential utility for risk stratification. Targeted strategies to mitigate these risks may be beneficial in this population.

Introduction

Valve calcification (VC) is characterized by the gradual fibro-calcific remodeling and thickening of the valve leaflets [1]. The pathophysiology of VC is complex, influenced by traditional risk factors such as age, sex, smoking, and comorbidities like hypertension or diabetes mellitus. In patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), inflammation, malnutrition, and calcium-phosphorus metabolism disorders play significant roles in vascular or soft tissue calcification. The prevalence of VC among ESKD patients is approximately 31.7%, with risk factors including long dialysis vintages, elevated serum calcium level, high blood pressure, diabetes, and reduced serum albumin level [2]. Aortic VC leads to left ventricular outflow obstruction, increasing ventricular workload and oxygen consumption and ultimately causing myocardial hypertrophy and fibrosis, while mitral VC causes dilation of the left atrium, and is associated with atrial arrhythmias, pulmonary congestion, and heart failure [3]. Therefore, VC is associated with increased cardiovascular (CV) and overall mortality, especially when VC progresses. In ESKD patients on maintenance hemodialysis (MHD), a higher degree of VC and an increased number of cardiac VCs are also associated with higher overall and CV mortality [4].

The cardiothoracic ratio (CTR) is a noninvasive and easily obtainable parameter, which represents the overall cardiac silhouette on a chest posteroanterior (PA) radiograph. It is commonly associated with left ventricular size; therefore, an elevated CTR is considered to be associated with left ventricular mass and may correlate with left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) [5]. In addition, a CTR above the value of 0.5 is used to identify cardiomegaly. The prevalence of CTR ≥ 0.5 among ESKD patients undergoing dialysis is approximately 55% [6]. CTR is also a recognized prognostic factor in various conditions, including hemodialysis patients. A large prospective cohort study of nearly 3,500 individuals with a four-year follow-up revealed that a higher CTR is associated with an elevated risk of both CV and overall mortality in this population [7]. Patients were stratified based on a CTR cutoff of ≥ 0.5. This threshold is a widely accepted and long-standing radiographic criterion for identifying cardiomegaly on standard posteroanterior chest radiograph and has been consistently associated with adverse outcomes [8]. Furthermore, another study also indicated that ESKD patients with a CTR ≥ 0.57 have an even greater risk of CV and overall mortality [9].

VC impairs leaflet motion, obstructs blood flow, and leads to LVH, which may in turn elevate the CTR [10]. In patients undergoing MHD, LVH may contribute to or aggravate VC due to increased circulatory stress during hemodialysis, which in turn promotes greater endotoxemia, more severe inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction [11]. Compared to the general population, ESKD patients have a significantly higher prevalence of VC and cardiomegaly [12]. Furthermore, VC and LVH are known to exacerbate each other, with a longitudinal interaction that elevates the risk of mortality and CV events in ESKD patients [11]. Despite the well-established link between VC, LVH, and CV risk, limited research has explored the combined predictive value of VC and CTR for clinical outcomes in this population. Therefore, this exploratory study aimed to investigate the combined prognostic value of VC and CTR for major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs), CV and overall mortality in MHD patients.

Materials and methods

Study population

We conducted a retrospective study to evaluate the association of VC (viewed on transthoracic echocardiography) and CTR (viewed on chest PA radiography) with MACEs, CV and overall mortality in patients undergoing MHD. Initially, a total of 345 patients with ESKD on MHD in a medical center who underwent transthoracic echocardiography and chest PA radiography at the same time between October 1 and December 31, 2018 were enrolled. Of them, 33 patients with ESKD who received MHD via tunneled cuffed catheter were excluded. Finally, 312 ESKD patients with functional arteriovenous access for hemodialysis in a medical center with available transthoracic echocardiography and chest PA radiography at the same time during the study period were selected for analysis. The remaining 312 patients were categorized into four groups based on the absence or presence of VC and CTR < 0.5 or ≥ 0.5. The numbers of these patients were 37, 47, 96, and 132, respectively, in the group of no VC with CTR <0.5, no VC with CTR ≥0.5, VC with CTR < 0.5, VC with CTR ≥0.5, which were shown in (Figure 1).

Collection of demographic, medical, and laboratory data of study population

After identification of enrolled MHD patients, we collected their clinical features, including the patient’s age, sex, body weight, vintage, and presence of type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease (CAD), peripheral artery disease, heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and malignancies. Laboratory parameters included serum albumin, aspartate aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, total cholesterol, triglyceride, fasting blood glucose, hemoglobin, platelet count, serum ferritin, transferrin saturation, aluminum level, uric acid, sodium, potassium, ionized calcium, phosphorus, intact parathyroid hormone, and the adequacy of dialysis efficiency based on second generation Daugirdas formula (Kt/V urea) [13]. Medications included angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers (ACEIs/ARBs), beta-blockers, calcium antagonists, diuretics, statins, oral antidiabetic medications, insulin and analogs, antiplatelets, and anticoagulants.

Measurement of valve calcification and collection of echocardiographic parameters of study population

Experienced CV physicians without prior knowledge of any patient’s information performed two-dimensional echocardiography. Except for left ventricular mass and left ventricular mass index, which was calculated using the Cube formula [14], all echocardiographic data, including aortic root, interventricular septum, left atrial diameter, left ventricular end-diastolic diameter, left ventricular end-systolic diameter, left ventricular posterior wall, left ventricular relative wall thickness, inferior vena cava diameter and ejection fraction, were acquired according to the guidelines of the American Society of Echocardiography and recorded by experienced cardiologists blinded to the clinical details [15]. The presence of VC was defined as bright, refringent lesions greater than 1 mm in thickness observed on echocardiography, located on the mitral or aortic leaflets or annulus of the heart. The measurements performed by different cardiologists on the same patients or the repeated measurements by the same cardiologist on the same patients did not show significant differences. Therefore, both of the inter-observer variability and the intra-observer variability were considered acceptable.

Detection of cardio-thoracic ratio of study population

All of the included patients received chest PA radiography, and the CTR was calculated by an experienced radiologist who was blinded to the clinical information of the patients. The maximal horizontal diameter of the heart was divided by the horizontal inner width of the rib cage and then expressed as a ratio. CTR ≥ 0.5 typically indicates an enlarged heart.

Outcome definitions

The primary outcome was MACEs, while the secondary outcomes were CV and overall mortality. MACEs included any of the following events: hospitalization for myocardial infarction, coronary revascularization, stroke, heart failure, or death from CV causes. Although death from CV causes was included as a component of the composite MACE endpoint, CV mortality was also analyzed as a distinct secondary outcome. CV mortality was defined as death attributable to myocardial infarction, heart failure, cardiac arrhythmia, sudden cardiac death, aortic dissection, aortic aneurysm rupture, cardiac tamponade, pulmonary embolism, or ischemic or hemorrhagic cerebrovascular accident.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics and echocardiographic findings of the four groups of MHD patients are presented as percentages for categorical variables and as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed continuous variables or median (interquartile range [IQR]) for non-normally distributed continuous variables. The chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables, while one-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare continuous variables among four groups with normal and skewed distribution, respectively. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to calculate the cumulative risk of MACEs, CV, and overall mortality for the four groups during the follow-up period, and the risks were compared using the log-rank test.

Univariate Cox regression was used to estimate the crude hazard ratio (cHR) and the 95% confidence interval (CI) for MACEs, CV, and overall mortality. In the multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, variables including age, sex, weight, vintage, CAD, albumin, alkaline phosphatase, triglyceride, hemoglobin, and ACEIs/ARBs from Table 1, were adjusted to calculate the adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) for outcome events across the four groups. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals, and no significant violations were observed. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22 for Windows (IBM Corp.). A two-sided p < 0.05 indicated significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

ESKD patients on MHD with VC were significantly older than those without VC, regardless of CTR (p < 0.001). ESKD patients on MHD with CTR <0.5 had a significantly lower proportion of females (p = 0.016), higher dry weight (p < 0.001), and shorter vintages (p = 0.014) relative to those with CTR ≥ 0.5 independent of VC status. As for baseline comorbid disease, lab data, and medications, ESKD patients on MHD with VC relative to those without VC had a higher prevalence of CAD (p = 0.006) and lower use of ACEIs/ARBs (p < 0.001). Compared to ESKD patients on MHD with CTR ≥ 0.5, those with CTR < 0.5 had significantly higher levels of serum albumin (p < 0.001), serum triglyceride (p < 0.001), and hemoglobin (p = 0.008), along with lower alkaline-P level (p < 0.001), which was shown in Table 1. There was no significant difference among 4 groups in other parameters.

The echocardiographic findings of the patients are shown in supplementary file. Patients with VC and with CTR ≥ 0.5 had significantly higher interventricular septum (p = 0.004) and LA diameter (p = 0.028) than did those in the other 3 groups. Conversely, patients with no VC and with CTR ≥ 0.5 had significantly higher left ventricular end-systolic diameter (p = 0.002), left ventricular posterior wall (p = 0.005), LV mass (p = 0.009), left ventricular mass index (p < 0.001), but had a significantly lower ejection fraction (p < 0.001) than did those in the other 3 groups (Supplementary Table S1).

Association of VC and CTR with MACEs

After more than 3 years of follow-up, 125 (39.9%) of finally enrolled MHD patients developed MACEs. The numbers of MACEs were 6 (15.8%), 16(34.0%), 36(37.5%), 67(50.8%) in the groups with no VC with CTR <0.5, no VC with CTR ≥0.5, VC with CTR < 0.5, and VC with CTR ≥0.5, respectively. During the follow-up period, MHD patients with VC with CTR ≥ 0.5 had the highest cumulative incidence of MACEs and those with no VC with CTR < 0.5 developed the lowest cumulative incidence of MACEs. Association of VC and CTR with CV and overall mortality

Regarding secondary outcomes, 63 (20.1%) of finally enrolled MHD patients died from CV causes, and 82 (26.2%) patients died during the follow-up period. The numbers of CV mortality were 2 (5.3%), 9 (19.1%), 17 (17.7%), and 35 (26.5%), while the numbers of overall mortality were 4 (10.5%), 12 (25.5%), 21 (21.9%), 45 (34.1%) in the groups with no VC with CTR <0.5, no VC with CTR ≥0.5, VC with CTR < 0.5, and VC with CTR ≥0.5, respectively.

During the follow up period, MHD patients with VC with CTR ≥ 0.5 had the highest cumulative incidence of CV mortality and those with no VC with CTR < 0.5 developed the lowest cumulative incidence of CV mortality (log-rank test, p = 0.004; Figure 2b). A similar trend of overall mortality was observed that MHD patients with VC with CTR ≥ 0.5 and those with no VC with CTR < 0.5, who developed the highest and lowest incidence of overall mortality, respectively (log-rank test, p = 0.004; Figure 2c).

In univariate Cox proportional hazard regression, relative to those with no VC with CTR < 0.5, the cHRs of CV mortality were 4.016, 3.736, 6.016 (95% CI, 0.868–18.59, 0.863–16.17, 1.447–25.02, respectively) and the cHRs of overall mortality were 2.683, 2.308, 3.869 (95% CI, 0.865–8.318, 0.792–6.725, 1.319–10.76, respectively) in groups of no VC with CTR ≥ 0.5, VC with CTR <0.5, and VC with CTR ≥ 0.5. After adjustment for age and sex (model [1], those with VC with CTR ≥ 0.5 remained a significant risk factor of CV and overall mortality (aHR, 6.426, 4.031; 95% CI, 1.503–27.48, 1.407–11.55, respectively). Those with VC with CTR ≥ 0.5 still had highest risk of CV and overall mortality but became insignificant after adjustment for age, sex, weight, vintage, CAD, serum albumin, alkaline phosphatase, triglyceride, serum hemoglobin, and ACEI/ARBs, which was shown in Table 2.

Discussion

Our study suggests that the coexistence of VC and CTR ≥0.5 is associated with adverse CV outcomes in MHD patients. Patients with both VC and CTR ≥0.5 exhibited the highest cumulative incidence of MACEs, although the associations with CV and overall mortality were attenuated in the fully adjusted models. These findings highlight the combined prognostic utility of VC and cardiac enlargement, suggesting that simultaneous assessment of these two factors may provide additional insights for identifying MHD patients at particularly high risk who may warrant further CV evaluation. In addition to these outcome findings, MHD patients with CTR ≥0.5 were more likely to be female, had longer dialysis vintage, higher levels of serum alkaline phosphatase, and lower body weight, albumin, triglyceride, and hemoglobin levels. Among MHD patients with CTR ≥0.5, those with VC were the oldest and had the lowest levels of serum albumin, triglyceride, and hemoglobin.

In our study, MHD patients with cardiomegaly were predominantly female. The sex difference in the prevalence of LVH among dialysis patients has been well-documented, possibly due to sex-specific differences in cardiac adaptation [16]. Additionally, dialysis vintage has been associated with LVH in a previous study [17]. Notably, while CTR reflects the overall cardiac silhouette and is significantly influenced by volume status and radiographic projectional factors, it is strongly correlated with LVH in dialysis patients [18]. Therefore, these underlying factors may partially explain the observations in patients with a CTR ≥ 0.5 in our cohort. We also observed that patients with a CTR ≥ 0.5 had higher serum alkaline phosphatase levels. However, the direct relationship between cardiomegaly and elevated alkaline phosphatase levels has not been firmly established in previous studies [19]. Furthermore, an enlarged cardiac silhouette can be a consequence of fluid overload [20], which is closely linked to malnutrition [21], resulting from overhydration-induced insulin resistance, muscle catabolism, and metabolic disruption, especially in dialysis patients [22]. Consequently, MHD patients with cardiomegaly exhibited lower body weight and reduced levels of serum albumin, triglycerides, and hemoglobin. Furthermore, age is a well-established risk factor for the prevalence and progression of VC [23] and VC has been demonstrated to be involved in inflammatory process, resulting in malnutrition in dialysis patients [24]. The findings of advanced age and lowest levels of serum albumin, triglyceride, and hemoglobin in those with cardiomegaly and VC are consistent with prior evidence. VC is strongly associated with CAD in both pre-dialysis and dialysis patients [25]. The association between VC and CAD may be attributed to systemic atherosclerosis and shared risk factors, including age, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia [26]. This aligns with our finding that MHD patients with VC had a higher prevalence of CAD. In addition, some studies suggest that ACEi/ARBs may provide benefits in patients with valve stenosis, likely due to their blood pressure-lowering effects. Elevated blood pressure accelerates the progression of valve damage like calcification by increasing mechanical and shear stress on valve leaflets [27]. In our study, patients with VC had lower use of ACEi/ARBs, which may partially explain our findings.

Echocardiographic findings showed that ESKD patients undergoing MHD with CTR ≥ 0.5 had a greater interventricular septum, left atrial diameter, left ventricular end-systolic diameter, left ventricular posterior wall, left ventricular mass, left ventricular mass index, but a lower ejection fraction. In dialysis patients, CTR may serve as a moderate marker of LVH [18], which was determined by left ventricular mass index calculated based on Cube formula [14]. The composite parameters included interventricular septum thickness, left ventricular end-systolic diameter, left ventricular posterior wall thickness. Additionally, left atrial enlargement often concurrently occurs with LVH as an adaptive response to pressure overload, resulting in impaired LV filling and relaxation secondary to LVH [28]. Systolic dysfunction in left ventricle, which results in increased left ventricular end-systolic volume, was highly correlated with LVH [29]. Therefore, the lower ejection fraction observed in patients with CTR ≥ 0.5 is likely a reasonable finding.

We observed that ESKD patients on MHD with VC and CTR ≥0.5 had the highest cumulative incidence of MACEs, CV mortality and overall mortality compared to other groups. Previous studies have shown that both LVH and VC independently predict CV and all-cause mortality, and that their coexistence exerts additive or even synergistic effects on adverse CV outcomes in dialysis patients [11,30]. Our findings are consistent with these observations but extend them by using CTR ≥ 0.5, a radiographic marker of cardiomegaly associated with LVH, to demonstrate a similar association between CTR and VC in predicting CV outcomes. Unlike prior studies that assessed LVH solely through echocardiography, our study introduces an easily applicable and widely available radiographic parameter that may serve as a practical surrogate for LVH in hemodialysis patients. The potential mechanisms underlying this interaction may involve LVH-related cardiac dysfunction, which limits left ventricular diastolic filling and reduces stroke volume, potentially increasing the risk of heart failure and adverse CV events [31]. In addition, LVH may heighten susceptibility to ischemic injury in the heart, brain, and peripheral arteries—a vulnerability that may be further exacerbated by the presence of VC or the accompanying vascular stiffness [11]. This bidirectional relationship may help explain the markedly elevated incidence of adverse outcomes in patients with both CTR ≥ 0.5 and VC. However, it is important to clarify that while the association with MACEs remained robust, the statistical significance for CV and overall mortality was lost after full multivariable adjustment. This suggests that while the coexistence of VC and high CTR is a strong indicator of composite CV risk, its independent association with hard endpoints, mortality, requires further confirmation in larger cohorts.

This exploratory study integrates echocardiographic and radiographic data to assess the combined prognostic value of VC and CTR in ESKD patients undergoing hemodialysis. By including multiple clinical and laboratory parameters and conducting stratified analyses based on VC and CTR, it offers preliminary insights into risk stratification. However, we acknowledge several important limitations. First, the single-center, retrospective design and the restricted enrollment period of three months may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, the echocardiographic definition of valvular calcification is simplistic, lacking differentiation by valve type or severity. Moreover, the lack of longitudinal data restricts our ability to evaluate the temporal progression of VC and CTR. Given the cross-sectional design, we cannot establish causal relationships or rule out reverse causality between cardiac structural changes and clinical outcomes. Third, the wide confidence intervals observed for CV mortality suggest limited precision, likely reflecting potential power limitations for secondary outcomes due to the relatively low number of mortality events. Finally, while the association with MACEs remained robust after adjustment for potential confounders, the statistical significance for mortality outcomes was attenuated in fully adjusted models, indicating that our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and warrant validation in future large-scale prospective studies.

Future research should prioritize prospective, multicenter cohort studies to validate the combined prognostic utility of VC and CTR across diverse hemodialysis populations and healthcare settings, thereby improving generalizability and causal inference. Longitudinal imaging with repeated echocardiography and chest radiography is warranted to elucidate the temporal dynamics and bidirectional progression of VC and cardiomegaly, as well as their mechanistic links to inflammation, mineral-bone disorder, volume overload, and left ventricular remodeling. Integration of advanced biomarkers (e.g., inflammatory indices, calcification propensity assays), detailed dialysis parameters, and medication adherence data may refine risk stratification and identify modifiable pathways. Moreover, randomized or pragmatic interventional trials targeting volume management, mineral metabolism optimization, and cardioprotective therapies should assess whether modifying VC and CTR trajectories translates into reduced MACEs and mortality. Finally, incorporating VC and CTR into explainable, AI-driven clinical decision support systems could enable scalable, low-cost screening and personalized risk prediction in routine dialysis care, while ensuring transparency and external validation prior to clinical implementation.

In conclusion, this exploratory study used a cohort of patients with ESKD to investigate the combined prognostic utility of VC and increased CTR for CV risk prediction. Our findings suggest that the coexistence of VC and CTR ≥ 0.5 is robustly associated with the composite MACE endpoint; however, this association was not independently confirmed for hard outcomes, mortality, after full multivariable adjustment. While identifying this specific subgroup may provide a basis for future research into targeted monitoring, these results currently serve as a basis for hypothesis generation rather than immediate practice-changing implications. In addition, both CTR and VC are low-cost, imaging-based prognostic markers widely used in CV risk prediction among hemodialysis patients, offering a potential avenue for future implementation in routine clinical screening, provided these exploratory findings of CV mortality and overall mortality are validated in large-scale prospective studies.
