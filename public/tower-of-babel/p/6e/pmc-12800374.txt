Leveraging molecular graphs for natural product classification
Authors: Alessia Lucia Prete, Barbara Toniella Corradini, Filippo Costanti, Franco Scarselli, Monica Bianchini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNatural Products (NPs) represent a rich source of bioactive compounds with high structural diversity and therapeutic potential. Automatic classification of NPs is critical to ensure safety, support regulatory compliance, inform product usage, and enable the discovery of new pharmacologically relevant molecules. However, traditional rule-based approaches and hand-crafted molecular fingerprints often fall short in capturing the structural and biosynthetic complexity of NPs, making their classification difficult.In this work, we explore the use of graph neural networks (GNNs) for NP classification by learning neural fingerprints directly from molecular graph structures. GNNs are well-suited for this task, as they can model both the topology and local chemical environments of molecules. We evaluate multiple GNN architectures on curated NP dataset and assess their ability to generalize across hierarchical classification targets. To promote reproducibility, we also examine implementation aspects such as graph construction, node feature selection, architecture tuning, and training strategies.Our results show that GNN-based models consistently outperform classifier based on traditional fingerprints, achieving superior accuracy and robustness. Model performance is strongly influenced by the choice of architecture and feature representation, emphasizing the importance of task-specific model design.These findings highlight the potential of GNNs as effective tools for NP classification. By leveraging graph-based representations, GNNs offer a scalable, data-driven approach that better reflects the structural and functional complexity of natural products. This work provides methodological guidance and encourages broader adoption of topology-aware deep learning algorithms in natural product research and drug discovery. Our code is available at the project page: https://bcorrad.github.io/ginestra25/.Keywords: Deep learning, Graph neural network, Natural products, Neural fingerprint, Molecular fingerprint

Introduction

Natural products (NPs) are compounds, characterized by high chemical variability, synthesized by living organisms such as plants, fungi and bacteria [1]. NPs, also known as secondary metabolites, are not directly involved in primary physiological processes, such as plant growth or reproduction, but play an essential role in ecological interactions and species survival.

Thanks to their diverse bioactivity, NPs have been used for thousands of years in traditional medicine and remain highly valuable in modern pharmacology. Their applications range from anti-inflammatory treatments to anticancer and antimicrobial therapies [2]. Indeed, more than 50% of all approved drugs are estimated to be derived from or inspired by NPs [3], while their therapeutic potential also supports approaches such as phytotherapy, a practice officially recognized by the World Health Organization (WHO) [4], [5]. Furthermore, recognition of the biological properties of thousands of secondary metabolites has led to a re-evaluation of these compounds from a nutritional perspective, since some NPs have vitamin or pro-vitamin functions, while many others owe their beneficial effects to their antioxidant activity, which varies depending on the chemical and physical properties of each molecule. Finally, numerous epidemiological studies have demonstrated that NPs, if consumed regularly in the diet and in significant quantities, can have important long-term effects [6].

Currently, there is also considerable industrial interest in secondary metabolites, as some of them constitute active ingredients in many commercially available products, in addition to pharmaceuticals, including pesticides and cosmetics. Therefore, in recent years, several databases—including ChemSpider [7], PubChem [8], NPAtlas [9], LOTUS [10], and ChEBI [11]—have been developed to support NP research. However, only a few offer curated hierarchical classifications based on their biosynthetic origin, mainly due to the lack of a rigorous and standardized categorization system.

Current structure-based ontologies, such as ChemOnt (employed by tools like ClassyFire) [12], enable the automatic classification of molecules based on chemical features. However, these systems were originally designed for broad metabolomics and bio-organic chemistry applications, and thus only offer a partial and chemically-oriented view of natural products. As a result, they often lack the semantic richness and biological context required for NP-specific research. In contrast, traditional NP classifications developed by domain experts over the past century are semantically rich and integrate biosynthetic pathways, source organisms and biological functions. However, despite their pharmacological importance, secondary metabolites remain vastly underexplored, while current databases capture only a small subset of existing natural products, and only a limited number of these are annotated with detailed structural, biosynthetic, and functional metadata. This gap is primarily due to the extraordinary chemical diversity of secondary metabolites and the inherent difficulties in isolating and characterizing them from complex biological sources.

To address this issue, and to enable a more semantically grounded automatic classification of natural products, a taxonomy has been introduced in [13], also taking into account the semantic and biological context of NPs. Such a taxonomy not only improves the interpretability in the identification of NPs but also enables keeping the focus on their functionalities—facilitating the identification of new drugs and the inference of bioactivity patterns [14], [15]. NPs have been organized into a hierarchical ontology, structured in a top-down manner into pathways, superclasses, and classes, based on their biosynthetic origins, chemical properties, and pharmacological activities, including antibiotic, antitumour, and immunosuppressive effects (polyketides; e.g., erythromycin, doxorubicin, tacrolimus) [16], [4], anaesthetic and anticancer (alkaloids) [17], anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and cardioprotective effects (terpenoids and phenylpropanoids) [18], immunomodulatory, antimicrobial, and antiviral properties (peptides and carbohydrates) [19], [20], and metabolic regulation (fatty acids) [21].

Traditional classification approaches for NPs often rely on structural similarity, hand-crafted rules, or the presence of functional groups [22]. Nevertheless, the characterisation and automatic classification of NPs remains a major challenge. This is largely due to their chemical complexity, diversity and hybrid biosynthetic origins, as many NPs arise from multiple biosynthetic pathways and undergo unique adaptive transformations, diversifying their molecular scaffolds. A notable example is Ilicicolin F, a fungal metabolite produced by species of Fusarium. Ilicicolin F exemplifies a polyketide-terpenoid hybrid, as it integrates a polyketide-derived aromatic core with a terpenoid side chain1 (see Fig. 1).

Machine learning and deep learning methods, particularly deep neural networks, have emerged as a promising solution by enabling the automatic extraction of high-level features directly from molecular representations. Traditionally, molecular classification has relied on hand-crafted features such as structural fingerprints (e.g., SMILES, Morgan Fingerprint), which encode the presence of substructures or physicochemical properties. While effective in many cases, these approaches are limited by their dependency on predefined rules and their inability to fully capture the complexity of molecular graphs. In contrast, deep neural networks can use the structural fingerprint to learn a neural fingerprint, a task-specific representation that enables more expressive and adaptable modelling of chemical information [23], [24], [25]. Among deep learning approaches, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as particularly effective for molecular tasks. Unlike traditional models, GNNs operate on graph-structured data, which allows them to capture atomic and bond interactions through topologically-aware molecular representations.

In this study, we investigate the capability of GNNs to learn neural fingerprints of NPs directly from molecular graphs, assessing the quality of the learnt fingerprint by performing NP classification as a downstream task. We follow the hierarchical taxonomy of [13], performing classification at the pathway, superclass, and class levels to evaluate how explicit molecular topology modelling affects predictive performance. Fig. 2 provides an overview of the graph-based classification pipeline adopted in this study: a graph representation of the molecular structures is processed by a GNN, which produces a neural fingerprint; the fingerprint is classified by a neural classifier. By benchmarking multiple foundational GNN architectures (GCN, GAT and GIN) against a standard feed-forward neural network trained on the counted Morgan Fingerprint (cMF), we show that neural fingerprints generally outperform traditional hand-crafted descriptors, particularly in fine-grained classification tasks requiring nuanced structural understanding. We also assess the informativeness of hand-crafted features like cMF in boosting performance of less expressive GNNs, providing practical insights to engineer deep learning models in cheminformatics.

Overview of the graph-based classification pipeline. Starting from the SMILES string representation, each molecule is converted into its corresponding 2D structure and then into a graph, where atoms are nodes and bonds are edges. This graph is processed by a Graph Neural Network (e.g., GCN, GAT or GIN), which learns a neural fingerprint encoding structural information. The fingerprint is then passed to a classifier that outputs graph-level predictions across three classification levels (e.g., pathway, superclass, and class), denoted as C0,C1,...,Cn.

Related work

The structural classification of small molecules and natural products (NPs) is a longstanding challenge in cheminformatics and metabolomics. Before the rise of graph-based deep learning, classification tasks in this domain were commonly tackled using hand-crafted molecular descriptors such as Extended-Connectivity Fingerprints (ECFPs) [26], PaDEL descriptors [27], and MACCS keys, coupled with traditional machine learning models like Support Vector Machines (SVMs), Random Forests (RFs) [28], and feedforward neural networks. These methods achieved strong performance in predicting molecular properties, toxicological endpoints, and structural categories across diverse chemical datasets.

One prominent example of a non-graph-based approach is NPClassifier [13], a deep learning-based tool that performs hierarchical classification of natural products using counted Morgan fingerprints and expert-defined ontologies. It enables multi-level predictions—Pathway, Superclass, and Class—on a wide range of NPs. NPClassifier sets a strong baseline for metabolite classification tasks without leveraging graph-based architectures. Similarly, models based on SMILES strings and sequence-based encoders, such as RNNs and Transformers, have also shown promise for molecular property prediction and class inference, particularly when trained on large corpora of chemical sequences [29] [30].

GNNs have emerged as a powerful class of deep learning models for processing complex, graph-structured data in a variety of domains. Their message-passing mechanism efficiently propagates information through graph edges, effectively capturing intricate relationships and node interdependencies. While early GNN models were based on recursive architectures, refining node representations iteratively across multiple iterations [31], the most recent GNNs mostly rely on convolutional approaches, which enhance computational efficiency and scalability by aggregating local spatial neighborhood information. These convolution-based architectures significantly enhance the ability of GNNs to approximate complex graph functions, such as Graph Edit Distance (GED) [32], [33], leading to improved performance in numerous practical applications, including user interaction prediction in social networks [34], drug discovery in molecular chemistry [35], and anomaly detection in computer networks [36].

In bioinformatics, GNNs have been proven effective in tasks such as protein-protein interface prediction [37] and node/edge classification [38], highlighting their versatility and making them particularly well-suited for applications in cheminformatics and drug discovery, where an accurate understanding of the molecular behaviour is critical [39]. Architectures such as Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) [40], Graph Attention Networks (GATs) [41], and Graph Isomorphism Networks (GINs) [42] have been successfully applied to various tasks, including the prediction of physicochemical properties [43] and biological activity [44] of molecules, and in drug–target interactions [45].

Given their success in processing molecular data, GNNs have increasingly been extended to different types of molecules, including natural products. In [46], a deep learning framework is proposed, including GNNs, for the de novo design of biosynthetic pathways, demonstrating that graph networks can accurately represent the structural complexity of NPs. However, the study in [46] focuses primarily on pathway prediction and generation, rather than structural or hierarchical classification. Other models have focused on metabolic pathway prediction, often relying on GCNs trained directly on molecular structures encoded as SMILES strings [47]. These approaches have demonstrated superior performance compared to traditional machine learning methods, such as Random Forests [48], by capturing the structural relationships inherent in molecular graphs. Building on this, more recent models based on Graph Attention Networks (GATs) have further enhanced predictive accuracy in this domain [49]. Nevertheless, most recent studies remain limited to a small number of predefined functional metabolic routes (e.g., glycolysis), typically derived from the KEGG database [50], which focuses on the functional roles of metabolites within metabolic networks.

A more structure-focused approach is presented in [51], in which a Molecular Graph Convolutional Neural Network (MGCNN) is used to classify alkaloids based on the biosynthetic origin of their precursor molecules. By utilizing molecular graph structures, this method significantly outperforms traditional approaches such as RFs and neural networks trained on ECFPs [26] or PaDEL descriptors [27]. However, the analysis performed was limited to a flat classification within a single metabolite class and did not explore hierarchical taxonomies.

In contrast, our work addresses two key gaps in existing literature: (i) the absence of graph-based models for hierarchical structural classification of natural products across multiple taxonomic levels, and (ii) the limited generalization of prior models to diverse biosynthetic classes. By integrating curated chemical ontologies with molecular graph representations, our method enables accurate, ontology-aware predictions across heterogeneous compound families. Building on the NPClassifier dataset [13], we extend the use of GNNs beyond single-path prediction to large-scale, multilevel structural annotation, bridging the gap between graph learning and natural product taxonomy, which is critical in cheminformatics and metabolomics applications.

Challenges in NP classification with GNNs

The classification of secondary metabolites of plants poses significant challenges due to their high structural diversity and functional heterogeneity. These compounds (such as alkaloids, flavonoids, and terpenoids) frequently exhibit a complex molecular structure, including multiple distinct functional groups and rare substructures. This structural complexity leads to several specific difficulties when using GNNs for graph classification. Secondary metabolites often possess highly branched and cyclic structures, with rings and bonds of varying sizes: capturing these intricate graph topologies requires GNNs to effectively model long-range dependencies and subtle structural variations, which can be challenging for standard message-passing mechanisms. Many secondary metabolites contain rare functional groups or substructures that may be underrepresented in training datasets. This imbalance can cause GNN models to struggle in generalizing or recognizing these unique patterns, leading to reduced predictive accuracy. In light of these challenges, we evaluate and compare three graph-based architectures (GCN, GAT, and GIN), each of which offers different levels of expressive power. In particular, GCN provides a basic convolutional approach, GAT introduces attention mechanisms to weigh the importance of neighboring nodes, and GIN is designed to match the expressiveness of the Weisfeiler-Leman graph isomorphism test, allowing for finer structural discrimination. This comparative evaluation allows us to assess the suitability of each architecture to capture the structural nuances of plant secondary metabolites.

Background

In this section, we briefly introduce the methods that can be used for embedding molecules, through fingerprints and graph-based representations, and the network architectures used in the hierarchical classification of NPs.

Molecular representation

The choice of molecular representation plays a central role in chemical informatics and machine learning applications in drug discovery. Molecular representations, indeed, enable the encoding of structural and physicochemical data into formats suitable for algorithmic processing and predictive analysis, acting as a link between chemical structures and computational models. In the following, we provide a description of two popular molecular representation methods, one based on vector encoding (Section 3.1.1), the other based on topology (Section 3.1.2), while the main characteristics of both are sketched in Table 1.

Vector-based representations

Traditional molecular fingerprints, such as Extended-Connectivity Fingerprints (ECFP, also known as counted Morgan Fingerprint, cMF), encode the presence or frequency of specific atom-centered substructures [26]. These representations are typically fixed-length binary or integer vectors. Each bit (or integer) corresponds to a hashed identifier of a circular substructure around an atom, up to a predefined radius. cMFs retain information on how often each substructure appears in a molecule, making them more informative for regression or classification tasks where frequency matters (see Fig. 3).

Computation of the cMF: atomic environments at increasing radii (r = 0,1,2) are extracted and hashed to generate a count-based fingerprint vector.

Despite being computationally efficient and chemically interpretable, these fingerprints are static and hand-engineered: the set of features is predefined and independent of the specific task at hand. Moreover, hashed fingerprints are prone to bit collisions and do not capture higher-order relational patterns or the global context of the molecular graph.

Graph-based representations

A graph is defined as a pair G=(V,E), where V represents the set of nodes or vertices and E⊆V×V is the set of edges. Nodes represent entities, while edges define the relationships between them. Both nodes and edges can be associated with attributes consisiting of values or vectors of values that describe node features: xv∈Rdn for each node v∈V, and eu,v∈Rde for each edge (u,v)∈E. The stacked versions of the node and edge feature vectors form the node feature matrix XV∈R|V|×dn and the edge feature matrix XE∈R|E|×de, respectively.

A molecule can be described as an undirected, labelled graph G=(V,E,XV,XE), where nodes represent atoms, with node features corresponding to atomic properties, and edges represent bonds between atoms, with edge features describing the type of chemical bond.

Graph–based representations offer a more flexible and expressive alternative with respect to vector-based representations. GNNs operate directly on molecular graphs, learning task-specific embeddings through iterative message passing between neighboring nodes [52], [53]. Unlike fingerprinting methods, GNNs learn representations in a data-driven manner, optimizing molecular embeddings according to the target task. These learned embeddings—often referred to as “neural graph fingerprints”—can encode both local substructures and global topological context, capturing nuanced features such as aromaticity, branching, ring systems, and long-range dependencies that are often lost in traditional fingerprints.

Neural networks

In this work, we adopt three widely-used GNN architectures: GCN [40], GAT [41], and GIN [42], which have demonstrated excellent performance in various structural graph classification tasks, including molecular graphs such as those representing secondary metabolites. A fundamental characteristic of GNNs is the message passing mechanism, where each node iteratively updates its representation by aggregating information from its neighbors. This enables the model to capture both local and global graph features, which is crucial for structural classification.

Graph attention networks. In GCNs and GINs, the aggregation mechanism combines all the features, without considering the fact that different neighbors may have a different relevance to the considered task and that the number of neighbors may change largely depending on the considered node. GATs compute a dynamic attention weight, for each neighbor, based on node features, enabling the model to prioritize more informative neighbors. This flexibility has been shown to improve performance, particularly in tasks where neighbor relevance is heterogeneous [41].

In this study, we employ the GATv2Conv variant of the standard GAT architecture. GATv2 [54] improves upon the original GAT by allowing a more expressive attention mechanism that is sensitive to both the features and relative positions of nodes. Unlike the original GAT, where attention weights are computed after applying a shared linear transformation to each node, GATv2 performs joint transformations on source and target node pairs before attention computation. This change enables the model to better capture fine structural differences, which is especially relevant for molecular graphs.

This approach follows the baseline strategy used in [13], which employs fixed vector-based molecular fingerprints for structural classification of NPs. Unlike GNNs, this architecture does not explicitly model molecular topology, offering a meaningful starting point to evaluate the benefits of structural information learned through graph-based methods.

Materials and methods

In the following, we describe in detail the collected dataset and the different network architectures used in the comparative experiments.

Dataset

We adopt the dataset in [13] as the basis for our classification task. It consists of 77,705 natural products collected from publicly available chemical databases, including PubChem [8], ChEBI [11], ChemSpider [7], and the Universal Natural Products Database (UNPD) [55]. Each molecule in the dataset is annotated at three hierarchical classification levels based on biosynthetic and structural features: pathway (7 categories), superclass (70 categories), and class (653 categories). Data augmentation. Unlike the original study [13], we did not apply any form of data augmentation. In [13], biosynthetically plausible analogs were generated by applying simple chemical transformations, such as (1) the addition of methyl groups to hydroxyl groups to form methoxy substituents, and (2) the replacement of methoxy groups with hydroxyls. The same augmentation strategy cannot be straightforwardly replicated for graph-based models. In the fingerprint setting, adding or removing small functional groups affects only a local bit pattern, leaving the overall descriptor nearly unchanged and thus inflating sample counts without truly increasing structural diversity. In a graph representation, instead, each transformation produces a new molecular graph with different node and edge configurations; this (i) alters the message-passing trajectories learned by the GNN, (ii) introduces topological artefacts that may not preserve the original biosynthetic labels. For these reasons, we opted for a more conservative and reproducible evaluation setup, using a fixed representation per molecule and no synthetic modifications.

Neural network architectures

A central goal of this work is to compare the neural fingerprints produced by different graph encoders for NP classification. To this end, we conducted a neural architecture search (NAS) over a broad architectural design space followed by a conventional grid search for scalar hyperparameters (learning rate, weight decay, drop out rate) via the procedure detailed in Section 5. Fig. 5 depicts the three best architectures it returned.

GCN and GAT use global mean pooling, while GIN uses global sum pooling (i.e., node-wise summation) to obtain a graph-level embedding. The search considered (i) the graph operator (GCN, GAT, GIN), (ii) activation function (ReLU, ELU, LeakyReLU), (iii) global pooling (sum, mean, attention), (iv) skip-connection topology (none, pre-act residual, dense), (v) batch–normalisation placement (pre- or post-activation), (vi) optimizer (Adam, AdamW). Depth was restricted to {3,4,5} layers; its influence is analysed in Section 6.1.

Each resulting architecture consists of two components: a GNN backbone, which maps the input graph to a neural fingerprint, and a shared feedforward classifier head. To ensure fair comparison, we employ the same two-layer classifier across all models: a fully connected layer projecting to 1024 hidden units, followed by a ReLU activation and dropout (p = 0.2), and a final linear layer mapping to the number of output classes.

As a non-graph baseline, we also include an MLP trained on cMFs, to assess the benefits of structural representations learned by GNNs.

In the following, we provide a detailed description of the selected GNN architectures.

GCN

The GCN model is considered for its simplicity and proven effectiveness. The architecture based on GCN consists of stacked GCNConv layers with ReLU activations. Each layer is followed by batch normalization and dropout for regularization. To aggregate information across the graph, we apply the global mean pooling on the final node embeddings, producing a fixed-size graph representation.

GAT

To enable dynamic weighting of neighbor information, we use a GAT architecture, which models relational dependencies in graph-structured data via attention mechanisms. Our implementation uses GATv2Conv, an enhanced variant of the original GAT formulation, where the attention mechanism is allowed to depend more flexibly on both source and target node features—leading to greater representational capacity.

Each GATv2 layer employs multi-head attention, followed by batch normalization, ELU activations, and dropout for regularization. Moreover, we employ residual connections throughout the network: each attention layer includes a parallel linear transformation of the input, which is added to the output of the attention mechanism to facilitate gradient flow and improve stability. For the graph-level representation, we apply global mean pooling to the final node embeddings, resulting in a fixed-size neural fingerprint.

GIN

The fingerprint based on GIN is motivated by its strong theoretical expressiveness and empirical performance on graph-structured data. In our implementation, each GINConv layer is instantiated with a two-layer MLP using GELU activations [56] and followed by batch normalization. The aggregation function is a sum over node features, preserving injectivity and aligning with GIN's original formulation. To improve representational capacity, we observed that the most informative neural fingerprints are obtained by concatenating intermediate representations across layers—rather than relying solely on the final layer output. MLP

As a non-graph baseline, we employ an MLP that operates on precalculated molecular fingerprints. The input is a fixed-length vector obtained by concatenating two standard structural descriptors: the 2048-dimensional cMF and a 4096-dimensional variant. Both fingerprints are generated using RDKit with standard parameters and capture local topological environments and atom-centered substructures.

The network comprises four fully connected layers with ReLU activations and batch normalization, promoting training stability. A dropout layer is applied before the output to mitigate overfitting. The final linear layer produces task-specific logits for multi-label classification. This model is trained under the same conditions, allowing for a controlled comparison of the learned versus fixed molecular representations.

Model explainability

To complement the evaluation of predictive performance, we employed GNNExplainer [58] to investigate the interpretability of the trained models. This method estimates feature attributions by assigning importance scores to nodes and edges that most strongly influence a given prediction. Explanations were obtained for a subset of high-confidence predictions and visualized by mapping importance scores onto the molecular graphs, providing qualitative insight into the structural motifs captured by the networks.

Experimental setup and results

We evaluate whether GNNs can effectively learn from molecular graphs to predict the class of natural products at their three hierarchical levels: pathway, superclass, and class. To this end, we reproduce the MLP baseline proposed in [13] and compare it against three GNN architectures—GCN, GAT, and GIN— specifically designed to incorporate molecular graph topology into the learning process. Our goal is to assess whether learning directly from molecular graphs via GNNs can capture structural features as effectively as traditional fixed fingerprints, such as the counted Morgan representation.

To ensure that each model was appropriately tailored to the characteristics of the target label space (pathway, superclass, or class level), we allowed minor architectural adjustments between classification tasks—such as the number of GNN layers and hidden dimensions. These variations are task-specific but follow consistent architectural principles; complete configurations for all models are given in Table A.13, Table A.14, Table A.15, Table A.16 (Appendix A).

All models were implemented using the PyTorch library, exploiting PyTorch Geometric for the implementation of GNNs. Training was performed using AdamW optimizer and binary cross entropy loss. Training used early stopping with a patience of 7 epochs and a minimum improvement threshold of δ=0.001 on the validation F1-score.

Hyperparameters were selected via a dedicated grid search, balancing validation precision, macro-F1 score, and model complexity. Final configurations were selected to maximize generalization performance on the test set while keeping the number of trainable parameters as low as possible. Effect of GNN depth on performance

In GNNs, each layer performs a message-passing step, where nodes aggregate information from their immediate neighbors. As a result, the depth of the network—defined by the number of these layers—directly determines how many rounds of message exchange occur, and thus how far information can travel across the graph from any given node, influencing the model ability to capture both local and global structural patterns. This is particularly relevant in molecular contexts, as some molecular properties are driven by local patterns, favouring shallow models, whereas others benefit from the broader context captured by deeper networks.

The optimal number of layers in GNNs is usually determined empirically. This is partly due to the difficulty of anticipating whether a given task depends more on local or global features, and partly due to known limitations of deep GNNs. Increasing the number of layers can lead to performance degradation due to phenomena such as oversmoothing, where node representations become indistinguishably similar across the graph [59], [60], and oversquashing, where long-range signals are compressed into bottlenecked representations, limiting expressiveness [61]. Moreover, deeper models are more susceptible to overfitting, particularly in low-data regimes.

We observe that deeper networks tend to perform better, especially on the finest-grained task of class-level classification, where capturing structural differences demands a greater receptive field and more expressive representations.

Discussion

The results in Table 6, together with the architectural and feature analysis, provide important insights into the strengths and limitations of the evaluated GNN architectures across varying classification tasks with different granularity levels (Pathway, Superclass, Class).

GCN shows strong performance at coarser classification levels (Pathway and Superclass) and benefits considerably from the inclusion of external global features, which compensate for its limited intrinsic expressiveness. GCN employs mean aggregation at the message-passing level and global mean pooling at the graph level, which can map different multisets of neighbors to the same average, thereby losing information about multiplicities and subtle structural differences. In contrast, GIN adopts injective sum aggregation and global sum pooling, preserving neighborhood counts and enabling a more expressive mapping. Consistently with this, our results (Section 5) show GIN outperforming GCN at finer taxonomy levels. Importantly, GCN remains lightweight in terms of parameter count (3.57M, comparable to GIN and an order of magnitude smaller than GAT, with 41.8M). However, unlike GIN, its intrinsic expressiveness is more limited, which explains the lower precision observed at the class level (0.7809 vs. 0.8034). Interestingly, when complemented with cMF, GCN approximately doubles its parameter count (8.56M) but consistently gains in performance across all targets, with class precision improving by more than 3 percentage points (from 0.7931 to 0.8326). This suggests that precomputed fingerprints provide global information that compensates for the weaker aggregation scheme of GCN, enabling it to match or even surpass the accuracy of more expressive models while still remaining far more parameter-efficient than GAT. In practice, this highlights the usefulness of combining GCN with molecular fingerprints in scenarios where efficiency is critical but fine-grained accuracy is still required. The improvements of GCN, however, saturate beyond four layers, reflecting the known over-smoothing and over-squashing tendencies of mean-based aggregators. The consistent advantage observed when integrating cMF supports the idea that complementary global descriptors can restore discriminative capacity otherwise lost in mean aggregation, making GCN a solid and efficient baseline for large-scale or resource-constrained applications.

GAT performance falls between GIN and GCN, with a notable trade-off observed when combining learned embeddings with fixed features: increased precision at the cost of decreasing recall. This suggests that, while GAT can leverage additional information to improve confidence, it may do so at the expense of sensitivity, especially in imbalanced datasets with under-represented classes.

The consistent underperformance of MLPs across all tasks highlights the importance of exploiting graph topology rather than relying solely on hand-crafted molecular fingerprints. This gap underlines the added value of GNNs in learning meaningful structural representations from molecular graphs.

In summary, model selection should take into account task granularity and data characteristics. GIN is well-suited for fine-grained classification tasks requiring detailed structural discrimination; GCN benefits notably from complementary fingerprints when global structural information is important, making it a practical choice in scenarios where efficiency must be balanced with accuracy; finally, GAT provides a balanced, though feature-sensitive, alternative. These insights can guide practical decisions in molecular property prediction and graph-based classification tasks. Hand-crafted descriptors (e.g., cMF) are efficient and interpretable, but they depend on predefined rules and cannot fully model higher-order relational patterns. Graph neural networks learn task-specific neural fingerprints directly from molecular topology, capturing atom-bond interactions and long-range context; in our benchmarks, this is especially beneficial at fine granularity, where topology governs subtle distinctions.

Model interpretability and applicability domain

Beyond predictive performance, we investigated whether the predictions of GNN models can be interpreted in chemically meaningful terms, and we assessed the extent of their applicability domain. These analyses are essential to address the perception of GNNs as “black-box” models and to delineate the categories of natural products for which predictions can be considered most trustworthy. We focused on the GIN model, which consistently achieved the best performance and whose reliance on node-level features (atom type, aromaticity, hybridization) makes attribution maps more interpretable.

Explanations were generated using GNNExplainer, which assigns importance scores to atoms and substructures contributing to classification. These highlighted regions reveal what the model has learned to discriminate, but they do not always coincide with the canonical motifs. In some cases, the model relies on peripheral substituents or correlated fragments that, while not chemically diagnostic, are statistically informative within the dataset. This reflects a common distinction between model interpretability and domain interpretability.

We restricted the analysis to the superclass and class levels. At the pathway level, predictive performance is consistently high, largely because categories are supported by thousands of compounds. However, pathways often encompass structurally diverse subfamilies (e.g., terpenoids, alkaloids, polyketides), and atom-level explanations tend to capture heterogeneous or overlapping motifs rather than a single diagnostic scaffold. In contrast, superclasses and classes correspond to chemically coherent groups defined by recurring substructures (e.g., aromatic rings, glycosidic linkages, cofactor-related motifs) [62], which are more directly reflected in attribution maps and align more clearly with domain expertise.

Conclusion

In this work, we investigated the potential of graph-based deep neural networks for the challenging task of natural product classification. We leverage a consolidated, hierarchically annotated dataset that assigns each molecule to its biosynthetic pathway, chemical superclass, and specific structural class. Following the success of graph neural networks in molecular representation learning, we adopt a topology-based approach and benchmark three GNN architectures, exploiting GCN, GAT, and GIN, respectively. As a non-graph-based baseline, we include an MLP trained on counted Morgan fingerprints.

Our results show that graph-based models can outperform the MLP baseline, particularly in fine-grained classification tasks where capturing molecular topology and finer structural details is crucial. Among the evaluated architectures, GIN consistently achieved the highest precision and F1-scores across all classification levels, highlighting its expressive power and capacity to capture subtle structural patterns in molecular graphs. GAT emerged as a competitive alternative, benefiting from attention mechanisms, though its performance varied depending on task granularity and feature augmentation. GCN showed marked improvement when augmented with cMF, indicating that hand-crafted descriptors can still complement less powerful models.

Furthermore, we conducted architectural and feature analyses to provide practical guidelines for model selection. In particular, we investigated the impact of model depth, the choice of node features, and the effect of augmenting GNNs with cMFs as a fixed molecular representation.

These results highlight the value of neural fingerprints learned from molecular graphs and promote the adoption of GNNs in the structural classification of metabolites. In addition to predictive performance, our interpretability and applicability analyses demonstrate that GNNs can provide chemically meaningful explanations when categories are defined by distinctive scaffolds, while also helping to identify domains where predictions are less reliable. This dual perspective strengthens confidence in the practical use of GNNs for natural product classification, offering both performance and transparency.

Future work will focus on joint hierarchical modeling across different taxonomic levels and exploring self-supervised or pre-trained GNN frameworks to improve generalization in low-data regimes or rare-class scenarios.
