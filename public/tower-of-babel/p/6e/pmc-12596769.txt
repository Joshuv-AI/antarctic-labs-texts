Single-center retrospective analysis of responsive neurostimulation outcomes
Authors: Stephen Jaffeee, Trent Shane Kite, Shaifali Arora, Chris Sanfillipo, Praveer Subhash Vyas, Navnika Gupta, James Valeriano, Timothy Quezada, Dorian Mateusz Kusyk, Alexander Whiting
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Responsive neurostimulation (RNS) is a promising treatment for drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE). RNS has been shown to be safe and effective in reducing seizure frequency in patients with DRE.Methods: We performed a retrospective cohort study of patients with DRE implanted with the RNS system at our institution between 2017 and 2024. The patients had at least 6 months of clinical follow-up and two RNS programming sessions postimplantation. We collected data on patient demographics and postimplantation seizure frequency. Efficacy was assessed as the median percent change in postimplantation seizure frequency compared to baseline, as well as responder rate defined as >50% reduction in seizures. A one-way analysis of variance test was used to assess differences in mean postoperative seizure frequency across all lead placement groups, and Fisher’s exact test was used to analyze differences in the proportion of patients achieving a postimplantation Engel classification of ≤2B.Results: Thirty patients were implanted with the RNS system at our institution between 2017 and 2024. Seventy-three percent of patients were male (n = 22), and the median age at RNS implantation was 36.5 years (interquartile range: 25–46). The mean seizure frequency reduction was 71.4 (±20.9), with a proportion of patients achieving a >50% seizure frequency reduction (responder rate) at 70% postimplantation, four patients had Engel 1A/1B outcome, 17 patients had Engel 2B outcome, and nine patients had achieved Engel 3A outcome. None of the patients had Engel 4 outcome at the last follow-up visit. Interestingly, the use of cenobamate had a statistically significant relationship with achieving a postoperative Engel 2B or better classification (P = 0.03) as well as prior vagal nerve stimulation placement (P < 0.001).Conclusion: RNS system is a safe and effective treatment for DRE. The responder rate in our cohort is consistent with the seizure outcomes reported in the literature. Additional studies need to characterize the differences between RNS responders and nonresponders.

INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy affects almost three million adults in the US. About 30–40% of patients with epilepsy are refractory to medications, meeting the criteria for drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE).[15,21,23,26] These patients are often candidates for surgical intervention with resective or ablative procedures, providing the best likelihood of seizure freedom.[5,22,24] However, these approaches are often not feasible or appropriate for certain patients for various reasons, including widespread or multifocal epilepsy networks, epilepsy networks involving eloquent cortex, or patient preference. Alternative neuromodulation options, including vagal nerve stimulation (VNS), deep brain stimulation, and responsive neurostimulation (RNS), have been shown to be effective in reducing seizure frequency in these patients.[3,6,11] RNS is a “closed loop” system implanted in the skull with one or two depth or paddle electrodes implanted in the key nodes in the epileptogenic network in patients with DRE.[1,5,7,12,15,16,18,21,23] In the RNS pivotal trial, Heck et al. reported a median seizure reduction of 44% at 1 year and 53% at 2 years during the open-label phase of the trial.[11] RNS has since been shown to be safe and effective in reducing seizure frequency in patients with DRE, with a long-term prospective open-label trial demonstrating a 75% median seizure frequency reduction and a responder rate (>50% reduction in seizure frequency) of 73%.[19] Advantages of RNS include the ability to target eloquent or disparate regions or networks with a presumed lower risk of postoperative neurocognitive deficits. Moreover, RNS allows for long-term electrocorticography monitoring in the targeted regions which may direct future therapies for the patient.[10]

Below, we describe a single center’s experience with the use of the RNS system and the overall outcomes. Our study aims to further characterize patient subgroups (e.g., bilateral hippocampal versus unilateral hippocampal lead placement) while controlling for extraneous variables, as this may prove helpful in defining which patients have the highest degree of improvement.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We performed a retrospective chart review of patients with DRE who were implanted with the RNS system (NeuroPace, Inc., Mountain View, CA, USA) at our institution between 2017 and 2024. Inclusion criteria were at least 6 months of clinical follow-up and two RNS programming sessions postimplantation. Data on patient demographics, epilepsy history, baseline seizure frequency, seizure outcomes, preoperative data, operative variables, use of cenobamate, and imaging were extracted from the electronic health record system. The study and data collection was approved by the Allegheny Health Network institutional review board (IRB 2021-314). Patient-reported seizure frequency after implantation was used to assess postimplantation seizure frequency.

At our institution, interested patients who meet the criteria for DRE are considered for surgical intervention and subsequently discussed at a multidisciplinary epilepsy conference. This conference includes epileptologists, epilepsy-trained neurosurgeons, neuroradiologists, and neuropsychologists. Patients with discordant noninvasive data and focal epilepsy are considered for stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG). SEEG implantation is performed using a robotic stereotactic assistance robotic neurosurgical system (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, Indiana, United States), utilizing high-resolution, thin-slice computed tomography angiogram and magnetic resonance imaging for planning purposes. The collected SEEG data are reviewed at a follow-up multidisciplinary case conference to determine the optimal therapeutic approach. The choice between surgical resection, laser ablation, or neuromodulation depends on multiple factors, including seizure network categorization, laterality, language dominance, preoperative neuropsychological assessments, and anticipated postoperative functional outcomes, in addition to shared clinical decision-making between the clinical team and patient.

Descriptive statistics were computed for all continuous variables. Fisher’s exact test was used to analyze differences between the proportions of categorical variables. A Shapiro– Wilk test was performed to assess the normality of continuous variables before analysis. Given that our data passed the Shapiro–Wilk test, we selected a parametric test to analyze differences across our continuous data. Subsequently, a one-way analysis of variance was performed to analyze differences in mean seizure frequency reduction across each lead placement group and seizure type. A Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (V.10.3.1).

RESULTS

Thirty patients with DRE underwent RNS system implantation between 2017 and 2024 at our institution. The median age of patients at the time of RNS implantation was 36.5 years (interquartile range: 25–46). Demographic data are compiled in Table 1. Many patients had a prior surgical intervention that included VNS implantation 8 (27%), temporal lobectomy 4 (13%), laser interstitial thermal therapy 1 (3%), and extra-temporal seizure focus resection 1 (3%). None of the patients had a prior corpus callosotomy or hemispherectomy. There were 93.3% of patients undergoing phase II invasive electroencephalography monitoring with SEEG.

Twenty-seven (90%) patients had two RNS depth electrodes placed, and 3 (10%) patients had cortical strip leads placed. There was significant variability in the RNS electrode lead placement in our cohort. Eleven (37%) leads were bilateral mesial temporal, 9 (30%) patients had cortical leads placed outside of the mesial temporal lobe, 3 (10%) patients had bilateral thalamic leads, 2 (6%) patients had both cortical and thalamic leads, and 5 (17%) of patients had unilateral mesial temporal leads [Tables 1-2]. Eight percent of patients had RNS placed with robotic navigation assistance. Hippocampal leads were primarily placed using the traditional posterior longitudinal approach beginning in the occipital lobe with one contact in the amygdala and attempting to place three contacts within the hippocampus and parahippocampal gyrus.

The mean seizure frequency reduction was 71.4 (±20.9) with an overall responder rate of 70%. There was no significant difference in the proportion of patients achieving an Engel classification ≤2B across the lead placement groups (two-tailed P = 0.57). There was no significant difference between the proportion of responders across lead placement groups (two-sided P = 0.56). No significant difference between mean seizure reduction frequency was demonstrated across lead placement groups (F = 0.22, P = 0.95).

The use of cenobamate had a statistically significant relationship with achieving a postoperative Engel 2B or better classification (two-tailed P = 0.03) as well as prior VNS placement (two-tailed P < 0.01); however, lobectomy was not significantly associated with postoperative Engel 2B or worse and there was no statistically significant difference in postimplantation seizure frequency reduction so cation.

With respect to seizure types, there was a significant difference between the responder rates (two-tailed P = 0.01). Results from multiple comparisons demonstrated that focal impaired awareness seizure (FIAS) versus FIAS/focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizure (FBTCS) cohorts accounted for this difference, with the FIAS group achieving a responder rate of 33% and the FIAS/FBTCS cohort achieving an 88% responder rate.

Regarding adverse events (AEs), there was no major implantation or stimulation-related AEs except for one patient requiring operative re-exploration and evacuation of an asymptomatic epidural hematoma in the immediate postoperative period. The cohort had no permanent morbidity or mortality, with no infections.

DISCUSSION

RNS system provides an attractive option for many patients with severe DRE who are not candidates for resective or ablative surgeries due to the potential risk of neurological injury or the likelihood of insufficient benefit. Potential RNS candidates include patients with true bitemporal epilepsy, patients with multifocal epilepsy or large regional epilepsy networks, patients with epilepsy networks involving eloquent cortex, patients with left-sided mesial temporal epilepsy at high risk for neuro-cognitive decline with resective surgery, and finally and perhaps, most interestingly patients who will not entertain any surgery that involves removal or ablation of a portion of their brain.[12,17,19] A recent study exploring patient experiences of resection and RNS for DRE reported that patients consider the risk of permanent loss versus reversibility while making therapeutic decisions for their epilepsy, Temporal lobectomies for DRE are some of the most efficacious surgeries in all of organized medicine with level 1 evidence and low complication rates.[4,5,8,9,25] Despite the evidence and dramatically increasing rates of DRE nationally, the rate of resections has actually decreased over the past decade.[8]

While RNS for most patients is a noncurative, palliative procedure, it certainly has clinical utility for many patients with DRE that otherwise will not or cannot undergo resective or ablative surgery. As such, it is important for clinicians to have a thorough and unbiased understanding of the efficacy and safety of RNS implantation, which is difficult to ascertain currently with a demonstrated positive bias in the literature and the small number of sizable patient series outside of the initial industry-sponsored trials. In addition, with the ability to place two electrodes or strips anywhere in the brain, the variability of implantations makes interpretation from the current literature difficult to extrapolate to individual patients. We hope that this study will add to the growing body of evidence while providing more granular data about implantations.

RNS demonstrated significant clinical efficacy in our cohort with low complication rates. The mean seizure frequency reduction was 71.4 (±20.9) in our real-world cohort, which is consistent with the reported literature at 1 year.[20] These findings are also similar to seizure reductions achieved at 5 and 6 years of treatment in the clinical trials.[12] There were no reported wound infections and only one return to the operating room for an asymptomatic epidural hematoma evacuation, which resulted in no morbidity or mortality. Our low infection rate may be a result of sampling bias or low sample size; however, we have found success with preparation that includes the use of betadine solution with copious antibiotic irrigation. Patients are discharged on a short course of oral antibiotics postoperatively as well.

While we were unable to capture a statistically significant difference in seizure reduction between seizure subtype and location of lead placement, the overall seizure reduction percentage was nearly equivocal which may indicate the generalizability of this technology and efficacy in treating various types of epilepsy.[15] Notably, pre and postimplantation seizure frequency was a subjective measure and is a limitation of this study and, for that matter, most studies of epilepsy outcomes.

We utilized cenobamate as a variable for subgroup analysis because it has demonstrated a high efficacy, with as much as a 20% rate of seizure-free patients, which has never been reached before in comparable trials with other ASMs in phase 2 randomized double-blind trials.[2,14] A 2-year open-label extension of the original trial showed that the efficacy was maintained over a long-term period.[13] Patients on cenobamate did have a statistically significant seizure reduction postoperatively. There was no statistically significant difference in postimplantation seizure frequency based on seizure type. Prediction of response rate to RNS based on epileptogenic network would likely require a higher-powered study. Of the 16 patients who were on cenobamate, 10 patients were on the medication before RNS placement, and six patients were placed on the medication after RNS placement.

This study had several limitations including small sample size and lack of long-term follow-up in patients who have had recent implantation compared to patients who had implantation at the beginning of the data collection window. Notably, RNS outcomes have been postulated to improve up to 2 years out. Future directions for this body of research include investigation of optimal RNS lead location based on epileptogenic network and the targeting of thalamic, neocortical, or mesial temporal locations. Further long-term studies will need to determine if seizure reduction and responder rate results in halting or slowing the neurocognitive decline seen in DRE. Loring et al. studied neuropsychiatric outcomes following RNS implantation and found that there was no significant cognitive decline in patients who underwent RNS at a 2-year follow-up with some improvements in verbal learning in patients with seizure onset from the mesial temporal lobes.[16] It will also be important to continue to monitor the rates of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) in patients who underwent RNS compared to patients undergoing other forms of surgical intervention. Notably, there were no reported cases of SUDEP in our cohort throughout our study.

CONCLUSION

RNS is a reasonably safe, effective treatment for seizure reduction in patients with severe medically refractory epilepsy. While this study only entails 30 patients, the overall seizure reduction percentage is in line with the efficacy described within the literature. Further investigation with a larger sample size will be needed to determine if specific patients perform better with RNS than others.
