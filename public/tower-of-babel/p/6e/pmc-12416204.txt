The ABC type fucose operon regulated by XtrSs through CcpA contributes to Streptococcus suis survival in macrophages
Authors: Song Liang, Yumin Zhang, Shidan Zhang, Qiankun Bai, Xinming Pan, Jianan Liu, Qibing Gu, Huochun Yao, Guangjin Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStreptococcus suis (S. suis), an important zoonotic pathogen, poses a huge threat to the pig industry and human health. The fucose operon (FCS) is found in many bacteria that utilize host glycosylation modifications, contributing to bacterial growth and infection. We previously discovered that the virulence associated streptococcal transcriptional regulator XtrSs could downregulate FCS transcription. In this study, the FCSs of S. suis were classified into ABC and PTS types, and XtrSs indirectly repressed ABC type FCS transcription. Importantly, we found that Catabolite control protein A (CcpA) was the intermediate regulator of XtrSs, but not the FCS upstream regulator FcsR, directly repressing ABC type FCS transcription by binding to a novel Cre site. Interestingly, although ABC type FCS responded to fucose, S. suis failed to proliferate in media with fucose as the sole carbon source. Notably, FCS mutant treated macrophages exhibited decreased fucosyltransferases transcription and impaired AMPK/mTOR signaling to a certain extent, which resulted in increased autophagy processes and ultimately decreasing S. suis survival in macrophages. In conclusion, our findings first confirmed the detailed regulatory mechanism of ABC type FCS and revealed that FCS contributed to the intramacrophage survival of S. suis by inhibiting autophagy processes.KEYWORDS: S. suis, fucose operon, XtrSs, CcpA, intracellular survival, autophagy

Introduction

Streptococcus suis (S. suis) is a Gram-positive bacterial pathogen that causes substantial economic losses in the global swine industry [1,2]. Moreover, S. suis is recognized as an emerging zoonotic pathogen that can cause meningitis, endocarditis, arthritis, and even death in humans [3,4]. Based on its antigenicity to capsular polysaccharides (CPS), S. suis is classified into 29 classical serotypes and 28 new cps loci (NCL1 to NCL27 and Chz) [5]. S. suis possesses hundreds of virulence-associated genes coordinated with pathogenicity [6,7], and the transcriptional regulation of virulence-associated genes contributes to its ability to cope with complex infection landscapes [8,9]. Recent work by our laboratories has demonstrated that a novel XRE family transcriptional regulator, XtrSs [10], widely distributed in Streptococci, is associated with S. suis intracellular survival, and transcriptomic data have revealed that XtrSs remarkably regulate fucose operon (FCS) transcription [11]. The transcription of FCS is reported to be regulated by several regulators, such as the neighboring operon regulator (FcsR/FucR) [12], FCS internal component protein FucP (permease) [13], and cyclic adenosine monophosphate-binding protein (Crp) [14]. However, the exact mechanism of FCS transcriptional regulation in S. suis is poorly understood. At the same time, FCS is recognized as a virulence-associated factor in several bacteria [15,16], but has not yet been reported in S. suis. Therefore, the regulation of FCS transcription and how FCS affects the pathogenicity of S. suis require further investigation.

As an invasive pathogen, the first common barrier encountered by S. suis is the mucous membrane covered by a highly glycosylated mucus gel [17,18]. Fucose, as an essential component of mucosal O-glycans, not only contributes to the mucosal barrier [19], but is also utilized by commensal bacteria through gene clusters related to fucose utilization, contributing to their growth and infection [20,21], such as Campylobacter jejuni (C. jejuni) [22] and Escherichia coli (E. coli) [23]. Fucose utilization-related gene clusters usually exist in the operon FCS, which is divided into four main functional regions: operon regulator, glycan hydrolysis, carbohydrate transport, and fucose processing [24]. In Streptococcus pneumoniae (S. pneumoniae), FCS has been classified into ABC type and PTS type based on the genetic composition of carbohydrate transport [25], and ABC type FCS potentially processes host-derived fucosylated polysaccharides [26], whereas PTS-type FCS is currently unable to utilize fucose [27]. Nevertheless, the distributional characteristics of FCS and whether fucose can be processed by FCS in S. suis remain unclear.

During bacterial infection, autophagy represents a conserved eukaryotic process wherein macrophages generate phagosomes that encapsulate the bacteria, which subsequently fuse with lysosomes, ultimately leading to the destruction of bacteria [28,29]. Importantly, both extracellular and intracellular pathogenic bacteria are thought to affect autophagy processes to benefit their intracellular survivals [30,31]. Several studies have demonstrated that fucosyltransferases are responsible for host fucosylation, and elevated transcription of fucosyltransferases impairs autophagy processes [32,33]. However, the specific connection between the bacterial FCS and host cellular fucosyltransferases and the role of bacterial FCS in cellular autophagy remains unclear. Current published studies have focused on two independent aspects: one is the contribution of the FCS operon to bacteria carbon source utilization and pathogenicity, and the other is the effect of host fucosyltransferases on various biological processes, including autophagy. Therefore, in this study, we investigated whether FCS affects autophagy via fucosyltransferases ultimately improving intracellular survival of S. suis.

In this study, we demonstrated that ABC type FCS is widespread in S. suis and its transcription is indirectly regulated by XtrSs through Catabolite control protein A (CcpA). Moreover, while ABC type FCS failed to assist S. suis in gaining a growth advantage in vitro, it could affect macrophage fucosyltransferases transcription and AMPK/mTOR pathway autophagy, ultimately increasing the intramacrophage survival of S. suis. Based on these results, we could gain a better understanding of the transcriptional regulation and functions of FCS in S. suis pathogenesis.

Result

XtrSs indirectly repressed the transcription of the fucose operon

The previous RNAseq analysis of S. suis revealed that the transcription of the fucose-processing genes cluster (RS05855–RS05900) was repressed by XtrSs [11]. These nine genes (Table 1) were organized in the same operon by co-transcription assays (Figure 1(A)) and were composed of a complete FCS. Subsequently, RNA from S. suis WT and ΔxtrSs respectively incubated with blood were extracted and subjected to quantitative real-time fluorescence PCR (qRT-PCR) to quantify the transcript levels of FCS component genes. The results showed that compared to wildtype (WT), the transcript levels of all FCS component genes were significantly higher in ΔxtrSs (Figure 1(B)), consistent with previous transcriptome results [11]. The transcriptional start site (TSS) of the FCS was subsequently determined using 5’ rapid amplification of cDNA ends (RACE) PCR. The ribosomal binding site (RBS), along with the −10 and −35 boxes, was accordingly deduced and represented in different colors in Figure 1(C).

Furthermore, β-galactosidase activity assay and electrophoretic mobility shift analysis (EMSA) were performed to confirm the transcriptional regulation of FCS. First, we inserted the entire promoter region of FCS into the pTCV-lac plasmid, named Pfcs. Next, we introduced the Pfcs plasmid into WT, ΔxtrSs and CΔxtrSs, and the assay revealed that the β-galactosidase activity in ΔxtrSs was distinctly higher than in the WT and CΔxtrSs (Figure 1(D)), confirming that XtrSs inhibited FCS transcription. XtrSs forming dimer exerts regulatory roles [11], so we carried out EMSA using XtrSsdimer and a 250 nucleotide (nt) DNA fragment containing the entire FCS promoter. The results (Figure 1(E)) showed that the FCS promoter was not shifted by the XtrSsdimer whereas the positive and negative controls were valid. Collectively, these results suggested that XtrSs indirectly repressed FCS transcription.

The distribution of FCS in S. suis

Based on the FCS classification of S. pneumoniae [25], FCS in S. suis can also be classified as ABC type and PTS type according to the two kinds of transporter systems (Figure 2(A)). The amino acids of FCS were aligned with the published genomes of S. suis using TBlastN to characterize the distribution of FCS in S. suis. The results showed that 42.5% (45/106) of all completely sequenced strains (n = 106) contained intact FCS, with ABC type (77.8%, 35/45) as the major. Importantly, we found that 69.7% (92/132) of the definite clinically pathogenic S. suis strains (n = 92) contained FCS, with both ABC (30.4%, 28/92) and PTS types (54.3%, 50/92) (Figure 2(B)). In addition, we found that several S. suis strains contained two types of FCS simultaneously, and these strains were more prevalent in clinically pathogenic S. suis (derived from U.S. swine herds with neurologic disease; 15.3%, 14/92) than in completely sequenced S. suis (downloaded from the NCBI database; 8.9%, 4/45). In conclusion, these results emphasized that the presence of FCS was probably associated with S. suis pathogenicity, and our work in this study mainly focused on ABC type FCS.

FcsR indirectly promoted ABC type FCS transcription

As the nearest regulator of FCS, FcsR has been proven to be the FCS regulator in several bacteria [12,22], but has not yet been confirmed in S. suis. In S. suis, FcsR is conserved upstream of FCS. To verify whether FcsR exerts a regulatory function for FCS in S. suis, we constructed the fcsR knockout strain (ΔFR) and introduced Pfcs into WT and ΔFR. Compared to the WT, the β-galactosidase activity of ΔFR was reduced, suggesting that FcsR promoted S. suis ABC type FCS transcription (Figure S1A). Further, we expressed FcsR (Figure S1B) to investigate its affinity for the FCS promoter DNA sequence (250 bp) in vitro. The EMSA showed that FcsR was unable to bind directly to the FCS promoter, suggesting that FcsR could indirectly affect FCS transcription (Figure S1C). These results were similar to the role of FcsR in enterohemorrhagic Escherichia coli (EHEC) FCS transcription [15].

CcpA directly upregulated ABC type FCS transcription

Catabolite control protein A (CcpA) is widely reported to regulate central carbohydrate metabolic pathways in Gram-positive bacteria [34,35]. To verify whether CcpA is involved in regulating FCS transcription, we constructed a CcpA knock out strain (ΔCcpA) and complementation of CcpA (CΔCcpA) as well as introduced Pfcs into ΔCcpA and CΔCcpA. Compared to WT and CΔCcpA, the β-galactosidase activity of ΔCcpA was significantly decreased, suggesting that CcpA probably facilitated ABC type FCS transcription in S. suis (Figure 2(C)). Furthermore, CcpA was expressed (Figure 2(D)), and subsequent EMSA showed that CcpA could bind to the ABC type FCS promoter, indicating that CcpA directly regulated FCS transcription in S. suis (Figure 2E).

CcpA was a mediator protein for XtrSs in the transcriptional regulation of ABC type FCS

It was thought-provoking whether there was a potential relationship between CcpA and XtrSs in the transcription of ABC type FCS. Therefore, we first inserted the promoter sequence of CcpA into the pTCV-lac plasmid, named PCcpA, and inserted PCcpA into the WT, ΔxtrSs and CΔxtrSs. Interestingly, β-galactosidase activity was significantly higher in ΔxtrSs than in the WT and CΔxtrSs (Figure 3(A)), suggesting that XtrSs suppressed CcpA transcription. In addition, qRT-PCR results showed that the transcript level of CcpA was upregulated in ΔxtrSs compared with WT, which was consistent with the results of β-galactosidase activity assay (Figure S2A). Furthermore, EMSA revealed that XtrSs significantly blocked the CcpA promoter sequence in a concentration-dependent manner, with no significant effect observed in the negative control (Figure 3(B)). These results indicated that XtrSs directly repressed CcpA transcription, implying that XtrSs indirectly inhibited FCS transcription through CcpA.

CcpA regulated FCS transcription by binding a novel Cre motif

CcpA regulates many carbohydrate metabolism-associated genes by binding Cre motifs in Gram-positive bacteria [34,36]. The Cre motifs in S. suis have been reported as Cre1 (WWGAAARCGYTTTCWW) and Cre2 (TTTTYHWDHHWWTTTY) [35]. Using BlastN, we found that the ABC type FCS promoter contained the Cre1 sequence, corresponding to 80% identity of “ATGAAAGCGGTAACAG” and Cre2 sequence, 81% identity of “TTTTATGTCAATATT” (Figure 3(C)). To further elucidate the binding site of CcpA, we site-mutated the published CcpA targets, Cre1 and Cre2, and fused them with the pTCV-lac plasmid, named Cre1M and Cre2M (Figure 3(D)). These fusion plasmids were individually introduced into the WT, ΔxtrSs, and ΔCcpA. However, the β-galactosidase activity assay (Figure 3(E,G)) and EMSA (Figure 3(F,H)) confirmed that XtrSs and CcpA still indirectly and directly regulated FCS transcription, respectively. These results demonstrated that the binding site of CcpA on ABC type FCS was different from the published Cre sites.

To further identify the precise CcpA binding site, we performed a maximum likelihood phylogenetic analysis of the FCS promoter region. Interestingly, the FCS promoter region was categorized into two distinct groups, which is consistent with the two types of FCS (Figure 4(A)). We then found a long 23 bp palindromic sequence (5’-TATTTTGAATAATTTTCAATATA-3”) existed at all the upstream of the −35 box with a low diversity (green area), which was likely to be the CcpA binding site. Moreover, we used online software based on deep learning (https://github.com/Vceternal/BTFBS) [37] to predict CcpA binding sites, which also found this 23 bp sequence gained the highest score and was the most likely binding site for CcpA. Therefore, we broke the palindrome sequence with the remaining GC content unchanged (5”-TTTATTGTTAATATTTCTTTATA-3’) and named it TPfcs (Figure 4(B)). EMSA showed that CcpA failed to block TPfcs shifting, while the positive and negative controls were reliable, suggesting that this 23 bp palindrome sequence was a direct binding site for CcpA to regulate ABC type FCS transcription (Figure 4(C)). In addition, AlphaFold2 database was used for 3D modeling of CcpA (Figure S2B), and the highest C-score 3D models were selected to uploaded to the GalaxyWeb server for further refinement. Ramachandran plot scores showed that 96.7% of residues were present in the most favorable regions of refined CcpA (Figure S2C), indicating a higher accuracy of the 3D models. Subsequent molecular docking showed that CcpA interacted with this 23 bp binding sequence in the highest-scoring model (Figure S2D), but not with TPfcs (data not shown), which is consistent with the EMSA results (Figure 4(C)). Furthermore, TPfcs was inserted into the pTCV-lac plasmid and introduced into WT, ΔxtrSs and ΔCcpA, named WT-TPfcs, ΔxtrSs-TPfcs and ΔCcpA-TPfcs, respectively. Interestingly, we observed that the β-galactosidase activity in WT-TPfcs was obviously reduced compared to that in WT-Pfcs, and with no significant difference when compared to that in ΔxtrSs-TPfcs and ΔCcpA-TPfcs (Figure 4(D)). In addition, we constructed ΔxtrSsΔCcpA double mutant strain and introduced the Pfcs recombinant plasmid, following the results showed that the β-galactosidase activities of ΔCcpA-Pfcs and ΔxtrSsΔCcpA-Pfcs were significantly lower compared with ΔxtrSs-Pfcs, and there was no significant difference between these two strains (Figure 4(E)). Moreover, there was no significant difference in the transcriptional levels of ΔCcpA-Pfcs and ΔCcpA-TPfcs (Figure S2E). These results showed that XtrSs failed to regulate ABC type FCS when CcpA was unable to promote ABC type FCS transcription, confirming that CcpA was an enforced factor for XtrSs to inhibit ABC type FCS transcription in S. suis by binding to this 23 bp sequence.

Further, we compared the β-galactosidase activity of WT-Pfcs, WT-TPfcs, WT-Cre1M and WT-Cre2M. The results showed that β-galactosidase activity was significantly decreased in the WT-TPfcs group compared to WT-Pfcs, whereas there was no significant change in WT-Cre1M and WT-Cre2M (Figure 4(F)). Subsequently, the homologous binding sites of all strains were used by the MEME website to create the logo, summarized as 5’-WWTWWWKMWYRAWWTYCAWTHKW-3,’ which was also identified as a novel Cre motif bound by CcpA to regulate metabolic pathways (Figure 4(G)). Moreover, we found that the PTS type FCS promoter region also contained the highly conserved sequences, with 69.5% identity to this novel Cre motif (Figure 4(H)). This suggested that CcpA possibly regulated not only ABC type, but also PTS type FCS transcription by binding to this novel Cre motif. In summary, these results confirmed that XtrSs inhibited CcpA transcription, and that CcpA ultimately repressed ABC-type FCS transcription by binding to the 23bp palindrome sequence (Figure 4(I)).

The transcription of S. suis ABC type FCS specifically responds to exogenous fucose

To investigate whether ABC type FCS responds to exogenous fucose, we first stimulated WT with different sugars and quantified the transcription level of genes by qRT-PCR. The results showed that only fucose significantly upregulated the transcription of ABC type FCS, but not glucose, fructose, and galactose (Figure 5(A)). In addition, when the WT was exposed to fucose, all genes of the ABC type FCS transcriptional levels were significantly higher than those without fucose (Figure S3), but not ΔCcpA (Figure 5(B)). These results demonstrated that the ABC type FCS of S. suis specifically responds to exogenous fucose, while the regulator CcpA played a key role. Interestingly, xtrSs transcription was downregulated (Figure 5(C)), and CcpA transcription was upregulated (Figure 5(D)) in response to exogenous fucose. Minor transcriptional changes in CcpA cause significant transcriptional differences in its target genes [34]. In conclusion, these results further confirmed that in the exogenous presence of fucose, S. suis could inhibit xtrSs transcription and thus upregulated CcpA, ultimately remarkably facilitating ABC type FCS transcription (Figure 4(I)).

S. suis unable to utilize fucose as the sole carbon source

The ABC type FCS shared high identity between S. suis and S. pneumoniae, for instance, ABC transporter substrate-binding protein (SBP), carbohydrate ABC transporter permease 1 (ABC1), and carbohydrate ABC transporter permease 2 (ABC2) formed the ABC type transport system of FCS, with 69.8%, 82.2%, and 86.1% identity, respectively (Figure S4A). Thus, we propose that ABC type FCS in S. suis acts in a sequential manner for processing fucose, similar to S. pneumoniae (Figure S4B). The transport system utilizes ATP to transport fucosylated glycans into the cell, and the glycan hydrolysis enzymes GH36A and GH29 catalyze the hydrolysis of these glycans to release fucose. Subsequently, the enzymes fucose isomerase (FcsI), fucose kinase (FcsK), and fucose-phosphate aldolase (FcsA) function synergistically to convert fucose into lactaldehyde and dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP), which are efficiently integrated into the glycolytic pathway to produce ATP. In summary, these coordinated enzymatic activities enabled S. suis to consume environmental fucose.

Next, we explored the effect of fucose on the S. suis growth. Intact FCS deletion strains (ΔFucose) and WT strains were grown in M17 supplemented with or without fucose, respectively. The terminal optical density of the bacteria in the medium containing fucose was similar to that in the medium without fucose (Figure 5(E)). In addition, we replaced equal amounts of the strains from the THB culture in the early log phase with no sugar DMEM, with or without added fucose, respectively (Figure 5(F)). The results showed that all strains were unable to survive in medium with fucose as the sole carbon source (Figure 5(G)), indicating that fucose was unable to sustain the growth of S. suis. Our evidence demonstrated that although S. suis has proper regulation of fucose exposure, S. suis cannot obtain growth benefits in the case of fucose as the sole carbon source.

ABC type FCS promoted cellular autophagy to improve intracellular survival of S. suis

FCS is known to contribute to various bacterial infections in the host, such as C. jejuni [22] and S. pneumoniae [16]. Therefore, we also performed intracellular survival experiments to investigate whether the ABC type FCS affected the survival of S. suis in macrophages RAW264.7. First, no significant difference in phagocytosis was observed between the WT and ΔFucose strains, indicating that the ABC type FCS had no notable effect on the RAW264.7 phagocytosis of S. suis (Figure S5A). Second, the intracellular survival of WT was significantly higher than that of ΔFucose (Figure 6(A)), which proved that S. suis could enhance its intracellular survival in macrophages through ABC type FCS.

Autophagy facilitates host clearance of harmful materials and reduces the intracellular viability of pathogens [38]. To determine whether S. suis could trigger autophagy in macrophages, we observed the ultrastructure of autophagosomes in RAW264.7 after S. suis infection by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), an indispensable tool for assessing autophagy. Rapamycin, an autophagy activator, was used as the positive control. TEM results showed that a pattern characteristic of the induction of autophagy (double-membrane autophagosomes and multiple membrane structures) [39] appeared in RAW264.7, after both WT and rapamycin treatments, indicating that S. suis could cause autophagy in RAW264.7 (Figure 6(C)). To further explore the role played by ABC type FCS in RAW264.7, autophagy-related proteins were detected by Western blotting. Importantly, the protein level of LC3-II in ΔFucose was higher than that in WT (Figure 6(B)). In addition, colocalization of LC3-II in different S. suis similarly showed that ABC type FCS was negatively associated with autophagy occurrence of RAW264.7 (Figure 6(D)). In summary, S. suis could stimulate macrophage autophagy, whereas ABC type FCS exhibited an inhibitory effect.

ABC type FCS stimulated fucosyltransferase transcription for promoting cellular autophagy associated with the AMPK/mTOR pathway

Several fucosyltransferases have been reported to regulate mTOR activity to coordinate the autophagic process [33]. We examined the transcript levels of several types of fucosyltransferases in macrophages after WT and ΔFucose interactions, respectively. The results showed 20-, 2.5-, 6-, and 3-fold transcriptional upregulation of FUT1, FUT4, FUT7, and FUT8, respectively, in the WT group compared to the ΔFucose group, whereas there was no significant difference in FUT2 transcription (Figure 6(E)). These results implied that S. suis ABC type FCS could significantly affect macrophage fucosyltransferase transcription levels, especially FUT1. As transcriptional changes in fucosyltransferase can affect mTOR activity, we detected the expression of phosphorylated mTOR (p-mTOR) and its upstream protein phosphorylated AMPK (p-AMPK). Figure 6(F) showed that p-mTOR was downregulated while p-AMPK was upregulated in the ΔFucose group compared to the WT group. Our results showed S. suis could stimulated cellular fucosyltransferase transcription through ABC type FCS to promote macrophage autophagy, and also proved that the macrophage fucosyltransferase transcription levels and autophagy process were negatively correlated, which was consistent with the findings of Yu et al. [33].

To further confirm S. suis could influence autophagy through the AMPK/mTOR pathway, the AMPK inhibitor Compound C was used to suppress autophagy in macrophage RAW264.7. The cytotoxicity assay (Figure S5B) and WB (Figure S5C) results revealed that 8 µM of Compound C was suitable for subsequent treatments. There was no significant difference in phagocytosis rate (Figure S5D), the expression of LC3-II (Figure S5E) and intracellular survival (Figure 6(G)) between WT and ΔFucose in macrophages after Compound C treatment, suggesting that after the inhibition of AMPK mediated autophagy, S. suis could not rely on ABC type FCS to increase intracellular survival. In conclusion, our results suggested that S. suis ABC type FCS stimulated cellular fucosyltransferases transcription, thereby contributing to S. suis escape macrophage killing, which was associated with a decline in autophagy processes through the AMPK/mTOR pathway.

Discussion

To date, FCS has been shown to be relevant in many highly invasive pathogenic bacteria to exert a complete pathogenic effect, but little is known in S. suis. Herein, we elucidated the common distribution of FCS in S. suis and focused on ABC type FCS as the main object in this study. We elaborated on the transcriptional regulatory mechanism of ABC type FCS in S. suis and preliminarily demonstrated that ABC type FCS could inhibit the autophagy of macrophages, thus greatly enhancing the intracellular survival of S. suis.

FcsR is located upstream of FCS and is known to regulate FCS transcription [12]. The FcsR homologue FucR can directly regulate operon transcription in Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron by DNA footprint analysis [40], whereas FusR indirectly controls FCS transcription in EHEC by EMSA [15]. In S. pneumoniae, FcsR can upregulate FCS transcription, and the binding sites were identified by a β-galactosidase activity assay [12]. Similarly, in our study, FcsR contributed to FCS transcription by β-galactosidase activity assay, but further visualized EMSA showed that FcsR failed to bind to the FCS promoter region (Figure S1), challenging FcsR to directly regulate FCS transcription. Furthermore, CcpA, which is responsible for central metabolic pathway regulation in Gram-positive bacteria, was identified as a key protein in regulating ABC type FCS transcription in this study. CcpA usually regulates target gene transcription by binding to Cre sites, but we found that CcpA was unable to regulate S. suis ABC type FCS by binding the two reported Cre sites. Subsequently, we identified a novel Cre site that was conserved in both types of FCS, and confirmed that CcpA, but not FcsR, directly upregulated S. suis FCS transcription. In addition, we also found that our negative transcriptional regulator, XtrSs, could directly control CcpA transcription and indirectly affect ABC type FCS transcription, indicating that S. suis developed complex regulatory mechanisms in response to variable nutrient environments.

Fucoses are widely present in mammalian digestive and respiratory tracts [19]. A few gastrointestinal pathogens can promote growth via fucose utilization and subsequently increase their competitive advantage against commensal bacteria in the host gut. For example, C. jejuni uses FCS homologues (cj0480c – cj0490) to metabolize fucose as the sole carbon source, thereby increasing its growth advantage in the host intestine [22]. EHEC relies on a fucose-sensing response regulator and fucose-sensing histidine kinase to form a two-component signaling system that regulates EHEC pathogenicity and metabolism [15]. Inconsistently, S. pneumoniae and S. pasteurii containing FCS were reported to be unable to grow in the presence of fucose as the sole carbon source [27,41], which was consistent with our findings in S. suis. It is easy to understand that Streptococci are fastidious bacteria that require complex nutrients (such as specific amino acids, vitamins, blood, or serum, etc.) for growth and naturally utilize preferring sugars such as glucose. Further studies revealed that ABC type FCS could assist S. pneumoniae adhesion by binding host fucosylated glycan [26], reflecting the non-metabolic function of ABC type FCS. Based on the high homology of ABC type FCS in S. suis and S. pneumoniae (Figure S4A), it is reasonable to hypothesize that ABC type FCS also performs a similar non-metabolic function in S. suis.

The FCS in both S. suis and S. pneumoniae can be categorized into ABC type and PTS type according to the transporter system. The ABC type FCS transporter system is an ABC transporter system consisting of solute-binding protein and two permease components (1 and 2), whereas the PTS type FCS transporter system is a phosphoenolpyruvate-dependent phosphotransferase system [26,27]. The absence of PTS type FCS in S. pneumoniae severely impairs the bacterium’s ability to cause acute respiratory disease in a mouse intranasal infection model [16]. Furthermore, subsequent study showed that PTS type FCS did not contribute to the adhesion of S. pneumoniae in the host nasopharyngeal, suggesting that PTS type FCS may be more prominent in S. pneumoniae invading the host [16]. Meanwhile, S. pneumoniae ABC type FCS binds fucosylated glycan on soluble blood group antigens and contribute to S. pneumoniae adhesion demonstrating a functional difference from PTS type FCS [26]. The functions of S. suis FCS were not reported yet. Based on the high homology of FCS in S. suis and S. pneumoniae (Figure S4A), it is worth exploring and we will verify the effects of PTS type FCS and ABC type FCS on invasion and adhesion of S. suis to the host in the future.

Eukaryotic fucosyltransferases (FUT) are currently categorized into 13 types (FUT1–11 and POFUT1, 2), among which FUT1, FUT2 and FUT8 were reported to be associated with autophagy processes [42]. Interestingly, we discovered that S. suis with the ABC type FCS suppressed autophagy and upregulated various types of FUT in macrophages, including FUT1, FUT4, FUT7, and FUT8 (Figure 6(E)). It is reported that AMPK involved in FUT2 upregulates autophagy [43]. Interestingly, despite no significant difference in FUT2 transcription between WT and ΔFucose-treated cells, the expression of p-AMPK was also significantly upregulated in WT-treated cells. In addition, FUT1, but not FUT2, regulates the fucosylation of the lysosomal membrane proteins LAMP-1 and 2 affecting lysosomal perinuclear localization, which consequently upregulates p-mTOR activity ultimately inhibiting autophagy [33]. Consistently, macrophages incubated with S. suis WT showed both FUT1 and p-mTOR upregulation, and downregulation of autophagy (Figure 6(F)), confirming that the AMPK/mTOR pathway participated in autophagy affected by S. suis. Additionally, the expression of FUT8 was upregulated threefold in macrophages treated with wild-type, and autophagy was suppressed (Figure 6), which is consistent with the report that silencing FUT8 leads to enhanced autophagy [32]. To our knowledge, the specific molecular mechanisms regarding bacterial ABC type FCS affecting host cellular autophagy remain unclear. In our study, ABC type FCS promoted multiple fucosyltransferases transcription and ultimately inhibited host autophagy via the AMPK/mTOR pathway. Honestly, other signaling pathways may also be involved in these processes, so we will endeavor to identify and knock down the dominant fucosyltransferase associated with S. suis FCS to fully elucidate the mechanism of S. suis FCS affecting host autophagy.

In summary, this study demonstrated the transcriptional regulation and the utilization of host glycosylation modifications of S. suis ABC type FCS. In addition, we highlighted that ABC type FCS can upregulate macrophage fucosyltransferases to inhibit autophagy, ultimately helping S. suis to evade macrophage killing. More importantly, this study revealed the potential pathogenic effect of S. suis ABC type FCS and shed new light on the role of the fucose operon in the infection process of pathogenic bacteria.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains, growth conditions, and cell culture

All strains included in this study were listed in Table S1. The highly invasive S. suis strain CZ130302 was isolated from the brain of a diseased piglet in Changzhou, China, in 2013 and has since been maintained in our laboratory [44]. S. suis were cultured in Todd-Hewitt broth (Hopebio, Qingdao, China) or on THB agar plates supplemented with 5% (v/v) sheep blood (Maojie, Nanjing, China) at 37°C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere, unless otherwise stated. Low-nutrient M17 medium [12] (Hopebio, Qingdao, China) and DMEM were used to monitor growth curves and prepare samples for the corresponding qRT-PCR assays. Additionally, 100 μg/mL spectinomycin or 10% (w/v) sucrose was used to screen mutant strains. The RAW264.7 cell line was cultured at 37°C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere in DMEM culture medium (Gibco, Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Invitrogen).

Co-transcriptional analysis

To determine CVO91_RS05860-CVO91_RS05900 were organized in an intact fucose operon, co-transcriptional analyses were performed as previously reported, with minor change [45]. In brief, total bacterial RNA was extracted during the log phase using TRIzol (Vazyme, China), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA was divided into two aliquots: one was reverse-transcribed into cDNA, while the other was retained as a negative control. Primers were designed to span CVO91_RS05860-CVO91_RS05865, CVO91_RS05865-CVO91_RS05870, CVO91_RS05870-CVO91_RS05875, CVO91_RS05875-CVO91_RS05880, CVO91_RS05880-CVO91_RS05885, CVO91_RS05885-CVO91_RS05890, CVO91_RS05890-CVO91_RS05895, CVO91_RS05895-CVO91_RS05900 (Table S2).

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

The detailed procedures for the qRT-PCR analysis have been described in our previous study [11]. To simulate in vivo conditions of S. suis-infected hosts, bacteria were lightly mixed 1:10 with fresh mouse whole blood (the Animal Center of Yangzhou University, China) containing 0.4% sodium citrate at a final concentration of 2 × 10 [7] CFU/mL. The samples were incubated at 37°C for 1.5 h with gentle mixing every 15 min.

In addition, S. suis was grown to log phase in M17 with or without 1% sugar to validate the transcript levels of multiple genes under different sugar conditions. Subsequently, bacterial precipitates from the different treatments were collected for RNA extraction using a method similar to co-transcription analysis. Total RNA was reverse transcribed to synthesize cDNA using the HiScript II cDNA synthesis kit (Vazyme, China). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using cDNA as the template on a QuantStudio 6 Flex apparatus (Thermo Fisher Scientific, China), with ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China) to verify the transcript levels of the selected genes. The transcription level of the housekeeping gene, GAPDH, was used as an internal control to normalize the expression of the target genes.

To assess the transcript levels of fucosyltransferases in macrophages, RAW264.7 cells were infected with either WT or ΔFucose (MOI 1:10) and subsequently incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 1.5 h. Each group was washed three times with DMEM, followed by a 3 h incubation with lysozyme (50 μg/mL) and vancomycin (5 μg/mL). Subsequently, the cells were treated with the TRIzol reagent for sample collection. Refer to the description provided in this section for the subsequent steps. The relative fold change in gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. At least three biological replicates were performed for each sample. Primers used in this study were listed in Table S2.

Rapid amplification of cDNA ends

The TSS of FCS was determined using the TransScript 5“/3” rapid amplification of cDNA ends (RACE) kit (TransGen Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA was extracted from WT cells cultured in the logarithmic phase within THB fluid, as previously described. Approximately 1 μg of RNA was reverse transcribed using gene-specific primers, and PCR was used to generate PCR products. The purified fragment was ligated to a linearized Blunt vector (TransGen Biotech). The 5’ end of the FCS was identified through sequencing (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) and subsequent comparison with the CZ130302 genome. The primers used are listed in Table S2.

β-galactosidase assay promoter activity

S. suis genomic DNA was used as a template, and recombinant plasmids were constructed by amplifying different promoter fragments using suitable primers for natural transformation into appropriate strains for the assay (Table S2). The β-galactosidase assay was performed according to previous reports, with minor changes [11]. Briefly, 400 μL of log-phase culture, 8 μL of lysozyme (2.5 mg/mL), and 25 μL of 4×Z buffer (containing 14 μL β-mercaptoethanol) were mixed and incubated at 37°C for 25 min. Each sample was subsequently combined with 400 μL of 1×Z buffer (containing 2.7 μL of β-mercaptoethanol and 2 mg of o-nitrophenyl β-D-galactopyranoside, ONPG) and incubated in a 37°C water bath until complete yellowing was observed. The reaction was terminated by the addition of 400 μL 1 M Na2CO3. The samples were then centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected for OD420 measurements. The β-galactosidase activity of the strains containing reporter plasmids was calculated using the following equation: (A420 ×1000 × VE)/(RT × VS × OD600) (RT, reaction time; VE, end volume; VS, volume of each sample). At least three biological replicates of each strain were tested for each experiment.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

The promoter fragments of the target genes and 16S rRNA genes were amplified from the WT strain genome using 2 × Rapid Taq Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and purified using the FastPure Gel DNA Extraction Mini Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). DNA probes and recombinant proteins were incubated in a 20 µL reaction mixture containing 10 mM Tris, 50 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 0.05% Triton X-100 and 2.5% glycerol (pH 7.5) for 30 minutes at 37°C, followed by electrophoresis on 6% native polyacrylamide gels in 0.5 × TB buffer (44.5 mM Tris-base, 44.5 mM boric acid, pH 7.5) at 200 V for 45 minutes. The gels were stained with Goldview (Biosharp, China) for 10 min, followed by scanning with a ChemiDoc XRS+ system (Bio-Rad, USA). The primers used in this study were listed in Table S2.

Bioinformatics analysis

To characterize the distribution of FCS in S. suis, the completely sequenced (12 July 2022) and reportedly clinically pathogenic S. suis genomes [46] were downloaded from the published NCBI database. The protein sequence of CZ130302 FCS served as the template for identifying homologous proteins through local TBlastN on the S. suis genome, which met 60% coverage and 50% identity were deemed homologous. Additionally, utilizing the transporter systems of CZ130302 (ABC type FCS) (CP024974.1) and ISU2514 (PTS type FCS) (CP030020) as templates, searching for homologous proteins by TBlastN were used to confirm the distribution frequency of FCS.

To identify the regulated sites, the FCS promoter regions of 45 completely sequenced S. suis strains were phylogenetically analyzed using the MEGA X neighbor-joining (NJ) method. We used the online website (http://palindromes.ibp.cz/#/en/palindrome) to predict the palindromic sequences of all FCS promoter regions. The MEME database (https://meme-suite.org/meme/) was employed to generate promoter consensus sequences and to generate DNA sequence logos.

Construction of mutant strains

A series of mutant strains were generated through natural transformation [47]. Initially, the upstream and downstream target genes, along with the sacB-spc cassette, were independently amplified and purified. Subsequently, these three fragments were ligated using fusion PCR and introduced into the parental strain via a specific signal peptide. Using spectinomycin, we screened for gene replacement-marked mutants (traced mutants). Furthermore, the fusion homologous fragment, lacking the sacB-spc cassette, was introduced into the traced mutant for the second round of transformation. Subsequently, 10% sucrose was used to select for traceless strains. The primers employed in this study were listed in Table S2.

Protein purification, modeling and molecular docking

XtrSs dimer protein purification was performed as described previously published studies [11]. Moreover, the CcpA and FcsR fragments were PCR-amplified from the DNA of S. suis CZ130302. The amplified fragments were then cloned into the pET28a(+) vector by BamHI and SacI digestion and ligation, resulting in the generation of the CcpA-pET28a(+) and FcsR-pET28a(+) plasmids. The plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) cells, which were cultured in Luria broth (LB) to the log phase, induced with 1 mM Isopropyl-β-D-Thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 5 h. Protein purification was then performed using a His Ni high-performance chromatography column (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). The purified proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE on 12.5% gels (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and subsequently stained with Coomassie Blue. A pre-stained marker (Vazyme, MP102) was used to determine the size of the proteins. The protein concentration was quantified using a BCA kit (TaKaRa, Beijing, China).

The 3D model of CcpA was created using AlphaFlod2 (https://colab.research.google.com/github/sokrypton/ColabFold/blob/main/AlphaFold2.ipynb) [48]. Furthermore, the models with high C-scores were uploaded to the GalaxyRefine server (https://galaxy.seoklab.org/) for refinement [49]. A Ramachandran plot analysis (saves. mbi.ucla.edu/) was used to evaluate the refined 3D model [50]. The HDOCK SERVER online site (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/) was used for molecular docking [51]. Pymol was used to visualize protein 3D structures and optimize molecular docking pattern maps.

Growth characteristics analysis

To precisely analyze the effect of fucose on the growth of S. suis, we used low-nutrient medium M17 12. WT and ΔFucose were grown in M17 medium to mid-exponential phase (OD600 0.4–0.6) and then transferred 1:100 to M17 medium supplemented with 1% fucose (Macklin, China) and M17 medium, respectively. The cultures were then incubated at 37°C shaking at 180 rpm. Growth curves were obtained by measuring the OD600 every two hours until the growth phase transitioned into the decline phase. Each strain was performed at least three independent experiments.

Sugar-free DMEM (Solarbio, China) was used to further investigate the effect of fucose on S. suis growth. Strains WT and ΔFucose, cultured in THB (OD600 0.6–0.8), were collected by centrifugation and subsequently washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, equal amounts of sugar-free DMEM and sugar-free DMEM containing 1% fucose were added. The cultures were incubated and measured OD600 according to the above conditions, and at least three independent trials were performed.

Intracellular survival assay

Intracellular survival was performed using RAW264.7 cells as described previously, with minor modifications [11]. RAW264.7 cells were grown in high glucose DMEM to achieve 80% confluence at 37°C, 5% CO2. Compound C (8 μM) was added if needed for 2 h before cell infection to inhibit the autophagy process. Following infection with WT or ΔFucose (MOI 1:10), RAW264.7 cells were centrifuged at 800 g for 10 min at 25°C and subsequently divided into two groups. The plates were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 1.5 h and each group was washed three times with DMEM, followed by the addition of lysozyme (50 μg/mL) and vancomycin (5 μg/mL). After 1 h of incubation, 0.25% trypsin-EDTA was added to one group and lysed with 900 μL of sterile deionized water. The initial phagocytic rate was recorded for this group. The other group was subjected to the above procedure of lysing cells after 3 h of incubation. All cell mixtures were diluted in THB plates for counting. Relative phagocytosis rate and intracellular viability were used to evaluate the degree of bacterial survival. The experiment was repeated thrice with three independent replicates.

Transmission electron microscopy

The processing steps for RAW264.7 and bacterial interactions were referenced the “Intracellular Survival” section. After 3 h, the samples were harvested with trypsin, washed three times with sterile PBS, and subsequently fixed in a buffer containing 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 24 h. The samples were then post-fixed for 1 hour in a solution of 1% osmium tetroxide and 0.5% potassium ferricyanide, prepared in cacodylate buffer. The specimens were then dehydrated with ethanol, embedded in resin, and polymerized by curing at 80°C for 24 h. Ultrathin sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and subsequently analyzed using a Hitachi HT7800 transmission electron microscope.

Western blot

The processing steps for RAW264.7 and bacterial interactions were referenced the “Intracellular Survival” section. Compound C (8 μM) was added if needed for 2 h before cell infection to inhibit the autophagy process. After 3 hours of treatment with antibiotics or rapamycin (5 μM), the cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and subsequently lysed in sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) sample buffer (Beyotime, China) on ice. Equal amounts of total protein were resolved by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were then incubated with the corresponding polyclonal antibodies and secondary antibodies. The antibodies used were LC3B (Abmart, Shanghai), AMPK (Abmart, Shanghai), p-AMPK (Abmart, Shanghai), mTOR (Abmart, Shanghai), p-mTOR (Abmart, Shanghai), Tubulin (Abmart, Shanghai) and β-actin (Abmart, Shanghai). Signals were detected using an ECL+ reagent substrate (Tanon, China).

The processing steps for RAW264.7 and bacterial interactions were referenced the “Intracellular Survival” section. After 3 h, the samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and washed three times with PBS at room temperature. The cells were then treated with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 15 min to enhance cell membrane permeability. The cells were then blocked with 5% BSA for 1 hour and incubated with the LC3B antibody, followed by the appropriate secondary antibody. Finally, the nuclei were stained with DAPI (1:500, Beyotime) and the cells were immediately visualized using a Nikon A1 microscope (Japan).

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) cytotoxicity assay

The cells were seeded in 24-well plates according to the previously described protocol. After the gradient addition of Compound C, the supernatant was collected and analyzed for cytotoxicity using the Lactate Dehydrogenase Release Assay Kit (C0016, Beyotime), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Lastly, absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA), and background absorbance was corrected at 630 nm.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA). All experiments were repeated at least thrice. Statistical methods for all experiments were described in detail in the corresponding figure legends. For all tests, a p value of less than 0.05 is considered statistically significant (ns p ≥ 0.05, * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, **** p < 0.0001).
