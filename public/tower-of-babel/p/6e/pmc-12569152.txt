OSMRβ-mediated signals on resident fibroblasts restore healing in diabetic skin wounds through promoting angiogenesis and granulation tissue formation
Authors: Yuko Ishida, Yumi Kuninaka, Tadasuke Komori, Sadahiro Iwabuchi, Mizuho Nosaka, Akihiko Kimura, Shinichi Hashimoto, Yoshihiro Morikawa, Naofumi Mukaida, Toshikazu Kondo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOncostatin M (OSM) has various biological activities through OSM receptor β (OSMRβ) in many conditions such as inflammation and tissue regeneration, but it remains elusive on its precise roles in skin wound healing. Especially, diabetic mice showed impaired wound healing with suppressed expression of OSM and OSMRβ. Here we show the pathophysiological roles of OSM-OSMRβ axis in skin wound healing utilizing wild-type (WT) and Osmrb−/− mice. Excisional wounds induced OSM expression in infiltrating neutrophils and macrophages and OSMRβ expression in fibroblasts. Compared with WT mice, Osmrb−/− mice exhibited delayed wound healing despite a similar extent of leukocyte infiltration. The analysis on bone marrow chimeric mice revealed that OSMRβ-expressing resident cells contributed to wound healing process. The absence of OSMRβ suppressed the expression of TIMP-1 and HGF in fibroblasts, which are associated with reduction in collagen deposition and neovascularization, respectively. Moreover, OSM induced TIMP-1 and HGF expression in a murine fibroblast cell line with MAP activation. Actually, topical OSM application in diabetic wounds enhanced the expression of HGF and TIMP-1 at the wound sites, eventually resulting in accelerated wound healing. Thus, the OSM-OSMRβ axis and/or its downstream signaling pathways may be a novel therapeutic target to treat diabetic skin wounds.Subject terms: Medical research, Experimental models of disease

Introduction

Skin wound healing is a highly complicated process consisting of leukocyte infiltration, fibroplasia, angiogenesis, and neo-epithelialization, and is tightly controlled by dynamic gene expression changes at the wound site. Leukocyte infiltration is followed by transient activation of dermal fibroblasts, which proliferate and produce a large quantity of extracellular matrix to repair skin wound. Transient fibroblast activation is presumed to be regulated by a variety of cytokines produced by infiltrating platelets, monocytes, T lymphocytes, and other inflammation-associated cells1. Diabetic conditions impaired multistep of skin wound healing, eventually resulting in severe retardation of the healing process2. Oncostatin M (OSM) is a 28-kDa glycoprotein originally isolated based on its inhibitory activity of tumor cell growth3. It is produced by activated monocytes, T lymphocytes, and polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMN)3–6, and has multiple biological activities such as modulation of proliferation, embryonic development, differentiation, inflammation, and regeneration of various tissues. OSM is further known to regulate the growth of various types of cells, such as fibroblasts7, vascular smooth muscle cells8, and Kaposi’s sarcoma cells9.

OSM belongs to the IL-6 family (gp130-dependent cytokines), which includes interleukin (IL)-6, IL-11, leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF), ciliary neurotrophic factor (CNF), and cardiotrophin 1. Specific receptors for these cytokines are composed of gp130 signaling subunit as well as additional ligand-specific chains10. OSM receptor subunit β (OSMRβ), a member of the signal transducing receptors for IL-6 type cytokines11, was identified as OSM-specific receptor component. Subsequent studies revealed that biologic activities of OSM are mediated by its receptor complex composed of gp130 unit and OSMRβ12–14. The OSMRβ subunit is widely expressed in various cell types and tissues, including epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and skin tissue12. Of note is that OSM can stimulate mitogenic responses of dermal fibroblasts and dermal endothelial cells15,16, and extravasation of PMN into skin tissue after subcutaneous application17. OSM was further reported to be a mitogen for murine NIH3T3 cells and human foreskin and synovial fibroblasts18. Moreover, OSM can induce the production of extracellular matrix (ECM) components such as collagen and glycosaminoglycan19,20. Furthermore, OSM has been reported to stimulate the synthesis of tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases (TIMPs) 1 and 321,22, the molecules that are presumed to be involved in collagen metabolism. Thus, OSM may have a role in skin response to external stimuli, particularly fibrotic changes. Consistently, transgenic mice overexpressing OSM in a tissue-specific manner exhibited a tissue-specific visceral fibrosis23. However, it still remains elusive on the pathophysiological roles of the OSM-OSMRβ axis in skin wound healing.

We investigated the role of the OSM-OSMRβ axis in skin wound healing in normal and diabetic mice and revealed that the suppression of OSM-OSMRβ signaling resulted in delayed wound healing in diabetic mice. In the next series, we examined wound healing process using Osmrb-deficient mice. Although the genetic disruption of OSMRβ had few influence on early leukocyte infiltration after wounding, Osmrb-deficient mice demonstrated the impairment of angiogenesis and granulation tissue formation with reduced expression of HGF and TIMP-1 on fibroblasts, eventually resulting in delayed skin wound healing. Furthermore, topical administration of OSM to diabetic wounds restored expression of HGF and TIMP-1, eventually accelerating wound healing. These observations suggest that molecular therapy targeting OSM may be a promising therapeutic strategy for difficult-to-heal wounds such as diabetic skin ulcers.

Results

Involvement of the OSM-OSMRβ axis in skin wound healing

When m+/db as control and db/db mice were injured as described in the section of Methods, diabetic mice showed significant retardation of wound healing, compared with m+/db one. Moreover, there was no significant difference in wound healing process between m+/db and WT (C57BL/6) mice (Fig. S1). Thus, as reported previously24–26, WT mice were used as a control in the subsequent analyses. The gene expression of Osm and Osmr at the wound sites was up-regulated in both strains (WT and db/db mice); those enhancements were significantly suppressed in db/db mice, compared with WT ones (Fig. 1A–D). These observations implied that delayed wound healing in diabetic mice might be attributable to the suppression of the OSM-OSMRβ axis. In the next series, we immunohistochemically examined OSM and OSMRβ expression at skin wound sites. OSM protein was detected in myeloperoxidase (MPO)+ neutrophils and F4/80+ cells (Fig. 1E, F), while OSMRβ was expressed by α-SMA+ myofibroblasts (Fig. 1G). Moreover, most F4/80+ cells at the wound sites were CD11b+ cells (Fig. S2A), which expressed OSM (Fig. S2B). On the contrary, pan-cytokeratin+ epithelial cells did not express OSM and OSMRβ (Figs. S2, C, D). In order to clarify the roles of the OSM-OSMRβ axis in skin wound healing, we made excisional skin wounds on both WT and Osmrb-/− mice. In WT mice, wound areas were reduced ceaselessly until 14 days after the injury, resulting in more than 95% closure by 14 days after injury (Fig. 1H, I). Wound healing, however, was significantly delayed in Osmrb−/− mice at 6 and 10 days, compared with WT ones. Wound closure was significantly reduced in Osmrb−/− mice, but histological analysis revealed no significant difference in re-epithelialization between WT and Osmrb−/− mice (Fig. S3). Consistently, the administration of neutralizing anti-OSM Abs delayed the wound healing rates, compared with control IgG-treated WT mice (the time required to close wound by 45% was 6 vs. 10 days in control IgG vs. anti-OSM-injected WT mice, respectively) (Fig. 1J, K). Collectively, these observations indicated that the lack of the OSM-OSMRβ axis in skin would impair wound healing in mice.

Gene expression profile in the wound tissues

Bulk RNA sequencing analysis (RNA-seq) was performed on skin wounds of WT and Osmrb−/− mice on day 6 (Fig. 2A–C). A total of 1894 gene expression was upregulated 1.5-fold higher of transcripts per kilobase million (TPM) in wounds of Osmrb−/− compared with WT mice and these genes were applied to Metascape27. The inflammatory response was significantly suppressed in wound of skin in Osmrb−/− mice (Fig. 2A). Unbiased gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was used to identify gene signatures regulated by the OSM-OSMRβ axis in skin wounds28. Among the down-regulated gene sets in Osmrb−/− mice, we focused on the inflammatory response and angiogenesis. Consequently, we validated their down-regulation by analyzing their RNA-seq data (Fig. 2B, C). Thus, the OSM-OSMRβ axis could be essential for induction of the inflammation and angiogenesis in the skin wound healing. We next examined the effects of Osmrb deficiency on leukocyte recruitment into wound sites, which is a major hallmark of skin wound healing, particularly at its inflammatory stage1. Accurate determination of neutrophils was hindered by trapping of neutrophils within the blood clot which is associated with healing wounds, and therefore, we measured MPO activities to evaluate the degree of neutrophil recruitment. MPO activity reached its peak on day 1 and declined thereafter to similar extents in WT and Osmrb−/− mice (Fig. 2D, E). Moreover, F4/80+ macrophages were present in small numbers in normal skin but their numbers were successively increased in the wound sites until 6 days after the injury, to similar extents in Osmrb−/− and WT mice (Fig. 2F, G). Similar observations were obtained on CD11b+ macrophages at the wound sites (Fig. S4). We further observed that the wound sites of WT and Osmrb−/− mice exhibited similar levels of mRNA expression of inflammatory cytokines (Tnf, Il1b, and Il6), chemokines (Cxcl1, Cxcl2, Ccl2, Ccl5, and Cx3cl1), and chemokine receptors (Ccr2, Ccr5, and Cx3cr1) (Fig. S5), the molecules that can crucially regulate leukocyte recruitment1,29,30. Evidence is accumulating to indicate the crucial contribution of bone marrow (BM) cells to skin wound healing31,32. To evaluate the roles of BM-derived and non-BM-derived cells to skin wound healing, we made several BM chimeric mice (donor BM into recipient); WT-BM into WT mice, WT-BM into Osmrb−/− mice, Osmrb−/−BM into WT mice, and Osmrb−/− BM into Osmrb−/− mice. BM-derived cells from donor mice were labeled ex vivo with PKH26 red dye and were injected i.v. into lethally irradiated recipients. Skin wounds were prepared at 4 weeks after BM transplantation, and frozen sections of wound sites were prepared and analyzed 6 days later, a time when BM-derived macrophages were predominantly present in the wound sites. Skin sections from uninjured BM chimeric mice contained few PKH26-labeled cells in the dermis. After wound injury, PKH26-labeled BM cells were apparently recruited into the wound sites in each BM chimeric mice, to similar extents regardless of the genotype of the transferred BM cells (Fig. 3A, B). Moreover, a substantial proportion of PKH26-labeled cells expressed F4/80 but not α-SMA at the skin wound sites (Fig. 3C, D), indicating that the absence of OSMRβ had few effects on BM-derived macrophage recruitment. Thus, leukocytes, particularly macrophages, were recruited into the wound sites independently of the OSMRβ axis.

Reduced granulation tissue formation and neovascularization in Osmrb−/− mice

We next examined granulation tissue formation and neovascularization, two characteristic features of skin wound healing process, particularly at its late stage. Granulation tissue formation and α-SMA+ myofibroblast proliferation were markedly reduced at the wound sites in Osmrb−/− mice, compared with WT ones at day 6 (Fig. 4A). We further measured hydroxyproline (Hyp) contents as an indicator of collagen accumulation, an essential step for granulation tissue formation, and revealed that there was no significant difference in Hyp content as in uninjured skin between Osmrb−/− and WT mice (Fig. 4B). However, at 6, 10, and 14 days after injury, Hyp content at wound sites was significantly smaller in Osmrb−/− mice than in WT mice at 6, 10, and 14 days after injury (Fig. 4B). We next examined the effect of OSMRβ deficiency on neovascularization, another typical skin wound healing process. No significant difference was observed on the vessel densities of uninjured skin between WT and Osmrb−/− mice, but the vessel densities were significantly lower at injured skin sites of Osmrb−/− mice than that of WT mice at 6 days after the injury, indicating impaired neovascularization in Osmrb−/− mice (Fig. 4C, D). These observations implied that the lack of OSMRβ reduced granulation tissue formation and neovascularization, eventually resulting in delayed wound healing. OSMRβ expression by α-SMA+ myofibroblasts at the wound sites (Fig. 1G) prompted us to determine the roles of each OSMRβ-expressing α-SMA+ myofibroblast in skin wound healing process. Irrespective of the BM cell genotypes, the absence of OSMRβ in recipient mice delayed wound healing, compared with WT recipient mice (Fig. 4E, F). Consistently, Hyp contents and neovascularization were significantly reduced at 6 days in Osmrb−/− recipient mice, compared with WT recipient mice, regardless of BM donor genotypes (Fig. 4G–I). Thus, resident OSMRβ+ myofibroblasts but not BM-derived OSMRβ+ cells might be crucial for skin wound healing. We further detected Osmrb mRNA in WT-derived primary dermal fibroblasts (Fig. S6). Moreover, when WT-derived primary dermal fibroblasts were injected into skin wound sites at 2 and 5 days after injury, the injection accelerated skin wound healing in Osmrb−/− mice but not in WT mice (Fig. 4J, K). These observations strengthened the assumption that OSMRβ-expressing fibroblasts have a crucial role in skin wound healing.

The absence of OSMRβ reduced collagen deposition through suppressed TIMP-1 expression

Granulation tissue formation is characterized by collagen deposition in skin wound site, the process that is regulated by the balance between collagen production and degradation. TGF-β is a profibrogenic growth factor, which can be expressed in fibroblasts and can promote collagen production in skin wound healing1. Hence, we examined the expression of TGF-β at the wound sites in WT and Osmrb−/− mice. Tgfb mRNA was detected to similar extents in the skin of WT and Osmrb−/− mice, both before and after injury (Fig. 5A). Moreover, there were no significant differences in both total and active TGF-β1 protein levels between WT and Osmrb−/− mice at the wound sites (Fig. 5B, C). Collagen degradation can be regulated by the balance between the activities of collagenolytic enzymes, particularly matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-2 and MMP-9, which are expressed in wound healing and venous thrombus33,34 and that of their inhibitor, TIMP-1. Gene expression of Mmp2 and Mmp9 was upregulated, to a similar content, in WT and Osmrb−/− mice, whereas Timp1 expression was significantly higher in WT wound sites than in Osmrb−/− wound sites (Fig. 5D–F). Consistently, MMP-2 and MMP-9 activities were enhanced to larger extents at the wound site of Osmrb−/− mice, compared with WT mice (Fig. 5G and S7). Moreover, double-color immunofluorescence analyses demonstrated that α-SMA+ myofibroblasts (Fig. 5H), but not F4/80+ macrophages (Fig. S8), were the main source of TIMP-1. Thus, the absence of Osmrb had few impacts on the expression of TGF-β1, a major collagen-inducing factor, but suppressed TIMP-1 expression in fibroblasts and eventually enhanced collagen degradation, leading to decreased collagen deposition and subsequent delayed wound healing. Reduced neovascularization in the skin wound sites of Osmrb−/− mice incited us to determine the expression levels of two major angiogenic factors in skin wound healing process, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and HGF34,35. Skin injury enhanced the gene expression of both factors (Fig. 5I, J), but no significant differences were observed on Vegf mRNA expression at the wound sites between Osmrb−/− and WT mice (Fig. 5I). On the contrary, injury-induced enhanced Hgf mRNA was significantly attenuated at 3 and 6 days after injury in Osmrb−/− mice, compared with WT ones (Fig. 5J). Similar observations were observed on HGF protein contents in skin wound sites (Fig. 5K). Moreover, a double-color immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated that α-SMA+ myofibroblasts were the main source of HGF (Fig. 5L). Collectively, the lack of OSMRβ suppressed HGF expression in fibroblasts and eventually impaired neovascularization.

OSM-OSMRβ axis upregulated HGF and TIMP-1 expression in fibroblasts by using distinct MAPK pathways

OSMRβ expression in fibroblasts at skin wound sites (Fig. 1G) prompted us to examine the effects of OSM on a murine fibroblastic cell line, NIH3T3 cell, which was confirmed to express OSMRβ (Fig. S9). RNA-seq analyses in OSM-stimulated NIH3T3 cells demonstrated that a total of 1179 genes were augmented (or 1.5-fold higher genes of TPM) in OSM-stimulated cells compared with unstimulated cells and the enhanced genes were applied to Metascape27. The inflammatory response (GO:0006954) was the second enriched gene set (Fig. 6A), and GSEA analysis uncovered inflammatory response and angiogenesis signaling pathway was also enhanced1. Among the enhanced gene sets in OSM-stimulated cells, we validated their RNA-seq data on the inflammatory response and angiogenesis (Fig. 6B, C). These RNA-seq data suggest that OSM-OSMRβ axis induces inflammation- and angiogenesis-related gene signatures in fibroblasts. We next examined the effects of OSM on HGF and TIMP-1 expression in NIH3T3 cells. Actually, OSM enhanced both Timp1 and Hgf gene expression in NIH3T3 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 6D, E). Consistent with the previous report that OSM can activate MAPK pathway in fibroblasts,36 OSM treatment induced the phosphorylation of ERK1/2, p38, and JNK in NIH3T3 fibroblasts (Fig. 6F–I and S10). Moreover, OSM-induced Hgf expression in NIH3T3 cells was canceled by PD98059 (ERK inhibitor) or SB203580 (p38 inhibitor) (Fig. 6J–L). On the contrary, OSM-induced Timp1 expression in NIH3T3 cells was suppressed by PD98059 (ERK inhibitor) or SP600125 (JNK inhibitor) (Fig. 6M–O). Collectively, the OSM-OSMRβ signals could induce Hgf and Timp1 expression using distinct MAPK pathways.

OSM administration was effective in improving wound healing in diabetic mice

Next, we examined the therapeutic effect of OSM in db/db mice. In the PBS-control group, wound healing was significantly delayed in db/db mice compared with WT mice, but this delay was markedly improved in the OSM-treated group (Fig. 7A, B). In WT mice treated with exogenous OSM, the histopathological changes in granulation tissue formation and angiogenesis were not significantly different from those in the control group, and in agreement with this, there were no significant differences in Col1a1 gene expression and neovascularization rate. On the other hand, in db/db mice treated with exogenous OSM, granulation tissue formation and neovascularization were markedly increased, compared to the control group (Fig. 7C–E). Furthermore, we found that Timp1 and Hgf expressions were significantly increased in db/db mice treated with OSM compared to controls (Fig. 7F, G). These results supported our experimental findings and demonstrated that the OSM-OSMRβ axis enhances HGF and TIMP-1 expression and has a therapeutic effect on delayed wound healing in diabetes.

Discussion

Several lines of accumulating evidence demonstrated that the topical application of OSM accelerated diabetic skin wound healing through the enhancement of reepithelialization37,38. In particular, Das et al. showed that OSM/STAT3 signal pathway promoted p63-dependent keratinocyte migration, eventually resulting in the acceleration of wound healing38. In the present study, we analyzed wound healing in Osmrb−/− mice and bone marrow chimeric mice between Osmrb−/− and WT mice. Although there was no significant difference in the reepithelialization between WT and Osmrb−/− mice, we clarified that OSM-OSMRβ axis on resident fibroblasts was a core factor that promoted wound healing via up-regulating TIMP-1 and HGF.

Leukocyte infiltration, one main feature of inflammation, is always observed in skin wound healing process, but its role still remains to be investigated. Reduced macrophage infiltration impaired tissue repair in skin wound sites39,40, and aged mice transferred with young mouse-derived macrophages exhibited accelerated wound healing41. On the contrary, the absence of type 1 TNF-receptor or IFN-γ accelerated skin wound healing despite attenuated macrophage infiltration42,43, and exaggerated leukocyte recruitment delayed wound healing30. Biological roles of OSM in inflammatory responses are also elusive44. OSM administration can block IL-8 expression, thereby reducing neutrophil infiltration45. Moreover, the absence of OSMRβ enhanced adipose tissue inflammation under normal diet conditions46. On the contrary, accumulating evidence implied the involvement of OSM in the pathogenesis of inflammatory diseases such as arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, psoriasis, and allergic airway inflammation47. Our present RNAseq analysis unraveled depressed inflammation responses but preserved gene expression of chemokines and their receptors in skin wound sites of Osmrb−/− mice. Moreover, there was no significant difference in leukocyte recruitment during skin wound healing between WT and Osmrb−/− mice. Thus, the OSM-OSMRβ axis has few roles in leukocyte infiltration but may have impacts on other aspects of inflammation, such as fibrosis and angiogenesis.

Both bone marrow-derived and resident cells contribute to skin wound healing29,48,49. OSMRβ was expressed in macrophages and α-SMA+ myofibroblasts. The analyses of BM chimeric mice between WT and Osmrb−/− mice, however, revealed that the OSM-OSMRβ acted on resident cells but not bone marrow-derived cells to regulate collagen accumulation and angiogenesis, two essential steps of skin wound healing. Moreover, adoptive transfer of OSMRβ+ fibroblasts into skin wound sites accelerated healing process. Thus, the OSM-OSMRβ axis has important roles in skin wound healing by mainly acting on resident fibroblasts.

OSM is a member of the IL-6 cytokine family, represented by IL-6. Like IL-6, it acts on a variety of cells and elicits diverse biological responses, including the regulation of cell survival, differentiation, and proliferation50,51. A potential activity of OSM on fibroblasts was proposed based on the observation that its expression is augmented in the lungs of patients with pulmonary fibrotic diseases, which are characterized by fibroblast-derived ECM accumulation52. OSM can also induce dermal fibroblasts to synthesize IL-653, which can eventually stimulate fibroblast mitogenesis. However, independently of IL-6, OSM can directly augment mitogenic responses of fibroblasts by activating MAPK pathway15. Thus, it remains elusive how OSM acts on fibroblasts in skin, particularly during wound healing process.

Collagen accumulation is essential for granulation tissue formation, which is crucial for repairment of defected tissues. TGF-β is a potent fibrogenic growth factor, and contributes to skin wound healing through Smad signal pathway42. Profibrotic roles of OSM were proposed based on the observations on mouse disease models54,55. OSM accelerated hepatic fibrosis by enhancing TGF-β and PDGF expression in hepatic macrophages55. Despite reduced collagen contents in Osmrb−/− mice, however, we could not detect any significant difference in TGF-β1 expression at both gene and protein levels between WT and Osmrb−/− mice. Thus, it is likely that the OSM-OSMRβ axis can regulate collagen expression independently of TGF-β1 pathway, as previously observed18,19.

Collagen contents can also be controlled by its degradation process, which is regulated by the balance between the activities of collagen lytic enzymes and those of their inhibitors. Among collagen lytic enzymes, particularly MMP-2 and MMP-9, were expressed in skin tissues and, as a result, have essential roles in collagen degradation in skin wound sites34,56,57. In contrast to the previous observations that OSM could induce the expression of MMP-2 and MMP-958,59, we could not detect any significant difference in the gene expression of MMP-2 and MMP-9 between WT and Osmrb−/− mice during skin wound healing. On the contrary, the absence of OSMβR significantly suppressed the expression of an MMP inhibitor, TIMP-160,61, in fibroblasts at skin wound sites, and consequently, the enzymatic activities of MMPs were augmented in Osmrb−/− mice to larger extent than in WT mice. Thus, it is likely that the OSM-OSMRβ axis can directly dampen collagen degradation in fibroblasts, thereby promoting collagen deposition at skin wound sites.

RNA-seq and histological analyses revealed that Osmrb−/− mice exhibited reduction in neovascularization, which is another indispensable event for skin wound healing. In contrast to the previous observation that OSM could promote angiogenesis by inducing the expression of a major angiogenic factor, VEGF15, the genetic disruption of OSMRβ had few effects on VEGF expression during skin wound healing. Cohen et al. demonstrated that OSM but not IL-6 and LIF participates in pulmonary repair by inducing human lung fibroblasts to synthesize HGF36, another potent inducer of angiogenesis62–66. Consistently, Osmrb−/− mice exhibited reduced HGF expression in fibroblasts at skin wound sites. Thus, it is probable that OSM can induce neovascularization at wound sites by inducing a potent angiogenic factor, HGF, in fibroblasts.

OSM expression in infiltrating leukocytes and OSMβR expression in skin fibroblasts would indicate that the OSM-OSMβR axis can induce skin fibroblasts at wound sites to express TIMP-1 and HGF in a paracrine manner, thereby accelerating skin wound healing. Guillemin et al. reported that GBT013, a collagen-based dressing, promoted wound healing in diabetic chronic wounds by increasing TIMP-1 expression and suppressing MMP-2 and 9 activities in fibroblasts67. On the other hand, it was shown that deficiency of integrin-linked kinase delayed wound healing in skin, but supplementation with HGF rescued the delayed wound healing68. These observations provided direct evidence for the role of TIMP-1 and HGF in wound healing.

OSMβR expressed on skin fibroblasts may be a candidate molecule for treating skin wounds in various conditions, such as surgery, chronic ulcers, and other relevant pathologic conditions. In fact, in an analysis using diabetic mice that exhibit delayed wound healing, we demonstrated that administration of OSM increased the expression of HGF and TIMP-1, which in turn increased angiogenesis and granulation tissue formation, thereby reversing the delayed wound healing in diabetic mice. Moreover, consistent with the previous observation that OSM can activate MAPK pathways36, our in vitro study demonstrated that OSM activated MAPK pathways in fibroblasts. Of interest is that OSM-mediated TIMP-1 and HGF expression exhibited distinct sensitivities to ERK, p38 kinase, and JNK inhibitors. Considering that drug discovery of a small molecule PI3K isotype agonist is recently under progress69, the elucidation on the role of each MAPK isotype in OSM-mediated wound healing acceleration may eventually lead to the development of a small molecule agonistic drug targeting a specific MAPK isotype, for the treatment of skin wounds.

Diabetic mice showed retarded skin wound healing with suppressed gene expression of OSM and OSMRβ, implying that the OSM-OSMRβ axis would play promotive roles in skin wound healing. Supportingly, the absence of the OSM-OSMRβ axis using knockout mice or neutralizing antibody impaired skin wound healing. Although, as reported previously70, hyperglycemia may have qualitative and/or quantitative effects on OSM signals, the delayed wound healing in diabetic mice is attributable to at least a quantitative impairment of the OSM-OSMRβ axis. Actually, the supplementation of exogenous OSM restored skin wound healing in diabetic mice. On the contrary, the topical OSM application failed to accelerate skin wound healing in non-diabetic mice, which might be explainable by the fact that OSM might be produced at the sufficient amounts at the wound sites of WT mice. Collectively, our observations suggested that the OSM-OSMRβ pathway might play a compensatory role under pathological conditions such as hyperglycemia. Thus, the therapeutic potential of this axis may be particularly relevant in the context of diabetic wound healing, rather than under physiological conditions.

Methods

Antibodies and recombinant OSM

The following monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) and polyclonal antibodies (pAbs) were used in this study: rabbit anti-MPO pAbs (Lab Vision, Fremont, CA); goat anti-mouse OSM pAbs (Abcam, Cambridge, MA); goat anti-mouse OSMRβ pAbs (R&D, Minneapolis, MN); rabbit anti-mouse HGF pAbs (Sino Biological, Beijing, China); rabbit anti-mouse TIMP-1 pAbs (AF7007, Affinity Bioscience, Cincinnati, OH); rat anti-mouse CD31 mAb (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ); rabbit anti-mouse CD11b mAb (ab133357; clone EPR1344; abcam, Cambridge, UK); rat anti-mouse F4/80 mAb (clone BM8; eBioscience, San Diego, CA); rabbit anti-human CD3 pAbs (DakoCytomation, Santa Clara, CA), which cross-react with mouse CD3; rabbit anti-mouse Pan-cytokeratin (Pan-CK) pAb (bs-1712R; Bioss, Woburn, MA); mouse anti-human α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) mAb (clone 1A4; DakoCytomation), which cross-reacts with mouse α-SMA; rabbit anti-p38 MAPK pAbs, rabbit anti-JNK pAbs (SantaCruz, Santa Cruz, CA); rabbit anti-p-ERK1/2 pAbs, mouse anti-p-38 MAPK mAb, rabbit anti-ERK pAbs (Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA); rabbit anti-pJNK pAbs (BioVision, Mountain View, CA); rabbit anti-mouse GAPDH mAb (Cell Signaling Technology); cyanine dye 3 (Cy3)-conjugated donkey anti-rat, -rabbit, -mouse, and -goat IgG, FITC-conjugated donkey anti-rat, -rabbit, and -goat IgG, and aminomethylcoumarin acetate (AMCA)-conjugated donkey anti-rat IgG pAbs (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA); goat anti-mouse OSM-neutralizing pAbs and control goat IgG (R&D). Recombinant murine OSM was purchased from Abcam (ab280335, Abcam).

Animals

The generation of Osmrb−/− mice has been described previously71. Male C57BLKS/J Iar-m+/+Leprdb (m+/db) and wild-type (WT, C57BL/6) as control, and diabetic +Leprdb/+Leprdb (db/db) mice were obtained from SLC Japan (Hamamatsu, Japan). All of male animals were used at 8–10 weeks of age and housed individually in cages under specific pathogen-free conditions during the experiments. Mice were kept in groups (5 mice per cage) at 22-25 °C, in 12/12 h light/dark cycle, with water and chow ad libitum. Next, mice were kept in individual cages since 1 week before testing, measurement, and tissue harvest. In all experiments, control and intervention group mice were treated in the same experimental settings. All animals were used under the auspices of a protocol approved by the Wakayama Medical University (Approved No. 1204).

Excisional wound preparation and analysis

Full-thickness wounds were created in the dorsal skin under sterile conditions. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with i.p. administration of ketamine and xylazine. After shaving and cleaning with betadine and 70% ethanol, the dorsal skin was picked up at the midline and punched through two layers of skin with a sterile disposable biopsy punch (4-mm in diameter; Kai Industries, Tokyo, Japan), generating one wound on each side of the midline. The same procedure was repeated three times, generating six wounds each mouse. Each wound site was digitally photographed at the indicated time intervals, and wound areas were determined on photographs using Adobe PhotoShop (CS3, Adobe Systems, San Jose, CA). Changes in wound areas over time were expressed as the percentage of the initial wound areas. In another series of experiments, WT mice received i.p. injection of neutralizing anti-OSM pAbs or control IgG (250 μg/mouse) every day from Day 1 to 5. In some experiments, wounds and their surrounding areas, including the scab and epithelial margins, were cut at the indicated time intervals with a sterile disposable 8-mm diameter biopsy punch (Kai Industries). Moreover, in order to examine the effects of OSMR+ fibroblasts on skin wound healing, primary dermal fibroblasts derived from C57BL/6 adult mice (approximately 5 × 10⁵ cells/25 μl/wound, C57-6067, Cell Biologics, Chicago, IL) were implanted subcutaneously at two opposite sites around the wound in recipient WT or Osmrb⁻/⁻ mice on days 2 and 5 post-injury. To ensure deposition into the wound site, the needle was inserted within 2 mm from the wound margin and advanced toward the wound center, where primary dermal fibroblasts were injected. In addition, to evaluate the effect of OSM treatment on cutaneous wound healing, recombinant murine OSM (100 ng/25 μl/wound, ab280335, Abcam) was administered subcutaneously at two opposite sites around the wound in recipient WT or db/db mice once daily from day 1 to day 10 after injury. To ensure local delivery into the wound bed, the needle was inserted within 2 mm from the wound margin and directed toward the wound center for injection.

Generation of BM chimeric mice

The following BM chimeric mice were prepared: male Osmrb−/− BM into female WT mice, male WT BM into female Osmrb−/− mice, male WT BM into female WT mice, and male Osmrb−/− BM into female Osmrb−/− mice72. BM cells were collected from the femurs of donor mice by aspiration and flushing. Recipient mice were irradiated at 12 Gy and then intravenously received 5 × 106 BM cells from donor mice in a volume of 200 μl sterile PBS under anesthesia. Mice were subsequently housed in sterilized microisolator cages and fed normal chow and autoclaved hyperchlorinated water for 4 weeks. To verify successful engraftment and reconstitution of BM in the transplanted mice, genomic DNA was isolated from peripheral blood and tail tissues of each chimeric mouse 4 weeks after BM transplantation with NucleoSpin Tissue Kit (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany). Then, we performed PCR to detect the Sry gene contained in the Y chromosome (forward primer, 5′-TTGCCTCAACAAAA-3′; reverse primer, 5′-AAACTGCTGCTTCTGCTGGT-3′). The amplified PCR products were fractionated on 2% agarose gel and visualized by ethidium bromide staining. After durable BM engraftment was confirmed, full-thickness skin wound was made in the recipient mice as described above. In some experiments, BM cells (5 × 106) labeled with PKH26 (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) were intravenously injected to recipient mice immediately after whole-body irradiation to delineate the trafficking of BM-derived cells.

Histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses

Wound specimens were fixed in 4% formaldehyde buffered with PBS and then embedded with paraffin. Sections were stained with HE for conventional histopathological analysis. Immunohistochemical analysis were also performed using anti-MPO, -F4/80, -CD3, -CD31, or -α-SMA Abs, as described previously29. In brief, deparaffinized sections were immersed in 0.3% H2O2 in methanol for 30 min to eliminate endogenous peroxidase activities. The sections were further incubated with PBS containing 1% normal serum corresponding to the secondary Ab and 1% BSA to reduce nonspecific reactions. The sections were incubated with Abs at a concentration of 1 μg/ml at 4 °C overnight. Normal rat, rabbit, goat, or mouse IgG was used as negative control. After the incubation the sections with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG, immune complexes were visualized using Tyramide Amplification Buffer Plus (22029, Biotium, Fremont, CA) with Tyramide Signal Amplification Kits (33020, Biotin-XX, Biotium) and LSAB2 Streptavidin-HRP (K1016, Dako, Carpinteria, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

A double-color immunofluorescence analysis

Wound sections were also analyzed by double- or triple-color immunofluorescence microscopy to determine the localization of OSM, OSMRβ, HGF, α-SMA, and TIMP-1 proteins as described previously29. Briefly, deparaffinized sections were incubated with PBS containing 1% normal donkey serum and 1% BSA to reduce nonspecific reactions. Then, the sections were further incubated with the combination of anti-OSM and anti-MPO, anti-OSM and anti-F4/80, anti-OSMRβ and anti-F4/80, anti-OSMRβ and anti-α-SMA, anti-HGF and anti-F4/80, anti-HGF and anti-α-SMA, and anti-α-SMA and anti-TIMP-1 Abs at 4 °C overnight. All Abs were used at a concentration of 1 μg/ml. After incubation with fluorochrome-conjugated secondary Abs (10 μg/ml) at room temperature for 30 min, the sections were observed by fluorescence microscopy.

Measurement of leukocyte recruitment and angiogenesis at wound sites

The wound bed was defined as the area surrounded by uninjured skin and fascia, regenerated epidermis, and eschar. The numbers of infiltrating granulocytes and macrophages within the wound bed were enumerated on 5 randomly chosen visual fields (×200) of the sections stained with anti-MPO and F4/80 Ab, respectively, and the average of the selected 5 fields was calculated. The area of vascular territory, defined as CD31 immunoreactivity in the wound bed, was measured using the freehand tool in Photoshop (Adobe Systems) and expressed as a percentage of the total wound bed area. All sample identifiers were blinded to the observer.

MPO assay

MPO activity was measured to evaluate neutrophil recruitment30. Skin wound sites were excised using a sterile disposable 8-mm diameter biopsy punch (Kai Industries) and were then homogenized in 1 ml of 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer solution with 0.5% hexadecyltrimethylamminium bromide (Sigma Aldrich) and 5 mM EDTA. The samples were freeze-thawed three times and centrifuged at 12,000 × g at 4 °C. MPO activities in the supernatants were assayed using the SUMILON peroxidase assay kit (Sumitomo Bekuraito, Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm, and MPO content was determined against a standard curve generated using known concentrations of reagent MPO (Sigma Aldrich). The data were expressed as OD per total protein (mg/ml).

Measurement of Hydroxyproline (Hyp) content

Hyp content was measured as the index of collagen accumulation at the wound sites, as described previously30. The excised wound samples were dried for 16 h at 120 °C and were then hydrolyzed in 6 N HCl at 110 °C for 12 h. Fifty-microliter aliquots were added to 1 ml of a solution containing 1.4% chloramine T (Sigma Aldrich), 10% n-propanol, and 0.5 M sodium acetate at pH 6.0. After 20 min incubation at room temperature, 1 ml of Ehrlich’s solution (1 M p-demethylaminobenzaldehyde in 70% n-propanol, 20% perchlorich acid) was added, and the mixture was incubated at 65 °C for 15 min. Absorbance was measured at 550 nm, and the amount of Hyp was determined against a standard curve generated using known concentrations of reagent Hyp (Sigma Aldrich). The data were expressed as amount (μg) per wound.

Real-time RT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from whole tissue or cultured fibroblasts using ISOGEN (Nippon Gene, Toyama, Japan), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using PrimeScript Reverse Transcriptase (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan) with oligo(dT)15 primers. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 2 μg total RNA with oligo(dT)15 primers using PrimeScript Reverse Transcriptase (Takara Bio). As described previously29, real-time PCR was carried out using SYBER Premix Ex Taq II (Takara Bio) with specific primer sets (Supplementary Table 1). Primers were purchased from Takara Bio. Amplification and detection of mRNA were performed using Thermal Cycler Dice Real Time System (TP800; Takara Bio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To standardize mRNA concentrations, transcript levels of Actb were determined in parallel for each sample, and relative transcript levels were corrected by normalization based on Actb transcript levels.

ELISA

Wound tissues were homogenized with 0.5 ml lysis buffer (10 mM PBS; 0.1% SDS; 1% Nonidet P-40; and 5 mM EDTA) containing Complete Protease Inhibitor Mixture (Roche Diagnostics, Tokyo, Japan). The homogenates were centrifuged at 18,000 × g for 15 min. The supernatants were subjected to ELISA. HGF and total and active TGF-β1 protein levels were measured with commercially available ELISA kits (Quantikine M; R&D) according to the manufacturer’s recommendation. The detection limit for each kit was as follows: HGF, > 4.38 pg/ml; and TGF-β1, > 4.6 pg/ml. Total protein in the supernatant was measured with a commercial kit (Piece BCA Protein Assay Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tokyo, Japan). The data were expressed as growth factor (pg/ml) relative to total protein (mg/ml) for each sample.

Stimulation of NIH3T3 cells with OSM

NIH3T3 cells were cultured in DMEM medium containing 10% FCS, seeded at 1 × 106 cells/well in six-well plates, and cultured overnight. After cells were stimulated further with various concentrations of recombinant mouse OSM (495-MO/CF, R&D, Minneapolis, MN) in the presence or the absence of an ERK inhibitor (PD98059, Sigma-Aldrich), a p38 inhibitor (SB203580, Sigma-Aldrich), or a JNK inhibitor (SP600125, Sigma-Aldrich) for 2 h at 37 °C, total RNA and protein were extracted for real-time PCR and Western blotting analysis, respectively.

Western blotting analysis

NIH3T3 cells were scraped and centrifuged, and the cell pellets were dissolved with SDS sample buffer (10 mM PBS (pH 7.4) containing 0.01% Triton X-100, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, and 0.1% SDS) and were centrifuged to obtain lysates. Thereafter, equal amounts of protein (10 μg) were separated by 5% or 15% SDS-PAGE and transferred to an Immobilon-P transfer membrane (Millipore, Billerica, MA). Postincubation of the membrane with anti-ERK1/2, -p-ERK1/2, -p38 MAPK, -p-p38 MAPK, -JNK, -p-JNK, or anti-GAPDH pAbs (SantaCruz), immune complexes were detected using an ECL reagent (Millipore), according to the manufacturer’s instructions72.

RNA sequencing analysis (RNA-seq)

RNA samples were extracted from tissues or culture cells by RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), and bulk RNA sequencing analysis (RNA-seq) was performed according to the established methods73. In brief, 4000 ng RNAs from each skin wound and culture condition were used to construct the libraries for sequencing with the use of TruSeq Stranded mRNA (Illumina, San Diego, CA, U.S.A.). The average size of the libraries was 313–343 bp. High-throughput sequencing of the samples was performed using the NextSeq 500/550 High Output Kit v2.5 (Illumina, 75 cycles pair-end, 40/40 cycles). The average sequence reads per sample were 21,622,557. The raw fastq data was analyzed using the CLC Genomics Workbench Version 12.0.2 (Filgen Inc., Aichi, Japan) by referred to GRCm38. The RNA-seq data were processed with the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis software based on the guidelines27,28. The RNA-seq analysis data used in this study were presented in Supplementary data 1.

MMP-2 and -9 activity assay

Intra-aortic MMP activities were determined by the Gelatin-Zymography Kit (AK47, Cosmo Bio, Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistics and reproducibility

Data were expressed as the mean ± SEM. For the comparison between WT and Osmrb−/− mice at multiple time points, two-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test, was used. When measurements were made over time in the same individual and compared (e.g., Figs. 1B, I, K; 4F, K; and 7B), repeated-measures (RM)-ANOVA was performed. To compare the values between two groups, unpaired Student’s t-test was performed. In case of the series of OSM stimulations of NIH3T3 cells for the in vitro, one-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test, was used. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using the Statcel3 software under the supervision of a medical statistician. The sample size and the number of experimental repetitions were detailed in the figure legend.
