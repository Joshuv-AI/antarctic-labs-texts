VV‐ECMO Combined With M‐TEER for the Treatment of Mitral Valve Papillary Muscle Rupture Following Acute Myocardial Infarction PCI: A Case Report
Authors: Zhenjiang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMitral valve papillary muscle rupture represents a rare yet critical complication of acute myocardial infarction, often leading to severe mitral regurgitation and subsequent pulmonary edema with high mortality. Despite optimal ventilatory support and pharmacological management, patients often exhibit refractory hypoxemia, rendering surgical intervention both technically demanding and high‐risk. A 40‐year‐old Asian male presented to the emergency department of Liaocheng People's Hospital following percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) for acute myocardial infarction at an outside facility. Shortly post‐procedure, the patient developed dyspnea and oliguria. Despite endotracheal intubation, mechanical ventilation, and medical therapy, his condition deteriorated, necessitating transfer. Echocardiography and transesophageal echocardiography confirmed mitral valve papillary muscle rupture with severe regurgitation, complicated by profound pulmonary edema and refractory hypoxemia. Venovenous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV‐ECMO) was promptly initiated, resulting in gradual improvement in oxygenation. Following a multidisciplinary evaluation, edge‐to‐edge mitral valve repair was successfully performed under VV‐ECMO support. ECMO was discontinued on postoperative day 4, and the patient was discharged after 14 days with marked clinical improvement. In cases of severe pulmonary edema secondary to mitral valve papillary muscle rupture post‐PCI for acute myocardial infarction, early VV‐ECMO implementation combined with edge‐to‐edge mitral valve repair under ECMO support may mitigate surgical risk and enhance outcomes. This integrated approach presents a viable therapeutic alternative for such high‐risk patients.Keywords: acute myocardial infarction, M‐TEER, pulmonary edema, VV‐ECMO

Introduction

Mechanical complications arising from acute myocardial infarction, including papillary muscle rupture, ventricular septal defect, and free wall rupture, are associated with substantial mortality. Current evidence indicates that the in‐hospital mortality rate for patients experiencing mechanical complications following ST‐segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) reaches 42.4% [1]. Although the incidence of papillary muscle rupture has declined with the advancement of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) reperfusion therapy, mortality rates remain persistently high. Conservative management alone yields an in‐hospital mortality rate of up to 80% [2], underscoring the necessity of surgical intervention as a critical therapeutic approach. Nevertheless, surgical intervention in cases complicated by severe pulmonary edema and refractory hypoxemia entails significant perioperative risk, for which extracorporeal membrane oxygenation may serve as a potential risk‐mitigation strategy. Herein, we present a rare case of papillary muscle rupture following PCI for acute myocardial infarction, complicated by severe pulmonary edema and cardiogenic shock. The patient was successfully managed through venovenous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV‐ECMO) support combined with mitral valve edge‐to‐edge repair. The clinical details are reported as follows.

Case History/Examination

A 40‐year‐old Asian male with a longstanding history of hypertension and a prior right lower limb amputation 20 years ago presented to the hospital on December 26, 2024, with a chief complaint of persistent chest tightness and precordial pain lasting over 24 h. The symptoms initially manifested as unexplained chest tightness, followed by the onset of severe precordial pain accompanied by diaphoresis three hours later. Upon evaluation at a local medical facility, the patient was diagnosed with acute STEMI and subsequently underwent PCI. Post‐procedure, the patient developed persistent dyspnea and oliguria, necessitating transfer to the intensive care unit (ICU). Despite pharmacological management and mechanical ventilation, clinical improvement was not achieved, prompting referral to our institution for further evaluation and management.

Physical examination revealed the following: temperature 36.9°C, pulse 118 beats/min, respiratory rate 25 breaths/min, and blood pressure 72/51 mmHg. Neurological assessment showed bilateral pupils measuring 2 mm with intact light reflexes. Pulmonary auscultation demonstrated coarse breath sounds with bilateral wet rales. Cardiac examination revealed a regular rhythm with an apical systolic murmur. Abdominal examination was unremarkable, with no tenderness or edema in the lower extremities.

Laboratory investigations demonstrated significant abnormalities: arterial blood gas analysis revealed metabolic acidosis (pH 7.28) with hypercapnia (PaCO2 52 mmHg), severe hypoxemia (PaO2 38 mmHg), elevated lactate (4.20 mmol/L), and profound oxygen desaturation (62.7%). Markers of myocardial injury were markedly elevated (troponin I > 80.000 ng/mL, NT‐proBNP 14,200 pg/mL). Inflammatory markers were significantly elevated (procalcitonin 5.14 ng/mL, CRP 120.92 mg/L, leukocytosis 33.66 × 109/L with 85.4% neutrophils). Hepatic and renal dysfunction were evident (AST 382 U/L, ALT 103 U/L, creatinine 164.2 μmol/L), along with metabolic derangements (hypokalemia 3.23 mmol/L, hypertriglyceridemia 3.54 mmol/L).

Investigations and Treatment

The patient was transferred from an external medical facility and admitted to the emergency intensive care unit. Upon arrival, the patient was receiving mechanical ventilation via an orotracheal tube, with ventilator settings at FiO₂ 100%, PEEP 12 cmH2O, and exhibiting a PaO₂ of 44 mmHg. Hemodynamic stability was maintained with a norepinephrine infusion (0.5 μg/kg/min), yielding a blood pressure of 100/65 mmHg. Initial diagnostic imaging, including chest computed tomography (CT), demonstrated extensive pulmonary edema (Figure 1). Transthoracic echocardiography revealed mitral valve prolapse with severe regurgitation and abnormal mitral valve morphology, suggestive of papillary muscle involvement (Figure 2). Subsequent transesophageal echocardiography confirmed the diagnosis of mitral valve prolapse with severe regurgitation secondary to papillary muscle rupture.

Given the patient's inadequate response to pharmacological intervention and mechanical ventilation at the referring institution, our emergency department's extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) team initiated venovenous ECMO (VV‐ECMO) therapy, which resulted in improved oxygenation. Following multidisciplinary evaluation, the patient underwent mitral valve edge‐to‐edge repair under ECMO support on the subsequent day. Postoperative echocardiography demonstrated a marked reduction in mitral regurgitation (Figures 3 and 4).

Postoperative status of edge‐to‐edge mitral valve repair.

By the fifth day of hospitalization (December 30, 2024), the patient exhibited significant clinical improvement, with a PaO₂ of 110 mmHg (FiO2 50%) and reduced pulmonary exudation on repeat chest CT (Figure 5). Lung ultrasound assessment predominantly revealed A‐lines, prompting discontinuation of VV‐ECMO support. Concurrently, norepinephrine dosage was tapered to 0.05 μg/kg/min. On the sixth day (January 1, 2025), follow‐up chest radiography demonstrated substantial resolution of pulmonary abnormalities (Figure 6), with an oxygenation index exceeding 350. Ventilator support was minimized, sedation was withdrawn, and the patient regained full consciousness, allowing for extubation and initiation of high‐flow nasal cannula oxygen therapy. Norepinephrine was discontinued, and renal function remained stable, with urine output ranging between 100 and 300 mL/h, leading to cessation of continuous venovenous hemofiltration (CVVH).

Outcome

On the eighth day of hospitalization (January 3, 2025), the patient was transferred to the Coronary Care Unit (CCU) for ongoing management. Serial echocardiographic examinations revealed persistent mild mitral regurgitation, accompanied by a progressive decline in troponin I and NT‐proBNP levels. The patient demonstrated clinical improvement and was subsequently discharged on the 14th hospital day (January 9, 2025) (Figure 7).

Discussion

Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO) is categorized into Veno‐venous Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (VV‐ECMO) and Veno‐arterial Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (VA‐ECMO). In recent years, the utilization of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) in intensive care settings has progressively expanded, accompanied by broadening clinical indications [3]. Venovenous ECMO (VV‐ECMO) is primarily indicated for severe respiratory failure refractory to conventional pharmacological interventions and mechanical ventilation [4], with established applications including acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), refractory hypoxemia/hypercapnia, acute lung injury, severe viral/bacterial pneumonia, and status asthmaticus [5, 6]. However, in contrast to venoarterial ECMO (VA‐ECMO), the clinical application of VV‐ECMO in acute myocardial infarction remains limited. Additional clinical evidence and research data are required to validate its therapeutic efficacy and facilitate wider clinical implementation.

In this case, despite early and aggressive coronary intervention in the patient presenting with acute myocardial infarction, rupture of the mitral valve papillary muscle occurred, precipitating severe mitral regurgitation. This complication subsequently led to refractory hypoxemia secondary to profound pulmonary edema and cardiogenic shock. Notably, despite maximal ventilatory support with high positive end‐expiratory pressure (PEEP) and 100% oxygen, the patient's oxygenation index remained critically low at 55 mmHg. According to the Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO) guidelines, VV‐ECMO should be considered when the oxygenation index persists below 80 mmHg despite optimal conventional management [7]. As the patient had already undergone intensive conventional therapy at the referring hospital prior to admission, we promptly initiated VV‐ECMO support following a thorough clinical evaluation. This intervention significantly improved oxygenation parameters, thereby providing additional time and reducing procedural risks for definitive management of the underlying condition.

This case is distinct from others in that VV‐ECMO was employed rather than venoarterial ECMO (VA‐ECMO), as the patient presented with severe respiratory failure. Despite optimal ventilator management, the patient's oxygenation index persisted below 80 mmHg, and computed tomography (CT) imaging revealed severe pulmonary edema with near‐total “white lung” consolidation, fulfilling the established criteria for VV‐ECMO initiation. VV‐ECMO effectively substitutes pulmonary gas exchange, thereby augmenting oxygen content within the pulmonary circulation. This mechanism reduces pulmonary vascular resistance and pulmonary artery pressure while optimizing pulmonary hemodynamics. Although the patient exhibited cardiogenic shock, hemodynamic stability was achieved with low‐dose norepinephrine support, precluding the need for VA‐ECMO. Furthermore, VA‐ECMO increases left ventricular afterload due to retrograde aortic flow, potentially inducing left ventricular dilation and elevated left atrial pressure, which may exacerbate pulmonary edema [8, 9] (Figure 8). Thus, the favorable clinical outcome in this case was primarily attributable to the timely and aggressive implementation of VV‐ECMO support.

Mitral valve papillary muscle rupture represents a rare yet critical complication of acute myocardial infarction, posing significant life‐threatening risks with high mortality rates in the absence of prompt and aggressive intervention. Early surgical management has been demonstrated to improve survival outcomes. According to the 2021 American Heart Association scientific statement, emergency mitral valve replacement is the recommended primary treatment for this condition. However, surgical intervention may be contraindicated or deemed high‐risk for certain patients, in which case transcatheter edge‐to‐edge mitral valve repair (M‐TEER) may serve as a viable alternative [10]. As a minimally invasive approach, M‐TEER provides a therapeutic option for high‐risk patients with mitral regurgitation [11]. Clinical evidence indicates that mitral valve repair in post‐myocardial infarction patients with acute mitral regurgitation is both feasible and safe, offering hemodynamic improvement and reduced one‐year mortality and rehospitalization rates compared to conservative management [12]. In this case, the patient presented with severe pulmonary edema and markedly diminished oxygenation upon admission. Following immediate initiation of VV‐ECMO support by our specialized emergency team, M‐TEER was successfully performed after multidisciplinary assessment, resulting in substantial clinical improvement.

Conclusion

Papillary muscle rupture following PCI for acute myocardial infarction, though uncommon, is associated with high mortality and warrants clinical vigilance. Surgical intervention remains the primary therapeutic approach, demonstrating significant improvements in patient survival rates. However, cases complicated by severe respiratory and circulatory failure present substantial surgical challenges, where extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) may extend surgical candidacy. In this case, the patient exhibited concurrent respiratory and circulatory failure, with respiratory dysfunction being predominant. Early initiation of venovenous ECMO (VV‐ECMO) prior to the onset of severe circulatory compromise, coupled with aggressive mitral valve repair, resulted in successful treatment. This case provides valuable insights: (1) Rigorous post‐PCI monitoring, including transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography, is essential for acute myocardial infarction patients; (2) Timely VV‐ECMO implementation may mitigate complications and facilitate early surgical intervention; (3) For severe mitral regurgitation secondary to papillary muscle rupture, the combination of VV‐ECMO support and mitral valve repair represents a viable therapeutic alternative.
