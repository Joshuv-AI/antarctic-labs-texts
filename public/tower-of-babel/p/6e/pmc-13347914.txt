Prediction of in-hospital cardiac arrest on general wards using calibrated machine learning
Authors: Wen-Ying Yu, Mei-Li Pan, Chung-Yu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify time-windowed clinical predictors of in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) and develop a temporally validated, calibrated machine-learning early warning for general wards.MethodsRetrospective matched case–control study at a tertiary hospital in Taiwan (2019–2021) including 115 IHCA cases and 115 controls matched on age and ward. Patients with do-not-resuscitate (DNR) orders were excluded. Demographics, comorbidities and laboratory results were extracted from electronic health records. Vital signs and level of consciousness were sampled in 16–24, 8–16 and 1–8-hour windows before the index time and summarised as the modified early warning score (MEWS). Models were trained with fivefold cross-validation and isotonic probability calibration and tested on a temporally held-out 2021 cohort.ResultsAtrial fibrillation (OR 6.30), heart failure (OR 2.98), end-stage renal disease (OR 2.54), potassium (OR 1.81 per 1 mEq/L) and white blood count (OR 1.06 per 1 k/µL) were independent predictors. MEWS was associated with IHCA up to 16 hours, whereas in the final 8-hour lower SpO₂ predominated (OR 1.30 per 1% decrease; 95% CI 1.08 to 1.54). Calibrated XGBoost achieved Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUROC) curve 0.89 and average precision (AP) 0.88; at sensitivity 0.95, specificity was 0.47 in the matched test set.ConclusionsCombining comorbidity burden, laboratory indices and time-windowed physiology enabled high-sensitivity IHCA warning. Because matching inflates event prevalence, AP, positive predictive value and calibration require recalibration to real-world prevalence; prospective multicentre validation is needed.Keywords: Machine Learning; Data Interpretation, Statistical

Introduction

In-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) remains a major global health concern. In the USA, about 292 000 patients experience IHCA each year, only 19.1% survive to discharge, and nearly one-third of survivors have neurological impairment.1 2 In Asia, IHCA incidence ranges from roughly 1–20 events per 1000 hospital admissions, reflecting differences in study design, healthcare system maturity, do-not-resuscitate (DNR) practices and illness severity among hospitalised populations.3,5 In Taiwan, the 2023 Patient Safety Reporting System recorded 838 unexpected cardiac arrest events, most occurring in general wards (73.5%), with a mortality rate of 59.8%.6 Although advances in resuscitation have improved short-term outcomes, many survivors still experience long-term neurological dysfunction requiring intensive care, creating substantial medical, familial and societal burdens.7

Patient characteristics and comorbidities are also associated with IHCA risk. The mean age of affected patients is about 66 years, with men accounting for 58% and women 42% of cases.8 9 Cardiovascular and pulmonary diseases are the most common comorbidities, followed by renal dysfunction, diabetes, hypertension and stroke.8 About 50%–60% of IHCAs are cardiac in origin, whereas 15%–40% are respiratory in origin.9 Laboratory abnormalities linked to IHCA include sodium, potassium and calcium imbalance.10 Elevated inflammatory markers, such as white blood count (WBC), and non-iatrogenic hypoglycaemia have also been associated with adverse outcomes and may signal underlying deterioration.11 12

Many cardiac arrests and unexpected clinical deteriorations are preceded by subtle changes in physiological and neurological status, such as abnormal respiratory rate, pulse, oxygenation and altered consciousness, which can be detected within 24 hours before the event.13 14 Early warning systems (EWS) operationalise these short-term warning signs using routinely measured vital signs and neurological status. The Modified Early Warning Score (MEWS) aggregates temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, systolic blood pressure, oxygen saturation, oxygen use and consciousness into a single score to support escalation decisions.15 However, conventional EWS primarily emphasise acute physiologic instability, generally do not incorporate comorbidity burden or routine laboratory indices, and are usually implemented as score thresholds rather than calibrated probabilities for IHCA,16,20 which limits their ability to provide individualised risk prediction and reduces clinical interpretability.

Data-driven models have attempted to extend EWS by using richer electronic health record inputs, including nested case–control approaches and machine-learning models for ward deterioration and IHCA prediction.16,20 Recent studies have applied machine learning to predict IHCA. One study developed a model using early hospitalisation data,21 while another incorporated multimodal physiological and clinical data from intensive care settings.22 However, these studies differed in patient populations, timing of prediction and model inputs. Most focused primarily on discrimination rather than calibration, limiting clinical interpretability. Further research is needed to develop well-calibrated models integrating comorbidities, laboratory results and time-windowed physiological data for general ward patients.

Although these methods have shown promising discrimination, their predictor sets, target populations and prediction horizons vary substantially, and many reports emphasise discrimination more than probability calibration.16,20 Current tools, therefore, remain difficult to compare across settings and may be insufficient for bedside implementation when clinicians need interpretable risk estimates that integrate baseline comorbidity burden, laboratory status and evolving short-term physiology. Traditional MEWS neither includes laboratory indicators nor emphasises probability calibration. Accordingly, this study aims to: (1) propose a modified EWS; (2) provide evidence for probability calibration and identify clinical predictors; (3) develop and compare machine learning models for early warning.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective matched case–control study was conducted at National Taiwan University Hospital Yunlin Branch, a 929-bed medical centre. The analytic study period for case–control sampling and model development was 2019 to 2021. Hospital ward occupancy (81.35%) and mean length of stay (6.57 days) are reported for 2021 as a representative preintervention year. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of National Taiwan University Hospital (IRB Number: 202208003 RIND).

Participants

Medical records from adult medical and surgical general wards were reviewed retrospectively. We excluded admissions from the emergency department, intensive care units, obstetrics/paediatrics, psychiatry and nursing-home/long-term care settings. Eligible patients were adults aged ≥20 years with hospital stays of at least 24 hours; those with DNR orders were excluded. IHCA cases were defined as ward cardiac arrests requiring resuscitation, such as chest compressions and/or defibrillation. For each case, one age-matched control without IHCA was selected from the same ward and calendar period. Controls were assigned an index time (T0) corresponding to the matched case’s event time, and predictors were extracted using the same prespecified preindex windows. Annual IHCA incidence was calculated separately from the full eligible ward cohort as the number of ward IHCAs divided by total eligible ward admissions, rather than from the matched case–control sample.

Sample size

Sample size was estimated using G*Power for logistic regression, assuming an OR of 1.5, alpha=0.05, and power=0.95, yielding a minimum of 230 patients. We identified 115 patients who experienced IHCA in the general wards during 2019–2021. For each case, one age-matched and ward-matched control was selected, yielding 230 patients (115 cases and 115 controls). Age matching was used because age is a strong confounder for IHCA risk, and matching improves comparability and statistical efficiency in a small retrospective dataset. We acknowledge that this sample is modest for complex machine-learning development. We used a matched design, cross-validation and temporal validation to improve robustness.

Data collection

Demographics, comorbidities, laboratory results, physiological and neurocognitive parameters were extracted from electronic health records. MEWS was calculated from seven routinely collected parameters (temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, systolic blood pressure, oxygen saturation, supplemental oxygen use and level of consciousness) (online supplemental table 1). Predictor variables were aligned to the index time (IHCA for cases, T0 for controls) and extracted in three prespecified windows: 16–24 hour, 8–16 hour and 1–8 hour prior to the index time (online supplemental table 2). Laboratory values were defined as the most recent measurement prior to the index time. When multiple physiological observations were available within a window, the value closest to the abnormal range (the worst value) was extracted. Observed-value completeness for the time-windowed physiological predictors before imputation is summarised in online supplemental table 3. Missingness was concentrated in SpO2, whereas the other physiological variables were nearly complete.

Statistical and machine learning analysis

Data preprocessing

All patient data were anonymised prior to analysis. Direct identifiers, including names, medical record numbers and personal identifiers, were removed. Each patient was assigned a unique study ID, and all data were processed in a secure, access-controlled hospital system. Continuous variables were standardised, and categorical variables were one-hot encoded. Missing values were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations. To minimise information leakage, imputation models were fit only to the training data and applied to the held-out test set; outcomes were not used as predictors.

Data partitioning and validation

The dataset was divided temporally to approximate real-world deployment. Matched case—control pairs from 2019 to 2020 were used for model development, hyperparameter tuning and internal calibration. Matched pairs from 2021 served as an independent temporal test cohort. No post-2021 data were included in model training or testing. External validation in other hospitals and prospective evaluation with real-world event prevalence are warranted to assess generalisability and clinical impact.

Model development

We developed four models (logistic regression, random forest, HistGradientBoosting and XGBoost). Given the modest sample size, we limited the model set to commonly used tabular methods and interpreted comparative performance as exploratory. Hyperparameters were tuned using a fivefold cross-validation grid search on the training set. To respect the matched design and avoid leakage, each matched case–control pair was kept within the same fold during cross-validation.

Probability calibration

Because raw probability outputs from machine learning models are often poorly calibrated, isotonic regression was used to calibrate the predicted probabilities using the training data, and the calibrated probabilities were then applied to the temporal test set. All models underwent isotonic regression calibration to enhance the reliability of predicted probabilities. This calibration step was intended to improve the alignment of probabilities within the matched study sample. Because case–control sampling distorts event prevalence, calibrated probabilities should be interpreted cautiously and require recalibration before clinical deployment.

Model evaluation

Model performance was assessed on the independent temporal test set. Discrimination was evaluated using AUROC and AUPRC/average precision. Calibration was assessed using calibration plots and the Brier score. Because case–control sampling inflates event prevalence, prevalence-dependent metrics (eg, positive predictive value (PPV)/negative predictive value (NPV)/precision and AUPRC) and probability calibration should be interpreted within the study dataset for model comparison and recalibrated to real-world prevalence for deployment. To reflect the clinical priority of minimising missed IHCAs, we illustrated operating points at a sensitivity of approximately 0.95 and complemented threshold-based reporting with ROC/PR curves and decision curve analysis.17,20

Study period and scope

This study was conducted before the introduction of the MEWS-triggered rapid response team (RRT) pathway. Accordingly, all predictive modelling and statistical comparisons were confined to this preintervention dataset. The subsequent RRT implementation (from 2022 onward) is mentioned only to provide contextual background on institutional quality-improvement measures and was not analysed as part of the predictive model. Any temporal trends after 2022 were descriptive and non-causal, reported separately from the analytic results.

Statistical analyses

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between IHCA and control patients, accounting for 1:1 matching (McNemar tests for categorical variables and paired t tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for continuous variables, as appropriate). These descriptive comparisons were used to characterise the matched groups and were not used as a univariate screening step for model inclusion. Logistic regression models were specified a priori in clinically defined blocks: (1) baseline demographics and comorbidities, (2) baseline laboratory variables and (3) separate time-windowed physiological models for 16–24 hours, 8–16 hours and 1–8 hours before the index time. Logistic regression results are reported as ORs with 95% CIs. Analyses were conducted using Python (scikit-learn V.1.4.0, XGBoost V.2.0.3) and R (V.4.3.1). We examined potential collinearity among predictors, particularly MEWS and its component physiological variables, using correlation diagnostics and interpreted overlapping variables cautiously. Because predictors were prespecified based on clinical plausibility, we did not apply formal multiple-comparison adjustments and considered findings hypothesis-generating.

Results

Association of variables with IHCA

Table 1 and online supplemental table 4 present descriptive comparisons between the matched IHCA and control groups and were not used as a univariate variable-selection step. Analysis of patient demographics revealed a significant difference in sex distribution between groups, whereas no differences were observed in mean age or length of hospital stay. With respect to comorbidities, atrial fibrillation was present in 22.6% of patients in the IHCA group compared with 3.5% in the non-IHCA group (χ²=18.55, p<0.001). Heart failure was more common in the IHCA group (29.6% vs 8.7%, χ²=16.18, p<0.001), as was end-stage renal disease (19.1% vs 7.8%, χ²=6.30, p=0.01). No significant differences were found for coronary artery disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diabetes mellitus, hypertension or stroke.

Laboratory data showed that the IHCA group had a higher mean serum potassium level compared with the non-IHCA group (4.0 vs 3.85 mEq/L; t=2.47, p=0.01). WBC was also significantly higher in the IHCA group (11.02 vs 8.59 k/µL; t=3.18, p<0.001). No significant differences were observed in sodium, calcium or glucose levels (table 1).

Regarding physiological parameters within 24 hours before IHCA, patients in the IHCA group demonstrated significantly higher body temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate and MEWS scores compared with controls. Oxygen saturation was significantly lower in the IHCA group only during the 8 hours preceding the event, whereas systolic blood pressure did not differ significantly (online supplemental table 4).

Predictors of IHCA

In the prespecified baseline multivariable model, male sex, atrial fibrillation, heart failure and end-stage renal disease were independently associated with higher odds of IHCA. Male patients had 2.2 times the odds of IHCA as females. Patients with a history of atrial fibrillation had 6.3-fold higher odds, those with heart failure had 2.98-fold higher odds, and those with end-stage renal disease had 2.54-fold higher odds compared with patients without these conditions.

In the prespecified laboratory model, higher serum potassium concentration and WBC were independently associated with higher odds of IHCA. For every 1 mEq/L increase in potassium, the odds of IHCA increased 1.81-fold, while each 1 k/µL increase in WBC count was associated with a 1.06-fold increase in the odds of IHCA (table 2).

In separate time-windowed physiological models adjusted for sex and comorbidity variables, MEWS was associated with higher odds of IHCA in the 16–24-hour and 8–16-hour windows. Each 1-point increase in MEWS was associated with 1.22-fold and 1.21-fold higher odds of IHCA, respectively. Within the 1–8-hour window, lower oxygen saturation was the only variable that remained associated with IHCA; each 1% decrease in SpO2 corresponded to approximately a 1.3-fold increase in odds. Given overlap between MEWS and its component physiological variables, this pattern should not be interpreted as evidence that SpO2 replaces MEWS. Rather, it suggests that late oxygen desaturation may mark imminent deterioration, while MEWS captures broader early warning signals over longer windows (online supplemental table 5).

Model performance comparison

Four models (logistic regression, random forest, HistGradientBoosting and XGBoost) were trained with fivefold cross-validation and isotonic calibration. In the temporally held-out test set, calibrated XGBoost performed best (AUROC 0.88–0.90; average precision (AP) 0.88) and achieved the highest specificity at sensitivity≥0.95 (0.45–0.50). We, therefore, selected calibrated XGBoost (threshold: 0.15; sensitivity: 0.94–0.95, specificity: 0.45) (online supplemental table 6), noting that matching inflates event prevalence, so PPV/precision, percentile rank (PR) metrics and calibration require prevalence recalibration before deployment. Given the modest sample size and single-centre design, these comparative ML results should be interpreted as exploratory. Online supplemental figure 1 shows ROC curves for all models, and online supplemental figure 2 shows precision–recall curves (matched prevalence) for comparative purposes.

Discussion

Three main findings emerged. First, comorbidities and routine laboratory indices improved prediction beyond short-term physiological changes. Second, MEWS was associated with IHCA earlier in the prearrest period, whereas late oxygen desaturation indicated imminent deterioration. Third, calibrated XGBoost achieved the best discrimination and high sensitivity in temporal testing, although absolute risk estimates require recalibration because of the matched case–control design. Male sex was associated with IHCA, but residual confounding is possible.2 23 Age and length of stay were not associated with IHCA, potentially reflecting the matched design. As the model was developed using pre-RRT data, subsequent reductions in IHCA cannot be attributed to the model. Prospective studies should evaluate its effectiveness when integrated with standardised response pathways.

Comorbidity profiles demonstrated that atrial fibrillation, heart failure and end-stage renal disease were associated with higher odds of IHCA. Atrial fibrillation, heart failure and end-stage renal disease have been associated with increased risks of cardiac arrest, arrhythmia and in-hospital mortality, likely reflecting greater cardiovascular burden and physiological vulnerability.23,25 Nevertheless, comorbidity associations in observational data may be influenced by unmeasured confounding (eg, overall illness severity, treatment limitations or monitoring intensity). These findings should, therefore, be interpreted as risk markers that may help prioritise surveillance rather than as causal determinants.

Laboratory findings further highlighted acute physiological risk. Higher potassium levels and elevated WBC were independently associated with higher odds of IHCA. Although potassium values in both groups fell within the normal reference range, patients in the IHCA group had significantly higher concentrations, underscoring that even mild elevations within the normal range may increase arrhythmogenic risk. Even subtle increases in potassium levels may increase arrhythmogenic risk, as potassium plays a critical role in myocardial excitability and cardiac conduction.10 24 Likewise, elevated WBC counts can indicate systemic inflammation, a condition linked to poorer outcomes in critically ill patients. Our findings are in line with previous reports identifying WBC elevation as a marker of clinical deterioration and worse outcomes.26

Regarding physiological parameters, MEWS provides an early integrated signal of physiological instability, whereas oxygen desaturation in late windows may indicate imminent clinical deterioration. Patients in the IHCA group had lower mean oxygen saturation (94% vs 97% in controls), with some values as low as 70%, indicating hypoxemia as a critical signal of near-term deterioration. MEWS scores were higher up to 16 hours before IHCA (online supplemental table 4). However, within the last 8 hours, oxygen desaturation was the strongest marker in the multivariable physiological model (online supplemental table 5). Because MEWS includes oxygenation and other vital-sign components, this late-window pattern may partly reflect collinearity and should not be interpreted as evidence that SpO2 should replace MEWS. Rather, we interpret it as reflecting imminent clinical deterioration, whereas MEWS may provide a broader warning signal earlier in the prearrest period. Temporal patterns across the 16–24-hour, 8–16-hour and 1–8-hour windows should be viewed as hypothesis-generating and warrant confirmation in larger prospective cohorts. These findings are consistent with prior studies, showing that abnormal vital signs, particularly deteriorating respiratory status and oxygenation, often precede ward IHCA and support the use of early warning scores.27 28

To improve predictive accuracy, we applied machine learning models, including logistic regression, random forest, histogram-based gradient boosting and XGBoost. XGBoost achieved the highest discrimination and the most favourable high-sensitivity operating characteristics in our temporally held-out test set. We illustrated operating points with a sensitivity of ≥95% because ward-based EWS prioritise avoiding missed catastrophic events. However, the choice of operating threshold should ultimately be guided by local resources, acceptable alert burden and prospective clinical evaluation. Because our matched case–control sampling inflates event prevalence, PR metrics and PPV/NPV from this dataset are not directly generalisable to real-world deployment without prevalence recalibration. Cross-study performance comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because model inputs, event definitions and patient populations differ across settings.29 30

Explainability remains relevant for future implementation, but it was neither a primary objective nor an evaluated outcome in the present study. Methods such as SHAP may help clinicians understand model outputs and manage alert burden during future deployment.31,33 However, we regard explainability here as a downstream implementation consideration rather than a central finding of this analysis.

This study has several limitations. First, its single-centre design may limit generalisability. Second, the matched case–control design inflates event prevalence; therefore, absolute risk estimates and calibration require recalibration in a full ward cohort. Third, the modest sample size may increase the risk of overfitting despite cross-validation, temporal hold-out testing and post hoc calibration. Fourth, the observed decline in IHCA after MEWS-based and RRT-based care was descriptive and does not establish causality. Fifth, retrospective electronic health record data are subjected to documentation errors, unmeasured confounding and variable observation frequency. Sixth, the interval between the last laboratory test and IHCA was unavailable. Finally, some physiological data, including oxygen saturation, were incomplete. Prospective multicentre validation and recalibration in ward cohorts with real-world event prevalence are warranted.

Conclusion

Using a temporally validated preintervention dataset, this study identified clinically relevant predictors of IHCA and found that combining comorbidities, laboratory indices and time-windowed physiology can support high-sensitivity early warning. Among the evaluated models, calibrated XGBoost performed best on the temporally held-out test set. However, because the matched case–control design inflates event prevalence and the sample size was modest, recalibration and prospective multicentre validation are essential before clinical implementation.
