Performance of eco-friendly geopolymer loadbearing bricks for thermally comfortable structures
Authors: Hala Emad Elden Fouad, Noha Fathi Elgamal, Hany A Dahish, Eyad Alsuhaibani, Ahmed Elmannaey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLoad-bearing masonry offers an eco-friendly alternative by reducing the use of cement and steel in the construction industry. However, bricks that satisfy structural requirements (high compressive strength and low water absorption) usually have high density and thermal conductivity, leading to greater energy demand for indoor heating and cooling. This study addresses that challenge by developing sustainable load-bearing bricks with improved thermal insulation. Bricks were fabricated and tested using ordinary concrete and geopolymer mixes based on industrial waste (fly ash and slag), incorporating lightweight coarse aggregate and air-entraining admixtures in various combinations. The compressive strength, water absorption, and thermal conductivity of each brick type were evaluated, and microstructural analysis (SEM/EDX) was performed. The results show that a fly ash-based geopolymer brick incorporating an air-entraining additive achieved the best balance between structural and thermal performance, exhibiting a compressive strength of 43 MPa, water absorption of 4%, and thermal conductivity of 0.7 W/m·K. These values satisfy load-bearing masonry requirements while providing lower thermal conductivity compared with typical conventional concrete bricks reported in the literature. The improved performance is attributed to the combined effect of geopolymer binder formation and the presence of distributed air pores that reduce heat transfer. These findings demonstrate that waste-derived geopolymer binders combined with lightweight aggregates and air-entraining admixtures provide a promising approach for producing high-performance, energy-efficient masonry units.Keywords: Geopolymer bricks; Energy saving; Slag; Fly ash; Thermal insulation; Lightweight aggregate, SustainabilitySubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

The increasing energy consumption in providing thermal comfort inside buildings is causing major negative effects on both the environment and the economy. Therefore, the use of thermal insulation materials greatly helps in reducing the amount of energy consumption1. Thermal insulation is the practice of reducing heat transfer (both gain and loss) between a building’s interior and its exterior environment. Bricks, a common building material, can be effectively fabricated to enhance energy efficiency, reduce heating and cooling costs, and create a comfortable indoor environment2.

Load-bearing bricks or blocks, which support wall-bearing buildings, are required to possess high compressive strength. The fabrication of such strong and dense bricks is always on account of their favorable thermal-insulation characteristics. Therefore, the production of the traditional bearing bricks has major detrimental effects on the environment: the first is the depletion of the natural resources that are consumed in producing clay or cement bricks3; the second is the high energy consumption and the high CO₂ emissions involved with this process; and the third is the higher thermal conductivity of the bricks and, consequently, the higher costs for creating a comfortable indoor environment inside these buildings. It was recently reported that the cooling and heating of buildings represents the largest energy consumption sector worldwide4.

To mitigate the effects of the production process of bearing bricks on the environment, geopolymer concrete (which is formed of the reaction of waste pozzolanic materials such as fly ash (FA), metakaolin, and silica fume with high-alkalinity sodium- or potassium-based activators) can be utilized instead of clay or cement3,5–10. The favorable mechanical and high durability characteristics of geopolymers encouraged the usage of geopolymers in a wide range of applications: fire- and aggressive media-resistant material, corrosion-resistant concrete11,12, and repair material for structural elements13,14. Moreover, the same idea was extended to cover soil stabilization applications15.

As the thermal conductivity of conventional concrete and clay bricks may reach about 2 and 1 W/m.K, respectively3,6,16, the use of entrapped air in the brick body presents an effective practice for boosting brick thermal insulation, as air acts as a thermal barrier17. This entrapped air can be introduced in the brick in two ways; the first is by modifying the shape of the brick itself by embedding air voids17. The second way involves incorporating air-entrapping additives into the brick material6,18. Furthermore, the use of lightweight aggregates that possess low thermal conductivity may play a key role in enhancing brick thermal insulation, as the thermal conductivity of normal aggregates may reach about 3.5 W/m.K16.

A study suggested the use of vertical and horizontal perforations in clay bricks with paper sludge additions. Then, the thermal behavior of the two configurations was numerically analyzed. The study concluded that using vertically perforated bricks with a 10% paper sludge additive was the most effective method for improving the thermal insulation of the bricks17. Another work concentrated on the fabrication of ferrosilicon slag geopolymer bricks, and various amounts of alumina waste were added. The main finding was that the increase of alumina waste enhanced the compressive strength of bricks at the age of 28 days and at the same time decreased brick thermal conductivity. This was explained as the addition of alumina boosted the geopolymerization process and consumed the unreacted silica, which can be considered the main reason for the bricks’ higher thermal conductivity3. But on the other hand, the maximum achieved compressive strength was less than 11 MPa, which is less than ASTM requirements for load-bearing bricks19.

Third research focused on using foaming agents to produce porous geopolymer bricks (i.e., sodium lauryl ether sulfate), and it was found that adding 5% of this foaming agent led to a decrease in the thermal conductivity of the brick to 0.32 W/m.K and an increase in compressive strength up to 7 MPa6. The high thermal conductivity of normal aggregate encouraged the researchers to replace it with waste materials such as rubber tires, glass, plastics, and organic fibers. A study adopted this trend and concluded that using these waste materials as aggregates can significantly reduce the conductivity of the brick but, at the same time, decreases its structural performance16. Furthermore, lightweight aggregate concrete with air-entraining additives of 0.5 to 1% showed excellent thermal and acoustic architectural purposes20.

The previous studies demonstrate that several approaches, such as the incorporation of foaming agents, waste aggregates, or perforated geometries, can significantly reduce the thermal conductivity of masonry units. However, these approaches often lead to a substantial reduction in compressive strength, producing bricks that cannot satisfy the structural requirements of load-bearing walls. For example, geopolymer bricks produced using foaming agents or lightweight waste aggregates typically exhibit compressive strengths below 10 MPa, which is lower than the requirements for structural masonry units specified in ASTM standards. Therefore, there remains a need for masonry units that simultaneously (i) satisfy load-bearing strength requirements and (ii) exhibit reduced thermal conductivity to lower envelope heat transfer. Here, this study addresses this gap by systematically evaluating how binder type (conventional concrete vs. FA/slag geopolymers), controlled porosity (air entrainment and perforations), and lightweight aggregate influence compressive strength, water absorption, and thermal conductivity, and by identifying combinations that achieve structural-grade performance with improved insulation.

Experimental program

Materials

In the current study, geopolymers were prepared based on FA and ground granulated blast furnace slag (S) in addition to conventional ordinary Portland cement (OPC) concrete mix (CEM I/52.5 N) in accordance with ASTM C15021. Clean crushed dolomite with a specific gravity of 2.6 and a nominal maximum size of 12 mm was used as coarse aggregate, and silica sand with a fineness modulus of 2.5 was utilized as fine aggregate for both cement and geopolymer concrete mixes. Both coarse and fine aggregates are in accordance with the quality and grading requirements of ASTM-C3322. To activate geopolymer chemical reactions, a mixture of sodium silicate (SS) and sodium hydroxide (SH) was utilized with a constant volumetric ratio of 3:1, for all geopolymer mixes. Sodium silicate was in the form of white pellets of 99% purity, while sodium hydroxide was mixed with water to prepare a 12-molar solution; this molar ratio was adopted based on a previous finding12.

To enhance the thermal characteristics of the concrete mixes, Sika Light Crete (SLC) was used to produce lightweight concrete mixes with densities ranging from 800 to 1600 kg/m3 and air pores that can safely reach 35%23. Also, lightweight coarse aggregate (LWCA) was used in a number of mixes instead of dolomite coarse aggregate. This type of aggregate is expanded clay with a porous surface and a maximum size of nearly 8 mm; its dry density is about 0.9 gm/cm3, and it has an absorption of ca. 17.5%. Mix proportions and sampling

All brick specimens were cast in molds with nominal dimensions of 200 × 100 × 60 mm. In addition to solid units, perforated brick specimens were also prepared with three uniformly distributed circular voids of 30 mm diameter, as shown in Fig. 2. The perforated configuration was selected because it represents a common commercial brick geometry used in the Egyptian market and, more importantly, allows the effect of internal voids on the thermal and mechanical performance of the units to be evaluated. The introduction of perforations creates controlled air spaces within the brick body, which may reduce heat transfer and improve thermal insulation. The mixing process was manually implemented; the activator was prepared a day before the mixing process to exhaust the exothermic temperature resulting from the chemical reaction between water and the activator. After that, the binder was mixed with sand and coarse aggregate for 3 min; then, the solution was gradually added to the mixture, and the mixing process continued for an additional 5 min until the mixture was homogenous. Finally, the blends were molded and compacted using an electric vibrator for 1 min.

The samples were demolded one day after forming; then, OPC samples were cured in a water bath at ambient temperature, while geopolymer samples were left in air until the time of testing. Testing

After preparing nine replicates of each brick type, the OPC samples were drawn out of curing water at the age of 28 days. Then, the samples were oven-dried for 24 h at 100 °C, and their dry weight was recorded. Also, geopolymer samples were submerged in water for 24 h, and the water absorption of all bricks was obtained. After that, the samples were tested in compression to assess the compressive strength based on their net area (Fig. 3)24. To determine the thermal conductivity of the bricks, a heating system was fabricated in the Misr University Science and Technology Materials Laboratory, and its experimental setup was based on steady-state heat conduction through the brick25.

Compressive strength test of brick samples.

The heating system consists of a heat source, which is a lamp of 10-watt power encased in a stainless-steel casing. This casing was adjusted directly beside the tested brick sample, which was carefully insulated and equipped with two Resistance Temperature Detectors (RTDs) (Omega-PT100 Class A sensor) spaced at 70 mm on the brick surface, and their signals were recorded using a four-channel data logger (OM-CP-QUADRTD). The whole setup was insulated with an outer layer of insulation to guarantee one-dimensional heat flow through the brick. This configuration was designed to minimize lateral heat losses and ensure that the measured heat transfer occurs primarily through the brick specimen. The spacing between the two RTD sensors (70 mm) was selected to allow accurate measurement of the temperature gradient along the heat flow path. Such steady-state heat conduction methods are commonly used for evaluating the thermal properties of building materials.

Results and discussion

Unit weight

The dry weight and the dry unit weight of each mix (based on net volume) are shown in Fig. 6. It is obvious that the densities of the bricks with and without voids are close enough, with a difference of no more than 1.5% (i.e., CB versus CB-V and SB-SLC versus SB-SLC-V). Furthermore, the least dry density was CB-LW, followed by SB-LW, and this was expected, as the density of LWCA was too low compared to dolomite aggregates. On the other hand, SB samples have the highest dry density of about 2.4 t/m3. This can be attributed to the insufficient levels of alumina in the slag, which is mainly responsible for the release of hydrogen during the geopolymerization process in the presence of calcium hydroxide and sodium silicate and generates pores in the matrix7. The effect of SLC material on the density was not as high as expected, and the reduction in brick density was in the range of 6 to 7% for all brick types. The rigorous compaction of the brick samples may be the main reason for the minor effect of the SLC additive on density.

Dry weight and density of different brick mixes.

Water absorption

Figure 7 presents the absorption percentage of the bricks, and it is obvious that the highest absorption is related to LWCA mixes with values of 8.9% and 5.4% for CB-LW and SB-LW, respectively. The lower density of CB compared to SB means that there are more voids in the CB matrix than in SB, which allowed the water to ingress the body of the brick and reach the LWCA voids. The presence of voids also increased the absorption percentage by about 22% for CB, 55% for both SB and FB, and 11% for SFB compared to solid bricks. This increase can be explained as the exposed surface areas to water of perforated bricks are larger than solid bricks, as the surface area of perforated bricks is higher than solid bricks by about 22%. Which facilitates greater water penetration compared with solid bricks. Furthermore, the wide variation in the change in the absorption between solid and voided bricks may be attributed to the manufacturing and especially the compaction process, as it is not possible to effectively finish holes’ inner surface and to guarantee its smoothness. Moreover, the absorbed weight of water for all brick types does not exceed 125 kg/m3, which complies with the ASTM standards for load-bearing bricks19.

Absorption of different brick mixes.

Compressive strength

For the sake of comparison of bricks’ compressive strength, Fig. 8 shows the compressive strength of each brick type. It is obvious that all the brick types fulfill the compressive strength requirements of ASTM for load-bearing bricks19. The highest compressive strength is related to the SB sample, about 72 MPa, as it has the highest density and the lowest void ratio, as formerly discussed. It is interesting to notice that several mixtures can be considered as high-strength concrete, as their strength nearly reaches or exceeds 60 MPa, i.e., SB, SB-V, SB-SLC, FB, and SFB26. These high compressive strength values are slightly higher than the available values in our previous work11–15. This can be attributed to brick dimension as its height is 60 mm, which leads to higher confinement under the jaws of the compression machines, which gives higher failure loads. The relatively high compressive strength observed in the fly ash-based geopolymer brick containing the air-entraining additive can be attributed to the formation of a dense geopolymeric gel network resulting from the effective activation of aluminosilicate phases in fly ash. SEM observations also will reveal a compact microstructure with improved bonding between the binder matrix and aggregates, which contributes to enhanced load-bearing capacity.

Compressive strength of different brick mixes.

On the other hand, the lowest strength is related to CB-LW, as the strength of the LWCA is too low, and the reduction ratio in compressive strength was noted to be in the range of 50–55% compared to solid brick mixes with no additives. The presence of voids lowers the strength of bricks compared to solid bricks for all brick types by a ratio ranging from 18 to 28%, and this can be attributed to stress concentration on the perforated surface during the loading process. Furthermore, the use of SLC material decreased the strength of all brick types by a value of 3.6% for CB, 6.7% for both SB and SFB; and by nearly 23% for FB compared to solid bricks. It can be noted that FB-SLC also has the least density when not considering LWCA samples, which may provide an acceptable reason for the high reduction in compressive strength compared to SB brick.

This may indicate heat leakage during testing of SB samples, although it was expected to have the highest k values, as it possesses the highest density among other samples. The relatively high thermal conductivity observed for the slag-based bricks can be attributed to their higher density and relatively compact microstructure compared with other brick mixes. Slag contains significant calcium content, which promotes the formation of dense reaction products that reduce internal pore volume within the binder matrix. Since heat transfer in solids occurs primarily through the solid skeleton of the material, the reduced porosity and improved particle packing in slag-based bricks result in higher thermal conductivity compared with other mixes containing more entrapped air or lightweight aggregates.

Also, the effect of voids on thermal conductivity is obvious, as the voids create a barrier against heat flow. For instance, the thermal conductivity of CB-V, SB-V, FB-V, and SFB-V was found to be 22%, 27%, 40%, and 28% less than CB, SB, FB, and SFB, respectively. In general, the voids slightly increase the required time to reach steady-state conditions. The same effect is also observed when using the SLC additive. The major effect on thermal conductivity occurred when using LWCA with CB-LW and SB-LW, as the conductivity decreased to about 0.7 W/m.K, resulting in decrease ratios of approximately 0.34 and 0.16 compared to CB and SB bricks. The additional voids in the aggregate itself helped enhance the brick’s thermal resistance.

A comparison of the investigated brick mixes indicates that their thermal and mechanical behavior is strongly influenced by the material composition and internal structure. Slag-based bricks (SB) exhibited the highest density and compressive strength, which explains their relatively higher thermal conductivity compared with other mixes. In contrast, fly ash-based bricks (FB) and mixtures incorporating air-entraining additives or lightweight aggregates exhibited lower density and improved thermal insulation due to the increased volume of internal pores. These findings indicate that the thermal conductivity of the bricks is closely related to their density and pore structure, while compressive strength is mainly governed by the compactness of the binder matrix and aggregate bonding. The microstructural observations presented later in this study confirm that mixes with lower thermal conductivity generally exhibit higher internal porosity.

The reduction in thermal conductivity observed in mixes incorporating air-entraining additives and lightweight aggregates can be attributed to the increased volume of entrapped air within the brick matrix. Since air possesses significantly lower thermal conductivity than solid mineral phases, the presence of distributed pores disrupts heat transfer pathways within the material. Additionally, the porous structure of lightweight aggregates further contributes to reducing heat conduction through the brick body.

SEM and EDX mapping

To thoroughly discuss the findings of the current study, SEM and EDX mapping were conducted for different brick samples in addition to the RB sample. The microstructural observations presented in this section are used to support the mechanical and thermal behavior discussed in the previous sections. Particular attention is given to the presence of pores, the morphology of hydration or geopolymerization products, and the distribution of major chemical elements within the brick matrix. These characteristics provide insight into the relationship between microstructure, density, compressive strength, and thermal conductivity of the investigated brick mixes. Figure 11 shows the morphology of the CB, CB-SLC, and CB-LW samples, in addition to XRD and EDX mapping. The Portland cement hydrated compounds in the CB sample can be observed in Fig. 11-a: CSH gel of irregular and needle-like shapes, CH (portlandite) of hexagonal plate-like crystals, and potentially minor needle-like shapes of ettringite. Furthermore, the traces of silicon and aluminum elements may refer to unhydrated cement compounds like C₂S, C₃S, and C₃A. In the case of the CB-SLC sample, Fig. 11-b shows that it has higher ratios of aluminum compared to the CB sample. This may indicate that the additive is an aluminum-based material that reacts with CH in cement and results in more pores in the matrix due to hydrogen release26. However, no visible evidence of expansive cracking or deterioration of the binder–aggregate interface was observed in the tested samples. The generated pores appeared to be relatively small and uniformly distributed within the matrix, which contributed to increased porosity without significantly compromising the structural integrity of the brick.

Moreover, the porous morphology of the area is obvious, and many voids are present in the shot. And for CB-LW in Fig. 11-c, a severe increase in the presence of silicon and aluminum elements compared to conventional Portland cement can be noticed. This can be explained by the presence of lightweight aggregates, which are clayey aggregates that are rich with silica and alumina compounds, and the SEM illustrates the aggregates’ porous nature. Also, the dramatic drop in the calcium element is due to the replacement of dolomite aggregates by clay-based aggregates.

The thermal conductivity values obtained in this study (approximately 0.7–1.2 W/m·K) are lower than those typically reported for conventional concrete bricks, which usually range between 1.5 and 2.0 W/m·K. Furthermore, the compressive strength achieved by the optimized geopolymer brick (43 MPa) exceeds the strengths reported in several studies on porous geopolymer bricks, which often range between 5 and 15 MPa. These comparisons demonstrate that the developed bricks provide a favorable balance between structural performance and thermal insulation.

Sustainability and environmental implications

This study supports sustainable construction through a dual pathway: reducing the embodied environmental impact of masonry units and improving operational energy efficiency in buildings. From an embodied-impact perspective, geopolymer bricks utilize industrial by-products (FA or S) as primary binders, reducing reliance on OPC and contributing to circular-economy material flows. From an operational perspective, the measured reduction in thermal conductivity for optimized mixes implies higher thermal resistance for the same wall thickness and therefore lower heat transfer through load-bearing walls. Consequently, these bricks can contribute to improved indoor thermal comfort and reduced heating/cooling energy demand, particularly in climates dominated by air-conditioning loads. While a full life-cycle assessment was beyond the scope of the present study, the combined embodied and operational benefits indicate that optimized geopolymer-based load-bearing bricks represent a promising strategy for low-carbon and thermally efficient building envelopes.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that sustainable geopolymer bricks can meet load-bearing requirements while offering enhanced thermal insulation. A range of green brick mixes was developed using slag-based, FA-based, and hybrid (slag and FA) geopolymer binders, along with design modifications such as internal voids (via air-entraining admixtures) and lightweight coarse aggregates. The study investigated the mechanical, thermal, and microstructural characteristics of load-bearing bricks produced using conventional concrete and geopolymer binders derived from fly ash and slag. Based on the experimental results, the following conclusions can be drawn:

Slag-based geopolymer bricks exhibited the highest compressive strength (up to 72 MPa) among the tested mixes due to their higher density, low water absorption, and relatively compact microstructure. But their high density led to thermal conductivity about twice that of conventional bricks, making them less suitable for improving indoor thermal comfort.

Overall, the results demonstrate that geopolymer (especially FA)-based bricks incorporating lightweight aggregates and controlled porosity can provide a favorable balance between structural performance and thermal insulation. And yielding durable load-bearing units that are significantly more thermally insulating than traditional concrete or clay bricks.

These findings have important real-world implications for sustainable construction; by replacing a portion of cement with industrial by-products and enhancing thermal performance, the developed bricks can lower both the embodied carbon and operational energy of buildings. They enable construction of thermally comfortable structures with reduced reliance on air conditioning, contributing to energy savings and greenhouse gas emission reductions.

Future work should explore using lightweight aggregates across all geopolymer formulations and evaluate full-scale masonry assemblies to verify combined structural and insulation performance in practice. Additionally, optimizing brick geometry (such as void distribution and shape) could further improve thermal efficiency without compromising strength, providing a roadmap for next-generation eco-friendly building materials.
