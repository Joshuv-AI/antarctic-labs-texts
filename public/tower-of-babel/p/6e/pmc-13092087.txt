Visceral segment interrupted constraining suture in physician-modified endografts for dissecting thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysms
Authors: Kenichi Hashizume, Mitsuharu Mori, Masataka Yamazaki, Toshiaki Yagami, Hirofumi Haida, Hideyuki Shimizu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSevere true lumen narrowing in chronic dissecting thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysms (dTAAAs) can compromise accurate fenestration alignment and hinder reliable bridging of the renovisceral branches. We evaluated the safety and feasibility of fenestrated endovascular aortic repair using a physician-modified endograft incorporating a visceral segment interrupted constraining suture. This permanent diameter-constraining modification is intended to stabilize the endograft within a narrowed true lumen and improve target vessel access. Sixteen patients with chronic dTAAA underwent elective repair using a low-profile Zenith Alpha Thoracic device. All 60 renovisceral branches were successfully bridged, resulting in 100% intraoperative technical success, defined as completion of the planned procedure with aneurysm sac sealing, with no 30-day mortality. Three patients required early reintervention for endoleak (1 type IC; 2 type IIIC). Follow-up imaging through 12 months demonstrated durable aneurysm exclusion and sustained branch patency. In this early experience, a visceral segment interrupted constraining suture provided reproducible diameter control and facilitated target vessel access in complex dTAAA treated by physician-modified endograft-based fenestrated endovascular aortic repair.Keywords: Chronic dissecting thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm, Endovascular aortic repair, Dissecting thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm, Fenestrated endovascular aortic repair, Physician-modified endograft

Physician-modified endografts (PMEGs) are an important option for treating complex aortic aneurysms, with reported low early mortality rates(2.6%) and durable target vessel patency rates (95%).1,2 Recent endovascular advances—including PMEGs and off-the-shelf (OTS) branched devices—have expanded treatment options with encouraging outcomes.3

In chronic dissecting thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysms (dTAAAs), prior reports have shown comparable technical success between dissecting and degenerative TAAAs.4,5 dTAAA procedures often require more complex catheter manipulation, and fluoroscopy times may be prolonged because of branch misalignment and incomplete graft expansion.

In our experience, bridging stent insertion can be challenging when fenestrations fail to expand fully within a severely narrowed true lumen (Fig 1). To mitigate these difficulties, we developed a novel permanent diameter-constraining modification using a visceral segment interrupted constraining suture (VICS) to stabilize the PMEG within the narrowed true lumen and facilitate safe renovisceral branch cannulation. Herein, we report the initial results of VICS-assisted four-fenestrated endovascular repair in 16 consecutive patients with chronic dTAAA.

Methods

Case report

Between June 2022 and March 2025, 16 patients with chronic dTAAA underwent endovascular repair using four-fenestrated PMEGs incorporating VICS. Indications included an aneurysm diameter of >55 mm, enlargement of >5 mm in 6 months, or symptomatic presentation (Table I); selected patients were treated at smaller diameters because of rapid aneurysm enlargement or high-risk morphological features associated with chronic dissection.

Procedures were performed at our hospital with institutional review board approval. Written informed consent for data publication was obtained from the patients.

Although three patients had Marfan syndrome, all were in the chronic phase with anatomically suitable visceral segments, and the proximal landing zone was secured within a previously implanted frozen elephant trunk, providing a stable prosthetic proximal seal. PMEGs were selected because open repair was considered prohibitively invasive owing to prior surgeries or comorbid conditions.

Morphological assessment

Preoperative computed tomography (CT) scan with multiplanar reconstruction was used to measure true lumen and total aortic diameters at the visceral level. Because no universal criterion currently exists, severe true lumen narrowing was pragmatically defined as a true lumen of ≤50% of the total diameter, based on our institutional experience. The mean true lumen-to-total ratio was 38 ± 7%, confirming marked narrowing in all patients.

To adapt the device to the narrowed true lumen, a VICS was applied to achieve permanent diameter control, yielding a true lumen-to-graft diameter ratio of 103 ± 5%. The constrained diameter was maintained at ≥20 mm to preserve sufficient flow, as no markedly narrowed true lumen (<20 mm) was observed (Fig 2).

Surgical technique

A 28-mm Cook Zenith Alpha Thoracic distal-type device, the smallest available diameter, was selected to ensure stable proximal and distal fixation and reliable rotational control. Because the proximal and distal ends of the graft are fixed to the inner sheath, intentional rotation by the operator allows for accurate alignment of the fenestrations without device torsion. Given the longitudinal heterogeneity of the true lumen diameter in chronic aortic dissection, a VICS was applied only to the visceral (fenestrated) segment; the remaining graft was left unconstrained. The constrained diameter was planned to be approximately 20 mm and generally ≥20 mm, based on preoperative CT-derived true lumen measurements and graft sizing to preserve flow capacity and maintain device deliverability. No cases with a true lumen diameter of <20 mm were included. However, this diameter should not be interpreted as a strict exclusion threshold. Patients with a true lumen diameter of ≤20 mm may also be considered appropriate candidates, depending on anatomical and clinical factors. The mean graft-to-true lumen diameter ratio after modification was 103 ± 5%.

Two 4-0 polypropylene sutures were placed on a strut-by-strut basis along the struts opposite the planned fenestrations to plicate them, thereby creating a visceral segment interrupted constraining configuration (ie, VICS) and achieving controlled graft conformity to the narrowed true lumen (Figs 2 and 3, A-C. Four fenestrations were then created on the graft and reinforced with radiopaque markers, which were circumferentially sutured to maintain fenestration shape and enhance intraoperative visualization. The PMEG was reloaded into the original delivery sheath and introduced via transfemoral access. During reloading, 5.0-mL and 2.5-mL syringes were used as spacers to orient the superior mesenteric artery fenestration at the 12-o'clock position. Target vessel cannulation was achieved using a coaxial system consisting of a steerable sheath and preshaped catheters. Bridging stents (preferentially self-expanding covered stents [Viabahn] and selectively balloon-expandable covered stents [VBX], W. L. Gore & Associates) were deployed from each fenestration to the respective target artery for branch perfusion and sealing. Completion angiography confirmed technical success with no type I or III endoleaks and preserved branch vessel patency (Fig 4, A-C).

(A) A conceptual model for calculating the circumference of a nominal 28-mm diameter stent graft (white arrow). The circumference is determined using the formula C = π × D, yielding 28 mm × π ≈ 87.92 mm. Each stent ring consists of 5 peaks and 5 valleys, forming 10 repeating peak-valley segments. Therefore, the arc length of a single segment is approximately 87.92 mm ÷ 10 ≈ 8.79 mm. When one dorsal segment is constrained by a visceral segment interrupted constraining suture (VICS) (ie, two adjacent peak-valley segments are effectively removed from the circumference), the effective circumference becomes 87.92 mm − (8.79 + 8.79) mm = 70.34 mm. (B) Two Z-stents were approximated using 4-0 monofilament polypropylene sutures. (C) Final calculation of the reduced effective diameter. Dividing the constrained circumference by π yields 70.34 mm ÷ π ≈ 22.3 mm. Thus, constraining a single segment results in a predictable diameter reduction of approximately 2.8 to 3.0 mm, without altering the stent's structural integrity. This provides a reproducible method for adapting the graft to narrowed true lumens in dissecting thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysms (dTAAAs) (white arrowhead). Postoperative ankle-brachial index indicated preserved limb perfusion, suggesting that the VICS did not cause a clinically relevant hemodynamic compromise.

Bridging stent strategy and device considerations

In cases incorporating a VICS, we prefer self-expanding covered bridging stents rather than balloon-expandable devices. The constraining modification results in a localized, noncircumferentially constrained fenestration geometry, and self-expanding endoprostheses more effectively accommodate this configuration, as well as the narrowed true lumen characteristic of dTAAAs. Their flexibility and continuous radial force maintain apposition and reduce kinking at curved visceral takeoffs, while permitting gentle postdilation if necessary. Given the frequent presence of narrow TLs and challenging vascular access in this population, low-profile devices adapted to dissecting morphology are preferred. To accommodate such constraints, we avoided inner branches and relied solely on fenestrations, which enabled the deployment of lower-profile PMEGs and smoother delivery through tortuous access routes.

Results

All 16 patients underwent successful PMEG implantation, achieving 100% intraoperative technical success with no operative deaths (Table I). A total of 60 target vessels were reconstructed through preoperatively planned fenestrations. The mean operative time was 334.5 ± 90.9 minutes. The mean bridging stent deployment times were 22.1 ± 18.6 minutes for the celiac artery and 18.9 ± 14.2 minutes for the superior mesenteric artery. Completion angiography and 30-day CT scans confirmed patency of all target vessels, including both renal arteries.

Early outcomes were favorable. Endoleaks occurred in three branches (5%): one type IC and two type IIIC, and all were treated successfully with secondary endovascular intervention. These endoleaks were associated with VBX stents; no endoleaks or device-related complications occurred with Viabahn. There were no 30-day deaths, target vessel occlusions, stent fractures, or graft failures. All patients were extubated in the operating room. At a median follow-up of 12 months, postoperative imaging confirmed complete aneurysm exclusion and sustained target vessel patency in all patients. There was no aortic-related mortality; two patients died of unrelated causes (Table II).

Discussion

Fenestrated endovascular aortic repair with PMEGs is a valuable endovascular option for managing complex aortic aneurysms, including selected TAAAs. In urgent settings, alternative strategies include chimney grafting and OTS thoracoabdominal devices. Compared with chimney grafts, PMEGs have been associated with lower complication rates and a substantial decrease in critical gutter-related endoleaks.6 Overall, both PMEGs and OTS devices can achieve acceptable outcomes in elective and emergent settings for complex aortic aneurysms.7

However, most current OTS devices are primarily designed for nondissecting TAAAs, and their application in dTAAAs remains limited owing to fundamental anatomical differences. In dTAAAs, severe true lumen narrowing, a rigid septum, and maloriented renovisceral branches can make accurate alignment and cannulation difficult when using fixed fenestrations or branches. Compared with OTS devices, PMEGs allow case-specific tailoring of coverage length, which may reduce the risk of spinal cord ischemia—an important consideration in urgent cases requiring complete aneurysm exclusion in a single-stage procedure.8

Even with PMEGs, achieving reliable fenestration alignment in a dissecting anatomy remains technically demanding. Severe true lumen narrowing may prevent full graft expansion at the fenestration zone and complicate target vessel cannulation. Moreover, oversizing of the endograft to achieve proximal sealing in the nondissected segment is often unavoidable, creating radial force mismatch and increasing the risk of infolding, malposition, or branch misalignment.

To address these limitations, we developed the VICS technique to selectively constrain only the fenestrated visceral segment while allowing reliable expansion and orientation at the fenestration zone. This targeted modification stabilizes graft geometry where branch access is required, promoting controlled luminal conformity in anatomically hostile dissecting morphologies. VICS enables patient-specific diameter adjustment based on preoperative CT measurements, thereby optimizing conformity at the fenestration segment while preserving proximal sealing.

In this series, we generally targeted a constrained diameter of approximately 20 mm (≥20 mm) to preserve flow capacity and maintain deliverability. This threshold is pragmatic rather than absolute. In carefully selected patients with very narrow true lumen anatomy, a <20-mm constrained segment may be technically feasible, provided there is meticulous alignment, a gentle staged postdilation strategy, and close postoperative surveillance. Although no true lumen diameter of <20 mm was encountered in this cohort, our sizing strategy yielded a mean graft-to-true lumen diameter ratio of 103%, providing close conformity while avoiding marked oversizing (>120%) and potentially limiting mechanical stress across Z-stent junctions.

By providing localized and durable diameter control at the fenestration zone, a VICS may facilitate more predictable device expansion and smoother renovisceral cannulation. Prior approaches to difficult branch access have included preloaded wire systems and transient diameter-reducing mechanisms (so-called transient constraining wires) to facilitate delivery and side branch catheterization in cases of fenestration-vessel misalignment.9 Although these methods can be useful, the VICS differs by providing a sustained, patient-specific constraining effect that remains after deployment, thereby supporting stable fenestration orientation throughout branch cannulation.

Given the noncircumferential waist geometry created by the VICS and the narrowed true lumen typical of dTAAAs, we preferentially recommend self-expanding covered bridging stents. Their flexibility and continuous radial force can improve conformability to the constrained segment and reduce kinking at angulated renovisceral origins.

Importantly, in our series, all junctional endoleaks occurred in branches treated with balloon-expandable covered stents (VBX), whereas no endoleaks or device-related complications occurred with the Viabahn, supporting our current preference. To reduce junctional endoleaks, balloon dilation to ensure adequate apposition between the bridging stent and the main endograft is generally required at the fenestration interface, except at the distal sealing end. Gentle, staged postdilation can be performed when necessary to optimize sealing and branch flow.

Balloon-expandable covered stents also offer advantages, including precise deployment and high acute radial force, and may be beneficial in selected anatomies. With further improvements in device flexibility, profile reduction, and conformability, balloon-expandable stents may become a reasonable option for use with a VICS in carefully selected cases.

To the best of our knowledge, this is a pioneering report describing the systematic use of a four-fenestration PMEG incorporating a VICS on a low-profile Zenith Alpha Thoracic distal-type platform for the treatment of dTAAAs. Although Seike et al10 focused on nondissecting aneurysms, their findings support the broader concept that low-profile devices may reduce embolic complications, suggesting a potential advantage of this platform in dissecting anatomies as well.

Preloaded wire strategies can facilitate target vessel cannulation, but typically require additional inner sheaths or wire channels, increasing the delivery profile (often around 20F). In contrast, the VICS supports precise fenestration alignment without a preloaded wire system, helping to maintain a lower-profile configuration and simplifying handling in tortuous anatomy or narrowed true lumens.

This study has limitations. It is a single-center, retrospective case series with a small sample size, no control group, and limited follow-up, and it was not designed to quantify differences in fluoroscopy time, radiation exposure, or learning curve effects. Nevertheless, our early results suggest that VICS-assisted PMEG repair is feasible and safe and may offer a reproducible strategy for treating complex dTAAA anatomy characterized by severe true lumen narrowing.

Conclusions

This study provides a pioneering description of the systematic use of VICS in PMEGs for dTAAA treatment. This simple and potentially reproducible modification provides controlled adaptation of endografts to narrowed true lumens, facilitating the feasibility and procedural consistency of PMEG repair in challenging dissecting anatomy. Although limited by the small sample size, single-center design, and short follow-up, our early results suggest that VICS-assisted PMEG repair is feasible and safe. Further patient accrual, comparative studies, and longer-term evaluation are essential to establish VICS-assisted PMEG as a reliable treatment strategy for complex dissecting aortic disease.
