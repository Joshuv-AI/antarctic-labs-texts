Frailty as a mediator of postoperative delirium in older adults undergoing orthopedic surgery: A causal mediation analysis
Authors: Inthira Roopsawang, Suparb Aree-Ue, Hilaire Thompson, Pawin Numthavaj
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative delirium has a high prevalence in hospitalized older adults. Frail older adults have an increased risk for developing it. Preoperative pain—a common symptom in older adults with orthopedic conditions—shows a connection with frailty through an inflammatory process. However, an association among preoperative pain, frailty, and postoperative delirium remains unexplored among older adults undergoing orthopedic surgery.ObjectivesTo examine the magnitude, direction, and mediation effect of frailty on the association between preoperative pain and postoperative delirium among older adults undergoing orthopedic surgery.DesignA secondary analysis of a prospective cohort study.Setting and participantsA cohort of 200 older adults (60 years and older) who underwent major orthopedic surgery at a university hospital in Thailand was recruited for the study.MethodsParticipants responded to the Demographic and Health Information Form, the Reported Edmonton Frailty Scale-Thai version, and the Thai version of the 4 A's test. Analyses were conducted using a causal mediation analysis with 95 % confidence intervals.ResultsWe found that 12.5 % of participants developed postoperative delirium. For the direct paths associated with postoperative delirium, statistically significant effects were observed for frailty, preoperative pain, and comorbidity. Considering frailty as a mediator of postoperative delirium, a significant positive indirect effect was identified from preoperative pain. Controlling for age and comorbidities, frailty mediated the association between preoperative pain and postoperative delirium in a statistically significant manner; the average direct effect was 0.014 (95 % confidence interval: 0.008–0.020), the average causal mediating effect was 0.002 (95 % confidence interval: 0.000–0.010), the total effects was 0.017 (95 % confidence interval: 0.010–0.020), and the probability mediation accounted for 14 % (95 % confidence interval: 0.031–0.300).ConclusionIntegrating the concept of age-related decline and frailty assessment may offer opportunities to provide disease-specific care and strengthen precision perioperative care, which ultimately enhance quality of life in older adults undergoing orthopedic surgery.Keywords: Frailty, Mediation analysis, Older adults, Orthopedic surgery, Preoperative pain, Postoperative delirium

What is already known about the topic

•Postoperative delirium is common, but its sequelae have negative impacts on health outcomes, resulting in long-term care needs and decreased quality of life in older adults undergoing surgery.

•Despite numerous interventions to address postoperative delirium, the prevalence of postoperative delirium remains high in patients undergoing major orthopedic surgery.

•The literature exploring risk factors that influence postoperative delirium has grown, but an understanding of specific mechanisms pertaining to the role of age-related decline is lacking.

What this paper adds

•The complex nexus links among frailty, preoperative pain, and postoperative delirium may be a key to strengthening preoperative care, surgical risk stratification, and postoperative care quality.

•Proactive multifactorial interventions targeting reversible risk factors, particularly frailty and preoperative pain, are needed to reduce the incidence of postoperative delirium and its consequences in older adults undergoing orthopedic surgery.

•Disease-specific advanced surgical care planning that incorporates biomarkers of aging, such as frailty, is important to improving personalized ortho-geriatric care.

Introduction

Postoperative delirium, which is an acute cognitive impairment manifesting in patients requiring surgical procedures, is a serious problem leading to poor health outcomes in older adults undergoing surgery (Bramley et al., 2021; Ho et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021). The incidence and prevalence of postoperative delirium vary depending on the surgical procedure. Notably, the highest prevalence rates of postoperative delirium (ranging from 4.2 % to 41.5 %) are primarily for major orthopedic (hip, knee, and spinal) surgery (Iamaroon et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2022). Researchers have revealed an upsurge, with more than 45 % of older adults who undergo hip and knee surgery experiencing postoperative delirium (Gao et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2022, 2021; Zhou et al., 2021). According to global statistics, older adults who experience postoperative delirium are more likely to require long-term care and have delayed functional recovery, increased health care costs, and drastically decreased quality of life (Gao et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2021). Prior researchers have demonstrated that postoperative delirium profoundly impacts a person's health and can result in permanent cognitive impairment, postoperative cognitive dysfunction, late-life dementia, and mortality (Ho et al., 2021; Oh and Park, 2019; Rengel et al., 2018; Song et al., 2019). Despite use of pharmacological or non-pharmacological interventions (Mossie et al., 2022), the incidence and prevalence of postoperative delirium remain high in orthopedic surgery (Gao et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2021).

Understanding the risk factors that influence postoperative delirium will help in addressing modifiable factors to prevent or detect postoperative delirium and minimize its consequences (Gao et al., 2020; Iamaroon et al., 2020; Jin et al., 2020; Mossie et al., 2022). In orthopedic surgery, increased age, preoperative mild to severe pain, multiple comorbidities, polypharmacy, and postoperative physical restriction are significant risk factors that influence cognitive changes. Such changes may accelerate postoperative delirium processes or increase their severity (Bramley et al., 2021; Iamaroon et al., 2020; Rong et al., 2021; Song et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2022). Although the etiology of postoperative delirium remains poorly understood, a higher risk of developing postoperative delirium is almost certainly owing to age-related decline, known as geriatric syndrome (Bramley et al., 2021; Ding et al., 2021; Fu et al., 2022; Menzenbach et al., 2022).

With the aging of the global population, geriatric syndrome has garnered interest in advancing care quality and preventing adverse events. The notion of frailty—an age-related decline across multiple bodily systems, leading to decreased homeostasis and increasing vulnerability—has emerged as an important geriatric syndrome (Khan et al., 2019). In the surgical population, preoperative frailty significantly predicts adverse events, poor health outcomes, dependency, and mortality (Fu et al., 2022; Roopsawang et al., 2020a). Furthermore, researchers have shown that preoperative frailty is associated with postoperative delirium (Esmaeeli et al., 2022; Fu et al., 2022; Roopsawang et al., 2020a). More recently, researchers have proposed that frailty may be linked to predisposing factors of postoperative delirium owing to age-related inflammation (Fu et al., 2022).

Because multiple interactions account for the causes of postoperative delirium, frailty may alter postoperative delirium in many directions. Age-related inflammation can result in frailty; therefore, frailty and postoperative delirium may share a common risk factor and may have other shared predisposing factors that involve inflammation and aging (Fu et al., 2022; Oh and Park, 2019). In older adults undergoing surgery, predisposing factors, such as surgical stress, multimorbidity, and preoperative pain, influence inflammation and may increase the risk of developing both frailty and postoperative delirium (Bramley et al., 2021; Jin et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2022). Preoperative pain is a common symptom in patients with orthopedic conditions that leads patients to seek care from healthcare services. The relationship between pain and inflammation is closely linked biochemically. The body's response to painful conditions can be detected from increasing systematic inflammation, which commonly composes a mechanistic basis for postoperative delirium and delayed neurocognitive recovery (Bramley et al., 2021; Ding et al., 2021). Pain significantly predicts frailty in hospitalized older adults and those with orthopedic surgeries (Ardoino et al., 2020; Esmaeeli et al., 2022; Fu et al., 2022). Evidence exists that older adults experience pain-induced systemic inflammation (Fang et al., 2023). Persistent preoperative pain may initiate systemic inflammatory responses, potentially leading to chronic inflammation and development of inflammation-related diseases such as depression, dementia, and diabetes (Baechle et al., 2023; Fang et al., 2023). Furthermore, in recent studies, researchers have highlighted that preexisting chronic inflammation, as indicated by elevated inflammatory markers, may not only exacerbate current comorbidities but also potentially increase onset of new conditions (Dagnino and Campos, 2022; Marshall et al., 2024).

Accordingly, the relationship among preoperative pain, frailty, and postoperative delirium appears to be incredibly complex; therefore, we selected preoperative pain to explore the intricate relationship. Frailty is a condition of age-related deterioration, but not all older adults are frail. Furthermore, the processes of frailty are unique to each individual (Khan et al., 2019). Frailty is considered a modifiable risk factor; hence, early detection of frailty and those at risk for frailty is essential. In addition, the presence of frailty may be inherent in the interactions of postoperative delirium among surgical older adults. Furthermore, the interplay of postoperative delirium with preoperative pain may be more intricate than previously estimated and requires in-depth analyses, particularly in older adults undergoing major surgical procedures.

To date, very little attention has been paid to the mediating effect of age-related decline on postoperative delirium, which is most prevalent in orthopedic surgery. Hence, exploring the links between preoperative pain and postoperative delirium mediated by frailty may be essential to improving preoperative care, surgical risk stratification, and postoperative care quality. Ultimately, the findings of this study may be beneficial for initiating specific interventions to improve perioperative care, strengthening postoperative delirium risk stratification, and promoting patient quality of life by integrating the notion of frailty in older adults undergoing orthopedic surgery. Therefore, the aim of this study was to examine the magnitude, direction, and mediation effect of frailty on the association between preoperative pain and postoperative delirium among older adults undergoing orthopedic surgery

Methods

Participants

The current study was a secondary analysis; we used information from a prior prospective cohort study, which was conducted in a university tertiary care hospital in Thailand. Participant information was derived from the cohort of hospitalized older adults aged 60 years and over who were scheduled for major orthopedic surgery in a previous study (Roopsawang et al., 2020a). The inclusion criteria were older adults, fluent in Thai, and scheduled for major orthopedic surgery (hip, knee, or spinal surgery) from July to November 2018. Exclusion criteria were patients undergoing emergency or minimally invasive surgery; those with cognitive impairment (Mini-Cog < 3 points); current psychiatric disorders; history of stroke, brain injury, or Parkinson's disease; or the presence of severe disability or being bedridden. Participants were individually evaluated for preoperative health, surgical treatment, pain, and frailty. From the time of admission, all participants were monitored daily for adverse events, such as postoperative delirium and others, until hospital discharge.

A total sample size of all 200 participants was targeted for final analysis, which was calculated based on the suggestion of 10–20 participants per parameter (Hair et al., 2021). This study included 17 parameters; therefore, the sample size should be 170–340 individuals. Thus, a total of 200 individuals was an adequate sample size.

Measures

The measures used in this study include tools for screening and data collection.

The Mini-Cog Thai version (Trongsakul et al., 2015) was translated from the original Mini-Cog and was used to screen cognitive impairment or dementia. The Mini-Cog is scored in two sections: a) three-item recall, and b) clock drawing. The total score is 5; a total score less than 3 indicates an increased risk of having cognitive impairment or dementia, whereas higher scores indicate a lower risk. The Mini-Cog Thai version shows good interrater reliability (Kappa [κ] = 0.80, 95 % confidence interval [CI] 0.50–1.00, p < 0.001) and positive concurrent validity with the Mini-Mental Status Exam Thai 2002 (r = 0.47, 95 % CI 0.37–0.55, p = 0.007) (Trongsakul et al., 2015).

The Demographic and Health Questionnaire was developed by the principal investigator based on a literature review related to major orthopedic conditions. The questionnaire obtained participants’ demographic and health information, such as age, sex, pre-operative pain intensity, the American Society of Anesthesiologists classification system, comorbidities (e.g., diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, or cardiovascular disease), and operative sites. The number of comorbidities is counted without weighted points. All information was retrieved from medical records.

The Reported Edmonton Frailty Scale-Thai version (Roopsawang et al., 2020b) was translated from the original scale, based on the accumulation deficit model (Hilmer et al., 2009). The Reported Edmonton Frailty Scale-Thai version was used to evaluate frailty in nine domains: general health status, cognitive function, functional independence, continence, medication use, nutrition, mood, social support, and self-performance. The total possible score on the scale is 18. The total score is classified for frailty, which includes severe (score 12–18), moderate (score 10–11), and mild frailty (score 8–9); apparent frailty (score 6–7); or non-frail (score 0–5). In the hospital setting, the Reported Edmonton Frailty Scale-Thai version shows good interrater reliability (linear weights κ = 0.87, p < 0.001). The internal consistency of this scale also indicates acceptable reliability (Cronbach's alpha = 0.73) (Roopsawang et al., 2020b).

The Thai version of the 4 A's test (Kuladee and Prachason, 2016) is a short screening instrument for diagnosing delirium in hospitalized older adults. The 4 A's test evaluates delirium and cognitive impairment in four domains: alertness, Abbreviated Mental Test-4, attention, and acute change or fluctuating course. Twelve is the total possible score. Scores range from 0 to 12; a score of 4 or more indicates possible delirium cognitive impairment, a score of 1—3 suggests cognitive impairment, and a score of 0 suggests unlikely delirium or cognitive impairment (but delirium may develop if any acute change or fluctuating course information is incomplete). The Thai 4 A's test shows satisfactory results in screening delirium, with 83.3 % diagnostic sensitivity (95 % CI: 62.6 %–95.3 %), 86.3 % specificity (95 % CI: 76.3 %–93.2 %), and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [ROC] 0.92. This test is a valid and practical instrument, even in older adults who are unable to undergo cognitive testing. Permission was granted for the use and translation for all measures.

Ethical considerations

The institutional review board of the hospital's ethics committee approved this study (no. COA.MURA 2022/461). A waiver of informed consent was granted for this secondary analysis. However, all information was kept confidential, following the ethical principles of research involving human subjects.

Statistical analysis

Demographic data were analyzed using descriptive statistics; frequency, percentage, and mean with standard deviation are reported. A causal analysis was conducted to validate the putative causal pathway (Fig. 1). On the basis of the causal model, mediation analysis was performed, in which age and comorbidities were external factors; preoperative pain was a predictor, frailty was a mediator, and postoperative delirium was an outcome.

Abbreviations: POD = postoperative delirium; RMSEA = root mean square error of approximation; WRMR = weighted root mean square residual; CFI = comparative fit index; TLI = Tucker-Lewis index;

The causal relationship analysis was constructed using Mplus. Mediation effects were estimated using the product coefficients of each pathway. Mediation analysis was also evaluated using RStudio Version 4.3.1. In all analyses, a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Goodness-of-fit of the hypothesized model was tested; p-values of χ2 > 0.05, comparative fit index [CFI] of ≥ 0.95, Tucker–Lewis index [TLI] of ≥ 0.95, root mean squared error of approximation [RMSEA] of < 0.08, and standardized root mean squared residual [SRMR] of < 0.05, demonstrating an acceptable fit. Sensitivity analysis for unobserved confounding was examined by testing the average causal mediation effect (ACME), which was required to confirm the assumption of sequential ignorability and examine the robustness of the empirical findings. The findings are reported in accordance with the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Results

Participants

Most participants were non-frail, women, and mean age of 71.00 ± 7.50 years, with multiple comorbidities and mild preoperative pain who underwent major orthopedic surgery (hip, knee, and spine). More than half of the participants (59.5 %, n = 119) were American Society of Anesthesiologists classification of class III (patients with severe systemic diseases; e.g., obesity, uncontrolled diabetes mellitus or hypertension, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease). Of the 200 participants, approximately 12 % developed postoperative delirium during hospital admission (Table 1). A little more than half of those who developed postoperative delirium experienced it during the first 24 hours, and nearly half experienced it by the discharge day. Discussion

From the findings, we revealed a complex model-based causal relationship of postoperative delirium with an excellent fit. Multiple factors—age, frailty, comorbidity, and preoperative pain—had direct effects on postoperative delirium. To our knowledge, we are the first to provide preliminary evidence that frailty mediated the association between preoperative pain and postoperative delirium. Controlling for age and comorbidity, the mediating effect of frailty on postoperative delirium remained statistically significant.

The causal relationships underlying the etiology of postoperative delirium in older adults remain unclear; however, from our findings, we support prior research regarding the complexity of postoperative delirium, tying together multiple predisposing factors (Bramley et al., 2021; Esmaeeli et al., 2022; Fu et al., 2022; Oh and Park, 2019). The most important clinically relevant findings were that the preoperative factors of frailty, multiple comorbidities, and preoperative pain have vital roles in the increased risk of developing postoperative delirium. These results are in line with previous studies (Bramley et al., 2021; Ding et al., 2021; Esmaeeli et al., 2022; Fu et al., 2022). A possible explanation might be that inflammation and chronic stress prior to surgery contribute simultaneously to postoperative delirium. Researchers have emphasized that inflammation and oxidative stress—age-related inflammation, neuroinflammation, neuro-aging, and homeostasis dysregulation—may perpetuate the complex mechanisms of postoperative delirium (Bramley et al., 2021; Ding et al., 2021; Esmaeeli et al., 2022; Fu et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2022). Moreover, a mounting number of researchers have demonstrated that acute or chronic pain, together with mild to moderate pain severity, are significant factors predicting frailty, emotional stress, increased risk of developing comorbidities, and postoperative delirium in older adults undergoing non-cardiac and orthopedic surgeries (Ardoino et al., 2020; Auckley et al., 2021; Esmaeeli et al., 2022; Esses et al., 2020; Fu et al., 2022; Mahanna-Gabrielli et al., 2020).

In addition, frailty is associated with age-related inflammation, which is known to influence postoperative delirium (Ardoino et al., 2020; Auckley et al., 2021; Fu et al., 2022; Guerriero and Reid, 2020). Preoperative frailty is also considered a risk factor for postoperative delirium and persistent pain post-discharge in older adults undergoing elective surgery procedures (Ardoino et al., 2020; Auckley et al., 2021; Esses et al., 2020, 2019). Notably, aging enhances susceptibility to stress responses. In doing so, impaired physiologic responding to surgical stressors—trauma, injury, and major surgical procedures—at pre- and post-surgery are significant risk factors that trigger processes of frailty, which may lead to the development of severe postoperative delirium in older adults (Esmaeeli et al., 2022; Fu et al., 2022; Mahanna-Gabrielli et al., 2020). Hence, pain or chronic pain, stress, multiple comorbidities, and frailty are likely causing inflammation and oxidative stress leading to postoperative delirium in this population. Undeniably, older adults who have musculoskeletal conditions with multiple comorbidities may be at greater risk of increased inflammation and chronic stress than other groups, which together can result in frailty. Because frailty and preoperative pain are modifiable risk factors, implementing interventions to address these factors during perioperative care may be critical in preventing postoperative delirium and its consequences.

Contrary to expectations, a causative link between age and postoperative delirium was not found in this study. Several possible explanations for this finding include differences in chronological age amongst studies and the complex etiology of postoperative delirium. Regarding chronological age, a number of researchers have revealed that older age significantly predicts postoperative delirium (Bramley et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2022). However, no reliable evidence on the impact of age on postoperative delirium exists. Some researchers have demonstrated that older age (> 65 years) is a risk factor predicting postoperative delirium, (Bramley et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2022), whereas others have specified that middle–older age (> 75 years) or oldest-old age (> 85 years) are more significant predictors of postoperative delirium (Iamaroon et al., 2020; Song et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2021). Our findings must be interpreted with caution because the sample in the present study mostly comprised the older adults, with an average of 72 years; hence, we cannot rule out a causal link between age group and postoperative delirium. According to the complex pathophysiology of postoperative delirium, multiple factors or inflammatory biomarkers would more effectively explain the underlying nexus of postoperative delirium mechanisms. Watt et al. (2018) conducted a meta-analysis and underlined that age-related decline conditions are more significant preoperative factors that predict postoperative complications than chronological aging. From our preliminary findings, we further support the idea that postoperative delirium is complicated; chronological age alone may not fully explain the mechanisms underlying postoperative delirium in older adults. Further research with greater emphasis on the casual link between biomarkers of cellular senescence and postoperative delirium is warranted for a more precise understanding of the mechanisms underlying aging and postoperative delirium.

From our findings, we confirm that frailty partially mediates the relationship between preoperative pain and postoperative delirium in this population, after controlling for age and multimorbidity. This mediating effect of frailty on postoperative delirium may be partly explained by inflammatory pathways that intensify the risk of developing postoperative delirium. In accordance with recent evidence, inflammatory states (e.g., chronic stress, preoperative or postoperative pain, neuronal damage, and frailty) are key in the pathophysiology of postoperative delirium (Rong et al., 2021). The alteration of inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein and interleukin-6 during the preoperative period could also be influential in postoperative delirium (Auckley et al., 2021; Bramley et al., 2021; Fu et al., 2022; Menzenbach et al., 2022; Oh and Park, 2019). Pain increases inflammation, putting older adults with orthopedic conditions at increased risk of frailty and postoperative delirium (Ardoino et al., 2020; Esses et al., 2020, 2019; Guerriero and Reid, 2020). Preoperative chronic-low-grade inflammation owing to aging-related decline (known as inflammageing) may intensify surgery-induced inflammation in older adults, leading to postoperative delirium (Bramley et al., 2021; Noah et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2022). Correspondingly, the biological mechanism of frailty involves inflammation as evidenced by elevated inflammatory markers (Khan et al., 2019; Noah et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2022). Undeniably, older adults undergoing orthopedic surgery are at greater risk of developing postoperative delirium owing to preoperative frailty. Despite these promising results on applying a multiple-dimensional frailty assessment preoperatively, doubts remain. A note of caution is due here since our initial findings are insufficient to demonstrate the specific impact of different frailty domains on postoperative delirium. Recent evidence has indicated that the cognitive domain of frailty may contribute to varying degrees of risk for postoperative delirium (Esmaeeli et al., 2022; Gracie, Caufield-Noll, Wang, and Sieber, 2021). A further study with more focus on the effect of different frailty domains on postoperative delirium is thus recommended to gain better understanding and mitigate the risk of postoperative delirium in older adults undergoing orthopedic surgery.

Limitations

Although we have elucidated frailty as a mediator of the association between preoperative pain and postoperative delirium, some potential limitations must be considered. First, preoperative laboratory tests, biomarkers, and other factors could not be specified in the analysis; we could not acquire information for all predisposing elements related to postoperative delirium owing to reliance on secondary data. Second, in this study, we could not address possible confounding by cognitive impairment in postoperative delirium because participants with suspected cognitive impairment were not included. Last, the data were obtained from only a single tertiary care hospital; our findings may not be generalized to older participants admitted in different settings or those undergoing different types of orthopedic surgical procedures. Further, it is noteworthy to acknowledge that the cross-sectional nature of data collection may impose certain constraints. For a more comprehensive investigation, a larger sample size with a longitudinal design involving a diverse population is necessary to elucidate of the inherent mediation effects of frailty.

Conclusions and implications

Overall, while our findings are preliminary, we have shed light on the inherent relationships among preoperative factors in postoperative delirium. Frailty was found to mediate the association between preoperative pain and postoperative delirium in a statistically significant manner when other external factors were controlled. Understanding the nexus relationships among frailty, preoperative pain, and postoperative delirium may be crucial for health professionals to develop proactive, multifactorial interventions targeting preoperative frailty assessment and reversible risk factors so as to prevent postoperative delirium.

We have highlighted the importance of a holistic approach to caring for older people that goes beyond chronological age. Integrating age-related decline and perspectives, such as frailty, is crucial for promoting disease-specific advance care, strengthening needs-based or precision perioperative nursing care, and enhancing quality of life in this patient population. Moreover, additional research is required regarding preoperative inflammatory markers, surgical type, and intraoperative and postoperative conditions for early detection of postoperative delirium.
