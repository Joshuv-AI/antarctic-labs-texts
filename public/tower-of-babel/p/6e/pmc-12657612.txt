One year follow up in siblings with TREX1-associated retinal vasculopathy with cerebral leukodystrophy
Authors: Georgios Mylonas, Ioannna Dimakopoulou, Johannes Iby, Gabor Deak, Marlene Hollaus, Wolf Buehl, Reinhard Lehner, Markus Ritter, Guenther Weigert, Ursula Schmidt-Erfurth, Stefan Sacu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo report macular vascular changes in two cases of retinal vasculopathy with cerebral leukodystrophy (RVCL).ObservationsThis report presents long-term follow-up findings in two sisters with RVCL, using conventional optical coherence tomography (OCT), widefield optical coherence tomography angiography (OCT-A), and ultra-widefield fluorescein angiography (UWF-FA). Ultra-widefield OCT angiography (UWF-OCT-A) was also employed during the most recent follow-up.Conclusions and importanceSeveral valuable monitoring tools, including UWF-FA and OCT-A, are essential for this rare disease. Ophthalmologists must be aware, as many RVCL patients first show isolated ocular findings detectable with OCT, and even earlier with UWF-OCT-A.

Introduction

Retinal vasculopathy with cerebral leukodystrophy (RVCL) is a rare and potentially fatal autosomal dominant disease that typically emerges in early adulthood. It is marked by progressive microvascular dysfunction predominantly affecting the retina and central nervous system.1 RVCL was initially described in 1988 within a family exhibiting an unusual history of brain tumors.2 Clinically, the disease manifests around the age of 35–40, featuring vascular retinopathy, Raynaud's phenomenon, migraines, and dysfunction in various internal organs, including the kidneys, liver, and gastrointestinal system. Additional complications encompass bleeding, anaemia, and subclinical hypothyroidism.3,4

The underlying genetic cause of RVCL was identified as heterozygous C-terminal frameshift variant in the Three Primer Repair Exonuclease 1 (TREX1) gene. This gene encodes a DNA-specific 3′–5′ exonuclease present widely in mammalian cells.1,5 Individuals with RVCL typically present with initial symptoms of visual disturbance, followed by the development of progressive focal and global brain disease. The disease may also manifest in systemic complications involving the kidneys and liver.

Due to the progressive nature of brain failure associated with RVCL, affected individuals often experience a decline in their general condition. Unfortunately, patients with RVCL frequently succumb to complications such as pneumonia or sepsis, increased mortality, typically occurring in the sixth decade of life.1,6

Key features of RVCL, including retinal manifestations and contrast-enhancing brain lesions, are commonly observed through magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).1,6 Retinal presentations encompass telangiectasia, microaneurysms, cotton wool spots, capillary nonperfusion, and retinal neovascularization. This report presents one year follow-up findings using optical coherence tomography (OCT) optical coherence tomography angiography (OCT-A) and ultra–widefield fluorescein angiography (UWF FA) in two sisters with RVCL. Ultra-Widefield OCT Angiography (UWF OCT-A) was also used in the last follow up visit.

Case 1

A 66-year-old female patient was referred to the Department of Ophthalmology from the Department of Neurology for the assessment of her newly diagnosed RVCL (Retinal Vasculopathy with Cerebral Leukoencephalopathy). The patient was originally referred to the Department of Neurology for further evaluation due to a family history of strokes. No reduction in visual acuity has been documented to this time, though the patient has described that subjectively visual acuity was inferior in the right eye compared to the left eye. Because RVCL is primarily caused by pathogenic variants in the TREX1 gene (see OMIM # 192315, GeneReviews: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK546576/), only the TREX1 gene was sequenced using Sanger sequencing in the patients' DNA. The analysis revealed the variant c.703dupG (p.Val235GlyfsTer6) in a heterozygous state in the gene TREX1. This variant has previously been reported in the literature as causative for autosomal dominant inherited RVCL.1,3,5,7, 8, 9, 10 Furthermore, this variant has already been classified as pathogenic in the ClinVar database (ID 449514).

The clinical phenotype of our patient closely matches previously published descriptions of individuals with RVCL carrying pathogenic variants in the TREX1 gene. This variant has been associated with the classical RVCL phenotype: progressive retinal vasculopathy with telangiectasias and ischemia, contrast-enhancing white matter lesions often misinterpreted as neoplasms, and systemic manifestations such as liver or kidney dysfunction and Raynaud's phenomenon.1,5,7,11

Her medical records indicate a history of two strokes, and unfortunately, both her mother and grandmother experienced fatal complications related to stroke. Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 20/25 in both eyes.

Anterior segment examination was unremarkable. Fundus examination showed narrow retinal arterioles and ghost vessels in the periphery on both eyes. Widefield fluorescein angiography (Optos) revealed small focal capillary nonperfusion in the posterior pole and larger confluent nonperfusion outside the vascular arcades, with small amount of dye leakage to the supero-temporal quadrant in the right eye, recognised as pathological retinal neovascularization due to the excessive retinal ischemia (Fig. 1a and b). OCT-A verified the capillary nonperfusion in the macular region (Fig. 2a und 2b). OCT revealed thinning of the macula region corresponding to the macular ischemic zone. Upon conducting a one-year follow-up examination of the patient, there was a significant decline in visual acuity in the right eye to 20/60, while VA remained stable at 20/25 in the left eye. Anterior segment examination yielded unremarkable findings, with no indications of iris rubeosis. Further assessment with UWF FA and OCT-A (Zeiss, Plex Elite) revealed a slight progression of retinal capillary non-perfusion areas in both eyes, with no signs of neovascularization (Fig. 1, Fig. 2c and d respectively). Notably the ischemic zone had expanded to include the foveal region in both eyes. Despite these developments, the patient's neurological condition remained relatively stable. Performance of the follow up UWF OCT-A images using DREAM OCT (Intalight) in this case were difficult in high-quality due to poor eye fixation.

Case 2

The sister of the patient (case 1), a 57-year-old female, was also screened, although no ocular symptoms were present. Again, RCVL was confirmed through Sanger Sequencing, which detected the same heterozygous variant in the gene TREX1. BCVA was 20/20 in both eyes. Fundus examination showed narrow retinal arterioles and ghost vessels in the periphery on both eyes. On the right eye some intraretinal hemorrhages could be detected in the superotemporal quadrant. Fluorescein angiography revealed capillary nonperfusion in the posterior pole of the right eye and in the peripheral retina outside of the vascular arcades in both eyes, with small amount of dye leakage to the infero-nasal quadrant in the left eye, recognised as pathological retinal macroaneurysms (Fig. 3). Using OCT-A the capillary nonperfusion in the macular region was verified (Fig. 4). OCT revealed thinning of the macula region corresponding to the macular ischemic zone. At the one-year follow-up examination, visual acuity remained unchanged in both eyes, and the anterior segment examination revealed no notable findings. There was a slight progression of retinal capillary non-perfusion areas in both eyes, as observed in both UWF-FA and OCT-A (Fig. 3, Fig. 4c, d, respectively), with no signs of neovascularization.

Despite this, the retinal macroaneurysms remained active, displaying leakage in the late FA phase (Fig. 3d). As a result, an argon laser re-treatment for the ischemic region surrounding the macroaneurysms region was scheduled. UWF OCT-A revealed ischemic areas in superficial and deep capillary plexuses in the macular region and in the periphery as well. Discussion

In this case report we describe macular vascular changes in two patients with RVCL using OCT, UWF-FA and for the first time to our knowledge using UWF OCT-A with a field of 26 × 21mm (Intalight DREAM OCT). The first description of the characteristic ophthalmic findings was by Grand et al. using traditional FA. Typical retinal findings include perifoveal capillary obliteration, peripheral focal capillary occlusion, and microvascular abnormalities, particularly involving the posterior pole.12

Previously Che-Yuan Kuo et al. presented a case of retinal vasculopathy with cerebral leukodystrophy and highlighted the usefulness of OCT-A in the assessment of long-term outcomes.13 They found a remarkable reduction of vessel density of the superficial capillary plexus and deep capillary plexus during the follow up time and pointed out the role of OCT-A in this rare disease.

Kernt and co-authors were the first to describe the effect of intravitreal bevacizumab injections on retinal neovascularization and macular leakage in a patient with CRV (cerebroretinal vasculopathy). They administered a single intravitreal injection of 1.25 mg bevacizumab and followed the patient for 8 weeks. Examination at 4 weeks post-injection revealed improved visual acuity, reduced retinal neovascularization, and decreased vascular leakage and exudation. Peripheral retinal perfusion also appeared improved. Eight weeks after treatment, visual acuity remained stable with no recurrence observed.14

However, Nagiel et al. described the potential efficacy of Anti-VEGF in preventing capillary occlusions, in a 31-year-old female patient with mild RVCL.15 The patient received six intravitreal bevacizumab injections over six months, resulting in improved capillary leakage but no changes in peripheral nonperfusion.

In our cases, UWF-OCT-A effectively showed ischemic areas in the superficial and deep capillary plexuses in the macular region and the mid-periphery as well. However, the field was not wide enough to demonstrate the retinal macroaneurysm in the periphery. Using UWF-FA it is possible to see changes in the periphery, too. A further caveat is that, poor fixation can make the capture of, high-quality images with large scan area very difficult.

In conclusion, there are several valuable monitoring tools available, especially UWF-FA and OCT-A for this rare disease. It is crucial for ophthalmologists to be aware of this condition, given that many patients with RVCL initially present with isolated ocular findings, which could be detected with OCT and even much earlier with UWF-OCT-A.
