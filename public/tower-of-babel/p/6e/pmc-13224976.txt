Flow-mediated pseudolesion mimicking an intracranial aneurysm secondary to cervical internal carotid artery dissection: illustrative case
Authors: Feng Zeng, Feng Wen, Kaikai Fu, Wenjie Lin, Qiyong Mei
Published: 2026

Intracranial aneurysms, defined as abnormal focal dilations of cerebral arteries, are a significant vascular pathology affecting 2%–5% of the global adult population.1 The prevalence of unruptured intracranial aneurysms (UIAs) in individuals aged 50–60 years is estimated at 3.2% (95% CI 2.9%–3.5%) based on pooled data from the Unruptured Cerebral Aneurysm Study (UCAS) Japan and International Study of Unruptured Intracranial Aneurysms (ISUIA).1,2 This prevalence increases to 7%–10% in high-risk cohorts, including patients with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) or familial intracranial aneurysm syndromes, necessitating age- and risk factor–stratified screening protocols (e.g., baseline MRA at age 30 for ADPKD).3,4

Digital subtraction angiography (DSA), particularly 3D rotational angiography, is recognized as the reference standard for intracranial aneurysm evaluation due to its submillimeter spatial resolution (0.2–0.3 mm) and dynamic hemodynamic profiling.5 This modality enables precise delineation of aneurysm morphology and spatial relationships with parent vessels, with detection sensitivity exceeding 95% for lesions < 3 mm. Although 65%–80% of UIAs remain asymptomatic during natural history follow-up, acute rupture results in 30-day case fatality rates of 35%–50% and permanent disability (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] scores ≥ 3) in 30%–40% of survivors per American Heart Association/American Stroke Association 2023 guidelines.6 Based on the UCAS Japan trial criteria, definitive treatment—microsurgical clipping or endovascular coiling/flow diversion—is strongly recommended for UIAs > 7 mm in maximum diameter, particularly those located in the posterior circulation or exhibiting irregular wall contours.7

Internal carotid artery (ICA) dissection (ICAD) is characterized by an intimal tear with intramural hematoma formation, which may progress to luminal stenosis, occlusion, or pseudoaneurysm development, ultimately leading to cerebral hypoperfusion and collateral circulation recruitment. Epidemiological studies have reported an ICAD incidence of 2.5–3.0 per 100,000 person-years, accounting for 2.5% of all ischemic strokes and 15%–25% of strokes in adults < 45 years.8 Although ICAD does not directly induce de novo aneurysm formation, its hemodynamic sequelae, including delayed contrast filling and flow redistribution, might confound angiographic interpretation of distal cerebrovascular lesions.9 To our knowledge, this report represents the first documented case of a hemodynamics-driven infundibular dilation of the posterior communicating artery (PComA) mimicking a PComA aneurysm secondary to proximal ICAD-induced flow perturbation, underscoring the necessity of multimodal imaging integration in neurovascular diagnostics. Hemodynamic assessment allows precise quantification of wall shear stress (WSS) gradients and distinguishes true aneurysms from hemodynamically mediated pseudolesions.10

Intracranial aneurysms are a significant vascular pathology, with DSA serving as the reference standard for their evaluation due to its superior spatial resolution and dynamic hemodynamic profiling. However, the diagnostic accuracy of DSA can be challenged by hemodynamic artifacts that mimic true pathology, particularly at complex vascular junctions like the circle of Willis. Among these pseudolesions, arterial infundibula, characterized as conical, funnel-shaped dilatations at the origin of a branching vessel, are a well-known diagnostic pitfall. While typically benign and distinct from saccular aneurysms by criteria such as a broad base, symmetrical shape, and the branch artery arising directly from its apex (Rhoton’s criteria), their angiographic appearance can be significantly altered by proximal hemodynamic disturbances.

ICAD is a leading cause of ischemic stroke in young adults. The resultant luminal stenosis or occlusion not only compromises cerebral perfusion but also elicits profound hemodynamic changes, including delayed contrast filling, flow redistribution, and the recruitment of collateral circulation. These alterations can generate abnormal WSS gradients in distal vessels, potentially distorting the morphology of preexisting infundibula and making them indistinguishable from true aneurysms on angiography. This report describes the first documented instance of a flow-mediated pseudolesion, where a PComA infundibulum masqueraded as a saccular aneurysm secondary to hemodynamic perturbation from a proximal ICAD. It underscores the critical importance of comprehensive hemodynamic assessment in neurovascular diagnostics to avoid unnecessary interventions.

Illustrative Case

A 51-year-old woman with chronic headaches underwent DSA at an external hospital, which revealed a 4 × 5–mm saccular aneurysm (neck width: 3 mm) located between the ophthalmic artery origin and the anterior choroidal artery. Absent PComA filling was noted on angiography (Fig. 1A and B). Preferring definitive surgical clipping over conservative management, the patient was referred to our tertiary neurovascular center for further treatment.

B:A magnified view of the initial DS angiogram provides a more detailed image of the suspected aneurysm and demonstrates the adjacent anterior choroidal artery (white arrow). C:Lateral view from a vertebral artery angiogram performed externally, suggesting the presence of anterograde collateral blood flow from the posterior cerebral artery via the PComA supplying the anterior circulation (yellow arrow), indicating hemodynamic compromise in the anterior circulation. The patient requested surgical scheduling as soon as possible. Our preoperative safety evaluation, based on the patient’s clinical profile, revealed retrograde collateral perfusion from the left vertebral artery to the right anterior cerebral artery (ACA) via the PComA (Figs. 1C and D 2A and B). The observed collateral pattern reinforced the imperative for intraoperative PComA preservation (confirmed by indocyanine green videoangiography) to prevent postoperative border zone infarction in the right anterior circulation.

To clarify these concerns, we performed DSA prior to craniotomy. As hypothesized, selective right common carotid artery angiography demonstrated a dissection with 50%–60% luminal stenosis at the origin of the ICA (Fig. 2C). Selective right ICA angiography revealed the absence of anterograde filling in the PComA at the communicating segment (Fig. 2C and D). However, the left vertebral artery injection demonstrated retrograde opacification of the right PComA, indicating collateral perfusion from the vertebrobasilar system to the right ACA (Fig. 2A and B).

We remained uncertain about the 3D spatial relationship between the aneurysm and the PComA. Specifically, our primary concern focused on aneurysmal incorporation of the PComA ostium (i.e., PComA arising directly from the aneurysm dome). If confirmed, clipping would obliterate the posterior-to-anterior collateral perfusion via the PComA, risking borderzone infarction in the right anterior circulation territories.

Given that ICAD is a leading etiology of ischemic stroke in young adults (< 50 years),11 we prioritized endovascular stenting for the dissection at the cervical ICA origin and reassessed the ostium of the right PComA. Under local anesthesia, the patient underwent right ICA stent placement (Fig. 3A and B), a conventional minimally invasive approach for carotid dissection.

A:Postoperative image demonstrating a stent within the left ICA (blue arrow), placed to address the dissection. B:Postoperative ICA angiogram confirming successful repair of the proximal left ICA, demonstrating a patent stent with complete coverage of the dissection and restored antegrade flow (green arrow). C:After carotid stenting restored antegrade flow, diminished competitive flow allows clear visualization of the left PComA arising from the apex of a focal bulge on ICA angiography. The previously suspected aneurysmal structure is confirmed as an infundibulum at the PComA origin (red arrow). D:Postoperative vertebral artery angiogram demonstrating diminished flow through the left PComA (yellow arrow), consistent with reduced collateral demand after carotid stenting.

Postprocedural angiography revealed the PComA originating from the apex of the previously suspected “aneurysm“ structure (Fig. 3C), while the left vertebral angiogram demonstrated resolution of posterior-to-anterior collateral perfusion via the PComA (Fig. 3D). This hemodynamic normalization, which restored anterograde ICA flow, likely eliminated the pathological WSS gradients that had mimicked aneurysm formation. The combined evidence—absence of collateral perfusion and anatomical confirmation of a PComA-originating infundibulum (Fig. 3C)—definitively established an arterial infundibulum diagnosis (Rhoton’s criteria), obviating surgical intervention.

Discussion

Observations

DSA is recognized as the reference standard for diagnosing intracranial aneurysms.12 Its imaging principle involves selective intra-arterial contrast injection via transfemoral access (Seldinger technique), followed by digital subtraction postprocessing to eliminate osseous and soft tissue interference, achieving submillimeter spatial resolution (0.2–0.3 mm) for precise delineation of aneurysmal morphology.13

DSA enables real-time characterization of cerebral hemodynamics and 3D aneurysm assessment through rotational acquisition with volume rendering. This technique provides detailed visualization of the aneurysm neck and adjacent perforating arteries, demonstrating > 95% sensitivity for detecting lesions < 3 mm when utilizing flat-panel detectors with noise-reduction algorithms per ISUIA.14,15

Despite its diagnostic superiority, the invasive nature of DSA (0.5%–2% risk of access site complications) and associated radiation exposure (effective dose 3–5 mSv) necessitate risk-stratified utilization in low-prevalence populations, where noninvasive modalities such as contrast-enhanced MR angiography (CE-MRA) may serve as first-line screening tools.16 According to the 2023 European Stroke Organisation–World Stroke Organization guidelines, CE-MRA exhibits 94% sensitivity for aneurysms > 3 mm in low-prevalence cohorts, justifying its role as a screening tool (class IIa recommendation).17

Per UCAS Japan, UIAs < 5 mm in maximum diameter demonstrated an annualized rupture rate of 0.5% (95% CI 0.3%–0.7%) over a median follow-up of 41 months.1 Despite this low rupture probability, aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) carries 30-day case fatality rates of 35%–40% and mRS score ≥ 3 disability in 50%–60% of survivors.18 Sensitivity analysis based on PHASES (population, hypertension, age, size of aneurysm, earlier SAH, site of aneurysm) and UCAS risk stratification models revealed that microsurgical clipping is the preferred strategy when annualized rupture risk exceeds 0.1% or patient age < 70 years;19–21 conservative surveillance is recommended if the procedural complication risk exceeds 11%. For anterior circulation aneurysms < 7 mm in patients < 70 years, microsurgical clipping demonstrates superior durability (98% 5-year occlusion rate) and is recommended as the first-line intervention.19 Posterior circulation UIAs > 5 mm exhibit a threefold higher rupture risk (1.5%/year), warranting distinct management algorithms based on ELAPSS (earlier SAH, location, age, population, size, shape) score stratification.22

Although advancements in neurointerventional devices have significantly enhanced the safety profile and long-term occlusion rates of endovascular aneurysm treatment, Cochrane systematic reviews and PRISMA-compliant meta-analyses demonstrate superior durability with microsurgical clipping (Raymond-Roy class I occlusion: 95% vs 65% for coiling at 5 years).23 The recurrence rate after endovascular coiling ranges from 20% to 40% (95% CI 18%–42%), whereas the 20-year cumulative risk of recurrence or de novo aneurysm formation postclipping is 9.6%–22.0% per ISUIA long-term data.24 Furthermore, mandatory dual antiplatelet therapy postcoiling—particularly for stent-assisted procedures (≥ 6 months duration)—is contraindicated in 15%–20% of patients due to bleeding diathesis or comorbidities, per American Heart Association/American Stroke Association guidelines.6 These factors establish microsurgical clipping as a cornerstone therapeutic strategy, particularly for younger patients (< 60 years) prioritizing anatomical cure over procedural morbidity. In our case, the patient opted for clip reconstruction after multidisciplinary counseling, emphasizing definitive neck exclusion and avoidance of lifelong antiplatelet dependency.

However, optimal surgical outcomes are predicated on exquisitely precise diagnostic characterization. Given the unequivocal diagnostic authority of DSA in intracranial aneurysm evaluation, clinicians rarely question DSA findings. This case exemplifies a critical caveat: had we proceeded with craniotomy for microsurgical clipping based solely on the initial DSA images per the patient’s request, intraoperative challenges in delineating the PComA origin would have arisen, potentially resulting in compromise of posterior-to-anterior collateral perfusion, a complication associated with significant procedural morbidity.

While DSA provides real-time dynamic visualization, multiplanar reconstruction capabilities, and 0.2-mm spatial resolution, its interpretation at hemodynamically complex junctions (e.g., circle of Willis) remains vulnerable to flow-related artifacts. Vortex flow patterns and pressure gradients in these regions could create pseudoaneurysmal opacification or obscure true neck anatomy, thereby critically influencing therapeutic decision-making.25

In this case, a power injection might have been more beneficial for the assessment of the pseudolesion. However, given the presence of a hemodynamically significant cervical ICA dissection, a power injection was not attempted due to the risk of dissection extension, embolization, or vessel injury. The safety of power injection in the setting of acute dissection is not well established, and we prioritized minimizing procedural risk.

Lessons

This case highlights that proximal hemodynamic alterations from pathologies like ICAD can create convincing pseudolesions that mimic intracranial aneurysms. A meticulous evaluation of the entire vascular tree and a high index of suspicion for flow-related artifacts are paramount. In selected cases, treating the proximal lesion and reassessing the distal vasculature can serve as both a therapeutic and definitive diagnostic step, preventing unnecessary and potentially harmful intracranial interventions.
