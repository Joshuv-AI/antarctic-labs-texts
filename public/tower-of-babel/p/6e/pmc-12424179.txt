Low-Viscosity, Dynamic Amidoamine Hardeners with Tunable Curing Kinetics for Epoxy Adhesives
Authors: Jonas Debuyck, Bram Daelman, Giulia Scurani, Susanne M. Fischer, Filip E. Du Prez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To address the irreversible cross-linking of amidoamine (AA) hardeners, dynamic alternatives have been developed, but high viscosities and nontunable curing kinetics limit practical applications. We present a novel strategy to reduce the viscosity of dynamic AAs by several orders of magnitude and simultaneously tune their curing kinetics with epoxy compounds. Specifically, polysuccinamide- and polyglutarimide-based curing agents were synthesized from bulk chemicals and end-capped with secondary amine functionalities. The curing kinetics of the amidoamine–epoxy system were tuned via the chemical environment of the secondary amine, while the viscosity of the hardeners was controlled by varying the average amidoamine chain length. The versatility of these dynamic hardeners was investigated in conventional bulk adhesive formulations. Tensile and lap-shear experiments on these resins demonstrated strong adhesion (up to 11 MPa) and excellent rebonding capabilities. This straightforward synthetic strategy enables an attractive drop-in technology toward rebondable amidoamine–epoxy hardeners, starting from low-viscosity formulations with tunable curing kinetics.

Introduction

Amidoamines (AAs) are a widely applied class of epoxy hardeners commonly used in industries such as protective coatings, adhesives, automotive, and construction. Typically, these curing agents are synthesized by reacting polyamines with fatty acids or their derivatives, creating a hybrid structure that incorporates both amine and amide functionalities. − Compared to traditional amine hardeners, AAs offer several advantages, including reduced hygroscopicity, lower volatility, and improved compatibility with epoxy formulations, while still retaining key properties of the final resin such as strong adhesion, flexibility, and chemical resistance. − As a result, AAs have emerged as a crucial class of epoxy hardeners that, because of their ease of production and versatility in starting monomers, can be tailored for a wide variety of applications. ,

Despite their desirable properties, conventional AAs create permanent cross-links upon curing, thereby limiting the reprocessing and reshaping potential of the resulting materials. To address this general issue of thermosetting polymers, research has been conducted over the past two decades to develop a new class of materials, namely covalent adaptable networks (CANs), which introduce reversible bonds within the material’s design. − Unlike traditional thermosets, CANs can undergo bond exchange when triggered by external stimuli such as heat or light. Depending on the underlying mechanism, both dissociative and associative CANs can be distinguished, each offering a specific set of material properties. −

Specifically in the context of amidoamine curing agents, our research group, alongside the group of Sijbesma, Heuts, and co-workers, has shown that polyamide backbones can be designed to undergo dissociative exchange without the need for external catalysts or additives. , Through condensation of specific dibasic esters (DBEs) with primary amines, two amide bonds originating from the same DBE can be positioned in close enough proximity, so that they can act as neighboring groups with respect to one another, thereby allowing reversible cyclization toward an imide and amine functionality (Figure ). , Moreover, in a previous work, it was shown that the distance between the amide bonds affects the rate of cyclization and ring-opening, giving a functional handle to control the dynamic properties of the network. ,

Comparison of the presented work with earlier published studies. Van Lijsebetten et al. pioneered the dynamic dicarboxamide chemistry of polyamides and introduced it through a straightforward condensation reaction of bulk chemicals into an epoxy resin. In this work, the amidoamine prepolymers are fully chain stopped to obtain low-viscosity curing agents, which, depending on the nature of the chain stopper, enable a tunable curing process with a multifunctional epoxy resin.

Building on these findings, several follow-up studies have explored the broader potential of this chemistry. ,− Van Lijsebetten et al. successfully introduced the reversible dicarboxamide chemistry into epoxy matrices, resulting in high-performance, debondable and rebondable polyamide-epoxy adhesives that via a simple condensation reaction could be synthesized starting from bulk chemicals (Figure ). While these advancements have significantly contributed to the field of debondable adhesives, broader application potential and industrial uptake still remain limited due to the high viscosities and nontunable curing kinetics of amidoamine formulations. −

To address those issues, several methods have already been reported to increase the processability of amidoamine hardeners. Common approaches include shortening chain length, introducing an odd number of methylene units between the adjacent amide groups, or using a mixture of isomers to induce H-bond mismatching. − Additionally, solvents, reactive diluters, or plasticizers can be added. ,,, Nonetheless, to the best of our knowledge, a one-pot approach to obtain additive-free, low-viscosity dynamic amidoamine curing agents with tunable curing kinetics has not yet been reported.

In this context, we introduce a novel strategy to reduce the viscosity of dynamic AA hardeners by multiple orders of magnitude, while simultaneously enabling control over their curing kinetics with epoxies. Specifically, it is shown how polysuccinamide (PSA) and polyglutaramide (PGA) curing agents can be synthesized from bulk chemicals and chain-stopped with secondary amine functionalities (Figure ), resulting in better control over the average chain length and, by extension, viscosity. , Furthermore, the cure rate of the amidoamine–epoxy mixture can be tailored within a 30–70 °C temperature window by modifying the steric environment of the secondary amine. Finally, the versatility of these PSA and PGA dynamic hardeners is demonstrated by their introduction into conventional bulk adhesive formulations. Tensile and lap-shear experiments on the obtained materials show strong and reversible adhesion, enabling an attractive drop-in technology to design rebondable amidoamine curing agents with low viscosities and tunable curing kinetics.

Results and Discussion

Fully Chain-Stopped Amidoamines

To achieve low-viscosity dynamic AA curing agents, the shortest possible amidoamine structures, containing an odd number of methylene groups to disrupt stacking, were synthesized via the straightforward reaction between 1 eq. of dimethyl glutarate and 2 eq. of various chain stopping amines at 75 °C (Figure a). , Chain termination of the resulting AAs was obtained by taking advantage of the varying nucleophilicity of the primary and secondary amines present in the chain stoppers. The corresponding amidoamine structures will be named after the abbreviation of the applied alkyl end group, methyl (M), ethyl (E), or isopropyl (I), followed by either ethylene (E) or propylene (P), indicating the number of carbon atoms between the end-capping secondary amine and amide functionality.

(a) Straightforward condensation reaction of 1 equiv of dimethyl glutarate with 2 equiv of various chain stopping molecules. (b) Dynamic temperature-dependent viscosity of these curing agents and (c) mixed viscosity measured when reacting N,N-diglycidyl-4-glycidyloxyaniline (TriN) with MP, EE, or IP amidoamines as a function of time.

All amidoamines, apart from ME due to lower selectivity between its amine functionalities, were obtained quantitatively with purities of 93% or higher, as confirmed by the NMR spectra shown in Figures S1–S5. The main identified side product is a tertiary amide, formed by the reaction between dimethyl glutamate and the secondary amine of the chain stopper. Despite this, all amidoamines exhibited comparable purities, and no residual MeOH or H2O was detected, enabling a reliable relative comparison of their thermal behavior by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). Surprisingly, even small variations in the molecular structure of the chain stopper molecules resulted in completely different properties. It was observed that MP, EE, and IP exhibited either no or low melting points (<45 °C) and did not recrystallize over the timespan of a DSC measurement, indicating slow crystallization kinetics (Figures S6–S10). IE and EP, on the other hand, displayed higher melting points (80–90 °C) and increased crystallization kinetics (Figures S8,S9). Despite these differences, no consistent pattern could be identified based on molecular weight, chain stopper type, or the number of methylene units between the amide and amine functionalities (Figure a). Nonetheless, MP, EE, and IP clearly exhibited the weakest intermolecular interactions, resulting in dynamic viscosities ranging from 500–1250 mPa.s at 50 °C and below 300 mPa.s at 70 °C (Figure b).

In the next step, these three amidoamines were evaluated in terms of their curing kinetics with an epoxy compound. For this, the amidoamines MP, EE, and IP were each mixed with a low-viscosity trisepoxide, N,N-diglycidyl-4-glycidyloxyaniline (TriN), at an epoxy-to-amine molar ratio of 1. The mixed viscosities of the resulting combinations, MP-TriN, EE-TriN, and IP-TriN, were monitored over time at various temperatures ranging from 30–70 °C to examine the curing kinetics in bulk (Figure S11). As shown in Figure c (kinetics at 50 °C), the type of chain stopper significantly influenced the curing speed of the epoxy/amidoamine system. Specifically, the MP-TriN formulation exhibited fast curing, even at ambient temperature, while IP-TriN required heating to around 70 °C before amine–epoxy curing proceeded quickly, as shown in Figure S11. EE-TriN displayed intermediate curing kinetics, reaching a mixed viscosity above 5000 mPa s in less than 15 min at 50 °C (Figure c). These results highlight how the design of the chain stopper enables tailor-made control over the curing kinetics, and thus the pot-life in a range from minutes to hours, of the epoxy-amidoamine mixture.

Chain Length of Amidoamine Structures

To further investigate how the chain stopping of the AAs affects the overall viscosity of the mixture, curing agents were synthesized with a similar content of dynamic bonds in comparison to previously published work (±60 mol % of NH bonds part of a dynamic amide functionality). For this, 0.33 eq. of any bifunctional primary amine was combined with 1 eq. of dimethyl glutarate (Glu) as DBE and 1.33 eq. of N-methyl-1,3-diaminopropane (MP) as a chain stopper. Since commercial curing agents are typically categorized as either aliphatic or cycloaliphatic/aromatic amidoamines, various bifunctional primary amines, among which Pria, Prop, TMHD, Pent, BAC, MXDA, IPDA, and DACH, were incorporated in the formulation to assess their specific impact on viscosity (Figure ). Additionally, MP was selected as a chain stopper, since it is the most cost-effective option and closely resembles the ambient curing behavior of most commercially available amidoamines (Figure S11). ,

Comparison of dynamic, temperature-dependent viscosities of amidoamines from this work made with various aliphatic, cyclic, and aromatic bifunctional primary amines, with the previously reported PGA3 curing agent.

In line with the fully chain-stopped amidoamines, all dynamic curing agents showed tertiary amide formation as the main side product. Specifically, the aliphatic dynamic curing agents (Figures S12, S13) exhibited purities comparable to their fully chain-stopped amidoamine counterparts (>91%), while the cyclic and aromatic variants, containing more hindered primary amines, formed slightly higher amounts of this side-product, with estimated purities above 88% (Figures S16–S18). Nonetheless, as illustrated in Figure , fully end-capping the amidoamines with secondary amine functionalities significantly reduces viscosity, independent of the nature of the selected Bisamine. Indeed, while maintaining a similar amount of dynamic bonds, the resulting curing agents exhibited viscosities that were reduced by multiple orders of magnitude compared to the PGA3 hardener applied in a previous study. Moreover, as expected, amidoamines synthesized with aliphatic bifunctional amines exhibited viscosities lower than those of their cyclic and aromatic counterparts, which can be attributed to increased chain flexibility and the use of isomeric mixtures (vide supra).

Moreover, the orders-of-magnitude lower viscosities observed in this work could be rationalized by SEC analysis, which confirmed that the fully chain-stopped amidoamines had much lower number-average molecular weights (M n) compared to the PGA3 hardener from previous work (Table S1 and Figure S22). It is also worth noting that the viscosity of MP-Glu-Pria, MP-Glu-Prop, and MP-Glu-BAC could not be measured due to gelling or crystallization (Figures S14,19).

In addition to the effect of the bifunctional amine, the influence of DBE on the overall viscosity of the mixture was investigated. For this, the viscosity of the MP–Glu–TMHD mixture was compared to similar AAs containing dimethyl succinate (MP–Suc–TMHD) and a 50/50 molar mixture of dimethyl glutarate and succinate (MP–GluSuc–TMHD). As shown in Figure S23, the choice of DBE had a minimal effect, with the ester mixture (MP–GluSuc–TMHD) exhibiting only a slightly lower viscosity. Besides the minor impact on the overall viscosity, a more significant effect of the DBE moieties on the dynamic material properties of the corresponding resins was anticipated. To investigate this, the three hardeners MP-Glu-TMHD, MP-Suc-TMHD, and MP-GluSuc-TMHD were cured with TriN at 70 °C for 8 h, followed by a short postcure at 100 °C for 1 h. Additionally, a nondynamic reference material (ref-TriN) was prepared using dimethyl pimelate. Those materials will be further referred to as Glu-TriN, Suc-TriN, GluSuc-TriN, and ref-TriN. As shown in Table and Figure S24, swelling tests in chloroform and FTIR measurements confirmed proper network formation of all epoxy materials, with low soluble fractions around 3% and FTIR spectra indicating the absence of epoxide stretch vibrations at around 905 cm–1. All materials exhibited glass transition temperatures (T g) in the range of 46 to 59 °C (Figure S25). Thermal stability was demonstrated by TGA analysis with degradation onset temperatures (T d5%) around 300 °C, as determined from ramped measurements. Isothermal TGA at 190 °C further confirmed the materials’ resistance to prolonged thermal exposure (Figure S26). The slightly higher weight loss observed for Suc-TriN and GluSuc-TriN was expected, as dynamic exchange attributed to succinimide formation is already activated at 190 °C. Additionally, swelling tests in water at 70 °C for 48 h showed low soluble fractions (<5%) and moderate swelling (∼50%), indicating good hydrolytic stability (Table S2).

Another important consideration when designing materials with fully end-capped amidoamines is whether they still exhibit good strength and nonbrittle behavior. From the stress–strain curves depicted in Figure a, it can be concluded that all materials exhibited very strong and tough behavior, with Young’s moduli and ultimate strengths ranging from 2.4–3.1 GPa and 43–63 MPa, respectively. , The slightly lower values for Suc-TriN can be attributed to the presence of some trapped five-membered imide bonds, as shown in Figure S24, while the lower values for ref-TriN can likely be attributed to its reduced cross-linking density and T g. Nonetheless, all amidoamine–epoxy materials demonstrated superior strength compared to previously published work, highlighting that fully chain-stopping the AAs does not negatively affect their mechanical properties.

Viscoelastic Behavior of Dynamic Amidoamine–Epoxy Networks

Depending on the ester mixture used, reprocessing of the dynamic networks by compression molding could be carried out at 190 °C, 210 °C, and 250 °C for Suc-TriN, GluSuc-TriN, and Glu-TriN, respectively (Figure b). To evaluate the effect of reprocessing on the material properties, temperature-sweep measurements were performed on both pristine and reprocessed samples. As shown in Figure S28, the results are consistent with the DSC data, with all materials exhibiting comparable storage moduli (G′) within their rubbery region. Additionally, the sharp tan delta peaks confirm uniform and homogeneous network formation before and after reprocessing.

Subsequently, stress-relaxation measurements were performed on the reprocessed materials, and the data were fitted to a stretched single Maxwell model. As depicted in Figure , a clear decrease in initial relaxation modulus (G0) was observed at elevated temperatures, which was expected because of the dissociative nature of the dicarboxamide exchange. Moreover, the ester monomer(s) used and thus the size of the cyclic intermediates formed during exchange significantly influenced the stress-relaxation profiles of the various materials. Specifically, Suc-TriN (Figure a), exhibited a noticeably faster relaxation compared to Glu-TriN (Figure c), as expected based on a previous report.

Non-normalized stress-relaxation data of (a) Suc-TriN, (b) GluSuc-TriN, and (c) Glu-TriN between 150 and 250 °C.

In contrast, GluSuc-TriN (Figure b) showed an intermediate relaxation profile, featuring both a rapid dissociation mode (attributed to succinimide formation) and a slower dissociation mode (associated with glutaramide formation), leading to a clear deviation from the stretched Maxwell single model fit. While previously reported work attributed a negligible impact to the use of different DBE’s, and thus cyclic intermediates during the dynamic exchange, to diffusion constraints, the present work clearly demonstrates that the choice of DBE for the much shorter amidoamine chains can indeed control the viscoelastic behavior of the dynamic amidoamine–epoxy networks. In other words, these findings highlight that, depending on the intended application, a tailored DBE mixture can be selected to achieve the desired viscoelastic properties, thus once more emphasizing the potential of fully end-capping amidoamine chains.

Adhesive Properties

With these results in mind, the potential for (re)­bonding of the modified epoxy adhesives was assessed using lap-shear experiments. For this, various amidoamine mixtures were speed-mixed with TriN in a 1:1 molar epoxy-to-amine ratio at 70 °C. Next, the adhesives were applied to aluminum substrates (100 × 25.4 × 1.62 mm) with a defined overlap area of 15.8 mm and a wet film thickness of 200 μm, following the ASTM D1002 standard. All adhesives were cured at 70 °C for 8 h, followed by a postcure of 1 h at 100 °C (Figure S30).

As shown in Figure , all epoxy materials showed good adhesive strength with values up to 11 MPa without the need for fillers or other additives. Moreover, lap-shear tests were repeated on the previously used and rebonded samples to demonstrate the healability of the dynamic adhesives. For this, the specimens were rewelded in an oven at their reprocessing temperature for 15 min using clamps (Figure S30). All dynamic amidoamine–epoxy materials recovered 80 to 85% of their original lap-shear strength, while the reference adhesive, which was rewelded for 15 min at 250 °C, showed only limited recovery under the same conditions (Figure ). Finally, it is also worth noting that the amidoamine–epoxy adhesives from this work demonstrated superior lap-shear strengths compared to previously published work, while retaining similar healability.

Conclusion

In summary, this work introduces a novel strategy to significantly reduce the viscosity of dynamic amidoamine hardeners, while simultaneously enabling control over their curing kinetics with epoxy matrices. Specifically, polysuccinamide and polyglutarimide amidoamine curing agents were synthesized, starting from bulk chemicals and fully chain-stopped by exploiting the difference in reactivity between primary and secondary amines. The type of chain stopper had a surprisingly strong impact on the thermal behavior and reactivity of the amidoamine hardeners. Slightly modifying the chemical environment of the secondary amine enabled extensive control over the curing kinetics and pot life when mixed with epoxies. Furthermore, a comparison with previously published research was made by slightly extending the AA’s chain lengths to achieve a comparable amount of NH bonds part of a dynamic amide functionality, using commercially relevant aliphatic and cycloaliphatic and aromatic bifunctional amines. This approach resulted in viscosities that were lowered by several orders of magnitude, with the relaxation profile of the resulting amidoamine–epoxy networks being strongly influenced by the choice of DBE. Finally, lap shear testing demonstrated that the obtained materials showed promising adhesive properties while maintaining their reversible binding characteristics.
