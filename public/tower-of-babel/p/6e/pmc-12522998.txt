Responses of stem cells isolated from human primary teeth to calcium-containing pulp capping materials
Authors: Pornchanok Intavongsri, Marcio A da Fonseca, Suphalak Phothichailert, Thanaphum Osathanon, Waleerat Sukarawan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulp capping materials are used in vital pulp therapy to protect the dental pulp, prevent bacterial infiltration, and promote the formation of a dentin bridge. These materials frequently contain calcium, which is vital for the mineralization process and may influence cell responses. This study aimed to investigate the effects of pulp capping materials on the proliferation, migration, and osteogenic differentiation of stem cells from exfoliated primary teeth (SHEDs) in an in vitro environment.MethodsThe study involved preparing five pulp capping materials, incubating them with a culture medium, And assessing calcium ion release over 7 days. SHEDs were cultured in the medium, And cell viability, as indicated by cellular metabolic activity, was evaluated using 3-(4, 5-dimethylthiazolyl-2)-2, 5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay and live/dead assays. Mineralization was examined through Alizarin Red and Von Kossa staining, quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) assay was used to analyze the mRNA expression of osteogenic gene markers. Results, presented as mean ± SD, were subjected to statistical analysis using Kruskal-Wallis, Mann-Whitney U tests, and Friedman tests.ResultsAll pulp capping materials released calcium ions in a time-dependent manner, with Biodentine™ exhibiting the highest levels, which were significantly higher than Dycal® at 24 h, 3, And 7 days (p < 0.05). Cellular metabolic activity decreased with increased concentrations of Dycal® and Theracal™ LC, suggesting potential toxicity to SHEDs. Despite higher toxicity levels observed with Dycal® and Theracal™ LC in the live/dead assay, live cells were still present, indicating a response to the materials. Calcimol LC, ProRoot® MTA, and Biodentine™ showed higher cell percentages than the controls. Pulp capping materials did not significantly affect cell migration, but Biodentine™ significantly increased mineralized nodule formation compared to Dycal® (p < 0.05).ConclusionsCalcimol LC, ProRoot® MTA, and Biodentine™ were less toxic to SHEDs compared to Dycal® and Theracal™ LC. Biodentine™ demonstrated a slight increase in cell proliferation and significantly increased in vitro mineralization compared to Dycal® and Theracal™ LC.Keywords: SHEDs, Pulp capping, Calcium silicate materials, Calcium hydroxide

Background and rationale

Pulp capping materials are applied for vital pulp therapy to stimulate the formation of a protective layer over the exposed vital pulp [1], prevent bacterial infiltration, induce minimal inflammation, and stimulate dentin bridge formation; therefore, they must be biocompatible and bioactive to support dental pulp stem cell activity and pulp healing [2, 3].

Calcium-containing pulp capping materials are generally classified into four main categories: (1) calcium hydroxide [Ca(OH)₂]-based materials, including hard-setting formulations such as Dycal® (Dentsply, North Carolina, USA) and light-curing setting such as Calcimol LC (VOCO GmbH, Lower Saxony, Germany); (2) resin-modified calcium silicate-based cements, exemplified by Theracal™ LC (Bisco Inc., Illinois, USA); (3) calcium silicate-based cements without resin, such as mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA; Dentsply, North Carolina, USA); and (4) tricalcium silicate-based materials, including Biodentine™ (Septodont, Paris, France). These pulp capping materials vary not only in the type of calcium, but also in their calcium concentrations and many other additives.

Calcium (Ca2+) is an essential element in the mineralization process and is an active component in pulp capping materials [4]. Different calcium concentrations released from pulp capping materials are hypothesized to have the most influence on cellular responses, while this is currently unproven. For instance, the effects of extracellular calcium alone on cell proliferation have been studied in numerous cell types, including bone marrow stromal stem cell (BMSCs), osteoblasts, human mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), dental pulp stem cell (DPSCs), and stem cells from exfoliated primary teeth (SHEDs) [5–8]. Previous [8] revealed Ca2+ concentrations in cultures of DPSCs had little effect on cell proliferation but can change the osteogenic differentiation processes of DPSCs.

Intriguingly, tests with the whole calcium-based pulp capping compound revealed distinct effects, as determined by in vitro mineralization and osteogenic marker gene expression [3]. The extra components in pulp capping materials or the specific optimal range of Ca2+ in the proliferation process are likely reasons of difference in the results of research employing Ca2+ and calcium-based pulp capping compound material. These hypotheses require further examination.

Several studies [3]– [4, 8, 13, 25]– [26] have examined the influence of pulp capping materials on dental pulp stem cells from permanent teeth, but only a small number of studies have utilized dental pulp stem cells from primary teeth [9, 10]. Proliferation, migration, and osteogenic differentiation vary between DPSCs and SHEDs. There are also distinct reactions to the pulp capping materials due to the modification of growth factor release, activation of signaling pathways, and regulation of transcription [10]. This in vitro study aimed to investigate how different pulp capping materials, when placed in direct contact with SHEDs, affect cell proliferation, cell migration, and osteogenic differentiation.

Methods

Cell isolation and culture

The isolation procedure for SHEDs received approval from the Human Research Ethics Committee at the Faculty of Dentistry, Chulalongkorn University, Bangkok, Thailand (No. 038/2023). Healthy primary teeth were extracted as part of the dental treatment protocol and stored in a culture medium. Dental pulp tissues were extracted, and cells were isolated and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, Grand Island, New York, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (HyClone, USA), 1% L-glutamine, 100 units/mL Penicillin, And 100µg/mL Streptomycin (Gibco). The cells underwent incubation at 37 °C within a humidified 5% CO2 environment. The media was substituted biweekly. Cells were detached at confluence using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco). In this study, cells from passages 3 to 6 were utilized to confirm stem cell characteristics and were assessed for their multipotential differentiation capacity through in vitro mineralization assays. The expression of surface proteins associated with mesenchymal stem cell markers and hematopoietic markers was assessed using flow cytometry.

Flow cytometry analysis

The cells were separated into individual cell suspensions using a 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution. Following centrifugation, the supernatant of the culture media was discarded. The cells underwent washing with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and were labeled using primary antibodies conjugated to fluorescent dyes, including anti-human CD44 (BD Bioscience Pharmingen, San Jose, California, USA), PerCP-CyTM5.5-conjugated anti-human CD90 (BD Bioscience Pharmingen), and PerCP-conjugated anti-CD45 (BD Bioscience Pharmingen). Flow cytometry analysis was conducted utilizing a FACSCalibur flow cytometer in conjunction with CellQuest software (BD Bioscience Pharmingen).

Materials and extraction medium preparation

Dycal® and ProRoot®MTA were purchased from Dentsply International Inc., Charlotte, North Carolina, USA. Biodentine™ was obtained from Septodont, located in Paris, France. Calcimol LC was obtained from Voco GmbH located in Cuxhaven, Lower Saxony, Germany, while Theracal™ LC was acquired from Bisco Inc. in Schaumburg, Illinois, USA. All materials were prepared And placed into a cylindrical mold with a height of 5mm And a radius of 2.5 mm, resulting in a surface area of 1.18 cm² And a volume of 0.1 cm³, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The mixtures were prepared under sterile conditions And incubated at room temperature for 24h prior to demolding. Each material was then incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in a humid environment with 5% CO2, using 1 mL of culture media as specified in ISO 10,993 Part 12. The extraction medium from each sample was filtered through a 0.1 μm filter and stored at −20 °C until required for use. Calcium ion release And the pH of the extraction medium were assessed before the experiments. Serial dilutions of the extraction medium were created at concentrations of 10%, 25%, 50%, And undiluted solution. After 24h of exposure to the extraction medium, a 3-(4, 5-dimethylthiazolyl-2)−2, 5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay was performed to assess the mitochondrial metabolic activity. A relative cell viability of ≥ 70% for the highest concentration of the test item extract indicates that the material is classified as non-cytotoxic [11]. Cells cultured in growth medium acted as the control group. A minimum of 70% cell viability from the extracted medium was necessary for conducting migration and osteo/odontogenic differentiation assays.

The pulp capping materials were placed in odontogenic media throughout the osteo/odontogenic induction assay. Odontogenic media were prepared by incorporating 50 µg/ml ascorbic acid, 100 nM dexamethasone, And 5 mM glycerophosphate into the growth medium. The odontogenic media extraction medium was prepared using the same method as the growth medium.

In vitro release of calcium ions

The optimal calcium concentration for cell viability was established through a calcium detection kit, while the in vitro release of calcium ions from the materials was assessed using a Calcium Colorimetric Assay (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA). Samples of the eluted medium were collected at 6 h, 1 day, 3 days, 5 days, And 7 days. The samples were placed in microtubes containing 50 µl of DMEM, which was collected from each experimental group for analysis, while the control consisted of DMEM devoid of any materials. The supernatants were stored at −20 °C prior to analysis.

Cellular metabolic activity assay

Subsequent media in 10-fold dilutions of these extraction media were prepared for Analysis. SHEDs were cultured in 24-well plates at a density of 50,000 cells per well, with five replicates for each pulp capping material. Cellular metabolic activity, as An indirect indicator of cell viability, was assessed at 1, 4, And 7 days utilizing the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. The medium in each well was removed And replaced with 300µl of MTT solution (USB Corporation, Cleveland, Ohio, USA) to promote formazan crystal formation. This was conducted for 15 min at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 5% CO2. The formazan was dissolved with eluting agents, specifically dimethylsulfoxide and glycine buffer. The optical density (OD) of the solutions was measured with a microplate reader (ELx800; BIO-TEK®, Winooski, Vermont, USA) at a wavelength of 570 nm.

Live/dead assay

SHEDs were cultured in 96-well plates at a density of 12,500 cells per well. Following a 24-hour period, the culture medium was substituted with extraction media comprising five repetitions of different pulp capping materials. On days 1, 4, And 7 of culture, SHEDs were washed with PBS, followed by the addition of 1 µL of the reconstituted fluorescent reactive dye to each well. The cells were incubated at room temperature or on ice for 30 min, shielded from light exposure. Cell images were obtained through light And fluorescence microscopy using a Nikon Eclipse 90-I microscope (Nikon Instruments Inc., Melville, New York, USA).

Migration assay

Cell migration was Analyzed through wound healing assays. SHEDs were cultured at a density of 25,000 cells per well in 6-well plates until confluence was achieved. A wound gap was established by scratching a vertical line through the cell monolayer with a 10 µL pipette tip. The culture medium was subsequently substituted with different pulp capping materials. Microphotographs of the scratch were captured at 0-, 12-, And 24-hours post-wounding using a phase-contrast microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Image analysis was conducted utilizing ImageJ software (National Institute of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) to evaluate the wound closure rate.

In vitro mineralization assay

SHEDs were cultured in 24-well plates at a density of 12,500 cells per well. Following a 24-hour period, the culture medium was substituted with osteogenic induction media that included five different pulp capping materials. The media was changed every 48 h. Alizarin Red S staining And Von Kossa staining were used to examine mineral deposition on day 14 of culture. Alizarin Red S staining involved washing SHEDs with PBS And subsequently fixing them in 4% formalin in PBS for 10 min. The cells were subsequently washed twice with deionized water And incubated in Alizarin Red S solution for 3min at room temperature. Following incubation, the cells underwent three washes with deionized water to eliminate excess staining. The dye precipitate was solubilized in a 10% w/v cetylpyridinium chloride solution, And absorbance was measured at 570nm. SHEDs were subjected to a single rinse with PBS And subsequently fixed in 4% formalin in PBS for a duration of 10 min for Von Kossa staining. The cells were rinsed twice with deionized water And subsequently exposed to a 1% silver nitrate solution, incubated under ultraviolet light, for 30 min. Subsequently, the cells underwent three washes with deionized water to eliminate excess staining and were dried overnight.

Real-time polymerase chain reaction assay

The expression levels of osteogenic marker genes were examined using Reverse Transcription followed by quantitative (real-time) polymerase chain reaction assay following 4 And 10 days in osteogenic induction medium containing different pulp capping materials. Trizol reagent was utilized for the extraction of total cellular RNA following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The ImPromII reverse transcriptase kit was utilized to synthesize complementary DNA (cDNA) (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin, USA). qPCR was performed using the FastStart Essential DNA Green Master Kit (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Baden-Württemberg, Germany) on a MiniOpticon real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, California, USA). Gene expression values were normalized to GAPDH expression and subsequently adjusted to control levels. The sequences of the primers utilized were.

RUNX2 forward: 5’ ATGATGACACTGCCACCTCTG 3’,

RUNX2 reverse: 5’ GGCTGGATAGTGCATTCGTG 3’,

OSX forward: 5’ GCCAGAAGCTGTGAAACCTC 3’,

OSX reverse: 5’ GCTGCAAGCTCTCCATAA 3’,

ALP forward: 5’ GACCTCCTCGGAAGACACTC 3’,

ALP reverse: 5’ TGAAGGGCTTCTTGTCTGTG 3’,

OCN forward: 5’ CCTGAAAGCCGATGTGGT 3’,

OCN reverse: 5’ GGCAGCGAGGTAGTGAAGA3’.

Statistical analyses

All experiments were conducted with cells from a minimum of three distinct donors (n = 3) to ensure biological replication, consistent with prior studies. All data were expressed using the mean and standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis utilized the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison And Mann-Whitney U tests. All statistical Analyses were conducted using Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA). A p-value of 0.05 was considered significant.

Discussion

Direct pulp capping is a dental procedure that aims to maintain the vitality of the exposed pulp, thereby avoiding root canal treatment. Frequently employed materials include calcium hydroxide and calcium silicate-based cement due to their efficacy in promoting pulp vitality preservation and facilitating dentin bridge formation. When selecting a material for vital pulp therapy, careful consideration of biocompatibility and bioactivity is crucial since it directly interacts with vital tissue [12].

This study investigated the effects of five available calcium-containing pulp capping materials. These materials are commonly believed to stimulate the healing or regeneration of dental pulp by promoting direct contact with remaining dental pulp stem cells, as suggested by various quantitative and qualitative evaluations of pulp capping materials [13].

The study results revealed that pulp capping materials have a different pattern of releasing calcium ions, displaying a time-dependent increase in release. Notably, Biodentine™ consistently exhibited the highest calcium ion release throughout the observation period. This finding is in agreement with other studies; for instance, Manaspon et al. [3] demonstrated higher levels of calcium ions from Biodentine™ compared to Dycal® after 24 h. However, there was no statistically significant difference in calcium ion release between Biodentine™ and ProRoot® MTA in their study. In contrast, the present investigation found statistically significant differences between Dycal® and Biodentine™ at 1, 3, And 7 days.

Moreover, this study found no significant difference in calcium ion release among Theracal™ LC, Calcimol LC, ProRoot® MTA, and Biodentine™ at 24 h. When considering each material individually, no significant difference was observed over various time intervals (1, 3, And 7 days), consistent with a similar study [3]. Therefore, the study planned to utilize extraction media collected after the 24-hour calcium release assessment for subsequent experiments aimed at evaluating cellular responses to the pulp capping materials.

As mentioned earlier, all pulp capping materials contained varying amounts of calcium. Calcium signaling pathways play a vital role in regulating essential cellular functions such as proliferation, differentiation, and migration. A previous study indicated no significant differences in the effect of calcium concentrations on the cell proliferation ability of SHEDs [8].

The study showed a decrease in cell viability with increasing concentrations of Dycal® and Theracal™ LC, indicating potential toxicity to SHEDs. At 7 days, all concentrations of Calcimol LC exhibited higher cell proliferation compared to the control. Similarly, 10% And 25% concentrations of ProRoot®MTA and Biodentine™ showed increased cell proliferation compared to the control. Therefore, the study data aligned with findings from Collado-Gonzalez et al. [9], indicating that MTA Angelus and Biodentine™ led to significantly higher cell proliferation rates in SHEDs compared to Theracal™ LC all concentrations. Additionally, Manaspon et al. [3] demonstrated that ProRoot® MTA and Biodentine™ appeared to promote proliferation of dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) at every concentration on day 7. This suggested that Calcimol LC, ProRoot® MTA, and Biodentine™ indeed exhibited superior viability for SHEDs compared to Dycal® and Theracal™ LC.

Following the ISO 10,993 protocol, the percentage of cell viability was ≥ 70%, indicating the non-cytotoxicity of the components. Due to the longer duration of most experiments, lasting longer than 7 days, we chose to assess the effect of pulp capping materials at non-cytotoxic concentrations over a period of 7 days. This approach aimed to ensure that the results accurately reflected the impact of the pulp capping materials, free from the influence of cell death. Through this procedure, the optimal concentrations of the extracted medium were determined. The findings revealed that treatment with 25% extracted medium of Calcimol LC, ProRoot® MTA, and Biodentine™ as well as 10% concentrations of Dycal® and Theracal™ LC, resulted in cell viability percentages of 70% or higher, affirming the non-cytotoxic nature of these materials. These findings were consistent with previous research, where treating DPSCs with 10% concentrations of Dycal®, Theracal™ LC, ProRoot® MTA, and Biodentine™ similarly resulted in non-cytotoxic levels and stimulated cell proliferation [3].

Prior research had investigated the effect of pulp capping materials on cell proliferation in numerous cell types using the MTT test. Our study revealed that all pulp capping materials exhibited a comparable cell proliferation rate to the control group on day 1. However, there was no significant difference observed among the pulp capping materials. Calcimol LC, ProRoot® MTA, and Biodentine™ demonstrated increased cell proliferation in SHEDs after 4 And 7 days. These findings aligned with a study on DPSCs [14]. Both ProRoot® MTA and Biodentine™ were found to be non-cytotoxic, with no significant differences observed between MTA Angelus and Biodentine™ extracts. Poggio et al. [15] demonstrated that Biodentine™ had superior cell biocompatibility compared to ProRoot® MTA, Calcimol LC, Theracal™ LC, and Dycal® after 72 h of culture in the mouse odontoblast cell line (MDPC-23), although no significant differences were seen between ProRoot® MTA and Biodentine™. Moreover, Collado-Gonzalez et al. [9] showed that Biodentine™ exhibited better cytocompatibility and bioactivity compared to MTA Angelus and Theracal™ LC.

The use of various concentrations for the five materials might have resulted in diverse cellular responses. Lower concentrations of the extraction medium could potentially release fewer calcium ions And additives compared to higher concentrations. Although no significant difference was observed between the 10% And 25% concentration, an undiluted solution was commonly used clinically. However, this study also revealed that cell viability responses were not solely dependent on the concentration of the material, as higher concentration did not always lead to improved cell viability. Therefore, further research is crucial to ascertain the optimal concentration for each pulp capping material.

Although Dycal® showed lower calcium ion release compared to the other materials, it exhibited the highest cytotoxicity. This suggests that calcium may not be the cause of this cytotoxicity. One potential explanation for the adverse effects of Dycal® was that the presence of residual or unreacted calcium hydroxide significantly affects the physical and chemical characteristics of the cement, such as alkalinity and biodegradability, as demonstrated by Zamanian et al. [16]. Increased pH levels can cause cell death due to the production of hydroxyl ions.

Additionally, two-paste calcium hydroxide systems are more prone to toxicity compared to one-paste systems, likely due to the presence of additional ingredients such as disalicylate, accelerator, and/or plasticizer [17]. Interestingly, a study on direct pulp capping materials revealed that self-setting calcium hydroxide material is more effective in protecting dental pulp than light-cured materials, regardless of composition. However, light-activated polymerization can leave residual monomers at the pulp capping site [18]. In line with the present results, Theracal™ LC exhibited higher cytotoxicity than Calcimol LC, potentially due to its more hydrophilic matrix, which results in a greater release of calcium hydroxide and a more significant increase in pH [19]. The elevated pH of Theracal™ LC was observed through the darker color change of the pH indicator strips in the extraction medium compared to other materials. Although Theracal™ LC is similar to Calcimol LC in terms of light curing, the additives released from Theracal™ LC during light curing may induce cell death by increasing the production of reactive oxygen species. Previous studies reported that contact with DPSCs results in reduced cell metabolism and incomplete tissue bridges due to methacrylate resin components altering cell membranes [20]. These findings contrast with those of Kim et al. [21], which suggested that Theracal™ LC exhibits better biocompatibility and bioactivity than Dycal®.

MTA/calcium silicate-based materials are less toxic than calcium hydroxides [22], exhibiting superior sealing ability, biocompatibility, and higher physical strength. They also reduce swelling, hyperemia, and necrosis while promoting the development of the odontoblastic layer and thicker dentinal bridging [23]. This is in agreement with ProRoot® MTA showing a higher cell count compared to Dycal® in the present study. Furthermore, the presence of different ions, such as Zn, in dental materials is involved in their cytotoxicity [24], The cytotoxicity of NeoMTA Plus and MTA Repair HP is related to the presence of Sr, Al, and S, while their low content or absence is associated with the biocompatibility of Biodentine™ [25].

In addition to proliferation, cells need to migrate to the site and differentiate into odontoblast cells in pulp exposure cavities to preserve or protect the dental pulp [26]. Therefore, dental materials should promote cell migration. This study found that at 24 h, all pulp capping materials exhibited a significant increase in wound closure compared to the initial time point. However, there was no significant difference compared to the control. Similarly, research evaluating the cell migration of DPSCs found no significant difference in the rate of cell migration at 24 h compared to the control group [27]. This contrasts with the findings of Collado-Gonzalez et al. [9] in SHEDs, who reported that treatment with different dilutions of Biodentine™ eluates for 24 h induced a slightly lower, although still significant, cell migration rate compared to the control. The extracts of Biodentine™ And MTA Angelus were observed to significantly promote wound closure after 48h of treatment, comparable to the control. This difference in results may arise from variations in the concentration of material tested and the starving condition, which might affect the cell responses.

Various methods, including the expression of osteogenic marker genes via qPCR, mineralization assessed through Alizarin red and Von Kossa staining, and the quantification of calcium content observed in Alizarin red staining, were employed to evaluate the effects of pulp capping materials. The present study revealed no statistically significant difference in the gene expression of RUNX2, OSX, ALP, and OCN at all time points. However, Calcimol LC, ProRoot® MTA, and Biodentine™ appeared to boost the expression of RUNX2, a critical regulator of osteoblastogenesis, and ALP, an early indicator of osteogenesis [28]. RUNX2 And OSX are involved in the initial stages of the mineralization process by osteoblasts. This enhancement observed on day 4 suggests that these pulp capping materials may have a stimulating effect on osteoblast proliferation and the early differentiation process. Furthermore, the findings suggested that the Biodentine™ group exhibited a higher level of calcified nodule formation on day 14 compared to the other groups. Previous studies have shown that Biodentine™ significantly promotes mineralized nodule formation in SHEDs, whereas the effect of MTA Angelus was less obvious [9]. Similarly, Bortoluzzi et al. [29] reported that Biodentine™ enhanced calcium nodule formation in DPSCs compared to Theracal™ LC. In line with Mora et al. [30], Biodentine XP upregulated osteogenic markers (ALP, DSPP) and led to greater calcium deposition than MTA Angelus and Theracal PT. Our findings further indicated that ProRoot® MTA did not significantly differ from the control group; however, both Biodentine™ and ProRoot® MTA demonstrated superior osteogenic potential in DPSCs [3].

Consequently, while Biodentine™ accelerated the process of mineralization in SHEDs and had some effect on osteogenic gene expression, these effects were not statistically significant in this study.

Studies aiming to identify the most biocompatible dental materials are crucial in dental practice. This in vitro study reported significant differences in the biocompatibility effects among five pulp capping materials. Previously, hard-setting calcium hydroxide pastes have been the material of choice in pulp capping procedures [31]. However, the usage of calcium hydroxide is known to be toxic to dental pulp cells and is not routinely recommended for primary dentition [32, 33]. Our study supported this conclusion, as our investigation into the effect of pulp capping materials on the cell viability of SHEDs revealed that Dycal® and Theracal™ LC exhibit significantly higher levels of toxicity compared to others. Despite their cytotoxicity, these materials may still have utility in cases of indirect pulp capping since they do not directly contact cells. Additionally, they are readily available, easy to use, and cost-effective. In cases where a clinic lacks materials other than Dycal®, a dentist might choose selective caries removal in deep cavities. This approach aims to minimize pulp exposure to Dycal®, a material that could be toxic to pulp cells.

Furthermore, Biodentine™ exhibited superior efficacy compared to Dycal® in enhancing both cell survival and functional differentiation of SHEDs. Therefore, Biodentine™ could serve as an alternative for direct pulp capping in primary dentition. Additionally, The American Association of Endodontists recommends the increasing utilization of calcium silicate cements, which have also been increasingly favored in vital pulp therapy procedures [34]. While it is more expensive than some other materials, Biodentine™ can be a superior long-term option for treating multiple teeth simultaneously, especially under general anesthesia (GA). However, because it increases the success rate of the treatment, which is crucial for preserving primary teeth for a longer period, it reduces the need for repeat GA or repeated visits to the clinic for retreatment, which are both very costly.

In recent years, advancements in pulp capping materials have improved controlled setting And physical strength. Light-curable resin-modified products offer these benefits, but controversy remains over their application to pulpal tissues. Thus, further studies on this topic involving SHEDs And other types of cells are also needed before drawing Any clear conclusions. Moreover, the optimal level of calcium ion release from pulp capping materials is essential, as previously mentioned. Since there is no relationship between the proliferation abilities of SHEDs and different calcium concentrations, the optimal range may be specific. While the current study focused on short term cellular responses, future investigations would greatly benefit from a more extensive characterization of odontogenic differentiation. Specifically, assessing the expression of additional late-stage odontogenic marker genes, such as dentin matrix protein 1 (DMP1) and dentin sialophosphoprotein (DSPP), is crucial. Such studies would provide deeper insights into the mechanisms facilitating odontogenic differentiation and could more definitively explain the processes leading to secondary dentin formation.

While this study provides valuable insights into the cytocompatibility And biological effects of pulp capping materials on SHEDs, this study is subject to several limitations inherent to its in vitro experimental design. First, the cell culture conditions do not recapitulate the complex biological environment of the dental pulp, including immune interactions, vascularization, And mechanical stimuli, which may influence cellular responses differently in vivo. Second, although the extraction media were prepared in accordance with ISO 10,993 Part 12, this may not fully reflect the variability encountered in clinical applications. For example, the amount of material applied, or the degree of pulp exposure may lead to differences in ion release And cytotoxic responses that are not captured in this model. Additionally, although calcium ion release was monitored for up to 7 days, longer-term release profiles, which are clinically relevant for materials intended to support dentin bridge formation over weeks, were not assessed. This short observation period may not fully represent the sustained bioactivity or delayed cytotoxicity of these materials. Another consideration is the use of static culture conditions for all assays. Unlike in vivo settings, where the dynamic movement of fluids and continual turnover of extracellular signals can influence material behavior and cellular responses, static conditions may cause accumulation of degradation products or unrepresentative exposure to ionic components, potentially affecting results. Additionally, although SHEDs represent a clinically relevant model for pediatric pulp tissue, inter-donor variability and passage-dependent cellular changes may influence outcomes. Future investigations employing extended observation periods, dynamic culture systems, and in vivo validation are warranted to enhance translational relevance.

The efficacy of these materials remains a crucial area for clinical investigation. While these findings were limited to an in vitro model system, it is important to take precautions when utilizing such materials in clinical practice, as they have the potential to induce irritation and cellular damage. This information may be useful in the development of pulp capping materials aimed at preserving cell viability and promoting SHEDs proliferation and functional differentiation.

Conclusion

The study findings showed that Calcimol LC, ProRoot® MTA, and Biodentine™ were less toxic to SHEDs than Dycal® and Theracal™ LC. Biodentine™, which released significantly higher amounts of calcium than Dycal®, demonstrated a slight increase effect in cell proliferation and significantly increased in vitro mineralization compared to Dycal® and Theracal™ LC. These findings implied that not only the calcium released concentration but also the additional factors or ingredients in pulp capping materials may also play a role in the mechanism of the hard tissue repair process.
